Real-world adherence to and persistence with ofatumumab, infusion, oral, and other self-injectable disease-modifying therapies in patients with multiple sclerosis
Authors: Ming-Hui Tai, Brandon Brown, Riley Taiji, Ariane Faucher, Francis Vekeman, Raluca Ionescu-Ittu, Abhijit Gadkari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment schedule complexity and route of administration may influence patient adherence and persistence. Limited real-world evidence exists comparing these outcomes across treatments for multiple sclerosis (MS).ObjectiveTo examine 18- and 24-month adherence/persistence of ofatumumab (OMB) and other approved disease-modifying therapies (DMTs).MethodsOptum Clinformatics® Data Mart data were used to identify adult patients with MS who initiated treatment with an approved DMT from August 20, 2019, to December 31, 2023. Patients were stratified into four treatment groups: OMB, infusion DMTs, oral DMTs, and other injectable DMTs.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, adherence to OMB was significantly higher than to oral DMTs (18 months: 73% vs. 51% [p < .001]; 24 months: 69% vs. 47% [p < .001]) and was also higher with OMB than with other injectable agents (18 months: 74% vs. 40% [p < .001]; 24 months: 70% vs. 35% [p < .001]). Adherence to OMB and infusion DMTs was comparable (18 months: 74% vs. 70% [p = .31]; 24 months: 70% vs. 65% [p = .24]). Similar patterns were observed for persistence between the groups.ConclusionThese findings suggest OMB is a convenient, effective, self-administered alternative to infusion therapies.Keywords: Adherence, persistence, ofatumumab, disease-modifying therapies, multiple sclerosis

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease that affects the central nervous system and is characterized by inflammation, demyelination, and axonal degeneration. The condition can manifest in severe neurological symptoms, such as impaired movement, vision issues, and pain, affecting approximately 2.9 million people worldwide in 2023.1–4

The treatment landscape for MS has evolved significantly over the past few decades, transitioning from general immunomodulatory therapies, such as interferons and glatiramer acetate, to highly specific therapies such as B-cell monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). 5 The mAbs target immune components critical to MS pathogenesis, significantly reducing relapse rates and slowing disease progression. Consequently, they have become the preferred treatments for MS. 6

The complexity of treatment schedules and route of administration of B-cell mAbs and other disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) may influence patient adherence and persistence. Oral DMTs, such as fingolimod, dimethyl fumarate, and teriflunomide, provide the convenience of at-home administration, but require strict daily adherence.7–9 Infusion DMTs, such as natalizumab and ocrelizumab, are administered monthly for natalizumab and biannually for ocrelizumab in a healthcare setting, which can require logistical planning for infusion appointments.10,11 Ofatumumab (OMB) represents a newer generation of self-injectables, offering a high-efficacy DMT in a monthly subcutaneous home injection. 12 This contrasts with older self-injectables that require more frequent dosing like glatiramer acetate (daily or 3 times a week) 13 and interferons (ranging from every other day to biweekly).14–17 Patients treated with older self-injectables may also experience increased rates of adverse events due to more frequent dosing, such as localized injection site reactions. 18

Real-world (RW) data examining how different administration routes and dosing frequencies influence adherence and persistence are needed, as these factors impact therapeutic success and patient quality of life.19,20 Due to its relatively recent approval, there is a paucity of RW data for persistence and adherence rates with OMB.

To address this gap, the current study aimed to compare 18- and 24-month adherence and persistence of OMB with infusion, oral, and other self-injectable DMTs in a RW sample of patients with MS using US administrative claims data.

Material and methods

Data source, study design, and patient selection

This study adopted a retrospective cohort design using Optum Clinformatics® Data Mart (CDM) data from August 20, 2019, to December 31, 2023 (study period; Figure 1), a longitudinal database of medical and pharmacy administrative claims for patients enrolled in commercial insurance and Medicare Advantage plans in the USA.

Overview of study design.

Abbreviations: DMT: disease-modifying therapy; FDA: US Food and Drug Administration; MS: multiple sclerosis; OMB: ofatumumab.

The study population included patients with MS who initiated treatment with a US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved MS DMT between August 20, 2020 (OMB FDA approval date) and December 31, 2021 (patient identification period). Patients were stratified into four treatment groups based on the index DMT: OMB, infusion DMT, oral DMT, and other injectable DMT.

Patients were required to meet the following eligibility criteria. (a) At least one incident claim for OMB, infusion DMT (ocrelizumab, mitoxantrone, natalizumab, or ublituximab), oral DMT (cladribine, dimethyl fumarate, diroximel fumarate, fingolimod, monomethyl fumarate, ozanimod, ponesimod, siponimod, and teriflunomide), or other injectable DMT (glatiramer acetate, interferon beta-1a, interferon beta-1b, and peginterferon beta-1a) in the patient identification period. The index date was set as the date of first claim for OMB, infusion DMT, oral DMT, or other self-injectable DMT. Alemtuzumab was excluded, as the treatment is limited to two courses 12 months apart, making it unsuitable for an assessment of 24-month adherence and persistence. Off-label rituximab use was also excluded. (b) At least two outpatient medical claims (≥ 30 days apart) with a diagnosis code for MS (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD-10] Clinical Modification code: G35) in any position or at least one inpatient claim with a diagnosis of MS in the first position in the 12 months before or 6 months after index date. (c) At least 18 years of age on index date. (d) Continuous healthcare plan enrollment ≥ 12 months before and ≥ 24 months after index date.

Demographic and disease characteristics, as well as treatment history, were balanced between the OMB treatment group versus the infusion DMT, oral DMT, and other injectable DMT treatment groups, respectively, using propensity score matching. Further details on patient selection and propensity score matching are provided in Supplemental Methods 1 and Supplemental Table 1.

Outcomes were assessed overall by treatment group and in selected subgroups, including commercially insured patients as well as commercially insured patients aged ≤ 65 years old with ≥ 2 fills/administrations of index DMT. The former subgroup was selected to assess how the inclusion of Medicare Advantage patients in Optum CDM impacted the overall persistence and adherence rates, whereas the latter was selected as a proxy for the study populations of Engmann et al. (2021) and Pardo et al. (2022) to allow comparability of results. Additionally, adherence and persistence were assessed by index DMT, where index DMTs were restricted to those representing ≥ 50% of the corresponding treatment group or with N ≥ 200. Subgroups were re-matched to ensure balance.

Study measures

Patient demographic and clinical characteristics measured at index date or in the 12-month period before the index date (pre-index period) were described by treatment group before and after matching. Covariates included age at index date, sex, race, region, payer type, DMTs used in pre-index period, MS relapses, Deyo-Charlson Comorbidity Index (DCCI), Psychiatric Diagnosis Group Index, MS-related symptoms and secondary conditions, and MS-related disability score. MS relapse and MS-related disability were calculated using validated claims-based algorithms. Relapse events were identified via criteria involving MS-related hospital or outpatient visits with high-dose corticosteroid or related treatment use. MS-related disability was classified as mild-to-severe based on Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS)-related symptoms and medical equipment use, weighted by severity.21–23

Outcomes included adherence and persistence and were measured in the variable ≥ 24-month period from index date until the earliest of the following: the end of continuous enrollment, death, or end of study period on December 31, 2023 (post-index period). Adherence was defined as proportion of days covered (PDC) ≥ 0.8. PDC was calculated as: (number of covered days in period/number of days in period). Persistence was defined as the time in months from index date until treatment discontinuation, where discontinuation was defined as a gap ≥ 60 days of index DMT or switch to another DMT. The end date of supply before the gap was taken as the discontinuation date. Switch to another DMT was defined as ≥ 1 claim for another MS-related DMT (including rituximab). The claim date of the other DMT was taken as the switch date.

For OMB, other injectable DMTs, and the majority of oral DMTs, days of supply were determined directly from claims. For cladribine and infusion DMTs, days of supply were imputed as the lesser of the FDA-indicated schedule or the interval between consecutive doses. Further details on construction of days of supply are provided in Supplemental Methods 2.

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize pre-index demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were summarized using mean (standard deviation [SD]) and categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Patient characteristics were compared before and after matching using the standardized mean difference (SMD).

The percentages of patients adherent and persistent at 18 and 24 months were estimated by treatment. For percentage adherent, unadjusted p-values were calculated using chi-square tests. For persistence, a Kaplan‒Meier approach was used and unadjusted p-values at each timepoint were calculated using log-rank tests on survival curves truncated at the given timepoint (18 or 24 months).

Results

Patient attrition

A total of 252 patients met inclusion criteria for the OMB treatment group, 831 for the infusion DMT treatment group, 825 for the oral DMT treatment group, and 225 for the other injectable DMT treatment group (Figure 2).

Patients treated with OMB were matched 1:1 with patients in the infusion DMT, oral DMT, and other injectable DMT treatment groups, respectively. After matching patients treated with OMB 1:1 with each treatment group, there were 237 matched patients in the infusion DMT group, 229 in the oral DMT group, and 129 in the other injectable DMT group.

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Patients in the OMB group had a mean (SD) age at index date of 49 (12) years (Table 1). Age at index date varied widely, ranging from 21 to 78 years. Most (77%) patients were female, 69% were White, and 39% were enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan (Table 1). The most common MS-related symptoms and secondary conditions were fatigue or malaise (38%), anxiety (37%), and sensory problems (25%; Table 1). The mean (SD) age-adjusted DCCI value of 0.9 (1.6) indicated a relatively low level of comorbidity burden in the sample (Table 1).

In the infusion DMT treatment group, 85% of patients had ocrelizumab as the index DMT, with the remaining 15% of patients using natalizumab (Supplemental Table 2). Treatment history

In general, the OMB treatment group received more prior DMTs than other treatment groups, whereas the other injectable DMT treatment group received the least (Figure 3). This is consistent with the later approval timing of OMB and the earlier approvals of older self-injectables such as glatiramer acetate and interferons. Sixty-nine percent of patients in the OMB treatment group received another DMT in the pre-index period compared with 35% in the infusion DMT treatment group, 48% in the oral DMT treatment group, and 25% in the other injectable DMT treatment group. After matching, treatment groups were balanced with respect to treatment history (Supplemental Table 3).

Adherence

Adherence to OMB and infusion DMTs was comparable in the matched OMB and infusion DMT treatment groups (18 months: p = .31; 24 months: p = .24; Table 2, Supplemental Table 4). After matching in the OMB and oral DMT treatment groups, adherence was significantly higher in the OMB treatment group (p < .001 for both 18 and 24 months; Table 2). Similarly, after matching in the OMB and other injectable DMT treatment groups, adherence was significantly higher in the OMB treatment group (p < .001 for both 18 and 24 months; Table 2).

Subgroup analyses

In the commercially insured subgroup of OMB (before matching), adherence at 18 and 24 months was 82% and 79%, whereas persistence was 78% and 73% (Supplemental Table 6). These rates were 7–8% higher than in the overall OMB treatment group, which includes Medicare Advantage patients (Tables 2 and 3). Comparisons of the commercially insured OMB subgroup with matched treatment groups (Figure 5, Supplemental Table 7) align with the overall findings. In summary, although including Medicare Advantage patients lowered adherence and persistence rates, the relative differences between OMB and other treatment groups remained consistent. Similar patterns were observed in commercially insured patients aged ≤ 65 years old with ≥ 2 fills/administrations of index DMT (Figure 5, Supplemental Table 7).

To assess whether the overlap of the study period with the COVID-19 pandemic affected results, a subgroup analysis was conducted comparing persistence and adherence rates of patients who initiated treatment during and after the acute pandemic phase. April 1, 2021 was selected as the cutoff, corresponding to the beginning of widespread vaccine access following FDA authorization of the Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna, and Johnson & Johnson vaccines. Persistence and adherence rates were consistent between patients indexed during and after the acute pandemic phase (Supplemental Figure 1).

Discussion

While clinical data can provide insight into persistence and adherence of selected populations in a highly controlled setting, RW studies reflect RW challenges and barriers to patient compliance. RW research investigating persistence and adherence of approved DMTs can be valuable for making informed disease management and treatment decisions in clinical practice. Due to its relatively recent approval, there is currently a need for RW studies investigating treatment persistence and adherence with OMB. This study provided insights into the adherence and persistence profiles of patients with MS treated with OMB, comparing OMB with other DMTs, including infusion, oral, and other injectable DMTs. OMB showed superior adherence and persistence at 18 and 24 months compared with both oral and other injectable DMTs. This indicated that the monthly self-injectable regimen of OMB may offer a more manageable treatment schedule than the frequent administration required by older injectables and oral DMTs.

Rates of adherence to and persistence with OMB were comparable with those observed with biannually administered ocrelizumab, which comprised 85% of the infusion DMT cohort, and monthly administered natalizumab. This similarity, despite the less frequent administration required by ocrelizumab, underscores the impact of administration route on compliance. The ability to self-inject OMB at home may improve its adherence and persistence by increasing patient autonomy and convenience.

Comparing these results with previously published estimates (see Supplemental Table 9), observed rates of adherence and persistence from the current study were 16–17% lower for infusion DMTs, 11–17% lower for oral DMTs, and 3–9% lower for other injectable DMTs than 18-month estimates 24 and 24-month estimates.20,25 There are two primary reasons for the lower rates observed in this study. First, eligibility criteria in the current study only required one fill/administration of the index DMT, whereas the previous studies required ≥ 2 fills/administrations.24,25 For ocrelizumab, specifically, past studies required two fill/administrations to align with the FDA-approved dosing schedule. Second, the current study included older (aged > 65 years old) patients and those enrolled in Medicare Advantage. In contrast, previous studies exclusively used data from commercially insured working-age populations sourced from PharMetrics and MarketScan databases.20,24

The results presented here are consistent with RW data published after the completion of this study. Hersh and colleagues investigated persistence and adherence rates in MS patients initiating OMB, oral DMTs, and platform self-injectables. 26 Similar to the results reported here, a higher proportion of patients were persistent on OMB compared with oral DMTs and other injectables at 6- and 12-month post-index. Adherence to OMB was also higher than other injectables at both 6- and 12-month post-index. However, adherence to OMB was similar to oral DMTs at these time points whereas increased adherence to OMB compared with oral DMTs is reported here.

Subgroup analyses were performed in which the infusion, oral, and other injectable DMT treatment groups were restricted to patients with ≥ 2 fills/administrations who were aged ≤ 65 years old and commercially insured. Estimates of 18- and 24-month adherence and persistence were within ±4% of previously published estimates,20,24 except for 24-month persistence for oral DMTs, which was 10% lower (see Supplemental Table 9). This suggests that the more selective patient populations used in previous studies largely contributed to higher rates of adherence and persistence for infusion, oral, and other injectable DMTs than those observed in the current study. By including patients with only one fill/administration and older non-commercially insured patients, the current study explored adherence and persistence across a broader and more representative sample of patients with MS, capturing the diverse challenges (e.g., in healthcare access) and behaviors encountered in RW settings.

This study has several limitations. Analyses using claims data were dependent on the accuracy and specificity of entered diagnostic codes. In addition, reasons for discontinuation or switching were not captured. Early discontinuation may be overestimated if treatment occurred outside the purview of the claims data source, for example, if patients continued treatment under a different payer structure, including cash payments or within a clinical trial. Data are primarily from employer-sponsored plans and therefore underrepresent patients aged > 65 years old and those on Medicaid, potentially limiting generalizability of findings. OMB received approval in August 2020, resulting in a relatively limited number of RW patients with sufficient follow-up to assess adherence and persistence. As this study was conducted between August 20, 2020 and December 31, 2021, it is possible that the results were impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Although a subgroup analysis comparing patients who were indexed before and after COVID-19 vaccines were made available found that the two groups did not differ in persistence and adherence rates (Supplemental Figure 1), the relatively short patient identification period and long follow-up windows resulted in all patients initiating index DMT prior to vaccine availability having part of their 18- and 24-month adherence and persistence outcomes extend into the post-vaccine period. Furthermore, this study assumed that all medications were given per the approved dosing schedule. Extended interval dosing of some IV therapies occurred as a risk-mitigation strategy during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may underestimate adherence and persistence. Future studies with longer data availability could more fully capture long-term adherence and persistence before, during, and after the acute pandemic period.

Conclusion

In a RW sample of patients with MS, OMB had superior adherence and persistence at 18 and 24 months compared with both oral and other injectable DMTs, and comparable adherence to and persistence with infusion DMTs. Patients starting OMB continued their treatment for an average of 37 months, with approximately three-quarters remaining adherent during the first 2 years. These results may support OMB as a convenient, effective, self-administered alternative to infusion therapies.
