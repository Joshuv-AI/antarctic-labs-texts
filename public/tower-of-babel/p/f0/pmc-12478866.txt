Tunable Plasmonic Circular Dichroism of Hierarchical Chiral Assemblies
Authors: Jaime Gabriel Trazo, Andrés Serrano Freijeiro, Mikhail Mychinko, Nika Kutalia, Marianne Impéror-Clerc, Sara Bals, Luis M. Liz-Marzán, Jorge Pérez-Juste, Isabel Pastoriza-Santos, Cyrille Hamon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plasmonic circular dichroism (PCD) is a powerful approach for enhancing chiroptical effects at the nanoscale with promising applications such as optical information processing, sensing, and catalysis. However, a key challenge remains in achieving both strong and tunable optical responses. While most strategies use either intrinsically chiral nanoparticles (NPs) or achiral NPs arranged into chiral assemblies, combining both offers a promising route to amplify chiral optical responses. Here, 4-fold-twisted gold nanorods (cGNRs) were assembled by depletion-induced self-assembly (DISA) into chiral superstructures with tunable PCD properties. By reducing cGNR helicity via chemical etching, we study how building block helicity affects hierarchical structure formation. Whereas static helical supercrystals were obtained on substrates upon evaporation-induced self-assembly (EISA), DISA allowed us to dynamically modulate the interparticle distance in solution from 13 ± 1.2 nm to 6.9 ± 1.0 nm. The assembly/disassembly process was found to be fully reversible, thereby enabling a modulation of the g-factor by 2 orders of magnitude with a switching rate of approximately 1 h. Beyond achieving high dissymmetry factors of 0.2, our approach establishes a reconfigurable PCD platform that is free from ligand exchange constraints and compatible with different NP shapes. Though response times remain too slow for optical computing, these findings are relevant for the development of optical modulators and memory devices based on dynamic chiral metafluids.

Introduction

Plasmonic circular dichroism (PCD) is emerging as a powerful tool for amplifying chiroptical effects at the nanoscale, which can be driven by various mechanisms. − Here, we focus on a combination of optical activity arising from geometrical chirality, which can be observed in colloidal nanoparticles (NPs) lacking mirror and inversion symmetry, with the assembly of colloidal NPs into chiral superstructures. Colloidal PCD materials enable energy-selective, dissymmetric interactions with circularly polarized light, opening potential applications in chiral sensing, enantioselective catalysis, and optical information processing. − Various synthetic protocols have been developed to produce chiral plasmonic NPs, often involving chiral molecules or micelles, , while different strategies have also been employed for the assembly of achiral NPs into chiral structures. − Although these two approaches have evolved independently, a few recent studies propose combining them to obtain materials with high and tunable dissymmetry factors. − Some of these recent works have focused on assembling chiral NPs into achiral structures to induce a tunable PCD response, but no significant increase in the PCD amplitude was reported. Instead, different outcomes were observed: either a loss of the PCD response or a spectral shift in the PCD signal due to plasmonic coupling. , PCD enhancement appears to depend critically on hierarchical chirality, as shown in another work, where the assembly of 4-fold twisted chiral gold nanorods (cGNRs) into helical superstructures led to enhanced PCD compared to individual cGNRs. In the latter work, the assembly process was driven by the irreversible aggregation of colloids upon electrostatic screening. We hypothesized that introducing depletion forces in such systems would offer greater versatility in tuning the interactions between cGNRs, thereby enabling active control over the PCD response.

Depletion-induced self-assembly (DISA) is driven by an entropic attraction between colloids upon introduction of smaller particles, because bringing the colloids closer together increases the free volume available to the depletants (typically polymers or surfactant micelles). − The range of accessible interparticle distances is given by the size of the depletant, whereas the strength of the interaction increases with the osmotic pressure and, hence, with the depletant concentration. In the plasmonic NP field, DISA has been primarily used for shape purification and for the formation of assemblies on substrates in conjunction with evaporation-induced self-assembly (EISA). Recent works have demonstrated that DISA can be used for the reversible assembly of plasmonic NPs in suspension into well-defined structures with tunable lattice parameters. ,− In this process, there is no need for ligand exchange because the same surfactant used during NP synthesis can also be used for DISA, where the micelles serve as effective depletants. Although various anisotropic NP shapes have been successfully assembled using DISA, the self-assembly of chiral NPs via this method remains unexplored.

Reconfigurable PCD materials offer precise control over spectral and dynamic aspects of the PCD response, which is essential for developing responsive chiral nanodevices. , While PCD can be manipulated by applying external stimuli (e.g., chemical, electrical, mechanical, or electromagnetic) to NPs attached onto a substrate, their structural conformation remains largely unchanged. In contrast, chiral assemblies dispersed in solution offer a promising alternative because colloids can be assembled, disassembled, and rearranged into tailored structures to modulate the PCD response. Beyond being fundamentally different from substrate-based strategies, such “metafluids” are isotropic, thereby avoiding the dependence of excitation incidence angle, and their ability to reconfigure in volume can be advantageous for chiral sensing and catalysis, by facilitating interactions between target molecules and NPs. Typically, existing reconfigurable systems rely on soft matter templates, like liquid crystals or DNA origami, to control the orientation of achiral plasmonic NPs. − However, these template-based approaches impose limitations on versatility and scalability, requiring processing steps such as ligand exchange and template design, which constrain the NP shape, size, and achievable assemblies. Moreover, the assembly of chiral NPs into higher-order chiral nanostructures is yet to be demonstrated by these methods. In contrast, DISA offers a template-free approach that can simply consist of modulating the concentration of the surfactant used during NP synthesis to drive their assembly. Here, we show that DISA can be used to assemble higher-order chiral structures with dynamically tunable chiroptical properties. This represents a significant step toward the creation of active chiral fluids capable of controlling light’s amplitude, frequency, and polarization in real time.

We report the reconfigurable assembly of cGNRs into hierarchical chiral structures. We synthesized cGNRs and controlled their helicity through precise chemical etching, resulting in nanostructures with varying lattice symmetries. Using EISA with the same cGNRs, we obtained supercrystals with a helical structure, whereas DISA allowed for tunable inter-NP distances by adjusting the depletant concentration, ranging from 13 to 7 nm. The g-factor of the assembled cGNRs was enhanced by 2 orders of magnitude compared to the isolated cGNRs measuring at the same wavelength, with these assemblies reaching a highest value around 0.2. Furthermore, the PCD response could be dynamically adjusted in solution by cGNR assembly and disassembly. This work demonstrates that DISA enables the formation of template-free, reconfigurable chiral materials with tunable chiroptical properties.

Results and Discussion

4-Fold-twisted cGNRs were synthesized by seeded growth on achiral single-crystalline GNR seeds, in the presence of a molecular chiral inducernamely, l-cysteine. To systematically adjust the helicity of the resulting cGNRs, controlled chemical etching was performed using Au3+ as an oxidant, which resulted in a gradual transformation of the twisted cGNRs into achiral nanorods with rounded edges (Figure a). Our study focused on three GNR batches: as-synthesized chiral nanorods (cGNR_1), partially oxidized nanorods (cGNR_2) obtained by etching for 3 h, and fully oxidized nanorods (cGNR_3) obtained by extended etching for 24 h. The dimensions of the resulting nanoparticles, determined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), in terms of length (L) and width (W) were as follows: cGNR_1 (L = 114 ± 11 nm, W = 59 ± 4 nm); cGNR_2 (L = 104 ± 10 nm, W = 55 ± 3 nm); and cGNR_3 (L = 89 ± 10 nm, W = 61 ± 3 nm) (Figure S1). Representative cGNRs from each sample were investigated by electron tomography (ET), and the 3D reconstructions evidenced that the degree of twisting decreased in the order cGNR_1 > cGNR_2 > cGNR_3 (Figure b). This trend was confirmed by quantitative analysis of the twisted morphology through computation of the helicity function H­(ρ,α) from the 3D reconstructions as recently reported (Figures c, see Supporting Information for details on the quantification). Indeed, total helicity values of H tot of 0.065, 0.045, and 0.018 were determined for cGNRs obtained at increasing etching times. The positive sign of the helicity measure indicates a right-handedness of the nanorods, as expected from seed-mediated growth in the presence of l-cysteine. As etching progressed, a blue shift of the longitudinal plasmon band was observed (Figure d), along with a reduction in circular dichroism (Figure e), indicating the loss of plasmonic chirality due to cGNR etching.

All three cGNR colloids were then self-assembled by using two methods: EISA on a substrate and DISA in solution. For the dried assemblies, the resulting nanostructures were analyzed via in situ small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Figure ).

We studied the self-assembly of cGNRs into supercrystals by EISA. A droplet of each cGNR sample in a 2.5 mM cetyltrimethylammonium chloride (CTAC) solution was allowed to evaporate slowly on a silicon substrate (Figure a). Upon complete drying, supercrystals with semielliptical shapes and of varying sizes were analyzed by SEM (Figures b,d and S4–S6). The average size of the supercrystals increased as the helicity of the rods decreased, ranging from 5.3 ± 2.5 μm2 for cGNR_1, to 38.1 ± 14.4 μm2 for cGNR_2, and 30.3 ± 10.7 μm2 for cGNR_3 (Figure S2). These results suggest that the packing frustration for cGNR_1 is more likely to introduce lattice defects than that for cGNR_3, which in turn causes the supercrystals to break at smaller sizes. For complementary structural analysis, we collected 2D SAXS images and processed the data to generate the integrated diffractograms shown in Figure c. In SAXS, the beam footprint measured approximately 175 × 300 μm2, which is significantly larger than that of a single supercrystal. As a result, each measurement represents an average over multiple supercrystals, providing a broader structural overview that complements the local observations obtained through SEM. For both cGNR_1 and cGNR_2, SEM micrographs revealed a layered helical structure (Figure d), which was indexed as a rectangularly centered (rhombic) lattice by SAXS (Figure c). This structure consists of stacked twisted layers of cGNRs, where each individual layer has a centered rectangular lattice. On the other hand, a hexagonal lattice structure was identified for cGNR_3 (Figure c). We also collected SAXS diffractograms at different positions on the sample, because the structure could vary between the edge and the center due to the coffee ring effect (Figure S3). − While the structure of cGNR_2 supercrystals remained consistent across the substrate, cGNR_1 supercrystals were more extended at the edges of the deposit. Additionally, the orientation of cGNR_3 gradually shifted from horizontal (long axis parallel to the substrate) at the edges to vertical (long axis perpendicular to the substrate) toward the center. Variation in nanorod orientation across the sample and semielliptical supercrystal shapes were observed across a range of nanorod systems with differing shapes and surface chemistries, − suggesting that the underlying mechanism is independent of these specific parameters. Although the coffee-ring effect is known to generate gradients in NP and surfactant concentrations during evaporation, , the precise role of these gradients in directing the formation and symmetry of supercrystals is not yet fully understood. Further studies are required to disentangle the contributions of evaporation dynamics, interparticle forces, and interfacial effects to the final superstructure morphology. Overall, our experiments demonstrate that supercrystals with hierarchical chirality can be achieved through EISA for building blocks with geometrical helicity. However, since the structures are dried, they are nonreconfigurable.

Our second approach involved the self-assembly of cGNRs in solution through DISA. , As CTAC micelles act as effective depletants, the cGNRs were exposed to various CTAC concentrations to induce the formation of colloidal assemblies (Figure a).

At 2.5 mM CTAC, the SAXS curves correspond to the form factor of noninteracting isolated NPs, indicating stable dispersions (Figure b). At CTAC concentrations above 60 mM, Bragg peaks emerge, indicating the formation of colloidal cGNR assemblies. The peak indexation revealed different structures depending on the nanorod helicity. The cGNR_1 sample exhibited a 1:2:3:4 peak progression, corresponding to the formation of one-dimensional (1D) nanostructures with an internal side-by-side nanorod alignment (Figure b). In comparison, cGNR_2 and cGNR_3 assemble in solution into 2D nanostructures, consistent with their EISA counterpart. While cGNR_2 assembles into 2D centered rectangular lattices (Figure S7a), the cGNR_3 lattice is hexagonal (Figure S7b). Debye–Scherrer analysis revealed that the extension of the lattices was consistently smaller for cGNR_1, compared to cGNR_3 (Figure S8). These trends align with the size analysis obtained for EISA supercrystals (Figure S2), yet the smaller size of assemblies formed in solution compared to those formed upon drying suggests that the slow drying process promotes the development of higher-order structures. Although lattice symmetries were similar between EISA and DISA samples, the inter-NP gap distances differed significantly between the two strategies. The inter-NP distance averaged 2.6 ± 0.9 nm in the EISA samples, whereas it ranged from 13.2 ± 1.2 nm to 6.9 ± 1.0 nm in DISA for cGNR_1 as the CTAC concentration in the colloidal dispersion was varied (Figure c,d). This shifting equilibrium distance results from the balance between attractive forces (depletion and van der Waals) and repulsive electrostatic interactions. At around 500 mM in CTAC, the inter-NP spacing approaches the thickness of the two surfactant bilayers coating adjacent cGNRs (≈6.4 nm). Overall, SAXS experiments indicate that the assemblies of cGNRs formed by DISA correspond to the helical assemblies formed by EISA in terms of lattice symmetry (but not inter-NP distance). To complement SAXS results, we carried out liquid-phase TEM (LPTEM) and cryo-TEM to image the assemblies of cGNR_1 in the direct space (Figures S9, S10). In both cases, a side-to-side arrangement was evidenced, supporting the formation of 1D nanostructures in solution. This set of experiments allowed us to confirm a larger inter-NP distance for assemblies obtained via DISA, compared to EISA, with approximately 10 nm observed in solution (Figure S10), versus 2.5 nm in the dried state, in good agreement with SAXS diffractograms. Although LPTEM and cryo-TEM confirmed the presence of large interparticle distances, they did not allow us to estimate the twist angles. This limitation stems from the tendency of such assemblies to reorganize, especially in confined environments where NPs align with the interfaces. We have previously studied this effect and demonstrated that assemblies formed via DISA can undergo rearrangement before and during TEM observation, mainly due to radiolysis effects.

We now turn our focus to the PCD response of the colloidal assemblies mediated by DISA (Figure a). As a control experiment, tests were conducted at a low CTAC concentration (10 mM), where no assemblies were observed, so both extinction and CD spectra remained unaltered (Figure b).

At CTAC concentrations above 50 mM, DISA induced a significant enhancement of the chiroptical response of the cGNR suspensions (Figures c and S11). Kinetic experiments were conducted by monitoring the CD signal throughout the self-assembly process. As shown in Figure c for 70 mM CTAC, assemblies formed rapidly within 30 s and continued to grow over the following 30 min. Kinetic SAXS experiments confirmed that the assemblies formed within 2 min (Figure S12). At the earlier stages of DISA, enhanced CD responses compared to individual cGNRs were recorded, together with a reversal of the bisignate peak of the assemblies compared to the individual NPs (Figures c and S11). For instance, the cGNR_1 assemblies achieved an optical asymmetry g-factor of 0.2 at 740 nm, which was 2 orders of magnitude higher than that of individual c-GNRs (0.0016 at the same wavelength). We infer that chiral amplification may arise from the formation of clusters rather than requiring extended order, as supported by SAXS experiments. Moreover, the inversion in chirality can be attributed to a gain in compactness when the cGNRs of a given helicity self-assemble into structures with opposite handedness. At later stages of the DISA process, the CD signal red-shifted and decreased in amplitude, likely due to the formation of larger assemblies that sedimented at the bottom of the cuvette (Figure a). Similar results were obtained for CTAC concentrations ranging from 50 to 756 mM (Figure S11). It should be noted that the decrease in the CD signal at CTAC concentrations above 100 mM is due to the extremely fast depletion and sedimentation of the particles, which does not allow for a reliable measurement of the CD response of the assembly. Interestingly, at any stage of the process, the assemblies sedimented in the cuvette could be redispersed, restoring a CD signal close to its initial maximum value, even after 10 h (Figure d). Analyzing the extinction and CD band during the self-assembly revealed a gradual red shift due to plasmon coupling between neighboring NPs (Figures c and S11). Since side-to-side NP coupling would result in a blue shift of the resonance modes, the red shift here suggests the formation of self-assembled structures with multiple coupling modes, including both side-to-side and end-to-end interactions. Interestingly, plotting the maximum CD as a function of CTAC concentration revealed a maximum when particles were dispersed in 90–200 mM CTAC (Figures e, S11).

From the SAXS analysis, we can conclude that the overall size of the assemblies did not change significantly with the added CTAC concentration (Figure S13), counting 3–4 cGNRs per assembly and with inter-NP distances varying between 10.9 ± 1.2 and 8.9 ± 1.1 nm within the 90–200 mM CTAC range. Therefore, the variation of the CD response with CTAC concentration could be ascribed to the assembly of cGNRs. Similar experiments were carried out with cGNR_2 and cGNR_3 at 70 mM CTAC. As shown in Figure S14, self-assembly led to an enhanced CD signal for cGNR_2, whereas the increment was lower than that for cGNR_1. In contrast, no significant CD response was detected for cGNR_3. LPTEM and Cryo-TEM analyses of the assemblies confirmed the presence of a ladder-type assembly with relatively large interparticle distances (see Figures S9, S10), but, unfortunately, these techniques did not allow us to obtain a reliable estimate of the twist angle. Recently, Liu et al. reported finite difference time domain (FDTD) simulations for assemblies comprising cGNRs with a similar morphology to those presented in our study. The authors studied in detail the effect of the number of particles forming the assembly, the gap distance between particles, and the rotation angle between adjacent particles. The results indicate the highest CD signal for assemblies containing 3–5 chiral nanorods, with gap distances of 3–6 nm and rotation angles between 20° and 30° (see Figure in ref ). Interestingly, the reported FDTD simulations are in good agreement with our experimental spectra. Therefore, even though the limitations of the DISA approach do not allow an in situ determination of the rotation angle, we propose that the FDTD simulations reported in ref sufficiently explain our experimental data.

We then leveraged the tunability of the depletion interactions to actively adjust the PCD response of these assemblies. Initially, cGNR_1 was self-assembled via DISA at 70 mM CTAC, and the resulting sediment was subsequently redispersed in a 1 mM CTAC solution inside the same cuvette (Figures f and S15). This cycle was repeated multiple times, leading to reversible g-factor variations spanning 2 orders of magnitude (from 0.0016 to 0.2 measured at 740 nm) as the system switched between disassembled and assembled states. Notably, at higher wavelengths (e.g., 880 nm), the dispersed cGNRs display almost no display chiroptical activity (g-factor around 0.001), whereas the assemblies display a high g-factor of 0.1. This demonstrates that integrating chirality across different length scales is a promising strategy for designing materials with strong chiroptical properties over a broad spectral range. Additionally, our approach enables modulation of the g-factor in solution, achieving a two-orders-of-magnitude change with a switching rate of approximately 1 h, which can be accelerated by centrifugation for 5 min at 2240 rcf.

Conclusions

This work demonstrates that cGNRs can be assembled into hierarchical chiral nanostructures via DISA, exhibiting enhanced, spectrally tunable, and temporally dynamic PCD responses. Gold nanorods with a gradual degree of helicity were obtained by colloidal synthesis combined with chemical etching, allowing us to investigate how the chirality of the building blocks influences the construction of higher-order chiral structures by self-assembly. Different hierarchical structures, ranging from smectic to centered rectangular, were obtained by EISA, notably forming extended helical supercrystals on the substrates. In solution, the cGNRs can be reversibly assembled by DISA, resulting in a wide tunability of the PCD response. In these experiments, the inter-NP gap distance could be tuned from 13 ± 1.2 nm to 6.9 ± 1.0 nm by adjusting the CTAC concentration. The reversible process of assembling and disassembling the NPs could be repeated multiple times inside the same cuvette, leading to variations of the g-factor by 2 orders of magnitude when switching between the disassembled and assembled states. This work shows that DISA can be used to form nanostructures with hierarchical chirality displaying enhanced, spectrally tunable, and temporally evolving PCD responses. Besides achieving a remarkably high g-factor, we demonstrate reconfigurable PCD response with a switching rate of about 1 h. While these rates are still too slow for display technologies or optical computing, , they open new possibilities for optical modulators and memory devices. Overall, DISA offers a promising pathway to achieve hierarchical chirality in plasmonic NPs, with the added advantage over purely electrostatic strategies of greater versatility, enabling reconfigurability and tunable optical responses.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

All of the starting materials were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification: hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB for molecular biology, ≥ 99%), hexadecyltrimethylammonium chloride (CTAC, 95% w/w aqueous solution), 1-decanol (n-decanol, 98%), hydrogen tetrachloroaurate trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O, ≥ 99.9%), silver nitrate (AgNO3, ≥ 99.0%), l-ascorbic acid (≥99%), hydroquinone (HQ, ≥ 99%), sodium borohydride (NaBH4, 99%), d-cysteine (d-cys, 99%), and l-cysteine (l-cys, 99%). Milli-Q-grade water (resistivity 18.2 MΩ cm at 25 °C) was used in all experiments. All reagents were used as received without further purification.

Characterization

TEM and low-magnification TEM images were obtained on a JEOL JEM-1400PLUS transmission electron microscope operating at an acceleration voltage of 120 kV. For UV–vis–NIR spectroscopy, extinction spectra were recorded using a Jasco V-750 spectrophotometer. Regarding circular dichroism spectroscopy, spectra were recorded using a Jasco V-1700 CD-spectrophotometer. For the characterization of colloidal dispersions, quartz cuvettes with an optical path of 5 mm were used for the extinction and CD equipment. Au0 concentrations of the cGNR suspensions were estimated using their measured extinction at 400 nm, taking a NP concentration of 0.5 mM in Au0 for a sample absorbance of 1.2.

Synthesis of cGNRs

cGNRs were prepared by a five-step seed-mediated growth approach that can be described as follows: (1) the synthesis of 1–2 nm gold seeds, (2) synthesis of small anisotropic seeds, (3) synthesis of “minirods”, (4) synthesis of larger achiral rods, and (5) synthesis of the cGNRs. Steps 1–4 were adapted from González-Rubio et al. with minor modifications, and step 5 was described by Bing Ni et al. with slight modifications.(1)Synthesis of 1–2 nm gold seeds: in a 50 mL glass beaker, 50 μL of NaOH 0.5 M was added to 20 mL of an aqueous solution containing 50 mM CTAB and 13.5 mM n-decanol at 27 °C to adjust the pH to 7.6. Then, 200 μL of a 50 mM HAuCl4 solution was added, obtaining a yellowish solution. Immediately after, 800 μL of fresh 20 mM NaBH4 was added under strong magnetic stirring (1000–1100 rpm). The reduction was allowed to proceed under stirring for 2 min until the solution turned brownish yellow. The solution was then left undisturbed for at least 1 h at 27 °C prior to use.(2)Synthesis of anisotropic seeds: in a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask, 200 mL of an aqueous solution containing 50 mM CTAB and 13.5 mM n-decanol at 27 °C was prepared. Subsequently, 1.6 mL of 10 mM AgNO3, 14 mL of 1 M HCl, 2 mL of 50 mM HAuCl4, and 2.6 mL of 0.1 M ascorbic acid were added at 25 °C. Next, 12 mL of the previously prepared seed solution was added under stirring, and the mixture was allowed to age for at least 4 h until it turned dark brown. After that time, the longitudinal LSPR band should be centered around 730 nm. The obtained particles were centrifuged at 23140 rcf for 45 min in 2 mL tubes and redispersed in 5.5 mL of 10 mM CTAB, yielding a final Au0 concentration of 10 mM.(3)Synthesis of “minirods”. In a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask, a 200 mL solution of CTAB 50 mM and n-decanol 13.5 mM was prepared. Then, 1.6 mL of AgNO3 (10 mM), 14 mL of HCl (1 M) (HCl volume must be adjusted as specified in [1]), 2 mL of HAuCl4 (50 mM), and 2.6 mL of ascorbic acid (0.1 M) were subsequently added at 25 °C. Then, 12 mL of the previously prepared seed solution was added under stirring, and the mixture was left for aging for at least 4 h until it turned dark brown. After that time, longitudinal plasmon should peak at 730 nm, and the final particles were centrifuged at 15000 rpm for 45 min in 2 mL tubes and dispersed in 5.5 mL of CTAB 10 mM for a final Au0 concentration of 10 mM.(4)Synthesis of achiral AuNRs: first, 174 mL of an aqueous solution containing 50 mM CTAB and 11 mM n-decanol was prepared. Then, 2 mL of 50 mM HAuCl4 was added, and the resulting yellowish solution was left undisturbed for 10 min at 16 °C. Afterward, 3 mL of 10 mM AgNO3, 20 mL of 1 M HCl, 1.6 mL of 0.1 M ascorbic acid, and 400 μL of 10 mM Au “minirods” were sequentially added. The resulting mixture was left undisturbed for 5 h to allow complete anisotropic growth. Then, the particles were centrifuged at 5750 rcf for 20 min in 50 mL tubes and redispersed in 5 mL of 1 mM CTAB, yielding a final Au0 concentration of 5 mM. The LSPR maximum was at 1150 nm.(5)Chiral gold nanoparticles. Typically, 200 μL of HAuCl4 (10 mM) was dispersed in 10 mL of a 10 mM CTAC solution and incubated at 40 °C for 8–10 min. Then, for the chiral nanoparticles with a larger aspect ratio, 200 μL of l-ascorbic acid (0.1 M), 40 to 60 μL of L-/d-cysteine (20 μM), and 12 μL of achiral gold nanorods with LSPR at 1150 nm (5 mM) were added sequentially. For chiral particles with a smaller aspect ratio, 200 μL of l-ascorbic acid (0.1 M), 120 μL of L-/d-cysteine (20 μM), and 14 μL of achiral gold nanorods with LSPR at 1040 nm (5 mM) were added instead. The particles were then centrifuged (10 min at 3980 rcf) and dispersed in 1 mM CTAC twice.

Oxidation of cGNRs

cGNRs with different degrees of oxidation were prepared by a procedure described by Jessica Rodríguez-Fernández et al.’s report with slight modifications. 2.5 mL of 2 mM cGNRs dispersed in CTAC 1 mM was added to a 20 mL solution of CTAB (50 mM). Subsequently, 100 μL of HAuCl4 (50 mM) was added with stirring (500 rpm). The oxidation was then stopped by centrifugation (10 min at 4700 rcf twice, dispersing the particles in 1 mM CTAC) at different times to yield cGNR_2 and cGNR_3 after 3 h and 24 h, respectively.

Self-Assembly by EISA

A 10 μL drop of cGNRs (30 mM Au0, 2.5 mM CTAC) was drop-cast onto a substrate (either a silicon wafer or a glass coverslip) and allowed for around 48 h. Whereas Si wafers were preferred for SEM characterization, glass coverslips were preferred for SAXS characterization. Throughout the drying process, the sample was placed in a covered container with added water droplets to maintain saturated humidity, thereby slowing down evaporation.

Self-Assembly by DISA

Two procedures were tested for DISA, resulting in slow or fast self-assembly kinetics, as described in the following. This was tested because slow kinetics are known to induce more extended assemblies, including in DISA. While the results displayed in Figure S13 show an effect of the self-assembly kinetics, the shape of the cGNRs induced more notable effects on the size of the assemblies.(1)Slow kinetics. Reconfigurable PCD

In a 10 mL centrifuge tube, 2 mL of CTAC 87.5 mM and 500 μL of cGNRs were added for a final concentration of 70 mM CTAC. The CD and extinction spectra were immediately measured one after another in order to calculate the g-factor at the optimal assembly stage. Then, the sample was either left undisturbed until total sedimentation of the nanoparticles or centrifuged at 2240 rcf for 5 min to accelerate the sedimentation process, yielding the same result. The supernatant was then carefully removed, and the remaining NPs were dispersed in 2.5 mL of 1 mM CTAC. The CD and extinction spectra were then measured again, followed by another centrifugation at 3980 rcf for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitates at the bottom of the tube were dispersed with CTAC 70 mM, completing one cycle as shown in Figure G.

Small-Angle X-ray Scattering

SAXS data was collected at the SWING beamline of the SOLEIL Synchrotron (Saint-Aubin, France), using a beam energy of 12 keV and a sample-to-detector distance of 6.22 m. Each measurement had an exposure time of 1000 ms. The beam size was approximately 500 × 200 μm2 (horizontal × vertical). For colloidal samples in capillaries, a z-scan of the capillary was performed, while for dried samples prepared via EISA on a glass substrate, a radial scan of the droplet was conducted, measuring multiple points from the edge to the center. All measurements were performed at room temperature (295 K). The scattered signal was recorded using an Eiger 4 M detector (Dectris Ltd., Switzerland) with a pixel size of 75 μm. Preliminary data treatment, including angular averaging and normalization, was performed using Foxtrot, the dedicated software developed at the beamline (https://www.synchrotron-soleil.fr/fr/lignes-de-lumiere/swing). The processed data yielded intensity as a function of scattering vector I­(q) in absolute units. Further SAXS data modeling and analysis were conducted using either custom Python scripts or SasView 6.0.0.

Transmission Electron Microscopy, Electron Tomography, and Helicity Measurements

Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and electron tomography experiments (ET) were performed using a “cubed” Thermo Fisher Scientific Themis Z instrument operated at 300 kV in high-angle annular dark field STEM (HAADF-STEM) mode. Electron tomography (ET) series were acquired over an angular range of ± 75°, with a tilt increment of 3°, and were used as an input for SIRT reconstruction using the Astra Toolbox 1.8 for MATLAB R2019A. Visualization of the 3D reconstructions was performed using Amira 5.4.0 software. The helical morphology of cGNRs was quantitatively characterized using an updated version of our previously reported method. Liquid and cryostudies of assemblies of chiral NPs were performed using a single-use sealable sample holder (K-KIT) and a 2550 Cryo Transfer Tomography Holder (Fischione Instruments), respectively.
