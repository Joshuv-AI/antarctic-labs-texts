Comparative effectiveness of opioid versus opioid-free analgesia after outpatient breast surgery: PAIN-Alt trial protocol
Authors: Julio F Fiore Jr, Samin Shirzadi, Karine Roversi, Ipshita Prakash, Stephanie Wong, Sarkis Meterissian, Ari N Meguerditchian, Christine Desbiens, Justin Rivard, Megan Delisle, Lisa Findlay-Shirras, Jad Abou Khalil, Allison Maciver, May Lynn Quan, Karine Verreault, Sarah Johnston, Liane Feldman, Emily G McDonald, on behalf of the Postoperative Analgesia Intervention with Non-opioid Alternatives (PAIN-Alt) Collaboration
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExcessive opioid prescribing after surgery can lead to adverse events and exacerbate the opioid crisis. Patients undergoing outpatient breast surgery are often prescribed opioids to manage pain at home; however, the value of this approach is uncertain. The Postoperative Analgesia Intervention with Non-opioid Alternatives (PAIN Alt) trial will address the following research question: among patients undergoing outpatient breast surgery, does opioid-free analgesia (OFA) result in non-inferior 7-day pain intensity and pain interference in comparison to opioid analgesia (OA)?Methods and analysisThis is a parallel, assessor-blind, open-label randomised trial conducted at seven university-affiliated hospitals in Canada. A sample of 540 adult patients (>18 years) undergoing outpatient mastectomy or lumpectomy will be included. Participants are allocated 1:1 to receive OA (around-the-clock non-opioids and opioids for breakthrough pain) or OFA (around-the-clock non-opioids, with adjustment of non-opioid drugs and/or non-pharmacological interventions for breakthrough pain). The co-primary outcomes are 7-day pain intensity and pain interference (measured using the Brief Pain Inventory). Secondary outcomes include adverse drug events, physical and mental health status, satisfaction with pain management, postoperative complications, chronic pain, opioid misuse, persistent opioid use, healthcare utilisation and costs. The primary statistical analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle and be conducted using mixed-effects modelling.Ethics and disseminationThis trial is coordinated by the McGill University Health Centre (ethics approval MP-37-2024-102530), with ethics approval being sought at all participating sites. Our results will be published in an open-access, peer-reviewed journal, presented at relevant conferences and disseminated to the public through press releases.Trial registration numberNCT06507345.Keywords: SURGERY, Breast surgery, Pain management, Patient-Centered Care

Introduction

Excessive opioid prescribing has contributed to a devastating opioid crisis in North America, which has now spread to other parts of the world.1 Surgery often serves as the initial event for opioid-naïve patients to obtain a first opioid prescription, which can eventually culminate in misuse and substance use disorder.2 3 Each year, an estimated 2 million people worldwide undergo breast resection surgery for malignant and benign conditions,4 5 with an increasing proportion discharged on the same day.6,8 These patients are particularly susceptible to opioid-related harms, as they are often prescribed opioids to manage postoperative pain at home.9 Under current prescribing patterns, approximately 10% of opioid-naïve patients undergoing common breast surgery procedures (ie, mastectomy, lumpectomy) become persistent opioid users postoperatively.10 11 Those who do not become persistent users may also contribute to the opioid crisis by diverting unused pills. Of all opioid pills obtained by patients undergoing breast surgery, 74% to 85% go unused and become a source of diversion.12 This is particularly relevant as over 60% of people who misuse opioids obtain the drug from friends or relatives with unused prescriptions.13 Given this concerning scenario, evidence-based strategies are required to support judicious opioid prescribing after breast surgery while ensuring effective postoperative pain management.

Recent literature suggests that, to prevent postoperative opioid-related harms, surgeons may consider prescribing only non-opioid interventions for postdischarge analgesia.14 This approach is common internationally, with postoperative opioid prescribing rates ranging from 0% to 5% in many European, Asian, South American and Middle Eastern countries, compared with 70–95% in the USA and Canada.15,20 Among patients undergoing breast surgery, an international cohort study (n=24 619) supported that >70% of patients in Canada and the USA fill an opioid prescription after discharge, compared with only 2% in Sweden.18 These findings suggest that the decision to prescribe opioids after breast surgery largely depends on local prescribing culture, surgeons’ habits and patient expectations.

To better inform prescription decision-making, our team recently completed a meta-analysis of 47 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (n=6607 patients undergoing any surgical procedure), which suggested that postdischarge opioid prescribing does not improve postoperative pain outcomes and instead increases adverse events (ie, nausea, vomiting, constipation, drowsiness).21 Nevertheless, the overall quality of the included trials was low (60% with a high risk of bias), and only one full-scale RCT focused on breast surgery.22 The Postoperative Analgesia Intervention with Non-opioid Alternatives (PAIN-Alt) trial aims to bridge this critical evidence gap.

Objectives

The PAIN-Alt trial will address the following research question: among patients discharged after outpatient breast surgery, does opioid-free analgesia (OFA) result in non-inferior 7-day pain outcomes (pain intensity and pain interference) in comparison to opioid analgesia (OA)? We hypothesise that OFA will be non-inferior to OA by a non-inferiority margin of 0.9 points (below the minimal clinically important difference (MCID)= 1 point23 24) on the Brief Pain Inventory Short Form (BPI) pain intensity and pain interference scales (scores range =0–10; higher scores = worse outcome).25

Secondarily, we will explore the effect of the interventions on adverse drug events, physical and mental health status, satisfaction with pain management, postoperative complications, chronic pain, opioid misuse, persistent opioid use, healthcare utilisation and costs.

Methods and analysis

Design

The PAIN-Alt trial is a parallel, two-group, assessor-blinded, open-label, multicentre, non-inferiority RCT conducted across seven university-affiliated hospitals in four Canadian provinces (Alberta, Manitoba, Ontario and Quebec). The study is planned to be conducted from December 2024 to December 2026. Trial feasibility was informed by a pilot RCT (n=76).26 27 Our protocol is aligned with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials Statement (online supplemental table 1)28 and has been registered a priori (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06507345). Any protocol amendments will be tracked and justified in the peer-reviewed manuscript reporting the trial results. The trial flow diagram is presented in figure 1. Trial reporting will adhere to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2025 statement and the CONSORT 2010 extension for non-inferiority trials.29 30

Trial flow diagram.

To maximise the generalisability and applicability of our findings, the trial is pragmatic (ie, designed to reflect real-world clinical practice rather than ideal experimental conditions). In line with the Pragmatic–Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary 2 (PRECIS-2) framework,31 pragmatic design decisions were made in close partnership with clinicians and patient partners. All PRECIS-2 domains were scored ≥4/5, indicating a high degree of alignment with usual care (online supplemental table 2). Key design features include broad eligibility criteria, routine recruitment pathways, flexible intervention delivery and trial implementation within standard clinical environments. The use of patient-reported outcomes, minimal follow-up burden and intention-to-treat analysis further supports the trial’s real-world relevance.

Participants

Adult patients (≥18 years) undergoing mastectomy (including total, modified radical, radical) or lumpectomy (such as excisional biopsy, partial or segmental mastectomy, oncoplastic breast-conserving surgery), for malignant (cancer) and non-malignant diseases (eg, granulomatous disease, fibroadenoma) are eligible for inclusion. Additionally, surgery must be scheduled as an outpatient procedure, with discharge planned for the same calendar day of the operation (ie, no planned admission to a hospital ward).32 In line with our pragmatic approach, patients with a planned stay in a postoperative recovery unit extending past midnight will remain eligible for inclusion.

We exclude patients who: (1) are planned to undergo immediate breast reconstruction, such as implant, tissue-expander or autologous/flap-based procedures33; (2) have contraindications to the drugs used in the trial as per Health Canada Monographs (ie, opioid use disorder, pregnancy, breastfeeding, heart failure, allergy/hypersensitivity, peptic ulcer, bleeding disorders, renal or liver impairment; as defined in online supplemental table 3)34,36; (3) used opioids within 30 days before surgery; (4) have cognitive impairment preventing patient-reported outcome assessment; (5) are difficult to reach after surgery (ie, limited access to a telephone or computer) and (6) need for postoperative hospitalisation decided prior to randomisation, due to medical (eg., intraoperative complications) or non-medical reasons (eg, lack of support at home).

Participants unexpectedly hospitalised after randomisation (eg, due to complications experienced in the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU)) will be included and analysed according to the intention-to-treat principle.

Recruitment

The recruitment procedures are as follows: (1) patients are informed about the study by a member of the healthcare team during the preoperative hospital visit; (2) those who agree to speak with study staff are contacted for further information and eligibility screening; (3) patients who are eligible and interested in participating are asked to sign the consent form (electronically, via prepaid mail or during the next hospital visit) and complete the study’s preoperative questionnaires electronically or by telephone. In some trial sites, the study introduction by the healthcare team may be bypassed if patients a priori signed a universal ‘consent to be contacted for research purposes”’.

Randomisation and concealment of allocation

Participants are individually randomised (1:1 ratio) to receive OA or non-OA after discharge. The random allocation sequence has been generated electronically using PASS V.2022 (NCSS, Kaysville, Utah, USA).37 The sequence was uploaded onto a password-protected web application (Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) randomisation module38) by an external statistician not involved in the trial. The randomisation uses permuted, balanced blocks of randomly varying sizes. The randomisation is stratified by (1) trial site, (2) type of resection (mastectomy, lumpectomy) and (3) concomitant axillary procedure (yes (biopsy or dissection), no).39

Treatment allocation remains concealed until the end of surgery, with randomisation conducted at the time of skin closure in the operating room (OR). All randomisations are conducted by research staff present in the OR or in remote contact with the OR team, using the REDCap web application accessed via computer or smartphone. Prior to patients’ discharge from the OR, the primary surgeon is informed of the participant allocation and provides a discharge analgesia prescription according to group assignment. To prevent performance bias during PACU stay (ie, differences in care arising from knowledge of treatment allocation), the discharge prescription signed by the surgeon is kept in a sealed opaque envelope until participants are ready to leave the hospital.

Interventions

OA group

Surgeons are asked to provide their usual discharge analgesia prescription inclusive of opioids. Discharge prescriptions for the OA group consist of non-opioid analgesics (acetaminophen and/or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAIDs)) and opioid tablets, ‘as needed’, for breakthrough pain (ie, pain that erupts while patients are already taking other analgesics). Non-pharmacological pain interventions (ie, icepacks,40 music,41 meditation42) may be recommended by some surgeons as part of usual care. The OA strategies used at the trial sites follow Health Canada standards for safety and efficacy.43 Given the pragmatic nature of the trial, the OA analgesia strategy may vary according to surgeons’ and/or participants’ preferences, surgical procedure and comorbidities. OFA group

Discharge prescriptions for the OFA group include standard non-opioid analgesics (acetaminophen and/or NSAIDs) and, in case of breakthrough pain, rescue analgesia may be provided by: (1) using evidence-based non-pharmacological pain interventions (ie, icepacks,40 music,41 meditation),42 (2) adding non-opioid drugs not included in the initial regimen or (3) switching drugs targeting individual variances in analgesia response.44 Details regarding the proposed non-pharmacological pain interventions are provided in online supplemental table 4. The OFA analgesia strategy may vary according to surgeons’ and/or participants’ preferences, surgical procedure and comorbidities. Examples of OFA regimens pragmatically considered by prescribers, proposed according to Health Canada safety standards,43 are described in table 1.

In both groups, treatment duration is pragmatic, according to surgeons’ discretion. Generally, breast surgeons prescribe pain medications for 2–7 days postoperatively, in line with existing guidelines on postoperative pain management.45,47 As per standard care at all the trial sites, patients in both groups participate in a predischarge education session with a nurse and are advised to fill the analgesia prescription at their community pharmacy. Medication education at the pharmacy is conducted as per usual care.

Management of persistent pain

If participants experience persistent uncontrolled pain despite the available analgesic prescription, management follows current standard care at the trial sites, regardless of treatment allocation. Generally, participants are advised to contact the surgical clinic or surgeon on call or seek emergency care at a hospital emergency department or walk-in clinic. Pain management optimisation may include patient education or prescribing new opioid and/or non-opioid interventions. Changes in pain management will be recorded, and crossover between trial arms (ie, participants in the OFA group ultimately receiving an opioid prescription on the clinician’s discretion) will be addressed according to intention-to-treat.

Other aspects of perioperative care

As this is a pragmatic trial, surgical techniques and in-hospital anaesthesia/analgesia interventions are at the discretion of the perioperative care team. Drugs to prevent analgesics’ side effects (eg, laxatives, proton pump inhibitors) are prescribed at the clinicians’ discretion. All relevant perioperative interventions and adjunct medication use are recorded.

Measurement strategy

The trial measurement strategy and time points of outcome assessment are outlined in table 2. Sources of data are hospital medical records, provincial health records and patient-reported information. The latter are obtained via electronic questionnaires or telephone interviews, according to the patient’s preference. Electronic questionnaires are completed remotely (via smartphone, tablet or personal computer) using the REDCap platform hosted by the coordinating site (Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre (RI-MUHC)). Participants receive a link to the questionnaires via text message or email (according to the patient’s preference) in the morning, with up to three reminders in case of no response. A phone call reminder will be conducted if the participant does not respond after the final reminder. Participants are asked to complete the questionnaires preferably in the morning to prevent bias associated with chronobiological variations in pain.48 Questionnaire completion via telephone occurs preferably before noon.

Patient, surgery and perioperative care characteristics

Information about patients, surgery and perioperative care characteristics (including preoperative use of analgesics in preparation for surgery, intraoperative anaesthesia/analgesia and analgesia use in the PACU) is obtained from medical records. Preoperatively, we also collect patient-reported information about demographics (eg, sex, gender,49 race50 and income51 52), preoperative pain intensity and interference (BPI45), preoperative physical and mental function (Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System 29 (PROMIS-29)53), preoperative use of analgesics at home, pain catastrophising (Pain Catastrophizing Scale54 55), risk for opioid use disorder (Revised Opioid Risk Tool),56 preferred treatment group and expectations for treatment effectiveness. Details about our measures of patients, surgery and perioperative care characteristics are provided in online supplemental table 5.

Co-primary outcomes

The trial’s co-primary outcomes are patient-reported pain intensity and pain interference with daily activities within 7 days of discharge. The focus on these outcomes is in line with current recommendations that pain assessment should reflect patients’ multidimensional pain experience.57 58 Failure to demonstrate statistical non-inferiority on any of these individual outcomes will be considered sufficient to reject the trial’s a priori non-inferiority hypothesis.59 The length of follow-up (7 days) was chosen based on the duration of postoperative analgesia treatment recommended by current guidelines60,62 and evidence suggesting that most patients undergoing day surgery (>90%) no longer require pain medications by postoperative day 7.63 64

Pain intensity and pain interference will be measured using the BPI-Short Form, a patient-reported pain questionnaire developed by the WHO.45 In this questionnaire, pain intensity is measured across four domains (worst, least, on average and current), and pain interference is measured across seven domains (mood, work, general activity, walking, relationships, enjoyment of life and sleep).25 Aggregated scores of both scales range from 0 to 10, with higher scores representing worse outcomes.25 The BPI intensity and interference scales have demonstrated robust construct validity, reliability and responsiveness when measuring postoperative pain.46 47 65 For both scales, MCIDs of one point have been established by studies using anchor-based and distribution-based methods.23 These MCIDs are in line with existing evidence focused on the use of 10-point scales for postoperative pain assessment.24 These measures are detailed in online supplemental table 6.

Secondary outcomes (exploratory)

The secondary outcomes include: (1) adverse drug events including potential side-effects of opioids, acetaminophen and NSAIDs (identified via medical records or patient-report,66 with subsequent classification using MedDRA67); (2) patient-reported adherence to the prescribed analgesic regimen; (3) time to stopping pain medications; (4) postoperative physical and mental function (PROMIS-29)53; (5) satisfaction with pain management (from the Revised American Pain Society Patient Outcome Questionnaire68]); (6) opioid misuse (Prescription Opioid Misuse Index69); (7) 30-day postoperative complications (medical records or patient-reported, classified according to Clavien-Dindo,70 converted into a numerical Comprehensive Complication Index71); (8) postoperative healthcare utilisation (medical records or patient-reported); (9) chronic postoperative pain (patient-reported pain in surgical site that persists for at least 3 months72); (10) persistent opioid use (prescriptions filled between 3 and 6 months after surgery identified via provincial health records73; and (11) care costs (Healthcare and Societal Resource Utilisation questionnaire51 52). These measures are detailed in online supplemental table 6.

Blinding

Personnel involved in outcome data collection and processing are blinded to group allocation. Due to the pragmatic nature of the trial, participants and prescribers (surgeons) cannot be blinded. Patient-reported outcome data collected electronically are transmitted directly to the REDCap database and verified by blinded personnel. Treatment adherence data collected electronically are verified by unblinded personnel. Similarly, for participants who prefer responding to questionnaires by telephone, outcome data are obtained via telephone interviews with blinded personnel, while treatment adherence calls are conducted by unblinded personnel. Any inadvertent unblinding will be reported. The effectiveness of blinding will be estimated by asking blinded personnel to guess participants’ group allocation 1 month after surgery.

Sample size

Our sample size calculation aims to ensure sufficient power to address the hypothesis that BPI pain intensity scores and pain interference scores are non-inferior in participants receiving OFA versus OA. Considering a SD of 3 points (for both scales, based on our pilot data26 and previous literature21), a non-inferiority margin of 0.9 points (below the MCID of one point for both scales23 24), α=0.025,74 75 and power=90%, a sample of 468 participants provides sufficient statistical power to address the trial’s co-primary outcomes. Conservatively, accounting for an attrition rate of ~15%, our trial will target a total sample of 540 participants (270 per group).

Data analysis

All statistical analyses will be performed by a blinded statistician, with group allocation protected by codes revealed only after all analyses are completed. Our primary analyses will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle to pragmatically assess the impact of the assigned treatment strategies in routine clinical practice. Given our non-inferiority design, comparisons will be based on 0.025-level one-sided tests, which correspond to the lower bound of a two-sided 90% CI.74 75 Linear mixed-effects modelling (LMM) for repeated measures will be used to assess between-group differences in 7-day pain intensity and pain interference (co-primary outcomes). These analyses will use an unstructured covariance structure with treatment group and time included as fixed factors and ‘patient’ included as a random factor. The model will also include treatment-time interactions and covariates concerning randomisation stratification factors (trial site, type of surgery, axillary procedure) and baseline (preoperative) values. Non-inferiority will be evaluated using the overall mean treatment effect across time—that is, the fixed main effect of treatment from the LMM, which reflects the average between-group difference in pain outcomes over the 7-day follow-up period. Non-inferiority will be concluded if the lower bound of the 90% CI for this average difference lies above –0.9, the prespecified non-inferiority margin. To explore non-inferiority at individual time points, the adjusted mean differences between treatment groups at each time point, along with corresponding 90% CIs, will be estimated using model-based contrasts derived from the fixed effects. LMM allows the assessment of all available data points and provides unbiased estimates under the missing-at-random assumption; therefore, our primary analyses will not use multiple imputations76; however, sensitivity analyses will address different methods of handling missing data (complete case analysis vs multiple imputations). In addition to the primary ITT analysis, an exploratory per-protocol analysis will be conducted to compare participants who used opioids postoperatively versus those who did not. To account for type I error inflation from testing multiple primary outcomes, false discovery rate procedures will be applied.77 We will conduct exploratory subgroup analyses to test the interaction of age, type of resection, axillary procedure and intraoperative use of nerve blocks with trial outcomes. We will also explore the potential interaction of sex, gender78 and race79 80 with trial outcomes; however, this analysis may be limited by small sample sizes in specific subgroups (eg, male (sex); man, non-binary (gender)).

Secondary (exploratory) outcomes will be assessed using LMM (for continuous repeated measures), generalised linear mixed modelling (for dichotomous repeated measures), linear regression (for single continuous measures), logistic regression (for single dichotomous measures) and Cox regression (for time-to-event measures). A trial-based economic evaluation is planned according to Canada’s Drug and Health Technology Agency (CADTH)81 and the Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research82 guidelines.

Trial team

The trial team includes scientists, surgeons, anaesthetists, internists, nurses, pharmacists, statisticians, research staff, trainees and patient partners who contribute actively across all trial sites. The trial is coordinated by the RI-MUHC Centre for Outcomes Research and Evaluation (CORE), with oversight from the principal investigators and operational leadership by a CORE-based research coordinator. Research staff at each participating site support recruitment and randomization, while follow-up is conducted centrally. Further details regarding the trial team’s structure and responsibilities are provided in online supplemental table 7). A contractual agreement has been established between the participating sites and the coordinating site, enabling secure data sharing via REDCap (combined dataset available only to the coordinating site) and establishing authorship eligibility criteria.

Trial steering committee

The trial steering committee consists of the principal investigators, the core trial coordinator, co-investigators representing each trial site (breast surgeons, knowledge users), two patient-partners and research trainees involved with the trial (online supplemental table 7). The steering committee meets every 2 months to discuss the trial progress, address challenges and set mitigation strategies.

Data safety and monitoring board

The trial independent Data safety and monitoring board (DSMB) comprises four members: a breast surgeon, an internist, a pain specialist and a biostatistician (online supplemental table 7). These members are not involved in the trial’s conduct and have no personal, academic or financial interest in the trial interventions. The DSMB will convene when 25%, 50% and 75% of the participants have completed the 30-day follow-up. During each meeting, the DSMB will evaluate the trial timeline, recruitment and retention rates, violations of blinding and adherence to the trial protocol at each site. To assess interim safety data, before each meeting, the DSMB statistician will be provided with a de-identified database containing information on (1) participants’ group allocation protected by codes, (2) adverse drug events (patient-reported or identified via medical records); (2) emergency visits; (3) hospital readmissions and (4) requirement for analgesia optimisation due to uncontrolled pain. No formal stopping criteria have been set a priori, but the DSMB may recommend trial discontinuation based on perceived safety concerns or logistical reasons (eg, futility in achieving the targeted sample size).

Patient and public involvement

In the proposed RCT, patient-partners and breast surgeons (knowledge users) are equal partners alongside researchers in developing the trial research questions, methodologic design and choice of outcome measures. The foundation of this partnership was built during our preliminary work, which included a user-oriented knowledge synthesis,21 and a pilot RCT with an embedded qualitative study to explore patients’ and clinicians’ perspectives.26 27

This trial protocol has been directly informed by patient-partners and surgeons, who provided valuable insights based on their experiences, preferences and priorities. Their input attests that the trial logistics, interventions and participation burden are acceptable to patients and care providers. Patient-partners and surgeons are part of the trial’s steering committee and will attend bi-monthly meetings to provide insights to optimise the trial’s conduct and progress.

Ethics and dissemination

This trial protocol has been approved by the coordinating site, the MUHC Research Ethics Board (MP-37-2024-10253), and approval will be obtained locally at all participating sites. The protocol has been tailored in accordance with the Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans.83 Our research methods are aligned with the harmonised standard of International Conference on Harmonization/Good Clinical Practices,84 protecting the rights, safety and welfare of all human subjects with respect to academic and clinical integrity.84 The trial informed consent form is available in online supplemental file 1.

Our trial results will be of great interest to a broad audience, including patients, surgeons, medical specialists, anaesthetists, pharmacists, nurses, researchers and policymakers. To reach clinicians and academic audiences, research dissemination strategies include, but are not limited to, conference presentations (national and international) and publication of our findings (open-access) in a peer-reviewed journal. To reach patient and community audiences, our findings will be publicised by our institutions’ communications department via patient portals, social media platforms and press releases.

Discussion

Overprescription of opioids to surgical patients is recognised as one of the driving forces behind the current opioid crisis. Patients undergoing outpatient breast surgery are frequently prescribed opioids after discharge, but the value of this practice remains uncertain. The PAIN-Alt trial is designed to generate robust evidence on the comparative effectiveness of opioid versus OFA and inform strategies to mitigate the downstream harms of opioid overprescribing after breast surgery.
