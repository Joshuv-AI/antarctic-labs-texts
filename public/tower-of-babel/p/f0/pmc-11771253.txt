Outcome of bariatric surgery in patients with unexpected liver cirrhosis: A multicenter study from China
Authors: Xia Sun, Libin Yao, Xing Kang, Weihua Yu, Fidele Kakule Kitaghenda, Mohammad Sajjad Ibn Rashid, Angeline Nogue Taguemkam, Jian Hong, Zhiyong Dong, Xitai Sun, Xiaocheng Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsLiver cirrhosis is a complex disease that may result in increased morbidity and mortality following bariatric surgery (BS). This study aimed to explore the outcome of BS in patients with unexpected cirrhosis, focusing on postoperative complications and the progression of liver disease.MethodsA retrospective study of bariatric patients with cirrhosis from four centers in China between 2016 and 2023 was conducted, with follow-up for one year after BS. The primary outcome was the safety of BS in patients with unexpected cirrhosis, while the secondary outcome was the metabolic efficacy of BS in this group postoperatively.ResultsA total of 47 patients met the study criteria, including 46 cases of Child-Pugh class A cirrhosis and 1 case of Child-Pugh B. Pathological examination confirmed nodular cirrhosis in 21 patients (44.68%), pseudolobule formation in 1 patient (2.13%), lipedema degeneration with inflammatory cell infiltration in 3 patients (6.38%), and chronic hepatitis in 1 patient (2.13%). The average percentage of total weight loss was 29.73 ± 6.53% at one year postoperatively. During the 30-day postoperative period, the complication rate was 6.38%, which included portal vein thrombosis, gastrointestinal bleeding, and intra-abdominal infection. Moreover, no cases of liver decompensation or mortality were reported during the follow-up period. The remission rates of comorbidities among 41 patients one year after surgery were as follows: dyslipidemia 100%, type 2 diabetes 82.61%, hypertension 84.62%, and obstructive sleep apnea syndrome 85.71%.ConclusionsBS can be safely performed in patients with unexpected cirrhosis in the compensated stage of liver disease, with low postoperative morbidity and no mortality observed during one-year follow-up.Keywords: Bariatric surgery(BS), Metabolic surgery, Liver cirrhosis, Obesity, Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2M)

Introduction

Obesity is a growing global public health concern that is strongly linked to hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), certain cancers, and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD). The prevalence of MAFLD is approximately 60%–80% among patients with obesity, and it can rapidly progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis with consequences of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a known risk factor for liver cirrhosis, accounting for an estimated 820,000 deaths annually worldwide, primarily due to HBV-related cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).8 HBV infection has declined over the past few decades, although it poses a considerable burden in the clinical setting when managing hepatic sequelae (fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC) associated with this infection.9,10 Despite an ongoing decrease in HBV prevalence, the coexistence of fatty liver disease and HBV infection is frequently observed in clinical practice as obesity is becoming increasingly prevalent in HBV-endemic areas. It is suggested that the coexistence of steatosis and HBV may accelerate the progression of liver disease and increase its severity, however, the impact of the concurrence of these conditions on liver disease is not well understood.11, 12, 13, 14, 15

Bariatric surgery (BS) is a well-established and effective treatment strategy for obesity, T2DM, hypertension, and the reduction of obesity-related cancer mortality. The outcomes of BS in patients with cirrhosis in the context of safety and benefit remain unclear. Previous data showed that the mortality rates for patients with decompensated and compensated cirrhosis undergoing BS are higher than those for patients without cirrhosis, with rates of 19.4%, 0.6%, and 0.1%, respectively.16, 17, 18, 19, 20

Currently, there is insufficient data to demonstrate the risks versus benefits of BS in patients with cirrhosis. It remains unclear whether the potentially higher risks involved can be attenuated by surgery or if there could be a significant improvement or reversal of this condition following BS.21 Moreover, it is an ongoing debate whether BS should be performed in patients with unexpected cirrhosis diagnosed during the surgery, and the type of BS to be performed, which poses a challenge for surgeons on the course of action in this group of patients.

This study aimed to assess the outcomes of BS in patients with unexpected cirrhosis, focusing on postoperative complications and the progression of cirrhosis.

Methods and materials

Ethical approval

All procedures performed in the studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional or national research committee and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration, along with its late amendments or comparable ethical standards. This study was approved by the ethics committee of our institution. The ethical approvals were obtained from the following institutions: the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, The Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Ethical approval No. XYFY2020-JS003-01); the Department of Pancreatic and Metabolic Surgery at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Nanjing University (Ethical approval No. 2017-030-30); the Department of Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery, The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University (Ethical approval No. KY-2023-019); and the Department of General Surgery, Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Ethical approval No. SRRSH-2023-0229). All participants in this study provided written informed consent prior to inclusion.

Patient information

From January 2016 to December 2023, a total of 9492 patients with obesity from four centers in China underwent BS. Among these, 40 patients were found to have unexpected or suspected cirrhosis intraoperatively, and 7 patients with known cirrhosis before surgery were also included in the current study.

Retrospective data were collected, including sex, age, body weight, body mass index (BMI), past medical history, preoperative comorbidities, laboratory data, history of cirrhosis, bariatric procedure, early postoperative complications, length of hospital stay, and follow-up outcomes. Before surgery, each patient was clearly explained the pros and cons associated with BS, and written informed consent was provided and signed by all patients. Furthermore, for those with unexpected cirrhosis found intraoperatively, consent to proceed with surgery was obtained from the patient’s family member who was authorized by the patient before surgery.

The primary outcome was to assess the safety of BS in patients with unexpected cirrhosis discovered intraoperatively. During the one-year follow-up period, liver function and adverse effects were recorded. The secondary outcome was the metabolic efficacy of BS in these patients, which included evaluations of changes in BMI, the evolution of T2DM, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (ⅰ) adult (men and women); (ⅱ) age between 18 and 64 years; (ⅲ) BMI >27 kg/m2 for patients with T2DM and BMI >32.5 kg/m2 for those without T2DM; (ⅳ) primary BS; (ⅴ) patients with suspected or unexpected cirrhosis found during surgery; (ⅵ) and patients with a known history of liver cirrhosis before surgery. All patients without unexpected diagnosis of liver cirrhosis found intraoperatively, or no history of liver cirrhosis before surgery were excluded from the study.

The diagnosis of liver cirrhosis was confirmed through intraoperative liver biopsy in all patients, and the Child-Pugh classification was used to categorize the stage of liver disease, as described previously.22 T2DM was diagnosed based on hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) > 6.5%, following the guidelines of the American Diabetes Association (2018).23 Hypertension was diagnosed when blood pressure >140/90 mmHg (systolic/diastolic). Dyslipidemia was defined by triglyceride >200 mg/dL, cholesterol >240 mg/dL, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) > 160 mg/dL, according to the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery.24 Obstructive sleep apnea was diagnosed based on the patient’s medical history.

Bariatric and metabolic procedures performed

Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), single anastomosis sleeve ileal (SASI), single anastomosis sleeve ileal with Braun (SASI-B), Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), and one anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) were performed in this study.

The standard LSG was performed using a standardized technique. Briefly, the division of the greater omentum from the stomach was accomplished using either ligasure or an ultrasonic surgical system. Resection started at 4 cm from the pylorus, with the final resection point being approximately 2 cm away from the gastroesophageal junction.

For patients scheduled for SASI and SASI-B, after completing the LSG, the small intestine was transected 260 cm away from the ileocecal valve for the creation of gastrointestinal anastomosis. The intestinal-intestinal (Braun) anastomosis was created 40 cm away from the gastrointestinal anastomosis followed by the closure of mesenteric defects.25

RYGB was performed as previously described.26 In brief, we created a 15-mL gastric pouch followed by the antecolic, antegastric 150 cm Roux limb. The jejuno-jejunostomy was performed 60 cm below the gastro-jejunal anastomosis. OAGB was completed by creating an ante-colic gastro-jejunostomy (GJ) 150–200 cm from the duodenojejunal flexure.27

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and frequency percentages for categorical variables. Additionally, t-tests and Mann-Whitney tests were used to analyze independent variables. A paired t-test was used to analyze changes from baseline to subsequent follow-up data at 6 and 12 months. P values < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.4.1, USA).

Results

From January 2016 to December 2023, a total of 9492 bariatric patients were admitted to the four centers in China, of which 47 patients with obesity and liver cirrhosis met the inclusion criteria for the study. Among the 47 patients, 7 (14.89%) had known cirrhosis before surgery (Fig. 1 A), while 40 (85.11%) were found to have unexpected (unknown) or suspected cirrhosis during surgery. Intraoperative exploration of cirrhotic liver manifestations, intraoperative pathological examination of liver tissues, and postoperative pathological diagnosis confirmed cirrhosis in 33 patients (82.5%). In the remaining 7 patients (17.5%), although intraoperative liver manifestations indicated a high likelihood of cirrhosis, the diagnosis was not confirmed in a few of these patients after pathological examination, and in others, liver biopsies were not obtained during surgery (Fig. 1 B). The causes of cirrhosis included MAFLD in 35 (87.5%) patients and HBV infection in 5 (12.5%) patients.

Comparisons of known and suspected liver cirrhosis.A: Laparoscopic image of the liver cirrhosis in a patient with known cirrhosis before surgery. B: Laparoscopic image of the liver in a patient with suspected liver cirrhosis during surgery, where pathological findings did not confirm the diagnosis.

A total of 47 patients were included in this study, comprising 19 women and 28 men, with an average age of 38.83 ± 11.86 years and an average BMI of 41.12 ± 5.20 kg/m2. Of these, 6 patients were followed up for less than one year (3–6 months after surgery). Preoperative comorbidities included T2DM (55.32%), hypertension (38.30%), dyslipidemia (42.55%), and OSAS (48.94%). During the preoperative assessment, the Child-Pugh classification of liver function showed that 46 patients (97.87%) had Child-Pugh class A, and a patient (2.13%) had Child-Pugh class B. Elevated transaminase was found in 26 patients (55.32%) before surgery, thrombocytopenia in 3 patients (6.38%), and prothrombin time was slightly elevated in 5 patients (10.64%) preoperatively.

The mean operation time was 116.71 ± 58.71 min, and the mean hospitalization time was 6.44 ± 3.26 days. Thirty days postoperative complications were reported in 3 (6.38%) patients: 1 patient presented with portal vein thrombosis (PVT), 1 patient had gastrointestinal bleeding, and 1 patient with intra-abdominal infection, which required readmission. Moreover, none of the patients experienced liver decompensation and mortality throughout the follow-up period. Pathological examination confirmed nodular cirrhosis in 21 (44.68%) patients, pseudolobule formation 1 (2.13%), lipedema degeneration with inflammatory cell infiltration 3 (6.38%), and chronic hepatitis in 1 (2.13%) patient. Forty-one patients (87.23%) successfully completed the one-year follow-up, including 27 patients (65.85%) with LSG, 4 (9.76%) with SASI, 2 (4.88%) with OAGB, 1 (2.44%) with SASI-B and 7 (17.07%) with RYGB. The average percentage of total body weight loss (%TWL) was 29.73 ± 6.53%. The postoperative levels of liver transaminases (aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)) decreased significantly compared to preoperative levels (P = 0.0052 and P = 0.0036, respectively); moreover, triglyceride levels decreased notably after surgery, with a difference that reached statistical significance (P = 0.0070). Interestingly, at the one-year follow-up, total bilirubin levels were higher than pre-surgery levels; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05); the baseline platelets levels decreased at the end of the follow-up, but the difference did not achieve statistical significance (P > 0.05). Fasting blood glucose decreased significantly and remained within the normal range (P = 0.0007). Furthermore, cholesterol levels decreased postoperatively but with a difference that did not amount to statistical significance ( P > 0.05). In contrast, high-density lipoprotein levels one year after surgery increased notably from the preoperative levels with a statistically significant difference (P = 0.0002). Glycosylated hemoglobin levels decreased within the normal levels, but the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The remission rates of comorbidities one year after surgery were as follows: dyslipidemia at 100% (n = 20), T2DM at 82.61% (n = 19), hypertension at 84.62% (n = 11), and OSAS at 85.71% (n = 18). The difference in the remission rates of all comorbidities did not amount to statistical significance (Table 2).

Discussion

Have you unexpectedly diagnosed liver cirrhosis? Have you suspected liver cirrhosis intraoperatively? Have all cases of liver cirrhosis been confirmed pathologically? In clinical practice, surgeons encounter unexpected diagnoses of cirrhosis in patients with obesity undergoing BS. As seen in this study, liver cirrhosis was either unexpected or suspected in 40 patients (85.11%) during surgery, after histopathological analysis, the diagnosis was confirmed in 33 patients (82.5%). However, in a few patients with suspected cirrhosis, the pathological findings did not confirm the diagnosis, even though their livers appeared very similar to those of patients with confirmed cirrhosis (Fig. 1A and B). This observation suggests the necessity of pathological findings for the accurate diagnosis of liver cirrhosis and highlights the fact that unexpected diagnoses of cirrhosis in patients undergoing BS may be more prevalent, especially considering that the incidence of MAFLD is estimated to be as high as 60%–80% in patients with obesity, according to current estimates.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

An unexpected diagnosis of cirrhosis can pose a significant challenge for surgeons in determining the best course of action, particularly whether BS should be performed and what type of surgery should be chosen. In scenarios of unexpected cirrhosis where an appropriate work-up is not available and the possibility was not discussed preoperatively with the patients, a reasonable course of action is to obtain a liver biopsy and abandon the planned surgery due to a potentially higher risk of intraoperative bleeding in this group of patients.20,28 However, BS may be considered in patients with unexpected cirrhosis in the compensated stage (Child-Pugh class A) of liver disease, provided that signs of severe portal hypertension, such as ascites, varices, and large dilated perigastric veins, have been excluded before surgery. Furthermore, bariatric and metabolic centers performing more than 100 procedures per year have shown superior outcomes with these high-risk patients,20,21,28 which could also apply to this study. Thus, surgeons in low-volume centers should consider transferring these patients to high-volume bariatric and metabolic centers.

Based on available evidence and our findings, LSG may be performed safely in patients with cirrhosis undergoing BS, as it is not a malabsorptive procedure and is associated with lower rates of surgery-related complications, mortality, and liver decompensation. A recent systematic review of 464 patients with cirrhosis found that LSG had significantly lower morbidity and mortality rates than RYGB (16.7% vs. 28.6% and 0.45% vs. 3.22%, respectively), while the rates of liver decompensation were similar (3.83% vs. 3.50%, respectively). Moreover, biliopancreatic diversion (BPD) was associated with higher mortality and liver decompensation rates than LSG (13.30% vs. 3.83% and 17.60% vs. 0.45%, respectively), affirming the hypothesis that a high risk of liver decompensation could be linked to BS with significant malabsorptive effects.20 Furthermore, LSG provides endoscopic access to the biliary system with minimal effects on drug absorption. It is less challenging to perform and has a shorter operative time.21,29, 30, 31 However, concerns about gastroesophageal disease and the potential for weight regain a few years after LSG may make some surgeons cautious about choosing this procedure. Due to the limited number of cases performed with OAGB, RYGB, SASI, and SASI-B in this study, we were unable to compare the outcomes of these bariatric procedures with those of LSG.

In contrast, gastric band (GB) and BPD do not appear to be favored in patients with cirrhosis. A Meta-analysis of GB outcomes reported a band slippage rate of 4.93% and a band erosion rate of 1.03%. Given the high risks associated with the procedure and the nature of patients with cirrhosis, reoperation may be unacceptable.20,32,33 Additionally, BPD is a bariatric procedure characterized by significant malabsorptive effects that may result in weight loss and metabolic benefits. However, as previously shown, the associated malabsorption might increase the risk of liver decompensation; therefore, it is contraindicated in this group of patients.20

In line with previous studies,21,28,29,34 our findings showed that BS can be safely performed in cirrhotic patients, resulting in improvements in comorbidities, significant weight loss, low morbidity, and no mortality; furthermore, no liver decompensation was observed one year postoperatively. It is worth noting that 97.9% of patients with cirrhosis included in this study were in the compensated stage (Child-Pugh class A) of liver disease, which was maintained throughout the follow-up period (6 months in a few patients, Table 3; and 1 year in others, Table 2). BS resulted in significant weight loss and remission of comorbidities such as T2DM, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and OSAS in our study. Additionally, metabolic parameters related to glucose and lipid metabolism showed postoperative improvement.

Regarding the effects of BS on liver enzymes, we observed a significant improvement in ALT and AST levels throughout the follow-up period, with a difference that reached statistical significance at one year after surgery. At one year postoperatively, our data showed a slight decline in platelet levels and an increase in bilirubin levels compared to baseline, although these differences did not amount to statistical significance, which seemed to imply the tendency toward liver deterioration. Although it was difficult to compare liver disease deterioration in patients with cirrhosis versus those without this disease in this study, our findings suggest that at the one-year follow-up, BS may slow down liver disease deterioration more than expected in concordance with the findings of liver enzymes.

HBV infection was prevalent in 5 (12.5%) patients in this study. Although this infection alone is a known contributing factor to liver cirrhosis, the relationship between HBV and hepatic fat accumulation (hepatic steatosis) remains poorly understood.14 Previous studies suggest that non-alcoholic hepatic steatosis inhibited HBV replication in an HBV-immunocompetent mouse model; moreover, hepatitis B virus X protein (HBx) increased lipid accumulation in the liver, potentially leading to severe hepatic complications.35, 36, 37, 38, 39 Whether the occurrence of HBV and hepatic steatosis will lead to more severe fibrosis of the liver or regression of hepatic disease is yet to be determined. Therefore, more studies would be needed.

It is well established that patients with liver cirrhosis are at a higher risk of postoperative complications and mortality after any major general surgery. In patients with cirrhosis undergoing BS, these risks seem to be initially dependent on the stage of liver disease. In our study, three cases (6.38%) of complications were assessed postoperatively during the first 30 days. One patient developed PVT and was treated with anticoagulation and thrombolytic therapy. Chronic liver disease, particularly cirrhosis, is a common cause of portal thrombosis, with an estimated overall incidence of 0.42% following BS.40 Independent predictors of PVT include a history of prior venous thromboembolism, liver disease, and sleeve gastrectomy.41 It was reported that rivaroxaban for thromboprophylaxis 7 days after BS was effective and safe in patients with a BMI >40 kg/m2.42 A recent Meta-analysis showed that prophylactic standard low molecular weight heparin was safer in patients undergoing BS.43 There is no consensus on the optimal dose and duration of thromboprophylaxis after BS; however, it is generally agreed that venous thromboembolism prophylaxis should be performed after BS. One additional patient developed bleeding from the gastrointestinal anastomotic site, while another patient had an intraperitoneal infection. After thorough examinations, it appeared that the causes of these complications were not related to cirrhosis in either case.

An acceptable postoperative outcome in our study may be partly because most patients in our study were in the compensated stage (Child-Pugh class A) of liver disease. Previous studies have shown that although all patients with cirrhosis undergoing BS are considered high-risk for postoperative morbidity and mortality, those with decompensated cirrhosis (specifically Child-Pugh class B and C) are far more likely to suffer adverse outcomes.16, 17, 18, 19, 20 Therefore, a decision regarding BS for patients with more advanced liver disease should involve the patient’s relatives, a bariatric center with multidisciplinary teams with regular check-ups, a dietitian, and a hepatologist. This also suggests that patient selection for BS is of great significance, as some patients with cirrhosis may be at greater risk than others. Current guidelines on the management of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis recommend BS only for cirrhotic patients in the compensated stage of the disease.29 Although no cases of liver decompensation were found in this study after surgery, previous reports have documented cases of liver decompensation after LSG in patients who were preoperatively in the compensated stage of the disease.20 Therefore, caution should be exercised in these patients when undergoing LSG.

The limitations of the current study worth mentioning include its retrospective nature, small sample size, and short-term follow-up period, which made it difficult for us to fully assess the long-term outcomes of BS in this high-risk cohort. Additionally, the limited number of non-LSG cirrhotic patients hindered our understanding of the outcomes of different BS in cirrhotic patients. Furthermore, the lack of liver biopsies during follow-up limited our ability to support the findings regarding disease progression.

Conclusions

Our study showed that BS can be performed in patients with unexpected liver cirrhosis in the compensated stage of liver disease (Child-Pugh class A), with low postoperative morbidity and no mortality at one-year follow-up. LSG appears to be safe in patients with liver cirrhosis associated with significant remission of obesity-related comorbidities in the short term after surgery. Further studies with larger samples and longer follow-ups would be needed to understand the benefits of BS in this high-risk group of patients in the long term.
