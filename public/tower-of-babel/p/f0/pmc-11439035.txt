Adaptive haze pixel intensity perception transformer structure for image dehazing networks
Authors: Jing Wu, Zhewei Liu, Feng Huang, Rong Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In the realm of deep learning-based networks for dehazing using paired clean-hazy image datasets to address complex real-world haze scenarios in daytime environments and cross-dataset challenges remains a significant concern due to algorithmic inefficiencies and color distortion. To tackle these issues, we propose SwinTieredHazymers (STH), a dehazing network designed to adaptively discern pixel intensities in hazy images and compute haze residue for clarity restoration. Through a unique three-branch design, we hierarchically modulate haze residuals by leveraging the global features brought by Transformer and the local features brought by Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) which has led to the algorithm’s widespread applicability. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach surpasses advanced single-image dehazing methods in both quantitative metrics and visual fidelity for real-world hazy image dehazing, while also exhibiting strong performance in cross-dataset dehazing scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73866-y.

Introduction

Haze images exhibit characteristics such as desaturation, reduced contrast, loss of distant semantic information, and challenges in feature extraction. These factors further degrade the performance of downstream visual systems, including object detection, semantic segmentation, autonomous driving, and traffic-monitoring. Consequently, single-image dehazing has emerged as a fundamental low-level visual task that holds immense importance in the field.

Currently, image dehazing algorithms primarily fall into two main categories: traditional non-learning methods and deep learning-based approaches. Non-learning algorithms, which include enhancement techniques, aim to restore clean clarity by enhancing image contrast through the utilization of relevant image processing operations such as histogram equalization1 and the Retinex algorithm2. While these methods do improve the visual appeal of low-quality images to some extent, they have certain limitations. On the other hand, prior-model-based image dehazing algorithms leverage prior knowledge in conjunction with physical models to restore hazy images, such like Dark Channel Prior (DCP)3, among others. However, when the conditions for prior establishment are not met, the dehazing outcomes of such methods often fall short of expectations.

In recent years, deep learning methods have demonstrated remarkable performance in various domains. Initially, some early approaches employed Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to estimate the transmission map4–6, followed by the utilization of traditional methods3,7 for estimating the atmospheric light. However, solving two unknown parameters from an ill-posed problem poses significant challenges. To address this issue, several algorithms8–12 have been developed to directly estimate the clean and clean image using CNNs. However, most of these networks adopt generic architectures such as Resnet13 and U-Net14, which may not be optimal for dehazing tasks. Therefore, it is crucial to leverage the common characteristics of hazy images and construct an algorithmic framework specifically tailored for dehazing tasks.

Transformer model has been extensively employed in various computer vision tasks15–17. However, despite its ability to capture long-range feature dependencies through self-attention mechanisms, it fails to adequately consider local information and thus overlooks the distinctions between regions with different levels of haze concentration. Consequently, directly applying this model to dehazing tasks may lead to issues such as blurred and coarse details in image reconstruction.

To address these challenges, we explored an effective hierarchical approach for processing haze images without overly incorporating semantic information. Based on this exploration, we designed a three-branch dehazing algorithm. The method comprises of Avg line, Max line, Min line and Fusion layer. In the Avg line, we utilize the outstanding global feature connectivity ability of self-attention18 to construct global haze features containing residual scene information to derive a coarse global haze residue. Within Max line, this is the most crucial branch, where we employ a dual-branch design to carefully integrate the excellent global feature extraction capability of Swin Transformer17 and the uniquely designed local feature modulation module. By combining them, we refine the haze features, removing the local scene semantic information from the rough global haze obtained by Avg Line and enhancing the overall haze characteristics. Min line ensures the preservation of intricate details in clean area of haze image through a series of CNN encoding and decoding layers, augmented by ReLU activation functions, thereby forming a residual block.

Lastly, we dynamically adjust the proportions of these three components within hazy images to decode the distant fine haze residue features, ultimately restoring clean images in the Fusion layer. Experimental comparisons serve to illustrate the effectiveness and progressiveness of our proposed approach.

Related work

Image Dehazing. For single-image dehazing, early methods rely on prior knowledge of haze or image degradation to estimate the transmission map and global atmospheric light. The accuracy of estimating these two values is crucial to the atmospheric scattering model, which forms the basis of the haze formation model. He et al.3 propose the Dark Channel Prior (DCP) algorithm based on extensive observations of outdoor clean images. Zhu et al.7 introduce a more accurate method for estimating atmospheric light. However, He’s method tends to exhibit pronounced color shifts and distortions in the sky region and around bright white objects due to inaccurate transmission estimation. Zhu’s method can result in an overall darker tone in the recovered image, leading to the loss of subtle information and features. In recent years, with the development of deep learning, researchers have shifted their focus to data-driven approaches. Early methods relied on convolutional neural networks to estimate unknown parameters in the atmospheric degradation model, such as Dehaze-Net4 and AOD-Net5. However, using deep learning methods to estimate degradation parameters can lead to error accumulation.

To avoid the accumulation of errors in the process of estimating multiple parameters, the method of directly estimating clean images using end-to-end networks gains attention. Qin et al.10 propose an end-to-end feature fusion attention network that bypasses the limitations imposed by the atmospheric scattering model and directly restores hazy images to clean ones. Chen et al.8 introduce an end-to-end gated context aggregation network, which also employs state-of-the-art dilated convolution techniques to help eliminate grid artifacts commonly caused by dilated convolutions, thus directly recovering the final clean image. With the development of transformers, some methods have begun to leverage its ability to capture long-range dependencies in algorithm design, such as Dehamer25. These methods have achieved excellent results in restoring paired datasets. However, when dealing with real hazy images, issues such as color distortion and algorithm failure often occur. To address restoration tasks in real-world scenarios, methods based on Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) have been proposed. For instance, D424 computes the transmission rate by estimating depth maps to accomplish dehazing tasks. However, these methods often fail to achieve outstanding dehazing results. Considering the strengths and limitations of previous methods, we reevaluated the features of haze images in imaging. Based on the significant differences in pixel intensities, we propose an adaptive branch framework to handle different semantic information layers in haze images. In processing haze features, we focus on constructing a dual-branch contrastive pathway consisting of Transformer-CNN to obtain locally optimal haze features with global refinement. With the network’s unique architectural design, it effectively restores real hazy images when utilizing artificial haze datasets as the foundation for data-driven approaches.

Adaptive integration of long-range and fine-grained haze residual features. The fusion of features is widely used in network design to enhance dehazing capabilities by incorporating features from different layers. Many image methods employ techniques such as dense connections or weighted summations8,10,12,18,19 for feature fusion. However, these methods primarily rely on pure CNN-layer design and are limited to local feature extraction without the ability to correlate global features. In Transformer-based feature fusion methods, Dehamer25 utilizes the powerful feature extraction capabilities of the Swin Transformer to extract haze features, while MB-TaylorFormer27 designs a lightweight Transformer network for feature extraction tasks. However, these methods have not explored the characteristics of hazy images, thus limiting their effectiveness in all dehazing tasks. In response to the deficiencies observed in existing feature fusion methodologies, we have introduced significant enhancements and presented an innovative Adaptive Feature Fusion Module. We incorporate Polarized Attention Mechanism (PSA)20 to facilitate pixel-level cross-scale fusion and regression. Furthermore, drawing inspiration from the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) architecture employed in transformers, we have transitioned linear layers into 1 × 1 depth convolutions to precisely localize and activate pixel features across successive layers. Finally, we employ the SoftMax function to judiciously allocate weights, thereby mitigating the risks associated with overexposure and distortion during the fusion process, all while maintaining computational efficiency. Through the architectural design of our three-branch dehazing network, our method achieves satisfactory restoration results in most hazy scenarios.

Method

Our research focuses on exploring effective feature fusion methods based on the pixel distribution characteristics of daytime hazy images to achieve end-to-end dehazing tasks. We specifically address daytime haze characteristics because the atmospheric light distribution differs between day and night, making nighttime dehazing a more distinct and specialized task. Therefore, our primary focus remains on daytime dehazing tasks. To extract haze residues with different properties, we employ a partitioning approach to categorize daytime hazy images into high, medium, and low pixel layers. By applying novel constraints, we selectively filter multi-channel feature layers. Through the refinement of Attention Layers and the exploration of effective fusion methods, we extract the haze residue map for dehazing operations.

Network construction analysis

The study conducted by Zhu et al.7 highlights that clean regions in a scene typically exhibit high saturation and moderate brightness, whereas hazy regions are characterized by a sharp decrease in image saturation, gradual fading of colors, and an increase in brightness. On this basis, we conducted an analysis of the pixel intensity distribution of a large number of classic haze images. As a result, there is a noticeable disparity in pixel distribution between the hazy and clean areas of the image. In Fig. 1, the histogram provides a detailed display of the pixel statistics for 200 haze images. Based on our statistical observations, higher pixel values in hazy images generally correspond to the sky and dense haze, whereas lower pixel values typically represent areas with light or non-haze. We present the visualization results of pixel distribution for one of the classic haze images in Fig. 2. It can be clearly observed that within the orange box, there are significant differences in the pixel intensity distribution of the same green vegetation. Based on our observations, we aim to propose a straightforward method for segmenting haze images into high, medium, and low pixel intensity layers. These layers correspond to dense haze sky areas, moderately hazy regions for dehazing, and light haze or haze-free areas, followed by further characterization of each layer to achieve an effective dehazing outcome, mitigating the issue of error accumulation when estimating atmospheric light and transmission.Fig. 1 The histogram displays the average pixel distribution of haze images under four different scenarios, which confirms that our hypothesis is correct.Fig. 2 Pixel intensity distribution labeling of green vegetation areas with different haze concentrations in real hazy images.

In order to explore an adaptive partitioning method for intensity layers, we need to further analyze the pixel representation in hazy images. Specifically, the specific approach involves partitioning the hazy image into multiple arbitrarily sized patches. These patches encompass both the bright hazy regions and sections with high-frequency information characterized by larger pixel values. This implies that a simple categorization of the hazy image into high, medium, and low pixel intensity layers for input into the network is not feasible. Such an approach would lead to the loss of channel-specific feature information and result in image disintegration during the dehazing process. We select two representative images as examples with Fig. 3.(a). being a synthetically generated hazy image, and the Fig. 3.(b) being an authentic hazy image. To ensure optimal comprehension for readers, we partition each image into four equally sized patches, indicated by red dashed lines. Subsequently, we delineate regions of varying sizes using solid lines of distinct colors and further subdivide them with red dashed lines. The roles of pixels in different patches are not consistent: a pixel that serves as the maximum in a smaller patch may become the minimum in a larger patch (The yellow patch indicated by the arrow in the reference image). This intensity disparity persists even in the sky region, confirming the effectiveness of our theoretical foundation.

In order to further develop the algorithm, we treat the three-pixel-intensity layers as globally variables with implicit constraints, and employ a deep neural network to decode these implicit constraints. Based on the above analysis, we cannot a priori separate the hazy image. Therefore, we propose a three-branch network to adaptively obtain three layers of pixel intensity. One branch is designed to learn global haze features, another branch to capture haze features in dense haze regions within the global haze features, and the final branch to extract residual haze features from light haze and haze-free regions. This approach helps avoid issues such as artifacts, fragmentation, and information loss in the dehazed image caused by layered processing, all while preserving the implicit constraints within the feature layers.

Network structure

The network architecture of STH consists of three distinct feature extractors, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The Avg-Block focuses on extracting coarse haze residuals from distant global regions while modulating them and filtering redundant features with the Max Block. The Min Block concentrates on extracting local haze residual in thin misty areas to effectively preserve semantic information in the image. In addition, we propose a PSAFFL module. We utilize the PSA module proposed by Liu et al.20 and incorporate convolutional MLP layers and SoftMax functions for pixel-level regression. This enables adaptive adjustment of weights across different layers to obtain haze residual feature layers with fine long-range connections. The enlarged visual results are show in Fig. 7.

Initially, preliminary feature extraction from the preceding feature map is performed using depth-wise separable convolution. Max block

We should particularly emphasize the high-pixel regions of the haze image, as they constitute the most significant portion of the haze. To extract fine-grained haze residuals with global coherence, we adopt a dual-path design here. Initially, we feed the rough global haze obtained from the previous step into the Swin Transformer for feature extraction. Subsequently, employing a multiscale max-pooling pyramid for redundant feature extraction, we modulate local features using multiscale residual convolutional layers to enhance pixel representation and clarity. We then utilize the proposed ACTN module to perform upsampling on this branch’s feature integration layer, as shown in Eq. 8:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_{ACTN}}={\text{PixelShuffle(Con}}{{\text{v}}_3}{\text{(Con}}{{\text{v}}_1}{\text{(Conv}}{{\text{T}}_3}{\text{(}}x{\text{)))}}$$\end{document}

We first use pixel shuffle to blend the multi-feature layers processed by max pooling, ensuring that upsampling does not generate redundant information. Then, we utilize 3*3 CNN and 1*1 CNN to perform feature fusion and pixel-wise denoising on the blended features. Such a combined design not only significantly reduces computational complexity but also avoids discarding meticulously modulated global haze features. To obtain fine-grained shallow features and compensate for the limitations of the Transformer, we devised an approach opposite to the first branch. We utilize a multiscale max-pooling pyramid and multiscale residual convolutional layers to filter initial haze features, which are then fed into the proposed MSDGN module. This module first stacks feature channels via deep convolution and subsequently, in the last module, replaces the downsampling of the Swin Transformer with upsampling for global upsampling. Finally, through a Transformer decoder, we combine the globally refined features obtained from the two branches with locally coherent features to obtain fine-grained haze features with global coherence. (as shown in Fig. 7.(c)).

Min block

In the layer with low pixel intensity, the presence of haze information is almost negligible, while J(x) contains relatively abundant semantic information. Hence, our objective is to preserve the high-frequency details in J(x) to a maximum extent while eliminating the minimal remaining haze. To achieve this goal, we will devise a unique method based on the theoretical foundation mentioned in Eq. (4). We perform a flip operation on I(x) to obtain 1-I(x), which is then fed into a set of CNNs with size of 3*3. After each convolutional layer, a non-linear activation function ReLU is applied to remove haze residuals and amplify the feature information from J(x). We perform channel fusion on the feature layers obtained after each iteration through the MGFB block to generate a deep feature map. MGFB is composed of multiple convolutional blocks and stacked max-pooling layers, used to amplify non-haze information features extracted by the AvgBlock. Subsequently, we utilize MCPIN to discretely separate the effective features in the depth map, resulting in a sparse representation of fine haze details (as depicted in Fig. 7.(d)).

Fusion layer

By employing different encoders and decoders, we have preliminarily acquired haze information at various pixel layers in the hazy images. However, designing an efficient fusion module to effectively integrate residual information poses a critical challenge. FFA-Net10 employs FA attention mechanism to acquire adaptive learning weights for feature fusion, enabling the retention of shallow information and its propagation to deeper layers. The weighting mechanism prioritizes haze regions, high and effective color features within the network. However, multi-channel feature is not being attended to, which may lead to a lack of information before obtaining dynamic weights.

Building upon these foundations, we enhanced this module, naming it PSAFFL, the detailed network structure can be seen in the PSAFFL module in Fig. 5. Firstly, we introduced the PSA. PSA is introduced to handle pixel-level regression tasks prior to weight acquisition. CNN attention mechanisms are typically relatively complex and not well-suited for image restoration tasks due to their high sensitivity to noise, PSA maintains higher resolution in both channel and spatial dimensions, resulting in less information loss from dimensionality reduction, and provides robust protection for pixel-level features (specific effects can be found in the ablation Experiments section). Finally, we replaced CA in FA with a convolution-based MLP layer to activate pixels available in both the PSA channel and spatially. Finally, we utilize the SoftMax function to further allocate weights, thus integrating the haze features extracted from the three branches into a cohesive whole, the final refined haze residual feature map and the dehazed image are shown in Fig. 7.(e) and Fig. 7.(f) respectively.

Experiments

Our method is implemented using PyTorch on the NVIDIA Tesla A100 GPU. For model training, we employ the Adam optimizer as the optimization algorithm with β1 and β2 set to 0.9 and 0.999, respectively. The batch size is set to 4, while the initial learning rate is initialized at 10 − 4 and adjusted using a cosine annealing function. To strike a balance between image processing speed and effectiveness, we conduct validation experiments to determine that the Avg-Group consists of four Avg-blocks in the residual layer.

Conclusion and limitations

Conclusion. In this paper, we propose a deep learning-based single-image dehazing method. The research aims to utilize pixel intensity differences in hazy images for segmentation and design a haze difference extractor suitable for different hierarchical characteristics. We carefully modulate Transformer to enhance dehazing capability and introduce various attention mechanisms to adjust local information, restore brightness, and eliminate haze-residual in the dehazing results obtained from the Baseline. Extensive qualitative results demonstrate that our method achieves state-of-the-art performance in dehazing from real hazy images, conducting quantitative comparisons on objective paired datasets, and dehazing capabilities across datasets.

One reason is that there is a significant difference between indoor and outdoor scene information, the mechanisms behind indoor hazy images differ from those in outdoor hazy scenes. We also conducted tests on the SOTS dataset, the detailed comparison of objective evaluation metrics is shown in Table 4. Unfortunately, although our method still demonstrates excellent dehazing capabilities on SOTS-outdoor, It does not achieve satisfactory results in terms of SOTS-indoor, as shown in Fig. 14.
