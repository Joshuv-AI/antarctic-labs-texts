AVD for older patients with classical Hodgkin lymphoma
Authors: Tamara Marconi, Sumita Ratnasingam
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Övergaard et al1 present a large comparative data set of chemotherapy regimens for older patients with classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL). Using population-based registry data from Sweden, Denmark, and Norway, they demonstrate that doxorubicin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine (AVD) is at least as effective as doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine (ABVD) and superior to cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (CHOP) and other nonanthracycline-containing regimens. These findings position AVD as the preferred chemotherapy regimen for this underserved population.

Treatment of older patients with cHL remains an area of significant unmet need. This population comprises ∼20% to 25% of all cHL cases, yet remains underrepresented in clinical trials and experiences substantially inferior outcomes compared to younger adults.2 The reasons are multifactorial: patient comorbidity, poor performance status, biologic differences (including an increased proportion of mixed cellularity histology and Epstein-Barr virus association), more frequent advanced-stage presentations, and heightened susceptibility to treatment-related toxicity. Unlike other aggressive lymphoma subtypes, a universally accepted treatment paradigm for this population has largely been absent. ABVD has been a widely used regimen but carries significant risks in older patients, with bleomycin identified as a key agent contributing to treatment toxicity.3 AVD is frequently used in older patients because of its more favorable safety profile; however, supportive evidence is lacking. The HD13 study of early stage favourable cHL was unable to demonstrate noninferiority of AVD compared to ABVD but included few patients aged >60 years.4 The RATHL study has established that fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–directed early de-escalation from ABVD to AVD yields equivalent 7-year outcomes with reduced pulmonary toxicity in the treatment of advanced-stage disease.5 CHOP is widely used for patients considered sufficiently fit to receive anthracycline but not to receive bleomycin; however a direct comparison of outcomes with ABVD or AVD has not been made.

In this analysis, 1569 patients aged ≥60 years, treated with curative intent between 2000 and 2021 were categorized by first-line regimen: ABVD (43%), AVD (8%), CHOP (31%), or others (13%). In general, the choice of regimen was according to national guidelines, with AVD being the recommended regimen in Sweden for patients aged >70 years from 2017. In multivariate analyses, AVD was associated with significantly improved overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) compared to ABVD1. Outcomes with CHOP were comparable to ABVD for PFS but appeared to be inferior for OS, whereas patients receiving other regimens had worse outcomes. Notably, AVD-treated patients were substantially older (median, 74 years) than the ABVD group (median, 66 years), with higher rates of advanced-stage disease. The population-based design, drawing on mandatory national registries with high coverage, minimizes the referral bias inherent in single-institution series.

These results gain further significance alongside emerging novel agent data. In the ECHELON-1 trial, brentuximab vedotin (BV) combined with AVD (BV-AVD) revealed no PFS advantage over ABVD in 186 patients aged over 60 years, with a 2-year modified PFS of 70.3% vs 71.4%, with increased neuropathy and neutropenia.6 Sequential administration of BV and AVD demonstrates an improved 2-year PFS of 84%, with improved tolerability and treatment completion rates; however, outcomes are heavily influenced by patient fitness. In contrast, the S1826 trial demonstrated benefit for nivolumab-AVD (N-AVD) in this age group: 2-year PFS was 89% vs 64% with BV-AVD, with 2-year OS of 96% vs 85%, lower nonrelapse mortality, and higher rates of treatment completion.7 A phase 2 trial of N-AVD exclusively in older adults reported 3-year PFS of 79% and OS of 97% at a median follow-up of 49 months.8 Meanwhile, approaches with frontline PD-1 inhibitor or with BV omitting a multiagent chemotherapy backbone have demonstrated suboptimal disease control in frail older patients,9 confirming that effective treatment in this population requires a chemotherapy component, particularly anthracycline exposure, in patients with sufficient fitness.

Important limitations warrant consideration. The AVD cohort was relatively small (n = 123) and likely inadequately powered to support a definitive conclusion. Furthermore, patients were predominantly treated in the most recent calendar period, resulting in shorter follow-up. Most notably, comorbidity data were incomplete, particularly among the AVD cohort, which precluded a proper comparison of fitness between the AVD and ABVD cohorts and could not be included in multivariate models. This is a meaningful gap because frailty, as determined using formal risk scores, is a strong predictor of OS in cHL,10 and geriatric assessment is now established as an essential component of pretreatment patient assessment in this population. The retrospective design introduces potential selection bias; however, this is minimized by adherence to national treatment guidelines. In addition, registry data cannot reliably capture dose modifications; some patients recorded as receiving ABVD may have had early bleomycin omission as per the RATHL study protocol,5 potentially impacting treatment group boundaries.

Despite these caveats, this study provides compelling real-world evidence that AVD achieves at least equivalent efficacy to ABVD, with the expected reduction in treatment-related toxicity among older patients with cHL. The cHL treatment landscape has recently evolved, with the incorporation of BV and PD-1 inhibitors into frontline regimens, with data favoring the use of N-AVD in this population. The take-home message from this study is that in resource-poor countries or in centers where novel therapies are not available, AVD remains an effective and tolerable treatment for older patients with early- and advanced-stage cHL. Of note, BV and nivolumab are not available currently for early-stage cHL; this study proves AVD is an appropriate option for these patients. The treatment of older patients with anthracycline-unfit cHL, however, remains an area of unmet need. These findings firmly support AVD as the preferred chemotherapy backbone for older patients, both as standalone therapy and as the platform upon which novel agent combinations are built.
