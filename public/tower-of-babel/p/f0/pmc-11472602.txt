Dietary alpha-linolenic acid supplementation enhances semen quality, antioxidant capacity, and sperm survival in aging breeder roosters
Authors: Cheng Long, Xiao-Feng Yin, Xi-Hui Sheng, Xiang-Guo Wang, Long-Fei Xiao, Xiao-Long Qi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aging in breeder roosters is often accompanied by a decline in semen quality, negatively impacting reproductive performance. This study aimed to investigate the effect of dietary alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), an essential omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid, on semen quality, antioxidant capacity, and sperm survival in aging breeder roosters. Roosters were divided into 4 groups and fed diets supplemented with 0%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2% ALA for 6 wk. Results indicated significant improvements in semen volume, sperm viability, and sperm density in ALA-supplemented groups compared to the control (P < 0.05). The 1% ALA group exhibited the most notable enhancements in sperm viability and density. Additionally, ALA supplementation increased the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, indicating enhanced antioxidant capacity (P < 0.05). Furthermore, ALA improved mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and reduced early and late sperm apoptosis, with the 2% ALA group showing the highest MMP and the lowest ROS-positive rate (P < 0.05). These findings suggest that dietary ALA supplementation enhances semen quality and antioxidant defenses, and mitigates oxidative stress, thus supporting the reproductive health of aging breeder roosters. This study underscores the potential of ALA as a dietary strategy to improve reproductive efficiency in poultry production.

INTRODUCTION

Reproductive performance in breeder roosters declines with age, leading to decreased semen quality and fertility rates, which are critical for poultry production efficiency. Aging in roosters is associated with oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, contributing to reduced sperm viability, motility, and overall reproductive capacity. As mitochondrial health is vital for energy production, cellular survival, and regulation of oxidative balance, strategies to enhance mitochondrial function and reduce oxidative damage are essential for improving reproductive outcomes in aging breeder roosters.

Alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), an essential omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (n-3 PUFA), has garnered attention for its potential benefits on reproductive health. n-3 PUFAs, including ALA, cannot be synthesized de novo in animal cells and must be acquired through diet. They are integral components of cell membranes, influencing membrane fluidity, function of membrane-bound proteins, and lipid-mediated signaling pathways (Los and Murata, 2004). Research has demonstrated that n-3 PUFAs can alter the phospholipid composition of sperm membranes, improve sperm density and quality, and ultimately enhance reproductive performance (Hosseini et al., 2019; Zanussi et al., 2019).

Previous studies have indicated that dietary supplementation with n-3 PUFAs can improve semen quality by enhancing sperm viability, motility, and density (Cerolini et al., 2005; Zadeh et al., 2020; Zanussi et al., 2019). These improvements are often attributed to the antioxidant properties of n-3 PUFAs, which mitigate oxidative stress and inhibit apoptosis, thereby preserving sperm integrity and function (Das, 2006b). In aging breeder roosters, oxidative stress is a significant factor leading to sperm damage, and n-3 PUFAs, through their incorporation into cell and mitochondrial membranes, can exert protective effects against oxidative damage (Gao et al., 2021a, 2021b; Surai et al., 2001).

The mitochondrial respiratory chain is a major source of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and aging leads to a substantial amount of electron leakage during the electron transfer process, increasing oxidative stress (Srivastava, 2017). n-3 PUFAs possess anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties (Mazza et al., 2007). Studies have shown that ALA extracted from flaxseed oil can reduce oxidative stress in various tissues (Pal and Ghosh, 2012b). ALA exerts its antioxidant effects by reducing lipid peroxidation and restoring the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and catalase (CAT) (Smink et al., 2008).

Given the established role of n-3 PUFAs in enhancing reproductive health and mitigating oxidative stress, this study aimed to investigate the effect of dietary ALA supplementation on semen quality, antioxidant capacity, and sperm survival rate in aging breeder roosters. By assessing parameters such as semen volume, sperm viability and density, antioxidant enzyme activity, and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), this study aims to elucidate the potential benefits of ALA in improving reproductive performance and mitochondrial health in aging roosters. The findings could provide insights into effective dietary strategies for maintaining reproductive efficiency in poultry production.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Birds and Diets

All rooster procedures were carried out by the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Beijing University of Agriculture (Beijing, China). The animal protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Beijing University of Agriculture. Ninety-six 52-wk-old Jingfen No. 6 layer breeder roosters with similar body weight and genetic background were randomly allocated to 1 of 4 dietary treatments, with each treatment having 6 replicates and each replicate containing 4 roosters. The prefeeding period of the experiment lasted for 2 wk (a corn-soybean meal-based diet), followed by a formal feeding period of 6 wk. The experimental groups were fed a corn-soybean meal-based diet (Table 1) containing different concentrations of ALA (0.5%, 1%, 2%), while the control group was fed with feed containing soybean oil (Yihai Kerry Arawana Holdings Co., Ltd., China). Feed was given 140 g/d/bird during the experiment period. In the treatment group, linseed oil (LO, Inner Mongolia Yishanyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was added by replacing an equivalent amount in the diet, with supplementation levels of 0 %, 1 %, 2 %, and 4 %, corresponding to actual ALA supplementation levels of 0 %, 0.5 %, 1 %, and 2 %, respectively. The experimental design is shown in Table 2. The linseed oil was substituted for soybean oil on an equal weight basis to equalize the metabolizable energy in all diets (Table 2). The fatty acid composition of the ALA source used in the diets is shown in Table 3. Sample Collection

Every 2 wk, 1 breeding rooster was randomly selected from each replicate, and its semen was collected through abdominal massage. The semen was then preserved in centrifuge tubes and immediately placed in a constant temperature environment at 37°C. The ejaculate volume of the breeding rooster was accurately measured using a disposable syringe. The quality of the semen was evaluated, including sperm motility (percentage of grade a + b sperm), sperm density, sperm forward progression, and sperm linear/curvilinear velocity. The sperm viability was assessed using the eosin-nigrosin staining technique on prepared slides. At the end of the experimental period, 1 breeding rooster was randomly selected from each replicate, and its semen was collected through abdominal massage. The semen samples were used for sperm MMP, ROS, and apoptosis detection. At the end of the experimental period, plasma samples were collected. Twelve hours before blood collection, the experimental breeding roosters were fasted and provided with water ad libitum. One breeding rooster was randomly selected from each replicate, and euthanized by exsanguination via the jugular vein. Blood was collected using a 10 mL clotting tube, filled to two-thirds capacity, and gently mixed to allow the clotting agent to take effect. The tubes were then placed flat at room temperature (25°C) for preservation. Subsequently, in the laboratory, the tubes were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min under conditions of 25°C. The supernatant plasma was slowly aspirated and aliquoted into 200 μL EP tubes, and stored at -80°C for subsequent measurement of antioxidant indices.

Semen Quality Analysis

Semen volume: The average volume of semen ejaculated by each breeding rooster was measured using a 1 mL syringe to collect the semen into a 5 mL centrifuge tube.

Sperm motility: Sperm motility was categorized as grade a + b, which included rapidly forward-moving sperm and slow or sluggish forward-moving sperm. To analyze sperm motility, 10 μL of semen was diluted in 490 μL of physiological saline, and 20 μL of the diluted semen was placed on a slide for analysis using a computer-assisted sperm analyzer (CASA) system (WLJX-9000 Weili Color Sperm Analysis System, Weili New Century Science & Tech Dev., Beijing, China) and with settings adjusted to detecting avian spermatozoa (Table 4). Five fields were observed, and a total of 200 sperm were analyzed. The specific analyzing procedures for semen samples were performed as described previously by Qi et al (Qi et al., 2019). Sperm viability: Sperm viability refers to the proportion of live sperm in the total sperm count. After diluting the semen (20 μL) in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, 1-2 drops of eosin staining solution were added and mixed, followed by a 30-min incubation period. Then, 3 drops of nigrosin staining solution were added and mixed, followed by a 30-60 second incubation period. The prepared semen was placed on a slide, and after drying, it was examined under a 40x microscope. The percentage of live sperm (white) and dead sperm (red) was counted at both ends and the middle of the slide, with 200 sperm analyzed to calculate the average.

Sperm density: Sperm density refers to the percentage of sperm count relative to semen volume. Ten microliters of semen were diluted in 490 μL of physiological saline, and 20 μL of the diluted semen was placed on a slide for analysis using a computer-assisted sperm analysis system. Five fields were observed, and a total of 200 sperm were analyzed.

Antioxidant Indices Assays

The antioxidant capacities of plasma were analyzed by the same method in duplicate as described before in our lab (Long et al., 2022a; Long et al., 2022b). Total superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using the xanthine/xanthine oxidase system for superoxide anion generation. This anion reduces nitroblue tetrazolium to formazan, which was monitored at 550 nm. Malondialdehyde (MDA) was assayed using the thiobarbituric acid method by measuring spectrophotometrically reactive products at 532 nm. Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity was determined based on the enzyme-catalyzed oxidation of GSH by cumene hydroperoxide coupled to the reduction of oxidized GSH by NADPH. Units of GSH-Px activity were expressed as micromole oxidized NADPH per minute. Catalase (CAT), hydroxyl radical scavenging ability (HRSA) and superoxide anion scavenging ability (SASA) in seminal plasma were measured using commercial Colorimetric kits according to the manufacturer's instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Sperm MMP detection

Freshly collected semen was diluted with sterile PBS to a concentration of approximately 1×107 sperm/mL, followed by flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson, CA, USA) staining. JC-1 was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a staining solution of 200 μM; 100 μL of suspension was taken from each sample tube to the bottom of a flow tube, approximately 1×106 sperm, and resuspended in 900 μL PBS to a total volume of 1 mL; a positive control tube was set up simultaneously, adding 1 μL of 50 mM CCCP, mixing well, and incubating at 37°C for 5 min; 10 μL of 200 μM JC-1 staining solution was added to each tube, mixed well, and incubated at 37°C for 30 min; 2 mL preheated PBS was added, mixed well, centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded; 500 μL preheated PBS was added, mixed well, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, CA). At least 10,000 sperm were acquired, and debris was excluded in the FSC vs SSC dot plot; Green (FITC channel) and yellow (PE channel) fluorescence signals were acquired, and instrument voltage and compensation conditions were set using blank controls; Cell gates were drawn in the dot plots of the green and red channels to analyze the proportion of sperm populations with changes in MMP.

Sperm ROS Detection

At least 10,000 sperm were acquired using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, CA), and debris was excluded in the FSC vs SSC dot plot; Green (FITC channel) fluorescence signals were acquired, and instrument voltage was adjusted using blank controls; Control samples were set up to determine ROS positivity rate and average fluorescence intensity.

Sperm apoptosis Detection

At least 10,000 sperm were acquired using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, CA), and debris was excluded in the FSC vs SSC dot plot; Green (FITC channel) and red (PI channel) fluorescence signals were acquired, and instrument voltage and compensation conditions were adjusted using blank control cells and single-stained detection tubes; A cross gate was drawn in the Annexin V-FITC/PI dot plot based on the blank control cells and cell clustering; In the plot, Annexin V-FITC-PI- represented live sperm, Annexin V+PI- represented early apoptotic sperm, and Annexin V+PI+ represented late apoptotic and damaged/dead sperm. The proportion of cells in each group was calculated to analyze sperm apoptosis damage.

RESULTS

Dietary ALA supplementation enhanced semen quality in aging breeder roosters

From Table 5, compared to the control group, the semen volume and sperm viability of aging roosters increased with ALA supplementation (P < 0.05). At 2 wk, compared to the control group, the sperm viability of 0.5 % ALA group and 1 % ALA group increased by 14.63% and 8.63%, respectively, with differences (P < 0.05). At 4 and 6 wk, the sperm viability of all treatment groups was higher than that of the control group (P < 0.05), and the maximum sperm viability was achieved when the ALA supplementation was 1%. The sperm density in the ALA groups were all greater than those in the control group, and at 6 wk, 1% ALA group and 2 % ALA group showed differences compared to the control group (P < 0.05), while there was no difference in 0.5 % ALA group (P > 0.05). Still, sperm density was higher than that of the control group (+0.242×108). DISCUSSION

n-3 PUFAs can alter the phospholipid composition of sperm membranes, improve sperm density and sperm quality, and eventually enhance reproductive performance (Qi et al., 2019; Zadeh et al., 2020). After ingestion, polyunsaturated fatty acids are distributed throughout nearly every cell, forming the lipids and fatty acids of cell nuclei and mitochondrial membranes. This, in turn, influences membrane structure and fluidity, the function of membrane-bound proteins, and lipid-mediated signaling (Li and Song, 2022; Los and Murata, 2004). Our previous research indicated that dietary supplementation with ALA significantly improved the semen quality of aging breeder roosters (57 wk old) (Qi et al., 2019), which is consistent with the results of this experiment. The current experimental results showed that the semen quality improved after dietary ALA was supplemented in the aging breeder roosters. n-3 PUFAs cannot be synthesized within animal cells and must be obtained from the diet. Therefore, the diet's quantitative and qualitative content of essential polyunsaturated fatty acids is crucial (Flachs et al., 2009; Simopoulos, 2010). Consequently, changes in the type and composition ratio of fatty acids in the diet will lead to changes in the fatty acid content of sperm (Asl et al., 2018; Cerolini et al., 2001; Cerolini et al., 2005; Smink et al., 2012). Studies have shown that the composition and content of different fatty acids in the testes and sperm affect the semen quality of male animals (Strzezek et al., 2004). Additionally, n-3 PUFAs can inhibit cell apoptosis, reduce ROS production, and are beneficial for sperm production (Das, 2006a).

The mitochondrial respiratory chain is a major source of ROS. Aging leads to a large amount of electrons leaking during the electron transfer process in the mitochondrial respiratory chain(Tabassum et al., 2020). n-3 PUFAs possess anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties(Mazza et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2010). Studies have shown that ALA extracted from flaxseed oil (at 0.5% or 1.0% for 2 wk) can reduce oxidative stress in rats' liver, kidneys, and blood induced by organic mercury (Pal and Ghosh, 2012a, 2012b). ALA exerts its antioxidant effects by reducing lipid peroxidation and restoring the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT (Smink et al., 2012). This experiment also confirmed that dietary supplementation with ALA can increase the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and GSH-Px, which is consistent with existing theories. SOD could catalyze the dismutation of superoxide anions (O2·-) into O2 and H2O2. Ultimately, CAT and GSH-Px convert H2O2 into oxygen molecules and water (Valko et al., 2007), maintaining oxidative balance in the body.

Mitochondria are crucial organelles within cells that play key roles, including energy production through the mitochondrial respiratory chain, regulation of cell survival and death, ion homeostasis, and the generation of free radicals (Park et al., 2018). In addition, Mitochondria play a significant role in the aging process because they are a primary source of ROS, key regulators of apoptosis, and central to energy production (Alessandri et al., 2006; De Caterina et al., 2006; Psota et al., 2006). Disruption of mitochondrial activity is an early marker of apoptosis, and changes in MMP (ΔΨm) occur due to the formation of pores in the mitochondrial membrane and the passage of ions, leading to uncoupling of the respiratory chain and release of cytochrome C. Both high inflammatory responses and mitochondrial dysfunction result in excessive production of oxidants. The oxidative stress hypothesis suggests that an imbalance in the dynamic equilibrium of oxidants/antioxidants leads to the production of toxic ROS. The large number of free radicals such as O2·-, H2O2, hydroxyl radical (OH·), and NO produced by ROS can cause cellular damage through chain reactions of membrane lipid peroxidation or changes in membrane fluidity (Puspita et al., 2017). Research has shown that PUFAs play an important role in determining the fluidity of cell membranes (Kojro et al., 2001; Peters et al., 2009). The current study showed that sperm survival rate enhanced, and the early and late apoptosis of sperm proportions all reduced after ALA supplementation in the aging roost's diet, these might be because ALA improved the high MMP proportion of sperm and reduced ROS-positive rate. Research has confirmed that BODIPY-labeled PUFAs can directly enter mitochondria and alter the MMP after entering cells. Further studies using MMP staining revealed that MMP significantly increased after n-3 PUFA treatment, indicating that n-3 PUFA treatment can resist mitochondrial damage in mouse germ cells (Hu et al., 2018). Additionally, in an acute testicular injury model induced by doxorubicin, n-3 PUFAs exhibited anti-apoptotic and anti-oxidative stress effects on germ cells, aiding spermatogenesis (Uygur et al., 2014). n-3 PUFAs (DHA) can also improve mitochondrial function by reducing oxidative damage to mitochondrial DNA and stress responses, and significantly increase mitochondrial content (Al-Gubory, 2012; Eckert et al., 2013; O'Shea et al., 2009), counteracting the harmful effects of palmitate on β-cell dysfunction (Bellini et al., 2018; Kato et al., 2008). These results suggest that n-3 PUFAs mitigate oxidative stress damage (Che et al., 2018).

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that dietary supplementation with ALA significantly enhances semen quality, antioxidant capacity, and sperm survival rate in aging breeder roosters. Key results from this study include the observation that ALA supplementation increased semen volume, sperm viability, and sperm density, with the 1% ALA group showing the most significant improvements. The best dosage recommendation was taken by the average x value, which was 1.24%, according to the corresponding regression equations of sperm quality parameters. Additionally, ALA supplementation improved MMP and reduced the rates of early and late sperm apoptosis, highlighting its protective effects on sperm integrity and function. These benefits underscore the importance of dietary n-3 PUFAs in maintaining reproductive health, particularly in aging populations.
