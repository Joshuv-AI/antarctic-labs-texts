Bactericidal effects of various root canal disinfectants and techniques on Enterococcus faecalis biofilms in extracted single-rooted human teeth: a systematic review
Authors: Yao Wang, Lanlan Ye, Xi Chen, He Meng, Irene Shuping Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess disinfectants and adjunctive techniques against Enterococcus faecalis biofilms in extracted single-rooted human teeth.MethodsA PRISMA-guided review of PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane, and Web of Science was conducted to identify in vitro studies utilizing extracted single-rooted teeth. Study quality was evaluated using a 13-item checklist adapted from JBI/CRIS guidance. Evidence was synthesized qualitatively and graded based on methodological limitations, relevance, consistency, and data adequacy.ResultsNinety-seven studies met the inclusion criteria. Mechanical preparation effectively reduced bacterial load but did not completely eradicate biofilms. Conventional irrigants produced variable results; however, combined protocols and activation methods often yielded the best disinfection outcomes. Physical activation enhanced irrigant penetration and antimicrobial effects. Antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) proved beneficial as an adjunct in certain protocols but was sensitive to specific parameters. Calcium hydroxide demonstrated limited efficacy, while triple antibiotic paste approached sterilization but raised concerns about resistance. Nanoparticle and natural irrigants showed potential but were generally less effective than sodium hypochlorite and require further validation. Only 28% of the studies were assessed as low risk of bias, with common shortcomings including inadequate blinding, inconsistent use of neutralizers, and heterogeneous outcome reporting.ConclusionsNo single method reliably eradicates Enterococcus faecalis biofilms; however, combined mechanochemical approaches with activation or aPDT appear most promising. Standardized, clinically relevant studies are necessary for further advancement.Keywords: Enterococcus faecalis, Biofilm, Root canal disinfection, Systematic review, Irrigants, Photodynamic therapy, Intracanal medicament

Introduction

Root canal treatment success is contingent upon the effective removal of microbial biofilms from the intricate root canal system; however, anatomical features such as isthmuses and accessory canals impede complete mechanical debridement and the penetration of irrigants [1–3]. Biofilms exhibit significant tolerance to antimicrobials due to an extracellular polymeric matrix that restricts the diffusion of agents and modifies microbial physiology, resulting in a level of resistance that surpasses that observed in planktonic cells, thereby complicating eradication efforts in endodontic contexts [4–7].

Among the pathogens implicated in endodontic infections, Enterococcus faecalis is frequently isolated from cases of persistent infection and retreatment, and is distinguished by its environmental resilience, ability to invade dentinal tubules, and capacity to form robust biofilms that diminish susceptibility to commonly employed disinfectants [8, 9], including low-concentration sodium hypochlorite and calcium hydroxide [10–12]. Research by Fisher et al. shows that Enterococcus faecalis can survive in high salt concentrations, withstand temperatures from 5 to 65 degrees Celsius, and thrive at pH levels between 4.5 and 10.0 [13]. These biological characteristics elucidate why mechanical preparation alone seldom achieves complete sterilization and why adjunctive chemical and activation strategies are extensively explored both in vitro and in clinical settings [14, 15].

Conventional irrigants and medicaments present various trade-offs: sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) provides tissue dissolution and broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity but raises concerns regarding cytotoxicity and dentin weakening at elevated concentrations [16–18]; chlorhexidine (CHX) offers substantivity but lacks tissue-dissolving properties and is incompatible with NaOCl [19, 20]; calcium hydroxide (CH) demonstrates limited efficacy against Enterococcus faecalis [21–23]; and triple antibiotic paste (TAP) can achieve high in vitro sterilization rates while raising concerns about antibiotic resistance and safety [24]. Adjunctive methods—such as mechanical activation (ultrasonic, sonic, laser), antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) [25, 26], nanoparticles, and natural extracts [27]—aim to enhance the penetration of irrigants or provide alternative antimicrobial mechanisms; however, their reported effectiveness varies and is often dependent on specific parameters [25, 28–30].

The heterogeneity of in vitro models, encompassing variations in tooth preparation, biofilm maturation protocols, baseline sterilization, use of neutralizers, sampling methods, and outcome measures, restricts comparability across studies and complicates the synthesis of evidence. Furthermore, many studies exhibit methodological limitations, including inadequate blinding and inconsistent reporting, which further constrain the interpretation of findings and their applicability to clinical practice.

In light of these challenges, a focused synthesis of in vitro evidence that limits inclusion to studies utilizing extracted single-rooted human teeth can elucidate the relative bactericidal efficacy of disinfectants and adjunctive techniques against Enterococcus faecalis biofilms under more standardized experimental conditions. This systematic review, therefore, compiles and critically evaluates such studies to summarize the current evidence, identify methodological deficiencies, and delineate priorities for standardized, clinically relevant research designs.

Methods

Research design

The present systematic review was performed according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses statement) [31].

Search strategy and data sources

We conducted a systematic search of the databases PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane, and Web of Science on May 23, 2025, employing a pre-defined search strategy to identify all pertinent clinical trials. The search utilized MeSH terms including ‘extracted human teeth’, ‘sterilization’, ‘disinfection’, and ‘storage’, in conjunction with relevant keywords: ‘tooth in vitro’, ‘disinfection’, ‘disinfestation’, ‘disinfestation’, ‘sterilizing agent’, and ‘storage’. Furthermore, we examined the references of relevant articles and reviews to identify additional eligible studies, and articles were retrieved through manual screening. Each study was evaluated by two independent reviewers, with any discrepancies resolved through consultation with a third reviewer.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This systematic review included in vitro experiments using extracted single-rooted human teeth as samples to summarize the antibacterial effects of various root canal disinfection methods. There are no restrictions on the languages or research designs of the studies included in the review.

We excluded studies that did not meet the predefined scope of this review. Specifically, the following types of reports were excluded: studies using non-extracted single-rooted human tooth models; studies that focused solely on planktonic bacteria without incorporating a biofilm model; studies that did not include Enterococcus faecalis or failed to report results specific to this bacterium; abstracts, conference proceedings, or reports for which the full text was unavailable; duplicate or overlapping publications from the same experiment, retaining only the most comprehensive report; and studies that did not provide relevant antibacterial or anti-biofilm outcome measures.

Quality assessment

In accordance with the guidelines established by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and the Review and Dissemination Center (CRIS) [32], and drawing upon the adaptation experiences from previous studies [33], this review has developed a comprehensive checklist consisting of 13 quality assessment parameters intended to evaluate the risk of bias in the sterilization tests for isolated teeth included in the analysis. These parameters encompass sample size calculation, establishment of a control group, age considerations, comprehensive information regarding experimental reagents, sample preparation conducted by a single operator, observer blinding, standardization of teeth, root canal preparation, bacterial cultivation, methods for preparing root canal contamination, utilization of neutralizers, post-sterilization sampling processes, and reporting of sterilization outcomes. If the authors provide the requisite information, the parameter will be marked as "Yes"; if no pertinent information is available, it will be marked as "No."

In accordance with the aforementioned 13 parameters, key aspects deemed critical in in vitro tooth disinfection studies—including tooth standardization, root canal contamination methods, bacterial cultivation, use of neutralizers, sampling procedures, and outcome reporting—were assigned a score of ‘2’ to emphasize their essential role in ensuring the validity and reproducibility of research findings [34, 35]. The omission of these components significantly increases the risk of bias.. Other parameters received a score of "1." The bias risk rating is classified into three tiers: a ROB score below 10 is designated as "high bias risk"; a ROB score ranging from 10 to 14 is classified as "moderate bias risk"; and a ROB score exceeding 14 is categorized as "low bias risk." The quality assessment was conducted by two independent reviewers, with any discrepancies in the evaluation process resolved through discussion. It is essential to recognize that the quality of the report is a significant reflection of the quality of the research; thus, information absent from the report cannot be obtained through alternative means.

Data extraction

The data extraction for this study was conducted by two independent researchers utilizing a pre-designed electronic spreadsheet that adhered to the PRISMA data elements. Before the formal extraction process, the team conducted calibration exercises to extract five randomly selected documents and reconcile discrepancies to establish a unified standard. If the two reviewers disagreed, they first attempted to resolve the issue through discussion. If consensus was not reached, a third reviewer made the final decision. All data were entered after reaching consensus. For unreported data, the authors were contacted for supplementary information, while the remaining data were made publicly available. The study characteristics include the first author's name, publication year, research country, research design, sample size, smear layer removal method, baseline sterilization method, target bacterial strain, baseline McFarland turbidity standard, culture temperature and duration post-bacterial infection, sampling time for colonies post-sterilization, and sterilization method. The extracted outcome indicators include the number of colonies following sterilization, the sterilization rate, and the P value of the comparison results among various sterilization methods.

Due to the varying data reporting formats of the studies included in the analysis, a meta-analysis could not be performed. Therefore, this study utilized the modified JBI & CRIS guidelines [32], along with evidence integration methods from previous in vitro reviews related to tooth studies [33], to summarize and organize the research findings on the sterilization effects of different methods on Enterococcus faecalis. The standards for evidence integration are as follows: 1) Strong evidence: The research question is supported by at least two high-quality studies, with more than 75% of these studies reaching a consistent conclusion on the issue; 2) Moderate evidence: The research question is addressed by one high-quality study and/or at least two low-quality studies, with more than 75% of these studies reaching a consistent conclusion on the issue; 3) Limited evidence: The issue is addressed by only one low-quality study; 4) Contradictory evidence: Fewer than 75% of the studies report consistent results on the issue.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Literature searches were conducted across four electronic databases, yielding a total of 2,700 publications. After removing 418 duplicate documents, the remaining 2,282 studies underwent a screening of titles and abstracts. This process excluded 208 reviews, trial protocols, meta-analyses, other designs that did not meet the criteria, and 1,869 studies reported irrelevant outcomes. The full-text screening involved 205 studies; however, 88 lacked full text, 16 did not report the sterilization effect of Enterococcus faecalis, 1 study focused on non-human teeth, and 3 did not assess the sterilization effect. We endeavored to acquire the full texts of 88 studies that provided only abstracts or whose original texts were not publicly accessible by broadening our database searches and reaching out to the authors; however, our efforts were unsuccessful. Ultimately, 97 studies were included in the systematic review (Table 1). The 97 studies included in this review were published between 2003 and 2025. The research sites encompassed 29 countries, with the principal contributors being Iran (n = 22) and India (n = 15). The number of tooth samples or sections utilized in the experiments varied from 18 to 400. The preparatory phase of this type of experiment can be broadly categorized into three steps: sample preparation of teeth, preparation of Enterococcus faecalis, and contamination of the tooth samples. The sample preparation stage consists of two essential steps: the removal of the smear layer and baseline sterilization of the tooth samples. Various concentrations and exposure durations of EDTA and NaOCl, either individually or in combination, were employed in the included studies to effectively eliminate the smear layer from isolated teeth. For baseline sterilization, high-pressure steam treatment at a temperature of 121℃ was implemented in 82 studies, while the remaining studies achieved sterilization through ethylene oxide, gamma radiation, or hydrogen peroxide gas plasma (PLASMA). The temperature for preparing contaminated Enterococcus faecalis was consistently maintained at 37℃ across 93 included studies. However, the duration of incubation exhibited considerable variability, ranging from overnight to 56 days. Furthermore, this study documented the turbidity of the bacterial-contaminated liquid following cultivation, as well as the bacterial sampling time of the tooth samples subsequent to the sterilization intervention, as delineated in Table 1. During the data extraction process, the inter-rater reliability among the two reviewers was notably high. The few discrepancies primarily pertained to the definitions of the outcome indicators, all of which were resolved through discussion or by the adjudication of a third reviewer.

Mechanical sterilization

This analysis includes four research reports that utilized mechanical preparation as the sterilization method, focusing on the effects of different techniques. Two studies specifically examined the sterilization efficacy of various brands and methods for single-use files, traditional Ni–Ti rotary instruments, the snake-shaped XP-endo Shaper, and the Hyflex EDM file (HEDM) produced by electrical discharge machining [90, 101]. Both studies found that while all types of instruments significantly reduced the presence of Enterococcus faecalis, none completely eliminated the biofilm. Additionally, they concluded that disposable files can provide an antibacterial effect comparable to traditional instruments, with differences based on brand and manufacturing processes.

The other two studies explored sterilization methods that combined mechanical preparation with chemical or physical irrigation. The 2006 study by V. B. Berber showed that high concentrations of NaOCl, when used with any mechanical preparation technique, could achieve a 100% antibacterial effect [40]. A separate study on combined root canal negative pressure irrigation reached a similar conclusion [103]. The specific quantitative data and qualitative conclusions of the four studies are reported in Table 3.

Natural ingredient irrigation

Using different concentrations of NaOCl and/or CHX as controls, four studies reported the root canal sterilization effects of five natural irrigating agents: curcumin, triphala, green tea polyphenols, Berberine chloride, and grape seed extract (GSE) (Table 6). All five natural irrigating agents demonstrated statistically significant antibacterial activity. The irrigation scheme combining Berberine chloride and CHX showed a sterilization effect comparable to that of high-concentration NaOCl [65]. Although the anti-Enterococcus faecalis effect of all tested products did not exceed that of the 3% to 5.25% NaOCl solution, the potential adverse properties of sodium hypochlorite suggest that herbal substitutes may could serve as an auxiliary method in dental pulp treatment [51, 53, 65, 108].

A total of 16 studies [38, 45, 47, 48, 54, 59, 66, 67, 78, 88, 89, 109, 110, 112, 118, 121] evaluated the sterilization efficacy of the laser activation method. The results indicated that the sterilization effect of a low-power diode laser alone was inadequate. However, lasers with continuous power ranging from 810 to 970 nm could effectively reduce the survival ability of Staphylococcus epidermidis biofilms within root canals. Nonetheless, the sterilization effect of lasers alone is inferior to that of NaOCl. In contrast, the irrigation method that combines laser irradiation with NaOCl, EDTA, or CHX achieved a sterilization effect comparable to, or even superior to, traditional chemical irrigation. Furthermore, results from three studies indicated that the power of laser-activated sterilization is positively correlated with its effectiveness.

Among the 23 studies, 10 included the sonic-activation group [39, 41, 57, 86, 95, 100, 107, 116]: 1 study on sonic-activation irrigation, 3 on ultrasonic-activated irrigation, 6 on passive ultrasonic irrigation, and 1 on subsonic-activated irrigation. There was no significant difference in efficacy between sonic-activation irrigation and needle irrigation or laser-activated sterilization. Both conventional syringe rinsing and 1% hypochlorous acid passive ultrasound rinsing can remove over 98% of Enterococcus faecalis biofilm from the root. Additionally, ultrasound combined with ozone shows some antibacterial effects (83.8%). The antibacterial efficacy of passive ultrasonic irrigation with NaOCl or CHX can reach 99% to 100%, but the sterilization effect is less effective when used alone (50.2%). The subsonic activation method is more effective than NaOCl rinse alone in germicidal effect in the root canal.

Three studies reported the antibacterial effects of two photoacoustic streaming sterilization methods: photon-initiated photoacoustic streaming (PIPS) and shock wave enhanced emission photoacoustic streaming (SWEEPS). PIPS alone is less effective than passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) and diode laser activation. However, combined with 2.5% NaOCl irrigation, its sterilization rate reaches 99.966%, surpassing traditional 2.5% NaOCl needle irrigation [39, 67]. One study also showed that ultra-short pulse (USP) SWEEPS combined with NaOCl irrigation achieves a strong antibacterial effect [94].

Antimicrobial photodynamic therapy

Among the 97 studies in the review, 28 specifically addressed the sterilization effects of antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) (Table 8) [43, 52, 56, 61, 62, 69–74, 76, 79, 81, 82, 84, 87, 91–93, 96, 97, 102, 113, 114, 119, 123, 125]. In contrast to laser-activated irrigation sterilization, aPDT uses light of a specific wavelength to activate non-toxic photosensitizers (PS). The activated photosensitizers transfer energy to adjacent oxygen molecules, generating a substantial amount of cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce bacterial death [129].

Among the 25 studies reviewed, 10 reported the sterilization effects of photodynamic therapy (PDT) alone. Two studies compared the sterilization effects of PDT using different light sources (LED and diode laser), while six studies utilized traditional chemical rinsing methods as controls. Two studies employed laser activation sterilization as a control, and another two used root canal filling drugs. One study examined the sterilization effects of varying durations of PDT. Regarding the PDT light source, two studies concluded that the aPDT sterilization effect using LED lamps was superior to that of diode lasers, although the difference may not have been statistically significant. Most studies suggested that while aPDT might not achieve complete sterilization, it could provide a sterilization effect comparable to or slightly inferior to traditional single-drug or combined chemical rinsing methods. However, Omid's 2014 study stated that aPDT with wavelengths between 635 and 650 nm was ineffective in destroying artificial microbial biofilms within the dental pulp and could not inhibit bacterial growth within a clinically beneficial time frame. Considering the positive correlation between PDT intervention time and sterilization effect identified in Chihan's 2013 study, along with the PDT treatment wavelengths of 660 nm included in the reviewed studies, we speculate that power and light duration may be important factors influencing the PDT sterilization effect. The sterilization effect of PDT was slightly better than that of the group using only laser treatment but not as effective as the sodium hypochlorite and helium/oxygen plasma treatment groups with laser activation. Additionally, the sterilization effect of aPDT was comparable to that of TAP/MTAP.

The 13 studies in the review reported results from the aPDT combined sterilization scheme. Two studies found that the antibacterial effects of aPDT combined with CHX were similar, indicating that this approach did not demonstrate stronger antibacterial properties than either CHX or aPDT alone. Six studies examining aPDT combined with NaOCL reported that the antibacterial effect of the combined scheme was comparable to NaOCL alone and significantly better than PDT alone or laser-activated NaOCL. Two studies on aPDT combined with PUI reached contradictory conclusions regarding whether aPDT could enhance the antibacterial effect of PUI. One study showed that aPDT combined with chitosan or TAP could enhance the antibacterial effect. Two reports on the research results of the SWEEPS technique combined with PDT concluded that this approach can enhance the antibacterial efficacy of PDT. Additionally, three studies investigated whether the use of optical fibers, application position, and innovations in photosensitizers influenced the antibacterial efficacy of PDT. Results indicated that the use of optical fibers and the depth of fiber insertion had minimal impact on the antibacterial effect of aPDT. Notably, Tennert's 2015 study showed that, compared to traditional PDT, using photosensitizers based on EDTA or citric acid, or PDT activated by ultrasound, could significantly reduce the count of Enterococcus faecalis.

Traditional drugs included calcium hydroxide, chlorhexidine, triple antibiotic paste (TAP), chitosan, ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA), and propylene glycol. Calcium hydroxide is the gold standard for root canal sealants. Of the 12 trials on traditional drugs [42, 46, 58, 75, 80, 85, 98, 99, 104, 111, 124, 130], 9 focused on the single use of calcium hydroxide or its combination with other agents. When used alone, calcium hydroxide's antibacterial effect was inferior to that of TAP (n = 4) and 2% CHX (n = 5), although its long-term effect surpassed that of CHX (n = 1). Calcium hydroxide concentration correlated positively with its antibacterial effect (n = 1). The combination of calcium hydroxide and CHX was less effective than either drug alone (n = 3), while the combination of calcium hydroxide and propylene glycol outperformed 2% CHX gel (n = 1). Four studies on TAP indicated that TAP alone had a superior sterilization effect compared to calcium hydroxide, chlorhexidine, and plasma, achieving complete sterilization in some cases. The combined use of propylene glycol with TAP also demonstrated a high sterilization effect. The single or combined use of chitosan or EDTA as natural substances showed potential as sterilization options (n = 2).

Natural substances for root canal sealants include propolis, Morinda citrifolia juice, curcumin, aloe vera gel, ginger glycolic extract, S. persica extract, and mustard gel [27–29, 63, 68, 77, 117, 127]. Five out of 8 studies verified the antibacterial effect of propolis, showing it was comparable to calcium hydroxide, significantly better than Morinda citrifolia juice and curcumin, but inferior to CHX. The combined use of propolis and chitosan could enhance the antibacterial effect. One study indicated that the sterilization effects of ginger glycolic extract, S. persica extract, and mustard gel were comparable to calcium hydroxide, with mustard gel exhibiting significantly better effects.

Five reports described three nanoparticle sterilization schemes: Ca(OH)2 combined with Gum Arabic nanocarrier (GAs) nanoparticles (NPs), Ca(OH)2 combined with silver nanoparticles (SN), chitosan-propolis nanoparticles (CPN) and nanochitosan [49, 50, 105, 115, 122]. The sterilization effect of the Ca(OH)2-GAs-NPs scheme was superior to Ca(OH)2 combined with nanoparticles alone (n = 1). Although the Ca(OH)2-SN scheme demonstrated a significant sterilization effect, its antibacterial effect was inferior to that of Ca(OH)2 combined with chitosan or CHX (n = 2). The antibacterial effect of CPN was comparable to that of 2% CHX gel (n = 1). Furthermore, the latest research published in 2025 confirms that the root canal disinfection effectiveness of nanochitosan gel is superior to that of Ca(OH)2 paste (n = 1).

Among the 27 studies, two focused on innovative root canal sterilization agents [36, 126]: medicated gutta-percha with CHX diacetate Activ Point and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (Lgg.LTA). The antibacterial test results for Activ Point indicated that CHX-containing agents are effective against both Enterococcus faecalis and Streptococcus mutans [36]. Furthermore, a recent study by Yoon in 2024 [126] confirmed the potential of Lgg.LTA, a probiotic, in root canal sterilization.

Strong evidence indicates that mechanical preparation using nickel-titanium (NiTi) instrumentation significantly reduces bacterial load, although it does not consistently eradicate biofilms. Selected natural ingredient irrigants, such as curcumin and berberine, exhibit reliable antibacterial activity, albeit generally less potent than sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl). Additionally, antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) is recognized as an effective adjunctive modality when appropriate photosensitizers and light parameters are utilized. Furthermore, nanoparticle-enhanced intracanal medicaments demonstrate superior short-term antibacterial efficacy compared to conventional calcium hydroxide.

Moderate evidence endorses the efficacy of traditional chemical irrigants (NaOCl, chlorhexidine), sonic and ultrasonic activation, as well as several intracanal medicaments, including triple antibiotic paste, based on consistent findings predominantly from studies with moderate ROB.

Limited evidence is available for isolated novel techniques, such as single studies on photoacoustic streaming or specific nanoparticle irrigants.

Contradictory evidence is evident in the case of laser monotherapy, where heterogeneous parameters and mixed outcomes hinder the establishment of a consistent conclusion.

Sources of heterogeneity

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across the 97 included studies, which limited direct comparability and precluded quantitative synthesis. The principal sources of variability included biofilm maturation time, which varied from overnight incubation to 56 days; irrigant type, concentration, and exposure duration, with sodium hypochlorite tested at concentrations ranging from one to six percent and exposure times varying from a few seconds to several minutes; activation parameters, encompassing modalities such as passive ultrasonic irrigation, laser activation, and photodynamic therapy, which differed widely in power settings and application durations; baseline sterilization methods, including autoclaving, gamma irradiation, hydrogen peroxide plasma, and ethylene oxide; and sampling timing and outcome measurement, where studies reported colony-forming unit counts, log10 reductions, percentage reductions, or confocal laser scanning microscopy assessments collected at intervals ranging from 24 h to seven days. Additional contributors to heterogeneity included inconsistent dentin standardization and varied smear layer removal protocols, as well as heterogeneous reporting formats for results. These methodological discrepancies not only increased variability between studies but also likely influenced apparent intervention effects; for instance, shorter-matured biofilms may have appeared more susceptible to irrigants, and differing sampling times could have masked bacterial regrowth. For these reasons, a narrative synthesis was conducted rather than a meta-analysis. Future studies should adopt standardized protocols for biofilm maturation, irrigant concentration and exposure, activation settings, sampling procedures, and outcome reporting to facilitate more reliable comparisons and quantitative evidence synthesis.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes data from ninety-seven in vitro studies evaluating disinfection strategies against Enterococcus faecalis biofilms in extracted single-rooted human teeth. The evidence indicates that no single method consistently achieves complete eradication of Enterococcus faecalis biofilms. Combined mechanochemical approaches, particularly those augmented by irrigation activation or photodynamic adjuncts, most frequently produce the largest and most consistent reductions in bacterial burden, a conclusion supported by multiple high-quality studies. Although the evidence for nanoparticle formulations and natural irrigants is promising, it is limited by a small number of studies, variable quality, and sparse safety data. Reports of near-complete eradication with triple antibiotic paste are inconsistent and raise concerns regarding clinical applicability due to issues related to antibiotic resistance and potential adverse effects.

Mechanical preparation significantly reduces bulk biofilm by physically removing infected dentin, typically resulting in a reduction of bacterial counts by approximately fifty percent in experimental settings [131]. However, the complex anatomy of root canals—including isthmuses, lateral canals, and accessory canals—often remains inaccessible to instruments, thereby serving as a reservoir for residual microorganisms [132]. These anatomical limitations elucidate why mechanical treatment alone is inadequate and highlight the necessity for integrating debridement with chemical and physical adjuncts to enhance irrigant delivery and biofilm disruption [90]. The selection of instruments should strive for an optimal balance between flexibility, cutting efficiency, and clinical practicality, while also considering operator skill and preference. Although certain protocols that combine instrumentation with simple irrigants have reported high antibacterial rates—for instance, saline in conjunction with instrumentation demonstrating antibacterial effects in most cases [133], and sodium hypochlorite at concentrations ranging from 0.5% to 5.25% achieving significant efficacy in numerous studies [40]—such laboratory findings warrant cautious interpretation when applied to clinical practice. The persistence of Enterococcus faecalis biofilms following initial therapy, attributed to the organism's tolerance of adverse conditions, penetration into dentinal tubules, and adherence at dentin–filling interfaces, poses a direct threat to the success of non-surgical retreatment by facilitating recolonization, perpetuating periapical inflammation, and diminishing healing rates [134, 135]. Consequently, retreatment strategies should explicitly aim to address biofilm resilience through extended or repeated disinfection, adjunctive activation or photodynamic modalities to enhance irrigant penetration, and the use of intracanal medicaments with proven dentinal tubule penetration.

Sodium hypochlorite is the most extensively studied irrigant due to its tissue-dissolving capacity, broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, and ability to penetrate dentinal tubules to significant depths [136]. Its bactericidal effect is strongly dependent on concentration and contact time; however, higher concentrations raise concerns regarding cytotoxicity to periapical tissues and detrimental effects on dentin's mechanical properties. Chlorhexidine provides sustained antimicrobial activity and can achieve comparable reductions after prolonged exposure, but it lacks tissue-dissolving ability and is incompatible with NaOCl in sequence [137, 138]. Comparative studies indicate no consistent superiority between hydrogen peroxide and chlorhexidine in antibacterial efficacy [37, 139, 140], with optimal chlorhexidine concentrations reported to be around two percent in some experimental contexts [138]. Alternative chemical agents, natural extracts, and nanoparticle formulations exhibit in vitro antibacterial activity comparable to traditional agents in select studies [44, 51, 53, 65, 106], yet heterogeneity in formulations, concentrations, and exposure times complicates firm conclusions about their clinical applicability. Methodological variability, including biofilm maturation duration, neutralizer use, and outcome metrics, further complicates interpretation and hinders reliable pooling of results.

Physical activation techniques enhance irrigant penetration and biofilm disruption by generating acoustic streaming, cavitation, and thermal effects that facilitate fluid exchange within complex anatomical structures [66, 107, 141]. Active irrigation methods generally demonstrate superior performance compared to syringe irrigation in laboratory comparisons; however, heterogeneity in device types, power settings, and application protocols impedes the establishment of a definitive ranking of activation modalities [141–144]. Some studies suggest a saturation effect for NaOCl activation, wherein further increases in dose or activation do not yield proportional gains in sterilization [145]. Antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) has shown potential as an adjunctive modality, producing substantial bacterial reductions in several studies, including synergistic effects when combined with NaOCl [70, 75, 146]. Reported reductions with aPDT range from significant single-method effects to near-complete eradication when combined with conventional treatment under specific experimental conditions [75]. The efficacy of aPDT is highly parameter-dependent, with factors such as photosensitizer choice, light wavelength, energy density, and biofilm accessibility critically influencing outcomes.

Intracanal medicaments provide prolonged antimicrobial exposure through diffusion into dentinal tubules; however, commonly used agents exhibit variable performance. Calcium hydroxide demonstrates inconsistent efficacy against Enterococcus faecalis, likely due to the organism's tolerance of high pH environments [24, 75]. Triple antibiotic paste can achieve high bactericidal activity in vitro, yet its clinical application is constrained by the risk of promoting antibiotic resistance and potential adverse effects [24]. Therefore, risks to periapical and periodontal tissues must be evaluated when selecting disinfection strategies, particularly regarding antibiotic stewardship. Chemical agents with strong antimicrobial properties may induce local tissue toxicity; for example, high concentrations of sodium hypochlorite are linked to cytotoxic effects on periapical tissues and alterations in dentin properties. Accidental extrusion of irrigants can worsen tissue injury and hinder healing [147]. Although triple antibiotic paste shows high in vitro bactericidal efficacy, its routine use raises concerns about selecting resistant organisms and disrupting local microbial ecology. Pereira et al. cautioned that TRIA should be used carefully as an intracanal dressing in clinical practice. In a mouse model, TRIA triggered a prolonged subcutaneous connective tissue reaction, marked by increased expression of inflammatory and angiogenic genes and a lack of a reparative response [148]. Therefore, when antibiotics are required, their use should follow principles of minimal effective exposure, targeted indication, and monitoring for adverse effects [149]. Future studies should report cytotoxicity, periapical tissue responses, and evidence of microbial resistance to facilitate assessments of benefits versus harms.

Nanoparticle formulations and natural medicaments present attractive alternatives due to their novel mechanisms of action and potential biocompatibility, although current evidence remains preliminary. Significant gaps include limited replication, inconsistent characterization of formulations, and inadequate assessment of cytotoxicity, tissue accumulation, and long-term safety [75, 77, 115]. Regulatory considerations and standardized manufacturing practices are prerequisites before recommending these agents for clinical use.

A primary limitation of the existing evidence base is the reliance on in vitro models that simplify the complex physiological, immunological, and anatomical environments present in clinical infections. Variability across studies in biofilm maturation time, irrigant concentration and exposure, activation parameters, baseline sterilization methods, and timing of sampling has resulted in substantial heterogeneity, precluding meta-analysis. Many included studies also exhibit methodological weaknesses, including inadequate blinding, unclear randomization, and a lack of sample size justification, thereby increasing the risk of bias and limiting confidence in pooled interpretations. The exclusion of studies for which full texts were unavailable may further introduce publication bias despite efforts to retrieve missing documents.

To enhance comparability and translational relevance, future research should adopt standardized protocols. Recommended minimum reporting items include a defined biofilm maturation period, explicit irrigant concentration and exposure time, detailed specifications for activation devices and settings, standardized smear layer removal and dentin preparation, dual outcome assessment utilizing colony-forming unit counts and confocal laser scanning microscopy to capture both quantitative and spatial effects, sample size calculations, blinding of outcome assessors, and uniform reporting formats such as log10 CFU reductions. Providing completed data extraction templates and comprehensive methodological details in supplementary materials will further enhance reproducibility and facilitate meaningful quantitative synthesis.

Conclusion

The combination of mechanochemical preparation with supplementary irrigation activation or photodynamic adjuncts has shown the most consistent reductions in Enterococcus faecalis biofilm burden. However, considerable methodological heterogeneity, the use of simplified laboratory models, and frequent reporting deficiencies limit the certainty and clinical applicability of these findings. A few studies reported complete eradication under specific laboratory conditions, but these results are not consistently reproducible and should not be seen as evidence of reliable clinical sterilization. Safety concerns, including potential cytotoxicity, adverse effects on dentin properties, and the risk of promoting antibiotic resistance with antibiotic-based medicaments, further limit immediate clinical implementation. Future research should focus on standardized in vitro protocols, rigorous evaluations of tissue compatibility and resistance development, and well-designed clinical trials that assess both microbiological and patient-centered outcomes. Until such data are available, clinicians should view in vitro efficacy as mechanistic support rather than a direct justification for changing routine practice.
