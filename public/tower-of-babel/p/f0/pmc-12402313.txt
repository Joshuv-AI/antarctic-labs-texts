Computational investigation of mechanical properties and adsorption behavior of sulfur-doped h-BN nanosheets
Authors: Azadeh Ghahremani, Zahra Tavangar, Mohammad Reza Zamani Seyfikar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Doping h-BN surface with sulfur has been shown to enhance its efficiency in photocatalytic reactions. Here, using density functional theory calculations, the various configurations of S-doped h-BN were investigated in terms of their formation energy, mechanical properties, structural, thermodynamic, and electronic properties, as well as their ability to adsorb metal atoms and hydrogen molecule. The formation energy of S-doped h-BN surfaces is only slightly more positive compared to the pristine surface. The band gap energy experiences the most significant changes when an S atom replaces an N atom. At low temperatures, the pristine h-BN surface has a higher vibrational Gibbs free energy compared to S-doped h-BN surfaces, but at high temperatures, it exhibits a lower value. The bulk modulus of S-doped h-BN surfaces was found to range from 151.2 to 163.0 GPa. Additionally, the Poisson’s ratio of the SN surface was found to be 0.237, which is slightly higher than that of the h-BN surface. The evaluated in-plane hardness of S-doped surfaces ranges from 221.8 to 196.1 GPa. Investigation into the adsorption of transition metals (Mo, Zn, Cd), post-transition metals (Ga), and metalloids (Ge, Se) on both pristine and S-doped h-BN surfaces revealed significantly enhanced binding energies on the S-doped surfaces. The strongest interactions occurred with the s-doped surface decorated with Mo and Ge metals. Hydrogen dissociatively adsorbs on the SN surface. Also, the hydrogen molecule binds more strongly to metal-decorated surfaces. The results show that the S-doping of h-BN surface improves its electronic characteristics and adsorption capability without significantly altering its mechanical propertiesSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17202-y.

Introduction

Crystalline hexagonal boron nitride is a synthetic material that was first successfully produced in the mid-nineteenth century1. This structure is the most stable polymorphic form of boron nitride at room temperature2, which has a structure similar to graphite. Strong covalent bonds bind boron and nitrogen atoms in each layer, whereas weak van der Waals interactions hold separate layers together3. Similar to graphene, a hexagonal boron nitride layer (h-BN) has a planar structure. The synthesis of high-quality h-BN thin films with excellent intrinsic characteristics remains an intriguing topic4. However, unlike graphene, this nanosheet has a wide band gap, which results in excellent chemical stability and oxidation resistance at high temperatures5,6. Therefore, despite its similarities to graphene, h-BN’s unique features make it superior. These unique properties, along with its high surface area, low toxicity7, and adjustable band gap8, leading to the investigation of its various applications, including in quantum emitters9, electrochemical measurements10, molecular switching11 as a solid-lubricating additive in self-lubricating composites12, as a corrosion protective coating13 gas adsorption and detection14,15, biosensing16, and in energy storage17–19.

The mechanical properties of the h-BN surface have been of interest since the beginning due to potential applications in some fields, such as polymer nanocomposites20. Depending on the method used, different computational studies have predicted different values in the range of 267.0–281.6 j/m2 for the in-plane stiffness of this surface. Additionally, Poisson’s ratio of the h-BN surface has been reported in the range of 0.213–0.29721–26. Examining the elastic properties of this surface through theoretical or experimental methods has also been done to estimate its bulk modulus27–30.

Despite the high chemical and thermal stability of h-BN, its high band gap limits its usage in some applications. Therefore, efforts have been made to alter its electronic characteristics through methods such as doping or decorating the surface with metal atoms31–33. Many computational and experimental studies have been reported on the h-BN surface, which have tried this method to improve catalytic activity34,35, optical and magnetic properties8,36,37, energy storage38,39, sensing properties40–42, and the adsorption or removal of pollutants by the surface43–45.

The effect of sulfur atom replacement on the properties of the h-BN nanosheet has been experimentally investigated. S-doping generally results in an increase in the h-BN surface’s reactivity, hydrophilicity, charge transfer, and optical absorption. As a result, hydrogen is produced on this surface more efficiently than conventional visible-light photocatalysts46. In addition, research has shown that the band gap and layer spacing of h-BN can be altered by regulating the amount of doped sulfur8. The structural and electronic properties of the S-doped h-BN have been studied through density functional theory (DFT) calculations47. Furthermore, the computational approach has been utilized to demonstrate the impact of co-doping with Fe and S atoms on the stability and catalytic activity of the h-BN surface48.

The fundamental understanding of S-doped h-BN surfaces, derived from a thorough characterization of their structural stability, electronic and optical properties, thermodynamic behavior, and mechanical characteristics, is critical for investigating their manifold applications. Although the S-doped h-BN surface has been studied concerning its optical properties, no research has been conducted on how S-substitution affects this surface’s mechanical properties or adsorption behavior. In the present study, a computational survey has been done on S-doped h-BN surface utilizing density functional theory. The mechanical and thermodynamic properties of various configurations of S-doped h-BN have been examined, as well as their structural and electronic characteristics. Furthermore, the effect of S substitution on the adsorption of some atoms on the surface was examined, as well as the hydrogen molecule interaction with the resultant surfaces. The investigated atoms in this study are Se, Ga, Ge, Mo, Cd, and Zn, whose adsorption on the h-BN surface has not been looked at. Density of states (DOS), partial density of states (PDOS), and band structure diagrams were used to investigate the effects of sulfur doping, metal decoration, and hydrogen adsorption on surface electronic properties. Charge transfers during these processes were investigated using Mulliken charge analysis and deformation charge density plots.

Computational methods

All DFT calculations were performed using the Dmol3 module in the Material Studio software package49. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) in the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional form was used to evaluate exchange–correlation energies50. The basis set used in this study is the double numerical polarizing functions (DNP)51, which has comparable accuracy to the Pople 6-31G** basis set. This basis set is commonly used in the study of 2D surfaces52–54. Grimme’s semi-empirical DFT-D2 method was utilized to take into account Van der Waals interactions55. A vacuum distance of 20 Å was considered in the z-direction to avoid intercellular interaction. In relaxation calculations, the k-points of the Brillouin zone were sampled using a 5 × 5 × 1 grid produced by the Monkhorst–Pack scheme56. This k-points grid was chosen based on its convergence with energy (Fig. S1). All calculations were performed using a global orbital cutoff radius of 4.9 Å. The values for the convergence threshold for maximum change in energy, force, and displacement were set to be 10–5 Ha, 0.002 Ha.Å−1, and 0.005 Å during geometry optimization, respectively.

To model S-doped h-BN surfaces, in line with experimental findings that confirm S–B and S–N bond presence in this surface8,46 (thereby implying sulfur substitution for nitrogen and boron), three configurations were designed: S-substitution for a B atom, S-substitution for an N atom, or S-substitution for a pair of adjacent N and B atoms. The resulting surfaces were named SB, SN, and SBN, respectively. The supercell constants of h-BN were found to be a = b = 7.554 Å, and B-N bond length and B-N-B bond angle were found to be 1.454 Å and 120°, respectively, which are in agreement with previously published results43,62,63. As Table 1 shows, substituting the S atom for B or N atoms increases the unit cell size, which is reasonable given the larger atomic radius of S compared to B or N atoms. In the SB structure, the sulfur atom is closer to the surface, resulting in a larger cell parameter. The unit cell parameter for the SBN structure shows a decrease compared to the h-BN structure. This decrease is anticipated and results from the substitution of one sulfur atom for two atoms of surface. The sulfur atom in SBN and SN has a completely symmetrical position relative to its neighboring atoms, but this is not true for SB.

In the SB and SN structures, the neighboring atoms of the S atom have a small distance from the surface (up to 0.19 Å), but the other atoms remain on the surface. The S atom in the SBN structure is located at an average distance of 0.50 Å from the surface. However, despite the slight distortion observed on the surface, the overall structure maintains its planar form.

A review of the reported formation energies in Table 1 reveals that all S-doped h-BN surfaces have a negative formation energy. Moreover, the S-doped h-BN surfaces’ formation energies are not significantly different from those of the h-BN surface, despite being more positive. So that the formation energy of the SB structure is only 0.23 Ha higher than that of the h-BN surface. The formation energies of SN and SBN are close to each other and are more different from the h-BN formation energy. Nevertheless, experimental studies also support the substitution of sulfur atoms instead of nitrogen atoms46.

In order to evaluate the electronic and magnetic characteristics of BN and S-doped surfaces, their band structures and PDOS plots were analyzed. Based on the band structure plot (Fig. S3), the h-BN surface is a p-type semiconductor with a large band gap energy of 4.66 eV, which matches the reported values47,62,64. The h-BN surface’s down-spin bands completely overlap the up-spin bands and show no spin polarization (Fig. S3), indicating the nonmagnetic property of the surface. According to the PDOS diagram of the h-BN surface, the p orbitals around the Fermi level play the main and decisive role (Fig. 2). These orbitals strongly interact with s orbitals in a wide energy range.Fig. 2PDOS diagrams of h-BN and S-doped h-BN surfaces. The Fermi level is set at zero energy.

PDOS diagrams of h-BN and S-doped h-BN surfaces. The Fermi level is set at zero energy.

The band structure diagrams show a significant change in surface electronic properties due to S-doping. The energy states formed around the Fermi level decreased the band gap energy to 0.36, 0.85, and 1.82 eV for the SN, SB, and SBN surfaces, respectively. Research indicates that lattice distortion significantly influences the energy gap65,66. Consequently, the observed reduction in the band gap of S-doped h-BN is likely attributable to structural distortions induced by sulfur substitution and the resultant alterations in the boron-nitrogen bonds within the unit cell. The replacement of the N atom with the S atom has turned the surface into an n-type semiconductor. Furthermore, the SBN surface’s spin-down and spin-up bands fully overlap. On the other hand, the spin polarization is plainly seen in the band structure of the SB and SN surfaces. As a result, SB and SN surfaces are magnetic in contrast to h-BN and SBN surfaces. The electronic properties of both SN and SB surfaces align with those reported by Asif et al.47, exemplified by the resemblance in their respective band structure diagrams to our findings. However, discrepancies in band gap energies are evident, likely attributable to the considerably lower sulfur concentration utilized in their structures.

PDOS plots of S-doped h-BN surfaces are presented in Fig. 2 A comparison of the PDOS diagram of S-doped surfaces with that of the h-BN surface reveals that the addition of sulfur has caused a shift in the peaks toward negative energies. S-doped h-BN surfaces, similar to the pristine surface, exhibit high peaks near the Fermi level, primarily attributed to the p orbitals of surface atoms. The d orbital peaks of the S atom strongly resonate with the s and p orbitals of this atom, which confirms their hybridization. The d orbital peaks of the S atom in the valence region indicate electron transfer to these orbitals. However, the positive charge on the sulfur atom in the SB and SBN surfaces indicates that overall, a charge transfer from this atom occurs due to the bonding with the surface. Furthermore, the overlap of the S atom orbital peaks with the s and p orbital peaks of surface atoms over a wide energy range provides evidence of strong bonding between them.

In order to examine the impact of S-doping on the surface’s thermodynamic properties, frequency calculations were performed for all surfaces at the Gamma point. These calculations show that the optimized surfaces have no imaginary frequency, which indicates the stability of these structures. The frequency outputs were used to determine the zero-point energy and thermodynamic properties. Figure 3 shows the thermodynamic properties graphs for vibrational degrees of freedom vs. temperature. It can be observed that the h-BN surface has a higher entropy than S-doped h-BN surfaces at all temperatures (Fig. 3a), and the entropies of the SB and SN surfaces are extremely close to each other. However, the entropy of the SBN surface is higher than that of the SB and SN surfaces up to 500 K, but SBN shows lower entropy at higher temperatures.Fig. 3Variation of (a) specific heat capacity (CV), (b) entropy (S), (c) Gibbs free energy (G), and (d) enthalpy (H) with temperature for h-BN, SB, SN, and SBN surfaces.

It is evident from Fig. 3d that the h-BN surface has the highest Gibbs free energy at low temperatures, whereas the SBN surface has the lowest value. The pattern changes, though, and the h-BN surface has the lowest Gibbs free energy, while the SBN surface has the highest at high temperatures.

Equations of state and mechanical properties

Biaxial compressive and tensile strains up to 25% were applied to the supercell of each surface. After optimization, the supercell energy and volume for different strains were used as input for the Gibbs2 program. Figure S4 shows the energy diagrams vs. supercell parameters for pristine and S-doped h-BN surfaces. Using the Debye model to incorporate the effect of temperature and Birch-Murnaghan polynomial averages, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{0}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{0}^{\prime}$$\end{document} were calculated for different pressures and temperatures using the aforementioned program.

For this purpose, compressive and tensile strains were applied along the x and y axes in the harmonic region, in the range of 0–0.02%, and each structure was completely optimized. The strain energy was calculated for each grid point, and the data were fitted to Eq. 3 using the least squares method.

In Fig. 5, strain energy is plotted against cell parameters for pristine and S-doped h-BN surfaces. As evident from the diagrams, the maximum strain energy corresponds to the condition where the compressive or tensile strain is the highest and is equal along both axes. In this condition, the strain energy of the pristine h-BN surface is higher compared to that of S-doped surfaces, and the strain energies of S-doped surfaces are very close. The strain energy of S-doped surfaces exhibits a larger difference when compressive strain is applied along one axis and tensile strain is applied along the other. Using the obtained coefficients for Eq. 3, Poisson’s ratio and in-plane hardness were calculated for different surfaces with the help of Eqs. 4 and 5. The calculated in-plane hardness values for h-BN, SB, SN, and SBN surfaces are 274.2, 221.8, 196.1, and 206.8 J/m2, respectively. Additionally, the evaluated Poisson’s ratio values for these surfaces are 0.224, 0.169, 0.237, and 0.216, respectively. For the pristine h-BN surface, the obtained values of Poisson’s ratio and in-plane hardness are in good agreement with previously published values22,25,26. Comparing the in-plane hardness of nanosheets shows that S-doping of the h-BN surface increases its elasticity. Accordingly, the SN nanosheet exhibits the highest elasticity compared to other surfaces. The comparison of Poisson’s ratio for the studied nanosheets shows that replacing a N atom or a pair of N and B atoms with an S atom (SN and SBN) does not cause much change in Poisson’s ratio. Replacing a B atom with an S atom decreases Poisson’s ratio, which means an increase in the resistance of the surface against deformation.

By elucidating the influence of S-doping on bulk modulus and in-plane stiffness, these findings offer fundamental guidance for the rational design of h-BN materials possessing tailored mechanical properties.

Conclusions

This research utilized DFT calculations to systematically examine the impact of S-doping on the electronic, thermodynamic, mechanical, and adsorption behavior of the h-BN surface. While the formation energy of the S-doped h-BN surfaces is more positive than the pristine surface, it is not much different from it. The impact of S-doping on the surface’s electronic properties is highly sensitive to the sulfur atom’s substitutional site. Replacing a single N atom with S reduces the band gap energy from 4.66 to 0.44 eV, whereas substitution for adjacent B and N atoms results in a band gap of 1.82 eV. The analysis of PDOS diagrams revealed that on S-doped surfaces, mainly the p orbitals of the surface atoms overlap with the p orbitals of the sulfur atom.

Across the entire temperature range, the vibrational entropy and enthalpy of pristine h-BN surfaces exceed those of S-doped h-BN surfaces. Regarding Gibbs free energy, the pristine surface exhibits higher values at low temperatures, yet demonstrates the lowest free energy at low temperatures. The bulk modulus of the h-BN surface decreases upon S-doping, most notably by 20.6 GPa for the SN surface. Furthermore, the in-plane hardness is reduced by 19% when an S atom replaces a B atom and by 28% when it replaces an N atom. The Poisson’s ratio for S-doped h-BN surfaces falls within the range of 0.169 to 0.237.

Adsorption of Mo, Ge, Zn, Ga, Cd, and Se on h-BN, SN, SB, and SBN surfaces shows significantly higher binding energy on S-doped surfaces compared to pristine h-BN, with one exception. Mo and Ge atoms exhibited the strongest interaction with the surfaces. PDOS diagrams of metal-decorated surfaces show electron loss from the Mo atom’s s orbital. The elongation of the H₂ bond length to 1.59 Å and its distance from the surface (1.33 Å) imply the chemisorption of H2 on the SN surface. Conversely, all adsorption parameters point to weak physisorption of hydrogen on the h-BN, SB, and SBN surfaces. The results indicate that there is a strong interaction between the H2 molecule and h-BN-Mo, SB-Mo, and SN-MO surfaces, which causes the hydrogen to dissociate upon adsorption.

All findings demonstrate that sulfur doping of the h-BN surface enhances its electronic characteristics without appreciably changing its mechanical ones. Additionally, this enhances the surface’s ability to adsorb hydrogen molecules and metals. Future research could focus on elucidating the influence of sulfur doping concentration on their fundamental properties, a comprehensive investigation into their capacity for hydrogen storage, their potential as electrode materials in advanced metal-ion batteries, and exploring their catalytic properties in various reactions. Their efficacy in environmental remediation, particularly for pollutant gas adsorption and sensing, offers another promising direction.
