Understanding crop performance and pest control under climate change requires considering interactions among warming, elevated CO2, and trophic interactions
Authors: Syuan-Jyun Sun, Xin-Yi Lee, Ying-Jie Wang, Wen-Po Chuang, Shaw-Yhi Hwang, Chuan-Kai Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestigating the combined effects of climate change and species interactions on species advances climate change research. However, such combined effects are understudied and may defy current hypotheses that focus on individual effects. We empirically examined how multiple environmental stressors (elevated temperature and CO2) and trophic interactions (herbivory and predation) interactively affect crop performance and pest control in a tri-trophic agroecosystem including soybean (Glycine max), aphid (Aphis glycines), and ladybeetle (Coccinella septempunctata). Temperature (control/+2 °C/+4 °C), CO2 (ambient/elevated), and trophic treatments (soybean/soybean-aphid/soybean-aphid-ladybeetle) were conducted in environmental chambers. Plant, herbivore, and predator traits (soybean nutrient content, defense, reproduction, and aphid and ladybeetle performance) were greatly influenced by interactions between abiotic (temperature, CO2) and biotic (trophic) factors, often contradicting current hypotheses. For example, aphids reduced seed number; ladybeetles generally suppressed aphids and rescued seed production. However, this biocontrol was compromised under elevated temperature and CO2, suggesting climate-induced changes in biocontrol effectiveness. We conclude: (1) To improve predictability, climate change research should include abiotic-biotic interactions as important mechanisms. (2) Empirical studies considering local climate-trophic interactions are critical for predicting regional food security. (3) While this study highlights species’ plastic responses to climate change, experimental evolutionary studies will be needed to understand potential evolutionary adaptation.Keywords: Abiotic-biotic interactions, Climate change, Biocontrol, Elevated CO2, Agroecosystem, Plant-animal interactions, Tri-trophic interactions, WarmingSubject terms: Climate-change ecology, Agroecology

Introduction

As one of the biggest global challenges in the Anthropocene, climate change has reportedly impacted individual performance, population abundance, species distribution, ecological communities, and ecosystem functioning1–6. To better understand and predict the impact of climate change on species, diverse studies have investigated individual climate components (e.g., warming). However, such studies provide limited insights because species will likely face the combined effects of multiple climate change components and species interactions. For example, elevated air temperature and atmospheric CO2 concentration are key components of current climate change6. Globally, mean surface temperature is predicted to increase by 1.8 to 4.5 °C (RCP4.5 and RCP8.5), and atmospheric CO2 concentration (recorded at ca. 414 ppm in 2020 (NOAA)) may reach 1000 ppm (RCP8.5) by the end of this century6.

Such drastic projections have prompted researchers to examine the individual effects of temperature and CO2 on various natural and agricultural systems. For example, elevated temperature can reduce crop production7, increase or decrease photosynthesis rate8,9, reduce animal body size4,10, advance spring phenology11, alter species interactions12,13, increase pest damage to crops14, affect ecosystem functions15, and drive poleward or upslope range shifts16,17. Elevated CO2 can change plant quality (e.g., chemical composition)18–20, increase plant water-use efficiency21, increase or decrease photosynthesis rate22,23, affect plant-herbivore interactions12, and increase crop production24,25. While these findings laid the groundwork for climate change ecology, it remains unclear whether such single-factor results apply to real-world scenarios, where the interactive effects of climate change components may determine species performance26–28. For example, the carbon-nutrient balance hypothesis29,30 predicts that elevated CO2 increases carbon-based plant defenses (e.g., phenolics). But does this hold under concurrent temperature and CO2 elevation? Given that multiple stressors (e.g., climate change components) may interact in non-additive ways, studies are needed to verify whether current single-factor hypotheses (e.g., the carbon-nutrient balance hypothesis) accurately predict outcomes under realistic conditions.

Besides abiotic interactions, climate change may also affect organisms by altering species interactions (i.e., abiotic-biotic interactions). Species interactions—such as bottom-up forces (e.g., plant effects on herbivores) and top-down forces (e.g., predator effects on herbivores)—shape individual performance, population dynamics, community structures, and ecosystem functions31–35. Investigating how climate change modifies these interactions is critical, as the indirect effects of climate change (i.e., through altered species interactions) may outweigh its direct effects on organisms36. Studies of plant-herbivore interactions have begun to reveal the important interaction between bottom-up forces and climate change components37–39. For example, elevated CO2 can reduce herbivore growth rate, pupal weight, and abundance by lowering host plant quality, such as increasing the plant C/N ratio and chemical defense (e.g., phenolics)12,38,40,41. Although the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated, elevated CO2 may lead to carbohydrate accumulation in plant tissues, thereby diluting foliar nitrogen (i.e., increasing the C/N ratio) and affecting defense allocation (e.g., foliar phenolics)26,42,43.

While bottom-up forces affect herbivores via plant-mediated effects, top-down forces regulate herbivores via natural enemies (e.g., predators and parasitoids), forming the foundation of biocontrol in environmentally friendly agriculture44,45. A timely and important question is whether natural enemies can continue to suppress herbivores (pests) and maintain crop production under climate change. Although more studies are needed, some studies suggest that climate change may alter biocontrol effectiveness34,46. Potential underlying mechanisms include warming-induced effects on organisms’ metabolic rates, which can scale up to affect population dynamics and predator-prey interactions47,48. Elevated temperature may also interrupt predator-prey interactions by altering organisms’ sensory systems28.

Despite its importance, research on climate change and species interactions (bottom-up and top-down forces) has primarily focused on relatively simple pairwise interactions (e.g., plant-herbivore interactions), leaving more complex interactions (e.g., plant-herbivore-predator interactions) understudied (but see34). Investigating more complex interactions (e.g., tri-trophic interactions) is crucial for two reasons: (1) Most natural or agricultural systems comprise at least three trophic levels and are regulated by both bottom-up and top-down forces49. Therefore, studying three trophic levels will provide deeper insights into community dynamics under climate change. (2) Investigating tri-trophic interactions helps reveal whether predators (biocontrol agents) continue to suppress herbivores (pests) and benefit plant (crop) production under climate change (i.e., trophic cascades), thereby informing sustainable agriculture.

In this study, we examined how climate change components (elevated temperature and CO2) and trophic interactions (herbivory and predation) interactively affect crop performance and pest control in a tri-trophic agroecosystem consisting of soybean (Glycine max, plant), soybean aphid (Aphis glycines, herbivore), and seven-spotted ladybeetle (Coccinella septempunctata, predator) (Fig. 1). We selected these species because soybean is among the most important crops worldwide50, soybean aphid is a major pest of soybean51, and the seven-spotted ladybeetle is a key natural enemy of aphids and has been commercially used as a biocontrol agent52,53. We experimentally manipulated climate change components (abiotic factors) and trophic interactions (biotic factors) in environmental chambers, including temperature treatment (control/+2 °C/+4 °C), CO2 treatment (ambient/elevated), and trophic treatment (soybean/soybean-aphid/soybean-aphid-ladybeetle) (Fig. 1a). To simulate field conditions, the temperature treatment included within-day variation based on 30-year hourly mean temperature in the field (see Methods for further details). Answering these two questions helps reveal more realistic effects of climate change on species and test current hypotheses related to plant-animal interactions. For example, regarding plant performance, the carbon-nutrient balance hypothesis29,30 would predict an increase in carbon-based defense compounds (e.g., phenolics) under elevated CO2. We tested this under more realistic climate change conditions involving both elevated temperature and CO2. Additionally, existing studies predict a 10–25% increase in plant (crop) loss due to herbivores (pests) per degree Celsius of warming in two trophic level systems (plants and herbivores)14. We examined whether this linear prediction is valid in tri-trophic systems that include predators. Regarding herbivore performance, studies suggest that warming may either increase or decrease herbivore population size14,54,55. We investigated whether these warming effects are mediated by elevated CO2 and predator presence. Regarding predator performance, warming may lead to predator starvation due to imbalances between metabolic and ingestion rates56,57. We investigated whether this hypothesis holds under both elevated temperature and CO2. Verifying these hypotheses would enhance our understanding of climate change impact on both natural and agricultural systems.

Results

Plant growth

We found that temperature treatment alone affected plant biomass (χ² = 40.05, df = 2, p < 0.001), such that warming by + 2 °C and + 4 °C both reduced soybean plant biomass compared to the control temperature (z = 5.28, adj. p < 0.001 and z = 5.66, adj. p < 0.001, respectively; Fig. 2a). CO2 treatment (χ² = 0.98, df = 1, p = 0.321) or trophic treatment (χ² = 4.42, df = 2, p = 0.110) alone did not affect plant biomass. However, the nutrient content of soybean plants (C/N ratio) changed under elevated temperature, depending on the trophic treatment (Temperature × Trophic: χ² = 14.97, df = 4, p = 0.005). Specifically, the plant C/N ratio remained generally unchanged under warming in the soybean or soybean-aphid treatment group. However, adding ladybeetles led to a reduction in plant C/N ratio (i.e., higher plant quality for herbivores) (z = 3.21, adj. p = 0.004; Fig. 2b), and this reduction was more evident under + 4 °C warming (z = 8.50, adj. p < 0.001; Fig. 2b). We found no effect of CO2 treatment on the C/N ratio (χ² = 1.67, df = 1, p = 0.196). The statistical results are summarized in Table 1 and S1.

Plant defense

Elevated temperature and CO2 interactively affected plant defense (foliar phenolic content), and their effects were mediated by trophic interactions (Temperature × CO2 × Trophic: χ² = 11.67, df = 4, p = 0.020; Fig. 2c). Specifically, adding aphids to soybean plants triggered an increase in foliar phenolic content under ambient CO2 at all temperatures (control: z = −2.49, adj. p = 0.034; +2 °C: z = −2.65, adj. p = 0.022; +4 °C: z = −3.82, adj. p < 0.001). Adding aphids alongside ladybeetles under ambient CO2 generated similar results (control: z = −4.67, adj. p < 0.001; +2 °C: z = −3.85, adj. p < 0.001; +4 °C: z = −2.86, adj. p = 0.012). These results under ambient CO2 suggest elevated phenolic content as an induced plant defense against herbivores. However, the response of phenolic content to aphid herbivory changed under elevated CO2 — adding aphids increased phenolic content only under + 2 °C warming (z = −3.50, adj. p = 0.001), but not under control temperature (z = 1.59, adj. p = 0.248) or + 4 °C warming (z = 0.88, adj. p = 0.651). The statistical results are summarized in Table 1 and S1.

Plant reproductive success

Elevated temperature and CO2 interactively affected the number of seeds per soybean plant produced, but the interactive effect also depended upon trophic interactions (Temperature × CO2 × Trophic: p < 0.001; Fig. 3a; Table 1 and S1). For the soybean treatment group under ambient CO2 level, warming by + 2 °C reduced seed number (z = 2.93, adj. p = 0.040); however, under elevated CO2, warming by + 2 °C (z = −0.49, adj. p = 0.997) or + 4 °C (z = 0.30, adj. p = 0.9997) had no effect on seed number (Fig. 3a). Adding aphids to soybean plants altered the response of seed number to environmental changes. For the soybean-aphid treatment group under ambient CO2, warming by + 2 °C improved the number of seeds produced (z = −3.10, adj. p = 0.024), whereas warming by + 4 °C had no such effect (z = 1.85, adj. p = 0.432); under elevated CO2, warming by + 2 °C (z = 2.59, adj. p = 0.099) or + 4 °C (z =−0.37, adj. p = 0.999) did not affect seed number.

The values represent the mean ± SE.

The seed number results also showed that ladybeetles limited aphid damage and produced positive trophic cascades on soybean plants. Specifically, adding aphids to soybean plants reduced seed number under all combinations of temperature and CO2 (i.e., soybean-aphid vs. soybean treatment groups; adj. p < 0.001 for all comparisons across all temperature-CO2 combinations; Fig. 3a). Interestingly, adding ladybeetles alongside aphids helped restore soybean seed production under almost all combinations of temperature and CO2 (i.e., soybean-aphid-ladybeetle vs. soybean treatment groups; Fig. 3a), except for an even higher seed production compared to the soybean treatment group at + 4 °C under ambient CO2 (z = −4.61, adj. p < 0.001) and a slightly lower seed production compared to the soybean treatment group at + 4 °C under elevated CO2 (z = 2.45, adj. p = 0.039).

While seed number (quantity) was affected by the interaction among temperature, CO2, and trophic treatments, the average seed mass (quality) was only affected by trophic treatment (χ² = 37.13, df = 2, p < 0.001; Fig. 3b; Table 1 and S1). Specifically, soybeans infested with aphids produced lighter seeds on average regardless of ladybeetle absence or presence (soybean-aphid vs. soybean treatment groups: z = 4.11, adj. p < 0.001; soybean-aphid-ladybeetle vs. soybean treatment groups: z = 5.88, adj. p < 0.001).

Temporal dynamics of aphid populations

We found that ladybeetle presence influenced the temporal dynamics of aphid populations, and that this influence was mediated by the interaction between temperature and CO2 treatments (Temperature × CO2 × Trophic × Time: p = 0.027; Fig. 4; Table 1 and S2). At ambient CO2, ladybeetles suppressed aphid populations, and the suppression appeared earlier and strengthened under warming conditions. Specifically, ladybeetles started suppressing aphid populations in week 6 under warming by 2 °C and 4 °C (+ 2 °C: t = 2.01, p = 0.046; +4 °C: t = 3.33, p = 0.001; Fig. 4a), which was one week earlier than that at the control temperature (t = 3.10, p = 0.002; Fig. 4a). Consequently, ladybeetles reduced aphid population size more under 4 °C warming than under control temperatures when soybeans were harvested in week 10 (soybean-aphid-ladybeetle group at + 4 °C vs. control temperature; t = 2.90, p = 0.048; Fig. 4a). At elevated CO2, ladybeetles suppressed aphid populations mainly at the control temperature (starting from week 7; t = 3.81, p < 0.001) but not under warming by 2 °C or 4 °C (Fig. 4b).

Effects of temperature treatment (control, + 2 °C, + 4 °C) and trophic treatment (ladybeetle absence vs. presence) on temporal dynamics of aphid population size under (a) ambient CO2 and (b) elevated CO2. The values represent the mean ± SE.

Ladybeetle fitness

In addition to the effects of temperature and CO2 treatments on herbivores (aphids), this study also investigated the aforementioned effects on predators (ladybeetles). We compared the percentage changes in the body mass of ladybeetles after being introduced to aphid populations under different temperature and CO2 treatments (see Experimental design). The results showed that temperature and CO2 interactively affected ladybeetle body mass (Temperature x CO2: χ² = 9.42, df = 2, p = 0.009; Table 1 and S3). At ambient CO2, warming by 4 °C marginally reduced ladybeetle body mass compared to the control temperature (z = 2.18, p = 0.075; Fig. 5). In contrast, at elevated CO2, warming by 4 °C marginally increased ladybeetle body mass compared to the control temperature (z = −2.16, p = 0.079; Fig. 5). The body mass change (weight gain) at + 4 °C under elevated CO2 was different from that (weight loss) at + 4 °C under ambient CO2 (z = −3.07, p = 0.002; Fig. 5).

Discussion

Predicting the impact of climate change on communities and ecosystems requires an understanding of how climate change (abiotic factors) and species interactions (biotic factors) jointly affect species performance. To fill this knowledge gap, this study examined how multiple environmental stressors (elevated temperature and CO2) and trophic interactions (herbivory and predation) interactively affect species performance in a tri-trophic agroecosystem, including soybean (plant [crop]), soybean aphids (herbivore [pest]), and seven-spotted ladybeetles (predator [biocontrol agent]). The results showed that most plant, herbivore, and predator traits were influenced by abiotic-biotic interactions (Temperature × Trophic or Temperature × CO2 × Trophic) (Table 1). Specifically, these interactions affected soybean plant nutrient status (C/N ratio), plant defense (phenolics), soybean reproduction (seed number), and aphid population size. For example, warming strengthened aphid biocontrol by ladybeetles under ambient but not elevated CO2 conditions (Fig. 4a vs. b). Taken together, our results indicate the following: (1) The interplay between multiple environmental stressors and trophic interactions (abiotic-biotic interactions) is a key mechanism shaping species performance under climate change. Consequently, our results (based on a more realistic climate change scenario) challenge existing hypotheses that omit such interactions, including the carbon-nutrient balance hypothesis, the concept of linear crop production loss to pests under climate warming, the concept of herbivore population dynamics under climate warming, and the concept of predator starvation under climate warming (details below). We encourage climate change assessment models to include abiotic-biotic interactions to improve their predictability. (2) Crop nutrient status (e.g., C/N ratio), defense (e.g., phenolics), production (e.g., seed number) and protection (e.g., biocontrol) are likely affected by these interactions. Therefore, crop-specific studies using local pest and natural enemy species should be conducted to accurately predict the impact of climate change on regional agricultural systems. Our findings and implications are further discussed below.

Effects of climate change and trophic interactions on plant (crop) performance.

Effects of climate change and trophic interactions on plant (crop) performance.

While many empirical and theoretical studies have examined the individual effects of abiotic and biotic factors on wild plants or crops (see references in Introduction), they generally overlooked the role of abiotic-biotic interactions (e.g., the interactive effect of elevated temperature, CO2 and trophic interactions on seed production). However, our study shows that these interactions play a critical role in determining plant nutrients (C/N ratio), defense (phenolics), and reproduction (seed number). This suggests that climate change experiments or models that overlook abiotic-biotic interactions may generate misleading results. To better understand the impact of climate change on plant (crop) growth, defense, and reproduction, we encourage future research to examine multiple environmental stressors and trophic interactions in various natural and agricultural systems.

Plant defense (phenolics) provides a clear example of how abiotic-biotic interactions critically shape plant performance. Studies focusing solely on abiotic factors predict that elevated CO2 will increase plant carbohydrate concentration and carbon-based plant defense compounds (e.g., phenolics), consistent with the classic carbon-nutrient balance hypothesis29,30. Likewise, studies focusing on biotic factors predict that herbivory will induce higher concentration of plant defense compounds, aligning with the induced defense concepts63,64. However, our study, which simulates a more realistic scenario where climate change and herbivory co-occur, demonstrates that abiotic and biotic factors interact in ways that cannot be predicted by abiotic- or biotic-factor studies in isolation. Specifically, we found that herbivory (aphid presence) increased phenolics across temperature gradients at ambient CO2, but this effect occurred only under + 2 °C warming at elevated CO2 (Fig. 2C) — a clear example of abiotic-biotic interactions (Temperature × CO2 × Trophic). The mechanism underlying this interaction cannot be simply explained by the carbon-nutrient balance hypothesis because of the lack of correlation between the C/N ratio (Fig. 2b) and phenolic concentration (Fig. 2c). Nor can it be explained by the induced defense hypothesis, because of the lack of induced defense at elevated CO2 (except for the + 2 °C warming treatment group). To improve the predictability of climate change impact assessment, new research hypotheses are needed to clarify how abiotic and biotic factors interactively affect plant performance. An integrated approach combing plant physiology and ecology, such as examining related molecular pathways, may facilitate this process.

Our empirical study also reveals that crop production under more realistic environmental change scenarios (e.g., co-occurrence of climate change and herbivory) may not align with current hypotheses, posing challenges for food security assessments. For example, crop yield losses to insect pests are predicted to increase by 10–25% per degree Celsius of climate warming14. However, our empirical results did not support this linear prediction (e.g., the soybean-aphid treatment group [yellow dots] in Fig. 3a), but showed that pest effect on crop yield under warming will depend on (a) trophic interactions (e.g., predator presence or absence), and (b) CO2 concentration. Specifically, at ambient CO2, predators (ladybeetles) suppressed pest (aphid) populations at both control and warming temperatures (Fig. 4a), thereby restoring crop production (seed number) from pest damage across temperature gradients (Fig. 3a). However, at elevated CO2, predators suppressed pests only at the control temperature, not under warming (see mechanisms discussed in subsection 2 below) (Fig. 4b), resulting in crop production recovery only at the control temperature, not under warming (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, our results show that different reproductive traits of the same crop (seed number vs. average seed mass) may respond differently to climate change. For example, soybean seed number was affected by the interaction of temperature, CO2, and trophic treatments (Fig. 3a), but average seed mass was affected only by trophic treatment (Fig. 3b). This implies that seed number may be more sensitive to climate change than seed mass — an observation that deserves further investigation for improving predictions of crop production under climate change. Taken together, our results suggest that trophic interactions, temperature-CO2 interactions, and trait-specific responses should be considered to refine climate change impact models and improve their accuracy in predicting crop performance and food security.

(2)Effects of climate change and trophic interactions on aphid (pest) performance.

Effects of climate change and trophic interactions on aphid (pest) performance.

Herbivores critically shape the primary productivity of natural and agricultural systems, which is why many climate change studies have examined herbivore responses to elevated temperature or CO2 14,30,54,65. In general, herbivore population responses to warming can be either positive14,54 or negative55, depending on factors such as voltinism, plasticity, adaptation, and species interactions65. Herbivore population responses to elevated CO2 can be neutral30 or negative41. Although these studies have provided important insights into herbivore dynamics under climate change, most lacked experimental designs capable of testing the combined effect of multiple environmental stressors (abiotic factors) and trophic interactions (biotic factors) — a more realistic scenario for this century (but see55).

The present study helps advance the study field by demonstrating the importance of abiotic-biotic interactions for herbivore (pest) populations (i.e., Temperature × CO2 × Trophic). Specifically, at ambient CO2, aphid populations were suppressed by ladybeetles under both control and warming temperatures (Fig. 4a). However, at elevated CO2, this suppression faded under warming (Fig. 4b). The striking patterns and underlying mechanisms could not be explained by previous studies. For example, while warming can increase predation efficiency and shift predator-prey interactions from Type III to II functional response48, this only explains the case at ambient CO2 (Fig. 4a) but not at elevated CO2 (warming reduced the predator effect; Fig. 4b). Warming may also increase or decrease the carrying capacity of organisms47,48; however, this temperature-dependent hypothesis does not predict the temperature-CO2-dependent results in our aphid populations. Furthermore, elevated CO2 has been suggested to reduce aphid escape behavior by impairing conspecific alarm signals66,67; however, this mechanism only explains the case at control but not warming temperature (red lines in Fig. 4a vs. 4b). To explain the aphid results, we propose a working hypothesis: elevated temperature and CO2 together increase plant trichome density, which provides aphids with more refuges from ladybeetle predation, thereby weakening top-down control of aphids by ladybeetles (Fig. 4b). This hypothesis is based on our observations of aphids hiding among trichomes and the fact that trichome density is environmentally plastic68,69.

As biocontrol programs become increasingly central to sustainable agriculture, a critical question is whether they will remain effective under climate change. Our aphid experiments demonstrate that climate change can alter the outcome of current biocontrol programs: warming enhanced biocontrol at ambient CO2 (Fig. 4a) but reduced it at elevated CO2 (Fig. 4b). To predict how various biocontrol programs will respond to climate change, we suggest conducting regional studies because crops, pests and natural enemies may exhibit species-specific, local-specific (population-specific), or seasonal-specific responses determined by their thermal performance curves. For example, in our subtropical system, warming at ambient CO2 had no positive effect on aphid population size (yellow lines in Fig. 4a), different from the positive effect commonly found in temperate systems70,71. The lack of positive effect in our system is likely because both control and warming temperatures fall within the plateau of the soybean aphid’s thermal performance curve (i.e., the optimum range)34. Meanwhile, warming is expected to elevate predator metabolic rates56,72 and increase aphid consumption by ladybeetles (i.e., enhanced biocontrol) (red lines in Fig. 4a). However, this temperature-dependent biocontrol effect was altered at elevated CO2 (Fig. 4b), possibly because increased aphid consumption under warming was outweighed by a stronger refuge effect due to trichome plasticity at elevated CO2, as proposed above.

(3)Effects of climate change and trophic interactions on ladybeetle (biocontrol agent) performance.

Effects of climate change and trophic interactions on ladybeetle (biocontrol agent) performance.

Predator-prey studies have warned that predators (e.g., biocontrol agents) may face starvation under climate warming because their metabolic rates can increase more rapidly than their ingestion rates56,57. This warming-induced starvation could negatively affect biocontrol programs and potentially threaten crop production. In this study, the concept of warming-induced starvation in predators is supported at ambient CO2, where a 4 °C warming marginally reduced ladybeetle body mass (Fig. 5), but not at elevated CO2, where a 4 °C warming marginally increased ladybeetle body mass (Fig. 5). The results suggest that the existing concept, which focuses primarily on metabolic demands, may be insufficient to predict predator performance under future climate scenarios. Therefore, we suggest that a new hypothesis is needed — one that considers not only warming effects on predator metabolism but also on prey abundance under predation pressure. In support of this idea, our results show that changes in ladybeetle body mass (a proxy for predator performance) closely mirrored changes in aphid population size (prey abundance) (red lines in Fig. 4 vs. Figure 5). We speculate that at ambient CO2, a 4 °C warming increased ladybeetle metabolic demands, but low aphid densities prevented energy intake from meeting this demand (red lines in Fig. 4a), resulting in weight loss (Fig. 5). In contrast, at elevated CO2, warming coincided with high aphid densities (red lines in Fig. 4b), allowing ladybeetles to meet or even exceed their energy demands, thereby gaining weight (Fig. 5). Another non-mutually exclusive possibility is that aphids may become more nutritious under elevated CO2 and 4 °C warming, further enhancing ladybeetle performance. However, studies on prey quality under climate change remain limited.

Conclusions

Predicting the combined impact of multiple environmental stressors (e.g., climate change) and trophic interactions on species performance is central to ecology in the Anthropocene. While many studies have investigated species performance under climate change, they often focus on individual stressors (e.g., elevated temperature or CO2) or simple pairwise interactions (e.g., plant-herbivore). These approaches may fail to capture the complex reality, where multiple abiotic and biotic factors interact simultaneously. Here, we demonstrate that plant (crop), herbivore (pest), and predator (biocontrol agent) traits are strongly influenced by the interactions between abiotic factors (elevated temperature and CO2) and biotic factors (trophic interactions) (with potential caveats presented in Supporting Information Note S2). In other words, species responses to both elevated temperature and CO2 differ markedly from their responses to each stress alone, and these responses are further mediated by trophic interactions. Furthermore, our findings suggest that current hypotheses, which overlook these abiotic-biotic interactions, may have limited predictive power. We draw three key conclusions: (1) Climate change impact assessment and integrated pest management (IPM) strategies should account for abiotic-biotic interactions to improve predictability. (2) Climate change and trophic interactions (herbivory and predation) interactively affect crop nutrients, defense, production, and protection (biocontrol) in this study. This suggests that crop-specific studies with local pest and natural enemy species are required to accurately predict climate change impact on regional agricultural systems and biocontrol programs. (3) This study examines species’ plastic responses to climate change. Long-term predictions will require experimental evolutionary studies to understand how species adapt over generations.

Methods

Study system

To understand the interactive effects of elevated temperature and CO2 on a tri-trophic system (plant-herbivore-predator), we evaluated the growth, defense, and reproduction of the soybean Glycine max (L.), population dynamics of the soybean aphid Aphis glycines Matsumura, and growth of the seven-spotted ladybeetle Coccinella septempunctata Linnaeus at National Taiwan University (Taipei, Taiwan). Soybean is one of the most important crops worldwide50. The soybean aphid A. glycines (native to Asia) is a major pest of soybean and has become one of the most economically important pests of soybeans in North America since its invasion in 2000 51,58. The seven-spotted ladybeetle C. septempunctata is commercially used as a biocontrol agent in North America, and is one of the most common arthropod predators in farmland in Taiwan (personal observations 2012–2014). In addition to applying chemical pesticides or developing aphid-resistant soybean cultivars, the use of natural enemies (e.g., ladybeetles) to control aphid populations has become increasingly effective58,59. In this study, we focused on a commercial soybean cultivar (G. max cv. Tainan No. 3) and populations of A. glycines and C. septempunctata that were originally collected from multiple field sites in Taiwan (see34 for further details).

Laboratory colonies of aphids and Ladybeetles

Soybean aphid colonies were originally collected from three sites in Taiwan (Songshan, National Taiwan University Experimental Farm, and Tainan) to reduce idiosyncrasy. They were then raised together on soybean plants in environmental chambers that reflected field conditions by simulating within-day variation in field temperature (17.1–22.5 °C, with a mean of 19.5 °C; Fig. S1). This temperature setting (hereafter control temperature) was based on the 30-year hourly mean temperature when soybeans were typically grown between January and April in Taiwan (1981–2010 data from the Taiwan Central Weather Bureau (2014)). The environmental chambers were set at constant humidity (70% RH) and photoperiod (12 L:12D). We grew soybeans from seeds, each placed in a plastic pot (16.7 [diameter] × 16.6 [height] cm) filled with soil (Kekkilä, Finland), and supplied with a mixture of tap water and fertilizer (HYPONeX No.2, Hyponex Japan Co. Ltd.). We used this potting soil because it has a low and stable nutrient content. This minimized background variation and ensured that each potted plant received a consistent amount of nutrients from our fertilization treatment. Water (100 mL) and fertilizer were applied once every two days and eight days, respectively. We maintained aphid colonies once a month by introducing two adult aphids and eight nymphs of the smallest size to soybean plants at three–four weeks old (vegetative stage of V1 and V2). The seven-spotted ladybeetles in this study were originally collected from multiple sites in Taiwan. To maintain the ladybeetle colonies, we introduced a pair of ladybeetles to half of the aforementioned soybean plants a week after aphid introduction. All pots were enclosed by polyester mesh fabric to prevent aphids and ladybeetles from escaping.

Experimental design

We applied a 3 × 2 × 3 factorial design including temperature treatment (control, + 2 °C, + 4 °C), CO2 treatment (ambient [500ppm], elevated [1000ppm]), and trophic treatment (plants only [plant], plants and aphids [plant-herbivore], plants, aphids, and ladybeetles [plant-herbivore-predator]) (Fig. 1a). There were eight replicates for each combination of temperature, CO2, and trophic treatments (18 combinations), generating 144 replicates in total. Six environmental chambers were used to create a combination of temperature and CO2 treatments at a constant humidity (70% RH) and photoperiod (12 L:12D). The control temperature treatment group simulated within-day variation in field temperature (17.1–22.5 °C; see details in laboratory colonies of aphids and ladybeetles). The elevated temperature treatment groups (+ 2 °C and + 4 °C) were based on the predicted warming by the year 2100 (approximately up to 4 °C)6. The + 2 °C and + 4 °C treatment groups had the temperature range of 19.1–24.5 °C and 21.1–26.5 °C, respectively (Fig. S1). To provide accurate temperature treatment, each environmental chamber was additionally equipped with a thermometer and temperature data logger, allowing us to monitor chamber conditions on a daily basis. The ambient and elevated CO2 treatment groups were set up at 500 and 1000 ppm CO2, respectively. The CO2 concentrations were maintained through an automatic CO2 system (YUH CHUEN CHIOU Industry L.T.D.), supplied by the external compressed air of CO2 (bottle concentration > 99.9%). The CO2 concentration in the ambient group was based on measurements in Taipei and Yilan City, Taiwan in 2016 (Ho, unpublished data); the elevated group was based on the prediction for the year 2100 (985 ± 97 ppm)6.

Soybean plants were grown from seeds following the approach described above (see laboratory colonies of aphids and ladybeetles) and were randomly assigned to each treatment group. After reaching the 3-week-old stage, the plants were covered with individual cages made of polyester mesh fabric. For the treatment groups with aphid presence, we introduced two adults and eight nymphs (1–2 instar) to plants aged 3–4 weeks (vegetative stage V1 to V2). For the treatment groups with ladybeetle presence, a ladybeetle (weighed to the nearest 0.001 g using a microbalance [Model AB104-S, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland]) was introduced to each plant one week after the initial 10 aphids were added. The densities of aphids and ladybeetles were within the range of our field survey (2012–2014). We checked the population size of aphids on soybean each week by visually counting all aphid individuals using tally counters. As the plants reached vegetative stage R5 (before harvest at R6), we determined the C/N ratio for plant nutrient status and total phenolics for plant defense status (see Chemical analysis below). Each experimental replicate was terminated when the soybean reached R6 (full seed). At this point, we weighed the final weight of each ladybeetle, if applicable, and harvested each soybean plant to determine its total biomass by measuring its dry weight after oven drying at 70 °C until constant weight. We also determined reproductive success by counting the number of seeds (seed quantity) and measuring seed mass (seed quality) in the same drying process.

Chemical analysis

To measure the C/N ratio, collected leaves were rinsed with tap water to remove any animal materials, dried with soft paper towels, oven-dried at 40 °C for a week, and ground and weighed. We prepared the powdered samples (2.50 ± 0.03 mg) by packing them in tin capsules (Sn) and then in 96-well trays, each of which had an index card between the capsules and tray lid to secure the capsules. All samples were shipped to the Stable Isotope Facility (SIF), Department of Plant Sciences, UC Davis, for analysis.

We determined the total phenolic content in soybean leaves using the Folin-Ciocalteu (FC) assay60. In brief, all petioles and mid ribs were removed from leaves, which were freeze-dried (lyophilized at −55 °C for 24 h) and then ground to powder. To extract phenolics, 2 mg of dried leaf powder was mixed with 1 ml of extraction solution (adding 1 ml HCl to 99 ml mixture of 80 ml methanol and 20 ml ddH2O) and placed the samples in an ultrasonic cleaner for 30 min. The supernatant was then obtained by centrifuging the samples for 10 min at 12,000 rpm. This extraction process was repeated, and the samples were stored at 4 °C before the FC assay. Total phenolic content was analyzed using gallic acid as a standard by adding 750 µL FC reagent to 100 µL sample/standard for 5 min at 22 °C. The reaction was terminated by adding 750 µL of sodium carbonate solution. After 1.5 h, we measured the absorbance of the 700 µL supernatant at 725 nm to determine the calibrated total phenolic content.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 3.4.3 (R Development Core Team, 2014), with generalized linear models (GLM) and generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) in the package lme4 61. Tukey’s post-hoc comparisons were performed using the lsmeans package62.

For plant performance, we used GLM to test whether temperature (control, + 2 °C, + 4 °C) and CO2 (500 and 1000 ppm) treatments interact, and whether their interactions depend on trophic treatment. Total plant biomass, C/N ratio, foliar phenolics, and average seed mass were analyzed with a Gaussian error distribution, whereas seed number was analyzed with a Poisson error distribution. For aphid performance, we analyzed temporal variations in aphid abundance using GLMM with a Gaussian error distribution. The ID of each potted soybean plant was included as a random effect, since each plant was repeatedly sampled for aphid abundance per week. For ladybeetle performance, we analyzed the percentage changes in body mass (i.e., [final body weight – initial body weight]/initial body weight]), using GLM with Gaussian error distribution. Non-normally distributed variables were log-transformed prior to the analyses to meet the model assumptions.

In all analyses, our models initially included the effects of temperature, CO2, trophic interactions, and their interaction terms as fixed effects, and were then simplified by removing non-significant interaction terms. If a significant effect was detected, Tukey’s post-hoc test was performed to examine the differences between treatment groups.
