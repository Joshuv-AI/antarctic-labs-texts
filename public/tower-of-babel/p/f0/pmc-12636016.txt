Enantioselective Synthesis of (+)-Auriculatol A
Authors: Jordan K. Thompson, Kala C. Youngblood, Yun Hao Shawn Teh, Conner M. Farley, Zhehao Zhang, Scott C. Virgil, Sarah E. Reisman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A total synthesis of the grayanane diterpenoid (+)-auriculatol A is reported. The synthesis features a convergent coupling strategy that joins two fragments by a vinylogous Mukaiyama–aldol-type reaction and then constructs the 7-membered ring by a Ni-catalyzed enolate alkenylation. Key findings include the development of a chemoselective Ni-catalyzed intramolecular 1,2-addition to access the bicyclo[3.2.1]­octane fragment and the use of electron-deficient olefin supporting ligands as uniquely effective for the Ni-catalyzed enolate alkenylation.

The grayananes comprise more than 400 oxidized diterpenes isolated from the Ericaceae family of plants. These diterpenoids are best known for their activity as modulators of voltage-gated sodium ion channels , and are the molecular constituents associated with poisoning from honey produced during the pollination of rhododendrons. Although toxic when ingested in large amounts, in lower doses, grayananes can induce antinociception in mice. In addition, grayananes and related compounds have been reported to inhibit PTP1B and carbonic anhydrase.

Auriculatol A (1) is a pentacyclic grayanane that was first isolated in 2019 by Yao and co-workers from the leaves of Rhododendron auriculatum and shown to possess antinociceptive properties (Figure a). Structurally, auriculatol A has a 5/7/6/5 carbon framework common to the larger grayanane family of natural products but is differentiated by the presence of a bridging E-ring acetal. In addition, 1 bears oxidation at the bridgehead C13 position, a less prevalent motif within the grayananes. Several elegant syntheses of related grayanane and rearranged ent-kaurane natural products have been reported; however, there have been no prior syntheses of 1 or grayananes containing an E-ring acetal. Here we report a total synthesis of auriculatol A that features an intramolecular Ni-catalyzed enolate alkenylation to form the central 7-membered ring. A key discovery was the use of Ni­(COD)­DMFU as the catalyst for this transformation, which has not previously been deployed for enolate alkenylation.

Synthesis of siloxyfuran 4 and aldehyde 5.

For the synthesis of auriculatol A (1), we designed a convergent approach that would join two fragments and form the central 7-membered B-ring through the sequential construction of the C5–C6 and C9–C10 bonds (Figure a). Retrosynthetically, 1 was expected to be accessible from lactone 2, which could be formed using a transition metal-catalyzed enolate alkenylation to forge the C9–C10 bond. To realize this plan, an intermediate such as 3, bearing a 1,2-disonant oxidation motif at C5–C6, was envisioned. Whereas Shirahama’s seminal synthesis of grayanotoxin III forged the analogous bond late in the synthesis through an intramolecular pinacol coupling, the corresponding intermolecular cross-pinacol coupling remains undeveloped. Instead, we hoped to leverage the reactivity of siloxyfuran 4 to affect a polarity reversal tactic. Prior studies have demonstrated the propensity of 2-siloxyfurans to react with electrophiles at the C5-position to deliver γ-substituted butenolides. Thus, a Mukaiyama–aldol reaction between 4 and aldehyde 5 was anticipated to serve as the fragment coupling. Finally, siloxyfuran 4 was traced back to commercially available 2,2-dimethyl-1,3-cyclopentadione, while the bicyclo[3.2.1]­octane of 5 would be forged from alkenyl bromide 6 by a Ni-catalyzed intramolecular 1,2–addition.

The synthesis commenced from known enantioenriched ketone 7, which was prepared by Noyori hydrogenation of 2,2-dimethyl-1,3-cyclopentadione and subsequent silyl ether formation. Alkylation with ethyl bromoacetate and saponification of the resulting ester afforded carboxylic acid 8 in 92% yield over two steps (Figure b). Treatment of 8 with thionyl chloride furnished chlorolactone 9, which was converted to butenolide 10 by LHMDS-induced elimination of the chloride and in situ olefin isomerization. The butenolide was processed to the siloxyfuran 4 using TBSOTf and NEt3, providing access to the first of two key building blocks in seven steps.

At this stage, attention turned to the synthesis of the bicyclo[3.2.1]­octane-containing fragment 5 (Figure c). Diene 11 was prepared in two steps from commercially available 1,4-dioxaspiro[4.5]­decan-8-one via ketone acylation and subsequent alkylation. Subjection of 11 to the Pd-catalyzed asymmetric decarboxylative allylic alkylation conditions developed by Stoltz and co-workers provided the desired product, which upon ketal hydrolysis furnished diketone 6 in 85% ee and 69% yield over two steps.

To access the bicyclo[3.2.1]­octane, a chemoselective intramolecular 1,2-addition was required (Figure c). Initial evaluation of Cu-mediated 1,2-addition provided low yields of the desired product. Other traditional methods involving lithium–halogen exchange resulted in complex mixtures and trace yields. Guided by Xu’s report of a Ni-catalyzed intramolecular 1,2-addition of aryl halides to 1,3-diketones, we turned to Ni-catalysis. Use of Xu’s conditions, which employed 2,2’-bipyridine (bpy) as a ligand, delivered only trace amounts of product 12a (entry 1); instead, diene 12b, arising from a competing Heck reaction, was the major product. We hypothesized that use of a tridentate ligand could disfavor the Heck process by preventing either the olefin insertion or β-hydride elimination steps. Indeed, use of 2,2’:6’,2”-terpyridine (terpy) exhibited remarkable selectivity for 1,2-addition, providing 12a in 43% yield, with only trace amounts of competing protodebromination (entry 3). None of the possible fused bicyclo[4.2.0] product (12c) was observed. Switching to a Ni­(II) catalyst system and removal of the MgII additive enabled the reaction to be carried out on 3.7 g scale in 68% yield (entry 5). Ketone 12a was then elaborated to aldehyde 5 by a 4-step protocol involving MEM-protection of the tertiary alcohol, enol triflate formation, Lemieux–Johnson oxidation, and Ni-catalyzed triflate-bromide exchange (Figure c).

With access to aldehyde 5 and siloxyfuran 4, fragment coupling by a vinylogous Mukaiyama–aldol was explored. Based on model studies using a simpler aldehyde (see Supporting Information), a solution of 4 and 5 was treated with ZnCl2 (1 equiv) at 0 °C, providing the desired adduct in 56% combined yield as a 7.7:1.7:1.0 mixture of diastereomers (Figure ). The major product (14a) had the required configuration at C5, with C–C bond formation occurring from the opposite face as the siloxy group. Unfortunately, 14a had the opposite configuration at the C6-carbinol as required for the natural product. However, we anticipated that the C6-configuration could be corrected at a later stage in the synthesis. In principle, 14a and 14b – both bearing the (R)-C5 configuration – could be converged later in the sequence; in practice, 14b was difficult to cleanly separate from 14c and thus, following silylation and chromatographic separation, only 15 was advanced. Selective reduction of the butenolide using the Cu–H conditions reported by Buchwald provided cis-lactone 16 as a single diastereomer.

Synthesis of auriculatol A.

Despite rich literature precedent for Pd-catalyzed arylation and alkenylation of enolates, early studies on a model system identified Ni-catalysis to be more promising for the desired bond construction (see Supporting Information). Guided by precedent from Buchwald, we investigated a variety of Ni0 precatalysts and ligands while using NaHMDS as base and ZnBr2 as an additive. Under these conditions, the desired 7-membered ring product (18) formed in varying amounts, in addition to a second product, determined to be the 5/6/6 system (17). Presumably, 17 is formed by a competing β-hydride elimination/olefin reinsertion pathway. Whereas the combination of Ni­(COD)2 and bis­(dicyclohexylphosphino)­propane (dcpp) favored enolate alkenylation to give 18 in low yield (entry 1), use of bis­(diphenylphosphino)­propane (dppp) favored 17 (entry 2). Extensive screening of phosphine ligands failed to improve the yield of 18. Use of Doyle’s TMEDA-supported Ni0 precursor led to higher yields, but as a 1:1 mixture of 17 and 18 (entry 3).

Inspired by a mechanistic study from Cornella and co-workers on low-temperature Kumada cross couplings using a 16e– Ni0 catalyst, we became interested in exploring olefin-supported Ni0 complexes. These reactions were proposed to involve a Ni–ate complex, which undergoes oxidative addition of an alkenyl halide, followed by facile reductive elimination. We hypothesized that the nucleophilic enolate generated from 16 could form a similar Ni–ate complex and therefore tested several olefin-supported catalysts.

Whereas Ni­(COD)2 (in the absence of phosphine) and Ni­(COD)­DQ resulted in poor conversion (entries 4–5), use of Ni­(COD)­DMFU furnished the desired enolate alkenylation product 18 in excellent yield with high selectivity over 17 (entry 6). It is possible that the electron-deficient DMFU ligand both increases the stability of the catalyst and the rate of reductive elimination relative to β-hydride elimination. Although further investigations are required, these studies implicate low-valent electron-deficient olefin-supported Ni complexes as promising catalysts for enolate alkenylation.

With the grayanane framework in place (18), the remaining challenges included epimerization of the C6 alcohol, installation of the C16 alcohol, hydrogenation of the C9–C11 alkene, and reduction of the E-ring lactone. Although other sequences were explored, the successful route proceeded first through selective Mukaiyama hydration of the exo-olefin to give 19 with excellent diastereocontrol. Cleavage of the TMS ether with PPTS furnished alcohol 20, which was oxidized with IBX to ketone 21. Reduction of 21 with DIBAL installed the correct C6-epimer and concomitantly reduced the lactone to furnish lactol 22. Following global deprotection and acetal formation using HCl in MeOH, the final olefin reduction was investigated. Using MHAT conditions developed by Shenvi and co-workers, (+)-auriculatol A (1) was formed in 43% yield as a mixture with (+)-9-epi-auriculatol A (24, 41% yield). We note that several attempts to hydrogenate the C9–C11 olefin on earlier intermediates failed to provide the required C9-ring fustion configuration; in these cases, high selectivity for 9-epi products were obtained. Although the precise factors leading to this change in selectivity remain unclear, it is possible that 23, which has the correct C6-configuration and is lacking all protecting groups, favors a conformation with the C9 β-face more accessible for hydrogen atom transfer.

In conclusion, the first total synthesis of (+)-auriculatol A was completed in 19 steps from 1,4-dioxaspiro[4.5]­decan-8-one. The synthesis forged the C5–C6 bond through a vinylogous Mukaiyama–aldol reaction, leveraging the intrinsic reactivity of siloxyfurans for γ-functionalization. An intramolecular Ni-catalyzed enolate alkenylation was used to construct the central 7-membered ring, providing access to the grayanane scaffold in a modular fashion. These studies identified Ni­(COD)­DMFU as an effective catalyst for butyrolactone enolate alkenylation. Finally, an intramolecular Ni-catalyzed 1,2–addition was developed to access the bicyclo[3.2.1]­octane fragment, a method that may be useful for the synthesis of other diterpenoid natural products.
