What factors impact patients' decision to undergo shoulder surgery and subjective feeling of recovery?
Authors: Anna R Redden, Justin T Childers, Benjamin T Lack, Amit Sagar, Aghdas Movassaghi, Garrett R Jackson, Vani J Sabesan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study was to both evaluate the influence of specific factors, including sleep, driving, and other activities of daily living, on a patient's decision to undergo shoulder surgery and their feeling of recovery after surgery and identify associations between participant demographics and their priorities in deciding to undergo shoulder surgery or feel recovered following surgery.MethodsAn anonymous survey was conducted from May to December 2022 among adults aged 18 and older. Participants ranked the importance of pain, shoulder function, sleep, driving, and self-care in their decision to undergo shoulder surgery and in their perception of recovery. They also estimated the expected time to recovery for each activity. Responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsThe cohort consisted of 107 respondents, with 51.4% identifying as male. When asked to rank factors in order of importance for decision to undergo shoulder surgery, shoulder pain (45.8%) was most frequently ranked as the most influential factor, followed by the impaired ability to perform daily self-care activities (20.6%), decreased shoulder function (14%), difficulty driving (10.3%), and difficulty sleeping (9.3%). The most important factor in the feeling of recovery was sleeping on the affected side (73.8%), followed by toileting (67.3%), driving (60.7%), and ability to do usual work (32.1%). Multivariate logistic regression modeling revealed female gender (P = .002), English as first language (P = .036), and Asian (P < .001) and Black (P = .019) races as less likely to consider shoulder surgery. Participants with an income level higher than $100,000 per year (P = .022) are more likely to consider undergoing shoulder surgery. Black race (P = .024) was associated with a higher likelihood of prioritizing pain in surgical decision-making, while being married (P = .003) or having greater confidence in completing medical forms (P = .009) were associated with lower likelihood.ConclusionPain was the primary factor influencing the decision to undergo shoulder surgery, followed by ability to drive. Black and Asian race, female sex, lower income, and English as a first language were linked to lower likelihood of pursuing surgery, while prioritizing driving increased likelihood. Among those who felt recovered, pain relief was most important, followed by self-care, function, sleep, and driving. Black participants placed greater emphasis on pain, married participants less so. Understanding these priorities can help guide goal-setting in surgical discussions.Keywords: Orthopedic surgery, Shoulder surgery, Recovery, Decision-making tool, Activities of daily living, Sleep, Pain, Driving

Given the breadth of indications for shoulder surgery to manage a wide range of pathologies, it is crucial to understand the influential factors behind a patient's decision to undergo surgery as well as what is deemed a successful recovery from their perspective. The decision to undergo shoulder surgery is multifactorial and has been shown to be influenced by a myriad of elements, including levels of pain, functional limitations, age, marital status, occupation, insurance type, substance use, and mental or physical disability.9,30 Previous literature has identified the “tipping point” of a patient's decision to undergo surgery as the point at which preoperative pain and functional limitation outweigh the risks, costs, and time investment of undergoing surgery.30 This tipping point is further influenced by demographic factors, such as younger age, gender, and insurance type, with patients in these groups—particularly younger individuals, males, and those with commercial insurance—more likely to delay surgery.30 Research has shown that patients who undergo surgery early in the course of functional decline tend to have better outcomes following shoulder surgery.14,27 Therefore, it is crucial for surgeons to accurately assess the factors behind a patient's decision to either pursue or avoid shoulder surgery, ensuring they provide the best possible information for the patient's specific needs.

Patient recovery following shoulder surgery is typically assessed using objective measures, such as patient-reported outcome measure scores. However, the preoperative expectations of patients are rarely considered when evaluating procedural success. Research has shown that patients experience better postoperative outcomes when (1) they are given realistic expectations about the recovery process and (2) they have strong beliefs in their eventual recovery, often fostered by their surgeon's guidance.7,20 While tools have been developed to broadly assess patient expectations, there is limited research on specific factors that contribute to a patient's feeling of recovery following shoulder surgery.32 Furthermore, studies have indicated that surgeons often overestimate the speed at which patients will feel recovered following various orthopedic procedures.4,12 To improve both patient outcomes and align expectations between both patient and surgeon, it is crucial to explore what factors influence a patient's sense of recovery following shoulder surgery.

The shared decision-making model of patient care emphasizes the importance of making sure patients are well-informed in all aspects of their care and able to make decisions based on their individualized preferences.6 To achieve this model of care, it is essential that surgeons understand the factors influencing a patient's decision to undergo surgery.13 In addition, to ensure patient's goals of surgery are realistic and achievable, it is crucial to identify factors that patients prioritize when determining whether they feel recovered post-surgery. This study aimed to assess the impact of specific factors, including sleep, driving, and other activities of daily living (ADL) on a participant's decision to undergo shoulder surgery and their feeling of recovery. Furthermore, the researchers sought to identify any correlation between participant demographics and the factors they prioritize when making decisions about shoulder surgery and evaluating their feeling of recovery.

Methods

Study design

An anonymous survey was delivered in person at various public spaces in Palm Beach County, Florida, using convenience sampling to capture a broad community sample. Data were collected from May 2022 to December 2022. Trained research team members systematically visited a list of predetermined public locations, including public libraries, shopping malls, public parks, outdoors markets, and public access beaches, on varying days of the week and times of day. Inclusion criteria consisted of subjects aged 18 years of age. Subjects who were not English speaking, had poor visual acuity, mental illness, or who were unable to complete the questionnaire were excluded. No compensation was offered for participation in this study. Participants completed a Google Forms survey on an iPad (10th generation; Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA) and were assisted in completing the survey if they were unfamiliar with the format or technology.

Data collection

The survey began with demographic information on participants' age, sex, race, ethnicity, location of residence, first language, marital status, annual income, the highest level of education completed, subjective health literacy, subjective health status, insurance status (private or commercial, Medicare, Medicaid, military or veterans, other), and if the subject had prior joint injury or surgery (Q1-17). Subjective health literacy was reported similar to Chew et al8 with a 5-point Likert scale, which asked, “How confident are you filling out medical forms by yourself?” (Extremely, Quite a bit, Somewhat, A little bit, or Not at all). Subjective health status was evaluated by asking “How is your overall health status?” (Excellent, Very Good, Good, Poor, or Fair). This question approximates the first question of the 36-item Short Health Form Survey questionnaire widely used to assess quality of life.25 Overall participants in the cohort were included and then analyzed based on demographic variables and patient-reported outcome measure score plus additional outcome variables looking at sleep and driving. Summative data for the cohort were presented as descriptive statistics, and additional analyses were then used to assess specific demographic factors that may influence surgical decision-making and impact on feeling of recovery. Demographic factors including sex, race, income level, highest level education, subjective health status, insurance status, and prior joint status were evaluated.

Subsequent questions were based on the commonly used shoulder outcome measurement tool, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scores,26 with the addition of a question regarding driving, which is markedly absent from shoulder function scores despite its potential to greatly impact patient recovery and outcome.24 Participants were asked to rank the variables of pain, shoulder function, sleep, driving, and self-care activities according to the anticipated importance and impact they would have on decision-making and assessment of recovery following shoulder surgery. The survey then asked participants, “Which of the factors listed below would be most important for you when deciding to undergo shoulder surgery?” and “Which of the factors listed below would be most important for you to feel like you have recovered after shoulder surgery?” The factors included pain relief, shoulder function, sleep, return to driving, and ability to perform self-care activities. Ranking was performed on a scale of 1 through 5, 1 being the most important, 5 being the least important. Each factor could only be ranked once.

The subsequent questions (Q19-Q21) allowed participants to identify the top five ADL that they deemed would be most important to them in their decision to undergo shoulder surgery and feeling recovered following surgery. The eleven activities they selected from represented the “functional” portion of the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score (ability to put on a coat, sleep on affected side, manage toileting, wash your back, comb your hair, reach a high shelf, lift 10 lbs. above your shoulder, throw a ball overhand, do your usual work, and do usual sport/leisure activity) with the addition of return to driving.26 The survey participants then ranked their most important top five activities 1 through 5, 1 being the most important, 5 being the least important.

Participants were also asked to predict the time to recovery (in weeks) of each activity on the affected side, sleep, return to daily self-care activities, and return to driving following shoulder surgery (Q22, Q24, Q26). For these same three activities, participants were asked, “If your doctor could offer you information about when you would return to X, how influential would this be on your decision to undergo shoulder surgery?” on a 5-point Likert scale (Not important at all, Somewhat unimportant, Neutral, Somewhat important, Very important) (Q23, Q25, Q27).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported for demographic information in averages and proportions. Proportions in percentages are reported for how frequently each factor was ranked #1, or most important, in both decision-making and subjective feeling of recovery surrounding shoulder surgery. Proportions in percentages were reported for how frequently each of the eleven ADL was ranked in participant's top five. The mean number of weeks was calculated for each predicting recovery question. Where appropriate, a sentiment score was calculated for Likert style questions (ie, 1 = Not important at all, 2 = Somewhat unimportant, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Somewhat important, 5 = Very important). Sentiment scores for Likert-style questions are calculated by multiplying each response's numerical value (1-5) by the number of responses it received, then dividing the sum of those obtained values by the total number of responses to the question. All statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel version 16.63 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to determine whether participant demographics including gender, English language proficiency, marital status, health care profession, health status, previous surgery, and confidence level determined the likelihood of certain answers to “Would you consider shoulder surgery?” as well as if these factors were predictive of a participant's preference for pain, function, sleep, return to driving when determining recovery. Logistic regression analyses served to answer three main questions: (1) Which demographics were most likely to consider shoulder surgery? (2) Which demographics ranked factors most important in their decision to undergo surgery? (3) How did each demographic value each factor when defining their recovery? Three separate multivariate logistic regression models were created using R (Version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to incorporate each individual participant characteristic as an independent variable and determine their prioritization of each factor in participant's decision to undergo surgery and their definition of being recovered from surgery. A P value of < .05 was considered significant.

Results

Demographics

The cohort consisted of 107 subjects with a mean age of 38.6 years (range, 18-82) and 51.4% of participants identifying as male (Table I); 77.4% were from Florida, 68.2% identified as White, 11.2% as Asian, 9.3% as Hispanic, 6.5% as African American, and 4.7% specified another racial or ethnic identity. Private or commercial health insurance was held by 74% of subjects and 97.2% rated their health status as good, very good, or excellent. In the cohort, 18.7% had previously undergone joint surgery of any kind and 29% reported currently having shoulder trouble including injury, pain, and limited range of motion.

Ranking results: decision to undergo surgery

When asked to rank factors in order of importance for decision to undergo shoulder surgery, shoulder pain (45.8%) was most frequently ranked as the most influential factor by the cohort (Table II). Notably, 20.6% of participants ranked the impaired ability to perform daily self-care activities as most influential, 14% selected decreased shoulder function, 10.3% chose difficulty driving, and 9.3% of participants deemed difficulty sleeping as the most important factors when deciding to undergo shoulder surgery. On average, pain was the highest-ranked factor, with a mean rank of 2.42 (on a scale of 1-5, where 1 represents the most important) followed by shoulder function (2.83), sleep (2.96), self-care (3.14), and driving (3.64) (Table II).

When looking at the specific influence of ADL on decision to undergo surgery, 80% of participants ranked the ability to sleep on the affected side in their top five as the most important factor. Other than difficulty sleeping, additional activities that were included the ability to manage toileting (77.6%), the ability to do usual work (72.9%), the ability to drive an automobile (60.7%), and ability to perform sport/leisure activity (51.5%).

Ranking results: feeling recovered

The same factors influencing the decision to undergo surgery were similarly important in determining a sense of recovery post-surgery. Alleviation of pain (48.6%) was most frequently ranked as a participant's top priority to feel recovered followed by self-care (20.6%), shoulder function (12.1%), ability to sleep (10.3%), and ability to drive (8.4%) (Table III). On average, pain relief was the most important factor in feeling recovered (mean score: 2.31 on a 5-point scale, where 1 = most important) followed by shoulder function (2.75), sleep (3.10), self-care (3.23), and driving (3.60) (Table III). When examining the specific impact of ADL on the subjective sense of recovery, the ability to perform usual work (78.5%) was the most frequently ranked factor within the top five, with 32.1% considering it the most important. Other key factors included the ability to sleep on the affected side (73.8%), manage toileting (67.3%), drive (60.7%), and engage in usual sports/leisure activities (54.2%).

Perception of return to activity

When asked how influential information about time to return to certain activities would be on decision to undergo surgery, participants felt information about return of daily self-care number one (sentiment score, 4.3), followed by driving (sentiment score, 4.0), and return of to sleep on affected side (sentiment score, 3.8). (1 = Not important at all, 2 = Somewhat unimportant, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Somewhat important, 5 = Very important). No significant correlation was found in the average perceived time to recover specific activities (sleeping on affected side, daily self-care activities, or driving) and how influential in deciding to undergo surgery that factor would be (r (105) = 0.12, P = .240, r (105) = 0.10, P = .304, r (105) = 0.16, P = .094, respectively). Average expected time to recover sleeping on affected side, daily self-care activities, and driving after surgery was 5.3, 5.2, and 3.9 weeks, respectively.

Impact of participant characteristics on decision to undergo shoulder surgery

Multivariate analysis was conducted on factors impacting a participant's decision to undergo shoulder surgery (Table IV). Female gender (P = .002), English as a first language (P = .036), Asian (P < .001), and Black (P = .019) races resulted in a decreased likelihood to consider undergoing shoulder surgery. A personal income level higher than $100,000 per year (P = .022) resulted in participants being more likely to consider undergoing shoulder surgery. Participants who ranked driving (P = .019) as the most important factor in deciding whether to undergo shoulder surgery were more likely to consider undergoing shoulder surgery.

Discussion

The key findings of this study indicate that pain is the most influential factor in a patient's decision to undergo shoulder surgery, followed by shoulder function, sleep disturbance, performance of ADLs, and driving ability. Notably, the same factors that influence surgical decisions also shape a patient's perception of postoperative recovery. Sleeping on the affected side, toileting, and work ability were the most frequently ranked ADL concerns for both surgical decision-making and recovery. These findings emphasize the need for surgeons to set patient-specific, realistic expectations during preoperative counseling.

As indications for shoulder arthroplasty expand, patient motivations must remain central to treatment planning. In this survey, pain (45.8% of participants) was the most important factor in driving the decision to undergo shoulder surgery. This finding aligns with existing literature18 where pain relief is consistently ranked as the most important goal of surgery and the key factor in determining postoperative recovery. Therefore, during initial consultations, it is essential for surgeons and their teams to not only assess and quantify a patient's pain levels but also provide realistic counseling on how a given intervention may impact pain relief.

While pain was the dominant concern, functional priorities may vary based on pathology and procedure. For instance, Karpinski et al found that patients undergoing rotator cuff repair prioritized range of motion, whereas our study's heterogeneous sample ranked decreased shoulder function as the most important factor in pursuing surgery only 14% of the time and in recovery only 12.1% of the time.18 Given this, surgeons should tailor counseling to individual patient concerns rather than assuming a one-size-fits-all approach.

Sleep disturbance was also a significant factor in surgical decision-making, aligning with existing literature emphasizing sleep's impact on overall well-being.29 It is well established that sleep disruption is a primary driver for patients undergoing shoulder surgery, as inadequate sleep affects nearly all aspects of daily life, making ADLs more effortful, labored, and emotionally less fulfilling.19,31 Given its broad impact, patients naturally prioritize sleep improvement postoperatively. Notably, better sleep correlates with improved outcomes following total joint arthroplasty.2,3,22 However, while better sleep was ranked as the most important factor by 9.3% (mean rank, 2.96) of participants, pain (45.8%; mean rank, 2.42) and shoulder function (14%; mean rank, 2.83) were consistently ranked as more important determinants. In contrast, 73.8% of respondents ranked the ability to sleep on the affected side as the most important marker of recovery. This disconnect suggests that while the general public may undervalue sleep disruption as a symptom, they strongly associate sleep comfort with successful recovery.

A similar discrepancy in treatment priorities has been observed in other studies. In a study by Karpinski et al, surgeons were surveyed about their goals of care, with 72% ranking pain relief as the most important postoperative outcome.18 Similarly, Marmura et al found that in anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, surgeons prioritized stability and reoperation rates, whereas patients focused on functional outcomes such as return to ADLs, sports, and work.21 These findings highlight a potential gap between how individuals imagine shoulder pathology during decision-making and how they define success after intervention.

This study also examined patient demographics influencing the likelihood of considering shoulder surgery. It was found that female gender, English as a first language, and Asian and Black races were associated with a lower probability of choosing to undergo surgery. In a prospective study of 384 patients seeking consultation for shoulder or elbow conditions, Modi et al found that lower income and living alone predicted lower surgical willingness, while English fluency was a positive predictor.23 In the existing literature, proficiency in the English language demonstrates an association with a greater degree of health care literacy in English-speaking countries such as the United States.28 While English proficiency typically correlates with greater health care literacy, our finding that English–first-language patients were less likely to consider surgery was unexpected. One explanation may be that these patients, with greater health care literacy, may have been more aware of and receptive to nonoperative treatment options.

Race, particularly African American race, has been previously associated with lower willingness to undergo surgery, often due to concerns about hospital course, pain, and postoperative function.15,17 Our results align with these previous findings, with Black participants more likely to decline surgery and rank pain as their top concern. Notably, Asian race and female gender were associated with lower surgical likelihood, although this is inconsistently reported in existing literature.5,16 Participants in this study also ranked the importance of receiving information on recovery timelines for various activities and how that information might affect their decision to undergo shoulder surgery. Survey participants ranked the ability to care for oneself (sentiment score, 4.3) as the most important activity that patients wished to be informed of recovery timeline, followed by return to driving (sentiment score, 4.0) and sleep on affected side (sentiment score, 3.8). This suggests patients prioritize a return to daily independence over activity-specific concerns. Notably, participants who ranked driving as the most important factor in their decision-making process were more likely to consider surgery. This finding suggests that patients who highly value their ability to drive are more willing to pursue surgery in order to maintain this ability. Consequently, surgeons should consider sex, age, body mass index, and procedure type (anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty vs. reverse total shoulder arthroplasty), as these factors significantly affect return-to-driving timelines, when setting realistic expectations during patient counseling.10

Lastly, survey participants were asked about their perceived expectations of timeline for recovery for key activities, including sleeping on the affected side estimated at 5.3 weeks, resuming daily self-care (5.2 weeks), and driving after surgery at 3.9 weeks. However, literature indicates sleep disturbances after rotator cuff repair can persist from six months to up to two years, while driving may be safe as early as two weeks postoperatively.1,11 This highlights the need that surgeons must address each patient and recovery milestone individually, as broad timelines like “returning to normal activities” misrepresent realistic expectations. In addition, these timelines should be adjusted and communicated if any intraoperative or postoperative complications were to arise that might dictate a prolonged recovery path.

The shared decision-making model of patient care emphasizes collaboration between physicians and patients to determining evidence-based treatment plan that best aligns with a patient's needs. The findings of this study strongly support the notion that communication between an orthopedic surgeon and their patient regarding the decision to undergo shoulder surgery must be highly individualized. While providing general recovery timelines applicable to a broad range of patient demographics can be helpful, patients have a right to understand what a realistic individualized recovery timeline might look like before making the decision to undergo shoulder surgery. Without clear, personalized guidance, patients are unable to accurately weigh the risks and benefits of shoulder surgery. As physicians, it is our responsibility to ensure we provide sufficient information beyond broad recovery estimates—including realistic expectations for pain, sleep, self-care, and driving. By doing so, we empower patients to make truly informed decisions about their care.

Limitations

This study provides exploratory normative data on how adults without formal shoulder diagnoses weigh surgical decision-making factors and recovery expectations. Given the single-region sample and small size, findings are not intended to be definitive but may inform future work in larger, more diverse cohorts. Sampling bias may also be present due to selection of survey participants; however, this was limited by enrolling a broad range of participants in a number of different locations and over time to prevent these sampling biases. In addition, the overall participant age was lower than the typical shoulder surgery patient; however, sentiments regarding shoulder should not differ substantially from the expected patient population according to the rest of the demographic make-up of our survey population. In addition, the lack of randomization of answer choices on survey questions could have predisposed to response bias, where subjects ranked the first answer choices higher and the last answer choices lower. Repeat question types for rank order were performed to minimize these biases as well.

Conclusion

Pain was the most prioritized factor in deciding to undergo shoulder surgery. A decreased likelihood of considering surgery was associated with Black and Asian race, female gender, lower income, and speaking English as a first language, while a greater emphasis on the ability to drive correlated with increased likelihood of pursuing surgery. For patients' feeling of recovery, alleviation of pain was most important, followed by self-care and shoulder function, with sleep and driving also playing a role. Shoulder specialists should consider these factors when setting expectations and aligning surgical goals with individual patient priorities.
