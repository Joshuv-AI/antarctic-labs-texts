A Computer Vision and Machine Learning Approach to Classify Views in Distal Radius Radiographs
Authors: Rohan Vemu, Dion Birhiray, Bassem Darwish, Raven Hollis, Sai Unnam, Srikhar Chilukuri, Lorenzo Deveza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdvances in computer vision and machine learning have augmented the ability to analyze orthopedic radiographs. A critical but underexplored component of this process is the accurate classification of radiographic views and localization of relevant anatomical regions, both of which can impact the performance of downstream diagnostic models. This study presents a deep learning object detection model and mobile application designed to classify distal radius radiographs into standard views—anterior‐posterior (AP), lateral (LAT), and oblique (OB)— while localizing the anatomical region most relevant to distal radius fractures. A total of 1593 deidentified radiographs were collected from a single institution between 2021 and 2023 (544 AP, 538 LAT, and 521 OB). Each image was annotated using Labellerr software to draw bounding boxes encompassing the region spanning from the second digit MCP joint to the distal third of the radius, with annotations verified by an experienced orthopedic surgeon. A YOLOv5 object detection model was fine‐tuned and trained using a 70/15/15 train/validation/test split. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 97.3%, with class‐specific accuracies of 99% for AP, 100% for LAT, and 93% for OB. Overall precision and recall were 96.8% and 97.5%, respectively. Model performance exceeded the expected accuracy from random guessing (p < 0.001, binomial test). A Streamlit‐based mobile application was developed to support clinical deployment. This automated view classification step reduces feature space by isolating only the relevant anatomy. Focusing subsequent models on the targeted region can minimize distraction from irrelevant areas and improve the accuracy of downstream fracture classification models.Keywords: diagnostic imaging, fracture, hand and wrist, modeling

Introduction

Distal radius fractures (DRFs) are among the most commonly encountered orthopedic injuries in primary care and emergency settings, posing a significant burden on the healthcare system [1]. These fractures are particularly prevalent in pediatric and elderly populations costing an estimated $2 billion per year in pediatric patients and $535 million in annual Medicare spending for the elderly community [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Despite their frequency, distal radius fractures encompass a wide range of injury patterns and severities, each requiring tailored management strategies based on clinical and radiographic features [9, 10].

A broad spectrum of treatment options is available for DRFs, as outlined in the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) and Appropriate Use Criteria (AUC). Nonoperative approaches include immobilization with or without reduction, depending on fracture alignment. Operative interventions range from percutaneous pinning and external fixation to more complex techniques such as dorsal or volar plating, dorsal spanning bridge plates, and fragment‐specific fixation [11]. The AUC provides a framework for guiding treatment selection based on patient factors and fracture morphology, while the choice of fixation technique should be individualized based on the specific clinical context [12, 13, 14, 15].

Radiographic imaging plays a central role in the diagnosis and management of DRFs. Standard views—including anterior‐posterior (AP), lateral (LAT), and oblique (OB) radiographs—enable assessment of key parameters such as radial height, inclination, and volar tilt, which are essential for evaluating fracture severity and determining stability [16]. Accurate interpretation of these radiographs and thus timely classification of fractures—particularly distinguishing intervention from conservative management—is essential for delivering appropriate patient care, optimizing allocated resources, and improving outcomes [13, 17]

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has revolutionized the field of orthopedics, particularly in the analysis of radiographs [18, 19, 20]. Rapid advancements in computer vision, including convolutional neural networks, object detection models, and image segmentation, have facilitated applications ranging from tumor detection to fracture classification [19, 21, 22]. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been employed to differentiate fracture patterns, identify implant types, and classify anatomical structures [23, 24, 25]. However, conventional CNNs treat the image as a single unit and lack the spatial specificity required to localize relevant features within the image [26]. Image segmentation provides enhanced spatial granularity by assigning a class label to each individual pixel, thereby enabling the precise delineation of anatomical boundaries and regions of pathology. Despite its precision, segmentation is computationally intensive and typically demands extensive manually labeled datasets, which may limit its practicality for tasks such as view classification [22].

Object detection models integrate both classification and localization by identifying regions of interest and returning bounding boxes along with associated class labels [22, 27]. In orthopedic imaging, this approach has been used to implants and anatomical landmarks [27, 28, 29]. In the present study, object detection is applied to localize the region extending from the second digit metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint to the distal third of the radius—an area that provides key spatial context for differentiating among AP, LAT, and OB wrist radiograph views and corresponds to the typical location of distal radius fractures. By isolating regions of interest, such as the site of a DRF, object detection serves as a foundational step in many AI pipelines and can improve the precision of downstream tasks.

In this study, we address an important but underexplored application of object detection: the automatic classification of wrist radiograph views and localization of relevant anatomy. Rather than identifying fractures directly, our primary aim is to build a deep learning model capable of classifying distal radius radiographs into standard views (AP, LAT, and OB) using a YOLOv5‐based object detection framework. We hypothesize that this model can achieve significantly higher accuracy than random chance and provide a foundation for more accurate downstream fracture analysis. We further present a user‐friendly mobile application to support deployment in real‐time clinical settings.

Creating a Distal Radius Data Set

A total of 1593 distal radius radiographs were collected from a cohort of 540 patients at a single academic institution between 2021 and 2023. The data set included both fractured and non‐fractured distal radii. Patients were identified by querying emergency department encounter logs for cases involving orthopedic consultations and documented receipt of wrist radiographs during the specified time period. The data set comprised 544 AP, 521 OB, and 538 LAT views. All images were saved in their original format, without annotations, and extracted directly from patient charts. Radiographs were captured as screenshots from the institution's PACS imaging system while ensuring that identifiable information was excluded from the visible image frame.

Labeling the Data Set

The 1593 images were annotated with bounding boxes to specify the view (AP, OB, and LAT) in Labellerr (San Francisco, CA), a cloud‐based platform for image annotation. Annotations were verified for accuracy by an experienced orthopedic surgeon. Labellerr was selected for its user‐friendly interface, secure data handling, and ability to facilitate collaborative labeling among multiple annotators. Bounding boxes were drawn consistently across the data set, extending from the second digit metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint to the distal third of the radius to capture the region most relevant for view classification.

Preprocessing of Annotations and Images

The Labellerr software outputs annotations in JSON format, where each entry contains metadata about the image (e.g., filename and dimensions) and labeled objects (e.g., class names and bounding box coordinates). The bounding boxes are represented as [x_min, y_min, x_max, y_max], denoting the top‐left and bottom‐right corners of the box. However, YOLOv5 requires annotations formatted as [class_id x_center, y_center, width (W), height (H)], where the coordinates are normalized relative to the image dimensions. This image size offers a practical balance between detection accuracy and computational efficiency, particularly for medical imaging tasks where relevant anatomical structures (e.g., the distal radius) occupy a smaller portion of the field of view.

Model Architecture

YOLOv5 is a state‐of‐the‐art object detection algorithm operating in real‐time, making it well‐suited for tasks requiring quick and reliable predictions, such as radiograph analysis [30, 31, 32]. Among the YOLOv5 variants, YOLOv5s (the smallest model) was chosen due to its lightweight architecture and faster inference times, which are ideal for scenarios with limited computational resources or where rapid processing is critical such as on a mobile application and in a primary care setting [31, 32, 33]. Additionally, YOLOv5 incorporates built‐in data augmentation techniques, such as scaling, translation, rotation, flipping, mosaic augmentation, and color jittering, to enhance model robustness and improve generalization during training [34].

Training and Validation

The data set was split into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% test subsets to ensure a robust training and evaluation of model performance. Using a dedicated test set guarantees that the model is assessed on data it has never encountered during training or validation. A 70/15/15 split is common in machine learning, but these proportions can be adjusted depending on task complexity; more challenging tasks may benefit from larger training sets and smaller validation or test subsets.

The YOLOv5 model was trained from scratch—without using pre‐trained weights—to allow it to learn directly from the specific visual characteristics of distal radius radiographs. Model performance during training was monitored using several key metrics: precision (the proportion of predicted positives that are correct), recall (the proportion of actual positives that are detected), and F1 score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall, reflecting the balance between false positives and false negatives). Additionally, mean Average Precision (mAP) was calculated at various Intersection over Union (IoU) thresholds to assess both detection and localization accuracy [35].

IoU measures the spatial overlap between the predicted bounding box and the ground truth, ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect overlap). Average precision (AP) represents the area under the precision–recall (PR) curve for each class, and mAP is obtained by averaging AP scores across classes and IoU thresholds. This provides a comprehensive metric that captures both classification and localization performance [35].

The validation set was used to fine‐tune hyperparameters such as batch size, numbers of epochs, and IoU training thresholds through analysis of the model′s predictions versus the labeled data. All training and validation were performed in a Google Colab Pro environment (NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU, 16GB RAM).

Statistical Testing

To assess whether model performance significantly exceeded random chance, a one‐tailed binomial test was conducted comparing the observed classification accuracy to the expected accuracy under random guessing (33% for a three‐class task). Statistical analysis was performed using the scipy library in Python (3.10).

Deployment of Mobile Application

The best weights file from training was integrated into Streamlit (Snowflake Inc, Bozeman, MT), an open‐source framework to deploy AI/ML tools. A user‐friendly and accessible mobile app for fracture detection was designed. The tool allows users to upload radiographic images, processes them using the trained YOLOv5 model, and provides real‐time predictions.

The model achieved an overall classification accuracy of 97.3%, significantly outperforming the 33.3% expected by random chance in a three‐class task (binomial test, p < 0.0001), indicating that its performance was highly unlikely to have occurred by chance. Additionally, this level of accuracy is comparable with performance reported in distal radius fracture classification tasks in orthopedic literature [27, 36, 37].

The validated model was loaded onto the Streamlit platform and is now available for physician use in any healthcare settings.

Discussion

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have significantly accelerated its integration into medical imaging, particularly for automating routine tasks such as radiograph interpretation [38]. In this study, we developed and evaluated a deployable object detection model and mobile application to classify distal radius radiograph views while localizing the anatomical region most relevant to distal radius fractures. The model demonstrated strong performance, achieving precision and recall values exceeding 0.93 across AP, OB, and LAT views, with an overall mAP50 of 0.987 and mAP50‐95 of 0.74. Among the three classes, OB views had the lowest accuracy, likely due to their intermediate positioning and overlapping anatomical features with AP views. Importantly, the tool is fully HIPAA‐compliant, as it operates on deidentified radiographs captured via screenshots from PACS with all patient identifiers excluded. This approach eliminates the need to export or process full DICOM files, allowing for rapid integration into clinical workflows while minimizing data storage and transmission burdens. The simplicity and speed of this method make the tool particularly well‐suited for point‐of‐care use and deployment in resource‐limited environments.

The clinical utility of this tool lies in its potential to serve as a preprocessing step for downstream tasks, such as automated fracture detection and treatment classification. By identifying the correct radiographic view and localizing the relevant anatomy, the model enables cropping of the image to reduce feature space, thereby enhancing the signal‐to‐noise ratio. This approach may improve the accuracy of subsequent models by eliminating irrelevant features and focusing analysis on the likely fracture site [22]. Moreover, view‐specific image routing facilitates ensemble‐based classification strategies tailored to each projection, improving both model interpretability and clinical applicability [39].

Such features are especially pertinent in the integration of this technology into fracture management, especially in primary care and rural settings. Healthcare disparities between rural and urban settings are well documented, and this gap is particularly pronounced in orthopedic care due to the specialized nature of the field and the limited availability of providers in rural areas [40, 41]. In these rural areas, point‐of‐care technologies and AI can serve as valuable tools to bridge the gap between diagnosis and definitive care [42]. With lengthy transfer systems and a complicated referral network from rural hospitals to urban centers, AI‐powered tools have demonstrated their utility in optimizing clinical decision support systems (CDSS) and enhancing healthcare outcomes [43, 44, 45, 46]. These technologies can assist triaging physicians by providing preliminary assessment support, such as guidance in managing distal radius fractures, enabling quicker diagnoses and expediting the initiation of the transfer process.

While these results are promising, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all radiographs were obtained from a single institution, which may limit generalizability to external datasets with varying imaging protocols or equipment. Second, the model was trained on approximately 500 examples per view class, which is below the commonly recommended 1000 instances per class for YOLOv5 [34]. Nonetheless, this limitation is partially offset by the relatively low complexity of the classification task, the robust data augmentation inherent to the YOLOv5 framework, and the observation that the model′s performance stabilized early during training (i.e., performance metrics and losses leveled off well before training completion), suggesting it had effectively learned from the available data.

Additionally, the accuracy of the model depends on the consistency of bounding box placement and correct labeling of radiograph views, which were verified by an orthopedic surgeon but could benefit from further review or consensus labeling. Although our data set was split into separate training, validation, and test subsets, model optimization—including hyperparameter tuning—was based on performance on the validation set. As a result, the model may still exhibit some bias toward features specific to the overall data set. Further validation on external datasets from other institutions, as well as prospective evaluation during real‐time clinical use, will be necessary to assess generalizability and real‐world performance. Finally, while view classification is a relatively straightforward task for trained orthopedic surgeons, this tool is intended primarily as a foundational step to support downstream AI/ML applications, and we acknowledge this as a limitation in terms of its immediate clinical utility.

In conclusion, this study addresses an underexplored challenge in musculoskeletal imaging by applying object detection to radiographic view classification in distal radius radiographs. The model achieved high accuracy and was integrated into a functional mobile platform, demonstrating the feasibility of real‐time clinical deployment. Future directions will focus on applying this detection model to enable downstream classification tasks, refining ensemble workflows, and validating performance on external datasets. In particular, oblique view misclassifications suggest an opportunity to improve class separation through targeted augmentation or view‐specific preprocessing. As this framework evolves, it has the potential to enhance fracture management by improving diagnostic consistency, streamlining clinical workflows, and ultimately supporting more timely and appropriate treatment decisions.
