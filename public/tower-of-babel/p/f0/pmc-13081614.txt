Risk factors and prediction model of emergence delirium in children after general anesthesia under preoperative sedation with uniform oral midazolam premedication: a prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Shuangyan Zhao, Ming Zhang, Bo Feng, Shunfen Liao, Yunxia Li, Liming Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmergence Delirium (ED) is a frequent complication following general anesthesia in children. It is multifactorial, with no single cause conclusively identified. However, limited research has examined the risk factors for ED in children under such a protocol. Therefore, our goal was to identify independent risk factors for ED in this unified preoperative sedation protocol.MethodsThis prospective observational study collected medical records of children undergoing elective tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia at Kunming Children’s Hospital between March 2023 and September 2023. Based on the Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED) scale, participants were categorized into a non-ED group and an ED group. Univariable logistic regression analysis was performed to compare the groups, and factors showing statistical significance were included in a multivariate analysis. Significant risk factors were identified, and a nomogram prediction model was constructed. The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, model calibration curve, and decision curve analysis were used to assess the model’s performance.ResultsAmong this single-center cohort of 143 children, 47 children experienced ED, yielding an incidence rate of 32.9%. Logistic regression analysis identified younger age, male sex, low-dose opioids, and absence of remifentanil infusion as significant risk factors for ED in children under preoperative sedation. The area under the ROC curve was 0.818 (95% CI 0.751–0.885), with a sensitivity of 87.2% and specificity of 63.5%. The Youden index was 0.508. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test demonstrated good calibration of the prediction model (P = 0.592), and the C index visualized the nomogram model was 0.818.ConclusionsIndependent risk factors for ED in children were younger age, male sex, low-dose opioids, and absence of remifentanil infusion. The prediction model based on these factors showed reliable performance in predicting ED in children undergoing preoperative sedation within our center.

Introduction

ED is a prevalent yet poorly defined complication following pediatric general anesthesia. It poses a significant challenge in pediatric anesthesia and may affect children undergoing surgeries across various ages and procedures. ED is an acute confusional state that arises during recovery from general anaesthesia; affected children may be disoriented, experience hallucinations, and exhibit either agitation or aimless hyperkinesis [1]. Typically, it begins 15–30 min after anesthesia ends and persists for variable durations. ED often manifests as sudden physical activity in bed, leading to self-injury, dislodging of intravenous cannulae or drains, and potential harm to caregivers. These outcomes result in increased nursing demands, parental anxiety, and reduced parental satisfaction with anesthesia [1]. Furthermore, ED has been associated with long-term perceptual and psychomotor disturbances, including nervousness, anxiety, eating disorders, and enuresis.

Emergence agitation (EA) is characterized by restlessness, disorientation, excitability, purposeless movements, inconsolability, thrashing, and incoherent speech in the same recovery window [2]. ED is therefore not synonymous with EA: it can present as hypoactive, mixed, or, as in EA, hyperactive phenotypes [3]. Nevertheless, the two terms are frequently used interchangeably in the literature and are commonly evaluated with identical assessment instruments [4, 5]. Consistent with much of the literature, we use the terms ED and EA interchangeably in this article.

Among various pediatric surgical procedures, otolaryngology and ophthalmic surgeries have demonstrated a particularly high incidence of ED. Notably, tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy in otolaryngology are associated with a significant occurrence of ED. Hence, this study specifically focuses on children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy. Investigating risk factors for ED is crucial for predicting its onset and mitigating its severe consequences. Previous reports suggest that midazolam can reduce postoperative agitation [1], and its use as a preoperative sedative has become increasingly common. This study aims to identify risk factors for ED in children who receive preoperative midazolam sedation and to establish a prediction model to assist in identifying children at high risk of ED in clinical settings.

Methods

Study cohort

This study was a prospective observational investigation approved by the Ethics Committee of Kunming Children’s Hospital (Contact Name of the ethic committee: Qi Qiu; Zhiyang Wang, approval number: 2023-03-197-K01). Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology guidelines were followed.

Study population

Inclusion criteria

The participants included children aged 2–16 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification I or II who underwent elective tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia at Kunming Children’s Hospital between March 2023 and September 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all enrolled participants.

Exclusion criteria

History of allergy to benzodiazepines, contraindications to conventional anesthesia drugs, chronic diseases, psychiatric disorders, cardiac disease, and prior brain surgery. Cases were further excluded based on the following conditions: (1) incomplete data collection due to unforeseen factors during the study; (2) severe preoperative respiratory obstruction caused by enlarged tonsils and adenoids, leading to significant daytime drowsiness or respiratory distress immediately before surgery; and (3) intraoperative hemorrhage that was challenging to control.

Criterion (2) reflected a pre-operative plan for post-operative tracheal intubation and direct ICU admission, whereas criterion (3) arose from an intra-operative decision made after sudden, uncontrollable bleeding. In both situations the children were transferred to the ICU immediately after surgery for safety reasons and could therefore not be observed in the PACU during the emergence period; consequently, they were excluded.

Preoperative data collection

The child’s preoperative general condition was assessed through medical history, review of the electronic medical record system, and analysis of sleep apnea monitoring reports. The data collected included gender, age, height, weight, history of anesthesia and surgery, history of upper respiratory tract infections, severity of obstructive ventilation dysfunction, and presence of sleep disorders. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated based on the child’s height and weight, with obesity defined as a BMI exceeding the 95th percentile for the child’s age and sex. Preoperative sleep disorders were identified by inquiring about symptoms such as difficulty falling asleep, interrupted nighttime sleep, and sleep pattern reversal [6]. The preoperative anxiety status for children was assessed using the modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale (mYPAS), which was completed by the anesthesiologist during the preoperative visit in the ward on the morning of the surgery. A score of > 40 on the mYPAS was considered indicative of preoperative anxiety.

Methods of anesthesia

Preoperative oral midazolam

In the preoperative waiting area, under the supervision of the anesthesiologist, children included in the study were given oral midazolam solution 30 min before anesthesia induction according to the protocol, at a dose of 0.4 mg/kg, with a maximum dose of 15 mg. The oral midazolam solution (20 mg/10 ml, Yichang Renfu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Hubei, China) was drawn by the anesthesia nurse using a 10 ml syringe, dripped into a dosing spoon, and handed to the parents to administer. No cases of children refusing to take the medicine or vomiting occurred. This study did not design a control group with other preoperative sedation protocols; therefore, it estimates the incidence and determinants of ED under a standardized preoperative oral midazolam regimen.

Induction and maintenance of anesthesia

Upon entering the operating room, routine monitoring of electrocardiogram (ECG), oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and blood pressure (BP) was initiated. Anesthesia induction commenced following the establishment of peripheral venous access and adequate preoxygenation. Anesthesia was induced with sufentanil (0.2–0.5 µg/kg) or oxycodone (0.1–0.15 mg/kg), cisatracurium (0.1–0.2 mg/kg), and propofol (2.5–3.5 mg/kg). For antiemetic prophylaxis, dexamethasone (0.1–0.2 mg/kg) was given intravenously. Tracheal intubation was performed once an adequate depth of anesthesia was achieved, and machine-controlled ventilation was maintained with a tidal volume (TV) of 6–8 mL/kg and a respiratory rate (RR) of 12–20 breaths/min, ensuring end-tidal CO2 (PETCO2) remained between 35 and 45 mmHg.

Anesthesia was maintained using one of the following two techniques at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist:Balanced (inhalational-intravenous) anesthesia: This consisted of inhaled sevoflurane (end-tidal concentration 0.8–1.3 MAC) combined with a continuous remifentanil infusion (0.1–0.5 µg/kg/min). Alternatively, in some cases, anesthesia was maintained with inhaled sevoflurane alone, with supplemental intravenous boluses of either sufentanil (0.1 µg/kg) or oxycodone (0.1 mg/kg) administered as needed based on clinical signs.Total intravenous anesthesia: This was achieved with a continuous infusion of propofol (4–10 mg/kg/h) together with remifentanil (0.1–0.5 µg/kg/min). In a subset of patients, remimazolam (0.5-1.0 mg/kg/h) was used as an alternative to propofol, combined with remifentanil infusion.

Doses of all anesthetic agents were titrated to maintain hemodynamic stability (defined as systolic blood pressure within ± 20% of baseline) and to keep depth-of-anesthesia indices within target ranges. Depth of anesthesia was monitored using a multiparameter brain function monitor (model HXD-I, Yifei Huatong Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The sedation index (WLi) and analgesia index (PTi) provided by this monitor were maintained between 40 and 70. Approximately 5–10 min before the end of surgery, intravenous boluses of either sufentanil (0.1 µg/kg) or oxycodone (0.1 mg/kg), or ketorolac tromethamine (0.5 mg/kg), were administered for analgesia. At the conclusion of the surgical procedure, all anesthetic maintenance infusions were discontinued, and the patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for recovery.

The intraoperative intravenous doses of opioids were all converted to morphine equivalents. The conversion formulas are: 1 µg of sufentanil = 1 mg of morphine, 1 mg of oxycodone = 1 mg of morphine.

Diagnosis of ED

The PAED scale, recognized as the best-validated instrument for detecting ED [7, 8], minimizes errors in assessing ED in children and is applicable across all age groups. The scale consists of five items: (1) eye contact with caregivers, (2) purposeful activities, (3) perception of surroundings, (4) ability to communicate needs, and (5) restlessness. Each item is scored based on the child’s behavior, ranging from 0 (normal performance) to 4 (abnormal performance). The PAED scale was used to assess ED, with professionally trained anesthesiologists calculating the PAED score at 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 min after emergence from anesthesia. Additionally, if any signs suggestive of emergence delirium appeared during these intervals, immediate assessments using the PAED scales were conducted, with a maximum score of 20. A PAED score of ≥ 10 points is considered diagnostic for ED.

Recovery from anesthesia

If a pediatric patient experienced ED, the attending anesthesiologist immediately conducted a structured assessment. This assessment prioritized identifying and treating potential causes, with postoperative pain being the primary concern. The child’s report of pain triggered the immediate administration of rescue analgesia. If ketorolac tromethamine had not been administered intraoperatively, a rescue dose of 0.5 mg/kg was given intravenously. If the drug had already been used, oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg was provided for pain relief. This approach ensured that inadequate analgesia was addressed as a potential contributor to ED without delay. If these were ineffective, 1 mg/kg propofol was administered. Children were observed in the PACU for at least 30 min after emergence from anesthesia. When a modified Aldrete score of 9 was reached, the child was discharged from the PACU.

Indicators observed and recorded

Gender, age, obesity, history of anesthesia, upper respiratory tract infection, sleep disorder, severity of obstructive ventilatory dysfunction, preoperative anxiety, inhalation anesthesia, continuous propofol infusion, continuous remimazolam infusion, continuous remifentanil infusion, opioid dose, use of dexamethasone, dexmedetomidine, duration of surgery, ketorolac tromethamine, use of patient-controlled analgesia pump, duration of surgery, and PAED scores. Based on their PAED scores, children were categorized into the non-ED and ED groups. Eighteen candidate predictive factors were identified, derived from studies or systematic reviews related to agitation during recovery from general anesthesia in children. These studies mentioned that these factors might be associated with ED [1, 7, 9–12].

Sample size and statistical analysis

Using G.Power 3.1 software, assuming an α error of 0.05, a power of 80%, Effect size f2 = 0.35, number of predictors = 18 and missing rate of 10%, a cohort of 82 patients was required. A total of 152 children underwent tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia following preoperative sedation. An effect size f²of 0.35 was selected. We initially positioned this study as an ‘exploratory cohort’, aiming to ensure 80% test power at a medium-to-large effect size level (equivalent to ‘large’ in Cohen’s classification), thereby obtaining preliminary signals with a smaller sample.

Analysis was conducted using SPSS 18.0 and RStudio statistical software. Data for normally distributed measures were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between groups were performed using the two-independent samples t-test. For continuous data with a skewed distribution, the median and interquartile ranges (first quartile and third quartile) were reported, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons. Count data were analyzed using the χ² test. ED was treated as the dependent variable, while each potential risk factor was analyzed as an independent variable. Factors with P < 0.2 in univariable analysis were included in a logistic multivariate regression analysis to identify significant risk factors for ED. The ROC curve was generated to calculate the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity. RStudio software was used to establish a risk prediction model and draw a nomogram model, and conduct the Hosmer-Lemshow test on the prediction model. A P value greater than 0.1 indicates that the model has a good fit. The C-index is used to evaluate the discriminatory power of a model. A C-index of 0.5 to 1.0 is considered, where 0.5 represents random prediction and 1.0 represents perfect prediction. The closer the C-index is to 1.0, the better the model fits the data. Draw the calibration curve to assess the agreement between the predicted results of the rating model and the actual results. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study initially enrolled 152 children. Of these, 7 cases were excluded due to incomplete scoring in the PACU, 1 case due to reoperation for postoperative hemorrhage, and 1 case due to transfer to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) for postoperative hypoxemia. In total, 9 cases were excluded, leaving 143 children who met the study criteria. Among them, 47 children developed ED, resulting in an incidence rate of 32.9% (Fig. 1).

Logistic regression analysis identified young age, male gender, lower intraoperative opioid doses, and absence of remifentanil administration as independent risk factors for ED following tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy with preoperative sedation (Table 2). For each additional year of age, the likelihood of developing ED decreased by 47% (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.38–0.75, P < 0.001). The incidence of ED in girls was 89% lower than in boys (OR 0.11, 95% CI 0.03–0.33, P < 0.001). For every 0.1 mg/kg increase in morphine, the likelihood of ED decreased by 0.8% (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.99–1.000, P = 0.04). Continuous remifentanil infusion reduced the probability of ED by 88% (OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.82, P = 0.030).

Discussion

In this study, the incidence of ED after tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia, assessed using the PAED scale, was 32.9%. ED is a prevalent and significant complication following pediatric surgery, substantially affecting the quality of recovery in children. Its underlying pathological and physiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Previous studies report an incidence ranging from 20% to 80% [13, 14], with variations largely attributed to the use of different assessment criteria during the recovery phase, such as the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) or Watcha scoring systems [15]. The PAED scale—the most thoroughly validated tool for emergence delirium—demonstrates high sensitivity and can be reliably applied to children of all ages [15].

This study was conducted under the specific condition of uniform preoperative sedation with oral midazolam. While midazolam is widely used to alleviate preoperative anxiety in children, and oral administration is recognized for effectively reducing tension and anxiety and some studies support its role in reducing ED [7, 16], its efficacy as a prophylactic agent against ED remains debated. A recent network meta-analysis indicated that midazolam alone may not be ideal for preventing sevoflurane-related ED in children, with potentially inferior efficacy compared to agents like dexmedetomidine [17]. Indeed, current international guidelines recommend several agents including midazolam for ED prophylaxis [18], yet the evidence for midazolam’s preventive effect is conflicting and questionable. Despite this, it remains the most commonly used premedication in many hospitals [19]. At present, there are few studies on the risk factors of ED in children receiving preoperative sedation, despite its wide use in pediatric anesthesia, particularly in Asia and Africa, where it is considered an indispensable part of practice [12, 20, 21]. Our study addresses this gap by investigating ED within this specific clinical pathway.

Factors such as separation from parents, unfamiliarity with anesthesia and surgery, and fear of pain contribute to severe preoperative anxiety in children [10], a condition that has been reported to increase the likelihood of ED during recovery by 3.5 times [11]. Therefore, employing techniques to minimize this anxiety, such as preoperative sedation, plays an essential role in ED prevention. However, the observed ED incidence of 32.9% in our cohort underscores that ED remains a frequent clinical problem even with routine midazolam premedication.

With the growing adoption of preoperative sedation in pediatric anesthesia, a nuanced understanding of its impact on ED risk factors is essential. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as identifying the factors (younger age, male sex, and analgesic management) associated with the residual risk of ED in the specific context of routine midazolam premedication, highlighting the importance of managing these other modifiable risk factors beyond sedation alone.

Accurately identifying children at high risk for ED and implementing early interventions can prevent severe adverse events, making it a crucial step in ensuring medical safety. The nomogram, a widely used clinical prediction model, offers the advantage of individualized risk assessment for adverse events. Currently, no single factor has been identified as the definitive cause of ED [9]. Research suggests that ED is associated with factors such as preoperative tension and anxiety, obesity, use of inhalation anesthetics, and postoperative pain [1, 9]. The findings of this study revealed that younger age, male gender, insufficient opioid dosing, and absence of remifentanil infusion were independent risk factors for ED in children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia. Developing a nomogram for these risk factors enables anesthesiologists to visually assess the relative contribution of each factor and facilitates the identification of high-risk children.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that preschool-aged children are more susceptible to ED, with younger age being a notable risk factor. Younger children are particularly vulnerable to unfamiliar environments and medical experiences due to their cognitive immaturity, as well as the disorientation associated with awakening in an unfamiliar setting. Research on ED in children suggests that the underdevelopment of the nervous system, including cholinergic function and hippocampal immaturity, contributes to an increased susceptibility to delirium in this age group [22]. This aligns with the results of this study, where the median age of children with ED was 4.1 years, compared to 5.5 years in those without ED. While the absolute difference is approximately 1.4 years, the associated effect size is clinically substantial: each additional year of age reduced the odds of ED by 47% (OR 0.53). In the context of rapid neurodevelopment during early childhood, even a one-year increment can reflect meaningful improvements in cognitive processing, emotional regulation, and ability to cope with the unfamiliar perioperative environment. Therefore, age is not only a statistically significant predictor but also a clinically relevant one, helping to identify the more vulnerable subset of preschool children who may benefit from targeted preventive strategies. Therefore, greater attention should be given to managing the recovery from general anesthesia in younger children.

This study also found that boys were more likely than girls to develop ED. Girls of the same age typically exhibit more advanced physical and psychological maturity, allowing them to adapt more readily to the recovery room environment and follow instructions from medical staff. Previous studies have also identified male gender as a risk factor for ED [1, 15].

Pain is a significant contributing factor to the development of ED, and effective pain management can reduce both its incidence and severity [23, 24]. Inadequate pain relief has been identified as one of the primary causes of ED, particularly following shorter surgical procedures [13, 25–27]. The use of opioids has been shown to significantly lower the incidence of ED [28]. A meta-analysis investigating the relationship between opioid administration and ED during recovery demonstrated that various opioids, including fentanyl, sufentanil, and remifentanil, effectively reduce ED occurrence [1, 29, 30]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Ahmani S et al. highlighted the preventive effects of analgesic drugs on ED [14, 31]. Conversely, anticholinergic drugs, inhalation anesthetics, and unmanaged postoperative pain are frequently implicated as contributors to ED in children after general anesthesia [14]. Consistent with these findings, this study revealed that an increase in intraoperative opioid dosage was associated with a lower incidence of ED.

It is crucial to emphasize that in our clinical protocol, proactive perioperative analgesia was a cornerstone of management. All children received planned multimodal analgesia, with intraoperative monitoring of analgesic depth and pre-emptive analgesic administration before the end of surgery. The rescue analgesia protocol for ED was designed to promptly address any breakthrough pain, aligning with the ethical imperative to keep children relatively pain-free.

Remifentanil, a potent and ultra-short-acting opioid, was widely used for prolonged infusion during general anesthesia because of its rapid onset and brief half-life. Przybylo et al. [32] observed that children receiving remifentanil during strabismus surgery awakened more calmly and exhibited lower postoperative behavioural disturbance scores. Similarly, Dong et al. [33] reported a 43% reduction in ED incidence with the use of remifentanil in children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia. Nevertheless, the role of remifentanil in preventing ED is complex. Despite our and others’findings, concerns persist that its rapid offset may precipitate an acute analgesic gap, potentially increasing agitation risk. Furthermore, remifentanil use—particularly at higher doses—is associated with the development of opioid-induced hyperalgesia, a dose-related phenomenon that could exacerbate postoperative pain and agitation [34]. The protective effect observed in our study likely reflects its integration into a proactive analgesic protocol involving pre-emptive analgesia administered before the end of surgery, which mitigated these risks. Therefore, the benefit of remifentanil may be contingent upon meticulous postoperative pain management and prudent dose titration.

In this study, univariable analysis was performed on all included factors, and statistically significant variables were subjected to multivariate logistic regression analysis. The ROC curve for the prediction model yielded an AUC of 0.818, with sensitivity and specificity values of 87.2% and 63.5%, respectively. The C-index, a measure of predictive performance, showed a value of 0.818, indicating that the model has strong clinical significance. A visual nomogram based on the identified risk factors was developed, further reinforcing the utility of this prediction model in clinical settings.

Clinical use recommendations

To facilitate bedside risk assessment, the nomogram can be printed as a pocket card or encoded into a free WeChat mini-program. To use it, the clinician first locates the patient’s value on each variable axis, draws a vertical line up to the “Points” axis to read the score for each item, and then sums the four item scores to obtain a total score. This total score is then projected down to the bottom risk probability axis to read the individualized predicted probability of ED. If an individual’s predicted risk exceeds the overall incidence rate of ED observed in this study (32.9%), intervention is recommended.

Limitations and future directions

Our study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center, observational study from a single ethnic population, which may limit generalizability and leaves room for residual confounding. Second, the sample size, while adequate for detecting medium-to-large effects in this derivation cohort, may be underpowered for factors with smaller effect sizes; this is reflected in the wide confidence interval for the remifentanil effect. Most importantly, the study design lacks a control group with alternative or no preoperative sedation, precluding direct comparisons of ED risk across different premedication strategies. Finally, the prediction model requires external validation in independent, multi-center cohorts to confirm its calibration and clinical utility before broader application.

These limitations define clear pathways for future research. Prospective, multi-center studies with larger samples are needed to validate and refine our nomogram. Furthermore, randomized controlled trials comparing uniform midazolam premedication against other regimens (e.g., dexmedetomidine) or no sedation are essential to determine the relative impact of premedication choice on the incidence and risk factors for ED.

Conclusions

In this single-centre derivation cohort, four variables—younger age, male sex, lower intra-operative opioid dose and lack of remifentanil infusion—were independently associated with emergence delirium. A nomogram built from these factors showed modest-to-good discrimination and acceptable calibration in internal validation. By flagging children at higher risk, anaesthesiologists can institute targeted preventive measures and guide parental counselling. Ultimately, larger-scale, multi-centre studies are necessary to verify and refine this prediction model before it can be confidently implemented across diverse clinical settings.
