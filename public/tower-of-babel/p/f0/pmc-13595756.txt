Comparing the health implications of heat exposure in pregnant women in rural and urban Tamil Nadu: An exploratory cohort study
Authors: Rekha Shanmugam, Vidhya Venugopal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncreased temperatures caused by climate change may have an adverse impact on pregnant women’s health outcomes. Understanding the disparities in heat vulnerabilities between rural and urban pregnant women (RPW and UPW) could help prioritize and develop tailored interventions to protect the most vulnerable.MethodsWe conducted an observational cohort study from 2018 to 2021 among 680 pregnant women in Tamil Nadu from six districts. We measured the wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) exposure and Heat Strain Indicators (HSI) such as urine specific gravity and core body temperature using standardised protocols. We used a validated questionnaire to elucidate the self-reported heat strain symptoms (SHSS), adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs), and adverse birth outcomes (ABOs).ResultsA higher percentage of RPWs had WBGT exposure (55%) above the permissible limit with an average WBGT of 26.8 °C ± 1.0 °C compared to the UPW (45%) with an average exposure of 26.9 °C ± 0.8 °C. RPWs were more likely than UPWs to report SHSS (84% vs. 71%) and HSIs (31% vs. 22%) among 680 women. After controlling for the potential confounders, heat exposed RPWs (N = 372) had nearly double the risk of APOs (95% CI 1.2–5.2) and thrice the risk of ABOs (95% CI 1.3–7.4). Furthermore, based upon the heat exposure during the first trimester the heat exposed RPWs (N = 248) had a 4-fold higher risk of miscarriages than the UPWs (95% CI 1.1–16.5). The lack of awareness and mitigation strategies against heat risk, low socioeconomic status, and reduced access to welfare facilities can contribute to RPW’s high heat risk vulnerability.ConclusionThe study underscores the necessity to protect RPW from heat and APOs. In-depth cohort studies are required to identify risk factors and develop protective strategies for these women.Trial RegistrationNot applicable.

Background

One of the most urgent global health challenges of the 21st century is climate change [1], and its effects on human health are particularly severe in vulnerable populations such as pregnant women [2]. Heat exposure poses substantial risks to maternal health and birth outcomes, notably in tropical regions like India, due to the rising temperatures [3]. Elevated temperatures further exacerbate the physiological stress that pregnant women already experience due to increased metabolic demands [4, 5], making them more susceptible to heat-related illnesses (HRIs) [6], and dehydration. An individual’s health can be compromised and they may be more susceptible to the adverse effects of elevated heat as a result of dehydration and inadequate fluid replacement [7]. The impact of pregnancy on a woman’s heat tolerance remains ambiguous; nonetheless, modified hormone levels and the heightened circulatory needs of the foetus may enhance her vulnerability to heat-related diseases [8], Additionally, the disruption of maternal-foetal heat exchange during pregnancy may pose a risk to fetal development due to heat-induced depletion [9], Disruption of heat balance generates heat-shock proteins, which trigger a series of biochemical and physiological reactions detrimental to maternal and perinatal health, including the newborn period [4, 5].

Researchers have linked heat exposure during pregnancy to a variety of adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes (APOs and ABOs), including miscarriages, low birth weight (LBW), preterm birth (PTB), stillbirth, Intra uterine growth retardation (IUGR), and maternal complications like dehydration, heat cramps, exhaustion, and heat stroke [10, 11]. Risk factors such as socio-economic status, geographic location, and prevailing infrastructural factors interrelate with heat exposure to maternal health. Rural communities have a higher share of these risk factors compared to an urban setting. However, urban areas present unique challenges such as the urban heat island effect, which leads to higher temperatures compared to rural areas due to changes in air circulation patterns, a decrease in vegetation cover, and an increase in concretization levels [12]. Pregnant women in densely populated cities experience elevated heat stress heat stress due to the urban heat island phenomenon, congestion, motor vehicle emissions, and air pollution [13].

Females residing in urban areas may have improved access to healthcare facilities and air-conditioned environments; however, their overall exposure to heat leads to adverse outcomes for the fetus, especially among low-income pregnant women living in overcrowded, poorly ventilated housing [14]. Moreover, the socio-economic divide in urban areas exacerbates these risks by disproportionately exposing the poorest segments of the population to heat. The lifestyle of rural pregnant women (RPW) may significantly increase their direct heat exposure due to their distinct responsibilities related to livelihood.

India is a tropical nation characterised by an intensely hot summer season that jeopardises the health of millions of women [15, 16]. We still lack a full understanding of the various factors that influence pregnancy, whether it occurs in rural or urban areas. It is necessary to contextualize the health hazards associated with pregnancy for women in both rural and urban settings, particularly in light of the emerging climate change regime characterized by increasing temperature changes. One of the southern states in India, Tamil Nadu, is highly vulnerable to extreme heat episodes, which have a detrimental impact on its population in both rural and urban areas. This study will compare the health outcomes of heat exposure among RPW and the urban pregnant women (UPW) in Tamil Nadu, with a focus on the variations in exposures to hazards, levels of exposure, and pregnancy outcomes in both populations. The objective of the study is to explore the differential considerations regarding the exposure of women to heat stress in rural and urban settings and the associated APOs and ABOs.

Methods

Study design

From 2018 to 2021, we conducted an observational prospective cohort study among pregnant women in several districts of Tamil Nadu (TN), India, to study the health consequences of heat exposure. This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration’s ethical protocol, and after obtaining the informed consent from all the women involved. The ethical review board at our institute Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research (SRIHER) accorded ethical approval (IEC-NI/21/FEB/77/37).

Study setting description

We recruited the pregnant women from various districts in TN, representing both rural (four districts: Tiruvannamalai, Salem, Nagapatinam, & Tiruvallur) and urban (two districts: Chennai, and Kancheepuram) areas. The study locations accounted for a variety of factors, such as geographical and climatic diversity, to provide a comprehensive understanding of the nature and effects of heat exposure in various environments. We consider the districts outside of the cities as rural, meaning it is less populated and has inadequate infrastructure and welfare facilities, while we consider the location inside the cities as urban, meaning it is densely populated and has adequate inbuilt infrastructure [17].

The temperature and the humidity pattern of Tiruvannamalai & Salem district experience a tropical climate, with an average temperature of 30–40 °C and 50% humidity; Nagapatinam experiences a tropical maritime climate, with an average temperature of 30–40 °C and 70% humidity; and Tiruvallur experiences a tropical climate, with an average temperature of 30–40 °C and a humidity of 60–65% during the summer months. The primary occupations of women in these regions are agriculture, saltpans, and brick kilns. Chennai has a tropical wet and dry climate with a humidity of 60–80%, and Kancheepuram has a tropical climate with an average temperature of 32–42 °C and a humidity of 50–70%. The primary occupations of women in these regions are domestic workers, textile industries, and clerical jobs.

Study population

The enrolled pregnant women were between 8 and 14 weeks of gestation. Sample size was calculated using our own previous study conducted among the working pregnant women in Tamil Nadu, India. The prevalence of APOs was 17.2% [18]. The sample size was calculated based on this prevalence and utilised the standard formula n=[Z2*(P*q)]/d2 to determine the sample size, where n is the number of study participants, P is the population prevalence, Z is the test statistic (1.96) and d is the precision. For P = 17.2%, q = 82.8 and d = 4. For an 80% power and a precision of 4, 340 women would be needed from each locality and recruited 680 in total [19]. The participants were recruited from antenatal care clinics established by state government hospitals and tertiary care hospitals (G Block OPD at Sri Ramachandra Medical Centre) in the selected districts. As recruitment was conducted through both public primary healthcare centres (PHCs) and a tertiary care facility, differences in healthcare access, service utilization, and clinical practices between these settings were acknowledged. The state government PHCs are equipped with only the essential infrastructure and resources required to manage routine antenatal care and normal deliveries, patients are typically referred to higher-level district hospitals during emergencies or complications. In contrast, the tertiary care hospital has comprehensive specialty services and advanced facilities to manage high-risk pregnancies and obstetric complications. These contextual differences were considered when interpreting the observed health outcomes. Based upon the inclusion criteria were as follows: pregnant women aged 18 to 40, who resided in either rural or urban areas of TN, had singleton pregnancies, were willing to participate in a cohort study, and provided periodic data on health and pregnancy outcomes. Additionally, they had to be free from pre-existing chronic conditions like cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or chronic respiratory disorders, as well as high-risk pregnancies like gestational diabetes and hypertension that could affect heat sensitivity.

Heat stress profiling

To ascertain trimester-specific heat exposure, three study visits were conducted for each participant, one in each trimesters-during which heat exposure levels were monitored at the household and, where applicable, at the workplace. A wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) (Model QuesTemp°34) monitor was utilised to quantify heat exposure. The American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) established criteria defining the WBGT heat stress index, a metric that includes dry bulb temperature, radiative/globe temperature, wet bulb temperature, relative humidity, and wind velocity, as a measure of the thermal discomfort resulting from heat exposure. After getting permission from the women, the WBGT monitor was placed in the study participants’ households, specifically the living room, bedroom, and especially the kitchen, to measure heat exposure. The WBGT measurements were measured between 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. and made at least 3–4 measurements to account for heat exposures over a day per woman at each house, which were then averaged and used for statistical analysis. If the pregnant woman is employed, we measured the WBGTs during her shift at work. WBGT measurements were recorded at each work location between 6:00 am and 5:00 pm, aligning with the usual shift timing in informal sectors. During this timeframe, two to three readings were taken across the entire shift and averaged to obtain daily mean values, resulting in approximately 15–20 measurements per day. The averaged WBGT values were then used to estimate the workers’ attributable heat exposure. We advised the participants to maintain a distance of 1.5 m from the instrument in order to reduce the quantity of radiant heat and temperature fluctuations.

A qualified industrial hygienist (author VV) evaluated the workload intensity (heavy or moderate) and classified each job type in accordance with the ACGIH screening limits for the corresponding work category. If the women are unemployed, they were categorised as moderate workload. We assessed the risk of heat stress exposures at each study visit to ascertain whether it exceeded or fell below the ACGIH threshold limit value (TLV) for WBGT.

Meteorological data for the entire gestational period, including ambient temperature and relative humidity (RH), were obtained for each study location using Climate CHIP (www.climatechip.org), which supplemented the on-site WBGT measurements. WBGT values from both Climate CHIP and direct on-site measurements were used to estimate the average heat stress exposure experienced by pregnant women during each trimester and across the full pregnancy duration. Since the proportion of exposure above the Threshold Limit Value (TLV) was comparable between both data sources, the directly measured WBGT values were selected for the final analysis. Participants were classified as “exposed” if the WBGT of less than 27.5 °C for a heavy workload and less than 28 °C for a moderate workload. If the WBGT was below the TLV, we classified participants as “less exposed”.

Physiological responses

We measured their height using the stadiometer, and their weight using the digital weighing scale (Gene model 5). We assessed the physiological heat strain indicators (HSI) such as the rise in core body temperature (CBT) using an infrared (Rossmax) thermometer and changes in urine specific gravity using a refractometer (ATC potable and clinical refractometer) [20], employing standard techniques [21]. The HSI of all recruited women was done three times during the study period, once during each trimester visit. Pre- and post-shift HSI measurements were done at their workplaces if the women were working or measured both morning and evening at their homes if they were unemployed. All these measurements were performed on the same day as the heat exposure measurements conducted.

Perception data on SHSS and the APOs

During recruitment (8–14 weeks)

Before initiating the study, we informed the participants about the risks and advantages of participating and obtained their informed consent to participate. We utilized the validated and modified High Occupational Temperature Health and Productivity Suppression (HOTHAPS) pregnancy questionnaire [22], to evaluate the participants’ health characteristics and previous gestational history. The comprehensive long questionnaire gathered information about (1) their perceptions on heat exposures self-reported heat strain symptoms (SHSS) including excessive sweating, tiredness/weakness, excessive thirst, cramps, headache, dizziness, and prickly heat, as well as urogenital issues such as burning sensations in the vulval area, itching, change in urine colour and volume, (2) any history of previous miscarriages, stillbirth, LBW, PTB, family monthly income, socioeconomic status (SES) using the Kuppusamy scale, geographic location, education, welfare facilities, ventilation status of the house and the workplace, primary health care access, and nutritional status (including details of fruit and vegetable consumption, iron/folic acid/calcium supplementation), and (3) the woman’s job designation; information on the working hours during pregnancy; and we obtained the residential address and contact number during the recruitment. We obtained the women’s antenatal health record from the participants or, alternatively, from the health center’s antenatal register to cross-refer any adverse outcomes. Trimester 2 (14–27 weeks): We administered a brief questionnaire during this period, which solely provided information on pregnancy outcomes, including spontaneous abortion and any congenital anomalies. The information from the second trimester anomaly scan report supplemented this short questionnaire. Trimester-3 (28 weeks-before delivery): We collected data on adverse outcomes, including preterm birth, stillbirth, and IUGR, using a brief questionnaire during the third trimester.

After Delivery: We administered a brief birth outcome questionnaire via telephone or in-person to collect the following data: birth weight, gender, season of birth (defined as “winter” (from September to January) or “summer” (from February to August), and the pregnancy’s outcome, including single live birth, ectopic birth, or stillbirth. The research team members reviewed each woman’s clinical discharge records to determine her pregnancy outcomes.

The case definitions and the outcomes ascertainment referred to for this study are mentioned in our previous study, such as miscarriage and spontaneous abortions. Stillbirth, Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR), Preterm Birth (PTB), LBW. We adhere to the World Health Organization’s guidelines, which define an APO as an event of preterm birth, stillbirth, or abortion [23–27].

Statistical analysis

We employed Microsoft Excel for entering and consolidating the data. We measured the heat stress (WBGT°C) as continuous data and classified it as a categorical variable according to ACGIH Guidelines and subsequently categorized it according to ACGIH Threshold Limit Value (TLV) guidelines to define exposure groups. Based on these criteria, participants were classified into heat-exposed (WBGT above TLV) and less-exposed (WBGT within TLV) groups, which largely corresponded to rural pregnant women (RPW) and urban pregnant women (UPW), respectively. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic, occupational, and environmental characteristics of the study participants. The Chi-square test was applied to compare categorical variables between rural and urban women, including categorized demographic and exposure-related factors. The dependent variables (APOs) are classified based on the presence or absence of outcomes like miscarriages, PTB, stillbirth, IUGR, LBW, birth defects. The ABOs are present or absent of outcomes like PTB, stillbirth, IUGR, LBW, and birth abnormalities. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the relatively low occurrence of individual outcomes, the use of composite outcome variables was considered methodologically appropriate to enhance analytical robustness without substantially altering the interpretation of individual outcomes.

Univariate analyses were conducted to assess the association between each exposure variable and the outcomes. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Variables associated with the outcomes at p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in multivariable modelling. To control for potential confounding, stepwise binary logistic regression analysis was performed. The general form of the model was: logit (P) = ln [P / (1 − P)] = β₀ + β₁(Heat Exposure) + β₂(Age) + β₃(Education) + β₄(Consanguinity) + β₅(SES) + β₆(Occupation) + β₇(BMI) + β₈(Anaemia); where P represents the probability of the outcome (presence of SHSS, HSI, APOs, or ABOs). Two adjusted models were developed: Multivariate Model A, adjusted for significant confounders such as age at recruitment, consanguinity, socioeconomic status (SES), and occupation; and Multivariate Model B, adjusted for all significant and non siginificant confounders such as age, education, consanguinity, SES, occupation, body mass index (BMI), and anaemia status base upon the proven confounders. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CIs were reported to identify independent predictors of APOs and ABOs. We conducted the statistical analysis using the R software 4.0.4 version and the 22.0 version of the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS).

Results

Demographic characteristics

In total, we recruited 797 participants. For a variety of unspecified reasons, we were unable to contact 31 women, 43 women relocated; 9 women declined to participate after the second trimester; we were unable to contact 15 women; and we were unable to collect birth outcome data from 19 women. After identifying participants without missing data, we were able to access heat exposure and outcome data for 680 pregnant women.

We evaluated a total study population of 680 women, rural (N = 429, 63%), and urban (N = 251, 37%). Table 1 shows the descriptive characteristics of the study population among rural and urban groups. The median age is 23, with the range between 16 and 36 years for both groups. Almost 3/4th of the women completed at least primary education. Both groups had an equal number of consanguineal marriages. About 49% of women belong to the lower socio-economic group, and half of the women have had multiple pregnancies. Heavy workloads were significantly greater among rural N = 204 (47.6%) compared to N = 90 (36.0%) in urban areas (p < 0.003) (Table 1).

Perception on Self-Reported Heat Strain Symptoms (SHSS)

Pregnant women residing in rural areas were more likely to report symptoms of heat stress, such as excessive sweating, excessive thirst, headache, dizziness, prickly heat, and fatigue (84%), compared to those in the UPW (71%; Table 2; Fig. 2). These SHSS were particularly pronounced during peak hot months (April to June), when outdoor temperatures and humidity levels were at their highest (29.7 ± 0.8 °C). The statistical results showed that RPWs had a 2-fold higher risk of SHSS compared to the UPWs (95% CI: 1.3–3.4). Table 3 illustrates the comparison of the heat exposed RPW and the UPWs (bivariate and multivariate). The heat exposed RPWs also experienced a substantially increased risk of dehydration (45%) and urogenital issues (77%), including vulval itching, a burning sensation, and changes in urine volume and color. The RPWs had a 2.4-fold higher likelihood of reporting symptoms of dehydration than UPWs after accounting for all confounders, and the results of this study unequivocally demonstrate that RPWs have a higher vulnerability to heat.

Physiological responses

The prevalence of measured HSI results suggests that RPWs experience higher physiological heat strain than UPWs (Fig. 3). Table 2 reveals a significant difference in physiological HSIs, such as CBT and urine specific gravity, a proxy of dehydration, between women living in rural areas and then those in urban cities. Compared to UPWs, RPWs were more likely to experience adverse HSIs (31%) and a rise in CBT (25%), and the risk was 2-fold higher than the UPWs (95% CI: 1.6–4.1). RPWs also suffered from dehydration, as evidenced by higher cross-day USG values (95% CI: 1.2–2.9) (Table 2). Even after taking into account all possible confounders, the measured HSI rate was much higher among RPWs who had been exposed to heat (AOR: 3.1; 95% CI: 1.8–5.1) (Table 3). Among the HSIs shown in Table 3, the risk of an increase in CBT (COR: 1.5; 95% CI: 1.5–3.2) and urine specific gravity (COR: 2.5; 95% CI: 1.5–4.1) was much higher for the RPWs who were exposed to heat.

Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes (APOs)

The study found that RPWs had a nearly three times higher risk of APOs (miscarriages, PTB, stillbirth, IUGR, LBW, and birth defects) than UPWs (AOR, 2.7; 95% CI: 1.7–4.7), even when factors like age, education, SES, occupation, Hb, and BMI were taken into account. RPW once again represented a higher risk of ABOs, including LBW, PTB, stillbirth, IUGR, and birth defects, relative to their urban counterparts (AOR, 2.3; 95% CI: 1.2–4.3). The stratified analysis clearly demonstrates that women from the rural heat-exposed group are at substantially higher risk across self-reported symptoms (AOR: 2.9; 95% CI: 1.4–5.7), measured physiological HSIs (AOR: 3.1; 95% CI: 1.8–5.1). Furthermore, despite accounting for all confounders, the prevalence of APOs (AOR: 2.5; 95% CI: 1.2–5.2) and ABOs (AOR: 3.2; 95% CI: 1.3–7.4) was significantly higher among heat-exposed RPWs (Table 3). One of the most concerning findings was the almost four-fold higher incidence of miscarriages among the heat-exposed RPW compared to UPW (AOR: 4.1; 95% CI: 1.1–16.5). These findings emphasize that exposure above the TLV in rural settings is strongly associated with increased maternal heat strain and poorer pregnancy and birth outcomes compared with the urban less-exposed group.

Discussion

The aim of this study is to investigate the disparities between the RPW and the UPW when they are exposed to elevated ambient heat stress in Tamil Nadu. Notably, rural women not only experienced higher levels of heat exposure, but they also had a greater incidence of adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes. These results underscore the vulnerability of rural populations to climate-related health risks, particularly during pregnancy, and call for targeted interventions to mitigate the impact of heat stress in these communities.

Heat exposure profile

This study observed that RPW temperatures ranged from 25.2 °C to 30.2 °C, whereas UPW temperatures varied between 25.7 °C and 29.7 °C. The RPW demonstrated WBGT values that exceeded the acceptable limit by 55%, in contrast to their urban counterparts. In addition to their exposure to heat, RPW are more susceptible to heat stress due to a lack of shade and limited access to cooling infrastructure or ventilated housing, thereby exacerbating their risks, notably the growing duration individuals spend outdoors without access to amenities like sheltered or air-conditioned environments [28]. This results from the heightened dependence of low-socioeconomic women on the informal economy for family support [29]. Previous studies in India indicate that this type of exposure among women elevates their risk if not addressed [18].

Excessive heat exposure during the first trimester poses a risk of congenital birth defects, with an incidence rate of 36.4%, due to the occurrence of organogenesis in this period. Certain instances lead to a spontaneous abortion during the early stages of pregnancy. IUGR represents a negative consequence of increased heat exposure during the middle to late stages of pregnancy, as evidenced by an international study [13]. During the third trimester, the average WBGT exposure for RPWs ranged from 22.0 to 29.8 °C, with 51.2% of women exceeding the threshold limit for heat exposure. This indicates that the women experienced increased temperatures during their pregnancy. Preeclampsia, stillbirth, and various other complications are associated with this [30], linked PTB and LBW to increased ambient temperatures, although the exact pathophysiological mechanisms in humans remain unclear. Consequently, further research is necessary, encompassing more comprehensive investigations of the impacts on maternal, fetal, and placental functions [31].

Conversely, urban women may face a lower risk compared to rural poor women due to their tendency to remain indoors in shaded or air-conditioned environments, which may mitigate their exposure to heat despite elevated urban temperatures. This disparity reflects broader socioeconomic inequalities that affect health outcomes among rural and urban populations, especially for pregnant women engaged in demanding occupations during pregnancy [32].

Self-reported heat strain symptoms (SHSS)

RPW reported a notably increased incidence of SHSS (84%), encompassing excessive thirst, dizziness, headache, fatigue, as well as dehydration symptoms (40%) and urogenital complications (72%). The symptoms are likely attributable to extended exposure to elevated temperatures and direct sunlight [21], (Table 2). This suggests that RPW may be more susceptible to SHSS during pregnancy, which could lead to dehydration and increase the risk of HRI and HSI, especially in those exposed to elevated ambient temperatures. The RPW that was exposed to heat had a higher risk of SHSS and dehydration compared to the UPW (aOR 3.4; 95% CI 2.1–5.3), which suggests that heat exposure is a stressor on the body. A majority of heat-exposed RPW (77%), possibly associated with APOs, exhibited urogenital symptoms.

Urban environments intensify heat stress; however, enhanced access to health services, water, and air-conditioned spaces can provide significant benefits for women in alleviating its effects. As a result, 71% of UPWs reported SHSS, highlighting that heat exposure remains a significant issue in both rural and urban settings. However, this only applies in the former context, where limited resources and few heat adaptation strategies are in place.

Heat Strain Indicators (HSIs)

Physiological heat strain responses, such as urine specific gravity and core body temperature (CBT), further demonstrate the increased vulnerability of RPW. The higher number of CBT results suggests that heat-exposed RPW had a harder time keeping their internal temperatures stable and their water levels high. For example, 30% of RPWs had high HSIs, while only 22% of UPWs did. Heat-exposed RPW exhibited increased susceptibility to dehydration, likely due to insufficient water intake during heat exposure, as evidenced by elevated USG values [33]. RPW’s heightened physiological stress highlights the cumulative impact of prolonged heat exposure and restricted access to healthcare, water, and cooling resources. Maternal physiological strain during pregnancy can intensify fetal heat strain, potentially hinder fetal development, and elevate the risk of ABOs [31, 34]. Therefore, future studies should examine blood diversion from the placenta to the skin at core temperatures below those previously recognized as teratogenic [35]. In contrast to the UPW, despite exposure to comparable WBGT levels, they had superior access to water and healthcare facilities, which may account for their lower rates of increased HSIs. RPW reported a notably elevated incidence of SHSS (84%), encompassing excessive thirst, dizziness, headache, fatigue, along with dehydration symptoms (40%) and urogenital complications (72%). The symptoms are likely attributable to extended exposure to elevated temperatures and direct sunlight (Table 2). This suggests that RPW may be more susceptible to SHSS during pregnancy, which could lead to dehydration. This, in turn, increases the risk of HRI and HSI, especially in those who are exposed to elevated ambient temperatures. The RPW that was exposed to heat had a higher risk of SHSS and dehydration compared to the UPW (aOR 3.4; 95% CI 2.1–5.3), which suggests that heat exposure is a stressor on the body. A majority of heat-exposed RPW (77%), possibly associated with APOs, exhibited urogenital symptoms.

Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes (APOs)

The study found that RPWs who were exposed to WBGT levels above the threshold levels had a higher rate of APOs like miscarriages, PTB, LBW, IUGR, birth defects, and stillbirths compared to UPWs. This finding aligns with previous observations in different contexts [30]. After taking into account possible confounders, rural women who were exposed to heat were almost twice as likely (AOR: 2.5; 95% CI: 1.2–5.2) to have bad pregnancy outcomes as their urban counterparts. This is in line with what other research has found [10]. These findings are consistent with the physiological strain heat exposure causes, as maternal dehydration and hyperthermia can impede foetal growth and development [36]. The stratified logistic regression analysis indicated that miscarriages occurred four times more frequently in heat-exposed RPW (95% CI: 1.1–16.5). The heat-exposed RPW report indicates a threefold increase in the incidence of ABOs relative to urban women, yielding a risk of 3.6 (95% CI: 1.3–7.4). This finding is of significant concern. Women in The Gambia experienced WBGT exposures exceeding TLVs throughout all trimesters, according to a recent observational cohort study. The study linked HSI during pregnancy to an embryonic heat strain, characterized by an increased fetal heart rate [31]. We do not yet understand the exact mechanism through which this foetal strain may elevate the risk of APO. Research has demonstrated a correlation between heat exposure during pregnancy and reduced gestational periods, as well as an increased risk of preterm labor [10, 11]. The increased risk of PTB aligns with this finding. Delayed diagnosis and inadequate healthcare access may exacerbate the risk of pregnancy complications for rural women [11]. Rural women often experience lower socioeconomic status, limited education, and restricted access to clean drinking water and the healthcare access especially during emergency situations. Additionally, inadequate infrastructure, including access to electricity and cooling devices, along with the physical demands of outdoor occupations like farming, contribute to their vulnerability. The absence of cooling mechanisms, such as fans or air conditioners, likely exacerbates dehydration rates, further increasing their susceptibility to heat-related risks. This was among the outcomes that were observed.

The Indian Ministry of Health and Family Welfare assists over 12 million impoverished pregnant women annually. The relationship between APOs and extreme heat waves in Indian women remains unexplored by researchers. Women form the foundation of unorganised sectors in India [37], and pregnant outdoor workers face a significant concern regarding heat protection, given that a 2.5 °C increase in global temperatures could potentially expose 1 billion people to severe heat stress unless we prioritise adaptation strategies [38].

This outcome highlights the substantial impact of heat stress on pregnancy viability, especially in environments where women experience prolonged outdoor heat without effective heat mitigation measures. The increased risk of APOs and ABOs among reproductive-aged women underscores the need for targeted interventions and specific action plans to reduce complications related to heat exposure during pregnancy. One limitation of this study is that it relied on self-reported data for heat strain symptoms, which may be subject to recall bias or underreporting, particularly among rural populations where awareness and health literacy may vary. Moreover, the heat profile of each woman was evaluated using WBGT measurements only twice daily; however, we were unable to capture the entire day’s exposure. This will be addressed in our future projects. The APOs observed may have been influenced by poor nutrition, genetic anomalies, and unmeasured environmental exposures including air pollution. Before deriving conclusions, we considered confounding variables such as BMI and medical history. The gold standard for core temperature, rectal temperature, and telemetry tablets which is not feasible in the field setup. Therefore, we employed a method that is acknowledged as being less reliable, which is the monitoring of variations in CBT using the temperature of the tympanic membrane. To resolve the issue, we measured the tympanic temperature three to five times and computed the average to guarantee the data’s reliability. We were unable to evaluate the impact of occupational heat exposure on early miscarriages prior to this gestational age, despite the fact that we report data on miscarriage. This is due to the fact that women were recruited between 8 and 14 weeks of gestation. All other data has been verified by institutional records, with the exception of a small number of women who reported abortions prior to seeking formal care in the health sector. Furthermore, the study was confined to six districts in Tamil Nadu, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions with different environmental or socioeconomic conditions.

Conclusion

The results of this study underscore the substantial influence of heat exposure on pregnant women in rural and urban Tamil Nadu. Elevated heat stress increases the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) and adverse birth outcomes (ABOs). These inequities are anticipated to exacerbate as climate change intensifies heat waves and global temperatures rise, with vulnerable populations, notably pregnant women in rural and low-resource settings, facing the most significant challenges. This investigation emphasises the urgent necessity of protecting pregnant women from the adverse consequences of heat exposure. The burden of APOs and ABOs can be significantly alleviated through strategies such as enhanced awareness of heat-related risks, targeted interventions to minimise exposure, and improved access to healthcare. Integration of heat-risk screening into routine antenatal care, especially during summer months, is essential. Public health systems should implement heat mitigation strategies such as ensuring access to safe drinking water, shaded rest areas, modified work-rest cycles for pregnant workers, and community-level heat awareness programs. Strengthening PHCs to recognize and manage heat strain symptoms, along with timely referral mechanisms, is equally important. Occupational guidelines for pregnant women working in high-heat environments should be developed in line with WBGT/ACGIH recommendations. Furthermore, longitudinal cohort studies are indispensable for the identification of location-specific risk factors and the development of customised protective measures for both urban and rural settings. It is essential to take immediate action in order to develop and execute targeted interventions and action plans that prioritise the health and well-being of millions of women and their anticipating offspring. Not only will this critical issue safeguard maternal and foetal health, but it will also represent a crucial stride towards the establishment of climate-resilient healthcare systems.
