Is equitable access to health and social care possible for Vietnamese older adults? Care providers’ perspectives
Authors: Man Thi Hue Vo, Keiko Nakamura, Dai Tri Han Tran, T J Robinson T Moncatar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite growing initiatives to support older adults, disparities in accessing health and social care services persist in developing countries such as Vietnam. Insufficient evidence exists on how to improve equitable access to these services. This study aimed to determine care workers’ perceptions of disparities in service accessibility and potential solutions in facilitating equal access for older adults.MethodsA qualitative study included 27 focus group discussions and in-depth interviews conducted using a semi-structured questionnaire with 171 health and social care workers in Thua Thien Hue Province, Vietnam in 2019. Inductive thematic analysis was used to identify the perceived disparities and solutions.ResultsThree main themes emerged concerning these disparities: socio-economic and geographic barriers, cultural stigma and access gaps for disadvantaged communities. To facilitate equitable access, it was perceived crucial to enhance social protection coverage and benefits, empower community-based care and localized programs and implement targeted interventions.ConclusionsThe findings indicate persistent disparities. However, effective implementation of social health protection, fostering community engagement and targeted interventions can be beneficial in narrowing these gaps. The findings suggest that achieving equitable access for Vietnam's aging population is within reach through a more comprehensive social protection system.Keywords: equality, healthcare access, health disparities, older adults, social care access, solution

Introduction

Vietnam, a middle-income country, is aging faster than many in the region. Since 2007, Vietnam has entered a ‘golden population structure’, with one dependent on every two or more working-age individuals,1 presenting an important opportunity to boost productivity and leverage economic development. However, this opportunity comes with several challenges. A sharp decline in fertility, following the Two-Child Policy in 1988, has led to rapid aging, shrinking the workforce and straining health and social systems. Fertility dropped from 3.80 children per woman in 1989 to 2.09 in 2019.2,3 Vietnam is projected to transition from an aging to an aged society by 2036—faster than Thailand, Lao PDR, the Philippines, Malaysia and even Japan.4 As of 2024, adults ≥60 y of age make up about 14% of the population, the third highest in Association of Southeast Asian Nations after Singapore and Thailand, despite Vietnam's lower economic level.5

In Vietnam, the long-term care system is still in its infancy and not yet an option for everyone. However, the government has committed to addressing this pressing issue by coordinating efforts across various ministries and civil society organizations.6 The Vietnam Elderly Law,7 established in 2009, sets out the rights and responsibilities of the society, government, older adults and families. Additionally, the National Action Plan for Older People (2012–2020)8 and related decrees9 aimed to provide comprehensive care, including health, social and spiritual support, through both community-based and institutional care models.10 Most recently, in 2024, an amendment to Vietnam's Social Insurance Law marked a significant step towards universal coverage, enhancing benefits for older workers and expanding social protection to more vulnerable groups.11

Despite recent efforts, Vietnam's current policies may fall short of ensuring a smooth transition to an aging society without a comprehensive long-term plan. This gap risks deepening inequalities and leaving many older adults without adequate support. In the absence of a formal long-term care system, care largely relies on household budgets and personal savings. While >91% of older adults have health insurance, fewer than one-third receive care for non-overnight illnesses or accidents.12 Informal caregivers face significant psychological and physical burdens, including poor mental health and life dissatisfaction.13 Depression is widespread, particularly among women, rural residents and the poorest.14 Moreover, women have much lower employment rates than men across all age groups, despite similar mortality rates,15 pointing to a persistent gender gap.

While aging research in Vietnam is expanding, significant gaps remain in understanding health disparities and unequal access to care. The healthcare financing system exposes many households, especially those with older members in rural areas, to financial hardship due to high out-of-pocket costs.16 Although national health insurance has broadened, it still offers limited financial protection, and many older adults rely on support from their children for medical expenses.17 Wealthier urban populations often use voluntary insurance, linked to greater outpatient use and out-of-pocket spending, while those with compulsory insurance face lower costs but still experience service quality gaps.18 Ethnic minorities and rural populations face the greatest barriers, with lower service use and higher rates of catastrophic spending, even when insured.19 Geographic and income disparities further limit access to quality care.20 As Vietnam develops a formal long-term care system, insights from frontline geriatric care providers are essential to inform effective, practical policies.

This study aimed to explore the perceived disparities in accessing health and social care for older adults in Vietnam and simultaneously identify potential solutions for equal access from the perspective of health and social care professionals.

Methods

Study design

To explore perceived disparities in various care settings, a qualitative case study approach21 grounded in social constructivism theory22 was selected to explore perceived disparities in various care settings. This theory suggests that individuals’ knowledge and understanding are influenced by interactions within cultural and social environments, making it relevant for examining how health and social care workers perceive healthcare disparities.

Sampling and participant recruitment

A purposive sampling strategy was used. The research team, in collaboration with local authorities, the Provincial Department of Health and the local university, obtained formal approval and selected major care facilities to ensure a diverse sample and capture varied perspectives. Further information on participant recruitment is described in the Supplementary File. In total, 26 focus group discussions and one in-depth interview were conducted, involving 171 participants, distributed according to urban/rural area and health system hierarchy, as shown in Table 1 and Figure 1.

Study area

The study was conducted in Thua Thien Hue Province, part of Vietnam's North Central Coast region, which had the country's second-highest proportion of older adults in 2019 (13.04%).23 In 2023, the province had a population of 1 166 547, with 52.8% in urban areas and a growth rate (0.54%) below the national average.24 Seven communes are classified as poor, with >12% of households lacking basic social services.25,26

All major care facilities were selected for the study, including 5 public hospitals, 29 community health centres (CHCs) and 1 nursing home. Details are provided in the Supplementary File.

Data collection procedure

The research team included two Vietnamese interviewers and a study supervisor from Japan. All interviewers were local and trained in community surveys, qualitative research, ethics, interview techniques and transcription. Interviews were conducted in participants’ workplaces to reduce anxiety and encourage openness, using private rooms with only the interviewers and participants present. Each session lasted up to 60 min. Open-ended questions and probes to explore barriers and solutions were also included. Sessions were structured to balance participation, and thematic saturation was confirmed. Details on the data collection process are provided in the Supplementary File.

Data analysis

Two researchers transcribed and translated the interviews from Vietnamese to English, and the data were analysed using NVivo 12 Plus (Lumivero, Denver, CO, USA). Coding was independently conducted by two researchers, with discrepancies resolved through consensus and peer debriefing, following Braun and Clarke's six-phase framework.27 Themes were refined through ongoing team discussions until analytical saturation was achieved,28 with selected quotations used to illustrate key points. Data were securely stored at the Institute of Science, Tokyo, Japan. Additional details are provided in the Supplementary File.

Disparities in health and social care accessibility

Socio-economic and geographical barriers driving health disparities

Health and social care workers highlighted disparities driven by factors such as socio-economic status, gender, social capital and geographical location. In rural and remote areas, as well as urban locations, older adults often delay or avoid medical visits due to financial constraints and transportation challenges, leaving private clinics as their only accessible option.

They (older adults) sometimes have to use private clinics because traveling to central hospitals is too far.

— Community health worker, urban CHC

Due to their conditions, they rarely visit for regular check-ups and only seek medical help when severely ill. Their health knowledge is limited, and they depend on their children. In here, very few have pensions, with 80–90% being dependent. The poverty rate in this district is high.

— Nurse, rural district hospital

Older women, who generally live longer than men, are vulnerable to age-related diseases, especially in rural areas where access to healthcare is limited. However, longer lifespan does not equal better health outcomes.

In the internal medicine department, about two-thirds of the patients are women. Elderly women tend to live longer than men, likely because they consume less alcohol and tobacco.

— Nurse, rural district hospital

Lack of social engagement is another challenge. Many older adults in rural areas have few opportunities for entertainment.

In Vietnam, entertainment options and clubs for the elderly are lacking. Many elders spend their days doing housework and have limited access to various aspects of life, especially in remote areas like this district.

— Doctor, rural district hospital

Cultural stigma impacting institutional care utilization

Informants noted that cultural stigma persists against institutional care homes, often viewed as a form of abandonment by families, contrary to traditional Vietnamese family values. This belief comes from deep-rooted Asian traditions that emphasize filial piety. As a result, many older adults remain in home settings where care may be inadequate.

Nursing homes are still social stigma here because they are associated with abandonment.

— Nurse, provincial hospital

Access gaps for remote areas, informal workers and boat communities

Limited service access for older adults and informal workers, especially in remote areas, remains a concern. Similar issues remain in urban areas, where resettlement efforts for vulnerable populations, such as those previously living on boats, have taken place. Despite these relocations to land, comprehensive policies targeting these groups are still lacking. Many lack stable jobs, forcing them to depend on their children's support. Even when health insurance is available, it often requires family-based enrolment.

Only pensioners have social health insurance, while individuals aged 80 and above receive free health insurance. However, for the rest, their access to health insurance relies on whether their children can afford to purchase it for them.

— Community health worker, urban CHC

Residents relocated from the outskirts of the city, many of whom live on boats, do not have jobs as they age. They have many children, and those under 80 years old lack health insurance for check-ups. Insurance requires family enrolment, but in the resettlement area, families are large and cannot afford it.

— Community health worker, urban CHC

Potential solutions by health and social care workers

Strengthening social protection coverage and benefits

Most participants agreed that a key solution to addressing disparities was strengthening social protection coverage and benefits, including expanding the coverage of Social Health Insurance (SHI) and Social Assistance (SA) and enhancing the SHI package to include services such as home care and home visits. Participants also highlighted gaps in the current insurance system, particularly in its restricted usability across levels.

Older adults hope that their insurance can be accepted at all levels of hospitals and health centres, from low to high. This would make it more convenient for health examinations, as currently, passing through any level is not permitted.

— Community health worker, urban CHC

Participants suggested that including home care in health insurance could relieve the burden on older adults. However, they acknowledged that implementation remains a challenge. Policy changes would need to occur at higher governmental levels.

The idea of health insurance covering rehabilitation services at a patient's home is sensible, but implementation is challenging. Change needs to originate from higher authorities.

— Patient, university hospital

Optimism about utilizing social insurance to cover nursing home costs was recorded, although affordability and cultural stigma remain obstacles to its broader adoption.

I believe nursing homes could become viable in the future. Presently, a lot of people voluntarily contribute to social insurance, which could potentially be utilized to cover nursing home expenses upon reaching retirement age.

—Patient, provincial hospital

If insurance covered home care, doctors could bring medication to patients, reducing the burden on older adults…This model is the most reasonable, but we must await health insurance policy adjustments.

— Nurse, rural district hospital

In the future, when society is more open, nursing homes might be accessible for everyone. But some can't afford them, even if they really need them. The old way of thinking (stigma) might change over time. I’ve seen cases where money, not tradition, is the reason…

— Social worker, nursing home

Empowering community-based care and localized programs

Localized programs are seen as essential due to the difficulty older adults face with long-distance travel and the challenges of managing community-based services in large areas. Some participants suggested educating family members to ease the burden on providers, enabling families to offer consistent home-based support alongside professional care.

Local programs within the ward are crucial, as long-distance travel poses challenges…When health check-ups are conducted locally, participation rates are higher.

— Patient, provincial hospital

Doctors are tasked with managing the patient list for the community-based rehabilitation program, but it's challenging for them to conduct home visits due to the large area in the commune. Therefore, managing by unit is more feasible. The important focus now can be on educating family members.

— Doctor, university hospital

Incentives were emphasized by many community health workers (CHWs) in both urban and rural CHCs as critical for improving care. While many are motivated by dedication to their communities, low or non-existent salaries make long-term commitment difficult. Several participants noted that compensation is often insufficient to cover basic expenses, discouraging others from taking on these roles.

We engage in this work out of a sincere desire to help our community. However, our salaries are insufficient even for basic expenses like coffee or petrol. Despite this, volunteers have remained committed for many years…It's challenging to find replacements, as younger individuals are not inclined to take on this responsibility. Although community leaders receive a monthly salary, recruiting individuals for such positions remains difficult due to the lack of compensation.

— Community health worker, urban CHC

Targeted interventions for the most vulnerable

Focusing on the most vulnerable older adults was seen as a priority by many participants. They stressed the need to prioritize healthcare for the younger-old, as early interventions could help prevent more serious issues later. Participants also highlighted the unique challenges faced by socially isolated older adults and those with complex conditions.

Older adults who require attention are those living alone and those with health conditions. We need a model to care for the lonely, and those with illnesses have specific needs like nutrition, ulcer prevention, and palliative care, which are currently unavailable.

— Doctor, urban CHC

Discussion

Despite differences in background, participants shared concerns about access disparities and emphasized improving social protection, localized programs and targeted support to enhance equity in care.

Participants commonly perceived that inequalities in access to health and social care were shaped by social determinants, observed as an ongoing global challenge.29–31 For example, older adults in low-income households often delay seeking care due to financial constraints and out-of-pocket payments and risking untreated chronic illnesses. National statistics show significant inequities between affluent and poor areas in the distribution of health workers and primary care services.32 Gender also plays a role, with older women facing greater financial insecurity due to unpaid caregiving or informal work. Studies report that Vietnamese women have higher mental health distress and lower health-related quality of life, with 66.1% reporting anxiety or depression,33 highlighting the need for more gender-sensitive social protection policies.

Nursing homes in Vietnam remain stigmatized, largely due to cultural expectations that adult children care for their aging parents,34 with 98.1% expressing positive views on filial duty.12 However, promoting nursing homes as a complement to family care is increasingly important, especially for older adults with low social support. This reduces caregiver burden and offers professional care, particularly as more older adults live alone.10 Quality of life for Vietnamese older adults depends not only on care services, but also on family and community connections.35 Research on end-of-life care preferences among Vietnamese cancer patients has confirmed the importance of full information, symptom relief and loved ones’ presence.36 Cost is also a major concern, as many families struggle to afford quality nursing home care. These findings suggest that improving nursing home environments to address medical, emotional and social needs could shift public perceptions. Further research on affordability is needed to ensure financial barriers do not limit access to essential care.

Informal workers and boat communities affected by a resettlement project, specific to this province, were noted to face major challenges. In 2021, informal workers represented 68.5% of Vietnam’s total workforce.37 This trend is particularly evident among workers ≥60 y of age, where >90% are engaged in informal employment. Only 2.1% participated in voluntary social insurance,30 leaving many older adults without adequate social protection or long-term security. The resettled boat community38 was also highlighted as vulnerable. One study reported high rates of health issues among those living on boats, linked to poor socio-economic conditions.39 These health burdens existed even before relocation, likely due to poor sanitation, limited healthcare and unstable housing. After resettlement, support from local authorities was inadequate, with few employment options and training programs that did not match the specific needs of the community.

Participants emphasized the importance of expanding social protection both vertically and horizontally, enhancing social insurance to include home care and visits and extending coverage to better support vulnerable groups. This approach aligns with Vietnam's recent push toward universal social protection. In 2023, the Central Committee adopted a resolution to reform social policies through 2030, with a long-term vision to 2045. Progress has been noted, with the share of older adults receiving social insurance allowances and pensions rising from 42.8% in 2022 to 43.5% in 2023. However, >22% of people with disabilities are ≥60 y of age and about 5.1 million still do not receive cash disability benefits. Among those who do, social assistance covers only around 36% of the poverty line.40 Figure 2 highlights Vietnam's social protection system prior to the revision, showing the ‘missing middle’ at the time of this study. After extensive efforts by the Vietnamese government, Vietnam revised its Social Insurance Law in July 2024, with support from partners like the International Labour Organization.11 The revised law introduces a multitier pension system, lowers the age threshold for social pensions from ≥80 y to ≥75 y, integrates non-contributory pensions into law, eliminates lump-sum withdrawals and extends compulsory coverage to more worker groups. While this marks critical progress, the ‘missing middle’ remains, indicating the continued need for policies that further strengthen social protection to ensure coverage for all.

Participants highlighted that the disparity in socio-economic conditions, particularly in remote areas, is closely linked to the lack of community-based integrated activities. As Vietnam is approaching a significant demographic shift, the government has undertaken reforms to shift the focus of healthcare from higher-level hospitals to improving community-based health and social care. However, this transition has not been equally beneficial across all regions, with impoverished areas facing significant inequalities in terms of healthcare resources.32 For instance, a recent study utilizing national survey data found that 20.2% of older Vietnamese adults experienced depressive symptoms and recommended integrating depression screening into primary care settings to enable early identification and treatment.14 To improve access and health equity, resources must be localized and decentralized. Many CHCs lack the capacity for basic care, causing patients to bypass them and overcrowd higher-level hospitals.41 Hence, enhancing CHC capacity and ensuring programs reach older adults across all socio-economic levels is essential, which may involve expanding social health insurance to include community outreach, home visits for those with mobility limitations and simplified administrative procedures.

Most participating CHWs emphasized the need for better incentives. As their roles are largely voluntary, adequate compensation is essential to maintain the CHW network and motivate these vital workers. Vietnam can learn from Thailand, where CHWs receive training and payment for their services.42,43 A regional review found CHW performance improves with incentives, training, supervision, integration and clear roles.44 Applying these lessons, Vietnam could strengthen its CHW workforce and attract younger participants through structured support and sustainable grassroots care.

Finally, some participants emphasized prioritizing interventions for the most vulnerable. The government has reformed targeted programs to reduce disparities among ethnic minorities.45 It has also recognized the problem of isolation and promoted older people's associations,8 which enhance well-being through community activities. Another initiative, the Inter-Generational Self-Help Clubs (ISHCs) by HelpAge,46 encourages older adults’ active involvement and plays a key role in meeting community needs. Given Vietnam's current resources, focusing on clear target groups is a practical strategy. Instead of resource-intensive expansions, directing support to high-risk individuals ensures more efficient and effective interventions. This approach aligns with Vietnam's social protection principles,11 which emphasize need-based support for disadvantaged groups.

Although our findings may reflect the experiences of professionals in other provinces, generalizability is limited due to regional differences. Also, the study may not fully represent the views of other stakeholders, such as policymakers. However, as the primary providers of care, the participants’ perspectives are essential in amplifying practitioner voices. To minimize bias, focus group discussions grouped participants with similar backgrounds to encourage open discussion of shared issues. A non-intimidating setting and diverse care levels ensured a broad range of insights.

Conclusions

This study identified disparities in service accessibility shaped by cultural stigma, geographic and economic barriers, gender inequalities and structural gaps in healthcare delivery. Coordinated health and social sector efforts, particularly expanding social protection, localizing care and strengthening CHWs were perceived as key strategies to improve equity and reach vulnerable groups. These efforts are essential to support the growing aging population and leverage the country's current potential.
