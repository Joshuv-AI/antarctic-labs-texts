Responsible Detection and Mitigation of AI-Generated Text Using Hybrid Neural Networks and Feature Fusion: Toward Trustworthy Content Management in the Era of Large Language Models
Authors: Raed Alharthi, Stephen Ojo, Thomas I Nathaniel, Nagwan Abdel Samee, Muhammad Umer, Mona M Jamjoom, Shtwai Alsubai, Jawad Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe proliferation of AI-generated text, fueled by large language models (LLMs), presents pressing challenges in maintaining content authenticity, safeguarding academic integrity, and mitigating misinformation. This paper proposes a responsible detection and mitigation framework that leverages hybrid neural networks and multi-feature fusion to distinguish AI-generated text from human-authored content. The proposed model integrates BERT-based semantic embeddings, convolutional features via Text-CNN, and statistical descriptors into a unified representation. A CNN-BiLSTM architecture is employed to capture both local syntactic patterns and long-range semantic dependencies. The framework emphasizes responsible AI (RAI) by prioritizing interpretability and reducing bias in detection decisions. Extensive evaluations on a balanced benchmark dataset demonstrate the model’s superior performance, achieving 95.4% accuracy, 94.8% precision, 94.1% recall, and a 96.7% F1-score—outperforming leading transformer-based baselines. The proposed framework is also evaluated on the CoAID external independent dataset to show generalizability. This study contributes to the responsible deployment of LLMs by enhancing transparency and robustness in AI-generated content verification, paving the way for secure and ethical integration of generative models into content management systems.Keywords: AI-generated text, Deep fake detection, Natural language processing, Feature fusion, Misinformation detection, Responsible artificial intelligence (RAI), Large language models (LLM)

Artificial intelligence (AI) focuses on creating systems that use machine learning, natural language comprehension, and algorithm development. Together, these components handle several issues. AI was developed to respond promptly and precisely to difficult questions [1]. Its product applications range in many fields, especially in education, where its impact and adoption have exponentially increased. AI has made significant progress since its origin, and many tools have emerged over time, such as the popular ChatGPT (Chat Generative Pre-trained Transformer). ChatGPT, which is a language model created by OpenAI to understand and solve problems, can generate human prosody mimicry in dialogue [2]. AI has started to gain significance in many academic domains where relevant data are available to them by supporting researchers to probe into datasets, research articles, potential collaborators, and candidates [3]. Academic research about AI along with its integration continues to be studied by researchers. The data-trained ChatGPT permits users to obtain answers through questions, but users need to understand its constraints. The model’s understanding extends only to its training data, while it also produces incorrect results from time to time. OpenAI claims that ChatGPT could create incorrect information about facts, persons, or locations [4].

ChatGPT, along with other AI technologies, enables better learning through assistance with writing tasks and by teaching students to fact-check but still requires proper education support. More students prefer ChatGPT for quick solutions instead of pursuing genuine study activities. A variety of automated systems have led to the quick development of academic books along with other educational resources. Such unethical use of ChatGPT in academic work presents ethical dilemmas, but the users exclusively bear the responsibility instead of placing it on the technology itself [5].

Academic integrity is defined as respecting ideals, beliefs, and norms that encourage honesty in education [6, 7]. The educational system faces declining standards because students fail to maintain academic integrity. AI technology deterioration has made this problem worse as it decreases student motivation levels. Students stay away from conventional library visits because ChatGPT provides them instant answers in mere seconds. Modern AI systems demonstrated the ability to produce research papers along with assignments and articles which generates concern about academic integrity both among society and educators. Distinguishing between human-created information and AI-generated content has grown more difficult, posing a substantial challenge to long-standing academic ethics norms [8]. This trend weakens characteristics such as hard work, perseverance, and devotion, which characterize extraordinary thinkers and researchers who have made important discoveries through rigorous research.

Recently, various AI tools have acquired popularity for differentiating between human-written and AI-generated material, helping to confirm the validity of text in many applications. Researchers have developed some tools to identify AI-generated text in research papers, articles, and assignments. Turnitin, Copyleaks, GPTZero, OpenAI’s text classifiers [9], Content at Scale, Writer’s AI content detector, Hugging Face Detector, Originality AI Chrome Extension, GPT Radar, Copyscape, Plagibot, and Copyleaks are a few examples of these products [10]. Related Work

The rapid development of AI and natural language processing (NLP) has increased the use of AI-generated text, raising severe concerns about its misuse in areas such as deception, plagiarism, and automated content generation. As a result, identifying AI-generated text has emerged as a key research area and deep learning algorithms are playing a critical role in addressing this challenge. Recent research has explored several methods for distinguishing between human-written text and machine-generated text. These approaches include supervised learning models, transformer-based designs, and adversarial training. This section examines current research on recognizing AI-generated text. It focuses on key procedures, data sources, and methods for evaluating findings. It also highlights the gaps and flaws in existing approaches, indicating that additional study is required.

Two primary groups define the most often used AI-generated text detectors: the necessity of statistical approaches and model optimization from the current state. These two groupings cover a broad range of approaches whereby every one of them has special advantages and limits. Fine-tuning pre-trained models such as BERT and RoBERTa on specific datasets or tasks can improve their ability to distinguish between machine-generated and human-written text [12]. On the other hand, statistical models, such as DetectGPT [13] and GLTR [14], use statistical analysis and pattern recognition to identify anomalies or unique features in AI-generated content. Researchers have refined both approaches to meet the increasing demand for AI-generated text detection across various fields. Using watermarks in the texts produced by AI, Kirchenbauer et al. [15] effectively countermeasures the above-described hazards caused by huge language models. From as little as five tokens, computers might be able to identify some of these invisible-to-human-readers watermarks, therefore determining the information derived from AI. They specifically designed their framework for private language models, ensuring that the addition of watermarks does not degrade the text’s quality. It is important to note that the identification process comes from an open-source method that does not require access to the model design or its parameters.

Gao et al. [16] compared scientific abstracts generated by ChatGPT to actual abstracts from five medical publications. Their study examined 50 abstracts (10 from each publication) with an AI-generated text detector, a plagiarism checker, and blinded human reviewers. ChatGPT was used to generate additional abstracts on the same subjects as the selected ones. The examination sought to identify AI-generated abstracts from human-written ones. The AI output detector recognized 99% AI-generated abstracts, but the plagiarism detector detected original abstracts with 100% accuracy. However, examining a combination of original and AI-generated abstractions revealed inconsistent findings. Similarly, Weber-Wulff et al. [17] evaluated many detection algorithms to establish their efficacy in distinguishing AI-generated text from human-written content. Their research compared 12 freely accessible detection programs to two commercial systems, Turnitin and PlagiarismCheck. The examination concentrated on accuracy and error type analysis. The results showed that none of the instruments were completely dependable or exact. While their research raises key problems, relying entirely on past studies without independent verification undermines the credibility of their assertions. The emphasis should not be on the number of detection tools available but on establishing highly effective and reliable systems capable of properly differentiating AI-generated material from human-authored work.

Yongqiang et al. [18] compared the scientific material produced by AI and humans. They created a descriptive framework to identify AI-generated text from human-authored material. They also tested numerous publicly accessible tools to determine the differences between AI-generated and human-generated scientific writing. According to the findings, AI-generated material has limits and cannot compete with human-written work in terms of quality. Similarly, Elkhatat et al. [19] investigated the efficacy of AI detection methods in identifying AI-generated text from human-generated text. The researchers utilized ChatGPT 3.5 and ChatGPT 4 to develop 15 paragraphs related to "cooling towers in the engineering process," and they provided five human-written samples for reference. OpenAI served together with Writer GPTZero, Writer, Copyleaks, and Crossplag as the detection technologies for this evaluation. The detection methods established an accurate assessment of writings produced by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 while concurrently labeling some human-written content as AI-generated thus producing incorrect positive results. According to the research, developing novel detection approaches according to the development of advanced artificial intelligence models is significant. Finding GPT-3.5 content was easier for current detection systems than finding GPT-4 content because they had trouble recognizing text generated by GPT-4. The evaluation showed that Crossplag proved superior compared to examined tools but still had difficulty detecting material brought forth by GPT-4. The study stressed that detecting advanced AI-created content has grown more challenging and established its main goal focused on differentiating AI-produced language from human text.

A researcher [20] evaluated the performance of five AI detection tools: GPTZero, Copyleaks, OpenAI Text Classifier, Writer.Com, and Giant Language Model Test Roomin. The study investigated how successfully these techniques identified text generated by ChatGPT, YouChat, and Chatsonic. These chatbots responded to English language-related prompts. To further validate the performance, the chatbot-generated replies were translated into German, French, Spanish, Southern Sotho, and isiZulu using Google Translate. The researchers tested the translated content using GPTZero while simultaneously evaluating the Spanish, French and German portions with Copyleaks. The results showed that Copyleaks performed better than the other four technologies in identifying AI-generated material. However, when translated into several languages, it incorrectly classified human-written material as AI-generated. The study emphasized the limits of current AI identification methods, including their low performance when dealing with multilingual translations of AI-generated material.

Another study [21] examined zero-shot machine-generated text and examined how well detectors performed when presented with large language model (LLM) outputs that they had not been trained on. It is challenging for detection systems to identify text written like human writing due to the growing usage of LLMs. To train the detector, the study team gathered human-like text samples from a variety of LLM platforms. The study team tested the detectors’ ability to apply their acquired knowledge to previously unknown and brand-new generators. Each detector displayed an intriguing pattern despite its subpar overall cross-detection performance. The study showed that detectors trained on medium-sized LLM datasets were able to detect outputs from bigger models that they had never seen before.

DetectLLM [13] provides insightful analysis of the link between the design of LLMs and AI-generated text. According to the research, AI-generated text frequently shows up in downward sloping regions of the log-likelihood function of the language model. These results provide the basis for their approach to separating human-written content from AI-generated materials. Based on this assumption, the authors found a way to check for changes in text by figuring out how word replacements affect log-likelihood values. This approach lets one investigate closely how small text changes influence the probability estimates of the model. The detection approach benefits from alterations to content that produce a continuous rise in log-likelihood production. Developing trustworthy detection methods to differentiate human-written from machine-written work has become essential due to increasing concerns about plagiarism and false information propagation. DetectLLM introduces two new zero-shot detection approaches that use log-rank information for this purpose.

Researchers examined different detection methods of AI-generated text through fine-tuned transformer models as well as statistical techniques and adversarial training. The conducted research generated important findings, although major uncertainties persist regarding the performance quality of present detection systems. Some problems with research exist because AI detection systems do not always work well with different types of materials, languages, and AI models. The accuracy of detection methods can probably be enhanced when deep learning [22] approaches are integrated with current specialized research methods. The majority of detection systems fail to recognize text that AI models produce from training the detection models did not train on. Research must address a significant knowledge gap regarding the development of adaptive multilingual AI text detection systems that possess powerful capabilities. Dataset

The research uses 25,572 tweets from 40 accounts available in the TweepFake dataset according to Ref. [23]. TweepFake segmented the data into 17 human-operated accounts and 23 properly marked bot-operated accounts. The bot-generated tweets organize their text generation methods into three categories: GPT-2 (11 accounts), RNN (7 accounts), together with other techniques (5 accounts). The human-generated tweets were collected from 17 accounts, and there are a total of 12,786 tweets. This clear labeling allows for precise analysis and comparison between human and AI-generated content. Classification Model

After feature extraction, they are processed through a deep learning-based classification model to distinguish between AI-generated and human-written text. To enhance the effectiveness of feature integration, we employ a feature fusion mechanism that combines multiple sources of information. The extracted BERT-based word embeddings, convolutional features from Text-CNN, and statistical text features are concatenated to form a comprehensive feature representation. To maintain contextual integrity, the preprocessing pipeline is designed in two stages. In the BERT branch, we did not use hard normalization, such as punctuation, casing, and stopwords were not removed, and the text was still run through the tokenizer in its raw form and the context is preserved. Meanwhile, little preprocessing (e.g., lowercasing, removal of stopwords) is only done to the statistical and CNN feature extraction trees, in which such cleaning enhances representation without adversely affecting semantics. In this way, the input given to BERT was raw and tokenized, as in the pretraining, whereas other branches enjoyed the advantage of task-specific normalization. This dual-track strategy ensured compatibility across feature types and prevented loss of contextual meaning in the BERT embeddings. Impact of Feature Fusion on Model Performance

Table 3 highlights the impact of different feature representations on the performance of the proposed CNN-BiLSTM for AI-generated text detection. The results show that using BERT-based word embeddings alone achieves a relatively high accuracy of 91.5%, indicating that contextualized word representations play a crucial role in distinguishing AI-generated and human-written text. However, convolutional features extracted from Text-CNN alone yield a slightly lower accuracy of 89.8%, suggesting that while CNNs capture local text patterns effectively, they lack the sequential context necessary for deeper understanding. On the other hand, statistical text features, which include properties such as word frequency distributions, sentence length, and punctuation usage, achieve the lowest accuracy of 86.4%, demonstrating that statistical patterns alone are insufficient for robust classification. When all three feature types are fused, the model achieves a significantly improved accuracy of 95.4%, with 94.8% precision, 94.1% Recall, 94.4% F1-score, and 96.7% AUC-ROC. This result validates the effectiveness of feature fusion, where combining contextual embeddings, local feature extraction, and statistical information creates a more comprehensive representation of text, leading to improved classification performance.

Discussion

The experimental findings show that the proposed CNN-BiLSTM with attention model is more successful than state-of-the-art transformer-based models such as RoBERTa, ELECTRA, DistilBERT, and ALBERT in identifying AI-generated text. Transformer models excel at capturing deep contextual linkages, but they may struggle with fine-grained language subtleties and local dependencies, which are critical for differentiating between human-authored and AI-generated material. In contrast, our hybrid approach efficiently incorporates BERT-based word embeddings, Text-CNN convolutional features, and statistical text characteristics, exploiting each representation type’s capabilities. The CNN layer detects local structural patterns in the text, while the BiLSTM layer represents long-term interdependence. The attention mechanism assigns varied significance to various phrases, improving model interpretability. The results show that while individual feature representations help with classification accuracy, feature fusion improves performance a lot by combining different parts of text representation. The proposed model achieves 95.4% accuracy, exceeding RoBERTa’s 93.1% and ELECTRA’s 92.7%, demonstrating its efficiency. Figure 4 presents the comparison of proposed hybrid model using feature extraction techniques. Furthermore, cross-validation results demonstrate the model’s generalizability, lowering the risk of overfitting. The proposed CNN-BiLSTM with attention model’s stability and robustness over several folds are shown in Fig. 5. The model successfully differentiates between AI-generated and human-written text, as evidenced by the consistently high accuracy across all fivefold. The results show how important multi-perspective feature extraction is for finding AI-generated text. They also suggest that more research be done on even more advanced fusion techniques, domain-specific fine-tuning, and adversarial training to make detection work better in real-world situations.

The proposed hybrid CNN–BiLSTM fusion model is implemented and trained on an NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU with 64 GB RAM. On the TweepFake dataset, average training time per epoch is approximately 6.8 min, with the full model converging within 20–25 epochs due to early stopping. Inference time for a single text sample is measured at 3.1 ms, enabling near real-time deployment in content moderation pipelines. Although the multi-branch fusion increases the number of trainable parameters compared to single-feature baselines, careful regularization and dropout ensured that training remained stable without excessive resource consumption. Furthermore, compared to heavier transformer-only baselines such as RoBERTa, the proposed framework demonstrated 14–18% lower GPU memory usage while achieving superior performance, highlighting its efficiency for practical applications.

In research, this approach can aid in the detection of AI-generated research articles, maintaining intellectual work integrity. Similarly, in journalism, it may be used to check the veracity of news stories, preventing the spread of false information. Moreover, companies handling content moderation might apply this method to filter false information or spam produced by AI. Moreover, the model can let experts in cybersecurity identify phishing emails and fake messages produced by artificial intelligence, thereby enhancing digital safety. Recognizing AI-assisted submissions helps the proposed system to evaluate student assignments and guarantee academic integrity in education. This model helps to develop more reliable AI-based systems and responsible AI use across industries by offering a high-accuracy and interpretable detection method.

Practical Implications

The proposed CNN–BiLSTM fusion framework demonstrates strong potential for real-world deployment. It does not require the use of transformers like transformer-based detectors, is compact and fast with an average inference latency of only 18 ms per input on a standard GPU, enabling near real-time detection. For resource-constrained platforms, techniques such as pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation can be applied to further reduce memory usage and computational demand. These features render the model applicable in large scale in the academic and journalism sector and also in cybersecurity where a fast and reliable detection is a pre-requisite.

External Validation on CoAID Dataset

To further assess the generalizability and robustness of the proposed framework, we performed external validation on the COVID-19 Healthcare Misinformation Dataset (CoAID) [33]. CoAID is a widely used benchmark dataset that contains both real and fake news articles and social media posts related to the COVID-19 pandemic. The dataset includes misinformation generated by bots and AI-driven accounts, making it highly relevant for testing AI-generated text detection frameworks in healthcare and misinformation domains.

In this study, we used a balanced subset of the dataset consisting of 6,420 records, with 3,360 labeled as real (class 0) and 3,060 labeled as fake (class 1). This distribution represents practical challenges in misinformation detection and enables the framework to learn discriminative features across classes. The proposed hybrid CNN–BiLSTM model with fused features (BERT embeddings, Text-CNN convolutional features, and statistical descriptors) was applied without modifications to this dataset. The results were consistent with those achieved on the TweepFake dataset, further reinforcing the framework’s adaptability. The model achieved an accuracy of 98.41%, F1-score of 98.34%, precision of 98.53%, and recall of 98.14%. The ROC–AUC score was also high at 0.986, indicating excellent discriminative capability between real and fake content.

The outcomes demonstrate that the model generalizes across domains, including healthcare misinformation, and that feature fusion improves robustness to diverse text patterns and noise. External validation shows that the framework can be used to identify AI-generated tweets and reduce healthcare misinformation, which can further support its use in trustful AI implementation.

Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presents a deep learning-based approach for identifying AI-generated text using three distinct feature types: statistical text characteristics, BERT-based word embeddings, and convolutional features from Text-CNN. Combining these factors utilizing a fusion strategy and a CNN-BiLSTM hybrid classifier helps our proposed method to outperform previous models in differentiating between AI-generated content and human-authored literature. Confirmed with a fivefold cross-validation approach, the experimental results reveal that our model beats top transformer-based baselines such as ALBERT, ELECTRA, DistilBERT, and RoBERTa. The findings show that various feature types have a considerable impact on detection accuracy, with BERT embeddings preserving contextual semantics, Text-CNN extracting local patterns, and statistical features giving further discriminative ability. However, their combination via feature fusion yields the maximum classification accuracy, highlighting the significance of multi-feature integration in AI-generated text recognition. Although encouraging results have been attained, especially in managing adversarially produced language closely resembling human writing. In the future, researchers may look into better fusion algorithms, add attention-based processes for better refinement, and increase the size of the dataset to include more types of text generated by AI. Using AI text detection models in the fields of academia, journalism, and cybersecurity can greatly lower the risks of AI-generated fake news, plagiarism, and automated content abuse. This study makes progress in AI-generated text detection by showing a complex deep learning model with lots of features that make classification more accurate and cuts down on false positives. As AI language models get better, detection methods will need to keep getting better to keep the authenticity of content made by humans in digital spaces. While the proposed feature fusion approach has shown strong results, future work could explore more advanced attention-based fusion strategies to dynamically weight different feature types, as well as adversarial training to improve robustness against intentionally obfuscated AI-generated text. These directions would further strengthen the adaptability and resilience of the framework in real-world applications.
