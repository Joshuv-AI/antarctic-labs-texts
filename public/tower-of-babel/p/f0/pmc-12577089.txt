Community pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and counseling practices regarding muscle-building supplements in Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohammed M. Aldurdunji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMuscle-building supplements (MBS) are increasingly used by athletes and young adults to enhance muscle mass, recovery, and performance. However, inappropriate use and misinformation pose significant health risks. Community pharmacists are well-positioned to provide guidance, yet their readiness in this domain remains underexplored in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted among licensed community pharmacists across Saudi Arabia. A structured, self-administered questionnaire assessed knowledge, attitudes, counseling practices, perceived barriers, and training needs related to MBS. Descriptive statistics, linear regression, and ordinal logistic regression were used to analyze factors associated with knowledge, attitudes, and counseling frequency. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsA total of 708 pharmacists participated. Higher knowledge scores were associated with male gender, age 26–35, over 10 years of experience, formal training, and frequent supplement-related inquiries. Positive attitudes were linked to male gender, mid-level experience, higher knowledge scores, and frequent customer questions, while formal training was paradoxically associated with slightly lower attitude scores. Counseling frequency was predicted by male gender, age 36–45, and frequent inquiries. Over half of participants supported MBS-related training (54.8%), curriculum integration (55.2%), and standardized counseling guidelines (54.4%).ConclusionCommunity pharmacists in Saudi Arabia demonstrate varied levels of knowledge and counseling behavior regarding MBS, influenced by demographic factors, training, and customer demand. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational initiatives and national guidance to optimize pharmacists’ role in ensuring the safe use of muscle-building supplements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25084-y.

Introduction

The global demand for muscle-building supplements (MBS) has risen substantially in recent years, reflecting increased engagement in resistance training, heightened health awareness, and pervasive exposure to fitness content on digital platforms. MBS products, including protein powders, creatine monohydrate, and branched-chain amino acids, are widely consumed to enhance muscle mass, accelerate recovery, and improve performance outcomes [1]. In Saudi Arabia, surveys indicate that MBS use is particularly common among young male gym attendees, often motivated by aesthetic goals rather than clinical need [2]. The sports nutrition industry is projected to surpass USD 78 billion by the end of the decade, driven by social media promotion, peer influence, and the perception that supplementation is integral to effective training regimens [3, 4]. This commercial growth has exceeded the pace of evidence-based guidance, underscoring the importance of healthcare professionals in supporting safe and rational use.

Although MBS are often perceived as safe, their unsupervised consumption carries significant health risks. Reported adverse effects include hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, cardiovascular complications, and endocrine disturbances [5, 6]. Moreover, undeclared pharmacologically active substances, such as anabolic steroids and stimulants, have been repeatedly identified in commercial products, raising concerns about adulteration and regulatory oversight [7]. These risks are compounded by consumer reliance on peer advice and online marketing rather than professional guidance, particularly among adolescents and young adults [8]. Harmful patterns of use, including poly-supplementation and reliance on unverified online sources, highlight the public health need for pharmacist intervention. In Saudi Arabia, food supplements are registered under the Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) before marketing; classification and labelling pathways are described in official guidance, while no pharmacy-specific counselling standard for sports or muscle-building supplements was identified in publicly available materials [9, 10].

Pharmacists are positioned as the most accessible healthcare professionals for individuals seeking advice on dietary and performance-enhancing products, yet multiple studies reveal deficiencies in their knowledge, confidence, and counseling practices. Evidence from Saudi Arabia, Europe, and neighboring countries shows that pharmacists frequently lack formal training in supplements, leading to low confidence in counseling and difficulty in distinguishing evidence-based products from those with unsupported claims [11–14]. Similar gaps have been observed with other supplement categories, including herbal products, where limited curricular coverage and continuing education hinder professional readiness [15–17]. Despite these challenges, pharmacists remain trusted sources of information, and their potential contributions extend to sports pharmacy settings, where they may advise on supplement safety and compliance with anti-doping guidelines [18, 19].

Within community pharmacy practice in Saudi Arabia, counselling has been described as variable and, at times, inadequate, with gaps linked to the absence of standardized training protocols and practical guidance [20–23]. Constraints commonly reported include time pressure, limited privacy, and perceived training insufficiency [21, 23]. These observations, together with the growing demand for sports-related supplementation, underline the need for clear, evidence-based counselling standards and targeted training for community pharmacists [24–26].

Despite these context-specific barriers, recent reviews confirm that inadequate training, lack of standardized guidance, and regulatory ambiguity constrain pharmacists’ engagement in supplement counselling [27, 28]. In Saudi Arabia, most available research has examined consumer use of dietary supplements, with less attention given to pharmacists’ preparedness to provide guidance on MBS specifically [29–36]. This gap is critical given the growing popularity of MBS and the risks associated with misuse, adulteration, and unsafe sourcing through unregulated online markets [7, 8].

In this context, the present study aimed to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes, and counseling practices of community pharmacists in Saudi Arabia regarding MBS. By identifying strengths and weaknesses in their professional readiness, the findings are expected to inform curricular development, continuing professional education, and public health strategies that strengthen pharmacists’ role as gatekeepers of safe supplement use.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted among community pharmacists in Saudi Arabia following ethical approval from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee at Umm Al-Qura University (Approval No. HAPO-02-K-012–2025-03–2592, dated 28 February 2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Data collection commenced immediately after approval and continued for approximately two months, Eligible participants included licensed pharmacists working in community pharmacy settings, regardless of gender, age, or years of experience. Recruitment was facilitated through professional pharmacy networks and large community pharmacy chains.

The minimum required sample size was calculated as 384 pharmacists, based on a 95% confidence interval, 5% margin of error, and an assumed prevalence of adequate knowledge of 50%. A total of 708 valid responses were collected during the study period and included in the final analysis. The survey system was configured to log only completed submissions. Incomplete or partially filled questionnaires were automatically excluded at the point of entry, ensuring that all analyzed responses represented fully completed surveys.

Instrument development and data collection

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was designed specifically for this study to address identified gaps in the literature and capture key aspects of pharmacists’ engagement with MBS. The questionnaire was developed in collaboration with clinical pharmacy faculty members and practitioners with expertise in sports pharmacology and community practice. Prior to the main survey, the questionnaire was piloted among 13 community pharmacists to assess clarity, content validity, and completion time. Feedback from the pilot resulted in minor wording adjustments to improve comprehensibility. Data from the pilot study were excluded from the final analysis. The final version included six main sections: demographic and professional information, knowledge of MBS, attitudes toward counselling, counselling practices, perceived barriers to effective counselling, and perceptions of educational needs. The survey combined multiple-choice, categorical, and Likert-scale items to facilitate both qualitative insights and quantitative analysis. The frequency of customer inquiries about supplements was categorized as: daily (≥ 5 times/week), several times/week (2–4 times/week), occasionally (< 1 time/week), or never. The full English-language version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Scoring methods

Knowledge was assessed using four items. Each correct answer was scored as 1 and incorrect answers as 0, yielding a total knowledge score ranging from 0 to 4. Attitudes were measured using four Likert-scale statements, each rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The sum of these responses constituted the attitude score, ranging from 4 to 20. Perceptions regarding training and education needs were assessed using three Likert-scale items, similarly, scored from 1 to 5, resulting in a total score range of 3 to 15. Higher scores indicated better knowledge, more favorable attitudes, and greater perceived need for training, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using RStudio (version 2024.9.1.394, Boston, MA, USA) with R version 4.4.2. Descriptive statistics were reported as counts and percentages for categorical variables. Knowledge and attitude scores were computed by summing item responses within each domain and analyzed using linear regression to identify associated factors. Counseling frequency was treated as an ordinal outcome and examined using ordinal logistic regression. Results were expressed as beta coefficients or odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and practice-related characteristics

The responses of 708 community pharmacists were included in the current study. Most of the participants were aged 26 to 35 years (42.1%), and two-thirds of respondents were male (67.0%). Regarding professional experience, 33.8% had worked as community pharmacists for 6 to 10 years and 27.7% for more than 10 years. Just over half of the participants (51.3%) reported having received formal training in dietary or sports supplements. In terms of customer interactions, 31.8% of pharmacists reported being asked about athletic or MBS daily, 30.9% a few times per week, 22.5% rarely, and 14.8% never (Table 1).

Knowledge assessment

When asked about the primary function of creatine supplementation, 52.4% correctly identified it as increasing muscle energy and strength. Regarding the potential harms of MBS, only 25.1% correctly identified anabolic steroids as being associated with liver and kidney damage. The statement that excessive protein intake beyond recommended levels will significantly enhance muscle growth was correctly identified as false by 61.9% of respondents. For the commonly used pre-workout stimulant, 26.8% correctly selected caffeine, while a similar proportion incorrectly chose creatine (27.1%) or vitamin D (29.0%). These results are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Predictors of high knowledge scores

Pharmacists aged 26 to 35 years had significantly higher knowledge scores compared to those aged 18 to 25 (β = 0.24, 95% CI 0.02–0.46, p = 0.035). Female participants had significantly lower knowledge scores than males (β = − 0.32, 95% CI − 0.48 to − 0.16, p < 0.001). Those with more than 10 years of experience also showed higher knowledge scores compared to those with less than 1 year of experience (β = 0.24, 95% CI 0.01–0.48, p = 0.042). Receiving formal training on dietary or sports supplements was associated with higher knowledge scores (β = 0.16, 95% CI 0.01–0.32, p = 0.041). Additionally, pharmacists who were asked about supplements rarely or a few times per week had significantly higher knowledge scores than those never asked (β = 0.34, 95% CI 0.09–0.59, p = 0.008 and β = 0.31, 95% CI 0.07–0.55, p = 0.012, respectively; Table 2).

Attitudes toward counseling on muscle-building supplements

More than half of the pharmacists (55.1%) agreed or strongly agreed that they should play an active role in advising patients on MBS. Similarly, 53.7% expressed confidence in guiding customers on selecting safe and effective athletic supplements. While 52.1% believed most MBS are safe when used appropriately, only 36.7% agreed or strongly agreed that current regulations in Saudi Arabia are sufficient to ensure their safety (Fig. 1).

Predictors of positive attitudes

Female pharmacists had significantly lower attitude scores compared to males (β = − 0.79, 95% CI − 1.32 to − 0.26, p = 0.003). Those with 6 to 10 years of experience had higher attitude scores than those with less than one year (β = 0.82, 95% CI 0.08–1.56, p = 0.030). Pharmacists who had received formal training on supplements had slightly lower attitude scores (β = − 0.52, 95% CI − 1.02 to − 0.02, p = 0.041). Compared to those never asked for advice, those who were asked a few times per week or daily had significantly higher attitude scores (β = 0.94, 95% CI 0.17–1.71, p = 0.017 and β = 0.88, 95% CI 0.08–1.68, p = 0.030, respectively). Additionally, higher knowledge scores were positively associated with more favorable attitudes (β = 0.75, 95% CI 0.51–0.99, p < 0.001; Table 3).

Counseling practices

Regarding counseling frequency, 43.6% of pharmacists reported that they were sometimes providing counseling on MBS, followed by 19.4% who always did so. The most common reason customers sought advice was to improve muscle mass (40.5%), followed by weight loss (25.0%). Reported barriers to providing more counseling included lack of knowledge (38.6%), time constraints (32.5%), low customer demand (27.7%), and absence of clear regulatory guidelines (23.0%). These findings are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Predictors of high frequency of providing counseling

Pharmacists aged 36 to 45 years were more likely to provide frequent counseling compared to those aged 18 to 25 (OR = 1.69, 95% CI 1.07–2.67, p = 0.025). Female pharmacists were less likely to report high counseling frequency compared to males (OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.52–0.98, p = 0.037). Compared to those never asked about supplements, pharmacists who were asked a few times per week or daily had significantly higher odds of providing frequent counseling (OR = 2.01, 95% CI 1.27–3.19, p = 0.003 and OR = 1.89, 95% CI 1.18–3.06, p = 0.009, respectively; Table 4).

Perceptions regarding training and education needs

More than half of the pharmacists (54.8%) expressed interest in attending training programs on the safe use of MBS. A similar proportion (55.2%) agreed or strongly agreed that formal education on sports supplements should be included in pharmacy curricula. Furthermore, 54.4% believed that pharmacists should have access to standardized guidelines on supplement counseling (Fig. 2).

Predictors of favorable perceptions regarding training and education needs

Female pharmacists were less likely to report favorable perceptions toward training and education needs compared to their male counterparts (β = − 0.64, 95% CI − 1.07 to − 0.21, p = 0.003). Pharmacists who were asked a few times per week were more likely to express favorable perceptions (β = 0.71, 95% CI 0.08–1.34, p = 0.026), and higher knowledge scores were positively associated with favorable perceptions regarding training and education (β = 0.70, 95% CI 0.51–0.90, p < 0.001). These results are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Discussion

This study highlights substantial gaps in community pharmacists’ knowledge and counseling practices regarding muscle-building supplements (MBS). Safety-related deficiencies were especially prominent. Only a small proportion of participants accurately identified anabolic steroid misuse as a cause of hepatic and renal damage or recognized caffeine as the prototypical stimulant in pre-workout products. In contrast, a larger fraction correctly answered the creatine-related item. These findings are concerning, as they relate directly to everyday patient safety and professional interactions. Comparable knowledge deficits have been documented internationally. In Tehran, fewer than half of community pharmacists had received formal training on dietary supplements, resulting in consistently moderate knowledge scores despite frequent consumer inquiries [30]. In Palestine, knowledge was similarly limited, while Italian pharmacists demonstrated awareness of recommended daily allowances and toxicity concerns in only one-third of cases [31, 36]. Such consistent shortcomings across regions emphasize the global nature of the issue.

Such knowledge deficiencies carry important clinical implications. High-dose caffeine has been associated with tachyarrhythmia, anxiety, and insomnia, whereas undeclared anabolic-steroid analogues in over-the-counter supplements have been linked to hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and cardiovascular events [7, 32]. These risks are often underestimated due to aggressive marketing that frames supplements as natural products. Contributing factors include limited toxicology coverage in continuing professional development (CPD) programmes and the absence of standardized supplement modules in pharmacy curricula. An audit of U.S. pharmacy schools found that only one fifth offered such training [33, 35]. Until these structural gaps are addressed, pharmacists’ ability to serve as safety gatekeepers in the context of MBS will remain constrained.

Gender and experience added further nuance. Female pharmacists were significantly less likely to provide routine counseling (adjusted OR 0.71; 95% CI 0.52–0.98), echoing Saudi data where male pharmacists counseled more frequently than females (74% vs. 58%) [34]. By contrast, no gender effect was seen in Italy or Iran [30, 36]. indicating that cultural and regulatory environments shape counseling behavior. While years of experience did not independently predict counseling in our multivariable model, customer demand proved decisive. Pharmacists receiving weekly or daily queries had nearly double the odds of counseling, underscoring the pivotal role of consumer engagement. Similar demand-driven patterns have been documented in Iran and Italy [30, 36]. These results suggest that knowledge may underpin confidence, but actual counseling practice is largely activated by patient inquiry.

The relationship between knowledge and attitude was also evident. Higher knowledge scores predicted more favorable counseling attitudes, mirroring findings from Ethiopia, Egypt, and Indonesia [37–39]. However, receipt of formal training was paradoxically associated with slightly lower attitude scores (β − 0.52; 95% CI − 1.02 to − 0.02). This “training paradox” may reflect heightened professional caution, as training often emphasizes risks of contamination, unregulated claims, and misuse [40, 41]. In this sense, reduced enthusiasm may not represent disengagement but a more evidence-informed stance that prioritizes patient safety. Supporting this interpretation, training effects in other domains, such as advance care planning and infection control, have shown that outcomes depend heavily on content design and delivery [42, 43]. Balanced educational strategies that integrate safety knowledge with communication skills and regulatory guidance could therefore foster both competence and measured confidence.

Additional nuances were observed in gender and experience. Female pharmacists not only reported lower counseling rates but also had lower attitude scores than males. In Tehran, male pharmacists self-rated their knowledge higher and demonstrated greater counseling confidence [30], whereas in Egypt gender was not a determinant of attitudes [39]. Mid-career pharmacists demonstrated more favorable attitudes than early-career colleagues, consistent with Ethiopian findings that experience moderated the knowledge–attitude relationship [38]. These results suggest that experience enhances professional maturity and confidence, but female and early-career pharmacists may require targeted mentorship and structured support to engage fully in supplement counseling.

Counseling behavior in this study was primarily demand-driven. Pharmacists who reported being asked about supplements weekly or daily had significantly higher odds of providing advice, a pattern mirrored in Saudi, Iranian, Italian, and U.S. studies [13, 30, 36, 44]. Training alone therefore appears insufficient to shift practice toward proactive counseling unless combined with public campaigns that encourage patient inquiries and systemic reinforcement of the pharmacist’s role.

This study is not without limitations. The cross-sectional design of this study restricts causal inference, while reliance on self-reported responses introduces potential recall and social desirability bias. The online format and voluntary participation also raise the possibility of selection and non-response bias. Additionally, although validated, the knowledge assessment tool captured only part of pharmacists’ competencies, and the exclusive focus on MBS limits generalizability to other supplement categories. These limitations, however, are offset by important strengths, including a large nationwide sample of 708 pharmacists, which enhances representativeness and mitigates some bias concerns, and the use of validated questionnaire items that support the internal consistency and construct validity of the findings.

The observed gaps point to a need for curricular reform and expansion of CPD opportunities tailored to dietary supplements, particularly MBS. Training programmes should balance safety awareness with practical counseling frameworks to mitigate the training paradox. Structured mentorship may be especially valuable for female and early-career pharmacists. Public awareness initiatives that stimulate patient demand could further normalize pharmacist-led counseling. Future studies should employ qualitative methods, such as interviews or focus groups, to explore contextual barriers in depth, and randomized controlled trials are warranted to evaluate the impact of targeted CPD interventions. Comparative research on different educational modalities may also help identify the most effective strategies for professional upskilling in this domain.
