Management of Inflammatory Bowel Disease Using E-health Technologies: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Anish J Kuriakose Kuzhiyanjal, Gaurav B Nigam, George A Antoniou, Francis A Farraye, Raymond K Cross, Jimmy K Limdi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsTechnological advances have provided innovative, adaptive, and responsive models of care for inflammatory bowel diseases [IBD]. We conducted a systematic review to compare e-health interventions with standard care in management of IBD.MethodsWe searched electronic databases for randomised, controlled trials [RCT] comparing e-health interventions with standard care for patients with IBD. Effect measures were standardised mean difference [SMD], odds ratio [OR], or rate ratio [RR], calculated using the inverse variance or Mantel–Haenszel statistical method and random-effects models. Version 2 of the Cochrane tool was used to assess the risk of bias. The certainty of evidence was appraised with the GRADE frameworkResultsFourteen RCTs [n = 3111; 1754 e-health and 1357 controls] were identified. The difference in disease activity scores (SMD 0.09, 95% confidence interval [CI]: -0.09-0.28) and clinical remission (odds ratio [OR] 1.12, 95% CI: 0.78-1.61) between e-health interventions and standard care were not statistically significant. Higher quality of life [QoL] [SMD 0.20, 95% CI: 0.05-0.35) and IBD knowledge [SMD 0.23, 95% CI: 0.10-0.36] scores were noted in the e-health group, and self-efficacy levels [SMD -0.09, 95% CI: -0.22-0.05] were comparable. E-health patients had fewer office [RR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.78-0.93] and emergency [RR 0.70, 95% CI: 0.51- 0.95] visits, with no statistically significant difference in endoscopic procedures, total health care encounters, corticosteroid use, and IBD related hospitalisation or surgery. The trials were judged to be at high risk of bias or to have some concerns for disease remission. The certainty of evidence was moderate or low.ConclusionE-health technologies may have a role in value-based care in IBD.

1. Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases [IBD] comprising Crohn’s disease [CD] and ulcerative colitis [UC], are chronic, relapsing and remitting, immune-mediated diseases affecting the gastrointestinal tract, with multidimensional and often negative effects on patients’ personal, psychological, professional, and social wellbeing.1 The disease, being characterised by intermittent flares, requires frequent outpatient follow-up visits for monitoring and/or adjustment of medications, and is also associated with emergency department attendance, hospitalisation, or surgery. A recent study from the USA demonstrated an almost 4-fold increase in health care spending in IBD, from $6.4 billion in 1996 to $25.4 billion in 2016, corresponding to a per-patient increase from $5,714 to $14,033, and with 56% of this spending attributable to inpatient and emergency department visits.2 In addition, wider appreciation of the potential disconnect between symptoms and objective measures of disease activity has redefined our definition of meaningful disease control, which now aims to achieve mucosal healing, known to be associated with reduction in hospitalisation, surgery, and cancer complicating IBD, and with the aim of reducing disability and improving the quality of life of our patients.1,3–5 Traditional, symptom-based models of care are frequently episodic and reactive, and have not been shown to alter the course of disease, paving the way for proactive and more participatory models of management.6,7 These newer management models harness the potential for digital health technologies, enabling remote monitoring, improving and increasing access to health care providers [HCPs], and also enhancing patient participation in driving their own care.8,9

The definition of telemedicine has undergone a series of changes over the years with the advent of novel technologies. An operational definition suggests telemedicine encompasses six essential components: use of information technology as an alternative to face-to-face [F2F] office visits; geographical separation between the health care provider and the patient; organisational structure of the system or network for delivery of health care; appropriate staffing including doctors and health care assistants as well as technical support staff to support the system; clearly defined protocols and pathways for triage and treatment; and adherence to standards for patient care and confidentiality.10

Existing models of care using telemedicine include diabetes mellitus, asthma, chronic obstructive airways disease, and congestive cardiac failure, among others, and have demonstrated improvements in disease course and quality of life, with higher levels of patient acceptance and lower cost of care.11,12

Telemedicine holds promise in IBD care by improving efficiency in health care delivery, providing close monitoring of treatment and adherence, convenience to the patient especially by reduction of travel to outpatient visits, early identification of signs of clinical deterioration, and coordinating multidisciplinary care.

The cataclysmic coronavirus disease 2019 [COVID-19] pandemic had an unprecedented, yet transformational effect on health care delivery, with telemedicine playing a pivotal role in hitherto ambulatory care medicine, demonstrating the potential of e-health in health care delivery.13

Multiple randomised, controlled trials [RCTs] have compared telemedicine with standard F2F office care since 2010. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Huang and colleagues in 2014 showed that telemedicine significantly decreased office visits for IBD patients but had no significant effect on hospital admission or relapse rates.14 However, additional RCTs with robust study designs have been conducted since this review was published, including the impact of telemedicine on clinical outcomes, remote monitoring, IBD-related quality of life [QoL], and health care utilisation.15,16

We conducted a systematic review with meta-analysis, to evaluate the role of e-health interventions in the management of IBD patients compared with standard of care [SoC].

2. Methods

2.1. Review reporting and protocol registration

This systematic review was in accordance with the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses [PRISMA] guidelines 2020 [Supplementary File 1].17–19 The protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews [PROSPERO] [CRD42020192428].

2.2. Eligibility criteria

Population: Individuals of any age suffering with IBD—ulcerative colitis [UC], Crohn’s disease [CD], or IBD-unspecified [IBD-U].

Intervention: E-health interventions including virtual clinics, telephone consultations, remote monitoring, or any web-based health platform for management of IBD.

Comparison/control group: Standard care for IBD, based on evidence-based guidelines including initial assessment, guideline compliant therapies, planned and urgent office visits, and supply of educational patient leaflets as required.

Outcomes: Our primary outcomes were disease activity scores and clinical remission at the end of follow-up. Disease activity scores are the quantified numerical value allotted by various disease activity indices incorporating patient symptoms. These scores were then used to determine clinical remission by predetermined cut-off values as per the respective disease activity index. For UC, the scores used included Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index [SCCAI], Partial Mayo score [PMS], and Seo index, with cut-off scores of <2, <1, and <120, respectively, to indicate clinical remission.20 For CD, a Harvey–Bradshaw Index [HBI] score of ≤4 was considered as clinical remission.21

Secondary outcomes were as follows.

Quality of life [QoL], defined as a measure of perceived health.22

Quality of life [QoL], defined as a measure of perceived health.22

IBD knowledge, measured using validated questionaires which included Crohn’s and Colitis Knowledge [CCKNOW] and visual analogue scales [VAS].23

IBD knowledge, measured using validated questionaires which included Crohn’s and Colitis Knowledge [CCKNOW] and visual analogue scales [VAS].23

Patient self-efficacy, defined as the perceived measure of an individual’s ability to manage a particular situation by making necessary behavioural changes.24

Patient self-efficacy, defined as the perceived measure of an individual’s ability to manage a particular situation by making necessary behavioural changes.24

Events, ie, endoscopic procedures, total health care encounters, corticosteroid use, and IBD-related hospitalisation or surgery.

Events, ie, endoscopic procedures, total health care encounters, corticosteroid use, and IBD-related hospitalisation or surgery.

Only RCTs on humans meeting the criteria for the research question were eligible for this review. Studies reporting telemedicine intervention relying solely on patient self-management, and those with inadequate data or not reporting at least one of the listed outcome measures, were excluded.

2.3. Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search of major electronic databases [Medline, Embase, PubMed, CINAHL, PsychInfo, British Nursing Index, Clinical Trials Registry, WHO trials registry, International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number [ISRCTN] registry, EU trials register, and Cochrane databases] was performed for articles published in English language, from inception until May 2020 with the assistance of a clinical information specialist [Appendix 1]. Additional studies were identified by screening the reference lists of studies identified in the primary search. Relevant abstracts derived from international and national conferences, namely Digestive Diseases Week USA, American College of Gastroenterology, British Society of Gastroenterology, United European Gastroenterology Week, and European Crohn’s and Colitis Organisation, were also reviewed and included if eligible. An updated search was performed in December 2021.

2.4. Selection process

The search results were imported into Mendeley software to facilitate review as well as to eliminate duplicates.25 Two authors, GBN and AJKK, screened the titles and abstracts independently to assess study eligibility. These studies were then categorised as ‘accepted’, ‘rejected’, or ‘not sure’, based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The ‘rejected’ studies were excluded, and the ‘accepted’ and ‘not sure’ articles underwent a further full-text review to confirm the eligibility. Discrepancies between the two authors [GBN and AJKK] were resolved in consensus with a third author [JKL].

2.5. Data collection process and data items

Once eligible studies were selected, data extraction was carried out by AJKK and verified by GBN. If a particular outcome was not reported, authors were contacted to obtain missing data. The data items extracted were as follows.

Study-related data: study name, author, journal and year of publication, country of study, single-/multi-centre, age group, and duration of the study.

Study population-related data: sample size, gender, mean age of control and intervention groups, disease phenotype and activity, socioeconomic and educational status.

Outcome data: disease activity scores, number of patients in clinical remission, QoL, IBD knowledge, self-efficacy, and event data.

For multiple published full-text articles and/or conference abstracts reporting outcomes from the same study population, outcomes were combined and included as a single study. If there was more than one arm involving e-health in a study, each arm was reported separately and compared against an equally divided control group as per Cochrane guidance for continuous and dichotomous outcomes, with a view to preserving the heterogeneity of the interventions in the study.26 For data reported across different time points, the last time point reported was used for analysis. If both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analysis were reported, the former was included for this review.

The compiled data extracted from the eligible studies were recorded using Microsoft® Office Excel® 365 software.27

2.6. Study risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for each study was assessed independently by two reviewers [GBN and AJKK] using the Cochrane’s revised tool for assessing risk of bias in randomised trials version 2 [RoB 2] using the RoB 2 Excel tool.28 This tool is structured to report bias for a specified outcome across five fixed domains: the randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. All domains have a set of signalling questions attached to them which would contribute to an algorithm facilitating a judgement on the ‘risk of bias’ for the specified outcome measure. A proposed judgment of ‘low’, ‘some concerns’, or ‘high’ risk of bias is generated by the algorithm. The assessor’s justification is also used to support the risk of bias assessment.

For this review, the risk of bias was performed for the studies reporting the primary outcome of clinical remission and disease activity scores.

2.7. Effect measures and synthesis methods

Disease activity scores, IBD knowledge, QoL, and patient self-efficacy were expressed as mean and standard deviation [SD] for the meta-analysis. If a different measure was reported for these outcomes, authors were contacted to obtain the mean and SD from their raw data. Alternatively, the mean and SD were calculated from the median, if available, using a calculator developed by Wan et al.29 Event data for endoscopic procedures, total health care encounters, corticosteroid use, and IBD-related hospitalisation or surgery were treated as count data as individual patients could have had more than one event during the follow-up period. There was also a difference in follow-up periods between different studies. Therefore, rate ratios [RR] and standard errors [SE] were calculated from the event data, number of patients, and months of follow-up and subsequently pooled for the meta-analyses.26

2.7.1. Unit of analysis issues

The individual patient was regarded as the unit of analysis.

2.7.2. Dealing with missing data

Respective corresponding authors were contacted for additional and missing information. If the data were not available nor computable using the available measures, then they were not included in the meta-analyses.

2.7.3. Measures of treatment effect

The Mantel–Haenszel statistical method was used to compare pooled estimates of dichotomous outcomes, utilizing odds ratio [OR] and 95% confidence interval [CI]. The inverse variance statistical method was used to calculate the pooled mean difference [and 95% CI] for continuous data. Owing to the different outcome measure indices used in the included studies, standardised mean difference was used when calculating the pooled effect estimates for continuous data. Additionally, due to the presence of conceptual heterogeneity between the studies, a random-effects model was used to calculate pooled effect estimates for both continuous and dichotomous outcomes. The RR for event data was pooled and analysed using inverse variance statistical model. We created a forest plot for each of the treatment effects, looking at a confidence interval of 95% as well as a p-value of <0.05.

2.7.4. Assessment of heterogeneity

Assessment for conceptual heterogeneity was undertaken by the authors considering the study design, study setting, interventions employed, enrolled participants, data metrics, and outcomes. Cochrane’s Q [χ2] was utilized to assess between-study heterogeneity.

The I² statistic was used to quantitively assess inconsistency across the studies. I2 was deduced as per the following reference.

a. 0%–40% = might not be important.

a. 0%–40% = might not be important.

b. 30%–60% = moderate heterogeneity.

b. 30%–60% = moderate heterogeneity.

c. 50%–90% = substantial heterogeneity.

c. 50%–90% = substantial heterogeneity.

d. 75%–100% = considerable heterogeneity.26

d. 75%–100% = considerable heterogeneity.26

2.7.5. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analysis was performed for disease activity scores to identify any difference between UC and CD disease activity scores. For QoL, we performed a subgroup analysis for age groups above and below 18. Subgroup analysis across socioeconomic groups and scholastic level of the participants was not performed due to the paucity of information from individual studies.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding individual studies from the meta-analyses to assess for any significant differences in outcome estimates [Supplementary Material 2].

2.8. Reporting bias assessment

Where more than 10 studies were included for an outcome, we evaluated for publication bias by visual assessment of funnel plot symmetry for each individual outcome. Conversely, if there were less than 10 studies, then publication bias was not assessed.26

2.9. Certainty of the evidence

The quality of the evidence was appraised for the primary outcomes of disease remission and disease activity scores using Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation [GRADE] criteria.30,31

3. Results

3.1. Study selection

From database search in December 2021, 2751 database reports were identified in addition to 31 reports from the trials registry. After exclusion of duplicates, 1559 reports remained. These titles underwent a further abstract screening after application of inclusion and exclusion criteria. A total of 59 studies underwent full-text review to assess if they fulfilled the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Fourteen studies were finally included in the systematic review and meta-analysis [rejected studies in Appendix 2]. The flowchart illustrating the study selection process is shown in Figure 1.

Study flow diagram for identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion of studies.

3.2. Study characteristics

The included studies were published between 2011 and 2021 and included adult [11 studies] and paediatric [three studies], The population totalled 3111: 2802 adult and 309 paediatric patients, 1754 e-health patients, and 1357 controls. These studies were conducted in France, Denmark, Ireland, Spain, The Netherlands, New Zealand, the UK and the USA. The studies’ follow-up duration ranged from 6 months to 24 months. Three studies [Hoyo et al., Elkjaer et al., and De Jong et al.] reported on the scholastic level of the participants, and only two studies [Bonnaud et al. and Elkjaer et al.] reported on the socioeconomic status of the participants.32–35

Four studies reported by Abutaleb et al., Cross et al., Bilgrami et al., and Schliep et al. used Telemedicine for Patients with Inflammatory Bowel Disease [TELE-IBD] as an e-health intervention in the USA.36–39 This e-health platform included a mobile phone-based interface for the patients and a decision support server with a website for the HCP. The website served as a link between the HCP and participants to provide personalised alerts and action plans for each participant. The participants in the e-health arm also received relevant educational text messages from the provider.37 The RCT design included three arms to assess the outcomes: TELE-IBD weekly [TELE-IBD W] and TELE-IBD every other week [TELE-IBD EOW] vs standard care as control.

In a separate study in the USA, Cross et al. used home tele-management in UC [UC-HAT], consisting of a home unit with a laptop and an electronic weighing scale which captured the patient information and relayed it to a decision support server and web-based portal for the HCP to review.40 Depending on patient-reported outcome measures, the HCPs were able to set up clinical alerts and customise action plans.

Heida et al., using a home telemonitoring system, enrolled patients between 10 and 19 years of age in The Netherlands.41 They used automated email alerts to stratify patients’ risk for flare-up, based on a home telemonitoring intervention using online disease-specific composite scores (paediatric ulcerative colitis disease activity index [PUCAI] and shortened paediatric CD activity index with faecal calprotectin [FCP] levels vs standard care of conventional face-to-face [F2F] follow-ups). As per the risk, the patients were advised to either repeat tests or a review with specialist services.

Hoyo et al. evaluated the impact of e-health by in Spain using Telemonitoring of Crohn’s Disease and Ulcerative Colitis [TECCU], a web-based secure app for electronic devices.32 Patients completed self-assessment questionnaires, and a prioritisation system generated alerts guiding HCPs to create individualised action plans [G_TECCU]. The study also included an additional arm for e-health: nurse-assisted telephone care [G_NT]. Both the G_TECCU and the G_NT arm were compared with standard care F2F visits while generating the pooled data for this meta-analysis. The study also captured information on the educational background of the participants. University level of education was recorded in 38.1% of the control group, 52.4% of the G_NT group, and 47.6% of the G_TECCU group. Secondary level of education was reported for 42.9% of the control group and 28.6% of both the intervention arms. The remaining had a primary level of education. The reported outcomes were adjusted for educational levels.

Carlsen et al. compared the use of a web app [young.constant-care.com] [YCC] vs standard care in management of IBD among paediatric patients in Denmark.42 The web app created a traffic light system [red, yellow, and green] generating automated alerts based on the patient recorded data (PUCAI and the abbreviated Paediatric Crohn’s Disease Activity Index [abbrPCDAI]) with use of an FCP sent out in post and blood parameters done at a local laboratory. The alerts guided HCPs to suggest appropriate action plans.

McCombie et al. compared use of smartphone-based health apps IBDsmart and IBDoc for virtual consultations vs F2F visits in New Zealand.43 IBDsmart recorded disease activity (Harvey–Bradshaw Index [HBI] and Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index [SCCAI]), and IBDoc was for home testing of FCP, providing necessary information for regular follow-up of patients remotely.

Atreja et al. reported the data on use of HealthPROMISE app, a mobile cloud-based platform, in the USA in an abstract form.44 The app recorded the QoL and symptom scores which could be tracked by HCPs and alerted them to any worsening.

Akobeng et al. compared telephone consultation vs standard care to evaluate impact on QoL and patient satisfaction, in paediatric patients [aged 8–16 years] with IBD in the UK.45 In the intervention group, patients received a phone call from the doctor at a previously fixed time. If the patient had any concerning symptoms in both groups, the IBD nurse was contacted for further advice and appointments.

Elkjaer et al. assessed UC patient disease activity and QoL using a web program [http://www.constant-care.dk] which served as a remote interface for communication between the patient and HCPs.33 The patients randomised to the web tool had access to a web-doctor, and the investigators conducted weekly web-ward rounds to identify patients who required urgent care, using patient-reported symptoms. The study was carried out in Denmark and Ireland. The results are reported separately for both countries for the purpose of meta-analysis. The socioeconomic parameters and educational background of the participants were analysed and reported separately for Denmark and Ireland. In Denmark, 99% of the intervention group had a form of income, including paid occupation, pension, and supported living, and this figure was 96.2% in the control group. In Ireland, the percentage of participants with income was 84.3% in the intervention group and 82.9% in the control group. In terms of education, Denmark recorded 1% and 7.5% of participants in the intervention and control groups, respectively, as having no education, whereas all participants in Ireland had some form of education.

De Jong et al. performed an RCT in The Netherlands using MyIBDcoach, which used a web page with a smartphone/tablet-based secure app.34 Patients were monitored at a frequency determined by the patient-reported symptom scores. Depending on patient-reported parameters, alerts were sent to HCPs to contact the patient and plan office visits, if required. Educational qualifications of the participants were reported: university level of education for 14% and 13%; a form of vocational education for 69% and 70%; secondary education for 15% each; primary education for 2% each; and missing data for 18% and 17% of the intervention and control arms,respectively. The recorded outcomes were modified to account for differences in education levels.

EasyMICI–MaMICI, a dedicated software for IBD management using telemedicine which allows for customised patient care and continuous medical training, was assessed in the study by Bonnaud et al. in France.35 Patients accessed EasyMICI via smartphone app or a web portal [MaMICI] to permit clinical evaluation and modification of treatment by the doctor. Bonnaud et al. reported the socio-professional status of participants, with 40% of the control group and 14% of the intervention group being unemployed. No data were available for 12% of the control group. The remaining participants were workers [skilled/ unskilled] or business individuals.

3.3. Risk of bias in studies and certainty of evidence

Bias due to missing outcome data predominantly contributed to the overall high risk of bias in most studies. 3.4. Results of individual studies and results of synthesis

3.4.1. Primary outcomes

3.4.1.1. Disease activity

Disease activity scores post-intervention were reported in three studies for UC and two for CD.37,40,43 Disease activity for UC was measured using SCCAI in the TELE-IBD and IBDsmart/IBDoc studies, and the UC-HAT trial used the Seo index.37,40,43,46,47 TELE-IBD and IBDsmart/IBDoc used HBI to measure disease activity for CD.37,43,48 None of the studies found a statistically significant difference between the intervention and control group for either UC or CD at the end of follow-up. Meta-analysis of pooled disease activity scores from three studies for both UC and CD patients [306 patients in e-health and 189 patients in the control group] did not show a statistically significant difference between the two arms [SMD 0.09, 95% CI: -0.09-0.28, p = 0.32, I2 = 0%]. A subgroup analysis of disease activity scores for UC [SMD 0.20, 95% CI: -0.10-0.49, p = 0.20, I2 = 0%] and CD [SMD 0.03, 95% CI: -0.20-0.26, p = 0.80, I2 = 0%] did not show statistically a significant difference between the study arms [Figure 2A]. The overall certainty of evidence was ‘moderate’, with subgroups of UC and CD having certainty of ‘low’ and ‘moderate’, respectively [Table 3].

Forest plot for [A] disease activity scores comparing e-health versus standard care; [B] patients in clinical disease remission for IBD [UC and CD combined] comparing e-health versus standard care. CI, confidence interval; CD, Crohn’s disease; IBD, inflammatory bowel disease, IV, inverse variance; SD, standard deviation; UC, ulcerative colitis; M-H, Mantel–Haenszel.

3.4.1.2. Clinical disease remission

Five separate studies reported data on clinical disease remission in IBD patients.32,35,37,40,43 The TELE-IBD study noted higher clinical remission rates across all arms for IBD patients at the end of the study period [12 months] compared with the baseline. TELE-IBD found no statistically significant difference in clinical remission rates between the intervention arms when compared with the control arm for either UC or CD patients [TELE-IBD W vs control, p = 0.39, TELE-IBD EOW vs control, p = 0.99].37 The UC-HAT study reported no statistically significant difference for clinical remission rates between the intervention and control group for UC at the end of follow-up [12 months] [p = 0.56].40 IBDsmart/IBDoc study did not report clinical remission rates at the end of follow-up [personal communication with the authors].43 Hoyo et al. observed higher rates of clinical remission among IBD patients in the TECCU arm as compared with the control group at the end of follow-up [6 months], albeit not statistically significant [p = .19].32 EASYMICI-MaMICI study reported 69.6% of the study participants in clinical remission for IBD at 12 months, with no statistically significant difference between intervention and control groups.35 Meta-analysis of the pooled data from five studies [374 patients in e-health and 229 patients in the control group] did not show statistically significant difference in clinical remission rates for all IBD at the end of follow up between the intervention and control groups [OR 1.12, 95% CI: 0.78-1.61, p = 0.54, I2 = 0%] [Figure 2B]. The overall certainty of evidence for this outcome was ‘low’.

3.4.2. Secondary outcomes

3.4.2.1. IBD knowledge

Comparison of IBD-related knowledge between e-heath and standard care was reported in two studies.34,36 The TELE-IBD study used the Crohn’s and Colitis Knowledge [CCKNOW] score to assess participant knowledge.23,36 Numerically higher improvement in IBD knowledge was noted for both the intervention arms [+2.4 in TELE-IBD EOW, +2.0 in TELE-IBD W] compared with standard care [+1.8] at the end of follow-up, with statistically significant difference being noted only for TELE-IBD EOW arm [p = 0.03] after adjusting for baseline variables. MyIBDcoach study assessed participant knowledge using visual analogue scales [VAS] and noted no statistically significant difference in IBD knowledge between the two arms after adjusting for variables and confounders [p = 0.074].34 Meta-analysis of the pooled unadjusted data from two studies [485 patients in e-health and 405 patients in the control group] showed increased IBD knowledge in the e-health intervention group [SMD 0.23, 95% CI: 0.10-0.36, p = 0.0008, I2 = 0%] [Figure 3A].

Forest plot for [A] IBD knowledge comparing e-health versus standard care; [B] quality of life comparing e-health versus standard care; [C] patient self-efficacy comparing e-health versus standard care. CI, confidence interval; CD, Crohn’s disease; IBD, inflammatory bowel disease; IV, inverse variance; SD, standard deviation.

3.4.2.2. QoL

QoL outcomes were reported in 10 studies.32,34,37,39–45 For TELE-IBD, QoL was reported in two separate studies, Schliep et al. and Cross et al.37,39 Schliep et al. reported generic QoL using a short-form 12 [SF-12] questionnaire.39 SF-12 has two components, namely a physical component summary [PCS] and mental component summary [MCS].49 The PCS component showed significant improvement only in the standard care group, and no difference was noted in MCS scores between the groups. These values were not used for the meta-analysis, which would require treating each component separately for QoL. Cross et al. reported a disease-specific QoL using the Inflammatory Bowel Disease Questionnaire [IBDQ].50 No statistically significant difference was noted between the intervention and the control groups [TELE-IBD W vs control, p = 0.63; TELE-IBD EOW vs control, p = 0.66].37 UC-HAT and IBDsmart/IBDoc study [who reported disease-specific QoL using IBDQ] noted statistical significant difference between the intervention and control group in the former [after adjusting for baseline differences] [p = 0.04] but did not note a statistically significant difference between the two groups in the latter study.40,43 Similar QoL scores were noted in the TECCU study between the control, G_TECCU, and G_NT arms, but the results could not be pooled due to insufficient data [G_TECCU vs control, p = 0.64; G_NT vs control, p = 0.62].32 MyIBDcoach study did not find significant differences in QoL between the study arms using the Shortened Inflammatory Bowel Disease questionnaire [SIBDQ] at the end of the follow-up period [p = 0.057].34 The HealthPROMISE app study also reported QoL as SIBDQ scores and noted significant improvement in the intervention group [p-value not reported].44 Health-related QoL was measured using the IMPACT questionnaire by Young constant care, home telemonitoring, and telephone consultation studies in paediatric and adolescent patients, and no statistically significant differences were noted between study arms at the end of the follow up in all the three studies.41,42,45,51 QoL scores at the end of follow up were not available in Carlsen et al., and hence could not be included in the meta-analysis. QoL scores were obtained by direct correspondence with authors of the Heida et al. study.

Pooled meta-analysis using unadjusted data from six studies [776 patients in e-health and 646 patients in the control group] showed higher QoL in patients in the e-health arm as compared with the standard care arm [SMD 0.20, 95% CI: 0.05-0.35, p = 0.008, I2 = 36%] [Figure 3B]. Subgroup analysis favoured higher QoL in the younger age group using e-health interventions [SMD 0.45, 95% CI: 0.20-0.70, p = 0.0004, I2 = 0%], while failing to reach statistical significance in the adult population group [SMD 0.10, 95% CI: -0.02-0.22, p = 0.09, I2 = 0%] [Figure 3B].

3.4.2.3. Self-efficacy

Data on patient self-efficacy was reported in two studies.34,38 The TELE-IBD study used a general self-efficacy scale, and the myIBDcoach study used an IBD-specific self-efficacy scale.52,53 No statistically significant differences were noted by both studies between the groups at the end of follow-up.

Meta-analysis of data from two studies [478 patients in e-health and 415 patients in the control group] did not show any differences between the two arms [SMD -0.09, 95% CI: -0.22-0.05, p = 0.21, I2 = 0%] [Figure 3C].

3.4.2.4. Health care encounters

Total health care encounters

Two studies reported total health care encounters. Both studies did not identify statistically significant differences between the study arms after adjusting for differences and confounders.37,42 Meta-analysis of RRs from the two studies did not show difference between the treatment arms [RR 1.04, 95% CI: 0.92- 1.16, p = 0.54, I2 = 75%] [Figure 4].

Office visits

Data on total office visits were reported in seven studies.32–34,37,41–43 All studies with the exception of TELE-IBD reported reduced outpatient visits in the e-health group as compared with the standard group.37 The total number of outpatient office visits were not available from De Jong et al. and could not be included in the meta-analysis.34 Data were obtained from the authors for TELE-IBD and IBDoc/IBDsmart for pooled analysis.

Meta-analysis of six studies showed significantly fewer office visits in the e-health intervention arm as compared with standard care arm [RR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.78-0.93, p = 0.0003, I2 = 22%] [Figure 5A].

Emergency visits

Data on emergency visits were reported in six studies.32–34,37,41,42 The constant care web program study reported significantly more acute visits in the standard care group as compared with the e-health arm from Denmark [p <0.0001]; however, in Ireland, no statistically significant differences were noted between the two arms.33 The Young constant care study reported numerically fewer acute visits in the e-health group as compared with the standard group [p = 0.13].42

Four studies [home telemonitoring, myIBDcoach, TECCU, and TELE-IBD] noticed similar numbers of emergency visits between intervention and control.32,34,37,41

Meta-analysis of five studies found fewer emergency and acute visits in the e-health group [RR 0.70, 95% CI: 0.51- 0.95, p = 0.02, I2 = 0%] [Figure 5B].

IBD-related hospitalisation

Data on IBD-related hospitalisation were reported in five studies.32–34,37,43 The Tele-IBD study reported reduction in IBD-related hospitalisation across all three-arms, with statistically significant difference noted only in the TELE-IBD W arm after making adjustments for baseline differences and confounders [p = 0.04].37

The MyIBDcoach study noted significantly lower mean numbers of hospitalisation in the e-health group as compared with the control arm, but the raw data were unavailable and it could not be included for pooled analysis [p = 0.046].34 The TECCU, IBDsmart/IBDoc, and constant care web program studies observed similar events between the control and intervention groups.32,33,43

Meta-analysis of four studies did not show a statistically significant difference in IBD-related hospitalisation between the treatment arms [RR 0.82, 95% CI: 0.52- 1.29, p = 0.38, I2 = 0%] [Figure 5C].

IBD-related surgery

Data on IBD-related surgery were reported in three studies.32,34,37 No significant differences in outcomes were noted between the intervention and control groups. Raw data were unavailable from De Jong et al. to calculate RR and hence could not be included for pooled analysis.

Meta-analysis of two studies did not show a statistically significant difference in IBD-related surgery between the treatment arms [RR 0.87, 95% CI: 0.44-1.72, p = 0.68, I2 = 0%] [Figure 6A].

Endoscopic procedures

Data on endoscopic procedures were reported in three studies.37,41,42 No significant differences in number of endoscopic procedures between the intervention and control groups were noted.

Meta-analysis of three studies did not show a statistically significant difference in the number of endoscopic procedures between the treatment arms [RR 1.00, 95% CI: 0.77- 1.31, p = 0.98, I2 = 0%] [Figure 6B].

Corticosteroid use

Data on corticosteroid use were reported in three studies.32–34 MyIBDcoach study did not find a statistically significant difference in corticosteroid use between the groups, but the RR could not be calculated due to lack of raw data for pooled analysis [p = 0.322].34

Meta-analysis of two studies did not show a statistically significant difference in corticosteroid used between the control and intervention arms [RR 0.97, 95% CI: 0.47- 1.98, p = 0.93, I2 = 0%] [Figure 6C].

4. Discussion

In this systematic review with meta-analysis of RCTs testing telemedicine in IBD, we made some key observations. We noted that e-health interventions had similar outcomes to standard F2F office visits for controlling disease activity and maintaining clinical remission. Furthermore, in a subgroup analysis for disease activity in UC and CD, we found no difference in outcomes between e-health intervention and standard F2F office visits for both diseases. However, better patient-reported QoL and IBD-related knowledge were noted in the e-health intervention group. Additionally, the e-health group had fewer office and emergency visits.

Several studies including RCTs have tested different forms of telemedicine such as video/virtual consultation, web- and mobile application-based systems, and automated text messages for assessing patient symptoms and disease activity in IBD.37,39,43 Notably, the outcome measures used to evaluate e-health technologies have been heterogeneous, reflecting the dichotomy between clinician perception of disease control [disease activity and medication adherence] and patients’ priorities, which often relate to quality of life and convenience. Using validated measurement tools is a cornerstone for accurate disease monitoring and assessing the effectiveness of e-health. The included studies used clinical scoring to determine disease activity as well as clinical remission. The use of different clinical scoring systems, such as the Seo index, Partial Mayo score, and SCCAI in the included studies, and the varying definitions of clinical remission therein, could have influenced our findings. Moreover, it is well recognised that there may be discordance between clinical and objective markers for disease activity.54 In line with our current paradigms placing greater emphasis on objective targets such as biochemical and endoscopic remission, future studies should incorporate these targets at a lower risk of bias compared with symptom-driven goals.1,5 Notably, all the included studies for this outcome measure excluded patients with a severe phenotype and patients with previous IBD surgery, as well as pregnant women with IBD, limiting the generalisability of our observations to all patients with IBD. The inclusion of patients in remission or with mild disease activity makes it more difficult for e-health to demonstrate a beneficial effect since the endpoints are weaker. Moreover, the duration of most e-health trials of 24 months is a relatively short period to demonstrate superiority over standard of care. Studies in patients at an early stage with moderate-severe disease and longer-term follow-up are needed. E-health technologies have also been shown to be effective in other immune-mediated diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, etc., and were found to be equally or possibly more effective than standard F2F visits, asserting an evolving role for telemedicine in chronic disease management.55

We noted better QoL and IBD knowledge in the e-health group. Subgroup analysis of the QoL data further suggested that e-health technologies performed better for paediatric and adolescent populations as compared with the adult population. Increased uptake and acceptance of technology by younger age groups, in addition to parental support to ensure adherence, could be the likely reason for this difference. Although the improvement in QoL in the e-health cohort did not reach statistical significance in the adult population, the improvement was clinically significant and beneficial to patients. Persistently low QoL has been shown to be associated with increased health care use.56 Furthermore, there is evidence to support that the relationship between QoL and disease activity is bidirectional.57 Accordingly, the willingness and satisfaction reported among patients and providers with use of digital technologies and the comparable QoL are encouraging, and align with our evolving paradigms with treating to target, wherein ‘normalisation’ of QoL has been suggested as a long-term treatment target.1

Improvement in IBD knowledge may be linked with the improvement in QoL.58,59 Good IBD knowledge plays a vital role in self-management of the disease, with improved knowledge impacting positively on treatment acceptance and adherence and social wellbeing.60–62 Park et al. reported better IBD knowledge was associated with increased adherence to continuous therapy and reduced requirement for step-up therapy.61 Acquiring and improving disease-specific knowledge is a continuous process, and e-health technologies facilitate this using a structured, regular, educational component by means of automated messages and emails, as well as push notifications on mobile apps. Improved access to information impacts positively on disease-related knowledge among participants compared with the information received in a shorter F2F interaction.63

Self-efficacy has an important role in management of chronic diseases, improving symptoms, psychological wellbeing through patient empowerment, engagement, and self-care. Having remote consultations did not negatively affect self-efficacy in IBD. Further inferences could not be drawn due to limited studies reporting these data, but this at very least is reassuring. Success of e-health technologies hinges on high adherence and low attrition, which in itself is a function of the characteristics of the intervention, its user, and specifics of the condition tested by the intervention.64 It must also be acknowledged that in this process, the patient is constantly reminded of the potential limitations posed by his/her chronic disease and that this may impact on adherence and attrition. Further studies evaluating the impact of e-health, using validated indices such as a patient management instrument, may shed light on whether these interventions have enough traction to improve self-management. Another major knowledge gap is the acceptance and applicability of these technologies in ethnic minorities and populations with lower literacy and limited resources, which needs further study.65,66

We noted that emergency and office visits were significantly lower in the e-health group when compared with the standard care group. This reduction may be the result of early intervention with use of e-health modalities to modify treatments as well as by avoiding office visits when in remission. As a result, e-health modalities could help triage office visits and allocate office time to those patients who might benefit most from a F2F visit. Despite the reduction in office visits and emergency visits, there was, however, no difference in total health care encounters between the e-health and standard care arms. The number of endoscopic procedures, corticosteroid use, and IBD-related surgery were also similar between the two arms, highlighting similar effectiveness of e-health and standard care in the management of IBD. It is worth noting here that seemingly ambiguous information requiring further clarity or indeed the need for a physical examination would trigger a F2F appointment, thereby influencing this outcome. Moreover, the differential access to health care is widely dependent on socioeconomic status, impacting on the generalisability across different socioeconomic groups and countries with different provisions for public health care.67 The potential for digital interventions in various settings, such as in those at high risk of emergency admission, health care utilization, post-discharge nurse practitioner-led telemedicine visits, monitoring patients on immunomodulatory and advanced therapies, implementing health maintenance, and collaboration with members of multidisciplinary teams caring for people with IBD, needs to be explored further.

E-health could help reduce the out-of-pocket expense for patients. Li et al. noted a significant drop in out-of pocket-expense per patient without affecting overall quality of care, after initiation of a telemedicine program for IBD patients attending their centre.68 A cost-effectiveness analysis done by De Jong et al. concluded that using an e-health platform [myIBDcoach] resulted in a mean cost reduction of €547/patient.69 In focusing on this outcome, it is important to consider that value is a ratio of quality/cost [V = Q/C], and indeed a non-inferior intervention to SoC that lowers overall costs would lead to a higher ‘value’.8 The implementation of e-health technology across the current health care systems requires both commitment and the necessary funding. A systematic review by Kruse et al., evaluating the barriers towards implementation of e-health technologies, noted that technical challenges, cost, and resistance to change were the major hindrances.70 These factors may eventually lead to poor adherence to the e-health programs and may result in exhaustion of resources without demonstratable benefit. The included studies did not report significant differences in the uptake of the e-health technologies in a randomised controlled trial setting with a limited follow-up period. Further studies are required to assess the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of e-health programs in real-world settings prior to implementation on a larger-scale.

We acknowledge several limitations in our review. We excluded non-English language published studies as well as unpublished studies [reporting or ‘file drawer’ bias from negative unpublished studies] which could be a source of bias. Moreover, the inability to blind the participants as well as the investigators, due to the nature of the intervention, could result in a bias, affecting treatment effect sizes. Consequently, RoB 2 assessments of included studies for the primary outcomes ranged from high risk of bias to some concerns, resulting in the overall low-to-moderate quality of evidence on GRADE assessment. Inherent in the nature of these studies are factors such as patient-reported outcomes, which are subject to recall bias, attrition leading to smaller sample size, and shorter duration of follow-up affecting the rate of adoption and effectiveness of digital health technologies. Most studies were conducted at large tertiary care IBD centres, with the high likelihood that the SoC group received excellent follow-up, potentially diluting the effect of the e-health intervention. Furthermore, the inadequacy of socioeconomic data from the included studies may limit the understanding of the impact of e-health interventions on different patient populations, which could limit identification of potential areas for improvement in the delivery of e-health services. In performing the meta-analysis, we noted significant conceptual heterogeneity due to the varied models of e-health delivery between studies. We attempted to mitigate this by using a random-effects model for our analysis. We could not obtain adjusted values for the outcomes measures and used the unadjusted measures provided in the published studies. As a result, this could affect the robustness of the pooled outcome. That said, our study has several strengths. We used the most up-to-date systematic review methodology including PRISMA 2020, RoB 2 tool, and the GRADE tool. Our search strategy comprised all major databases to include all relevant studies. The inclusion of only RCTs ensured a better grade of evidence synthesised. Furthermore, including only e-health modalities with active HCP monitoring and input helped reduce the patient bias often encountered among self-management e-health tools.

5. Conclusion

E-health interventions may have the potential to serve as an adjunct to standard care in management of IBD, offering unique opportunities to enhance care for patients with IBD. Methodological limitations notwithstanding, e-health interventions appear ‘at least as good’ as standard of care for clinical controlling clinical activity. Their positive impact on quality of life and IBD knowledge are encouraging, and should serve as an impetus for future studies exploring specific aspects of the setting, design, delivery, and cost-effectiveness of such interventions. Accumulating robust outcome data will inform future iterations of this technology, improving and exemplifying participatory medicine.
