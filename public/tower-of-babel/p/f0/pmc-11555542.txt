Phenotyping Kidney Function in Young Adults With High Blood Pressure: The African‐PREDICT Study
Authors: Anja Degenaar, Ruan Kruger, Adriaan Jacobs, Catharina M C Mels
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBiomarkers of kidney function, including glomerular, tubular, and fibrotic markers, have been associated with blood pressure in elderly populations and individuals with kidney and cardiovascular diseases. However, limited information is available in young adults. In this study, we compared levels of several kidney function biomarkers between normotensive and hypertensive young adults and explored the associations of these biomarkers with blood pressure within these groups. In this cross‐sectional assessment, twenty‐four‐hour (24‐h) blood pressure measurements of 1055 participants (mean age = 24.6 years) were used to classify hypertension as per the 2018 ESC/ESH guidelines. Biomarkers of kidney function included estimated glomerular filtration rate, urinary albumin, alpha‐1 microglobulin (uA1M), neutrophil gelatinase‐associated lipocalin (uNGAL), uromodulin (uUMOD), and the CKD273 classifier. All urinary biomarkers, except for the CKD273 classifier, were standardized for urinary creatinine (Cr). In the hypertensive group (61.0% White; 73.2% men), urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (uACR), uNGAL/Cr and uUMOD/Cr were lower than the normotensive group. In multiple regression analyses, 24‐h systolic blood pressure (SBP) (β = 0.14; p = 0.042), 24‐h diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (β = 0.14; p = 0.040), and 24‐h mean arterial pressure (MAP) (β = 0.16; p = 0.020) associated positively with uA1M/Cr in the hypertensive group, while 24‐h MAP positively associated with uACR (β = 0.17; p = 0.017). In exploratory factor analysis, positive associations of 24‐h DBP and 24‐h MAP with a factor pattern including tubular biomarkers were observed in the hypertensive group (24‐h DBP: β = 0.18; p = 0.026, 24‐h MAP: β = 0.17; p = 0.032). In the setting of hypertension, high perfusion pressure in the kidneys may play a role in the development of proximal tubule damage and promote early deterioration in kidney function in young adults. Introduction

Sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA) has an alarmingly high prevalence of cardiovascular diseases [1, 2]. Primary hypertension is becoming an increasing concern among young SSA adults, with one in 10 adolescents (10–19 years) found to be living with elevated blood pressure in 2023 [3]. Given that the kidneys play an essential role in regulating and maintaining circulatory volume and blood pressure, it is no surprise that hypertension and deterioration in kidney function are closely linked [4, 5, 6]. Investigating kidney‐related biomarkers and hypertension at an early stage of disease development are therefore needed, particularly in young populations living in low‐resource settings such as SSA.

Although the relationship between blood pressure and kidney dysfunction has been studied extensively, studies primarily used the most recognized measures of kidney function and injury, that is, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (uACR) [6, 7, 8, 9]. However, these kidney function biomarkers mainly indicate glomerular damage which is generally only present at a later stage of disease progression [10, 11, 12]. Thus, it is important to consider other kidney function biomarkers that can provide insights into subcellular alterations involved in the early stages of deterioration in kidney function. Several biomarkers have emerged that can be utilized to assess tubular health [13]. Some of these biomarkers represent proximal tubule dysfunction (urinary alpha‐1 microglobulin [uA1M]), tubular injury (urinary neutrophil gelatinase‐associated lipocalin [uNGAL]), and impaired loop of Henle synthetic function and tubular reserve (urinary uromodulin [uUMOD]) [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. In addition to tubular dysfunction and injury, kidney fibrosis is also a predominant pathological process involved in the deterioration of kidney function [20, 21, 22]. In turn, the CKD273 proteomics classifier has been found to be a useful biomarker of extracellular matrix remodeling that can identify and predict fibrosis at an early stage of disease development [20, 21]. While studies have investigated the link between these kidney biomarkers and blood pressure in older individuals and in individuals with established kidney and cardiovascular diseases [14, 15, 23–25], limited information is available in young apparently healthy adults. The aim of this study was to profile kidney function biomarkers (eGFR, uACR, uA1M, uNGAL, uUMOD, and CKD273 classifier) in young adults stratified by hypertension status. The secondary aim was to investigate associations between blood pressure and these kidney function biomarkers in the same groups.

Methods

Study Population and Organizational Procedures

This study formed part of the African Prospective study on the Early Detection and Identification of Cardiovascular Disease and Hypertension (African‐PREDICT) [26]. The African‐PREDICT study is a prospective study focusing on characterizing and monitoring early pathophysiology associated with hypertension development. The recruitment of participants was done via trained field workers, through the workplace, and advertisements in local newspapers, noticeboards, and on radio stations. Young, apparently healthy Black and White men and women between the ages of 20 and 30 years were invited to take part in the study. Interested participants underwent a screening phase and those who met the criteria were included in the study. The specific inclusion criteria were being a permanent resident of Potchefstroom or surrounding areas (North West province, South Africa), clinic normotensive in the screening phase (office blood pressure < 140/90 mm Hg), HIV‐uninfected, no self‐reported pregnancy or lactating women, no self‐reported previous diagnosis of chronic disease nor using any medication for chronic disease, no recent surgery or trauma (within the past 3 months) or a previous history of cardiovascular disease. This sub‐study cross‐sectionally examined baseline data from the African‐PREDICT study. Participants with missing data for twenty‐four‐hour (24‐h) ambulatory blood pressure (N = 82) and biomarkers of kidney function (eGFR, uACR, uA1M, uNGAL, uUMOD, or the CKD273 classifier) were excluded (N = 125), resulting in a total of 1055 participants.

The African‐PREDICT study (NWU‐00001‐12‐A1) as well as this cross‐sectional study (NWU‐00149‐22‐A1) were approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee of the North‐West University, adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was registered in a clinical trial registry. All procedures were thoroughly explained and written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before any measurements were taken. Data were collected and managed using the REDCap electronic data capture system.

Questionnaires and General Demographics

Data on age, sex, ethnicity, smoking, alcohol intake, medication use, and family history of cardiovascular disease were obtained using a general demographic and health questionnaire. Socioeconomic status (SES) score was calculated using a point system that was adapted from Kuppuswamy's SES Scale [27] for a South African context.

Anthropometric and Physical Activity Measurements

All anthropometric measurements were performed based on the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry guidelines. Measurements were taken to obtain body height (SECA 213 Portable Stadiometer, SECA, Hamburg, Germany), weight (SECA 813 Electronic Scale, SECA, Hamburg, Germany), and waist circumference (Lufkin Steel Anthropometric Tape (W606PM); Lufkin, Apex, USA). Subsequently, body mass index (BMI) (weight (kg)/height (m2)) and waist‐to‐height ratio (WHtR) were calculated. Participants were also fitted with a compact, chest‐worn accelerometric device (ActiHeart 4 CamNtech Ltd., UK) which was used to quantitatively measure physical activity for the calculation of activity energy expenditure (AEE) (indexed by weight and expressed as kCal/kg/day). The ActiHeart device was worn for a maximum of 7 days.

Cardiovascular Measurements

We measured 24‐h blood pressure by using an ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) device (Card(X)plore, Meditech, Budapest, Hungary). Ambulatory measurements included systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and pulse pressure (PP). In addition, mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated using the following formula: (DBP) + (0.412*PP). An appropriate‐sized cuff was fitted to the participants’ non‐dominant arm and participants were given instructions on how to ensure successful inflations across the 24‐h period. The ABPM device was programmed to take recordings every 30 min during the day (06:00–22:00) and every hour during the night (22:00–06:00). The mean successful inflation rate for this study population over a 24‐h period was 88.4 ± 11.7%. Participants were also given an ambulatory diary card where they reported all activities that might have influenced blood pressure during the 24‐h period.

We used the 2018 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and European Society of Hypertension (ESH) Guidelines for the management of arterial hypertension to measure blood pressure [28]. Participants were classified as hypertensive with an average 24‐h blood pressure ≥130/80 mm Hg over the 24‐h period, ≥135/85 mm Hg for the daytime (awake) average, and ≥120/70 mm Hg for the nighttime (asleep) average.

Office blood pressure was measured by using the Dinamap Procare 100 Vital Signs Monitor (GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, USA) with appropriately sized GE Critikon latex‐free Dura‐Cuffs. Before the measurement commenced, participants were requested not to have eaten, smoked, or exercised for at least 30 min beforehand. The first measurement was taken on the left arm after the participant had been in a seated resting state for 5 min (with their arm supported at heart level). Thereafter, blood pressure was taken in duplicate on the right arm and a final measurement was done on the left upper arm. There was a 5‐min interval between the first two measurements and the duplicate measurements. The mean of all four blood pressure measurements were used and for each measurement; the SBP, DBP, PP, and heart rate were recorded and MAP was calculated.

Blood Sampling and Biochemical Analyses

Participants were requested to fast overnight. Blood samples were taken by a registered research nurse in the early morning and participants were requested to provide a spot urine sample. After biological samples had been collected, all samples were taken to the on‐site research laboratory to be prepared, aliquoted into cryovials, and stored in bio‐freezers (−80°C).

Basic serum analyses were performed for C‐reactive protein (CRP) (high‐sensitivity), gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), albumin, creatinine, cystatin C, and lipids (high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL‐C], low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL‐C], total cholesterol, and triglycerides). The triglyceride to HDL‐C ratio was also calculated. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was analyzed in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) whole blood samples and glucose levels were determined in sodium fluoride plasma samples. All the above measurements were performed with the Cobas Integra 400 plus analyzer (Roche, Basel, Switzerland).

Given that serum cystatin C it is not influenced by non–kidney‐related factors such as muscle mass and that it improves the sensitivity and specificity by which adverse changes in kidney function are identified [29], we used the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD‐EPI) cystatin C formula to calculate eGFR. Albumin and creatinine levels were determined in spot urine samples, which was then used to calculate uACR. Spot urine samples were used to measure uA1M and uNGAL as part of a bead‐based multiplex immunoassay, which allows the simultaneous quantification of multiple markers. These immunoassays were performed on a Luminex 200 system (Luminex, Austin, Texas, USA). The uUMOD concentration was measured by ELISA as previously described [30] with a standard curve generated from human UMOD (stock solution, 100 µg/mL; Millipore). Urinary A1M, NGAL, and UMOD were indexed to urinary creatinine to account for urine tonicity. For urinary proteomics, spot urine samples were analyzed by Capillary Electrophoresis Time‐of‐Flight Mass Spectrometry (CE‐TOF‐MS) as previously described [31].

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 software (IBM Corporation; Armonk, New York, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 5.03 (GraphPad Software Inc., California, USA) was used for the graphical illustrations of the data. Variables were tested for normality by using Q–Q plots and logarithmically transformed if skewed. Log‐transformed variables included total cholesterol, LDL‐C, triglycerides, triglyceride to HDL‐C ratio, glucose, CRP, GGT, uACR, uA1M/Cr, uNGAL/Cr, and uUMOD/Cr. Continuous data with a normal distribution were reported as the arithmetic mean and standard deviation, logarithmically transformed variables were presented by the geometric mean and 5th and 95th percentile intervals and categorical data as proportions.

Normotensive and hypertensive groups were compared using independent t‐tests for continuous variables and Chi‐square tests for categorical variables. Pearson and partial correlations were used to explore associations between 24‐h blood pressure (24‐h SBP, 24‐h DBP, and 24‐h MAP) as the dependent variable and kidney function biomarkers (eGFR, uACR, uA1M/Cr, uNGAL/Cr, uUMOD/Cr, and CKD273) as main independent variables, adjusted for potential confounders (age, sex, and ethnicity) in the case of partial correlations. Backward multiple regression analyses were performed to determine independent associations of 24‐h blood pressure with different kidney function biomarkers. Variables considered for entry in the multivariable regression analysis were chosen based on exploratory Pearson correlations. Covariates included in the models were age, sex, ethnicity, SES score, waist circumference, triglyceride to HDL‐C ratio, glucose, CRP, GGT, self‐reported smoking, and AEE. A sensitivity analysis was performed by adding eGFR as a covariate in the multiple regression models with uA1M/Cr, uNGAL/Cr, uUMOD/Cr, or CKD273 classifier as main independent variables to determine if the associations with blood pressure are independent of glomerular function. Partial correlations were used to determine how biomarkers of kidney function (eGFR, urinary albumin, uA1M, uNGAL, uUMOD, and CKD273) associated with one another, while adjusting for urinary creatinine.

A sensitivity analysis was done by performing exploratory factor analyses with the different kidney function biomarkers. We included the individual urinary biomarkers without correcting for urinary creatinine to avoid multicollinearity. Principal component analyses were used and factor patterns with an eigenvalue > 1 were retained. The Oblimin with Kaiser normalization rotation was used to obtain independent interpretable factor patterns. A factor loading ≥0.5 was used to interpret the factor patterns. Subsequently, the identified factor patterns were used in multiple regression analyses to determine associations with 24‐h blood pressure.

The normotensive and hypertensive groups differed significantly in terms of sex (p < 0.001) and ethnic (p = 0.001) distribution, with the hypertensive group consisting mostly of White (61.0%) men (73.2%). As expected, individuals in the hypertensive group had higher measures of adiposity, glucose, GGT, and an adverse lipid profile (all p < 0.001) compared to the normotensive group. In terms of kidney function biomarkers, uACR (p = 0.004), uNGAL/Cr (p < 0.001), and uUMOD/Cr (p < 0.045) were lower in the hypertensive group than the normotensive group.

Discussion

In young adults, stratified by hypertension status, we compared kidney function biomarkers (eGFR, uACR, uA1M/Cr, uNGAL/Cr, uUMOD/Cr, and CKD273 classifier) and determined associations with 24‐h blood pressure. Even though we included clinically normotensive participants, some individuals already had elevated blood pressure based on 24‐h ABPM values. The hypertensive group consisted mainly of White men and had lower mean levels of uACR, uNGAL/Cr, and uUMOD/Cr compared to the normotensive group. Furthermore, 24‐h blood pressure (24‐h SBP, 24‐h DBP, and 24‐h MAP) was associated positively with uA1M/Cr, while 24‐h MAP also positively associated with uACR, in the hypertensive group. When using exploratory factor analysis, we found positive associations of 24‐h DBP and 24‐h MAP with a factor pattern consisting of tubule function and injury biomarkers in the total and hypertensive groups.

Although the link between blood pressure and uA1M have been explored in elderly populations and individuals with kidney, cardiovascular, and other diseases [15, 23, 24, 32], our study is one of the few to observe a significant relationship in young adults (under 30 years of age) without overt cardiovascular disease. The positive associations of 24‐h blood pressure with uA1M/Cr in the hypertensive group also confirm the findings from another South African study conducted in Black and White children between the ages of 5–9 years [33]. In addition, our results were independent of eGFR, indicating that uA1M may provide additional information on kidney health that may be particularly relevant in the setting of hypertension. Since A1M is a low‐molecular‐weight glycoprotein freely filtered through the glomeruli and extensively reabsorbed in the proximal tubule cells, it is an ideal biomarker for proximal tubule function [34, 35, 36]. Any increase in the urinary excretion of A1M is indicative of a reduction in the reabsorption capacity of the proximal tubule cells, suggesting that uA1M is a sensitive, non‐invasive biomarker of kidney tubular function [17, 34, 36]. The proximal tubule cells play a key role in maintaining water and electrolyte (including sodium) balance and any alterations in reabsorption capacity will therefore disrupt urinary homeostasis [7, 37]. Furthermore, the systemic high blood pressure present in the hypertensive group could potentially lead to increased systemic vascular resistance and gradually damage the arteries in the kidneys [5]. Given that the associations of 24‐h blood pressures with uA1M/Cr were only present in the hypertensive group of our study, our results suggests that even in young adults, the potential onset of early proximal tubule damage is already evident in the presence of hypertension.

It is well established that hypertension is closely related to albuminuria [8, 9, 38, 39]. Generally reported as uACR, albuminuria is indicative of increased glomerular permeability and has been suggested to be a biomarker of glomerular endothelial damage [10, 11]. The positive association between 24‐h MAP and uACR in the hypertensive group of our study is therefore in line with the literature. Our results further confirm previous findings that have demonstrated uACR levels, even within the normal range (<30 mg/g), are already associated with hypertension [38, 39]. Therefore, in the setting of hypertension, high perfusion pressure at the glomerulus may already promote glomerular endothelial damage and contribute to the development of early kidney damage.

We used exploratory factor analysis to derive factor patterns from the kidney function biomarkers included in this study. In both the total and hypertensive group, we found that 24‐h DBP and 24‐h MAP were only associated with a factor pattern reflecting kidney tubule biomarkers. This factor pattern comprised uA1M, uNGAL, and uUMOD concentrations, which are indicative of proximal tubule dysfunction, tubule injury, and impaired tubular reserve and loop of Henle synthetic function, respectively [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Another factor pattern was generated and included a biomarker of glomerular function (eGFR) and kidney fibrosis (CKD273 classifier). However, no associations were observed in terms of blood pressure and this factor pattern in any of the groups. Our results are in agreement with previous studies which indicated that assessing a combination of kidney tubular biomarkers may provide novel insights into early alterations in kidney function that are distinct from conventional glomerular biomarkers (eGFR and uACR) [7, 40, 41]. Furthermore, these results highlight that biomarkers of tubular health may be important to consider during early deterioration in kidney function, particularly in the presence of hypertension.

When comparing kidney function biomarkers between normotensive and hypertensive groups, we found lower levels of uACR, uNGAL/Cr, and uUMOD/Cr in the hypertensive group. The latter result is in line with previous studies that have suggested lower uUMOD levels to be associated with an increased risk of hypertension and cardiovascular disease development [15, 41, 42]. However, given that increased uACR and uNGAL is generally considered to be indicative of deterioration in kidney function, the lower levels in the hypertensive group compared to the normotensive group were surprising. It is important to note that our study population consisted of young apparently healthy adults without any established kidney or cardiovascular diseases. Therefore, even though differences in biomarker levels were found, the concentrations were still within normal physiological ranges and not regarded as pathological. In addition, our participants did not have sustained hypertension and therefore the increased pressure and subsequent level of injury in the kidneys may have been insufficient to induce the upregulation of these kidney damage biomarkers. Furthermore, our hypertensive group consisted mainly of men, which in turn may also account for the lower levels of these biomarkers. This is supported by previous studies that indicated participants with higher uNGAL levels were generally female [39, 43, 44]. The authors proposed that sex differences in the excretion of uNGAL may reflect an estrogen‐mediated difference in the expression thereof in the kidneys [43]. Future studies are needed to explore the associations between uACR, uNGAL/Cr, hypertension, and the mechanisms through which sex may modify this relationship in young adults.

Our findings must be interpreted within the context of its strengths and limitations. This is a cross‐sectional study, limiting our ability to determine exact mechanisms and causal relationships. Our hypertensive group consisted mostly of White men, highlighting the need for future studies to include larger hypertensive populations with participants who are more representative of the general population. Our study only included apparently healthy individuals from the North West province of South Africa and therefore our findings cannot be generalized to the entire South African population or to populations with established kidney and cardiovascular diseases. We used the 2018 ESC/ESH guidelines to measure 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure and therefore our measurement frequency was less than the recent 2024 practice guideline recommendations. Although the biomarkers of kidney function were measured by validated assays, they were only measured once. In addition, even though we identified a factor pattern consisting of tubular health biomarkers through exploratory factor analyses, the cumulative percentage of the factor pattern was below 50%. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution and the factor pattern needs to be validated in other cohorts. This was a well‐planned study that was executed under strict conditions in a controlled environment and well‐equipped research facility. Furthermore, the importance of other biomarkers, aside from eGFR and uACR which are clinically relevant, is emphasized by this study's findings whereby other markers of early kidney damage may be more sensitive to identify at‐risk individuals from young ages.

Conclusion

Our results highlight that in young adults with hypertension, higher perfusion pressure may already promote the onset of proximal tubule damage, further contributing to early kidney dysfunction. Since both hypertension and kidney disease are silent conditions, it is important to investigate how uA1M can be utilized in a clinical context, particularly in early preventive screening and intervention strategies.
