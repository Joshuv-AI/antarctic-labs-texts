Comparative analysis of prognostic scoring systems for cats with suspected pancreatitis (472 cats)
Authors: Harry Cridge, Julia Winser, Julia Wells, Timothy Bolton, Sue Yee Lim, Ashley Kibodeaux, Ileana Michel, Betty Chow, Jared A Jaffey, Cameron Prior, Fergus Allerton, Sarah Guess, Ruby Prince, Andrew Mackin, Sichao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVarious prognostic factors and schemes are proposed for use in cats with pancreatitis, but these have yet to be independently evaluated in a broad group of cats.Hypothesis/Objectives(1) To compare predicted prognosis for death and prolonged (≥5 days) hospitalization across biochemical variables and composite schemes and (2) to calculate discriminatory ability for length of hospitalization.AnimalsFour hundred seventy-two client-owned cats with suspected pancreatitis.MethodsMulti-institutional retrospective study (2019-2024). Prognostic factors included potassium, feline pancreas-specific lipase, total bilirubin, total calcium, and total magnesium. Prognostic schemes retrospectively calculated were the Acute Patient Physiologic and Laboratory Evaluation (APPLE)full and fast, and the modified feline activity index (MFAI).ResultsTotal calcium (median: 9.3 [IQR: 8.7-9.9] vs 9.6 [IQR: 9.0-10.1] mg/dL, P = .03), APPLEfull (median: 41 [IQR: 35-46] vs 36 [IQR: 30-40], P < .001), APPLEfast (median: 20.5 [IQR: 14.5-27.0] vs 14.0 [IQR: 12.0-19.0], P < .001), and MFAI (median: 5 [IQR: 4-8] vs 4 [IQR: 3-6], P = .011) were significantly different between fatal and nonfatal cases. Acute Patient Physiologic and Laboratory Evaluationfast had the greatest area under the curve for case fatality (0.695; 95% CI, 0.617-0.773) while MFAI had the greatest concordance index with length of hospitalization (0.673; 95% CI, 0.639-0.706). Overall negative predictive values were high and positive predictive values (PPVs) were low for both case fatality and prolonged hospitalization.Conclusions and clinical importanceComposite scoring schemes appear to be of greater prognostic value than individual variables in cats with pancreatitis. Because of the low PPVs for case fatality and prolonged hospitalization, prognostic factors should be interpreted cautiously on an individual cat basis and should not serve as the basis of clinically consequential decisions.Keywords: feline, MFAI, outcome, pancreatic lipase, Spec fPL

Introduction

Pancreatitis is increasingly recognized in cats and presents with a broad clinical manifestation.1 This broad clinical manifestation results in highly variable disease outcomes with some animals having minimal clinical signs while other cats rapidly progress towards death. Case fatality rates in specialty care cohorts can be 9%-41%.2–5 This broad clinical manifestation and variable disease progression make it challenging to predict outcome in individual cats and likewise makes it challenging to account for disease severity in statistical models. This becomes particularly relevant when evaluating the efficacy of potential therapeutic interventions.

Despite these challenges, several studies have attempted to identify prognostic indicators in cats with pancreatitis, ranging from individual clinicopathologic abnormalities to composite scoring schemes. One of the most commonly reported negative prognostic indicators in cats with pancreatitis is ionized hypocalcemia.2,3,6 Hyperkalemia has also been identified as a potential prognostic indicator.4 In addition, dysmagnesemia might be a negative prognostic factor in broad groups of hospitalized cats.7 The presence of jaundice, biochemically manifested as hyperbilirubinemia, is also a commonly cited negative prognostic factor in cats with pancreatitis and likely represents the consequence of extrahepatic biliary duct obstruction, concurrent hepatic lipidosis, or a necroinflammatory hepatobiliary condition—or a combination thereof.3,8 Anecdotally, markedly elevated pancreatic lipase concentrations (Spec fPL) might be associated with a poor outcome, but there are limited data to support this assertion.4 Many of the studies that derive the aforementioned reported prognostic factors are limited by small geographically restricted cohorts, a lack of external validation, or anecdotal reports of subsequent poor performance, and as such these factors warrant reinvestigation. In dogs, composite clinical scoring schemes are frequently utilized, rather than individual clinicopathologic variables. This approach might offer a more comprehensive assessment of disease severity. The modified canine/clinical activity index (MCAI) has been shown to be significantly different between dogs that die and dogs that survive pancreatitis.9 Non-pancreatitis-specific schemes such as the Acute Patient Physiologic and Laboratory Evaluation (APPLE) scoring scheme are predictive of outcome in dogs with pancreatitis.9 It is therefore plausible that equivalents of these schemes could be prognostic in cats with pancreatitis.

The proliferation of existing and newly developed schemes likely suggests that no single method has clearly and consistently demonstrated superiority to date. Thus, the aims of our study were: (1) assess the above prognostic factors and feline equivalent scoring schemes for predicting prolonged hospitalization and case fatality in a broad group of cats with suspected pancreatitis and (2) to calculate concordance index between each factor or scheme and length of hospitalization. These aims might help to elucidate whether a superior scheme exists or if further refinement of existing schemes is needed in the future.

Materials and methods

Animals and case definitions

Cases were identified by searching the medical record systems at 6 academic teaching hospitals and 2 private referral hospitals for cats with an acute onset of pancreatitis between January 1st, 2019, and December 31st, 2024. Geographic regions included cats from the Midwest, Southeast, Southwest, and West Coast of the United States, and a cohort from the United Kingdom. All practices were multi-specialty referral hospitals that are thought to practice a similarly high level of medicine. Pancreatitis was defined based on an overall assessment of the available history, clinical signs, pancreatic lipase quantification, and the results of abdominal ultrasonography or computed tomography (CT).10 Specifically, all cats had to have a minimum of a compatible clinical history/physical examination, and either a pancreatic lipase concentration above reference interval (7 sites utilized Spec fPL) or activity (1 site utilized DGGR lipase or Spec fPL), or imaging evidence of pancreatitis (ultrasonography or CT), or both. This mimics the criteria utilized in a prior study of 157 cats with suspected pancreatitis.3

Data collection and disease severity indices

Medical records were analyzed, and the following data were extracted from each cat: age, sex (male neutered, male intact, female spayed, and female intact), breed, body weight (kg), clinical signs, physical examination findings, clinicopathologic data, diagnostic imaging results (ultrasound, CT, or both), length of hospitalization, and outcome (alive vs dead or euthanized). The presence of known comorbidities was also recorded and was broadly grouped into the following categories: cardiopulmonary, endocrine, gastrointestinal, hematologic and immune-mediated, hepatobiliary, renal and urinary, systemic infectious, systemic neoplastic, and miscellaneous disease. Multiple categories were permitted if > 1 comorbid disease was present.

The following data were specifically noted from each medical record (where available): pancreatic lipase concentration via Spec fPL (μg/L) or DGGR lipase activity (U/L), total bilirubin (tBili) concentration (mg/dL), total calcium (tCa) concentration (mg/dL), potassium (K) concentration (mmol/L), and total magnesium (tMg) concentration (mg/dL). In addition, it was recorded whether the above variables were below, within, or above the specific laboratory reference interval. For each cat, the following prognostic scoring systems were retrospectively calculated from the first available data in the medical record: APPLEfast, APPLEfull, and MCAI.11–13 The MCAI comprises assessment of the animals activity, appetite, vomiting status, cranial abdominal pain, dehydration, and stool characteristics.11 Throughout the remainder of the manuscript MCAI will be referred to as the modified feline activity index (MFAI) due to the species studied in this manuscript, and is considered an exploratory analysis. Acute Patient Physiologic and Laboratory Evaluationfast comprises assessment of mentation, body temperature, blood pressure, lactate, and PCV.13 Acute Patient Physiologic and Laboratory Evaluationfull comprises the above data, with the addition of urea, chloride, and the presence or absence of cavitary effusions.13 When a point-of-care fluid assessment was not available on presentation, subsequent diagnostic imaging reports were evaluated for the presence of cavitary effusions as part of determination of the APPLEfull score. Otherwise, missing data was treated as such, and the corresponding score system that lacked data was not utilized for statistical analysis. Duration of hospitalization and case fatality was used as surrogate markers of disease severity. If death was recorded > 90 days after initial diagnosis it was excluded from prognostic calculations due to concern for being an unrelated event, unless the cause of euthanasia or mortality was expressly stated as being due to pancreatitis in the medical record. Limiting the window to 3 months was designed to reduce the likelihood that death due to another unrelated factor or disease was not inappropriately attributed to pancreatitis.

Statistical analysis

Study cohort characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics and are reported as median, IQR, and range (minimum–maximum). Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to compare median scores for each of the scoring systems between cats that died or were euthanized and those that survived. Similar analyses were also performed for cats that were hospitalized for < 5 days compared to those that were hospitalized for ≥ 5 days (“prolonged hospitalization”), mimicking cut-offs for prolonged hospitalization in a prior study.9 Each scoring system was also used to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the binary outcomes of death and prolonged hospitalization (≥5 days).14 Optimal cutoff values were selected based on Youden index. For length of hospitalization, Harrell’s concordance index (c-index) was calculated for each score system to determine the discriminatory ability of each prognostic scoring system. This was derived from Cox proportional hazards models. C-index expresses the probability that, a randomly chosen pair of observations, will have their length of hospitalization accurately ranked by the model. All analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software (v. 4.4.1; R Core Team 2024) (https://www.R-project.org/).15 For all analyses, a P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Animals

Four hundred seventy-two (n = 472) cats were included in the study. The median age of enrolled cats was 12 years (IQR: 8-14 years, range: 3 months-20 years). Two-hundred and ninety-three cats were male (288 castrated and 5 intact) and 179 were female (175 spayed and 4 intact). The median weight was 4.5 kg (IQR: 3.4-5.5 kg, range: 1.3-13.1 kg). The median duration of hospitalization was 2 days (IQR: 0-4 days, range: 0-19 days). One hundred eight (22.9%) cats were hospitalized for ≥ 5 days. The median duration of follow up after discharge was 23 days (IQR: 4-166 days, range: 0-1972 days). Seventy cats (14.8%) died or were euthanized within 90 days of pancreatitis diagnosis (all-cause case fatality). Missing data resulted in the following numbers of cats utilized in each prognostic assessment: K (n = 458), Spec fPL (n = 378), tBili (n = 454), tCa (n = 452), tMg (n = 218), APPLEfast (n = 430), APPLEfull (n = 422), and MFAI (n = 472). Many cats had one or more comorbidities as anticipated in a cohort of older cats. One-hundred and fifty-two cats (32.2%) had renal or urinary disease, 143 cats (30.3%) had gastrointestinal disease, 137 cats (29.0%) had endocrine disease, 110 cats (23.3%) had cardiopulmonary disease, 104 cats (22.0%) had hepatobiliary disease, 59 cats (12.5%) had hematologic or immune mediated disease, 47 cats (10.0%) had systemic neoplasia, and 34 cats (7.2%) had systemic infectious disease. Seventy-six cats (16.3%) had miscellaneous disorders.

Differences in prognostic factors between outcome groups

Death

K (P = .61), Spec fPL (P = .80), tBili (P = .18), and tMg (P = .57) concentrations were not significantly different between cats that died and those that survived.

Concordance between prognostic markers and length of hospitalization

Harrell’s c-index is an overall assessment of the capacity of each factor or scheme to predict hospitalization length independent of an ROC selected cut-off value. The MFAI had the greatest c-index with length of hospitalization (0.673; 95% CI, 0.639-0.706), followed by tBili (0.655; 95% CI, 0.621-0.690), APPLEfast (0.646; 95% CI, 0.614-0.679), and APPLEfull (0.631; 95% CI, 0.597-0.665). The Spec fPL, tCa, K, and tMg all had c-index with length of hospitalization of < 0.600. C-index was not calculated for prediction of death due to low case numbers in each cohort.

Discussion

Our study aimed to evaluate the prognostic capability of various biochemical variables and scoring schemes in a large cohort of cats (n = 472) with suspected pancreatitis. The only biochemical variable that was significantly different between cats that died or were euthanized and cats that survived was tCa, with a lower median value identified in those cats that went on to die or were euthanized. Lower tCa concentrations likely occur in pancreatitis due to sequestration of calcium in peripancreatic fat and increases in plasma free fatty acid concentrations.16,17 Total calcium concentrations were also significantly different between cats with prolonged vs non-prolonged hospitalization. The results of our study mirror the finding of previous studies in identifying serum calcium concentration as a prognostic indicator in cats with pancreatitis.2,3,6 It is important to note that our study specifically investigated tCa whereas prior studies have investigated iCa as a prognostic factor in cats with pancreatitis.2,3,6 Our study demonstrates only a small AUC (0.582) with regard to the relationship between the ROC optimized tCa cut-off and 90-day case fatality, and a small AUC (0.610) for prolonged hospitalization. This combined with the overall poor discriminatory ability (c-index) between tCa and length of hospitalization suggests that while tCa is significantly different between the aforementioned cohorts, its overall predictive capacity is rather limited. The overlap in tCa concentrations between cats with and without poor outcomes also suggests limited predictive utility on an individual cat basis.

In contrast to prediction for case fatality, several studied biochemical variables investigated were different between cats with prolonged vs non-prolonged hospitalization, including Mg, K, tBili, and Spec fPL concentrations. Significant overlap, however, was noted between the cohorts. The difference in predictive performance of these variables for case fatality vs prolonged hospitalization could be due to the study being underpowered for detection of case fatality; however, this is considered less likely since this study remains one of the largest studies of pancreatitis in cats to date. The difference could also be due to the advanced medical care received by the cats in which factors that would otherwise affect prognosis were managed appropriately through early recognition and therapeutic manipulation (eg, electrolyte monitoring/supplementation and control of losses).

Serum tBili concentrations had the greatest AUC (0.654) for prolonged hospitalization of all evaluated biochemical variables and prognostic schemes, despite it not being significantly different between survivors and non-survivors. The c-index between tBili and length of hospitalization was also the second highest (behind MFAI) at 0.655. This might suggest that patients with higher tBili concentrations often receive more prolonged in-hospital monitoring or require more prolonged supportive management, without a significantly detectable increase in case fatality. Thus, tBili might be a useful indicator for resource planning or intensity of care rather than as a case fatality predictor. This is of relevance as extrahepatic biliary duct obstruction, clinically manifested as hyperbilirubinemia, is often considered to have a poor prognosis in cats.18,19 The difference in study findings likely reflects variations in the degree of tBili elevation between study groups, this study’s focus on biochemical hyperbilirubinemia rather than surgically managed extrahepatic biliary duct obstruction, and the inflammatory nature of any extrahepatic biliary duct obstruction, if present, in our cohort. Studies note that case fatality is lower in cats with non-neoplastic (inflammatory) rather than neoplastic causes of extrahepatic biliary duct obstruction.19,20 Other potential causes for the tBili elevation include concurrent hepatic lipidosis or necroinflammatory hepatobiliary disorders, or both.

In dogs, composite scoring schemes have been studied as prognostic indicators in pancreatitis.9,11,21–24 In a study of over 500 dogs with pancreatitis, APPLEfull and MCAI were significantly different between survivors and non-survivors and those schemes had the greatest discriminatory ability with the length of hospitalization.9 This prompted the assessment of APPLEfull, APPLEfast, and MFAI in our cohort of cats with suspected pancreatitis. While only one biochemical variable was significantly different between survivors and non-survivors, all 3 composite schemes were different, suggesting that composite schemes might predict case fatality better than individual biochemical variables in cats. This is logical since composite schemes are more likely to provide a thorough assessment of an animal’s clinical status, as compared to a single biochemical variable. Similarly, all 3 schemes were different between cats with prolonged vs non-prolonged hospitalization. However, their overall predictive capacity is more comprehensively assessed when evaluating AUC data for fatality and prolonged hospitalization and via assessment of the overall c-index between each scheme and length of hospitalization. Acute Patient Physiologic and Laboratory Evaluationfast had the greatest AUC for case fatality (0.695), though it was only marginally improved compared to the APPLEfull scoring scheme (0.648). Both APPLE schemes had a greater AUC than MFAI (0.595), although MFAI performed better than tCa (0.582) for prediction of case fatality. Modified feline activity index (MFAI) also had the greatest overall c-index with the length of hospitalization (0.673). The degree of discriminatory ability between MFAI and the length of hospitalization is logical as MFAI represents a numerical sum of a cat’s activity, appetite, presence of vomiting, presence of cranial abdominal pain, dehydration, and changes to their stool, all of which are factors often considered when determining whether an animal requires ongoing inpatient care or whether they can be discharged for at-home care. Thus these factors represent management considerations as well as consequences of disease severity.

In our study, NPVs for case fatality were high across all 3 composite schemes (78.9%-89.5%) suggesting that if a cat had a score below the derived cut-off value for case fatality it had a high, but not perfect, probability of survival. In contrast PPVs for case fatality were low (18.9%-35.7%) suggesting that a score above the derived cut-off for case fatality resulted in only a mild-to-low-moderately elevated risk of fatality on an individual cat basis. The NPVs (79.8%-90.1%) for prolonged hospitalization demonstrated similar patterns to those for case fatality, but the PPVs were relatively improved (57.0%-65.4%), but still considered inadequate. Consequently, the authors state that composite scoring schemes should be used on a population basis and not on an individual cat basis when making clinically consequential decisions. The NPV and PPV results are dependent on the case fatality and prolonged hospitalization rates in our study. It is plausible that case fatality and prolonged hospitalization rates could be higher or lower in alternate practice types and Figures 4 and 6 can be used to generalize the results of our study across a variety of clinical settings.

This study was limited by its retrospective and multi-institutional nature, which resulted in non-standardized data collection, treatments, and follow-up. In addition, this study focused on referral institutions and while attempts were made to broaden the application of these results to other practice types (Figures 4 and 5), the input data likely reflected only the more severely affected cases and this could influence the result of the study. Scoring systems were retrospectively calculated based on available case data, meaning that different numbers of cats were used to assess the prognostic capability of each scoring system. This meant that the prognostic capacity of tMg was only able to be assessed in 218/472 cats, whereas other schemes such as MFAI were able to be analyzed in all cats. Future prospective studies that comprehensively collect all data points in all cats would allow for better head-to-head comparisons between prognostic factors and schemes. Factors other than disease severity, including the presence of comorbidities and pre-evaluation treatment, could have affected the length of hospitalization or case fatality in our study. Future studies should objectively quantify pre-evaluation treatments and account for these when considering length of hospitalization. Given the study design our prediction of prolonged hospitalization is restricted to post-evaluation hospitalization and does not include any pre-referral institution treatments. The potential effect of nutrition and variations in supportive care were not specifically evaluated for in our study given its retrospective design; however, these factors could affect the length of hospitalization and case fatality rates and should be controlled for in future prospective comparisons of prognostic schemes. Case fatality in this study included both natural death and within 90-day euthanasia, unless otherwise stated that the euthanasia was due to pancreatitis. Including euthanized cats inherently introduces the potential that factors outside of disease severity such as finance, might have contributed to the poor outcome; however, it is our experience that natural death is overall uncommon and more frequently severely affected cases are euthanized and as such excluding euthanized cats would also introduce bias. Prospective explicit recording of cause of mortality and rationale would be beneficial in prospective studies. While analyzer specific reference intervals were utilized given the inclusion of 8 referral hospitals and a 5-year study timespan multiple analyzer types were likely utilized and this could lead to analytical variability in the quantification of biochemical analytes. Given the number of institutions and timespan of the study it was not possible to identify with confidence the specific analyzer used for each case and as such the potential for analytical variability acts an unaccounted for covariate in our statistical analysis. It is plausible that analytical variation could influence the results of our study. We are hopeful that the number of cats in the study helps to mitigate the influence of these factors on study findings.

In conclusion, composite scoring schemes appear to have an improved prognostic capacity when compared to individual biochemical variables for cats with pancreatitis. In referral institutions where comprehensive data are routinely collected and available, the APPLEfull and APPLEfast scoring schemes appear to be the best population-level indicators of disease severity. In contrast, MFAI might be more broadly applicable across practice types, particularly where point-of-care ultrasound, lactate, and blood pressure are not routinely available. Given the low PPVs for case fatality and prolonged hospitalization in our study, prognostic factors should be interpreted with caution on an individual cat basis, and are not intended to be the basis of clinically consequential decisions.
