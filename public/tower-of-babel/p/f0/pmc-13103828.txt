Selective Identification of Maneb and Mancozeb via Mn2+ and Zn2+ Coordination and Mass Fragmentation Mechanism
Authors: Siddihalu Lakshitha Madunil, D. R. M. C. Vijani Dissanayake, Jithya Wijesinghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maneb and mancozeb fungicides have very similar structures, with the main difference being the presence of an additional zinc ion in mancozeb. Specificity is the constant challenge raised in previously developed methods for identifying these compounds. Therefore, a more reliable and effective identification method was introduced, enabling the sensitive and accurate detection of structurally similar manganese-containing fungicides. In this study, 2-imidazolidinethione (ETU) was detected at a retention time of 9.22 min as the hydrolyzed product, and permanganate was formed as the oxidized product of those two fungicides, which were used to confirm the ligand and the manganese ion, respectively. Additionally, simple direct UV–visible measurements successfully identified the characteristic spectra of both maneb and mancozeb. The limit of detection (LOD) values of maneb and mancozeb were 0.007 and 0.008 ppm, respectively, and the determination coefficient (R 2) for maneb and mancozeb was 0.9999. Interday and intraday precision were 1.58 and 1.62%, and 1.41 and 1.57% for maneb and mancozeb, respectively. The developed method demonstrated high accuracy for both fungicides, with recovery rates ranging from 99.2 to 99.8% (±1.1–1.6%) across concentrations of 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 ppm. A significant degradation was observed following hydrolysis, with 95.63 and 96.67% degradation recorded for maneb and mancozeb, respectively. Moreover, complete oxidation (100%) of each fungicide was achieved. The most important aspect of this study was the precise differentiation of maneb and mancozeb by studying the effect of zinc (Zn2+) as well as manganese (Mn2+) ions on the ethylenebisdithiocarbamate moiety.

Introduction

Maneb and mancozeb are dithiocarbamates (DTCs), classified under the ethylenebisdithiocarbamates (EBDCs) subgroup, which are characterized by the linkage of two DTC functional groups through an ethylene bridge. Maneb consists of a manganese ion (Mn2+) coordinated with sulfur atoms from its two DTC functional groups, while mancozeb is a coordination complex that includes both Mn2+ and Zn2+. Both are widely used fungicides in agriculture to control various fungal diseases in crops due to their easy synthesis, low manufacturing cost, availability of starting materials, and multisite efficacy. Alarmingly, maneb and mancozeb have been implicated in cases of suicide and homicide in recent decades, highlighting the need for selective identification.

Various analytical strategies have been established to identify EBDCs in complex matrices. In the reported gas chromatography (GC)-mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, DTC residues have been specially converted into carbon disulfide (CS2), one of the hydrolysis products of DTCs, allowing for the indirect quantification of total DTC as CS2. Nevertheless, this approach is nonspecific as all the DTCs produce CS2 during acid digestion. In contrast, CS2 exhibits only a single peak at m/z = 76, associated with the molecular ion; consequently, mass spectral selectivity is low due to the absence of other fragment ions. Thus, the lack of structural details complicates the confirmation of CS2.

Techniques like high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) are prominent due to their sensitivity. − In previous studies, maneb and mancozeb have been indirectly identified by introducing only the EBDC ligands into the aforementioned instruments after isolating them from the metal–ligand complex. Subsequently, identifying the precursor ion for maneb or for the derivatized product of EBDCs, ethylenebisdithiocarbamic acid dimethyl ester, would not be ideal for the selective identification, as the entire EBDC subgroup yields identical organic ligands.

Furthermore, complex formation followed by spectroscopic determination has been widely applied to determine maneb. For example, UV–visible measurement of the red colored supramolecular complex, Mn2+–PAN (1-(2-pyridylazo)-2-naphthol), has been used as a reliable strategy for identifying maneb. It should be noted that PAN can form reddish chelates with certain transition metal ions, including Ni2+. Another method involves the reaction of sodium molybdate with Mn2+ in maneb, resulting in the formation of a blue colored complex. However, it has been reported that zineb also forms a blue colored complex with molybdate, complicating the reliance on the blue color complex for selectively identifying maneb. Moreover, various other compounds, such as ammonia, phosphate, oxalic acid, bipyridine, and phenanthroline, may develop the same colored complexes with molybdate. , Additionally, the absorbance measurement at 500 nm for manganese-PAR (4-(2′-pyridylazo)­resorcinol)-cetyltrimethylammonium bromide ternary complex has also been utilized, but the complex formation reactions entirely depend on Mn2+. The specificity of any complex formation reaction would be low due to the potential of forming a particular color complex with other metal ions or ligands.

Flame atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS) has been employed to quantify Zn2+ and Mn2+ in maneb and mancozeb; nevertheless, as a destructive technique, it lacks structural insight into metal-EBDC complexes. The reaction of maneb with malachite green-periodate indicator monitored via stopped-flow spectrophotometry18suffers from interferences caused by certain metal ions, anions, and protons. , Ultrasound-assisted dispersive micro solid-phase extraction (UA-dμSPE) combined with spectroscopy for the analysis of EBDCs21 requires careful control of ultrasound parameters and temperature to avoid ligand degradation. Photoacoustic spectrometric-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (PAS-FTIR) aids in the identification of mancozeb but is surface-limited and time-consuming. Electrochemical techniques, including cyclic voltammetry offer sensitivity but require complex instrumentation for detecting low concentrations. In addition, differential pulse adsorptive stripping voltammetric (DP-AdSV) method has shown the oxidation of both maneb and mancozeb at nearly the same potential, indicating the absence of a molecular recognition mechanism. A nanorod-based electrochemical sensor exhibits high sensitivity toward mancozeb, although the characteristic responses of maneb and mancozeb were not compared.

In evaluating these approaches, it is crucial to address the potential drawbacks arising from the lack of specificity in identifying the particular Mn2+-EBDC complex. In addition to the specificity, most of the methods require extensive sample preparation and maintaining critical conditions. The methods provide selectivity of a particular EBDC by comparing its response to those of other EBDCs, each containing only a single distinct metal species. Furthermore, limited studies have been reported on determining maneb or mancozeb by specific analytical methods, resulting in distinguishable responses for each particular compound.

This study presents the development of a reliable and sensitive identification method for maneb and mancozeb by addressing the limitations of existing approaches as summarized in Table . The specificity in the analysis was achieved sequentially via the identification of 2-imidazolidinethione (ETU) and then by Mn2+ in the sample. Following that, further confirmation was carried out to identify maneb and mancozeb in a more specific manner using direct UV–visible measurements. As a result, the characteristics of UV spectra for maneb and mancozeb were recognized, and this finding can be successfully applied to quantify the two fungicides. Furthermore, the effects of Zn2+ and Mn2+ on the metal-EBDC complex and isolated EBDC ligand were studied. To our knowledge, this comprehensive study is the first to investigate and apply the effects of Zn2+ and Mn2+ to achieve greater specificity in differentiating two fungicides. Additionally, a combined method of GC-MS with UV–visible detection was employed to analyze commercially available samples and forensic samples.

Experimental Section

Standards and Reagents

The analytical standards of maneb (90.3%), mancozeb (99.6%), and ETU (99.9%) Pestanal were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). The analytical grade zinc sulfate (ZnSO4, 99%), manganese sulfate (MnSO4, 99%), ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH, 99.5%), and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl, 99.5%) were acquired from Fluka Chemika (Buchs, Switzerland), while potassium permanganate (KMnO4, 99%) was obtained from Merck, UK. Analytical grade tartaric acid (99.5%), chloroform (CHCl3, 99%), petroleum ether (95%), nitric acid (HNO3, 70%), perchloric acid (HClO4, 70%), acetonitrile (ACN, 99.9%), potassium periodate (KIO4, 99.8%), EDTA–Na2 salt (99%) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. The industrial-grade kaolinite was obtained from Caveman Redux (Colombo, Sri Lanka), and industrial-grade talc powder was purchased from Hind Exports (Mumbai, India). Milli-Q unit (Merck Millipore, USA) was employed to produce deionized (DI) water.

Commercially available samples of maneb (80%) and mancozeb (80%), manufactured by A. Baur & Co. (Pvt.) Ltd., were randomly collected from a local market in Colombo, Sri Lanka.

GC-MS

A GC (7890A) interfaced with a single-quadrupole MS (5975C) and an autosampler (7693) from Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA, USA) was utilized to analyze the hydrolyzed samples. The GC column was DB-5 ms (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm film thickness, J/W Scientific, Agilent Technologies, Folsom, CA, USA). The analysis was performed with a 1.0 mL/min helium flow rate. A 1.0 μL aliquot of the sample solution was introduced into the GC-MS, and the split ratio of the inlet was maintained at 20:1. The oven temperature began at 50 °C with a 1 min holding period, followed by an increase of 20 °C/min up to 200 °C and then at 10 °C/min to 260 °C. Thereafter, the oven was finally maintained at 260 °C for 5 min. The GC inlet and transfer line were configured at 250 and 290 °C, respectively. The temperatures of the ion source and the quadrupole were 230 and 150 °C, respectively. Mass detection was performed using a 70 eV electron ionization (EI) source, and the analysis covered the m/z range from 29 to 550.

UV–Visible Spectroscopy

A GENESYS 50 dual-beam UV–visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) was adopted for the purpose of absorbance measurements. The spectral range and bandwidth of the instrument were 190–1100 nm, and 2 nm, respectively. A standard quartz cuvette with a 1.0 cm path length was used.

Acid Hydrolysis

The hydrolysis was carried out at pH 5.0 by diluting each 10.00 mL aliquot of fungicide standard (50.0 ppm) with 10.00 mL of acidified water (1.00 mL of 10% (v/v) tartaric acid +9.00 mL of DI water).

The hydrolysis reaction was monitored at different boiling times (0, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min), and the effect of pH was also investigated at pH 7, 6, 5, and 4.

The acidified standards were boiled in a water bath for 30 min, allowed to cool to ambient temperature, and then extracted with CHCl3. Each organic phase was collected and dried using nitrogen evaporation. The dried samples were reconstituted in 1.00 mL of ACN and subjected to GC-MS analysis.

Extraction recovery was studied using a placebo spiked with 25.0 ppm of ETU (n = 6). A 10.00 mL aliquot from each fortified sample was extracted with CHCl3. The recovery was measured using a calibration curve prepared in ACN.

Calculation of the limit of detection (LOD) was performed with reference to the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N ≥ 3, n = 6), in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization (ICH, 2022) guidelines.

The degradation studies were performed according to ICH guidelines using 50 ppm standard solutions of maneb and mancozeb (n = 6).

Oxidation of Fungicide Standards

Each fungicide standard (0.100 g) was boiled with a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of conc. HNO3 (25.0 mL) and conc. HClO4 (25.0 mL) until a clear solution was obtained. After cooling the digest to room temperature, 40.00 mL of DI water was added to the solution. Subsequently, 0.500 g of KIO4 (≈0.024 mol dm–3) was directly added to the mixture at room temperature and allowed to complete the oxidation until the solution turned purple. The resulting solution was transferred into a 100.0 mL volumetric flask and made up to the mark with DI water. Next, the absorbance was measured within the wavelength range of 250 to 650 nm against a blank solution prepared using the same amounts of conc. HNO3, conc. HClO4, and KIO4 as used in the sample. Furthermore, the S/N ≥ 3 (n = 6) approach was employed to determine the LOD.

For the degradation study, 0.100 g of each fungicide was subjected to the oxidation procedure described above. The reaction mixture was diluted to 100.0 mL with DI water. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured in the wavelength range of 210–650 nm, as well as at 523 nm, against the same blank used in the above oxidation study. The completion of oxidation was verified by measuring the absorbance at 523 nm at regular time intervals (0, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min) after the solution turned purple. Another 0.100 g of KIO4 was added to the solution, and the same procedure was repeated. Thereafter, the change of the absorbance was monitored at 523 nm.

Direct UV–Visible Spectroscopic Measurements

Individual stock solutions of maneb and mancozeb (100 ppm) were prepared by accurately weighing 10.0 mg of each fungicide (±0.1 mg) and dissolving in 100.0 mL of DI water. Each stock solution was diluted to 0.5 ppm by transferring 0.50 mL of the stock solution into a 100.0 mL volumetric flask and making up to the mark with DI water. The absorbance was recorded over the wavelength range of 190–600 nm, using DI water as the blank. All solutions were protected from light by using amber glassware covered with metal foil and stored at −20 °C until the analysis.

Effect of Zn2+ and Mn2+ on the EBDC Ligand

Two individual solutions were prepared: 10.00 mL of 0.5 ppm maneb and mancozeb standards were mixed individually with 10.00 mL of 1.5 and 0.6 ppm EDTA, respectively. Approximately a 3-fold molar excess of EDTA relative to the fungicide standard was maintained, and the solutions were buffered at pH 10.0 using 0.55 mol dm–3 NH4OH/NH4Cl. The EBDC ligand released from each fungicide was extracted with petroleum ether by introducing the above solutions individually into a separatory funnel (250 mL). After evaporating the ether layer under nitrogen flow, each dried sample was dissolved in 3.00 mL of DI water for the absorbance measurements.

The aforementioned EDTA-based ligand extraction technique, specific to each fungicide was repeated. Next, both standards and the extracted organic ligands (10.00 mL of ≈ 0.5 ppm) from two fungicides were individually treated with 0.5 ppm of Zn2+ in a 1:1 molar ratio. Then, the treated standards and organic ligands were further mixed with additional Zn2+ to obtain both standard-to-metal and ligand-to-metal molar ratios of 1:2 and 1:5. The same procedure was carried out with 0.5 ppm of Mn2+. The volumes of the Zn2+ or Mn2+ required to maintain the aforementioned molar ratios are shown in Table S1 in the Supporting Information. The absorbance values of the samples were measured against a blank containing the same concentration of the relevant metal ion in DI water across the wavelength range from 190 to 600 nm.

Actual Sample Analysis

Five samples suspected to be involved in a suicide attempt associated with maneb were received from the court. The samples consisted of three empty containers labeled P01, P02, and P03, a partially consumed packet (P04) containing 23.549 g of brown-colored powder, and an empty cup labeled P05. The brown-colored patches were observed on the inner walls of all three containers (P01, P02, and P03) as well as on the empty cup (P05). Each of the samples, P01, P02, P03, and P05, was thoroughly washed with 20.00 mL of DI water while gently scraping each inner surface using a clean glass rod.

An aliquot (8.00 mL) of sample wash from each container and the wash from the cup was subjected individually to acid hydrolysis for GC-MS analysis, as described in the “Acid Hydrolysis” section. Subsequently, another 8.00 mL portion from each sample wash was oxidized with KIO4 for absorbance measurements according to the procedure described in the “Oxidation of Fungicide Standards” section.

Additionally, the direct UV measurements were carried out individually on the remaining 4.00 mL portions (sample washes) of P01, P02, P03, and P05 against DI water as a blank over the range of 190–600 nm.

Homogenized P04 (0.100 g) was dissolved in 1 L of DI water to prepare a 100 ppm stock solution. The stock solution was diluted 4-fold, and two 10.00 mL aliquots of the diluted solution were subjected to oxidation and hydrolysis separately. The same sample preparation steps, dilutions, and analysis procedures were applied for two commercially available samples. Under the assumption of 100% purity, 0.5 ppm solutions of the two commercially available samples and P04 were prepared using their 100 ppm stock solutions. The direct wavelength scan was performed from 190 to 600 nm, and the quantification was conducted at 206 nm.

Analytical Parameters for Method Development

To assess linearity, LOD, and limit of quantification (LOQ), two series of diluted standard solutions (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0, 1.2, 1.3, and 1.5 ppm) were prepared from 100 ppm stock solutions of maneb and mancozeb for the absorbance measurement at 206 nm. The UV measurements were performed in five replicates (n = 5) at each concentration, and analytical curves for maneb and mancozeb were constructed by plotting the mean absorbance vs concentration.

The linear regression method was applied to calculate the determination coefficient (R 2). The slope (s) of the calibration curve and the standard deviation of the y-intercept (σ) were calculated using the Microsoft Excel LINEST function. LOD and LOQ were then estimated using the formulas 3.3 σ/s and 10 σ/s, respectively. Additionally, the calculated LOD was further confirmed by comparing the signals of prepared standards at concentrations equivalent to the calculated LOD with the baseline noise at 190 to 210 nm (S/N ≥ 3, n = 5). All these measurements were carried out based on the ICH, 2022 guidelines. In order to prepare the placebo solution, 5 mg of kaolinite and 3 mg of talc were blended and subsequently dissolved in DI water to serve as the blank. Then, specificity was assessed by measuring the absorbance of the placebo solution from 190 to 600 nm. Furthermore, the interference for the quantification of maneb and mancozeb in the presence of the placebo was checked at 206 nm. Accuracy was evaluated via recovery studies at three different concentration levels of both standards (0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 ppm). The known amounts of maneb and mancozeb standards were individually added to the separate placebo solutions, and assays were performed using UV measurements with three replicates (n = 3) for each concentration level. The accuracy was determined by calculating the percentage relative error between the measured and theoretical concentrations, reported as percentage relative standard deviation (RSD%). To determine the precision, studies focusing on intraday (repeatability) and interday (intermediate precision) variability were carried out. Repeatability was implemented (n = 6) at 1.0 ppm on the same day under identical conditions, while intermediate precision involved six determinations (n = 6) at 1.0 ppm across three consecutive days. RSD% served as the measure of precision.

Results and Discussion

GC-MS Analysis

Consistent with the literature, the direct injection of maneb and mancozeb into GC-MS proved ineffective in this study, possibly due to their polar functional groups and polymerization upon heating. The issues arising from the GC-MS analysis can be overcome by converting maneb and mancozeb into ETU through acid hydrolysis, which increases entropy and results in a negative Gibbs free energy (ΔG). Moreover, the reported activation energy for the formation of ETU from maneb and mancozeb is relatively low at moderate pH and temperature conditions. , Thus, acid hydrolysis is both kinetically and thermodynamically favorable, without the need for extreme conditions. The maximum yield of hydrolysis was observed after 30 min of boiling at pH 5. Both fungicides were successfully hydrolyzed to ETU, resulting 95.63 and 96.67% degradation, respectively, in accordance with ICH guidelines.

During the extraction, ionic products remained in the aqueous layer, while ETU was extracted into CHCl3 with a 96% ± 0.98 recovery rate. The tautomerism of ETU between its thione and thiol forms enhances the selectivity in the extraction process by reducing its tendency to ionize. This extraction approach lowers matrix interferences, resulting in a smooth GC baseline. ETU was detected at the retention time (RT) of 9.22 min, as shown in the total ion chromatogram (TIC) in Figure A. The GC analysis demonstrated excellent separation, characterized by a symmetric, sharp peak for ETU and a minor peak at 7.55 min for tartaric acid. Furthermore, the presence of two additional small peaks corresponding to byproducts in real samples facilitated clear identification, indicating satisfactory chromatographic performance.

Typically, the sulfur atom is more prone to ionization than the nitrogen atom; therefore, ETU possesses an ionization energy of 8.15 eV, indicating its relative ease of ionization. Figure B illustrates the mass spectrum of ETU obtained via electron ionization (EI), which relies on the inelastic scattering of electrons. During this process, a portion of the 70 eV energy is transferred to the ETU molecule, resulting in its ionization and subsequent dissociation. Although molecular ions are often challenging to observe, the molecular ion (M+) at m/z = 102 was prominently detected with the highest intensity. The stability of the molecular ion of ETU can be attributed to its cyclic structure and the presence of heteroatoms, which facilitate resonance delocalization of the positive charge (Figure S1). Consequently, ETU proves to be an ideal candidate for the peak of the molecular ion and the considerable number of fragment ions observed in mass spectrometry. The M+2 isotope peak with an intensity of approximately 4.4% indicates a single sulfur atom, while the even mass (m/z = 102) suggests an even number of nitrogen atoms. Both observations are crucial for determining the molecular formula of ETU. Furthermore, the fragmentation pathway enables straightforward fingerprinting. The fragment ions appeared due to ring-opening reactions involving the breaking of the C–N bond in the thioamide group and a hydrogen radical shift. This fragmentation pathway facilitates the assignment of signal peaks to the fragment ions C2SNH2 +, CSNH2 +, CSH+, C2NH5 ·+, and CH4N+ at m/z values of 72, 60, 45, 43, and 30, respectively. In addition, the m/z of 73 is generated from two possible fragmentation pathways that lead to the formation of cyclic (Figure S1, path 1) and acyclic (Figure S1, path 2) fragment ions of C2SNH3 ·+. The positive charge on the fragment ions is stabilized either by conjugation or alkyl groups. For instance, the second intense fragment ion at m/z = 30 is significantly stabilized by the adjacent CH3 group (Figure S1). ETU was detected at RT of 9.22 min with a sufficient library match (94%) and the LOD of 0.1 ppm. However, library matches may vary depending on the matrix, chromatographic, and instrument conditions. Hence, the fragmentation pathway is essential for accurate structural elucidation and result confirmation. A LOD similar to that in our study has been reported for direct GC–MS analysis of ETU, likely due to its low volatility and thermal decomposition. Therefore, most quantification methods rely on derivatization, which enables ppb-level detection using various derivatizing agents and instrument settings instrument settings, such as injectors, columns, and MS modes. , In this study, GC-MS analysis provides confirmatory information through the fragmentation studies. According to the literature, there appear to be no previously reported studies on the analysis of the mass-fragmentation patterns of ETU, the primary hydrolysis product and metabolite of maneb and mancozeb.

UV–Visible Spectroscopic Analysis of Oxidized Products

The absorption spectrum of MnO4 – exhibited five distinct peaks in the visible region at 485, 505, 523, 542, and 565 nm, arising from a combination of vibrational fine structure and electronic transitions, with a maximum Franck–Condon factor occurring at 523 nm. Additionally, two other broad bands in the longer UV wavelengths (310 and 340 nm) provide added specificity for the identification (Figure ). The ligand-to-metal charge transfer transitions probably generate all these resulting peaks in the UV and visible regions. −

UV–visible spectra of oxidized samples, (a) maneb standard, (b) mancozeb standard, (c) commercially available maneb sample, (d) commercially available mancozeb sample, (e) P01, (f) P02, (g) P03, (h) P04, (i) P05, and (j) blank.

The identical absorption spectrum of MnO4 – was detected for the oxidized products of two fungicide standards since KIO4 serves as a potent oxidizing agent, facilitating the transformation of Mn2+ to MnO4 – with an associated LOD of 0.05 ppm. The positive (standard cell potential) ΔE° value corresponds to a negative ΔG, indicating a thermodynamically favorable reaction. Therefore, the resulting unique absorption spectra of MnO4 – at specific wavelengths, along with their intensity ratios, indicate the presence of Mn2+ in the original samples. ETU, the hydrolyzed product of maneb and mancozeb, does not exhibit a characteristic UV–visible spectrum. During the oxidation process, the organic part of the fungicides mostly oxidizes into their volatile oxides, while the metal ions remain in their higher oxidation states. The interesting oxidized product is MnO4 –, which has a significant molar absorption coefficient and a characteristic spectrum. However, none of the inorganic metal ions is suitable for direct GC-MS analysis due to their nonvolatility. Therefore, UV–visible analysis of the oxidative products and GC–MS analysis of the hydrolyzed products are informative, whereas UV–visible analysis of the hydrolyzed products and GC–MS analysis of the oxidative products may provide limited structural information about the fungicides.

In the degradation study, a constant absorbance at 523 nm was observed after resulting a color change (t = 0), and no change in the absorbance was detected upon the addition of 0.100 g of KIO4, confirming the complete conversion of Mn2+ to MnO4 –. Additionally, the characteristic absorbance patterns corresponding to maneb or mancozeb were not observed in the wavelength range of 210–330 nm after the oxidation step. Therefore, the formation of a characteristic color of MnO4 – indicates the completion point of the oxidation reaction.

Direct UV–Visible Spectroscopic Measurements

The UV–visible spectrum of the untreated standard of maneb exhibited four characteristic peaks at 206, 256, 286 nm, and a low-intensity peak at 326 nm. In contrast, the spectrum of mancozeb standard showed only three distinct peaks at 206, 286, and 326 nm, notably missing the 256 nm peak observed for maneb, as shown in Figure .

UV–visible spectra of (a) mancozeb standard, (b) maneb standard, (c) organic ligand of mancozeb, and (d) organic ligand of maneb.

The difference in the UV absorption of these two fungicides can be explained by focusing on their possible transitions. The peak at 206 nm for maneb and mancozeb can be attributed to the n → σ* transition involving the C–S bond. Furthermore, the peaks at 256 and 286 nm are due to π → π* transitions associated with the partial CN and CS bonds, respectively, during resonance stabilization. , Nitrogen atom can acquire a partial positive charge through resonance stabilization. The positive charge reduces the electron density on the N atom, resulting in a larger energy gap for the π → π* transition of the partial CN bond (256 nm) compared to the partial CS bond (286 nm). The low-intensity peak at 326 nm may arise from the n → π* transition involving the lone pair electrons on the sulfur atom, as n → π* transitions are commonly forbidden. Hence, the disappearance of the 256 nm band for mancozeb can be attributed to the low probability for the π → π* transition of the partial CN bond.

The combined techniques deliver a series of robust confirmatory evidence at each stage. First, GC-MS results confirm the presence of EBDCs through the identification of ETU. Subsequently, the UV–visible spectroscopic determination of the oxidized product offers further evidence of manganese in the EBDC samples, suggesting that the sample could be either maneb or mancozeb. Ultimately, after following the aforementioned confirmation, the entire complex can be identified precisely with greater selectivity through direct UV analysis, which can specifically determine whether the sample is maneb or mancozeb.

Accordingly, the comprehensive approaches, characterized by their unique absorption spectra, provide strong confirmatory evidence regarding the selectivity in the identification. Moreover, the characteristic vibronic patterns for maneb and mancozeb arise from the unique interactions between metals and EBDC ligands, highlighting the importance of studying the effects of metals on the ligand.

Effect of Zn2+ and Mn2+ on the EBDC Ligand

Upon extraction of the EBDC ligand from maneb and mancozeb, four significant absorption peaks were identified at the same wavelengths as those in maneb. These findings confirm that the electronic transitions in both maneb and mancozeb are similar after the removal of metal ions, as shown in Figure . Therefore, the absorption characteristics are suggested to be primarily due to the electronic transitions within the organic ligand, independent of the metal ions.

When maneb, mancozeb, and the isolated organic ligand were each treated separately with Mn2+ at standard or ligand-to-metal ion molar ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:5, no significant differences were observed in the absorption spectra across these treatments (Figures S2–S5). On the contrary, treatment of maneb or the extracted ligand with Zn2+ in a 1:1 ratio resulted in a spectrum exhibiting three distinct peaks at 206, 286, and 326 nm, closely resembling the absorption spectrum of mancozeb. Increasing the ratio of maneb or the ligand to Zn2+ (1:2) resulted in a prominent peak at 206 nm and a low-intensity peak at 286 nm. However, further increasing the ratio to 1:5 resulted in the disappearance of all peaks (Figures S6–S8). Maintaining the mancozeb standard with Zn2+ in a 1:1 ratio did not result in any notable changes. Subsequently, an increase in mancozeb to Zn2+ ratio from 1:2 to 1:5 caused the 286 nm peak to disappear at the former ratio, while all the bands disappeared at the latter ratio (Figure S9). Hence, the study provides verifiable evidence that Zn2+ significantly affects the spectral properties of the ligand, whereas Mn2+ does not.

EBDC ligand can undergo electronic modification upon complexation with metal ions. Mn2+ is classified as a hard acid, whereas Zn2+ is a borderline acid. Moreover, the N atoms in the EBDC ligand, which function as secondary amines, are recognized as hard bases. The lone pair on the nitrogen atom is delocalized during resonance stabilization, making it a borderline base, which may result in more effective binding to Zn2+. Additionally, the smaller ionic radius of Zn2+ (0.74 Å), relative to that of Mn2+ (0.80 Å), may allow Zn2+ to enter the cavity of the EBDC ligand and form a tetrahedral complex with two N atoms from the ligand and with two water molecules. Studies have shown that [Zn2(NH3)6(μ–OH)]3+ exhibits a tetrahedral environment around each Zn2+ center, coordinated by nitrogen atoms from amines and bridging hydroxide ligands, whereas Mn2+ forms an octahedral geometry with the ligand containing both amine and oxygen-bearing groups, e.g. 1,8- naphthylridine in which the metal is coordinated by two nitrogen and four oxygen atoms. Therefore, Zn2+ tends to form tetrahedral coordination, whereas Mn2+ favors octahedral coordination when interacting with ligands incorporating N and O donor atoms. , This tetrahedral configuration is more favored as it is less sterically demanding than the octahedral complexing with Mn2+. , Thus, when Mn2+ and Zn2+ are parts of an EBDC ligand, Zn2+ preferentially binds with nitrogen atoms and completely inhibits the π → π* transition at 256 nm. As the concentration of Zn2+ increases, it subsequently bonds with free sulfur atoms, ultimately blocking all four transitions.

Reporting different activation energies for their hydrolysis reactions at mild conditions (36 kJ/mol for maneb and 7 kJ/mol for mancozeb) would provide an additional clue that maneb and mancozeb possess two distinct complexes. It is noted that this study offers a strong foundation for demonstrating the effects of two metal ions on the absorption of each fungicide. This unique binding ability can differentiate maneb and mancozeb using UV–visible spectroscopy, making it a powerful analytical tool.

Conclusions

The developed method demonstrates high specificity, excellent sensitivity, and low LODs for identifying maneb and mancozeb, offering a significant advantage over more expensive and time-consuming techniques. Unlike other reported methods that require complex sample pretreatments, this approach benefits from negligible matrix interferences, allowing for simple sample preparation without sacrificing accuracy.

Since maneb and mancozeb cannot be directly determined by GC-MS, ETU serves as a suitable representative marker owing to its stable molecular ion and unique fragment ions. The oxidation of Mn2+ to MnO4 – provides characteristic absorption bands, which easily identify the metal ion without the effect of the ligand. The hydrolyzed product analyzed by GC-MS, along with the oxidized product analyzed by UV–visible spectroscopy, can enhance the selective identification of manganese-containing EBDCs. Because GC–EIMS library searches highly depend on the instrument settings, their reliability for identification is limited, making the fragmentation pattern of ETU essential for confirming its mass spectrum. The molecular ion of ETU is stabilized through resonance, while the fragment ions are stabilized either by conjugation or by an alkyl group, resulting in prominent m/z peaks.

In this study, spectroscopic measurements suggested that Zn2+ can bind directly to the nitrogen atoms of the EBDC ligand, which might alter their electronic transitions. This preferential binding disrupts the conjugation system of mancozeb, effectively suppressing the characteristic π → π* electronic transition at 256 nm, whereas Mn2+ does not interfere with this transition. This distinct binding behavior produces characteristic absorption spectra when these metal ions bind to the ligand. Therefore, mancozeb exhibits a distinct spectroscopic profile compared to maneb, which contains only Mn2+. This insight advances the understanding of ion-ligand interactions and supports the capability of the method to distinguish maneb from mancozeb. Quantification is achieved using the main absorption peak of the fungicides, supported by two or three additional peaks to verify their identity and ensure accurate measurements. Overall, this technique presents a reliable and efficient alternative for the quantitative and qualitative analysis of various matrices.

The outcomes of this work highlight not only the analytical strength of the developed method but also its potential utility for forensic, agricultural, and food safety monitoring, where rapid and accurate detection of fungicide residues is critical. Compared to more expensive and time-consuming techniques, this method offers a reliable and efficient alternative that can be readily adopted in laboratories with limited resources. Therefore, the analytical method also demonstrates its applicability for routine analysis, where the selectivity is crucial.
