Nutritional status and associated factors in Ethiopian children with congenital heart disease: a systematic review and meta – analysis
Authors: Berhanu Wale Yirdaw, Maritu Ayalew Agmas, Fasika Alemu Mekonen, Seferash Yohannes Worku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn low-resource settings like Ethiopia, Congenital Heart Disease is frequently complicated by a high yet poorly defined burden of malnutrition. To address this gap, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to rigorously quantify the pooled prevalence of undernutrition and identify determinants driving this condition among affected Ethiopian children.MethodsAdhering to PRISMA guidelines and a prospective registration, this systematic review and meta-analysis included non-randomized studies reporting on malnutrition in Ethiopian children with congenital heart disease, which were identified through a comprehensive search of PubMed, Medline, HINARI, ScienceDirect, Scopus and Google Scholar up to November 15, 2025. The risk of bias was appraised using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and data synthesis was performed in Stata version 17 using a random-effects meta-analysis to calculate pooled prevalence and Odds Ratios with 95% Confidence Intervals. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, while the robustness of the findings was evaluated through subgroup and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. To address publication bias identified via funnel plot asymmetry, a non-parametric trim-and-fill analysis was applied to generate a more robust adjusted pooled prevalence, with forest plots utilized to visualize all individual and aggregate results.ResultsA total of 1,849 children across seven studies were included in the analysis. The overall pooled prevalence of undernutrition among Ethiopian children with CHD was estimated at 44.48% (95% CI: 38.44% − 50.52%, I2 = 95.3%). Significant associated factors for undernutrition were the presence of heart failure (OR = 4.12, 95% CI: 3.74–4.50, I2 = 4.2%), pulmonary hypertension (OR = 2.53, 95% CI: 1.93–3.13, I2 = 89.1%) & comorbidities (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.25–1.54, I2 = 93%).ConclusionUndernutrition impacts approximately 45% of Ethiopian children with congenital heart disease, primarily driven by clinical challenges like heart failure and pulmonary hypertension, which necessitates a shift toward integrated, multidisciplinary pediatric care. However, the reliability of this prevalence rate is undermined by significant statistical heterogeneity and the non-randomized nature of existing research, creating a high risk of bias and limited generalizability. To address these critical limitations, it is recommended that both clinical and research frameworks adopt standardized malnutrition diagnostic criteria and more rigorous study designs to ensure that future interventions are based on consistent, high-quality data rather than fragmented estimates.Trial registrationCRD420251230006.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06707-8.Keywords: Nutritional Status, Congenital Heart Disease, Ethiopia, Associated Factors, Children

Background

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common birth defect, affecting approximately 0.8% to 1% of newborns globally [1–4], and it fundamentally alters a child’s physiological and nutritional stability [5–11]. The relationship between CHD and malnutrition is complex and bidirectional; the cardiac defect precipitates nutritional deficits, which in turn exacerbate the severity of the heart condition. This imbalance stems from a perfect storm of increased energy expenditure and inadequate intake. Children with CHD experience hypermetabolism due to hemodynamic stress, heart failure, and the increased work of breathing [8, 10, 12–15]. Their caloric intake is often compromised by feeding and swallowing difficulties, gastroesophageal reflux, fatigue during feeding, and medically necessary fluid restrictions [10, 14, 16–19]. Consequently, these children burn calories faster than they can consume them, leading to undernutrition.

The prevalence of malnutrition among children with congenital heart disease (CHD) varies significantly across global regions and age groups. The prevalence of malnutrition in the congenital heart disease (CHD) population places a heavy burden on low-to-middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in Africa where pooled undernutrition rates in children with CHD reached alarming highs in Nigeria (77.8%) and Ethiopia (40.9%) [20]. A global systematic review indicated a pooled prevalence of 34.3% for underweight, 30.8% for stunting, and 25.5% for wasting [21]. Specific age-related data showed that underweight was highest in infants at 38.3%, while stunting and wasting peaked in the toddler-to-preschool group at 32.5% and 27.8%, respectively [21]. Regional studies highlight severe burdens in certain areas: African children exhibited the highest rates with overall undernutrition at 65.14%, underweight at 45.76%, wasting at 39.37%, and stunting at 39.38% [20]. Similarly, a study in Indian children under six reported that 62.46% were underweight, 53.93% were wasted, and 41.3% were stunted [22]. In contrast, lower estimates were found in China [23], where pooled estimates were 27.4% for underweight, 24.4% for stunting, and 24.8% for wasting, while a Turkish study observed a general malnutrition range of 25–55% [24].

Primary research in Ethiopia underscores a severe and persistent burden of undernutrition among children with congenital heart disease (CHD), evidenced by varying magnitudes of prevalence across different nutritional indicators. The prevalence of underweight children across different findings was recorded at 29.3% [25], 40.3% [26], 43.1% [27], 49.1% [28], 54.5% [29], and 79% [30], while wasting occurred at rates of 25.9% [25], 36.52% [26], 38.6% [27], 41.3% [28], 53.9% [29], and 62% [30]. Furthermore, stunting was documented at 33.5% [26], 34.8% [25], 35.9% [27], 39.7% [29], 43% [28], and 52% [30] with one study specifically identifying severe acute malnutrition (SAM) in 51.8% of the cases [31]. Several clinical and demographic factors significantly increase the risk of malnutrition in this population. Medically, the strongest predictors of undernutrition are pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) [20–22, 25, 27–29, 32], congestive heart failure (CHF) [28–30, 33, 34], anemia [20, 27, 31, 33, 34] and infection [26, 30, 32]. Additionally, this nutritional crisis is associated with low socioeconomic status [30, 33], rural residence [26], female gender [26], and a lack of parental nutritional education [26]. Furthermore, age [20, 21, 27, 28, 31] and timing of intervention [25] are critical variables; young children are particularly susceptible to severe malnutrition, while delayed corrective interventions increase the likelihood that older children will remain significantly undernourished.

Despite the critical nature of the issue, Ethiopia currently lacks a comprehensive, synthesized estimate of the nutritional status and associated factors among children with congenital heart disease (CHD). To address this significant gap and the limitations of isolated studies, this study aims to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to generate precise, representative data on malnutrition prevalence and its determinants. The resulting evidence is crucial for empowering policymakers, clinicians, and public health professionals to develop targeted interventions and guidelines, thereby breaking the cycle of malnutrition and improving the survival and developmental trajectories of affected Ethiopian children.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were designed and reported in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [35, 36]. To promote transparency and avoid duplication, this systematic review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251230006).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Using the PECO framework, the review included studies focusing on (P) Children with Congenital Heart Disease in Ethiopia, with the (E) presence of various associated factors. The (C) of this review is absence of the associated factors. The primary (O) outcome was malnutrition.

Included studies were English-published, original, non-randomized research articles conducted in Ethiopia that involved children aged 18 years or younger and reported any form of malnutrition.

Exclusion criteria for the review comprised case reports, case series, reviews, editorials, and letters. Furthermore, studies were excluded if data specific to Ethiopian children could not be disaggregated from the broader results.

Search strategy and study selection

A comprehensive systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, Medline, HINARI, and ScienceDirect, as well as relevant gray literature websites, for all studies published through November 15, 2025. The search was executed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and relevant text keywords; the comprehensive strategy is documented in Supplementary File 1. To ensure literature saturation, the reference lists of all included articles were manually screened for further relevant studies. The complete process of selection, screening, and inclusion is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Supplementary File 2).

Quality assessment and data extraction

The quality of included studies was assessed by three reviewers (BWY, MAA and SYW) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) Critical Appraisal Checklist [37, 38]. All included studies achieved a score of seven and higher, as detailed in Supplementary File 3.

Two reviewers (BWY and FAM) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. A standardized data extraction form was used to collect information on: first author’s name, study region, study design, study participant, number of participants, prevalence and types of undernutrition (Supplementary File 3).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata version 17.0. Due to significant heterogeneity confirmed by Cochran’s Q and I² statistics, a random-effects meta-analysis model was used to calculate the pooled prevalence of undernutrition [39, 40]. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore sources of variation. Forest plots were generated to visualize the pooled estimate and individual study results [41]. For associated factors, pooled odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Publication bias was initially evaluated through visual inspection of a funnel plot [42, 43], which revealed evidence of asymmetry. Consequently, a non-parametric trim-and-fill analysis was conducted to account for missing studies and to calculate a more robust, adjusted pooled prevalence [44, 45]. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the pooled prevalence and to determine if any single study disproportionately influenced the final estimate [46, 47].

Results

Study selection and quality

The systematic search across multiple databases (PubMed, Medline, HINARI, ScienceDirect, Scopus, and Google Scholar) initially yielded 7,654 records. After removing 52 duplicates and 38 unrelated records, 7,564 studies underwent title and abstract screening. From these, 67 reports were sought for retrieval, leading to a full-text eligibility assessment of 41 studies. Studies were primarily excluded due to target population (n = 19) and unclear outcomes (n = 16). Ultimately, 7 studies meeting the inclusion criteria were incorporated into the final review. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram, provided in Supplementary File 2. Methodological quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) indicated that all included studies were of moderate to high quality, with scores of seven and above (Supplementary File 3).

Characteristics of included studies

The meta-analysis synthesized data from seven studies comprising a total of 1,849 children. These studies employed a range of methodological frameworks, including institution-based cross-sectional (IBCS), cohort and case-control designs. Geographically, the research was distributed across several Ethiopian regions, including Amhara, Southern Ethiopia, Oromia and Addis Ababa.

Prevalence of undernutrition in Ethiopian children with congenital heart disease

The overall pooled prevalence of undernutrition among Ethiopian children with congenital heart disease (CHD) was estimated at 44.48% (95% CI: 38.44%–50.52%). High heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I2 = 95.3%, p < 0.001, Fig. 1).

Subgroup analysis

To investigate the sources of observed heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted across four key dimensions: the form of undernutrition (Fig. 2), geographic region (Fig. 3), study design (Fig. 4), and study population (Fig. 5). Regarding the clinical presentation of undernutrition, underweight emerged with the highest pooled prevalence at 49.30%, followed by wasting (44.28%) and stunting (39.80%). Geographically, significant disparities were observed, with the Southern Ethiopia region reporting the highest burden (51.80%) and the Oromia region recording the lowest (36.73%), while the capital, Addis Ababa, remained near the overall mean at 44.60%. Methodological factors also appeared to influence prevalence rates; case-control studies yielded the highest estimate at 64.40%, whereas cohort studies reported a notably lower prevalence of 29.82%. Finally, analyses by study population revealed that prevalence fluctuated significantly by age, peaking at 64.4% in the 1 month to 14 years cohort. Collectively, these results suggest that the variations in reported undernutrition were primarily driven by regional inequalities, the specific methodological rigor of the study designs, and the age-specific vulnerabilities of the populations sampled.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the pooled prevalence of undernutrition, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed (supplementary file 4). The results indicated that the overall estimate was not significantly altered by any single study, with the combined prevalence remaining stable at 44.48% (95% CI: 38.44%–50.52%) across all iterations.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Fig. 6) revealed significant asymmetry, suggesting the potential presence of publication bias among the included studies. To further investigate and adjust for this bias, a non-parametric trim-and-fill analysis was conducted (Fig. 7). This method resulted in the imputation of six additional studies to balance the distribution around the pooled estimate. Despite these imputations, the overall conclusion regarding the prevalence of undernutrition remained robust, though the adjustment provided a more conservative estimate of the true effect size.

Factors associated with undernutrition in Ethiopian children with congenital heart disease (CHD)

The meta-analysis identified the following significant factors: the presence of pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, and comorbidity.

The presence of comorbidities in children with congenital heart disease (CHD) significantly increased the likelihood of undernutrition, making them 1.39 times more likely to be undernourished compared to children without comorbidities (Pooled OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.25–1.54) (Fig. 8).

Association between comorbidity and nutritional status in Ethiopian children with congenital heart disease

The presence of heart failure in children with CHD was associated with a very high risk of undernutrition, increasing their odds of being undernourished by over fourfold (Pooled OR = 4.12, 95% CI: 4.50–3.74) (Fig. 9).

Association between heart failure and nutritional status in Ethiopian children with congenital heart disease

The presence of pulmonary hypertension significantly elevated the risk of undernutrition in CHD patients, with these children having 2.53 times the odds of being undernourished (Pooled OR = 2.53, 95% CI: 3.13–1.93) (Fig. 10).

Discussion

This discussion synthesizes the findings of our systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence and determinants of undernutrition among children with congenital heart disease (CHD) in Ethiopia, structured according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.

Interpretation in the context of other evidence

This study establishes that undernutrition is a pervasive comorbidity in Ethiopian pediatric cardiac care, with a pooled prevalence of 44.48% (95% CI: 38.44% − 50.52%). This figure confirms that nearly half of the children with CHD in Ethiopia face compromised nutritional status, aligning with the broader perfect storm observed in low-to-middle-income countries (LMICs). Our findings mirror high rates seen in Nigeria, where undernutrition rates can reach as high as 77.8% [20]. Conversely, significantly exceeding those in middle-income countries like China [23] and Turkey [24]. This disparity is likely driven by the synergy of hypermetabolism, where Heart Failure and Pulmonary Hypertension exponentially increase caloric demand and the systemic food insecurity prevalent in the region.

Our findings establish a robust correlation between disease severity and nutritional status, identifying advanced clinical symptoms as the primary predictors of growth failure in this population. Children with Heart Failure were found to be over four times more likely to be undernourished (OR = 4.12). This aligns with established pathophysiology where congestive heart failure precipitates a hypermetabolic state and fatigue-induced feeding difficulties, making weight gain nearly impossible without aggressive intervention [33, 34]. Similarly, Pulmonary Hypertension (PAH) was identified as a critical risk factor (OR = 2.53), corroborating findings from previous primary studies [20, 23, 32]. The hypoxia and increased work of breathing associated with PAH divert energy away from growth, creating a vicious cycle where malnutrition further weakens cardiac muscle function.

The presence of comorbidities such as infections and/or anemia (OR = 1.39) further compounds this risk, creating a cycle where illness exacerbates nutritional deficits, which in turn weakens the child’s ability to recover or undergo surgical intervention.

Limitations of the included evidence

The evidence synthesized is primarily derived from non-randomized, observational studies, which inherently limits our ability to establish definitive causal relationships between CHD and undernutrition. There is a high degree of statistical heterogeneity (I2) across the included studies, with prevalence rates fluctuating between 29% and 79%. This variability is likely secondary to differences in study settings. Furthermore, the presence of unmeasured confounding factors may have influenced the primary outcomes.

Limitations of the review process

The review process encountered several constraints that may impact the interpretation of the results. Specifically, we identified a notable risk of publication bias. Additionally, a potential geographical bias exists: the majority of primary studies were concentrated in specific regions with specialized cardiac facilities, which may not accurately reflect the nutritional status of children in more remote or underserved parts of Ethiopia. These factors necessitate a cautious approach to generalizing these pooled estimates to the entire national population.

Implications for practice and policy

The high prevalence of undernutrition demands a shift from siloed cardiac treatment to integrated nutritional and cardiac care. Clinically, the quadrupled risk of malnutrition in children with heart failure suggests that pediatric cardiac protocols must prioritize early nutritional screening and aggressive caloric supplementation. At a policy level, the Ethiopian Ministry of Health should advocate for multidisciplinary management teams: comprising cardiologists, nutritionists and social workers, within pediatric cardiac centers to address both the hemodynamic complications and the metabolic demands of CHD.

Recommendations for future research

To move beyond the limitations of current evidence, future research should prioritize prospective longitudinal cohort studies to better delineate the temporal relationship between cardiac symptoms and nutritional decline. There is also a critical need for intervention-based research, to evaluate the efficacy of specific nutritional formulas in this high-risk population. Finally, future studies should adopt standardized, diagnostic criteria for malnutrition and expand geographical coverage to include rural Ethiopian populations to ensure a more representative understanding of the disease burden.
