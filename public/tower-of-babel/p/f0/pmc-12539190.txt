Unintentional boric acid exposure: a case report and boron level monitoring
Authors: Giordano Spacco, Valeria M Petrolini, Azzurra Schicchi, Anna Ronchi, Lucia Bernasconi, Emanuela Piccotti, Carla Debbia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBoric acid (BA) is found in various household products. Paediatric intoxication via unusual routes remains a concern. Background

Boric acid (BA), also known as orthoboric acid, is a weak, monobasic Lewis acid of boron with chemical formula H3BO3. It exists as colorless crystals or a white powder readily soluble in water.

Due to its weak bacteriostatic propriety, boric acid finds application as an antimicrobial agent in various settings, is sold in pharmacies both in pre-prepared 3% solutions and in solid form, which needs to be diluted before use. In solid form, it is available in 30-gram sachets to be diluted in 1 L of water. A 3% aqueous solution is used to treat minor burns, cuts, sores and other skin lesions, including acne. Dilute solutions are also employed as an eye wash and vaginal douche for treating vaginal candidiasis. Sodium borate, a related compound, is a common ingredient in household cleaning products, pesticides and wood preservative.

Boron is considered toxic; however, its precise mechanism of toxicity remains unclear. Initial symptoms frequently manifest as gastrointestinal distress. Severe cases can progress to more systemic effects, including flushing, erythema of the palms and soles (potentially becoming generalized), bullous formation and significant desquamation, mimicking toxic epidermal necrolysis [1–3]. Neurological symptoms may include headache, central nervous system (CNS) irritability, convulsions and delirium [4]. More serious consequences such as coma, rhabdomyolysis and kidney damage are also possible [5]. Long-term occupational exposure has been linked to alopecia [6].

Historically, serious boric acid intoxications were most common in newborns due to repeated topical applications, but cases in adults, even with single exposures, have also been described [3, 7]. Currently, the risk of intoxication is more frequently due to accidental ingestion of aqueous solutions of boric acid at different concentrations. We describe a paediatric case of boric acid poisoning through an unusual route of exposure, with documented blood and urine boron levels and description of the clinical course.

Case presentation

An 8-year old boy (weight 36.5 Kg, weight-for-age percentile: 96%; height 130 cm, height-for-age percentile: 64%; BMI = 21.6, overweight) and his mother visited his grandparents’ home for lunch. His mother prepared 90 g of dried homemade pasta (flour, eggs and oil) for him using a pot of water she found in the kitchen. The boy consumed all the pasta.

Three hours prior, the grandfather, had dissolved 30 g of boric acid powder, purchased at a pharmacy in solid form, in 1 L of water, creating a 3% solution. He used a portion to clean his ears, leaving the remainder in the pot, subsequently used for cooking pasta.

One hour after lunch the boy developed nausea and abdominal pain, so his mother, upon realizing what happened, in accordance with Poison Control Centre (PCC) recommendations, transported him to the emergency department of the referring local children’s hospital. On admission, routine blood and urine analyses revealed normal creatinine (0,46 mg/dL) and urea (32 mg/dL) levels.

During pasta preparation, dried egg pasta absorbs from 100 to 300% of its weight in water. Therefore, considering the 90 g of pasta consumed, we estimated the boy ingested 2.7–8.1 g of boric acid, a potentially toxic dose.

Intravenous fluid therapy was started at a rate of 2000 ml/m²/day. Close monitoring of his clinical status and blood chemistry began, and samples were collected for sending to PCC in order to perform boron level assessment. On the first day, the boy experienced a single episode of vomiting and a frontotemporal headache, successfully treated with acetaminophen. No other symptoms appeared in subsequent days and clinical evaluations remained consistently unremarkable. Blood tests (focusing on creatinine and urea levels), and venous blood gas were performed daily for six days. The results were always within the normal range until follow-up at 30 days after ingestion.

The intravenous fluid infusion rate was adjusted daily based on boric acid levels and fluid balance monitoring (in the range of 2000 ml/m2/day to a minimum of 1500 ml/m2/day). Intravenous furosemide was administered on three occasions when a positive fluid balance exceeding 500 ml was observed. A gastroenterology consultation and fecal occult blood test were performed to rule out possible gastrointestinal complications. To confirm boron ingestion and evaluate its elimination kinetics, serial boron measurements (total boron, including borate ions (BO₃³⁻) and complexes) were performed on blood and urine (spot sample). The initial measurements, taken 2 h post-ingestion (Day 1), revealed boron concentrations of 94 mg/L in blood and 2013.4 mg/L in urine. Subsequent blood and urine samples were collected systematically every 24 h on days 2 and 3. Given the progressive decrease in boron concentrations, additional samples were collected on days 5 and 9 post-ingestion. Results of boron concentration determination on blood and urine

Boron levels in both blood and urine samples were determined using an Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometer (ICP-MS NexION 2000B, Perkin-Elmer Instruments). Samples underwent a microwave pre-treatment in to minimize interferences caused by matrix. Digestions was performed using HNO3 in a microwave sample preparation system CEM (Corp., Matthews, NC, USA). After complete digestion and cooling, samples were diluted with deionized water before ICP-MS analysis. Total boron concentrations were then converted to mg/L boric acid.

Discussion and conclusions

BA is readily absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract; ingestion of a 3% aqueous solution results in near-complete absorption (92–94%) [8, 9]. Renal excretion is the primary elimination pathway, with approximately 50% of an oral dose excreted within 12 h and 35% eliminated over 5 to 7 days [10]. Therefore, prompt hydration is crucial to facilitate efficient renal clearance.

The BA acute single oral toxic dose is not defined. Lethal poisoning has been reported in newborns (1–3 g), infants (5 g) and adults (20 g) [2, 11], although survival after ingestion of higher amounts has been reported. A retrospective analysis of 784 acute boric acid ingestions indicated that doses ≥ 3 g were tolerated in children under 6 years and doses exceeding an estimated 8 g were tolerated in adults [12]. It is also important to consider that the exact dose taken is often difficult to ascertain. More recent studies have described lethal and severe intoxications following ingestion of undefined doses [3, 13]. This highlights the need for a careful clinical evaluation to ascertain risks.

In this case, the patient’s estimated boric acid intake ranged from 2.7 to 8.1 g. Hospitalization was deemed necessary for clinical observation and intravenous fluid therapy to enhance renal elimination.

Although borate poisoning can produce gastrointestinal, central nervous system, skin and hepatic toxicity, our patient remained asymptomatic from the second day of admission onward. Initial creatinine level, measured in ED almost 4 h after boric acid ingestion, was 0,46 mg/dL. A maximum of 0,54 mg/dL was reached after 8 days, a slight increase deemed insignificant Consequently, routine follow-up tests were concluded one-month post-discharge.

Serial blood and urine boron levels were assessed on days 1–3, 5, and 9. Upon admission, a plasma boron concentration of 16.5 mg/L (equivalent to 94 mg/L borate) was measured, decreasing to one-third the next day. This rapid decline, followed by a slower decrease in subsequent days (Fig. 1), is consistent with expected pharmacokinetics. Urinary boron concentrations, though initially elevated, decreased at a slower rate. Based on the patient’s diuresis, we estimated the excretion it is possible to assume the excretion of at least 5 g of boric acid in the first two days.

There are no validated reference values for borate in biological samples. Concentration values reported in literature are sometimes expressed as boron and sometimes as borates. It should be considered that each milligram of boric acid contains 0.17 mg of boron.

Very old data indicated an average boron concentration, expressed as borates, of 0.56 mg/L (range 0.2–2.2 mg/L) in a sample of 147 unexposed healthy adult men [14] and of 1.43 mg/L (range, 0–7.15.15) in 34 unexposed children [15].

he normal range of urinary boron has been reported as 0.04 to 6.6 mg/L, that means 0.2 to 37.75 expressed as borate [14]; borate blood values of 14.8 to 48.6 mg/L were detected in chronic intoxicated infants with mild symptoms [4]. Much higher levels (171.6 to 340 mg/dL) were found in paucisymptomatic pediatric patients undergoing dialysis [12, 16].

In a severe intoxication after suicidal ingestion of about 21 g of boric acid reported by Teshima et al. the serum boric acid peak was 465 mg/L [17]. Blood level of 560.49 mg/L was found in a patient who coingested mirtazapine and manifested a cardiac arrest. The patient manifested hair loss and corneal ulcers, signs of boron toxicity [18].

In contrast, borate values of 194.5 mg/L were detected in a patient with severe intoxication with epidermal necrolysis and severe rhabdomyolysis [3]. Postmortem heart blood borate concentration of 83.16 mg/L were found in a lethal case [13].

However, considering the rapid elimination kinetics of boron, any consideration of predictive value cannot be separated from knowledge of the time interval since intake. Moreover, since dialysis is very effective in removing BA [16, 17, 19], it is not possible to know what the course of these would have been in patients in the absence of this treatment.

Our patient presented with mild symptoms following intake of a dose of boric acid that cannot be precisely quantified, but certainly exceeded 2.7 g. Treatment with forced diuresis was started immediately. Boron dosages that were available later confirmed the intake and good elimination of the toxicant with ongoing therapy. Follow-up ruled out late effects.

In the context of a reported intake of a dose of BA greater than 3 g hyperhydration and monitoring should be initiated immediately. Hemodialysis shortened the half-life of BA, but should always be supported by the availability of blood dosages although there is no consensus on a correlation between serum boric acid levels and clinical manifestations. Therefore, decisions concerning therapy must be taken after a global consideration of the amount ingested, the serum levels and the clinical manifestations. Hemodialysis can be reserved for the most serious cases. No cases of delayed toxicity are described in the literature, so a follow-up period of one month, with clinical evaluation and renal function tests, should be sufficient.

Further studies are needed to better define the toxic dose of BA and the correlation between blood levels and clinical manifestations. This could be better understood by analysis of case histories with dosing performed at known time intervals after ingestion. To date, because of the possible toxicity of BA, any exposure must be prudently evaluated.
