Determinants of uncontrolled seizures among pediatric epilepsy patients in Debre Tabor comprehensive specialized hospitals Ethiopia
Authors: Tigabu Eskeziya Zerihun, Fisseha Nigussie Dagnew, Sisay Sitotaw Anberbr, Getachew Yitayew Tarekegn, Abel Temeche Kassaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe cornerstone of achieving remission for epileptic patients is anti-seizure medication (ASM). An effective anti-seizure pharmaceutical therapy can lead to seizure independence by reducing or eliminating symptoms. This study aimed to evaluate treatment outcomes for epilepsy and related factors among pediatric patients in Debre Tabor Comprehensive specialized Hospital.MethodA cross-sectional study involving 300 children with epilepsy was conducted. Structured questionnaires were employed to collect data through interviews and a review of the patients’ medical records. EpiData version 4.6.6 was utilized for data entry, while SPSS version 25 was utilized for analysis. The relationship between the independent and dependent variables was established using logistic regression, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultIn this study, 300 pediatric patients were included, with a mean age of 8.2 ± 4.2 years. The most common type of seizure (66%) was a generalized seizure. Monotherapy was utilized by 64.3% of patients, with phenytoin prescribed in 47.7% of cases. The current study revealed that 62.3% of patients had uncontrolled seizures. Female gender (AOR = 0.515, 95% CI: 0.285–0.931, P = 0.028), primary education of caregivers (AOR = 0.436, 95% CI: 0.192–0.99, P = 0.047), and family history of epilepsy (AOR = 0.363, 95% CI: 0.153–0.857, P = 0.021) decreased the odds of uncontrolled seizures, while polytherapy with anti-seizure medications significantly increased the odds of uncontrolled seizures (AOR = 6.79, 95% CI: 3.221–14.311, P < 0.001).ConclusionThis study found that a significant proportion of pediatric patients with epilepsy experienced poor seizure control, with 62.3% reporting uncontrolled seizures. Factors such as female gender, primary education of caregivers, and a family history of epilepsy were associated with a lower likelihood of uncontrolled seizures. Conversely, polytherapy with anti-seizure medications (ASMs) significantly increased the risk of uncontrolled seizures. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions aimed at improving seizure management, particularly for patients on multiple medications. Enhancing caregiver education and awareness of seizure triggers may also contribute to better treatment outcomes. Future research should explore specific strategies to address these risk factors and enhance treatment outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-05989-8.Keywords: Anti-seizure medications, Epilepsy, Treatment outcome, Pediatrics, Ethiopia

Introduction

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder characterized by abnormal neuronal connections, affecting over 50 million people worldwide, including 10 million children [1]. A seizure is a transient occurrence of signs and symptoms resulting from abnormal, excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain [2]. It is particularly prevalent in developing countries, including Ethiopia, where exposure to infectious diseases contributes to its incidence [3–5]. 4–10% of children experience seizures in their first 16 years, with 1% developing epilepsy [3, 6].

An estimated 10.5 million children worldwide suffer from epilepsy, with 80% of them residing in developing nations [7]. Cognitive and physical impairments frequently accompany the condition, and between 6% and 14% of children develop intractable epilepsy [8–10]. In Ethiopia, epilepsy is strongly associated with low levels of education and markers of poverty [11].

Mortality due to epilepsy tends to increase in Africa; it is particularly related to poorly controlled seizures [7, 12]. Previous studies have identified several causes and risk factors for childhood epilepsy [13]. Case-control research shows a substantial link between childhood epilepsy and risk factors such as developmental delay, newborn distress, severe head trauma, and family history, despite 50% of epilepsy cases having unknown causes [13].

In Ethiopia, the prevalence of epilepsy is approximately 5.2 per 1,000 [14]. Only 5% of those affected receive adequate treatment, largely due to stigma, limited access to healthcare, and socio-economic challenges [5, 15]. These barriers lead to significant morbidity and mortality, making epilepsy a critical health concern for children [16].

It might be difficult to diagnose epilepsy early, especially in the absence of witness testimony. Based on the patient’s and caregivers’ history of epileptic seizures as well as the type of seizures revealed by tests like electroencephalography, epilepsy is usually diagnosed clinically [17].

Effective treatment options include anti-seizure medications (ASMs), which are crucial for controlling seizures and improving quality of life [18, 19]. However, many children do not achieve seizure control, with about 30% continuing to experience seizures despite medication [8, 20]. This indicates a significant gap in understanding treatment outcomes and factors influencing seizure control in the pediatric population, globally, most studies are focused on the adult population and are scarce in children, According to author’s best literature search there are no studies published in north west Ethiopia and we observed a difference in the determinant factors between studies to close this gap and to provide evidence-based insight, intervention, and recommendations that enhance treatment outcome. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the treatment outcomes of pediatric epilepsy at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, focusing on the effectiveness of ASMs and identifying factors that contributed to uncontrolled seizures.

Method

Study area and period

The study was conducted from September 2021 to February 2022. Conducted at Debre Tabor Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital, located in Debre Tabor Town, the capital of the South Gondar Zone in the Amhara Region, approximately 100 km from Bahirdar and 667 km from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia’s capital. Established in 1920, the hospital has a rich history of providing comprehensive inpatient and outpatient care to over three million patients. Debre Tabor Hospital serves a diverse patient population and offers a wide range of medical specialties, including internal medicine, pediatrics, gynecology/obstetrics, surgery, dentistry, psychiatry, ophthalmology, radiology, dermatology, and head and neck surgery. Notably, the hospital has developed a specialized epilepsy clinic that addresses the needs of patients with epilepsy, providing tailored care and support. Additionally, the hospital functions as a teaching facility for the Debre Tabor University Medicine and Health Science College, contributing to the education and training of future healthcare professionals. This dual role enhances the quality of care provided to patients while fostering a robust learning environment for medical students.

Study design, population, inclusion and exclusion criteria

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted among pediatric patients with epilepsy at Debre Tabor Comprehensive specialized Hospital through medical record review and Patients/caregivers’ interviews. All pediatric patients visiting the pediatric clinics of Debre Tabor Comprehensive specialized Hospital were the source population for the study. All pediatric epileptic patients who are available during the data collection period. Pediatric patients who were diagnosed with epilepsy, Patients aged ≤ 18 years with the diagnosis of epilepsy/seizure disorders, and Patients who have been taking at least one ASM for a minimum of 1 year were included in the current study. Medical records with incomplete data were excluded from the study, as well as patients and caregivers who could not respond or chose not to participate. This means that a certain number of records were left out. While this decision makes sense, it could lead to bias by missing important views from those who were excluded.

Study variables

Treatment outcome (seizure control status) was the outcome variable, while, Socio-demographic variables (age, sex, residence, religion, primary caregiver, marital status of caregivers, caregivers’ educational level, occupation of caregivers, source of medications, parents’ income level, family history of epilepsy), Clinical-related variables (investigations, age at diagnosis of epilepsy, seizure triggers, seizure types, duration of illness, comorbidities, frequency before initiation of treatment, frequency of seizure, seizure-free period), and Medication and treatment outcomes related variables (types of ASMs, treatment duration with ASMs, treatment modalities, adverse drug effects related to ASMs) were the predictor variable.

Operational definition

Treatment outcomes: - It is interpreted as a controlled or uncontrolled seizure control status.

Uncontrolled seizures: - If the patient/child experiences greater than or equal to one episode of seizure in the last 1 year after the start of treatment, despite taking anti-seizure medications [22, 23].

Controlled seizures: - If the patient/child experiences no seizure episode in the last 1 year after the start of treatment, despite taking anti-seizure medications [22, 23].

Pediatrics: According to the WHO, the age group is between births to 18 years.

The one-year threshold for defining seizure control is commonly used in clinical practice and research to provide a clear timeframe for evaluating treatment efficacy. This duration allows for a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s response to therapy, as it accounts for variations in seizure frequency and the potential for medication adjustments [24].

Data collection instrument and procedure

A structured questionnaire was developed after a data abstraction checklist reviewed relevant medication and medical data from the patient’s medical file. A questionnaire was developed following an analysis of several pediatric literature sources [21, 25]. In addition, a professional translator who knows both languages then translated it back to English using checklists from the Epilepsy Foundation (SAP) and guidelines (ILAE 2017) into the local language (Amharic).

In addition to questions concerning clinical characteristics, medication and treatment outcomes, as well as the sociodemographic information of patients and caregivers or parents of children, the questionnaire also includes details from the patient’s medical records and an Amharic version. Primary caregivers were asked about seizure triggers. Adverse Drug Events (ADEs) were identified through medical records previously noted by the doctor, along with complaints from patients and their parents. Data were collected by five trained health professionals, specifically nurses, who conducted interviews and reviewed medical records. The principal investigators provided thorough training that covered the use of pre-tested questionnaires to ensure data completeness, effective interviewing techniques for gathering reliable information, systematic methods for extracting relevant data from medical records, and ethical considerations, including patient confidentiality and informed consent. Reliability was measured using Cronbach’s alpha to assess internal consistency. This structured training approach ensured that data collectors were well prepared to perform their roles effectively, thereby enhancing the quality of the data collected.

Data quality control

The principal investigator provided extensive training to data collectors regarding questionnaire content, data collection techniques, and ethical considerations for one day at each study site for both data collectors and supervisors. A pretest was conducted at the University of Gondar comprehensive specialized Hospital on 5% (15 patients) of the sample with comparable features before beginning patient recruitment and data collection to verify the completeness of the instruments. The evaluation tool was updated in response to the pre-test results. For example, the comorbid disease category was changed, and the adherence test, considered in the pre-test, was eliminated due to the non-validity of the instruments. The pre-test data were excluded from the final analysis. The principal investigator oversaw the gathering of data and provided comments and adjustments.

Data entry and analysis

Following a completeness check, Epi data version 4.6.6 was used to code, enter, and clean the data. The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 was subsequently utilized for analysis. Sociodemographic, disease-related, and medication-related data were summarized using descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation (SD)). Tables and charts were employed to present the results. In the logistic regression, continuous variables were transformed into categorical ones. Univariate analysis was conducted to determine whether a relationship existed between each independent variable and the treatment outcome. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to pinpoint characteristics associated with the treatment outcome, with all variables having a p-value of less than 0.25 in the univariate analysis included. Logistic regression analysis reported the Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR), along with its p-value and 95% confidence interval. The omnibus test of the model coefficient indicates that the model is statistically significant overall, χ2 [22] = 100.876, p < 0.05. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test assesses the null hypothesis that the model’s predictions align perfectly with observed group memberships. Therefore, the value in the model shows no statistically significant difference, indicating a good fit. P-values of less than 0.05 in the multiple logistic regression analysis were regarded as independent predictors of treatment outcome.

Result

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the patients

Among the 300 patients included in the analysis, 64.3% were male. The average age of the patients was 8.2 ± 4.2 years. Over half (61.7%) of the participants paid for their prescription drugs. Most pediatric patients (87.3%) have no family history of epilepsy. The majority of patients (66%) experienced generalized seizures, which were followed by mixed seizures (13.3%), focal seizures (12%), and unclassified seizures (8.7%). The majority of patients (42%) had 1–2 times the frequency of seizure occurrences before starting ASM medication, and the most prevalent age group at epilepsy diagnosis was 1–5 years (48.7%). Developmental delay was the most common comorbidity, at 11.3% (Table 1).

Predictive factors of uncontrolled seizures

All clinically relevant variables were entered into the univariate model. Those variables with P value <0.25 were taken into the multivariable logistic regression model. Multivariate logistic regression revealed a significant association between the sex of the child (females), educational status of caregivers (primary education), and family history of epilepsy were found to be protective for uncontrolled seizures whereas seizure-triggers and type of therapy (polytherapy with ASMs) were negatively associated with uncontrolled seizures.

Females were less likely to develop uncontrolled seizure compared to males (AOR= 0.515, 95% CI: 0.285-0.931). The odds of developing an uncontrolled seizures among parents/caregivers of patients with primary education decreased by 56.4% as compared to those patients who didn’t have formal education (AOR=0.436, 95% CI: 0.192-0.99). However, it was not statistically significant for patients whose educational level was secondary school and above. The odds of developing uncontrolled seizures among patients with a family history of epilepsy decreased by 63.7% as compared to those patients without a family history (AOR=0.363, 95% CI: 0.153-0.857). The odds of developing uncontrolled seizures among patients with seizure triggers were increased by3.63 times than those without seizure triggers (P<0.001, 95% CI: 1.99-6.614). Patients on polytherapy with ASMs were 6.79 times more likely to develop uncontrolled seizures compared to patients on monotherapy (P<0.001, 95% CI: 3.221-14.311) (Table 4).

Discussion

Despite the high incidence of epilepsy in pediatric patients, this is the first study to assess treatment outcomes and their associated factors among pediatric epilepsy patients at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals. Our findings revealed that 62.3% of patients experienced uncontrolled seizures, and there were significant correlations between uncontrolled seizures and factors such as female gender, primary education of caregivers, family history of epilepsy, and the use of multiple anti-seizure medications.

The current study found that generalized seizures were the most common type of seizure (66%), followed by mixed seizures (13.3%). This aligns with a study conducted in Malaysia, which found that generalized tonic-clonic seizures were the most frequently reported seizure (67.4%), followed by mixed (5.7%) [26]. However, a higher prevalence was reported compared to studies conducted in Sudan (17.3%) [27] and Sri Lanka (32%) [28]. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in professional qualifications and the availability of diagnostic instruments used for classifying seizure types, as well as the fact that focal epilepsy is the most common form of epilepsy in early childhood, even though the age group included in this study is up to 18 years.

The majority of patients were put on phenytoin (40.7%), which is in line with studies done in India (35.5%) [29], Jimma (42.2%) [30] and Gondar (42.9%) [21]. The reason could be the availability and cost of the medications. It differs from WHO recommendations in developing countries, where the most commonly recommended first-line ASM is phenobarbitone due to availability and affordability, particularly in pediatric populations, the difference might be due to fear of adverse effects including behavioral disturbance and drowsiness from phenobarbitone even though these two drugs are equally effective and preferred medications in pediatrics.

The current study revealed that 62.3% of the patients had a seizure-free period of below one year (uncontrolled seizures), which is in line with the study done in south-west Uganda (60.4%) [31]. While it was lower than the studies done in Norway (59%), patients had a seizure-free period of one or more [32]. This high rate of uncontrolled seizures may be influenced by various contextual factors, including limited healthcare access, challenges in medication adherence, and a lack of awareness about epilepsy management among caregivers. These factors can contribute to delays in seeking appropriate treatment and hinder effective control of seizures. In our context with limited resources, all could be contributing factors.

This study found that uncontrolled seizures were found to be significantly correlated with the educational attainment of the parents, a family history of epilepsy, polytherapy with ASM and female participants.

The current study found that sex of the child (females) less likely to experience uncontrolled seizures as compared to male participants. A similar study was reported in Gondar, Iran, and Pakistan [21, 33, 34]. This might be because females are more attentive than males and think about everything critically. As a result, they may be adherent to their medications, which may be related to successful outcomes.

In this study, a positive family history of epilepsy reduces the chances of experiencing uncontrolled seizures. The majority of studies reported the reverse result; no association was found [35]. The possible explanation for family history to be a protective factor against uncontrolled seizures could be that families of patients/children who understand the nature of the disease or have awareness about medical conditions (epilepsy) take their children to a healthcare center as soon as possible and resulting in decreased delayed presentations to the health facilities, this is not surprising because in developing countries such as Ethiopia families with little knowledge of disease conditions take their children to alternative treatment areas rather than taking to health care facilities, particularly in the case of epilepsy which could lead to delayed presentations to the health facilities.

The present study revealed that the odds of developing uncontrolled seizures among patients whose parents with primary education decreased by 56.4% as compared to those patients whose parents did not have formal education, which is supported by a previous study [36]. The possible reason could be that illiterate parents may not query their children's healthcare providers about their sickness and treatments, and they may be unaware of the disease's progression.

This study found that the odds of developing uncontrolled seizures among patients with seizure triggers were increased by 3.63 times as compared to those without seizure triggers, which is supported by a study conducted in pediatrics [37]. This might be due to the parents of children and the child’s lack of awareness about seizure triggers, identifying and handling those factors that have an impact on the control of seizures.

The current study found that Patients who were put on polytherapy 6.79 times were more likely to develop uncontrolled seizures compared to monotherapy, which is congruent with different studies [35, 38]. This is because patients who are exposed to more than two anti-seizure medications are more prone to side effects, which leads to medication discontinuation (non-adherence to therapy), which influences the treatment outcome indirectly. Another reason that is specific to children and adolescents could be pill burden and refusal to take drugs, which becomes worse as the number of medications increases.

The study highlights the need for improved caregiver education, particularly among those with primary education, to enhance awareness and treatment adherence for pediatric epilepsy. Tailoring treatment regimens, especially minimizing polytherapy, is crucial to reduce side effects and improve outcomes. Involving families in the management process can empower them to seek timely care, while educating them about seizure triggers is essential for better control. Gender-sensitive approaches may also enhance adherence, particularly for female patients. Additionally, addressing resource allocation in healthcare, increasing access to essential medications, and implementing community awareness programs can facilitate early diagnosis and reduce stigma. Overall, these implications underscore the necessity for ongoing research and policy development to improve the quality of life for pediatric epilepsy patients.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Comprehensive data collection through structured questionnaires and medical record reviews, which enhance the reliability of findings, the large sample size of 300 participants allows for more generalizable conclusions within the pediatric epilepsy context and this study was conducted in children, an area of interest that needs to be addressed due to the complex response of pediatrics to anti-seizure medications were the strength of the study. However, the study also has limitations. First, its cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified factors and treatment outcomes. Additionally, the reliance on caregiver-reported data may introduce recall bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of the information gathered. The study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare settings. Furthermore, adherence to anti-seizure medications was not measured, as validated tools for assessing adherence in children were unavailable. These limitations highlight the need for caution when interpreting the results and suggest that further prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings and explore additional factors that may influence treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

This study found that a significant proportion of pediatric patients with epilepsy experienced poor seizure control, with 62.3% reporting uncontrolled seizures. Factors such as female gender, primary education of caregivers, and a family history of epilepsy were associated with a lower likelihood of uncontrolled seizures. Conversely, polytherapy with anti-seizure medications (ASMs) significantly increased the risk of uncontrolled seizures. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions aimed at improving seizure management, particularly for patients on multiple medications. Enhancing caregiver education and awareness of seizure triggers may also contribute to better treatment outcomes. Future research should focus on developing specific strategies to address identified risk factors and improve the quality of care for pediatric epilepsy patients in Ethiopia.

Recommendations

Providers should assess and monitor for adverse drug events associated with anti-seizure medications to enhance treatment outcomes. To improve pediatric epilepsy management, educational programs should be implemented for caregivers, particularly those with primary education, to increase their awareness of epilepsy, treatment adherence, and seizure triggers. Additionally, healthcare providers should prioritize monotherapy over polytherapy whenever possible to minimize side effects and improve seizure control. Involving families in the management process can empower them to seek timely care and adhere to treatments. For patients with refractory seizures, alternative modalities should be considered. Furthermore, therapeutic drug monitoring, along with large sample follow-ups and interventions, should be conducted for patients experiencing uncontrolled seizures and adverse drug events. Lastly, further prospective and interventional studies that include adherence measurements are essential for advancing this field.
