Nutritional care experiences of family caregivers for infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia: a qualitative study
Authors: Luoxu Cen, Fangyan Lu, Yan Wang, Jingyun Wu, Yuan Liu, Huafen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia significantly compromises outcomes in infants undergoing liver transplantation. Effective nutritional care, crucial for combating sarcopenia, is primarily managed by family caregivers. The aim of this study was to explore the real experiences and needs of family caregivers for infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia during the nutritional care process, and to provide a reference for formulating targeted intervention strategies for nutritional rehabilitation and catch-up growth.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted. Between June and September 2025, 15 family caregivers of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia were purposively sampled from a liver transplantation center at a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, China. In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted twice per participant (one day before discharge and 2–3 weeks after discharge). The interview data were analyzed and summarized via content analysis.ResultsThree themes and seven subthemes were identified: (1) nutritional knowledge deficits and fragmented information sources (subthemes: cognitive blind spots regarding the nutrition-disease relationship, fragmented and non‑professional knowledge acquisition); (2) dualities of nutritional care: burden and growth (subthemes: emotional burden and financial burdens, synergistic support networks and positive growth); and (3) unmet needs: deficiencies in the nutritional support system (subthemes: lack of systematic nutritional course training, discontinuity in follow-up after discharge, insufficient nutritional assessment services).ConclusionsFamily caregivers of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia experience multifaceted strain that compromises nutritional care. Our findings underscore the critical need to address the interconnected gaps in knowledge, caregiver burden, and healthcare system support to promote catch-up growth and optimal development in this vulnerable population.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Background

Sarcopenia is a syndrome characterized by a progressive decline in skeletal muscle mass and function [1]. Although it is traditionally considered a condition associated with aging, recent evidence indicates that malnutrition and various disease states can also precipitate sarcopenia, including in the pediatric population [2]. The prevalence of pediatric sarcopenia is reported to be approximately 24–41% [3]. Its impact extends far beyond the muscular system: it not only slows metabolism and impairs skeletal health and neurodevelopment but also significantly reduces quality of life, prolongs hospital stays, increases the incidence of complications and mortality, and leads to increased healthcare costs [4].

In children with chronic liver disease, sarcopenia is closely associated with the degree of liver cirrhosis, forming a vicious cycle that further accelerates disease progression, increases the risk of complications, and compromises both quality of life and long-term survival [5, 6]. Liver transplantation has become a curative intervention for children with end-stage liver diseases and congenital metabolic disorders, with 5-year survival rates now reaching 91% because of advancements in surgical techniques and perioperative management [7, 8]. However, successful surgery does not guarantee favorable long-term outcomes. Research shows that sarcopenia is highly prevalent among children awaiting liver transplantation and is a key factor influencing pre- and posttransplant complications—including increased risk of infection, hepatic encephalopathy, prolonged hospitalization, and reduced survival rates—regardless of liver function status [9]. Studies have shown that children with sarcopenia before liver transplantation have a doubled risk of postoperative rejection [10]. The incidence of posttransplant sarcopenia remains high, at 30–50% [5, 6]. This condition can lead to long-term growth retardation, neurocognitive impairment, and a reduction in 5-year survival of up to 15% [6, 11]. Nutritional rehabilitation serves as the cornerstone of sarcopenia management [12, 13], and is effective and directly depends on family caregiving capacity—for example, Rocio et al. [14] confirmed that targeted nutritional interventions can significantly improve muscle mass and reduce the risk of complications.

Nutritional care encompasses developmentally appropriate feeding practices to support physical growth and neurological maturation. Infants are a uniquely vulnerable group with distinct and critical nutritional needs due to their rapid physical growth and ongoing neurodevelopment. In the management of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia, parents, as the primary executors, must independently perform these tasks before and after discharge, including enteral feeding and the introduction of complementary foods. The quality of nutritional care directly determines the infant’s nutritional status and the risk of complications [13, 14]. In the context of China, this role is even more complex: most parents of pediatric liver transplantation are also living donors [15], and post-operatively, they often face complications such as pain, fatigue, and pleural effusion [16]. This forces them to coordinate their own recovery with the nutritional management of their infants while bearing the dual identity of “donor-caregiver”. Research has confirmed that excessive dual burden not only harms the quality of life of parents but also indirectly jeopardizes the children’s recovery by reducing the quality of care [17]. Although parental nutritional knowledge and abilities are crucial for prognosis, existing evidence on nutritional care in this population remains limited. Liu et al. identified deficiencies in nutritional awareness among parents of pediatric liver or kidney transplant recipients but did not focus specifically on infants with sarcopenia or explore caregivers’ real-world experiences and needs [18]. Other qualitative studies on pediatric liver transplantation have examined general caregiving experiences [19] or social and medical needs [20], yet none have specifically addressed the nutritional care journey of families managing both post-transplant recovery and pre-existing sarcopenia.

Therefore, a systematic understanding of parents’ experiences in providing nutritional care is essential for the development of scientific and effective nutritional management strategies, which are critical for addressing malnutrition and optimal growth and development in these infants. This study employs semi-structured interviews to explore the nutritional care experiences of parents of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia, aiming to provide an evidence base for the development of individualized, family-centered nutritional support interventions.

Methods

Design

This study used a descriptive qualitative research design with semi-structured interviews to explore the real experiences and needs of families of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia during the nutritional care process. This study is reported according to the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) [21].

Study setting and standard nutritional care practices

This study was conducted in a liver transplantation center at a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, China from June 2025 to September 2025. The center performs more than 200 pediatric liver transplants annually for children under 18 years of age. The center provides routine care for infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia, including the following components: (1) Routine nutritional risk screening: nutritional risk is screened using the STRONGkids (Screening Tool for Risk on Nutritional status and Growth) tool. STRONGkids, developed for hospitalized children aged > 1 month, comprises four items (subjective clinical assessment, high-risk disease, nutritional intake and losses, and weight loss/poor weight gain) and classifies patients into low (0 points), moderate (1–3 points), or high (4–5 points) risk categories. It is a validated screening tool with demonstrated sensitivity and interobserver reliability for identifying nutritional risk in hospitalized children. In our center, nutritional risk screening is performed for infants and young children at admission, during changes in clinical condition, and once weekly. Pediatric patients at high risk of malnutrition are referred to the Nutrition Department for consultation. Height and weight are measured and monitored weekly. (2) Infant feeding guidance: the center has developed a perioperative nutritional health education booklet for pediatric liver transplantation. Breastfeeding is promoted, with age-specific feeding frequency recommendations (e.g., every 3 h for infants aged 4–5 months). For infants unable to breastfeed, age-appropriate formula feeding volumes and frequencies are provided. Complementary feeding guidance advises starting at 5–6 months with thin porridge, egg yolk, cooked vegetable purees, and fish, following a stepwise approach of introducing one new food every 3–5 days. If the mother is the living donor, breastfeeding is temporarily suspended after transplantation as per center protocol. The suspension period typically lasts 2–4 weeks, depending on the mother’s postoperative recovery (e.g., pain control, wound healing, and resolution of complications such as pleural effusion). Nurses provide feeding guidance to caregivers at admission, pre-operatively, and post-operatively. Family members of patients in general wards are allowed 24-hour accompaniment, while the daily visiting time in the intensive care unit is 30 min. (3) Discharge preparation: before discharge, the responsible nurse and the dedicated follow-up nurse will conduct one-on-one bedside health education and guidance to caregivers. Caregivers are also invited to join a WeChat group (“The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Pediatric Liver Transplantation Group”) to facilitate peer experience sharing. A dietitian is available for consultation upon referral for high-risk cases; however, routine dietitian has not systematically participated in the nutritional support process of the discharge plan. Most of the related interventions are scattered and non-standardized oral suggestions. (4) Post-discharge nutritional follow-up: after discharge, clinical nurses conduct follow-up via telephone, WeChat, the internet hospital platform, and scheduled outpatient visits. Nutritional assessment includes monitoring of height and weight.

Participants and sampling

A purposive sampling method was used to select the participants, and maximum variation in terms of demographic characteristics was considered. Sampling variation for parents and infants involved a variety of factors, including sex, age, admission weight, discharge weight donors, education level, profession, diagnosis, transplant type and feeding pattern after transfer. We selected the parents of these infants to participate in the qualitative study.

The inclusion criteria for the pediatric patients were as follows: (1) having a preoperative diagnosis of sarcopenia [22], sarcopenia is diagnosed when the Z-score of total psoas muscle area (tPMA) measured by CT at the L4-5 lumbar level is less than − 2. (2) underwent liver transplantation for the first time, and (3) were aged ≤ 5 years. Notably, although the upper age limit was set at 5 years, all enrolled patients’ mean age in this study was 7 (range, 3 to 19) months. Thus, the term “infants” is used throughout this manuscript to accurately reflect the study population. The exclusion criteria included: (1) combined multi-organ transplantation, such as liver and small intestine transplantation, (2) the presence of other severe comorbidities, such as congenital heart disease or malignancies, and (3) the inability to complete the perioperative process and follow-up.

The inclusion criteria for family caregivers of infants were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) being responsible for the care of infants, with a daily care time exceeding 12 h, and (3) providing informed consent. The exclusion criteria included: (1) lack of normal communication ability, and (2) withdrawal or inability to participate in the entire study.

Interview protocol

Based on the research purpose and prior literature review, an interview protocol was drafted. After it was reviewed by experts in pediatric liver transplantation care, the interview protocol was revised. Two parents of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia, who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, were selected for pilot interviews. To fully understand the nutritional care experience of the parents after discharge, the research team decided to conduct two interviews with each parent, which were carried out one day before the infants’ discharge and within the second to third weeks after discharge respectively. The interview protocol was further revised according to the feedback from these pilot interviews, and a formal interview protocol was ultimately developed. Data collection and quality control

Data were collected using semi-structured interviews. The interviews were carried out in Chinese by the first author, who had received training in qualitative research and possessed expertise in interview techniques. The study adhered to the principle of informed consent. Before starting the interview, appointments were scheduled with parents, and the study background, purpose, topics, and privacy protection measures were explained. The interviews were initiated and recorded after consent was obtained and agreements were signed. The interviews were recorded on a device and transcribed verbatim.

Two parents of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia, who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, were selected for pilot interviews, data from these two pilot interviews were not included in the final analysis. Pre-discharge interviews were conducted face-to-face in a quiet conference room, while post-discharge interviews were conducted via telephone or WeChat. The interview duration ranged from 25 to 35 min. The interviewers adapted the order of the questions flexibly on the basis of the participants’ responses, maintained neutrality, listened attentively, asked follow-up questions, and noted nonverbal cues such as emotions and gestures. To enhance trustworthiness, we employed member checking: after each interview, the transcribed text was returned to the participant for verification of accuracy and completeness. Additionally, peer debriefing was conducted through regular research team meetings (weekly during the data collection phase), where preliminary findings were discussed and challenged. The data collection was completed when no new information emerged. Specifically, after completing interviews with the 12th participant, the research team noted that no new codes or themes were identified. Subsequent interviews with three additional participants (No. 13–15) were conducted to verify saturation, and no further novel concepts emerged. The same process was applied to post-discharge interviews. Data from both time points were analyzed concurrently to capture both anticipatory and experiential dimensions. The two primary coders (CLX and LY) independently reviewed each new transcript and confirmed thematic saturation through team consensus.

Data analysis

Within 24 h after the interview, the recording was listened to repeatedly, transcribed into textual data, and returned to the interviewee for verification. Two researchers from the research group (CLX and LY) independently analyzed and coded the data of each interviewee while removing identifying information. NVivo 12 was used solely as a data management tool to organize codes, store coded segments, and facilitate retrieval. All coding and theme development were performed manually by the research team; no automated analysis was conducted. Content analysis was subsequently performed to analyze the data [23]. First, meaning unit identification: All raw data were read carefully multiple times to gain a holistic understanding. Meaning units (words, sentences, or paragraphs related to nutritional care experiences and needs) were identified. Second, condensation and coding: Each meaning unit was condensed while preserving its core content, and a code was assigned. Third, categorization: Codes with similar meanings were grouped into subcategories, then into categories. Final, theme development: The underlying meaning of categories was interpreted to develop themes. Coding disagreements between the two researchers were resolved through discussion and consensus; when agreement could not be reached, a third researcher (WHF) was consulted for arbitration. To ensure trustworthiness, peer debriefing was conducted through regular research team meetings (weekly during the data analysis phase), where preliminary findings, coding decisions, and emerging themes were discussed and challenged. The final themes were confirmed by the research team consensus.

Ethical approval

This study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants agreed to participate in the study and provided informed consent. Ethics committee approval was granted by a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou before initiating the study (IIT20250126B-R1).

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 15 family caregivers, namely, 7 mothers and 8 fathers, participated in the study. Their mean age was 32.2 (range 26 to 39) years, 66.67% (10/15) had a high school education or below, and only 13.3% (2/15) held a bachelor’s degree or above, rural households accounted for 46.67% (7/15), and 66.67% (10/15) of families reported a monthly income of 5,000 yuan or less. Approximately 53.3% (8/15) of the liver allografts were from living-related donors (from the mother or father). Theme 1. Nutritional knowledge deficits and fragmented information sources

Subtheme 1.1 Cognitive blind spots regarding the nutrition-disease relationship

According to the interview results, parents universally recognized the importance of nutrition for recovery (n = 15), but demonstrated significant knowledge gaps regarding sarcopenia. The majority of participants (12/15) had never heard the term, attributing malnutrition solely to liver disease progression. Most parents lacked an understanding of dietary requirements, restrictions, and supplementation for various vitamins and nutrients, thus preventing them from properly planning their infants’ diet and nutritional supplements. Moreover, they struggled with scientific feeding practices, often equating increased food intake with adequate nutrition while overlooking nutritional balance and proper proportioning.

When buying formula for our child, we didn’t carefully examine the ingredient list; the store clerk said this brand helps with weight gain, so we purchased it.(No. 5).

Subtheme 1.2 Fragmented and non‑professional knowledge acquisition

Parents relied on a mix of fragmented, non‑professional sources to acquire nutritional knowledge, including online platforms (Baidu, Douyin), relatives, friends, and patient support groups. The diversity of information sources triggered a crisis of trust in non‑professional information, causing hesitation in practice. Access to systematic hospital‑based education was limited due to scheduling conflicts.

Theme 2. Dualities of Nutritional Care: Burden and Growth

Subtheme 2.1 Emotional burden and financial burdens

Emotional burden was reported by 12 of 15 caregivers. The emotional toll on parents was significant, particularly because of the dietary restrictions that infants with sarcopenia must adhere to following a liver transplant. These restrictions are essential for the infants’ health but can lead to a range of negative emotions in parents. Parents experienced feeding-related guilt when they denied food requests:

My child sees other kids eating and is envious, often fussing with me. Although I know I can’t give him food, it’s still hard to bear, and I feel very guilty. (No. 3).

My child sees other kids eating and is envious, often fussing with me. Although I know I can’t give him food, it’s still hard to bear, and I feel very guilty. (No. 3).

Before, the child had diarrhea due to eating and became dehydrated. Now she has been relying on intravenous nutrient solutions to stay afloat. I dare not give her anything else to eat anymore (No. 15).

Before, the child had diarrhea due to eating and became dehydrated. Now she has been relying on intravenous nutrient solutions to stay afloat. I dare not give her anything else to eat anymore (No. 15).

Uncertainty surrounding the infants’ nutritional status and the impact of dietary restrictions can cause significant anxiety and stress for parents. The type, sequence, amount and duration of use of complementary foods can all affect the intestinal rehabilitation and growth and development of the infants. Some parents said that without the guidance of medical staff, they often did not know how to add complementary foods and felt powerless.

I’m worried about balancing nutrition after discharge, giving the baby too much is not good; too little is also not good. (No. 11).

I’m worried about balancing nutrition after discharge, giving the baby too much is not good; too little is also not good. (No. 11).

When I got home and started adding complementary foods to my child’s diet, if they experienced bloating or diarrhea, I wouldn’t dare to continue adding them, not knowing if the amount I added was appropriate. (No. 12).

Financial strain was reported by 11 of 15 caregivers. The economic pressure on families of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia not only directly affects family financial stability but also leads to family role conflicts and emotional detachment, creating a “double crisis” of economy and relationships. The families interviewed incurred large medical expenses, including expenses for specialized medical formula milk powder and immunosuppressive agents, which are long-term consumables.

Subtheme 2.2 Synergistic support networks and positive growth

Positive growth and support network experiences were described by 10 of 15 caregivers. Despite the significant challenges, the family has actually strengthened intergenerational cooperation and understanding, where grandparents reconfigured traditional roles to prioritize nutritional management. This practical assistance alleviated time conflicts for most primary caregivers, particularly those balancing a dual role of donor and recipient. Peer networks transformed into knowledge reservoirs, and nurses’ intentional engagement transcended technical guidance. Most parents gradually mastered nutritional care skills and developed deeper confidence.

Theme 3. Unmet needs: deficiencies in the nutritional support system

Subtheme 3.1 Lack of systematic nutritional course training

Unmet needs regarding systematic training and accessible educational resources were expressed by 13 of 15 caregivers. Parents hoped hospitals would provide systematic, multi-format training (e.g., manuals, QR-code videos) delivered by trusted professionals. They believe that the nutrition-related knowledge shared by medical staff was comprehensive and practical, offering essential support for making informed nutritional decisions and planning diets for their infants.

We would really appreciate it if doctors and nurses could guide us on nutrition. They are professionals, and we are willing to follow their advice. (No. 6, 8).

We would really appreciate it if doctors and nurses could guide us on nutrition. They are professionals, and we are willing to follow their advice. (No. 6, 8).

I think it would be quite good to make it into a manual or make a table. It would be best to write in detail for us how much each thing we eat every day. In this way, it will be easier for us to understand. It would also be best to have videos to watch (No. 13).

I think it would be quite good to make it into a manual or make a table. It would be best to write in detail for us how much each thing we eat every day. In this way, it will be easier for us to understand. It would also be best to have videos to watch (No. 13).

Subtheme 3.2 Discontinuity at follow-up after discharge

High-quality post-discharge follow-up is crucial for enhancing parents’ ability to provide nutritional care at home. However, 11 of 15 caregivers reported that during follow-up visits, nurses focus too much on the infants’ current growth and development and not enough on home-based nutritional care. They hope medical staff could promptly address their questions and offer guidance on nutritional care. Nevertheless, the current follow-up system emphasizes “data collection” over “problem-solving”, failing to cover the entire process from the acute phase to recovery.

The dietitian visited once during my hospital stay, but I couldn’t reach them after discharge. I felt abandoned. (No. 13).

The dietitian visited once during my hospital stay, but I couldn’t reach them after discharge. I felt abandoned. (No. 13).

Nurses followed up regularly after discharge, but they only inquired about growth and development data and couldn’t resolve actual issues. (No. 9).

Nurses followed up regularly after discharge, but they only inquired about growth and development data and couldn’t resolve actual issues. (No. 9).

Subtheme 3.3 Insufficient nutritional assessment services

Systematic nutritional assessment is essential for creating a rational nutrition plan and evaluating treatment efficacy for infants. It enables timely treatment-strategy adjustment and is valued by parents. Some parents (n = 9) have called for standardized nutritional assessment services to gain a comprehensive understanding of their infants’ nutritional status. This would aid healthcare workers in devising targeted, individualized nutritional-support strategies.

In addition to height and weight, what indicators should I monitor to assess my child’s nutritional status? (No. 2).

Discussion

This qualitative study explored the nutritional care experiences and unmet needs of family caregivers of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia. Three key themes were identified, including substantial nutritional knowledge deficits and reliance on non-professional information sources, the coexistence of heavy emotional and financial burdens alongside positive growth and social support, and multiple gaps in the institutional nutritional support system. These findings underscore the complexity of nutritional care in this vulnerable population and highlight the pivotal role of family caregivers in postoperative nutritional management.

Improving nutritional literacy through structured education for family caregivers

Parents of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia are the primary decision-makers for nutritional care, and their health literacy fundamentally shapes the effectiveness of dietary interventions. The study revealed significant knowledge gaps among caregivers: many had never heard the term “sarcopenia,” equated increased food intake with adequate nutrition, and lacked awareness of micronutrient requirements. These findings are consistent with global reports on transplant populations [24]. During the early postoperative period, caregivers largely depend on healthcare providers for nutritional guidance but often fail to master core skills due to limited engagement. Although the study center provides perioperative nutritional booklets, educational videos, and informal bedside teaching, these resources are delivered passively without assessing caregivers’ comprehension or skill mastery. Caregivers also face restricted access to systematic hospital-based education due to time constraints. Furthermore, healthcare providers tend to prioritize surgical outcomes over specialized nutritional support, leading families to seek information from informal channels such as online platforms, relatives, friends, and peer support groups. While easily accessible, such sources lack scientific rigor and reliability, in line with previous observations, consistent with the findings of Liu et al. [18]. Our findings suggest that a shift from passive education to a practical, competency-based training model with verifiable outcomes may be beneficial for this population. Brief bedside instruction with return demonstrations by caregivers may be adopted. In addition, digital resources such as short instructional videos may be developed for on-demand access to accommodate time constraints among caregivers. The function of WeChat groups for peer support can be further optimized through administration by dedicated nurses or dietitians, who provide evidence-based responses to common feeding-related questions.

Paying attention to caregivers’ dynamic emotions and providing timely emotional support

Family caregivers in this study experienced a dynamic interplay of negative emotions and positive growth during nutritional care. Feeding-related guilt, anxiety over nutritional balance, family conflicts, and financial strain related to specialized formula and immunosuppressive medications were commonly reported [25]. Notably, several caregivers were living liver donors, who bore the dual burden of postoperative recovery and infant nutritional management. Physical discomfort, fatigue, and role conflict may further compromise caregiving capacity and psychological well-being. These findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting the heavy burden and psychological distress among caregivers of pediatric liver transplant recipients [26]. Meanwhile, positive growth was also observed, including strengthened family cohesion, enhanced caregiving skills, and support from peer networks and healthcare providers. Grandparents adjusted traditional feeding practices to support evidence-based nutritional care, and peer groups served as important sources of experiential information. Some caregivers gradually developed confidence and proficiency in feeding management, reflecting adaptive resilience. Therefore, healthcare professionals need to focus on the nutritional care experiences of parents, encourage them to express their inner feelings, and provide timely psychological support through methods such as mindfulness-based stress reduction and solution-focused counseling to alleviate their negative emotions [27–29]. Additionally, healthcare professionals should establish a family-centered holistic care model, regularly organize family meetings, guide family members to participate together in infants’ recovery, and implement peer support by regularly organizing parent fellowship meetings, encouraging parents with similar experiences to share their caregiving experiences and emotional experiences, and promoting positive communication among parents to increase family resilience and reduce parents’ psychological burden [20, 30].

Formulate a follow-up plan for discharge and provide family nutritional support

Some interviewees in this study expressed their desire for healthcare professionals to provide home-based nutritional care guidance during post-discharge follow-ups, hoping for timely and continuous family nutritional support services from medical staff. Research has confirmed that structured nutritional follow‑up and continuous nursing interventions are critical for optimizing nutritional outcomes in pediatric liver transplant recipients. A recent study, for example, indicated that Internet+-enabled continuous nursing effectively ameliorates children’s nutritional status after liver transplantation, alleviates stress and depressive symptoms in primary caregivers, and boosts family treatment adherence [31]. Such results offer solid evidence-based rationale for developing long-term, family-centered nutritional care frameworks for this patient group. Although the study center has established a post-transplant follow-up protocol, current follow-up practices focus primarily on the infant’s post-transplant recovery outcomes rather than proactively understand feeding-related problems such as abdominal distension, diarrhea, or food refusal. Moreover, while height and weight are monitored, comprehensive nutritional assessment is not routinely integrated into every follow-up visit, aligning with caregivers’ call for more systematic services. Based on these findings, the following improvements are suggested. Nutritional assessment should be incorporated into routine post-transplant procedures. Before discharge, comprehensive training with demonstration and return demonstration should be provided to ensure caregivers master safe and appropriate feeding techniques. After discharge, follow-up should shift from simple data collection to targeted problem-solving using structured questions about feeding experiences. Standardized nutritional assessment checklists covering dietary intake, feeding behaviors, and growth parameters should be used during outpatient visits within the first three months post-transplant. An online consultation platform staffed by dietitians may be established to provide timely, evidence-based answers to individualized feeding questions. Healthcare providers may assist families in developing personalized dietary plans with clear guidance on food selection, cooking methods, and portion sizes. Such integrated, continuous nutritional support is expected to improve caregivers’ competence, optimize infants’ nutritional status, and promote long-term recovery.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it employed a single center qualitative design, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other settings with different care models, patient populations, or healthcare systems. Second, the sample size was relatively small (15 caregivers) and selected purposively, which may not capture the full spectrum of experiences among family caregivers of infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia. Third, post discharge interviews were conducted via telephone and WeChat rather than face to face; this approach, while practical for maintaining contact after discharge, prevented the observation of non-verbal cues (e.g., facial expressions, body language) that might have enriched the data. Finally, only family caregivers were interviewed; In the future, perspectives from medical professionals (such as nurses, dietitians, and transplant surgeons) can be incorporated, which will help to more comprehensively understand the difficulties and favorable factors in nutrition support management.

Conclusions

This qualitative study highlights three critical challenges to effective nutritional care for infants who underwent liver transplantation with preoperative sarcopenia: profound deficits in family nutritional literacy, significant emotional and financial burdens that impair caregiving capacity, and systemic discontinuities in follow-up support. Addressing these challenges requires systematic nutritional guidance, proactive psychological support, and continuous follow-up to enhance families’ capacity for nutritional care, thereby supporting catch-up growth and optimal development in these infants. Future research should focus on implementing and evaluating family-centered nutritional follow-up models to improve these post-discharge outcomes.
