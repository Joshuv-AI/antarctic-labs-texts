Introduction

Graduating pediatric residents lack sufficient pediatric musculoskeletal knowledge [1]. This training gap has been identified by both pediatric residency training programs and orthopaedic surgeons. In 2004, Reeder et al. reported that up to 65% of pediatric orthopaedic referrals were deemed inappropriate by American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines [2]. Over the past two decades, this has not significantly changed. Balzas et al., in 2019, reported only 52% of referrals to pediatric orthopaedic surgeons were appropriate [3,4]. Both studies recommended improved pediatrician education as an approach to solving this issue. It is important to note that medical school and primary care residency curricula historically lack sufficient musculoskeletal education and training, contributing to the general lack of understanding and lack of confidence in treating orthopaedic conditions across many nonsurgical specialties [5,6].

There are many barriers and conflicting interests in pediatric residency programs including other Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) requirements, duty hour restrictions, electronic medical record burden, and burnout [7]. There have been initiatives such as the annual AAP section on orthopaedics Musculoskeletal (MSK) bootcamp to address this knowledge gap, focused on practicing pediatricians [8]. This initiative began in 2016 at 1–2 rotating academic sites across the country. In 2021, Crenshaw et al. reported that attendees responded favorably and had improved confidence in diagnosing musculoskeletal conditions postattendance. Limitations of this course include limited audience size, 1-day length, geographic access, and the physicians’ time and funding available for Continuing Medical Education (CME) courses. In 2024, this curriculum was offered as a precourse at the AAP National Conference and Exhibit in an effort to increase ease of access and efficiency for attendees and defray their costs. The course filled quickly, and there were plans to increase the attendance capacity for 2025. Longitudinal educational data are needed to measure the impact of this educational format on practice and referral trends in the primary care management of pediatric musculoskeletal conditions.

Pediatric orthopaedic surgery clinics are traditionally busy and may have long wait times. In 2012, almost half of the conditions seen in pediatric orthopaedic clinics were considered primary care variants, and only 7% of cases needed surgery [9]. Primary care variant referrals can contribute to longer wait times within the clinics as well as the length of time to first-available appointment upon referral. One of the key roles of pediatric orthopaedic medicine (POM) specialists is to reduce wait time, improve access, and work with orthopaedic surgeons in a complementary, collaborative fashion. Having physicians trained in both primary care and orthopaedics available every day in the clinic to evaluate and manage the spectrum of musculoskeletal conditions can streamline clinical workflow for the surgeons and allow them more time with patients needing operative consultation and treatment.

Primary care sports medicine (PCSM) was the first nonsurgical field outside the orthopaedic surgical subspecialty to integrate into orthopaedic practices [10]. PCSM training focuses on musculoskeletal injuries in physically active individuals and athletes of all ages. Several pediatric residency programs offer an elective in pediatric PCSM. In contrast, there are far fewer training programs that offer a primary care elective for evaluation and management of the full spectrum of pediatric orthopaedic conditions: fracture diagnosis and management, congenital anomalies such as hip dysplasia and clubfeet, normal developmental variants, common gait abnormalities, acquired structural deformities such as scoliosis, and neuromuscular conditions in infants and children. A physician trained in POM helps bridge this gap.

There have been several pediatricians and physiatrists trained in POM by pediatric orthopaedic surgeons in an apprenticeship model who were then hired into that specific group [1]. As this initial model has evolved significantly over the past 20 years with the creation of formal non-ACGME fellowships, POM physicians now have taken on the role of training POM fellows in addition to other musculoskeletal stakeholders. The first fellowship of this kind began in 2005 at the University of Wisconsin. Trainees have gone on to practice throughout the nation. POM specialists are trained in the full spectrum of pediatric orthopaedic conditions, from infancy through skeletal maturity. To date, no one has shared data capturing the practice scope of these physicians.

The workforce survey aimed to obtain information on the demographics, training, practice setting, and conditions managed. This report summarizes the findings of the inaugural POM workforce survey of the AAP Section on Orthopaedics (AAP-SOOr).

Methods

Board-certified members of the AAP-SOOr developed a survey to define and describe the POM subspecialty and its workforce. These members included 6 POM physicians and 3 pediatric orthopaedic surgeons with collectively over 20 years of experience. The survey included 79 questions concerning demographics, training, clinical practice, and profile of activities. All the questions were generated, reviewed, and refined by the nonsurgical subcommittee of the AAP-SOOr with the approval of the AAP-SOOr executive leadership. The survey was administered to all known POM physicians between July 18, 2023, and August 31, 2023 as defined by an email list provided by senior author and first POM physician. Nonrespondents received 2 reminders to complete the survey. Responses were reviewed for this summary descriptive report. Data analysis was performed using STATA 14.0 to generate descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions and measures of central tendency. This study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board of the AAP.

Professional training

Seventy-eight percent of respondents had earned an allopathic degree, and 22% had osteopathic degrees. Twenty-one respondents were trained in pediatrics, one in family medicine, and two in physical medicine and rehabilitation. Two respondents were originally matched in a surgical specialty and later transferred to a primary care residency. One person completed an additional pediatric chief residency year. The majority reported completing fellowship training in POM (87%), with a minority trained in an apprenticeship fashion by a pediatric orthopaedic surgeon (4%). A small proportion completed additional fellowship training in sports medicine (13%).

At the time of the survey, POM physicians in academic practice held a spectrum of academic ranks and titles. Fifty-six percent were assistant professors, 28% were associate professors, 11% were clinical instructors, and 6% were adjunct professors.

Location, practice composition, and salary

Fifty-seven percent of the respondents practiced in large metropolitan areas, 30% in medium-population area, 9% in smaller cities, and 4% in rural areas. Fifty-five percent were employees of children's hospitals, 32% university group, 1% with private practice, and 9% were “other.” Exclusive academic practice was the most common (58%), followed by participation in a multispecialty group (17%) or community hospital (4%).

Most POM physicians reported working within larger orthopaedic surgical practices, with the number of pediatric orthopaedic surgical partners varying from 2 to more than 15 surgeons. Sixty-eight percent of respondents practiced alongside primary care sports medicine specialists. Sixty-eight percent of study participants were the sole POM specialist in their group practice; the remaining had 1-3 POM partners. Most POM physicians worked full-time, and a majority held 4–5 clinic days per week. All respondents endorsed clinical activities, 83% had teaching responsibilities, 39% participated in research activities, 35% held leadership positions at their institutions, 30% performed community service, and 74% had protected administrative time (Fig. 1). No providers reported having overnight on-call responsibilities, though some reported having evening or weekend hours. The mean annual base salary reported was $232,000 ($178,000 to $375,000). This does not include the value of their benefits or incentive pay. Of the 12 respondents who reported Relative Value Units (RVUs), the mean was 4,970 (1200–9500 RVUs). They report a wide spectrum of pediatric orthopaedic conditions and procedures performed in the outpatient orthopaedic clinic (Tables 2a and b).

Discussion

The results of this survey of POM specialists revealed physicians with representation across different age groups, races, professional backgrounds, practice settings, and academic ranks. These findings provide valuable insights into the characteristics of this evolving pediatric specialty, which can inform strategies for workforce development and recruitment in the field of POM.

Many POM physicians teach fellow pediatricians and family medicine doctors, primary care residents, fellows, and medical students. Primary care providers and trainees learning from pediatricians with expertize in orthopaedics may help bridge the knowledge gap more effectively. There are currently 5 fellowship programs in the country; however, there are challenges in filling these spots given that this subspecialty is largely unknown, undermarketed, and not routinely included in medical training. Given the small number of pediatric orthopaedic medical physicians in the nation, it is rare that a trainee has early exposure to the field. Other disciplines and newer fellowships have seen significant growth during the past 20 years. Developmental Behavioral and Child Abuse fellowships started in 2002 and 2009, respectively and have grown tremendously [11]. Since 2014, between 19 and 27 fellowship positions were offered annually in the child abuse program, with about 8 unfilled positions each year [12]. These programs' marketing plans may provide insights into the POM training programs.

POM specialists care for patients with a broad spectrum of orthopaedic problems across the full range of ages from infancy through young adulthood. They have a solid foundation of medical knowledge drawn from training in primary care that can expand and enhance the scope of care provided in a pediatric orthopaedic clinic. Many are skilled in common procedures: fracture reduction, splinting and casting, joint aspiration, and injection, clubfoot casting, the performance of percutaneous Achilles tenotomy, botulinum toxin injection, bedside ultrasounds, and choosing appropriate bracing for injuries, congenital malformations, and neuromuscular conditions. POM specialists play a role in complex care through participating in multidisciplinary clinics, which provide a broader educational opportunity for trainees and more holistic medical care for children with complex needs seen by pediatric orthopaedists. POM physicians also collaborate closely with and refer to their surgical colleagues in the care of orthopaedic patients whenever indicated.

For the first time, the 2024 Match saw a significant decrease in those matching into categorical pediatric residency [13]. Harold K. Simon M.D., M.B.A., FAAP, chair of the AAP Committee on Pediatric Workforce stated, “The large number of unmatched positions is concerning for our general pediatrician and pediatric subspecialty pathways.” In an era of physician burnout, these quality-of-life factors may drive trainees' decisions about what subspecialty to choose. While compensation varies, POM physicians are compensated as academic pediatric subspecialists. Additional benefits include zero or minimal call coverage, night, and weekend work. The potential drop in the pediatric workforce could further impact the field of POM.

The major limitation of this study was the responses were based on participant recall, which introduces recall bias. Only 50% of respondents had information about their salary composition due to varied practices and reimbursement models; many were unaware of their RVU production. Additionally there may be regional differences and recall bias limits generalizability, but these features represent the opportunity to develop practices based on needs within the participants’ groups and organizations, as well as individual interests and strengths. Future studies could identify barriers to POM practice. Issues such as reimbursement, the perceived value within the pediatric orthopaedic surgical community, and more data exploring access will be important to address in the future.

Conclusion

This inaugural workforce survey captured information defining the new specialty of POM. It provided information on the current demographics, clinical practice settings, and reported broad orthopaedic conditions managed by POM physicians as a group who are largely full-time academic physicians with research and teaching activities. These findings provide valuable insights into the characteristics of this group, which can inform strategies for workforce development and recruitment in POM.
