Machine learning prediction of increased healthcare utilization in colectomy for inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Gustavo Salgado-Garza, Elliot Ballato, Cynthia Araradian, Maura Walsh, Sandy H. Fang, V. Liana Tsikitis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeHealthcare spending for patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has steadily increased over the past few decades. While some of the increase is due to the rise in the use of biologics, surgery and postoperative care contribute significantly to healthcare spending. We aimed to analyze the preoperative factors associated with increased healthcare resource utilization (HRU) by developing and internally validating machine learning prediction models for increased HRU.MethodsWe analyzed the ACS-NSQIP database from 2018 to 2022 to identify preoperative factors that could predict increased HRU in patients undergoing non-emergent colectomy for Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis. Increased HRU was defined as a composite of either (a) discharge destination other than home, (b) readmission within 30 days of surgery, or (c) prolonged length of stay. A total of six machine learning algorithms were utilized to find the best-performing model determined by area under the curve. Models were internally validated with an 80:20 training and testing split.ResultsA total of 7535 patients were analyzed. The overall rate of increased HRU was 33%. The best-performing model was the random forest, achieving an AUC of 0.75, an accuracy of 0.73, and an F1 of 0.82. The most important preoperative variables for model accuracy were albumin, hematocrit, and creatinine.ConclusionMachine learning may assist in identifying IBD patients at risk for increased healthcare resource utilization. Here, we propose a predictive model in the preoperative setting that may allow targeted perioperative planning to optimize outcomes and resource use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00384-026-05184-w.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC), and indeterminate colitis, is a multifactorial, chronic pathology that affects millions of patients worldwide [1]. Recent estimates report 2.39 million Americans have IBD [2], and prevalence has only increased in the last decade in both developing and developed countries [3]. In the past decade, treatment of IBD has evolved as recent advances in biologic therapy options for IBD have decreased the need for steroid use outside of acute attacks [4]. The efficacy of new therapies has also led to a steady decrease in the number of patients requiring surgical management [5, 6] as evidenced by decreasing rates of non-urgent surgery [7–9]. However, around 50% of patients will undergo surgery to treat their IBD across their lifetime—with rates as high as 80% for those with Crohn’s disease [10]. In patients with UC, the 10-year colectomy rate was found to be as high as 19% for those with extensive colitis [11].

Despite decreasing rates of surgical management, direct and indirect costs for IBD patients are significant and increasing [12]. Initially, the cost of biologics contributed a large percentage of healthcare costs for IBD patients. However, after normalization in pharmaceutical costs, a shift has been reported in the percentage of healthcare expenditure owed to inpatient and outpatient care [13]. Other important drivers of cost of care include both the need for and history of surgery. Inpatient and emergency department care accounts for over half of total spending in IBD [13, 14]. To date, no study has leveraged machine learning techniques to predict increased healthcare resource utilization (HRU) following IBD surgery.

HRU among IBD patients includes hospitalizations, medications, surgical interventions, healthcare services, ED visits, and readmissions. In our study, we defined HRU as discharge to healthcare facilities other than home, unplanned readmission, and prolonged length of stay in the postoperative setting.

Identification of surgical patients with high risk of increased HRU may guide decision and policy making to strengthen the medical infrastructure. We aim to identify preoperative factors through machine learning models to predict increased HRU in those undergoing surgery for inflammatory bowel disease.

Methods

The American College of Surgeon’s National Quality Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP)-targeted colectomy database from 2018 to 2022 was filtered to include cases of elective colectomy for inflammatory bowel disease. CPT codes for total (44150, 44151, and 44155–44158) and partial (44140, 44141, 44143–44147, and 44160) colectomy were included. The ACS-NSQIP is a nationally validated, risk-adjusted, outcome-based program that prospectively collects detailed clinical parameters for major surgical procedures. Data are captured by trained clinical reviewers and undergo regular audits to ensure fidelity, making it a gold-standard resource for surgical outcomes research.

The outcome variable of increased HRU was defined as a composite of either (a) discharge destination other than home (b) readmission within 30 days of surgery or (c) prolonged length of stay. In ACS-NSQIP, non-home discharge is defined as any discharge to a skilled nursing facility, physical rehabilitation living facility or separate acute care unit. Prolonged length of stay was defined as any length of hospital stay greater than the 75th percentile in the included cases. If any of the conditions were met, the outcome of increased HRU would be marked as present [15]. Patients that met multiple criteria for increased HRU were only counted once for our analysis. All the predictive variables, including laboratory values, that entered the models are preoperative.

A total of six machine learning algorithms were compared prioritizing the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) as metrics to identify the best performing model. We chose AUC as the guiding metric because it offers both statistical robustness and interpretability for surgeons as it is increasingly more commonly seen in clinical publications [16]. Accuracy was defined as how often the model correctly predicted increased HRU. F1 is calculated from positive predictive value and sensitivity and was evaluated as a model metric [17]. We then selected machine learning models that were both available to use in our data and have been successfully used in surgical prediction tasks in other publications [18, 19]. The models included random forest, XGBoost, logistic regression, bagged trees, light GBM, and C5. Numerical missing data were handled by k-nearest neighbor imputation, while categorical missing data were handled by pairwise deletion. The data were randomly split into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets. Calibration plots were utilized to visually evaluate model probability outputs. All data handling, machine learning algorithm construction, and analysis were performed using R Statistical Software version 4.3.3. The R packages that were utilized can be found referenced here [20–22].

The Oregon Health & Science University is a participating institution in the ACS-NSQIP, and the authors had full access to data submitted by all contributing sites. The project was exempt from full IRB oversight per OHSU’s IRB determination (#28374), in accordance with institutional guidelines for ethical research, due to the de-identified nature of the ACS-NSQIP dataset.

Results

A total of 7535 patients who underwent colectomy for IBD were identified, of which 6217 were for CD and 1318 for UC. 1853 (25.2%) patients underwent total colectomy, with 775 being for CD and 1078 for UC (12.5% and 81.8% of their respective groups). Patients with CD were more likely to have a history of smoking (22.2% vs 7.7%, 19.7% overall) while patients with UC were more likely to have a history of diabetes or hypertension (10.8% vs 4.2% and 25.7% vs 19.7% respectively). Model performance

The random forest model outperformed all other models in terms of accuracy. It demonstrated strong performance in sensitivity, precision, F1, and AUC. Receiver operating characteristic curve for the random forest model showed an AUC of 0.752. Most of our models achieved a relatively low specificity. This reflects our prioritization of identifying patients at risk of increased HRU at the tradeoff of false positives. The model with the poorest performance was C5 algorithm with an AUC of 0.697. For the random forest model, the calibration plot shows no calibration issues. We can see minor underestimations at low probabilities and slight overconfidence at very high probabilities. The calibration plot can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 1. Additional evaluations of calibration with Brier score for this model were 0.183 (0 representing perfect prediction). Parameters used for our random forest models were as follows: 500 as the number of trees, minimum number of data points in a node before splitting: 10. These parameters were used to reduce variance and avoid overfitting.

We also looked at the independent variables that drove the model’s accuracy in the random forest model. Serum albumin, hematocrit, and creatinine as preoperative laboratories were the variables that drove the random forest model’s accuracy. For inherently surgical variables, the need for ostomy was more significant than type of colectomy performed for model accuracy.

To explain the output of our best performing machine learning model, we used Shapley Additive explanations (SHAP) values. Positive values increase the model’s prediction for increased HRU, and negative values reduce it. For example, higher hematocrit values (yellow) are associated with lower predicted increased HRU, while lower hematocrit (purple) shifts predictions towards increased HRU. To evaluate the individual components that comprise the main outcome, we performed a sensitivity analysis for each of these components. We ran three separate random forest models with the same parameters as the composite outcome analysis. Non-home discharge analysis revealed an AUC of 0.80, prolonged length of stay AUC of 0.77, and 30-day readmission of 0.62. The sensitivity analysis revealed that the random forest model was most effective at predicting the immediate and medium-term postoperative outcomes. Discussion

In this study, we found that patients undergoing colectomy for IBD have a high rate of increased HRU. Although surgery was more frequent among patients with Crohn’s disease, patients with ulcerative colitis experienced significantly higher proportion of increased HRU (45% vs. 31%, p < 0.001). The random forest model was adequately accurate in predicting increased HRU using only readily available preoperative parameters. The top driver of model accuracy was albumin, particularly identifying a higher risk of increased HRU in those with lower preoperative albumin values. Five of the six most important variables for model metrics were laboratory parameters. This highlights our model’s theoretical widespread applicability in the preoperative setting. As such, patients identified preoperatively as having albumin, creatinine, sodium, platelet, white blood cell, or hematocrit derangements could be counseled that their hospital stay may be longer than expected or that they have an increased chance of discharging to a skilled nursing facility. Specifically, higher levels of albumin, hematocrit, and sodium were strong predictors of no increased HRU, whereas elevated blood urea nitrogen and white blood cell counts predicted an increased HRU.

A clinically significant finding from our analysis is the identification of low preoperative creatinine as a primary predictor of increased HRU. While elevated creatinine is a well-known marker of renal impairment, a growing body of evidence indicates that in the context of chronic illness and surgery [23, 24], low serum creatinine serves as a powerful surrogate for reduced muscle mass, a key component of sarcopenia and the broader frailty syndrome. Creatinine is a byproduct of muscle metabolism, and its serum concentration is directly proportional to total body muscle mass. Frailty, a state of decreased physiologic reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors, is a known independent risk factor for a wide range of adverse postoperative events including prolonged length of stay, non-home discharge, and readmission [25]. These are the very components of the HRU outcome. Our model’s ability to identify low creatinine as a key predictive feature suggests it is capturing this underlying state of vulnerability, which may be more influential than some traditional comorbidity measures. This finding provides a potential biological basis for our model’s predictions, underscoring the potential for preoperative screening for frailty and prehabilitation programs to mitigate the risk of high HRU in this population.

Machine learning has facilitated and augmented surgical prediction models that use big data. However, the “black box” nature of these algorithms often limits their clinical utility by obscuring the underlying decision-making processes. To address this, we used SHAP values to transform opaque predictors into interpretable insights. Unlike simple feature importance rankings, SHAP plots visualize the unique contribution of each variable, revealing directionality and magnitude of effect on the risk of increased HRU. By showing how high or low values of variables like albumin or hematocrit shift the prediction, these plots allow clinicians to verify that the model’s decision making aligns with established clinical insight.

For our sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table 2) with single-outcome prediction models, patient frailty also plays a key role. This is exemplified by the performance variance between the best and worst-performing single-outcome models on subanalysis. In ACS-NSQIP, non-home discharge is defined as any discharge to a skilled nursing facility, physical rehabilitation living facility or separate acute care unit. This is consistent with previously published literature that links frailty with non-home discharge for patients admitted from home [26, 27]. Contrary, 30-day readmissions might be driven by postoperative complications not uniformly captured by preoperative predictors [28]. We propose that while patient-intrinsic and laboratory-based preoperative variables effectively characterize frailty, aggregating three outcomes into a single composite variable ensures a balanced prediction that accounts for factors unobservable in the preoperative setting.

Previous studies have shown worse colectomy outcomes with preoperative low hemoglobin levels and white blood cell count [29–32]; however, these reports do not include the current use of advanced immunotherapies. Our study thus utilizes data from the current era of medicine with broader adoption of biologics, accurately depicting the current landscape of IBD treatment. Our work is unique in leveraging machine learning approaches as opposed to more conventional statistical methods to capitalize on accuracy using big data. A recent report underscored the association between patient-reported symptoms and increased HRU [33]. To our knowledge, there are no prior studies that have intentionally examined healthcare resource utilization in the surgical context for the IBD population [33]. We argue that the use of highly accurate and validated predictive models can guide resource allocation and surgical preparedness. Here we have identified a use case to better predict where and when increased HRU will occur. A 2016 study found that IBD ranks 36th among healthcare spending in the USA with estimated total healthcare spending of $25.3 billion in 2016, highlighting the importance of this issue [13, 34]. Further research aimed at externally validating a predictive model such as the one we present is certainly warranted.

Notable limitations of our study include its retrospective design and the limited granularity of the ACS-NSQIP database. Although ACS-NSQIP captures CPT and ICD coding, it lacks IBD-specific variables, including Montreal or Mayo classifications and details regarding patient-tailored biologic therapy [35, 36]. Inclusion of these data could better characterize disease-specific factors and provide important contextual information for incorporation into future machine learning models. In addition, our model was internally validated within the NSQIP dataset; external validation using institutional datasets not included in ACS-NSQIP would further strengthen the generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, ACS-NSQIP remains a robust, nationally validated, risk-adjusted, outcome-based database. Future validation using ACS-NSQIP data releases beyond those included in this study would also help establish the temporal validity of our findings.

Conclusions

Readily available preoperative parameters can accurately predict increased healthcare resource utilization among patients undergoing colectomy for IBD. In our final machine learning model, hematocrit, albumin, and creatinine were the primary drivers of predictive accuracy. Validated predictive models for increased healthcare resource utilization may enhance patient counseling and support more effective healthcare system resource allocation.
