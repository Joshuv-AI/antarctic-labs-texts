Research needs, barriers, and related factors among resident physicians: a multicenter survey in Southern China
Authors: Pingting Yang, Lei Yan, Yanhong Jang, Hui Chen, Wenzheng Peng, Bo Wu, Qian Su, Qingnan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntegrating research training into residency programs can help resident physicians gain insights into cutting-edge treatment options and technologies, effectively improving patient outcomes.MethodsResidents from several hospitals affiliated with leading medical universities in a province in southern China were surveyed using online questionnaires about their current research status, attitudes toward research activities, and barriers they face.ResultsOf the total 546 resident physicians surveyed, 103 (18.86%) had prior experience participating in research projects, and 82 (15.02%) had published articles. The average (mean ± SD) scores for “research knowledge” and “total research attitude” were 3.25 ± 2.16 and 43.13 ± 7.42, respectively. These reflect a moderate level of research knowledge and a high level of positive attitudes toward the usefulness of research. Multivariate analysis revealed that factors including gender, types of resident physician (clinical professional masters, commissioned resident physician, socialized residents), highest degree, preferred hospital for post-training employment, and prior participation in research were associated with both research knowledge scores and research attitudes. The average “total score for research barriers” was 46.56 ± 10.21. The four highest-rated barriers were “lack of time to do research because of clinical tasks,” “lack of good research ideas,” “insufficient research skills,” and “lack of familiarity with research studies/statistical analysis.”ConclusionDespite their positive attitudes toward research, the resident physicians in this study faced challenges such as lack of time, guidance, and skills in research practice. Therefore, ensuring dedicated research time and providing training opportunities can help reduce the barriers faced by resident physicians. Stratified training based on competence and needs can better enhance their productivity under resource-limited conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07802-7.Keywords: Medical research, Resident physicians, Research knowledge, Attitudes, Barriers

Background

Medical research education is a core component of the medical training system, and its paramount value lies in cultivating a cohort of physicians who are proficient not only in clinical skills but also in research capabilities. These research-savvy physicians, by virtue of their unique dual identities as both clinicians and researchers, successfully bridge the gap between theoretical research and clinical practice, thereby contributing to the enhancement of medical service quality and the advancement of scientific research endeavors. Through direct participation in research activities, doctors gain profound insights into cutting-edge treatment protocols and technologies. This invaluable knowledge can be integrated into their clinical practice, ultimately leading to significant improvements in patient outcomes [1].

For resident physicians, accumulating research experience serves as the foundation for evaluating medical literature. By mastering scientific research methods and data analysis skills, they can cultivate critical thinking, accurately assess research findings, and precisely determine their applicability to specific patient populations or communities. Globally, research experience has become an indispensable component of medical education program certification, ensuring that all medical trainees receive comprehensive education that includes research training. Participation in research activities also significantly enhances the competitive advantage of medical students, residents, and researchers in their future careers, demonstrating their steadfast pursuit of academic excellence and positioning them favorably in the intense competition of their careers [2, 3].

In 2014, China officially launched a comprehensive and Standardized Residency Training system (SRT) as a core component of medical student education. After several years of relentless efforts, China has successfully established a SRT for clinical doctors at the doctoral, master’s, and bachelor’s levels [4, 5]. In accordance with the requirements of this system, medical graduates must Complete a rigorous 33-month training program in multiple departments within their chosen specialty area and pass the physician qualification exam. This has become an important pathway for medical graduates to delve into specialized subdomains of their profession. Only by successfully completing this training can resident physicians obtain the qualifications to practice independently in hospitals. This stage not only marks a crucial period of transition from medical students to mature doctors but also represents an educational journey that deeply integrates theory with practice and clinical work with research [6, 7].

For resident physicians, participating in academic research and activities is a vital pathway to staying abreast of medical advancements, deepening their understanding of professional knowledge, laying a solid foundation for their future careers, and significantly enhancing their competitiveness in applying for scholarships and academic positions [8]. However, the challenges faced by resident physicians far exceed general expectations, encompassing heavy clinical workloads, high-pressure work environments, strained doctor–patient relationships, and a lack of professional identity. They must endure longer training periods, higher assessment standards, and stricter evaluation requirements while also potentially grappling with financial pressures, uncertainties about career prospects, and complex training and evaluation procedures [9]. In this context, research training and activities often become additional burdens beyond their clinical responsibilities. The attitudes of resident physicians toward research and the obstacles they encounter are crucial factors influencing their research outcomes. Negative attitudes can hinder their learning progress, while insufficient interest, a lack of knowledge about research methods, poor time management, heavy clinical duties, inadequate mentorship, and limited resources all constitute bottlenecks that restrict the conduct of their research activities [10]. Currently, empirical research in this field in China is still insufficient, and further in-depth exploration is urgently needed to optimize the research environment and training effectiveness for resident physicians.

This study focuses on the resident physician population in multiple affiliated hospitals of a leading medical university in a southern province of China. Through a questionnaire survey, the current research status of resident physicians, their attitudes toward research activities, the barriers they face, and the related influencing factors. By listening to the authentic needs of frontline resident physicians, targeted suggestion schemes are proposed, aiming to enhance the research capabilities and comprehensive qualities of resident physicians and to cultivate clinical medical talent that meets the requirements of the new era.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional, descriptive survey was conducted with the primary objective of delineating the research knowledge, attitudes, and perceived barriers among resident physicians across various hospitals in southern China. The reporting of this study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist, ensuring rigorous and comprehensive documentation [11]. This study was approved by the Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (No. 24005).

Setting

For this study, an online questionnaire was distributed using the “sojump” survey tool, with a data Collection period spanning from December 1st, 2024, to December 20th, 2024. Responses Completed in less than 200 s were deemed invalid to ensure the quality of the data Collected. The survey was conducted anonymously, and upon completion, the participants were rewarded with a random red envelope containing an average amount of RMB 4 as an incentive.

Participants and enrollment

In this study, we designated three foremost teaching hospitals and one municipal hospital in the province as our subcenters. The sample size allocation for each hospital was proportionally determined according to the number of resident doctors at each institution, as illustrated in the flowchart in Fig. 1. Our study population comprised solely residents engaged in standardized residency training programs; individuals not participating in such programs were excluded from the survey. All participants were meticulously invited to complete the questionnaire through personalized invitations facilitated by members of our research team, utilizing both online and offline methods.

Sample size

The questionnaire of this study has a total of 52 questions, of which 7 are skip questions, which some people do not need to answer, so the number of questions in this questionnaire is 45–52, and the sample size is determined to be greater than 500 people according to the number of respondents as 5–10 times the number of questions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26. Continuous variables, such as individual Likert scale responses, were presented using measures of central tendency, specifically the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables, such as gender, were summarized using frequency tables. To test the relationship between scores and demographics, the normality of the score distribution was tested using the Shapiro‒Wilk test, all of which were nonnormal. Consequently, the Mann‒Whitney U test and Kruskal‒Wallis H test were utilized to analyze the associations between knowledge, attitudes, and barrier scores with demographic variables. The median values of the knowledge score, total score for research attitude, and total score for research barriers were used as cutoff points to categorize participants into groups with high research knowledge, positive research attitudes, and significant research barriers, respectively. Logistic regression models were then applied for multivariate analysis to evaluate which factors were associated with these three aspects.

Results

Descriptive statistics and population characteristics

A total of 546 residents Composed the study sample, of which 47.44% were males, with the largest percentage of 56.4% in the 20–25-year-old group. The sample was recruited from four hospitals and encompassed various training specialties, including internal medicine, surgery, pediatrics, obstetrics and gynecology, general practice, otorhinolaryngology, and medical technology. There were three types of residents: (1) Clinical professional masters integrated academic postgraduate education with clinical residency, awarding dual qualifications; (2) Commissioned resident physicians were institution-sponsored trainees bound by contractual obligations to return to their sponsoring organizations (e.g., rural hospitals) post-training; (3) Socialized residents entered through competitive national/regional matching systems without institutional affiliations or guaranteed employment, maintaining full post-graduation Career autonomy. The socialized residents were the most numerous at 42.12%. Those in their first year of training were 46.52%, 24.52% in their second year, and 28.94% in their third year. The percentage of graduates from project 211 universities was 25.82%. Previous participation in research programs was 13.55%. The complete demographics are shown in Table 1.

Research practices of residents

A total of 103 residents (18.86%) had prior experience in research projects, with 58 (10.62%) having participated in two or more projects. Among them, 82 residents (15.02%) had published articles, and 69 (12.64%) had attended research training. The most frequently engaged research activities were specific execution and data analysis, while the most common types of research projects they participated in were basic research and review writing.

Knowledge regarding research basics among the resident physicians

Attitudes of the residents toward research

The mean score for all residents in the “Positive Research Attitude” dimension was 37.53 ± 7.78, while the mean score in the “Negative Research Attitude” dimension was 5.60 ± 1.90. The “Total Score for Research Attitude” averaged 43.13 ± 7.42. The total score for research attitudes was higher for females (43.72 ± 6.85) than for males (42.48 ± 7.98), and the total score for research attitudes was higher for non- project 211 university graduates (43.78 ± 7.09) than for project 211 university graduates (41.28 ± 8.05). Residents who had previously participated in research projects scored higher (44.93 ± 6.43) in total research attitude than those who had not (42.72 ± 7.58). Third-year residents (41.11 ± 8.15) had a lower research attitude score than first-year (43.95 ± 7.28) and second-year residents (43.96 ± 6.31). Residents aspiring to work in community hospitals in the future had significantly lower research attitude scores than those aiming for other types of hospitals. All these differences were statistically significant (all p < 0.05). Shown in Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5.

Perceived barriers towards research practice

The mean score for all the residents in the “lack of skills/support in research” dimension was 29.64 ± 6.86, while in the “lack of interest/confidence in research” dimension, it was 16.91 ± 4.29. The “Total score for research barriers” averaged 46.56 ± 10.21. The four highest-rated barriers were “lack of time to do research because of clinical tasks”, “lack of good research ideas”, “insufficient research skills” and “lack of familiarity with research studies/statistical analysis”. Residents with undergraduate degrees (47.23 ± 10.26) perceived greater barriers to research than those with postgraduate degrees (45.17 ± 9.99). Additionally, there were significant differences in perceived research barriers across different training hospitals, with residents from the two leading hospitals in the province reporting fewer barriers than those from the other two hospitals. All these differences were statistically significant (all p < 0.05). Shown in Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5.

Through this analysis, the authors focused more on which factors are associated with the ‘low’ knowledge level. With males as the reference group, females presented an OR of 1.679 (95% CI 1.088, 2.589). Older age was associated with lower research knowledge; Compared with the 20–25 years age group, those older than 30 years had an OR of 0.233 (95% CI (0.085, 0.640). Relationship between demographics and research attitudes

Logistic regression analysis was used to estimate the OR and 95% CI for the associations between demographics and research attitudes, as shown in Table 6. Gender, types of resident physicians, years of standardized residency training, highest degree, preferred Hospital for post-training employment, and prior participation in research were found to be Correlated with research attitudes. Through this analysis, the authors focused more on which factors are associated with the positive research attitude. With males as the reference group, females presented an OR of 1.468 (95% CI 1.024, 2.104) for a more positive research attitude. Clinical professional master’s students demonstrated the least favorable research attitude; when they were used as a reference, Commissioned resident physicians and socialized residents had ORs of 1.931 (95% CI 1.106, 3.371) and 1.684 (95% CI 1.045, 2.715), respectively, indicating a more positive attitude toward research. Residents in their first year of training had the most positive research attitude; Compared with those in their second and third years, those in their second and third years had ORs of 0.814 (95% CI 0.488, 1.360) and 0.502 (95% CI 0.299, 0.842), respectively, suggesting a decline in research attitude over time. With a bachelor’s degree as the reference, individuals with a master’s degree had an OR of 0.633 (95% CI 0.406, 0.986). A stronger preference for higher-tier Hospitals for future employment was associated with a more positive research attitude; with tertiary A-level teaching Hospitals as the reference, tertiary hospitals, secondary hospitals, and Community hospitals had ORs of 0.604 (95% CI 0.393, 0.930), 0.564 (95% CI 0.294, 1.082), and 0.449 (95% CI 0.239, 0.843), respectively. Prior participation in research projects was significantly associated with a more positive research attitude; Compared with those who had not participated, those who had participated had an OR of 2.298 (95% CI 1.389, 3.802).

Relationships between demographics and research barriers

The barriers to research were not found to be related to any demographic factors except graduation from a 211 Project university. Barriers to research were found to be greater for residents from non-211 Project universities, with an OR of 0.631 (95% CI 0.413, 0.964) (reference group: 211 Project university graduates).

Discussion

This study analyzed the research knowledge, research participation, attitudes toward research and barriers to research encountered by residents who were trained in the best Hospital in a province in China. The results indicated that the overall level of research knowledge within this sample was moderate, with only 18.86% of residents having previously participated in research projects and even fewer having published articles. There was a high level of agreement that research is useful and has positive attitudes, but the burden of barriers they perceived was high, as evidenced by a lack of time and good research ideas, inadequate research skills and unfamiliarity with research methods/statistical analysis.

Approximately 20% of the residents in this study had previously participated in a research project, 15.02% had published articles, and 12.64% had participated in research training, with project participation sessions generally Consisting of specific implementation and data analysis. Globally, the level of resident involvement in research varies by country. A study of the research output of neurosurgery residents in the United States reported that they authored an average of 11± 14 papers, with the most common types of research including retrospective cohort studies (24%), laboratory/bench studies (19%), and case reports (18%) [16]. This reflects the activity and diversity of neurosurgery residents in research in the United States. Another national survey of ophthalmology residents in Saudi Arabia that the vast majority (96.4%) had participated in at least one research project prior to starting their training, and the main focus was on the conceptual and design phase (72.5%) and the proposal preparation phase (85.9%) [17]. Among undergraduate students in the Country, 33.7% had participated in research projects, with an average of 0.5 papers published per student, and cross-sectional studies were the most common type of research conducted by students (38.6%), followed by case reports (23.9%) [12]. This suggests that in Saudi Arabia, the involvement of residents and medical students in research is not only common but also deep, potentially benefiting from the nation’s high regard for and support of medical research. In Canada, the research participation of pediatric residents mirrors the findings of our study, with approximately 20% of residents having abstracts of their articles accepted at national or international Conferences and 12% of their manuscripts submitted to peer-reviewed journals. The type of research participation was dominated by retrospective clinical studies (22%) [3]. In Romania, a quarter of undergraduate medical students are involved in writing research papers, and the percentage of students involved in research projects is 21% [18]. However, research participation is weaker among medical students in some developing Countries, such as Nagpur and Rajasthan in India. Only 8.4% of students in Nagpur Conducted research projects, and only 5.7% of them published their research [19]. In Rajasthan, only 7.3% of medical students had participated in any previous research project, and of these participants, only 25.9% had published in journals [10]. The majority (93%) of the residents in Oman, India, had a positive attitude toward research, but only 27.8% of the residents had published as first authors or coauthors [20]. These data indicate that in these regions, opportunities and achievements in research among resident physicians and medical students remain limited and are potentially influenced by factors such as resources, the educational environment, or the cultural context.

The mean knowledge score of the sample in this study was 3.25 ± 2.16. Multivariate analysis revealed that gender, age, types of resident physicians, highest degree, preferred Hospital for post-training employment, participation in research before, and medical licensing exam were associated with the level of research knowledge. Females scored higher than males, younger individuals outperformed older individuals, those with graduate degrees scored higher than those with undergraduate degrees, participants with prior research experience scored higher than those without, and those trained in leading Hospitals or with high expectations for their future employment hospital demonstrated better mastery of research knowledge. In studies utilizing the same scale, the mean knowledge score among medical undergraduates in Arab Countries was 2.1 (SD 1.59) [12], whereas the mean score for medical undergraduates in Jordan was 3.76 (SD 1.78) [21]. Similarly, they found differences across gender and grade level. Other studies did not use a scale for assessment but rather used grades on the basis of the number of questions answered Correctly. An Indian study concluded that 74.2% of medical students had poor knowledge of research basics and that only 9.2% were satisfied with their knowledge of research basics [10]. Another study in the Arab region Concluded that approximately 55% of the residents had a low level of knowledge and that 44% had a moderate level of knowledge [20]. Some studies have concluded that residents or medical students have good knowledge of the basics of research [22–24].

In this study, the authors conducted a multidimensional assessment of resident physicians’ attitudes toward research. The mean score of “total research attitude” was 43.13 ± 7.42. The authors found that gender, types of resident physicians, years of standardized residency training, highest degree, preferred hospital for post-training employment, and prior participation in research were correlated with research attitudes. Residents who were female, commissioned resident physician, undergraduate, those in their first year of training, and those with a high level of hospital for post-training employment in the future had more positive attitudes toward research. Consistent with other studies on research attitudes, the majority of resident physicians expressed enthusiasm or interest in research [16, 25] and recognized the importance of research training during residency and undergraduate internships [15, 18]. They believed that research would enhance their critical thinking skills, increase their medical knowledge, prompt them to delve deeper into medical unknowns, apply the latest research findings to clinical practice, and improve their diagnostic and treatment capabilities. However, some studies revealed a more neutral attitude toward research, with over half of the participants holding positive views [19, 26], while others perceived research as an additional burden [21]. For clinical professional master residents, the dual burden of simultaneously experiencing clinical rotation pressures and research requirements essential for degree conferral not only depletes their limited time and energy resources but may also significantly compromise their inherent research interest and intrinsic motivation. This potentially adverse effect could further impede the long-term cultivation and development of their scientific research capabilities. Indeed, the active participation of residents in research activities is essential to their professional development. Research experience not only increases the chances of becoming a physician-scientist but also plays a key role in shaping a well-rounded medical professional.

This study investigated and rated the barriers to research encountered by residents, and the four highest scoring barriers were extensive clinical workload leaving insufficient time, lack of good research ideas, inadequate research skills, and unfamiliarity with research methods/statistical analysis. Multivariate analysis revealed that among the demographic factors, only whether the graduation institution was a Project 211 university was associated with resident physicians’ perceived research barriers. the clinical workload being too high [10, 19, 26, 27], too many other educational activities [27], lack of statistical support in their department [15, 26, 27], lack of protected time for resident research/scholarly activity [27–29], lack of research method training [10, 21, 25, 29], insufficient financial resources [10, 15, 19, 21, 26, 28, 29], lack of time for faculty to provide guidance, barriers posed by the COVID-19 pandemic [30], and lack of sufficient research opportunities [21]. It is evident that despite the benefits of research, many residents face significant challenges that may adversely affect the quantity and quality of research outputs, thereby impacting the broader field of medicine. The barriers faced by residents need to be addressed if they are to improve their research capacity, such as:

Protected research time: establishing research time free from clinical responsibilities during medical education and residency training, which is crucial for this group to fully dedicate themselves to research work without the burden of clinical tasks and is essential for generating research outcomes and academic development.

Stratified training programs: This resource-efficient approach maximizes research capacity through five key components:1) Competency assessment: Baseline evaluations using standardized tests, mentor feedback, and portfolio reviews to objectively map skill levels. 2) Tailored training programs: Advanced learners receive training in complex methodologies (multivariable modeling, trial design), while foundational trainees focus on core competencies (literature critique, basic statistics). 3) Strategic resource allocation: Targeted investment in residents demonstrating both research aptitude (measured competence) and sustained engagement (self-identified interest), ensuring optimal use of limited resources. 4) Collaborative learning systems: Cross-tier mentorship programs pairing novices with advanced peers, supplemented by faculty-led workshops to foster knowledge exchange. 5) Dynamic program optimization: Continuous outcome monitoring through trainee assessments and feedback loops to iteratively refine training content and delivery.

This stratified model addresses the dual challenges of heterogeneous skill levels and constrained resources while maintaining scientific rigor. By decoupling enthusiasm from capability assessments, it ensures equitable access while recognizing that sustained research productivity requires both inherent aptitude and cultivated motivation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the overall response to the survey was positive, the overall response rate was relatively low at 38%. The responses to the survey may have been biased toward individuals who are more interested in research, potentially leading to sample bias. Second, participation in research activities was self-reported, and the number of published articles and research projects involved was not verified, thus creating potential recall/reporting bias. Third, the responses were limited to a Likert scale format; therefore, qualitative research on this topic could have revealed more about the respondents’ views and attitudes. Survey participants may have tended to give positive responses to the questions (default bias); however, the responses were anonymous, which reduced the risk of participants giving answers they thought the researchers wanted. Fourth, the participants were offered a small incentive, and a low time commitment was required to complete the questionnaire, which may also have introduced some bias.

Conclusion

This study focused on investigating the research knowledge, attitudes toward research, and barriers to research among resident physicians in tertiary-level teaching hospitals in a province of China, including the three leading three hospitals in the province. The findings revealed that resident physicians in these high-level hospitals in China had a moderate level of research knowledge. Despite their positive attitudes toward research, they faced challenges such as lack of time, guidance, and skills in their research practices. It was also found that different graduation universities, education levels, and future expectations of their employment had an impact on research ability and needs. Therefore, ensuring dedicated research time and providing training opportunities could help reduce the barriers faced by resident physicians, and stratifying training according to ability and needs could better increase their productivity when resources were effective.
