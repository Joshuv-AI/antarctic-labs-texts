Enhancing Community‐Based Nursing Decision Support: Machine Learning Models for Diabetes Risk Prediction Using Home Health Nursing Notes
Authors: Doyeon LIM, Aeri KIM, Hana LEE, Kyungmi WOO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aimed to identify high‐risk factors for type 2 diabetes and develop a machine learning (ML)–based diabetes prediction model using nursing notes from home health care.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SampleA total 7896 medical records from 1747 patients aged ≥ 20 years who received home health care at a university hospital in South Korea over 10 years.MeasurementsThe patient's sociodemographic characteristics, diagnosis codes, and clinical history as well as narrative nursing notes detailing nursing assessments and descriptions of the patient's condition were recorded. Logistic regression was used to identify risk factors, and five machine learning models were constructed using 10‐fold cross‐validation.ResultsFemale sex, depression, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, nursing services such as dressing and nutritional care, and dysuria were identified as prediabetes‐related symptoms extracted from nursing notes using natural language processing (NLP). The Random Forest model demonstrated the highest predictive performance (AUC = 0.985).ConclusionsNursing documentation is a valuable resource for early diabetes risk screening. High predictive performance was achieved by integrating structured and unstructured data, prediabetes‐related symptoms extracted from nursing notes. This approach enhances nurses’ ability to deliver timely, personalized interventions and improve preventive care outcomes, with significant benefits for patients receiving home care who require continuous support.

Background

The global incidence of diabetes reached approximately 537 million people (10.5% of the population) in 2021 and is expected to rise to 643 million by 2030 (Ogurtsova et al. 2022). The economic burden of diabetes is also substantial, with global medical expenditures increasing from USD 232 billion in 2007 to USD 966 billion in 2021 (Ogurtsova et al. 2022). Diabetes leads to several health complications, including vascular disease, retinopathy, neuropathy, heart disease, and stroke, and may ultimately result in death (Gregg et al. 2016). Early recognition and diagnosis of prediabetic signs and symptoms are essential for preventing the progression of diabetes and reducing the risk of complications. Lifestyle interventions—considered the cornerstone of prediabetes management—are cost‐effective and clinically beneficial (Watson 2017).

However, implementing early detection in practice remains challenging, particularly in community and home‐based care settings. These challenges result from the decentralized nature of care delivery, along with limitations such as inadequate home infrastructure, limited physical space, and other environmental constraints (Shahrestanaki et al. 2023). Home health care nurses often work in these environments and are positioned to detect subtle signs of disease progression, but lack of decision‐support tools hampers their capacity to intervene early.

In home healthcare, nurses play a pivotal role by synthesizing clinical information and environmental cues to detect early signs of deterioration and initiate timely interventions (Gray et al. 2018). Notably, they serve as the primary healthcare providers in the home care setting, where nursing notes constitute the most substantial portion of electronic health records (EHRs) (Hobensack et al. 2023). These notes are a valuable source of unstructured data, providing not only patient information but also detailed insights into treatment responses and nursing assessments. Leveraging this data through ML has the potential to significantly enhance the accuracy and clinical relevance of predictive models in real‐world nursing practice (Huang et al. 2021).

Recently, machine learning (ML) methods have gained attention for their potential in disease prediction and early detection (Lai et al. 2019; Tate et al. 2020). ML can analyze large, complex datasets and identify patterns that traditional statistical methods may miss, driving its use in clinical research (Shouval et al. 2014). However, existing models rely heavily on structured data—such as age, sex, and clinical measurements (Lai et al. 2019; Tate et al. 2020)—while overlooking the rich, narrative insights found in nursing documentation. Unstructured data—such as free‐text nursing notes— comprise approximately 80% of all medical data yet remain largely underutilized in predictive modeling (Kong 2019).

Natural language processing (NLP), a subfield of computer science and artificial intelligence, encompasses a range of computational techniques designed to analyze and represent human language (Chowdhary 2020). When applied to nursing notes, NLP can extract critical information—such as length of stay or risk of mortality—thereby adding valuable context to predictive analytics in healthcare (Gray et al. 2018). As the demand for home care services grows, Hobensack et al. highlighted the potential of combining NLP and ML to support clinical decision‐making in home healthcare (Hobensack et al. 2023). Integrating narrative data extracted through NLP with structured clinical variables significantly enhances predictive performance (Song et al. 2021).

Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of ML in predicting clinical events or healthcare behaviors in public health contexts (Gaikwad et al. 2025; Devi et al. 2024). However, few have integrated narrative nursing documentation into predictive models—particularly in home health settings where nurses serve as the primary care providers.

Research Questions

Despite these advancements, to the best of our knowledge, no prior study has developed or validated an ML‐based diabetes prediction model using home health nursing data, particularly incorporating unstructured free‐text nursing notes written in Korean. Therefore, we aimed to: (1) identify factors associated with the onset of diabetes; (2) develop multiple ML algorithms to predict diabetes based on sociodemographic characteristics, prediabetes‐related symptoms, psychological factors, nursing service variables, and clinical indicators; and (3) evaluate and compare the predictive performance of these algorithms using home health nursing data to determine the most effective model. These findings may inform the development of practical tools that support community health nurses in identifying high‐risk individuals, facilitating early intervention, and improving population‐level diabetes prevention efforts.

Methodology

Study Design and Description Data

This retrospective cohort study analyzed EHRs from patients who received home healthcare provided by a national hospital in South Korea. This dataset reflects the real‐world documentation practices of community health nurses, who play a critical role in identifying early health risks during home visits. Each care episode included the patient's sociodemographic characteristics, diagnosis codes, and clinical history. Additionally, the records contained 200–450 words of narrative nursing notes, which detailed nursing assessments and included descriptions of the patient's condition, as reported by both the patients and caregivers during home visits.

Between January 1, 2011, and March 31, 2021, a total of 29,888 home healthcare visits were recorded for patients aged 20 or older. However, multiple visits for the same condition resulting in duplicate records with similar variables, primarily due to routine services, such as wound dressing and laboratory sampling, were excluded to minimize data redundancy, which could compromise the performance of ML models (Zhou et al. 2017). The final dataset that we analyzed included 7896 visits from 1747 unique patients. The data preprocessing procedure is illustrated in Figure 1.

Outcome Variable

The outcome variable in this study was dichotomous, indicating whether each visit included a diabetes diagnosis, either as a primary or secondary condition. To systematically define patients with diabetes, we applied the Korea Standard Classification of Disease (8th revision), which aligns with the International Classification of Diseases. Episodes were classified as diabetes‐related if they included diagnostic codes from E10.x to E14.x. We also incorporated codes for diabetes‐related complications—such as G63.2 (polyneuropathy in diabetes mellitus) and H36.0 (diabetic retinopathy). A complete list of all diagnostic codes used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Predictor Selection

We adapted the research framework originally proposed by Black (Black 2002) to identify diabetes risk factors and tailored it to suit home health nursing data. Black's framework includes a broad range of variables, such as genetic susceptibility, sociodemographic characteristics, and behavioral factors. However, since our dataset did not include genetic susceptibility and behavioral information, we excluded these components and incorporated predictors specific to home health nursing records, such as prediabetes‐related symptoms and the types of nursing services provided. The final set of 26 variables was selected based on the modified version of Black's framework (Black 2002) and is illustrated in Figure 2.

Modified research framework adopted from Black (Black 2002).

Previous studies have underscored the essential role of home health nurses in recognizing early signs of clinical deterioration, especially among individuals transitioning back into the community (Jeon et al. 2023). In this context, the early recognition of prediabetes symptoms within home healthcare settings becomes particularly important (Jeon et al. 2023). Based on the findings of Kim et al. (2024), the five most common symptoms associated with prediabetes: constipation, abdominal pain, nausea, dysuria, and weakness, we incorporated these prediabetes‐related symptoms as predictor variables in our model. Furthermore, considering the established association between prediabetes and stomach, pancreatic, and lung cancers (Kim et al. 2024), we added a composite cancer variable encompassing these three types.

Age was categorized into five groups based on the criteria set by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC): young adults (20–44 years), middle‐aged adults (45–64 years), youngest‐old adults (65–74 years), middle‐old adults (75–84 years), and oldest‐old adults (≥ 85 years) (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2022). Family members, whether biological or chosen, play a crucial role in patient care through continuous interactions (Denham et al. 2011). The primary caregiver variable was categorized as “yes” or “no,” depending on whether patients received care from family members, such as spouses, children, siblings, or parents. Similarly, the long‐term care insurance (LTCI) variable was classified as “yes” or “no,” based on its presence or absence. LTCI provides a comprehensive package of home‐ and facility‐based care for older adults requiring assistance with daily activities in Korea (Kim and Kwon 2020). Hospitalization history was recorded as “yes” if the patient had been admitted to a hospital within the year preceding their receipt of home health nursing services. The length of the hospital stay was determined by the number of days between the admission and discharge dates. To evaluate comorbid conditions, we used the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), a widely accepted measure of clinical severity (Charlson et al. 1987). The CCI includes 17 conditions, such as myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, and peripheral vascular disease, with each condition assigned a specific weight based on its relative risk. For each episode, the CCI score was calculated using the primary and secondary diagnoses in the dataset (Quan et al. 2005). Detailed definitions of each variable and the corresponding diagnostic codes are available in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to examine the characteristics of each episode. To compare episodes of patients with and without diabetes, we used a chi‐square test for categorical variables and an independent t‐test for continuous variables. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess the relationship between each independent variable and the risk of diabetes. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated from the multiple regression models. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2), with a p‐value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Data Pre‐Processing

Since patients with diabetes accounted for only 7.9% of the total episodes, the class imbalance could potentially lead to the underrepresentation or misclassification of the minority class, negatively affecting the predictive performance of the classification algorithm (Thabtah et al. 2020). To address this issue, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over‐sampling Technique (SMOTE), which generates synthetic minority class samples based on the nearest neighbors rather than simply duplicating existing data (Thabtah et al. 2020).

Prediabetes symptoms were extracted from free‐text nursing notes using a rule‐based NLP algorithm previously developed by Jeon et al. (2023) for the same dataset. This algorithm used bilingual (Korean‐English) prediabetes‐related vocabulary. No missing values were present in any episode.

Development of the ML Model

Five different models were constructed to examine the prevalence of diabetes using supervised ML algorithms in Weka version 3.8.5: Naive Bayes (“NaiveBayes” in Weka), Decision Tree (“J48” in Weka), Logistic Regression (“Logistic” in Weka), Random Forest (“RandomForest” in Weka), and MultiLayer Perceptron (“MultilayerPerceptron” in Weka). Weka is an open‐source software that supports visualization and data preparation, enabling the implementation of various ML algorithms (Hall et al. 2022). The performance of these algorithms was evaluated using 10‐fold cross‐validation, a widely recognized method known for its relative accuracy in classification and regression models (Gunasegaran and Cheah 2017). The process of building an ML model to predict diabetes incidence is illustrated in Figure 1.

Evaluation Methods

We evaluated the performance of each ML prediction model using the following measures: accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). These metrics allow for evaluating model performance in a way that reflects the practical needs of community‐based home health nursing.

In this study, sensitivity (also known as recall) refers to the proportion of actual diabetic episodes correctly identified, while specificity represents the proportion of nondiabetic episodes accurately predicted. PPV, or precision, indicates the proportion of predicted diabetic episodes that are truly diabetic. NPV reflects the proportion of predicted nondiabetic episodes that are truly nondiabetic. The ROC curve plots sensitivity against 1‐specificity for different cutoff values, with AUC serving as a reliable metric for overall diagnostic accuracy (Mandrekar 2010). The F1 score, which balances both precision and recall, provides a comprehensive measure of the model's predictive performance (Dong et al. 2020). (1) Accuracy=TP+TNTP+FP+TN+FN (2) SensitivityRecall=TPTP+FN (3) Specificity=TNTN+FP (4) PPVPrecision=TPTP+FP (5) NPV=TNTN+FN (6) F1score=2×Precision×RecallPrecision+Recall

Ethics Statement

Data analysis and research were conducted with the approval of the institutional review board (IRB) of the medical center involved in the study (IRB no.: H‐2104‐149‐1213). Since the study used secondary, anonymized medical records, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

The results revealed statistically significant differences between diabetic and nondiabetic episodes across various factors, including sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, sex, informal family caregivers, the presence of LTCI, and the types of home health nursing services provided, such as physical assessment, foley catheter or cystostomy care, dressing changes, medication management, respiratory care, and laboratory sampling. All five prediabetes‐related symptoms (constipation, abdominal pain, nausea, dysuria, and weakness) showed statistically significant differences between the two groups. Psychological factors, such as depression, and clinical factors—including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, physical disability, CCI, and the length of hospital stay—also exhibited significant variations.

The average age of patients with diabetic episodes was 84.7 years, which was higher than that of patients without diabetic episodes (74.5 years). Although the sex ratio was similar in nondiabetic episodes, 386 episodes (61.7%) were associated with female patients in the diabetic group, which was approximately 1.6 times higher than that of men's episodes. In the diabetic episodes, primary caregivers were predominantly family members (56.4%).

Weakness was the most common prediabetes‐related symptom in both diabetic and nondiabetic episodes. Episodes of weakness and dysuria accounted for 12.5% and 11.0% of patients with diabetes, respectively, approximately twice as high as the other three prediabetes‐related symptoms: constipation (5.3%), abdominal pain (2.4%), and nausea (3.8%). This suggests that weakness and dysuria may be key indicators of diabetes risk in home healthcare settings.

Laboratory sampling, respiratory care, and dressing were the three most commonly provided nursing services in both groups. Notably, dressing services were provided more often in diabetic episodes than in nondiabetic ones (60.7% vs. 32.9%).

The proportions of episodes with hypertension (18.2% vs. 4.8%), hyperlipidemia (14.9% vs. 1.8%), and depression (5.1% vs. 2.5%) were statistically higher in diabetic episodes than in nondiabetic episodes. The average CCI score was 2.14 for diabetic episodes and 1.42 for nondiabetic episodes, indicating that the clinical severity was higher in patients with diabetes than in those without diabetes.

After adjusting for potential confounders, several factors were significantly associated with the presence of diabetes. Female patients had a 1.35 times higher risk of developing diabetes as compared to male patients (OR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.11–1.64). The presence of dysuria, depression, and hypertension was also more likely in diabetic episodes than in nondiabetic episodes (OR = 1.51, 95% CI = 1.12–2.02; OR = 2.06, 95% CI = 1.34–3.17; and OR = 2.05, 95% CI = 1.54–2.73, respectively). Notably, patients with hyperlipidemia had a significantly higher likelihood of experiencing diabetes (OR = 5.09, 95% CI = 3.63–7.15) than those without hyperlipidemia.

Certain nursing services during home health visits, such as dressing services, were 1.78‐fold higher, and nutritional care services were 1.46‐fold higher among diabetic episodes (OR = 1.78, 95% CI = 1.46–2.16; and OR = 1.46, 95% CI = 1.13–1.87, respectively). In contrast, respiratory care and medication management exhibited lower ORs of diabetic episodes (OR = 0.47, 95% CI = 0.38–0.60, and OR = 0.36, 95% CI = 0.26–0.50, respectively), suggesting a weaker correlation with diabetes. The CCI score demonstrated a significant positive association with diabetic episodes, with each one‐point increase in the CCI score corresponding to a 1.38‐fold higher likelihood of diabetic episodes. (OR = 1.38, 95% CI = 1.31–1.45).

Ranking of the Features

To identify the most informative predictors prior to ML modeling, the ‘InfoGainAttributeEval’ method in Weka 3.8.6 was employed. This method calculates the entropy, or information gain, for each predictor of diabetes, yielding a value ranging from 0 (no information) to 1 (maximum information) (Hussain et al. 2018). Features with higher entropy values contribute more significantly to diabetes prediction. The top 10 most informative variables are presented in Figure 4. Among them, the CCI score had the highest information gain (0.199), followed by age (0.104), dressing (0.024), and respiratory care (0.017), suggesting that clinical severity and age were the most influential predictors of diabetes among home healthcare patients.

Comparison With the Performance of ML Algorithms

Table 3 presents the detailed results of each ML algorithm. Among the five models, the Random Forest algorithm achieved the highest accuracy, with an AUC of 0.985 for predicting diabetes onset. This was followed by the decision tree (AUC = 0.934), multi‐layer perceptron (AUC = 0.886), and logistic regression (AUC = 0.828). The Multi‐Layer Perceptron method employed a single hidden layer, a commonly used architecture in this type of analysis. In the decision tree model, the presence of hyperlipidemia was selected as the root node, indicating that it was the most influential predictor within that model. In contrast, the ‘InfoGainAttributeEval’ method in Weka identified the CCI score as the most informative variable for predicting diabetes.

Discussion

Most home healthcare patients are older adults with multiple comorbidities and varying degrees of limitations in activities of daily living, placing them among the most vulnerable populations in the community (Algera et al. 2004). The present study identified these characteristics as significant contributors to diabetes onset. Given these findings, nurses should closely monitor patients for early signs of diabetes onset and progression during home visits, as early detection can facilitate timely and appropriate interventions.

To explore these factors, we analyzed home health nursing data to compare episodes in patients with and without diabetes. We also examined the risk factors for diabetes onset and developed five ML models to predict diabetes risk. By applying these predictive algorithms for early screening, disease progression can be prevented, enabling more timely and targeted nursing interventions.

Multivariable logistic regression identified several potential risk factors for diabetes development. Women were more likely to have diabetes, which aligns with previous studies reporting a higher prevalence of diabetes among women aged 65 years or older (Bae et al. 2022). However, the findings regarding the impact of sex on diabetes development are mixed. One study suggested that brown adipose tissue, which helps reduce insulin resistance, is more active in women, potentially lowering the risk of diabetes. In contrast, another study found that factors specific to women, such as pregnancy and a history of gestational diabetes, may increase the risk of diabetes (Kautzky‐Willer et al. 2016; Kautzky‐Willer, Leutner, and Harreiter 2023). Therefore, the relationship between sex and diabetes development is complex, and further research is required to clarify the underlying mechanisms and better understand the differences in diabetes prevalence between sexes.

Aging, hypertension, depression, and hyperlipidemia were found to significantly increase the risk of diabetes. Aging reduces insulin sensitivity and beta‐cell production capacity (Suastika et al. 2012), while high blood pressure contributes to microvascular dysfunction, which plays a key role in the pathophysiology of diabetes (Kim et al. 2015). Depression leads to physiological changes in cortisol levels, which, in turn, reduce insulin sensitivity and insulin secretion in beta cells (Tabák et al. 2014). Hyperlipidemia is associated with higher fasting plasma glucose and 2‐hour plasma glucose levels, driven by elevated serum triglyceride and total cholesterol levels (Chen et al. 2015). In our study, hyperlipidemia demonstrated the strongest association with diabetes (OR = 5.09), was ranked highest in ‘InfoGainAttributeEval,’ and was selected as the root node in the decision tree model. These findings highlight the importance of recognizing hyperlipidemia as a significant risk factor for diabetes onset and incorporating it into the monitoring and preventive care strategies.

Each one‐point increase in the CCI score resulted in the increased odds of diabetic episodes in our study. This may be due to the fact that diabetes is a known risk factor for conditions such as cancer, heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease (Leon and Maddox 2015; Maida et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2022), all of which are factored into the CCI calculation.

Among the prediabetes‐related symptoms, dysuria showed a strong association with diabetes progression. A previous study identified the mechanism by which the inflammatory response, which can cause dysuria, leads to insulin resistance (de Luca and Olefsky 2007). This link highlights the clinical importance of inflammatory symptoms, which are often overlooked in conventional screening. Unlike the classical triad of polyuria, polydipsia, and polyphagia (Balaji, Duraisamy, and Kumar, Balaji et al. 2019), dysuria may serve as an early and subtle indicator of diabetes onset, particularly in high‐acuity home healthcare patients with multiple comorbidities.

Our study found that providing simple or pressure ulcer dressings by nurses was strongly associated with diabetes. This may be due to the fact that diabetes causes wounds and vascular dysfunction, and neuropathy limits oxygen supply (Bai et al. 2020), slowing the healing process. As a result, dressings were performed more frequently for patients with diabetes. Consistent with the findings of a previous study, our results identified nutritional care as a risk factor for diabetes development. A previous study reported that tube feeding and total parenteral nutrition administration made it challenging to control glucose metabolism, often leading to hyperglycemia (Donner and Flammer 2008). In home parenteral nutrition, hyperglycemia is common, making close blood glucose monitoring essential during nutritional care provided by nurses (Edakkanambeth Varayil et al. 2017). These findings suggest that specific nursing interventions—particularly wound and nutritional care—may serve as early clinical indicators of disease progression, reinforcing the importance of nurse‐led surveillance in home healthcare.

When respiratory care was provided, patients were less likely to have episodes of diabetes. This finding aligns with a previous study reporting that glycosylated hemoglobin levels were lower in patients who received continuous positive airway pressure (Hassaballa et al. 2005).

Among the five ML models, Random Forest demonstrated the best predictive performance, followed by the decision tree. The evaluation was based on AUC, a commonly used metric in the initial assessment of new diagnostic tests (Mandrekar 2010). This is consistent with a previous study, which found that the predictive power of Random Forest outperformed single learning models such as Decision Tree and Logistic Regression (AlKaabi et al. 2020). The improved performance of Random Forest is attributed to its ability to weigh the relative importance of each class through a downsampling procedure, selecting a balanced subsample of positive and negative cases. This procedure was particularly useful in our study, where the event of diabetes onset was less than 10% (Muchlinski et al. 2016).

Additionally, Random Forest enhances predictive performance and consistency in classification tasks, demonstrating robustness to noise and the ability to effectively handle non‐linear relationships. These characteristics contribute to its overall superior performance. However, the Random Forest is a more complex black‐box model than the decision tree, which may limit its interpretability (Shaikhina et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2020). Despite this, in home healthcare settings where various patient factors and nursing interventions interact, the model's ability to identify complex, non‐linear patterns is especially valuable. This suggests that Random Forest could serve as a practical tool for home health nurses to identify patients more accurately and precisely at high risk for diabetes.

Furthermore, the Random Forest model achieved an AUC of 0.985, surpassing the AUC values reported in previous diabetes prediction studies using Random Forest (0.834 and 0.872) (Lai et al. 2019; Talebi Moghaddam et al. 2024). In addition to AUC, minimizing false negatives (i.e., failing to identify actual patients) is crucial in medical datasets, making sensitivity an important evaluation metric (Thabtah et al. 2020). The Random Forest model demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.790, which was higher than that reported by Lai et al. (2019). Furthermore, the model achieved an F1‐score of 0.850, indicating a well‐balanced performance between sensitivity and PPV. These findings suggest that the proposed model has the potential to become a reliable decision‐support tool in clinical nursing practice. Beyond diabetes prediction, other studies have also used Random Forest to predict hypertension and mental health problems, with AUC values of 0.869 and 0.739, respectively, both of which were also lower than that of our model (Tate et al. 2020; AlKaabi et al. 2020). The better AUC value in our study can be attributed to the fact that previous studies relied solely on sociodemographic characteristics and biochemical data, such as region, sex, age, and laboratory test results. In contrast, our study also incorporated diabetes‐related symptoms extracted from nursing notes using NLP as predictors.

A prior study demonstrated the advantage of incorporating NLP‐embedded features from computed tomography scan reports into a logistic regression model, demonstrating a 25% and 18% improvement in the AUC for predicting 30‐day readmission and complication rates following ovarian cancer surgery, respectively. This approach outperformed models that solely relied on age, laboratory results, CA125, and comorbidity as predictors (Barber et al. 2020). Similarly, features derived from NLP‐applied nursing notes have shown significant potential in predicting the early detection of patient emergencies (Song et al. 2021).

In addition to demographic and clinical factors, nursing services during home healthcare and specific symptoms experienced by patients also influenced the development of diabetes in the present study. To address the limitation of relying solely on quantified and coded variables when building ML models from EHRs (Song et al. 2021), we included the top five prediabetic symptoms extracted through NLP in a previous study (Kim et al. 2024) as noncoded variables as predictors.

Our study successfully developed an ML model with higher predictive power than did the previous studies, further emphasizing the valuable contribution of clinically relevant data from nursing notes in predicting patient prognosis (Song et al. 2021). This model could assist home health nurses in detecting early signs of diabetes and initiating preventive care, leading to improved patient outcomes.

Despite its contributions, this study has some limitations. First, since only home health nursing data from a university hospital in Seoul, Korea, was analyzed, the findings cannot be generalized. Second, this study could not take account of other potential predictors influencing diabetes development, such as family history, daily activities, dietary habits, essential biochemical data, and genetic information. To better account for the diverse risk factors contributing to diabetes, future research should integrate additional variables, such as laboratory results from EHRs, into the ML model. Third, to address class imbalances in ML, we applied an SMOTE filter. While this method improves the algorithm's performance, it is important to consider the potential risk of overfitting (Meng and Li 2022). Therefore, a cautious interpretation of the results is necessary, and validation through further research is recommended.

Nevertheless, this study successfully integrated both structured clinical data and unstructured narrative nursing documentation, extracted using NLP, to develop an ML model with enhanced predictive performance. This novel approach offers home health nurses with an effective tool for detecting early signs of chronic illness more effectively. It supports clinical decision‐making and aligns with the growing demand for data‐driven, patient‐centered care in an aging society. Future research should validate this model across diverse home healthcare settings to enhance its generalizability and practical applicability. Moreover, the proposed model and methodology are expected to be transferable to other countries with comparable home healthcare systems and electronic health record infrastructures.

Conclusion

This study utilized home health nursing data to identify key predictors of diabetes onset and to develop a ML model with high predictive performance. By integrating structured clinical variables with unstructured information extracted from nursing notes using natural language processing substantially improved the prediction accuracy of the model. It offers a practical decision‐support tool tailored to real‐world community health care settings. By enabling early identification of high‐risk individuals, the model strengthens clinical judgment and reinforces the essential role of home health nurses in proactive disease prevention and chronic condition management at the community level. Integrating predictive analytics into routine home nursing practice may support timely interventions, reduce preventable hospitalizations, and alleviate long‐term burdens on health systems. This approach aligns with national public health priorities for aging populations and presents scalable opportunities for data‐driven, person‐centered nursing care.
