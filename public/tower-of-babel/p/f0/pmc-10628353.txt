Two pills and women’s health in Japan
Authors: Aya Goto, Kenichi Satoh, Michael R. Reich
Published: 2023

On March 24, 2023, the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare (MHLW) postponed for a second time its meeting to discuss the abortion pill package (MEFEEGO Pack), due to incomplete analysis of public comments. The product was submitted by Linepharma International on December 22, 2021, and a January 2023 meeting approved production and marketing. In February 2023, the MHLW collected public comments and then conducted a simple analysis. Their report showed only a simple counting of the number of supporters, opponents, and others, and provided a simple response of one sentence each to brief summaries of 17 selected comments without any explanation of how these were selected and defined as the “main” opinions.1 On April 28, 2023, the medical abortion pill package was approved—with the condition that “hospitalization or outpatient observation at a facility with beds is required” until a post-marketing surveillance system is established.

Here we present our analysis of the MHLW’s collected public opinions on medical abortion pills and suggest ways to improve decision-making related to women’s health in Japan. We analyzed the text of all publicly available and electronically entered opinions (n = 9375) about the medical abortion pill package that were collected in February 2023 and made public on the MHLW website in May 2023.1 Classification of supporters, opponents, and others followed the MHLW’s categorization. The data were analyzed with the R package “quanteda” in combination with “nmfkcreg” (published by the second author in GitHub) for topic modeling based on NMF (Non-negative Matrix Factorization).2 The method performs both regression and soft-clustering for crosstabulation of the three positions (supporters, opponents, and others) and words used. The response variables are frequencies of words in each position, and optimized explanatory variables are interpreted as two major topics, with a coefficient of determination of 0.99998, indicating a sufficient fit.

The number of supporters in our analyzed data was 7816 (83.4%) and that of opponents was 1504 (16.0%), leaving 55 (0.6%) as others (not specifying a position). As shown in Fig. 1, the top 5 words that appear frequently in Topic 1 were “price,” “spouse,” “consent,” “agree,” and “abortion method” and those in Topic 2 were “disagree,” “fetus,” “dangerous,” “society,” and “reason.” The probability of Topic 1 and 2 was 0.964 and 0.036 in the supporter group, 0.000 and 1.000 in the opponent group, and 0.790 and 0.210 in the others group. The World Health Organization (WHO) includes medical abortion in its Model List of Essential Medicines and recommends avoiding curettage,3 which is used for one-fourth of early abortions in Japan.4 The Phase III study of medical abortion pills in Japan reported a high success rate (93.3%) and a low frequency of bleeding as a severe complication (0.8%).5 In addition, a study in the Netherlands reported a higher satisfaction (answering “very much”) among women choosing medical abortion (45%) compared to vacuum aspiration (31%).6

Japan’s MHLW acted appropriately in deciding to collect public opinions on the medical abortion product, but the decision was made late in the process (over one year after the submission), the analysis was simplistic (just counting the number of submissions according to the position of support, opposition, or other), and the counting was apparently time-consuming (to the extent that the March 24 meeting was postponed on the day it was scheduled).

The collection of public opinions about women’s health products in Japan should not be ad hoc or an after-thought just before approval. The collection should start immediately after the application of the product, especially when the approval is lagging behind many other countries and expected to draw high interest among the public, and the analysis of opinions should be incorporated fully into the decision-making process. Collected data should be analyzed with appropriate methods, such as topic modeling, which is gaining attention for analyzing policy-related textual data and grasping key patterns of responses and trends.7,8 Our presented analysis, for example, took only a short time to conduct and it can be repeated easily and quickly with additional opinions. If the Japanese authorities had used an appropriate combination of public participation and text-data analysis, the approval of medical abortion could have occurred earlier and could have avoided a last-minute delay of a milestone meeting.

Japan’s MHLW also made public, a file containing handwritten or printed opinions that were mostly from opponents; we did not include these opinions in our analysis. These non-electronically submitted opinions could, however, be included in an analysis if a decision is taken early in the review process to convert them into text data.

This extra file of non-electronic opinions included a letter from a regional council addressed to the national cabinet opposing the approval for the following reasons: death cases and severe complications reported in the United States, and the possibility that approval in Japan could accelerate sexual violence against women and a loss of respect to life among the younger generation. This letter, however, was dated February 10, 2023, so it could not be the reason for the abrupt meeting cancelation, but it does illustrate the underlying complexity of the political aspects of discussions about women’s reproductive health products in Japan.

A similarly contorted decision-making process was observed more than two decades ago, when oral contraceptives (OCs) were approved in Japan after a multi-year review process.9 MHLW stated in late 1997 that it would approve low-dose OCs, but then decided to conduct an open forum and delayed the process further due to concerns about possible health risks and potential environmental pollution. In early 1999, news media reported that OCs might be approved soon, but the decision was again postponed to prepare prescription guidelines. Finally, on June 16, 1999, the MHLW approved OCs for contraceptive use. In both cases, the review process was prolonged and involved a tentative approval followed by added discussion calling for public opinions and an additional time to review prescription procedures.

When we wrote about Japan’s OC approval in 1999,9 we recommended that the government collect data and conduct analysis on the state of women’s health in Japan. Unfortunately, this recommendation still remains valid today, 24 years later. For the future, we recommend that the Japanese government collect public opinions early in the decision-making process related to reproductive health products and that the materials be analyzed with qualitative data analysis techniques. In this way, public opinions can be effectively incorporated into health policymaking in a timely manner, as is appropriate for good quality public participation.
