Catalytic Promiscuity and Cascade Reaction Mechanism of Ketosteroid Isomerases for Addition Reactions of Cyclic Enones
Authors: Xuemei Li, Mengsha Li, Yangyang Chen, Weidong Liu, Yiyin Liu, Rui Zhang, Chuanfang Wu, Xiang Sheng, Jinku Bao, Jinhui Feng, Qiaqing Wu, Dunming Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSteroidal medicines are a vital class of pharmaceuticals, with their active ingredients primarily derived from natural steroids. A key step in their biosynthesis involves the isomerization of ∆5−3-ketosteroids to ∆4−3-ketosteroids, catalyzed by ketosteroid isomerase (KSI). In this study, we discover that KSI exhibits an unexpected catalytic promiscuity, efficiently converting cyclic enones into homo- and hetero-addition products. Crystal structures of KSI variants and mutagenesis reveal the homo-dimeric interface as another active center. Molecular dynamics simulations and quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics calculations suggest a cascade catalytic mechanism by a single enzyme, in which isomerization occurs in the monomeric pocket, while the addition reaction preferentially occurs at the homo-dimeric interface due to reduced steric hindrance. These results not only provide valuable insights into the catalytic diversity of KSIs, but also inspire further exploration of new-to-nature and diverse catalytic properties of enzymes, thus expanding their applications in biocatalysis and drug development.Subject terms: Enzyme mechanisms, Biocatalysis, X-ray crystallography, Isomerases

Introduction

Steroidal drugs represent a class of important medicines second to antibiotics in the global pharmaceutical market. There are fifteen steroidal medicines with over 15 billion annual sales in the top 200 small molecule pharmaceuticals of 20241. The steroidal active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) are mainly produced from natural steroids. In recent years, the integration of steroidal biotransformations and chemical reactions into the synthetic routes of steroidal APIs has attracted widespread attentions. In particular, the efficient microbial transformations of phytosterols into key steroid intermediates2–16, such as 4-androstene-3,17-dione (AD), androst-1,4-diene-3,17-dione (ADD), 9α-hydroxy-androsta-4-ene-3.17-dione (9α-OH-AD), hydroxy-23,24-bisnorchol-4-ene-3-one (4-HBC) and sitolactone (HIL), have been commercialized, and phytosterols have gradually replaced sapogenins as the major starting materials in steroidal pharmaceutical industry. In these biotransformation processes, there is a pivotal step of isomerization of 5,6 double bonds of ∆5-3-ketosteroids to the 4,5 position via ketosteroid isomerase (KSI) after oxidation of the 3-OH group to a carbonyl group (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1)17,18. The KSI-catalyzed isomerization of ∆5-3-ketosteroids also plays important roles in metabolism and functions of steroids in the biological systems19–25.

The ketosteroid isomerase is a homo-dimeric enzyme26–31, which is stabilized by the hydrophobic and polar interactions among interfacial amino acid residues. The KSI-catalyzed isomerization reaction follows a two-step conventional acid-base mechanism, that often involves catalytic dipolarity or hydrogen bond cooperation. Despite sharing only 34% homology32, KSICt from Comamonas testosterone (Protein ID: WP_003078309) and KSIPp from Pseudomonas putida (Protein ID: P07445.1) have similar active sites and reaction mechanisms24,33–38. Taking KSIPp as an example, the active center is a deep hydrophobic region including the key catalytic residues Y16, D40 and D103 in the monomer, where D40 acquired the C4β-proton from the substrate to form a dienolate intermediate and transferred it to C6β, while Y16 and D103 formed hydrogen bonds with the oxygen atom of the 3-carbonyl group of the substrate to stabilize the transition states (Fig. 1a)19,20,23,25,39–43.

The KSI exhibited catalytic promiscuity, possessing both isomerization19,20,23,29,43 and Kemp elimination activities44. Different from the isomerization mechanism, in the Kemp elimination D99 serves as a general base to acquire a proton and weaken the O-N bond by proton transfer, thereby opening the five membered ring and extracting a proton (Fig. 1b)45,46.

In this work, we discovered that KSIs catalyzed the addition reactions of α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds (Fig. 1c). Various studies, including biochemical property assays of enzymes, crystal structure analysis, site directed mutagenesis, as well as molecular dynamics and quantum chemical calculations, were carried out to investigate the catalytic promiscuity of KSI. The results suggest a unique reaction mechanism that isomerization and addition occurred in the pocket of monomer and the interface of dimer, respectively. The unusual cascade catalytic process of KSI not only reveals the diverse catalytic properties of enzymes, but also provides valuable guidance for the design of novel enzymes and reactions.

Results and discussion

KSI-catalyzed addition reaction

When we studied the effect of KSI enzyme on the yield of 9α-OH-AD in the biotransformation of phytosterols, a non-natural reaction between 9α-OH-AD or AD and phenazine methosulfate (PMS) was unexpectedly discovered using steroid delta-isomerase (KSIMn) from Mycolicibacterium neoaurum NRRL B-3805 (B3805). The products were purified and characterized through liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS) with m/z of 496.2734 and 480.2791 [M + H]+, which were speculated as the addition products of 9α-OH-AD or AD with PMS, respectively. However, the structure of these products was not determined due to their instability. To explore this unusual reaction, we examined the KSIMn-catalyzed reaction of steroidal compounds (AD and 9α-OH-AD) with the quinones (1, 4-6), which may also act as electron acceptor (Fig. 2a). Wet whole cells expressing KSI genes were used in the reactions. In the presence of 5 or 6, KSIMn showed reactivity toward AD and 9α-OH-AD, especially with 89-95% conversion of 9α-OH-AD (Table 1). Confusedly, when 1 or 4 was presented, AD or 9α-OH-AD was not consumed, but a product was detected.

Subsequently, the substrate 1–9, 11, 12, 19 and 28 were examined using wet whole cells of KSIMn. KSIMn exhibited catalytic activity towards substrates 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 (Fig. 2a and Table 1). Complete conversion of 5 and >90% conversion of 1 were achieved, whereas 2, 4 and 6 remained largely unreacted, indicating a distinct substrate preference. Next, steroidal compounds (11–31), single-ring (10) or two-ring substrates (8–9) were tested with methyl quinones 5 and 6, respectively (Fig. 2a). In the presence of 5 or 6, KSIMn showed reactivity toward 19 of the 24 tested substrates, especially those with a hydroxyl group or without C19-methyl, and two-ring substrate 8, reaching >70% conversion (Table 1). The products were identified by liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC–MS) mainly as addition products (Supplementary Figs. 2–4 and Supplementary Table 3). The steroidal compounds with a C17 side chain or C6-substitution exhibited low or no activity.

Furthermore, the products for the transformation of 1 were purified and characterized as the two-molecule addition products 1a and 1b (Fig. 2b). The methyl quinone 5 was transformed to 5a by KSIMn, which was identified as a trimolecular addition product (Fig. 2c). In the reaction of 9α-OH-AD with methyl quinone 6, the activated enol-type intermediate of steroidal compound triggered a C6-substitution reaction, resulting in construction of a C-O bond to generate product 12a of α-configuration (Fig. 2d). Besides, in the reaction of cyclic enone 8 and methyl quinone 5, the product 8a was identified as a hetero-addition product, showing that 8a was formed by a C-C bond coupling of α-configuration (Fig. 2e).

For the control reactions of substrate 1 using E. coli cells without KSIMn gene or in the absence of cells, 20–30% conversion of 1 was observed, but the products 1a and 1b were not detected (Supplementary Table 4). These results suggested that the formation of 1a and 1b was catalyzed by KSIMn. To investigate whether the reaction is O2-dependent, we performed the reaction under aerobic and anaerobic conditions. The reaction mixtures showed distinct colors (Supplementary Fig. 5), and the yield of 1a was significantly decreased under anaerobic conditions, suggesting that O2 exerted effect on the KSIMn-catalyzed reaction of 1 (Supplementary Table 5).

KSICt and KSIPp genes were expressed at similar level with KSIMn gene in E. coli (Supplementary Fig 6), and the resulting wet whole cells were used to test their catalytic performance with 1, yielding 1a with an analytical yield 26% and 22%, respectively, which were lower than that by KSIMn (64%) (Supplementary Table 4). Similar results were observed for 5. KSICt and KSIPp catalyzed the addition reaction of 5, and the product 5a yields were lower than that by KSIMn which gave 60% yield (Supplementary Table 6). In the reaction of 8 with 5, the 8 was consumed to generate the product 8a in 53%, 54% and 39% analytical yields by KSIMn, KSIPp and KSICt, respectively (Supplementary Table 7). The lower analytical yield of 8a than the theoretical yield might be resulted from the instability of 8 and quinones in Tris-HCl buffer. The results indicated that the catalytical performances of KSIMn and KSICt were different. We compared seven residues in the active center of KSIMn (Y15, Y31, D39, F56, Y57, E97 and W114) and KSICt (Y14, F30, D38, F54, Y55, D99 and F116) (Supplementary Fig. 7)19,33,34, three residues were different. The variants F30Y/D99E/F116W of KSICt was constructed and achieved slightly higher yield of 8a (45%) in the two-component reaction of 8 and 5 (Supplementary Table 7). Unexpectedly, this variant decreased the yield of 1a and 5a for the one-component reaction of 1 and 5 (Supplementary Table 4 and 6). These results demonstrated that KSIs not only catalyzed the isomerization reaction19,20,23,29,43, but also had the ability to catalyze the addition reaction.

Crystal structures of KSIs revealed an unusual binding site in dimeric interfaces

Although the attempts to obtain the crystal structures of KSIMn bound with substrate and KSICt bound with the substrates quinones (1, 5 or 6) and steroid 12 were unsuccessful, the crystal structures of the variant of KSIMn-E97Q with similar catalytic ability as KSIMn and less catalytic performances KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W were solved, including the structures of KSIMn-E97Q (PDB: 9VWL, resolution 2.75 Å), KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W (PDB: 9VWH, resolution 1.65 Å), KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W complexed with 5 (PDB: 9VX5, resolution 2.35 Å) or 6 (PDB: 9VX4, resolution 2.20 Å) (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 8). The obtained crystal structures exhibited homo-symmetric dimer by two independently folded monomers packing together with hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions, that presented similarities to the structures of KSICt and KSIPp29,48. No electron density was observed near the bottom of the cavity in monomeric structures of KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•5 and KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•6, but the homo-dimer interface contained electron density that was matched with 5 or 6, indicating that the binding domain of 5 or 6 located at the interface of homo-symmetric dimer interface (Fig. 3). In detail, the phenyl and methyl groups of 5 interacted with H100 of monomer A and H100 of monomer D through π-π stacking or π-alkyl interaction, respectively. Whereas the carbonyl group of 5 formed a conventional hydrogen bond with R113 of monomer A, and the methyl group of 5 formed alkyl interaction with R113 and L115 of monomer D (Fig. 3a). For KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•6, the similar π-π stacking and π-alkyl interaction were observed by H100 and 6 (Fig. 3b). The carbonyl group of 6 formed conventional hydrogen bond with R113 of monomer A. But the interaction between the 6 and L115 was not observed, which might be due to the different position of alkyl group. Superimposition of the wild-type KSICt, its variant KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W, KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•5 and KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•6 revealed that mutation led to a significant impact on the overall protein conformation (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c). Whereas, the binding of methyl quinone exhibited minimal impact on the overall protein conformation (Supplementary Fig. 9d–i).

Location of 5 or 6 at the dimeric interface is significantly different from the previously observed substrate-binding of KSIs and variants, in which the steroidal and non-steroidal substrates bind in the bottom of catalytic cavity in the monomer, i.e. the active center for isomerization (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 10)28,29,48. KSIMn-E97Q exhibited a similar homo-dimeric interface that was observed in KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•6. However, the comparison of KSIMn-E97Q and KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•6 revealed a significant discrepancy on the protein conformation and key residues (Fig. 4). Most obviously, the polar residues H100, R113 and hydrophobic L115 of dimeric interface in KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W were replaced by hydrophobic V98, polar residues K111 and Y113 in KSIMn-E97Q, respectively (Fig. 4c).

Both monomeric pocket and dimeric interface were essential for the addition reaction

Unlike the known active centers in the monomer23–32,47,48, the structures of KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W with quinones (5 or 6) revealed the dimeric interface might be another active center, providing insights for investigation of addition catalytic mechanism. The unexpected binding of methyl quinones 5 and 6 in the homodimer interface raised a question whether the active pocket of monomer still participated in the addition reaction. To verify this, we compared seven key residues in the pockets of KSICt (Y14, F30, D38, F54, Y55, D99 and F116)19,32,47, KSIPp (Y16, Y32, D40, F56, Y57, D103 and W120)20,23,25,39,40,49 and KSIMn (Y15, Y31, D39, F56, Y57, E97 and W114) (Fig. 4b). Site-directed mutagenesis was performed on these residues of KSIMn. The catalytic ability of the variants toward the addition reaction were determined by measuring the yield of 1a and conversion of 1 (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 9). Mutating Y15, Y31, D39 and F56 residues to alanine significantly reduced the yield of 1a, or even almost caused loss of the activity, whereas the yield of 1a slightly decreased for the reaction with variants Y57A, E97A and W114A. These results suggested that the monomeric substrate-binding pocket was essential for the addition product formation of 1, which may catalyze the isomerization of 1.

KSIs-catalyzed addition reaction that is uncommon for the known isomerase functions, and the binding of methyl quinones 5 and 6 in the dimeric interface was observed (Fig. 3). In crystal structures of KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F114W•5 and KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F114W•6, the interfacial residues within the 5 Å range of methyl quinones were V40, E77, A79, I98, E99, H100, R102, R113 and L115. These interfacial residues of KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F114W were replaced by V41, E78, A80, I96, Q97, V98, T100, K111 and Y113 in KSIMn-E97Q (Fig. 4c), and substituted by F42, C81, A83, I102, D103, V104, R106, Q117 and Y119 in KSIPp (Supplementary Fig. 11), respectively. Alanine scanning mutagenesis was performed on these dimeric interfacial residues to investigate the function of dimeric interface in the addition reaction of 1. For KSIMn, replacement of Y113 with A resulted in the yield of 1a lower than 3%, indicating almost loss of the enzyme activity, while other variants of alanine had no significant impact (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 10). To investigate the function of Y113, we replaced Y113 with D, E, F, L, S and T. Comparing to wild-type KSIMn, the yield of 1a was lower than 10% in variants Y113D, Y113E, Y113S and Y113T. At the same concentration of enzymes, the partial rescue by Y113L was detected, and Y113F showed no effect on the yield of 1a (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Table 11). The results suggested that the phenyl of Y113 serves a function of binding or positioning the hydrophobic substrate. Furthermore, we also examined the dimeric interface residues of KSICt and KSIPp, revealing that the dimeric interface was crucial for preserving the addition activity (Fig. 5e, f). The key interface residues of KSIMn, KSICt and KSIPp were varied. Y113 of KSIMn exhibits significant impact, H100, R113 and L115 of KSICt play a key role, while F42 and Y120 in KSIPp were critical, reflecting the complexity and diversity of substrate-binding in the enzymatic catalytic processes. Since L115 in KSICt is different from the corresponding residues in KSIMn (Y113) and KSIPp (Y119), mutants L115Y of KSICt and Y119L of KSIPp were constructed and tested for the addition reaction of 1. As expected, compared to WT, L115Y of KSICt increased the yield of 1a to 165% and that of Y119L of KSIPp decreased to 42%, respectively. Mutation of Y113 of KSIMn to F showed no impact. These results might suggest that Y113 of KSIMn and Y119 of KSIPp play a key role in the addition reaction by π-π interaction with the isomerized intermediate. In conclusion, these results suggested that both the substrate-binding pocket of monomer and the dimeric interface were necessary for maintaining addition activity. Therefore, the KSI-catalytic addition reaction might proceed by a unique mechanism, where the pocket of monomer was cooperated with dimeric interface to catalyze the non-nature reaction.

Catalytic mechanism

As discussed above, site-directed mutation experiments demonstrated that Y15, Y31, D39, F56 and E97 of the monomeric pocket, as well as Y113 at the dimeric interface of KSIMn, played critical roles in the addition reaction of 1. Furthermore, crystal structures of the highly homologous enzyme mutant of KSICt (KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•5 and KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•6) revealed that substrates 5 or 6 bind at the interface of the homodimer. Based on the information, we propose that both the monomeric pocket and the pocket at the dimeric interface participate in the KSIMn-catalyzed addition reaction of 1. A combination of computational approaches was employed to elucidate the detailed catalytic mechanism.

First, quantum mechanical/molecular mechanical (QM/MM) calculations were performed to investigate the chemical steps of the 1 reaction, on the basis of the representative snapshot from 50 ns MD simulations. We constructed a model with two 1 molecules bound in the monomeric pocket of KSIMn, as it is well-established that the isomerization, which is the natural reaction of isomerases and the initial step for the 1 reaction, takes place at this site. In the QM/MM optimized enzyme-substrate complex (ES, Supplementary Fig. 12), the methyl moiety of 1 forms a hydrogen bond with D39, which further forms hydrogen bonds with Y15 and Y57 via water molecules. The isomerization step, involving proton transfer from the 1 methyl group to the catalytic base D39 and a concerted ketone-enolate tautomerization, was calculated to have an energy barrier of 18.5 kcal mol−1 (TS1, Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 12). After the resulting intermediate Int1, the reaction was proposed to proceed with stepwise C–C bond formation processes to generate the five-membered ring in the final product. However, this cycloaddition was calculated to be with a prohibitively high barrier of 68.5 kcal/mol (Supplementary Fig. 12), indicating that this pathway is energetically unfeasible. Analyzing the geometry of the transition state reveals significant steric hindrance in the pocket, explaining the improbability of the reaction proceeding through this mechanism.

Inspired by the crystal structures of KSICt, in which 5 and 6 bind at the dimeric interface, it is reasonable to speculate that the cycloaddition reaction of 1 might occur at the dimeric interface of KSIMn. To examine this hypothesis, we constructed a model with one 1 molecule and one isomerized 1 molecule bound at the dimeric interface (Supplementary Fig. 13). In the QM/MM optimized structure, the oxygen atom of isomerized 1 forms a hydrogen bond with Y113, and interacts with another 1 molecule by π-stacking. These interactions play important roles in constraining and orientating the substrates in a catalytically favorable pose. The calculated energy barriers for the two C-C bond formation steps involved in the addition reaction are 10.7 and 18.0 kcal/mol (TS2 and TS3, Fig. 6), demonstrating that the cycloaddition is energetically feasible at the dimer interface. Structural analysis revealed that Y113 is involved in a stable hydrogen bond with the substrate throughout the reaction, which thus contributes to orienting the substrate in the optimal position and neutralizing its negative charge. This mechanistic role is further supported by experimental evidence that the Y113A mutant exhibits drastically reduced catalytic activity compared to the wild-type enzyme.

The final step of the reaction is the keto-enol isomerization of cycloaddition intermediate Int3. Notably, the calculated barrier for this process is as high as 31.3 kcal/mol at the dimeric interface (Supplementary Fig. 13), but it drops to just 15.9 kcal/mol in aqueous solution with the assistance of two water molecules (TS4, Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Table 13). These results clearly demonstrate that the keto-enol isomerization is strongly favored in solution rather than at the interface.

For the reaction to proceed through the described pathway above, two critical substrate translocation events must occur, including that the isomerized 1 (Int1) migrate from the monomeric pocket to the dimeric interface, and the transfer of the cycloaddition intermediate (Int3) from the dimeric interface to solution. To evaluate the feasibility these processes, Steered MD (SMD) and umbrella sampling (US) simulations were performed. These methods have been widely used to investigate the free energy profile of substrate transport50,51. The calculations show that the release of isomerized 1 from the monomeric pocket to solution is associated with a feasible transition barrier of 10.5 ± 1.2 kcal mol−1 (Supplementary Fig. 15a) and the transport from solution to the dimeric interface has an even low barrier of 3.5 ± 0.7 kcal mol−1 (Supplementary Fig. 15b). For the release of the cycloaddition intermediate from the dimeric interface to solution, the free energy barrier is significantly low with a value of 1.0 ± 1.1 kcal mol−1 (Supplementary Fig. 15c). The US simulations results clearly show that the substrate can readily translocate during the reaction.

Another piece of the computational investigation is the binding preference of 1 and the isomerized 1 toward the monomeric pocket and the dimeric interface, using MM-PBSA calculations (Supplementary Table 14). The results reveal that the binding affinity of the 1 in the pocket (−1.06 kcal mol−1) is stronger than that at the interface (−0.26 kcal mol−1), suggesting that substrate favors to bind in the pocket to undergo the keto-enol isomerization. In contrast, the binding affinity of the isomerized 1 at the interface (−5.30 kcal mol−1) is stronger than that in the pocket (−4.50 kcal mol−1), indicating that the isomerized 1 favors to bind at the interface to undergo the cycloaddition reaction. These binding energy differences, together with the results on reaction barriers, provide a comprehensive explanation for the observed site-specific reactivity. Namely, the isomerization of 1 takes place in the monomeric pocket where it binds more strongly, while the Int1 migrates to the dimeric interface where this form has higher binding affinity.

Our computational results demonstrate that KSIMn employs a two-site cascade catalytic mechanism for the isomerization and addition reaction of 1, occuring in the monomeric pocket and the dimeric interface, respectively. The complete pathway can be summarized as, after the isomerization of 1 in the monomeric pocket, the isomerized 1 releases to the solution and then enters into the dimeric interface for the stepwise cycloaddition reaction, and the resulting intermediate releases to the solution for the final isomerization.

In conclusion, ketosteroid isomerase (KSI) catalyzes the isomerization of ∆5-3-ketosteroids to ∆4-3-ketosteroids in the biosynthesis and metabolism of steroids. In this study, we discovered an unexpected catalytic promiscuity of KSIMn for the homo-addition reaction of methyl quinones such as 1, 5 and 6, and their hetero-addition with other cyclic enones. The crystal structural analysis of KSI variants with 5 or 6 revealed an unusual substrate-binding site at the dimeric interface. Site-directed mutagenesis and computational studies suggested that the addition reactions might procced via an unusual cascade catalytic mechanism involving isomerization and subsequent addition reaction in two distant substrate-binding pockets in a single enzyme. In details, the ketone-enolate isomerization reaction of methyl enone occurs in the substrate-binding pocket of the monomeric unit, the isomerized enolate intermediate is released to the solution and then enters into the dimeric interface for the subsequent addition reaction. The unprecedented cooperation of two distant catalytic pockets not only expands the catalytic promiscuity of KSIs, but also inspires further exploration of novel cascade reactions and new-to-nature catalytic activities of other enzymes.

Methods

Construction and expression of KSIs and mutant libraries

The KSIMn gene was amplified from M. neoaurum B-3805 genome DNA using primer pairs F and R (Supplementary Table 1). After purification, the PCR fragments were ligated into pET28a(+) line fragment that was digested with NcoI and XhoI, through a ClonExperss II One Step Cloning Kit to generate the expressive plasmids pET28a-KSIMn. Subsequently, these recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3), respectively. The KSICt and KSIPp genes were synthesized by General Biosystems (GENEWEI (Suzhou) Co. Ltd.), cloned into pET28a(+) plasmid in NcoI and XhoI, and transformed into an E. coli BL21(DE3). All of the mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis using the primers in Supplementary Table 1 and amplified with Super Pfx DNA Polymerase. These PCR products were purified using an Omega Biotek Cycle Pure kit, and then transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) after digesting with DpnI restriction enzyme at 37 °C for 2 h. These strains harboring the plasmid were selected under 50 μg/mL kanamycin and cultured at 37 °C until OD reached 0.6–0.8, followed by incubation at 20 °C or 25 °C for 12–16 h with 0.1 mM IPTG to induce genes expression.

Biotransformation of cyclic enones by KSIs and variants

The homo-addition reaction system contained cyclic enones (5 mM 11, 12, 19, 28 or 10 mM 1, 5–10), 5% co-solvents (MeOH or DMSO), 40–60 mg/mL wet cells (crushed by ultrasonic) in Tris-HCl buffer (500 μl, 50 mM, pH 8.0). For hetero-addition reaction, the system contained 5 Mm or 10 mM cyclic enones (8–31), 10 mM methyl quinones 5 or 6, 10% MeOH, 40 mg/mL wet cells (crushed by ultrasonic) in Tris-HCl buffer (500 μl, 50 mM, pH 8.0). The reaction mixtures were shaken at 30 °C with 200 rpm shaker for 16 h, and 500 μl ethyl acetate was added to stop the reaction. The organic phase was separated by centrifugation at 10,000g for 5 min and dried at room temperature. The resulting samples without ethyl acetate were analyzed in methanol by TLC, HPLC and LC-MS to determine the products.

Analytical methods

All samples were dried and subsequently re-dissolved in methanol prior to analysis. TLC was developed in a mixture of petroleum ether and ethyl acetate (4:1, 3:2, 2:3, v/v). HPLC analysis was performed using Agilent 1200 or 1260 equipped with a C18-column (250 mm × 4.6 mm × 0.5 μm, Agilent, USA). A mixture of methanol and water (80:20, 70:30, 65:35, 60:40, 55:45, v/v) was used as mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.8 ml/min (Supplementary Table 2). The signals were detected by a diode array detector (DAD) at the wavelengths of 210 nm, 230 nm and 254 nm. The products were also analyzed by LC-MS with an Agilent 1200 HPLC and a Bruker micto TOF_QII high resolution mass spectrometer (HRMS) equipped with an ESI ionization source. Masses were recorded over a 50–1000 m/z range in positive mode.

Isolation and identification of products 1a, 1b, 5a, 8a and 12a

In order to identify 12a, a mixture of 9α-OH-AD (5 mM), 6 (10 mM), 10% MeOH, KSIMn (wet cells 40 mg/ml) in the Tris-HCl buffer (200 mL, 50 mM, pH 8.0) was incubated at 30 °C for 16 h with shaking 200 rpm. The reaction mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (100 mL × 3) and concentrated under reduced pressure. The products were isolated and purified by flash chromatography on silica gel (PE/EtOAc = 3:1 to 2:1, v/v). The crude product was crystallized in a mixed solvent of DCM and EtOAc to afford the product 12a that was confirmed as C-O coupling product.

For the preparative scale of 8a was performed starting from 8 (15 mM), 5 (20 mM), 10% MeOH, KSIMn (wet cells 40 mg/ml) in the Tris-HCl buffer (100 mL, 50 mM, pH 8.0). The reaction was shaken at 200 rpm and 30 °C for 16 h. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (100 mL × 5). The organic phase was concentrated by evaporation under reduced pressure and purified by flash chromatography on silica gel (PE/EtOAc = 10:1 to 3:1, v/v) to afford the product 8a that was identified as C-C coupling product using NMR analysis in CD3Cl and crystal diffraction.

Procedure for the identification of 1a and 1b catalyzed by KSIMn, a mixture containing vitamin K3 (15 mM), DMSO (5%, v/v), KSIMn (wets cell 60 mg/ml) in the Tris-HCl buffer (200 mL, 50 mM, pH 8.0) was incubated at 30 °C with shaking. After 16 h, the reaction mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (100 mL × 3), and the combined organic layers were concentrated under reduced pressure. The products were isolated and purified by flash chromatography on silica gel (PE/EtOAc = 10:1 to 3:1, v/v) to afford the homo-addition products. The products 1a and 1b were confirmed using NMR analysis by CD3Cl as the solvent. Furthermore, 1a was further confirmed by crystal structure analysis.

Procedure for the synthesis of 5a, a mixture containing 5 (10 mg/ml), KSIMn (wet cells 40 mg/ml) in the Tris-HCl buffer (100 mL, 50 mM, pH 8.0) was incubated at 30 °C for 18 h with shaking. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (100 mL × 3). The combined organic layers were concentrated under reduced pressure. The initial product was isolated and purified by flash chromatography (PE/EtOAc = 6:1, v/v) to obtain the crude product. Subsequently, the crude product was purified by preparative reverse phase liquid chromatography (Agilent 1260, C18-column, a mixed solvent of Methanol and water with 62:38, v/v, a flow rate of 11 mL/min, wavelengths 254 nm and 210 nm). The 1H and 13C spectra of 5a were recorded at 400 MHz with Bruker Avance III devices using Methanol-d4 as the solvent. To further assign the structure of 5a, the crystal was obtained in a mixed solvent of DCM and EtOAc and determined by crystal diffraction using high power single crystal X-ray diffractometer FRX. All the NMR analysis were recorded by 400 MHz with Bruker Avance III devices. The crystal structures of products (1a, 5a, 8a and 12a) were identified by diffraction with high power single crystal X-ray diffractometer FRX.

Gene expression and protein purification of KSI and their mutants

For KSIMn, KSICt and their mutants (E97Q, Y113L and Y113F of KSIMn, KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W) gene expression, the seeds were cultivated in 20 mL of LB medium with kanamycin (a final concentration of 50 μg/mL) at 37 °C for 6–8 h, and then transferred to 800 mL LB medium containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin and grown at 37 °C in 200 rpm shaker. When OD 600 reached to 0.6–0.8, the culture was induced by adding IPTG with a final concentration of 0.1 mM and cultured for another 14 h at 25 °C in 200 rpm shaker. The cells were harvested by centrifuging at 6000g for 10 min, and washed once with Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 8.0). Then the cells were resuspended in Buffer A (50 mM Tris, 0.5 M NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) and lysed by a high-pressure homogenizer. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 10,000g and 4 °C for 25 min and filtered with a 0.22 μM filter. Affinity chromatography with a Ni Sepharose 6 Fast Flow column (GE, USA, 5 ml column volume) was used for protein purification of KSI and their mutants. The sample was loaded with the same flow rate after Ni-column was pre-equilibrated with a flow rate of 3.0 mL/min by Buffer A. The progress of elution was carried out. After washing with 5 times column volume Buffer A, the bounded target protein was washed with Buffer B (50 mM Tris, 0.5 M NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) increasing from 0 to 100 % within 30 min. The target protein fractions were collected and desalted with a desalt column (GE, USA, 50 ml column volume) and concentrated through ultrafiltration tube (10 kD). Finally, the pure enzyme solutions were stored at −80 °C after adding 10% (v/v) glycerin for activity assay or further purified by one more exclusion column (Superdex 200 16, GE Healthcare, 24 ml column volume) with the elution Buffer C (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) for crystallization. The concentration of purified enzymes were determined with BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, China).

Protein crystallization and structure determination

All initial conditions of crystallization were screened by sitting drop vapor diffusion method with Hampton Research Crystal Screen Kits at 25 °C in 96-well plates. The crystal structures of proteins complexed with small molecular substrates (1, 5, 6, 8, 12) were perfomed by co-crystallization that the purified enzyme (15–20 mg/mL) were incubated with 40 mM small molecular substrates on ice for 6 h. Crystals for date collection of KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W and KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•6 in apo formed by optimization after 4–7 days in 24-well plates, with the same conditions of mixing 2 μl protein solution (15 mg/ml KSICt-Mu2 without or with 40 mM 6) and an equal volume of reservoir solution (20% 2-propanol, 0.15 M MES (pH 6.0), 25% w/v Polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether 2000). Crystal of KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W•5 was grown under the conditions of 2 μl protein solution containing 15 mg/ml KSICt-Mu2 and 40 mM 5 mixed with 2 μl reservoir solution consisting of 0.1 M sodium acetate trihydrate (pH 4.5), 30% w/v Polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether 4000, which was obtained after 4–7 days in 24-well plates at 25 °C. And the crystal of KSIMn-E97Q was obtained in the conditions of 2 μl protein solution (20 mg/ml KSIMn-E97Q) mixed with 2 μl reservoir solution consisting of 10% TacsimateTM (pH 5.0), 20% w/v Polyethylene glycol 3350, which was obtained in 24-well plates at 25 °C after 4–7 days. We also tried to co-crystallize the enzyme (wild-type of KSIMn and KSICt, variant KSIMn-E97Q and KSICt-F30Y/D99E/F116W) with substrates 12 (12 and 5 or 6), 8 (8 and 5 or 6) and 1. Unfortunately, no high-resolution crystals were obtained.

When transporting protein crystals, first puting the crystals into the freezing loop, soaked them in the cryoprotectant (reservoir solution containing 5–10% glycerol, v/v), and then placed them in the liquid nitrogen storage tank. All X-ray diffraction datas were collected with wavelength of 0.978–0.979, at 100 K, from the BL02U1/BL10U2/BL17B/BL18U2/BL19U1 beamline of Nation Facility for Protein Science in Shanghai (NFPS) at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). Then, HKL2000 was used to process the diffration image52. Using the Phaser program53 in CCP454 and the protein structure predicted by AlphaFold255 as the search model, the molecular diaplacement (MR) method was employed to solve the crystal structure of the protein. Refmac556 and Coot57 were used to furhter refine the structure, which were ramachandran disallow 0 and favor 97.16-99.39. Before refining the structure, 5% of the randomly selected reflection were reserved to calculate Rfree58 as the monitor. Supplementary Table 8 summarizes the data collection and refinment statistica. All data were programmed using PyMOL.

Computational methods

The structure of the enzyme KSIMn was predicted using AlphaFold255. The substrate-enzyme binary complex was obtained by placing ligands into the binding pocket of KSIMn, guided by the substrate-binding mode of homologous enzymes in their crystal structures. All molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed with the AMBER 22 software package59. Representative snapshots from the MD trajectories served as the starting structures for subsequent quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) calculations. These QM/MM calculations were carried out using ChemShell v7.3.160, which combines ORCA v5.0.261 for the QM region and DL_POLY62 for the MM region. The QM region was treated with the B3LYP-D3(BJ) functional, and the MM region was described using the AMBER ff19SB force field. To explore substrate transport during the reaction, classical MD simulations, steered MD (SMD)63,64 and umbrella sampling (US)65,66 were employed. Detailed computational methods are provided in the Supporting Information. The structures involved in the computations, including the optimized structures corresponding to the proposed mechanism as well as the initial and final structures from the MD simulations, are provided in PDB format in Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Data 2, respectively.
