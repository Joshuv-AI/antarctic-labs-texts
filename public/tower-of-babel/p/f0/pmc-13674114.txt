Monitoring and feedback using data in transfusion medicine: A study of practices from the BEST Collaborative
Authors: Ryan A. Metcalf, Allison Mo, Sandra K. White, Gustavo C. Duarte, Nancy M. Dunbar, Katie Hands, Anil K. Panigrahi, Jessica L. Poisson, Nabiha H. Saifee, Hua Shan, Erica M. Wood, Michael F. Murphy, Simon J. Stanworth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt is imperative that health systems monitor transfusion practice quality and implement improvements when evidence‐based practice gaps are identified. Despite the importance of data‐driven quality activities, little is known about approaches employed by hospitals. This study aimed to describe strategies used by hospitals to acquire, analyze, and apply transfusion‐related quality data.Study Design and MethodsA pre‐piloted survey was developed by the core team, and Biomedical Excellence for Safer Transfusion (BEST) Collaborative attendees of meetings were invited to participate. Topics spanned five domains: hospital characteristics, data acquisition, quality metric prioritization, data analysis/visualization, and feedback processes. Responses were summarized to identify patterns and variation across institutions.ResultsNine hospitals of varying size, specialty mix, and transfusion volumes from three continents participated. Most transfusion services acquired transfusion data from both the electronic health record and the laboratory information system (6/9, 67%). Although all sites tracked the number of transfusions, there was wide variation in other metrics prioritized. Transfusion appropriateness was prioritized by 7 of 9 centers, but few reported monitoring antifibrinolytic agent use (2/9, 22%), anemia management (4/9, 44%), and consent (2/9, 22%). All hospitals performed some form of data analysis, but chart/table types and functionalities differed. Feedback was typically provided by transfusion medicine specialists or hematology, but frequency and means differed between sites.DiscussionThis descriptive study highlights significant variation in how selected hospitals use data to monitor transfusion practices and provide feedback. There are many opportunities to enhance optimal uses of data to deliver effective quality improvement.

INTRODUCTION

Transfusion teams have a core responsibility to ensure that blood transfusions, which have challenges of supply and risks to recipients, as well as costs, are used appropriately and for evidence‐based clinical indications. 1 , 2 , 3 Monitoring and feedback to prescribers across multiple clinical settings and patient populations is therefore an essential activity of transfusion medicine services and patient blood management programs. However, there is considerable uncertainty in how this is done and delivered in contemporary practice, including how different hospital systems use data to track and ensure the effective implementation of good transfusion practice. Although approaches to data collection and monitoring have traditionally involved manual processes and audits, there is interest in the use of electronic systems with automated capabilities. 4

Many national and international organizations promote and define the adoption of best practices, such as the Association for the Advancement of Blood and Biotherapies (AABB), the International Collaboration for Transfusion Medicine Guidelines (ICTMG), and the International Society of Blood Transfusion (ISBT). Quality metrics considered pertinent may also be developed from evidence‐based guidelines, 1 , 2 , 3 standards, 5 , 6 , 7 regulations, 8 as well as national recommendations, such as in England, for which a core list of key national quality standards exist. 9 Examples of these and additional sources from which quality metrics may be derived are provided in Table 1. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

Relevant data for metrics need to be acquired from reliable sources, and then organized and (ideally) validated prior to local analysis. Following analysis, teams may generate visualizations to provide insights into practice trends and the impact of implemented interventions. Finally, and importantly, changes in transfusion prescribing behavior require feedback of data to relevant clinicians and health care professionals. At present, we have little insight into many elements of these processes, starting with which metrics are prioritized between hospitals and transfusion teams. How transfusion teams perform the critical element of quality improvement feedback remains largely unexplored. The value of interactive quality dashboards to visualize practice performance and provide feedback that have been reported by some hospitals remains unclear. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22

With this background in mind, the aim of this descriptive study was to present more granular information on the approaches for data collection, monitoring, and feedback at selected hospitals. This study would inform further projects in the broad area of quality improvement and feedback to advance our understanding of how data can be more effectively used and to identify opportunities for and to inform future studies for optimizing transfusion practice.

METHODS

Participants/site selection

A study to describe how selected hospitals acquire, organize, and use data to monitor transfusion practice and drive quality improvements was developed. Institutional review board management was contacted and determined that a formal submission for exemption was not required given that patient‐level data were not collected and the survey captured the programmatic approaches of each site rather than information about individual respondents. Sites were recruited following presentations at a Biomedical Excellence for Safer Transfusion (BEST) Collaborative biannual meeting (conferences 68 and 69). Meeting attendees were asked to contact the lead investigators (RAM, SJS) by email to express interest regarding participation.

Survey development and administration

Members of the working group drafted and piloted an initial survey form containing key domains, each of which included multiple questions. Following iterative pilots, the final survey questionnaire was administered from May through July 2025 to those expressing interest via email as an attached document (Microsoft Word, Redmond, WA). The documents were filled out manually by respondents and returned to the corresponding author.

The five key domains of interest explored in the survey were study site characteristics, data acquisition, clinical transfusion metrics considered important, data analysis and visualization, and use of the synthesized data to provide feedback with the aim of driving improvements in practice. The survey fields of entry included a range of response options, including binary responses (e.g., yes/no), a list of “drop down” options, and free‐text narrative descriptions. A full copy of the survey is provided in Appendix S1.

Survey responses and data

Survey responses were collated, reviewed, and summarized in tables, with each site given a number to maintain anonymity. The response data were summarized by providing qualitative information, with identification of patterns, variability, or perceived gaps.

Information from survey responses was reviewed and interpreted to provide an impression of how participating hospitals from various countries employ data acquisition and analysis to understand and enhance institutional transfusion practice quality.

RESULTS

Hospital characteristics

Nine hospitals expressed interest and participated in this descriptive study (Table 2). The co‐authors of this manuscript provided responses for their respective sites. Three continents were represented, including Oceania (2 sites), Europe (2 sites), and North America (5 sites). A majority of centers reported treating patients of all ages and providing transfusion services for a variety of complex patient populations. The size of participating institutions, as measured by the number of hospital beds, varied, as did the number of patient discharges and surgeries performed per year. Numbers of transfusions of each blood component also varied by institution.

Data acquisition and organization

In terms of data sources used to evaluate transfusion practices, most centers extract data from both the electronic health record (6/9, 67%) and the blood bank laboratory information system (8/9, 89%) (Table 3). Data extraction was usually performed using a combination of manual and automated processes (6/9, 67%). For automated data extractions, the frequency of updates on transfusion usage varied from daily to monthly or “as needed”. Four centers reported use of an electronic querying approach, with three of these specifically using Structured Query Language (SQL), a standardized approach to electronically manipulate a relational database.

Metrics reported

There was wide variation in the types of metrics prioritized for analysis and reporting (Table 4). As expected, 100% of respondents reported evaluating numbers of transfusions administered. Most centers reported on transfusion appropriateness, such as guideline adherence (7/9, 78%). It was less common for sites to evaluate metrics related to antifibrinolytic use (2/9, 22%), patient consent for blood (2/9, 22%), and the diagnosis and management of anemia (4/9, 44%). There was wide variation in availability or extraction of data linked to other prioritized metrics, apart from blood wastage, which most reported was tracked (7/9, 78%).

Analysis and visualization

All sites reported that they performed some form of analysis of the data they obtained (Table 5). There was significant variation in descriptions of analyses performed, including the use of tables and visualizations (i.e., chart types). Nearly all sites reported the use of a data dashboard, but again, the levels of complexity and design varied considerably. Most of these were interactive for the user (5/9, 56%) and had some sort of filtering functionality or ability to zoom to view metrics at hierarchical levels within a health system (7/9, 78%). It was common for transfusion medicine specialists and patient blood management professionals to routinely review the data analyses and visualizations.

Feedback to clinicians

The frequency of feedback to clinicians varied from ad hoc to short intervals (e.g., weekly) to relatively long intervals (e.g., annual cadence) (Table 6). This feedback was typically provided by transfusion medicine and patient blood management team members. Recipients of feedback included physicians in various practice settings, transfusion committee members, leaders of physician groups such as division chiefs, and leaders over the broader health system. Mechanisms of feedback included emails and meeting environments ranging from small numbers of people to grand rounds presentation settings. The perception of the impact of feedback to improve practice varied substantially, from no clear change to evidence of improved guideline adherence, cost savings, reduced adverse events, and increased rates of anemia screening.

DISCUSSION

The acquisition and use of data is essential to monitor and drive improvements in transfusion practices, but little is known about what hospitals are actually doing in this regard. In our survey, we identified significant variation by site in areas of data acquisition, metric prioritization, analysis, and feedback to health care professionals. It should be noted that the participating sites in this study are likely to be supported by motivated transfusion specialists because of the method used to offer participation in this study through BEST, and hence demonstrate more willingness to engage with quality improvement initiatives. However, we imagine that wider variation in data practices is more likely to be found if the survey was circulated to a wider array of hospitals. The findings underscore many opportunities for improvement and standardization in monitoring and feedback but represent an important starting position to further study ways to identify optimal processes for data collection and analysis that could bridge gaps in effective monitoring, feedback, and benchmarking of transfusion practices.

All sites performed most clinical services and there did not appear to be clear patterns in variation based on site characteristics. Most or all sites prioritized metrics related to blood utilization and the appropriateness of blood use. The former tends to be required by regulatory bodies, while the latter is generally considered important because blood transfusion is a very common intervention that comes with important risks and where it is known that inappropriate use is substantial. Exactly how hospitals evaluate transfusion appropriateness (e.g., guideline adherence) is an important research question which we believe will be best addressed by a dedicated follow up study.

The selection of metrics and their definitions was variable between sites. Several additional metrics were reported as prioritized by at least one site, and many sites did not report prioritizing metrics that other sites considered important. Data sources in healthcare are complex, and the acquisition and analysis of pertinent data for key metrics can present technical and resource challenges. Again, there was variation in how relevant data are acquired and organized by site. Overall, although standards for metrics of the quality of transfusion practice exist, it does not appear common for hospitals to monitor all of them.

Furthermore, dashboards to display information for review by clinicians varied between sites. There are key principles groups may consider when designing a quality dashboard in healthcare, 23 but little information about how these relate to transfusion practices. When first viewing a dashboard, it may be helpful to have quick views of clearly labeled important summary metrics automatically displayed for users. Interactive functionality to explore high‐priority questions and easily create reports, as well as the ability to jump to different hierarchical levels of a health system to visualize performance, may also be beneficial. We also have a limited understanding of how hospital clinicians perceive and whether they apply data‐driven feedback into their daily practices. It remains unclear if feedback actually improves transfusion practices across a hospital. 4 , 18

This study has limitations. As mentioned, one clear limitation is the selection of sites that were motivated to participate in this project through BEST, such that there may be selection bias and the responses may not be representative of the range of sites and practices that exist worldwide. Nevertheless, we might expect that, if anything, there could be greater practice gaps or variation in a more broadly representative sample. The sample size was small, but as a descriptive study, this allowed more detailed reporting for each individual site. Nevertheless, reliance on free‐text survey responses may still limit the capture of certain nuances of institutional practices and the consistency of the data collected.

In conclusion, this descriptive study is an initial step that highlights significant gaps and opportunities in how hospitals use data to monitor and improve practice. We found wide variation among the reporting sites with respect to how relevant data are acquired, analyzed, and applied. There are many avenues for future research, including steps to optimize chart types, the science of feedback and benchmarking, use of data for economic evaluations, and need for qualitative research, including a better understanding of technical and resource barriers (Table 7). There is a need to address concerns if hospitals assume that, although practice could improve, it may be difficult to change, or perceptions that practice may already be optimized in a given area such that it may not be worthwhile to expend energy and resources to validate and analyze the relevant data. This area of practice change is specifically challenging since transfusion is the most performed procedure in hospital systems and many diverse populations of patients receive blood, which means that feedback needs to consider multiple clinical specialties and practitioners. The benefits of moving toward optimal transfusion practices include improved patient outcomes, minimizing many different clinical populations at risk of the impact of blood supply shortages, and a reduction in healthcare‐related costs.
