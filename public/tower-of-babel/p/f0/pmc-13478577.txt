Sensitive infrared photodetection enabled by in-sensor background-offset cancellation in a mixed-dimensional van der Waals heterostructure
Authors: Xin Li, Jian Wang, Jintao Fu, Xingsi Liu, Feilong Yu, Jin Chen, Yu Zhao, Jie Wang, Jiaoyang Guo, Yuxing Song, Jiaji Yang, Cheng-wei Qiu, Guanhai Li, Xiaoshuang Chen, Wei Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoom-temperature infrared photodetection has long been constrained by broadband thermal radiation, which injects background photons and drives detectors toward a background-limited regime. Beyond stochastic photon fluctuations, this radiation produces a deterministic mean photocurrent offset that can overwhelm weak signals under ambient conditions. This limitation originates from unipolar spectral integration, where background photons across the response band contribute with the same photocurrent polarity. Here we demonstrate an in-sensor background-offset cancellation mechanism enabled by spectral polarity inversion. By engineering wavelength-dependent photocurrent reversal, photons from different spectral bands generate opposite-signed responses within a single device, suppressing the mean background-induced current under fixed blackbody illumination without external subtraction or balanced readout. We realize this concept using an optically reconfigurable mixed-dimensional Ge/black phosphorus van der Waals heterostructure photodetector. The zero-bias device achieves a room-temperature detectivity of 7.89 × 1010 Jones, within a factor of 4.11 of the background-limited benchmark for unity-quantum-efficiency integration-type detectors.Subject terms: Two-dimensional materials, Sensors

Introduction

Infrared photodetection under ambient conditions is fundamentally constrained by broadband thermal radiation from the environment1–6. At room temperature, ubiquitous blackbody emission injects a large background photon flux into infrared detectors, inevitably giving rise to both stochastic photon fluctuations and a substantial, deterministic background-induced photocurrent offset. This background burden manifests in two coupled ways: the random arrival of background photons establishes a fundamental photon-noise floor, while the large mean photon flux drives the detector to operate at an elevated photocurrent level, where current-dependent excess noise, dynamic range limitations, and readout constraints can become increasingly pronounced. Crucially, irrespective of advances in material quality, device architecture, or interface engineering, environmental background radiation fixes conventional infrared detectors in a background-limited operating regime, making the detection of weak infrared signals under ambient conditions intrinsically challenging7. This limitation represents a principle-level bottleneck for infrared sensing technologies. Rather than being alleviated through incremental improvements in absorption efficiency, it originates from how broadband optical excitation is transduced into electrical signals within the detector8,9.

A central but often implicit assumption underlying conventional infrared detectors is that all photons with energies above the bandgap contribute to the electrical readout with the same polarity. This unipolar, broadband integration of optical excitation renders the background-induced photocurrent unavoidable under fixed blackbody illumination, regardless of improvements in material quality or absorption efficiency10–14. Empirically, this behavior is summarized by the widely used Law 19 benchmark, which delineates the background-limited performance envelope of infrared detectors under typical ambient conditions9. Importantly, Law 19 is defined within the framework of integration-type detectors, in which the detector response performs a uniform spectral summation of incident radiation. Within this paradigm, further performance enhancement is achieved primarily by suppressing internal electrical noise or by reducing the optical background through external means, rather than by modifying how background photons are converted into electrical signals.

Various approaches have been explored to mitigate background effects in infrared detection. System-level strategies, including optical filtering, temporal modulation, and computational background subtraction, have been extensively investigated15–17. In parallel, two-color and multiband infrared detectors provide spectral discrimination by separating photocurrents from different wavelength bands into independent channels. Balanced or differential photodetection schemes further suppress common-mode background fluctuations by subtracting matched detector outputs or optical channels at the readout stage. While effective for rejecting low-frequency drifts and technical noise, these approaches share a common limitation: within each channel, background photons are still fully converted into large unipolar photocurrents before subtraction. As a result, background suppression is implemented downstream of photoelectric conversion, rather than being intrinsically encoded in the detector response itself.

Here, we address this limitation by introducing spectral polarity inversion directly into the detector response. Instead of uniformly integrating background photons into a single-polarity electrical signal, we engineer a photodetector whose spectral responsivity reverses sign as a function of wavelength. In this work, photons from different spectral bands generate photocurrents with opposite polarity within a single device, enabling intrinsic suppression of the mean background-induced photocurrent offset under fixed blackbody illumination. This polarity inversion is realized through optically driven band-structure reconfiguration in a mixed-dimensional heterostructure, where wavelength-dependent photoexcitation induces a reversible inversion of the internal electric field. By embedding polarity inversion into the photoelectric conversion process itself, the detector departs from the conventional unipolar spectral integration framework underlying background-limited infrared detection. As demonstrated below, this response-level spectral engineering enables substantial reduction of background-induced offset and provides a route toward sensitive infrared photodetection under ambient conditions.

Results

Spectral-polarity architecture

To establish the physical basis of spectral polarity engineering for background-limited infrared detection, we first consider the broadband thermal radiation environment under ambient conditions. Figure 1a plots the spectral power density of 300 K blackbody radiation, which constitutes an unavoidable background photon flux for uncooled infrared detectors. Importantly, such background illumination introduces not only stochastic photon fluctuations but also a large deterministic background-induced photocurrent offset when transduced by conventional detectors. In a polarity-inverted architecture, the detector responsivity is intentionally designed to exhibit opposite signs in separated spectral bands, such that the deterministic mean background-induced terminal current can be reduced through signed spectral integration at the photoelectric conversion stage. This reduction is visualized by an offset-equivalent polarity-engineered reference near the spectral-compensation condition.

The underlying mechanism is illustrated schematically in Fig. 1c, where broadband background photons drive same-sign carrier transport in unipolar detectors, resulting in unavoidable additive accumulation of background current. In contrast, a polarity-engineered detector produces oppositely directed carrier transport in different spectral bands, leading to destructive integration of background-induced current contributions already during photocarrier generation and collection. Consistent with this picture, Fig. 1d shows that, for a fixed bipolar EQE profile, spectral polarity reversal induces a zero-crossing point in the mean background-induced current, where the net mean background contribution approaches zero. Figure 1e presents a cross-sectional TEM image of the Ge/BP bipolar detector that realizes such polarity-inverted response. Finally, Fig. 1f summarizes the noise-mitigation landscape for infrared photodetection. Conventional strategies mainly reduce device-level electronic noise or decrease the incident background photon flux, whereas spectral polarity engineering suppresses the mean background-offset burden through in-sensor background-current cancellation, thereby bringing the detector response closer to the residual photon-number-fluctuation floor set by the unsigned absorbed background photon flux.

Spectral polarity reversal in Ge/BP detectors

To physically implement deterministic spectral polarity switching at the detector, we fabricate a van der Waals heterojunction by dry-transferring few-layer black phosphorus (BP) onto bulk p-doped Ge (Fig. 2a). The assembly is performed under inert conditions to suppress oxidation. Atomic force microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 1) confirms uniform BP thickness, while the optical micrograph reveals a well-defined junction area. Elemental mapping and Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3) corroborate the structural integrity of the heterojunction.

Long-wavelength excitation induces hole accumulation in BP, forming a localized valence-band barrier and reversibly inverting the internal electric field. d Measured EQE spectrum exhibiting deterministic wavelength-dependent polarity inversion, with a zero-crossing wavelength at λR ≈ 3.1 μm. The red and blue shaded regions indicate the positive- and negative-polarity photoresponse regimes, respectively. e Schematic illustration of wavelength-dependent photoresponse regimes: (i) λ < λR, forward carrier extraction yields positive photocurrent; (ii) λ ≈ λR, field compensation results in minimal response; (iii) λ > λR, light-induced field inversion drives carriers in the opposite direction, producing negative photocurrent. Before the formation of the heterojunction, the band arrangement of BP and Ge (Fig. 2b), with the BP valence band located above the Ge valence band, provides a basis for the subsequent formation of type II junctions. Kelvin probe force microscopy measurements (Supplementary Fig. 4) reveal an interfacial potential offset of ~60 meV. To examine the response under optical excitation, we perform TCAD simulations (Fig. 2c), which show that long-wavelength illumination induces hole accumulation on the BP side. This carrier redistribution creates a localized valence-band barrier and reversibly inverts the direction of the internal electric field, resulting in a light-induced inversion of the electrostatic potential profile at the heterointerface. The TCAD simulation theoretical model can be found in Supplementary Note 2, and the analysis of light induced band inversion is in Supplementary Note 3 (Supplementary Figs. 5–7).

The optical consequence of this field inversion is directly reflected in the measured EQE spectrum (Fig. 2d), which exhibits a clear polarity reversal at λR ≈ 3.1 μm. For wavelengths shorter than λR, photogenerated carriers are extracted along the original built-in field, producing a positive photocurrent. At longer wavelengths, the light-inverted internal field drives carriers in the opposite direction, yielding a negative photocurrent. The wavelength-dependent open-circuit voltage VOC provides an additional electrical verification of the polarity reversal (Supplementary Fig. 8). VOC evolves from negative values to near zero around λR and becomes positive at longer wavelengths, consistent with the zero crossing in the EQE and short-circuit photocurrent. This bidirectional EQE profile therefore constitutes a direct electrical signature of wavelength-dependent field inversion at the detector. The polarity switching is reproducible across devices with varying doping levels (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10), confirming that it is intrinsic to the heterojunction design. Supplementary Fig. 11 further verifies the Ohmic contacts. Spatially resolved photocurrent mapping at 520, 1550, and 4000 nm further confirms that the wavelength-dependent sign reversal occurs within the same Ge/BP heterojunction region rather than at the contacts or electrode edges; this point is further discussed in Supplementary Note 4 (Supplementary Figs. 12, 13). Under broadband illumination, however, the device response should not be understood as the coexistence of multiple independent macroscopic built-in fields. Instead, the short- and long-wavelength photoresponses arise from locally distinguishable and relatively independent transport processes with distinct spatial origins and dominant driving mechanisms. These local processes can preserve their wavelength-dependent response signs, which are primarily determined by their own local carrier-generation regions, local fields, and transport pathways, but they do not correspond to separate global junctions or independent terminal-current channels. As a result, the measured terminal signal is a single steady-state net current jointly determined by these opposite-polarity local response components, rather than the outcome of externally subtracting two independent channels (Supplementary Note 4). This steady-state net-response formation is the physical basis of the in-sensor background-offset cancellation discussed in this work.

The underlying mechanism is summarized schematically in Fig. 2e. Rather than arising from trap-assisted transport, photothermoelectric effects, or readout-level subtraction, the observed spectral polarity switching is governed by deterministic, light-induced modulation of the internal electric field. Supplementary Fig. 14 shows the wavelength-dependent transient terminal photoresponse of the device. These transient signals reflect the overall photoresponse process, including carrier generation, redistribution and collection, rather than a direct time-resolved measurement of microscopic field-inversion kinetics. The observed microsecond-scale response supports a carrier-transport-dominated process and is inconsistent with slow thermally driven mechanisms such as photothermoelectric effects, as further discussed in Supplementary Note 5 (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16). This internal-field-driven polarity inversion establishes a robust and reversible route to wavelength-dependent photocurrent sign control, providing the physical basis for spectral polarity engineering used to suppress background-induced offset in infrared photodetectors.

To electrically validate the presence of spectral polarity inversion and examine its tunability under controlled simultaneous illumination, we perform photocurrent measurements under monochromatic infrared excitation with additional visible illumination. In these experiments, monochromatic infrared light (2000 nm or 4000 nm) serves as the signal channel, while additional visible illumination is introduced to modulate the steady-state carrier population within the heterojunction, thereby probing how spectrally distinct photoresponse components are transduced into the same terminal output.

Under 2000 nm excitation (Fig. 3a), the device exhibits a positive photocurrent that increases monotonically with added background intensity. This behavior reflects conventional unipolar spectral integration, where both signal- and background-induced carriers are transported along the same direction of the internal field and contribute constructively to the net current. By contrast, under 4000 nm excitation (Fig. 3b), the photocurrent is negative and its magnitude decreases with increasing background illumination. Here, background-induced carriers are transported with opposite polarity to the signal-induced carriers, leading to a background-dependent reshaping of the operating current rather than uniform additive integration. This behavior provides direct electrical evidence that the detector no longer performs unipolar spectral summation, but instead implements polarity-dependent spectral weighting of background photons.

Electrical validation and doping-tunable spectral polarity inversion in Ge/BP heterojunctions.

a Photocurrent under 2000 nm excitation as a function of added visible illumination, showing monotonic current increase consistent with unipolar spectral integration. b Photocurrent under 4000 nm excitation, exhibiting visible-illumination-dependent current suppression due to opposite-polarity carrier transport induced by field inversion. c Time-resolved photocurrent responses measured from 2700 to 4200 nm for a device with Ge doping of 1 × 1017 cm−3. A polarity transition at λR ≈ 3.1 μm marks the onset of bidirectional photoresponse driven by light-induced internal electric-field inversion. Inset: schematic band diagram illustrating the photon-energy-dependent field-inversion condition at this doping concentration. d Doping-dependent tunability of the spectral inversion point. Reducing the Ge doping concentration to 6.8 × 1014 cm−3 blue-shifts λR to ≈ 1.95 μm. Inset: schematic band diagram illustrating how reduced Ge doping modifies the photon-energy-dependent field-inversion condition.

To map the wavelength at which this polarity transition occurs, we perform time-resolved photocurrent measurements across the mid-infrared range from 2700 to 4200 nm (Fig. 3c) on a device with a Ge doping concentration of 1 × 1017 cm−3. A clear reversal of photocurrent polarity is observed at λR ≈ 3.1 μm, in agreement with the EQE-derived zero-crossing in Fig. 2d. The shaded region denotes the crossover window associated with light-induced internal electric-field inversion. No measurable response is detected beyond 4200 nm, consistent with the intrinsic absorption edge of BP (Supplementary Fig. 17).

The spectral inversion point is further shown to be structurally tunable through doping engineering. For a device with a reduced Ge doping concentration of 6.8 × 1014 cm−3 (Fig. 3d), λR shifts to ~1.95 μm. This blue-shift arises from an extended depletion width and reduced built-in field, which lower the photon-energy threshold required for hole accumulation and subsequent field inversion. The schematic inset illustrates the corresponding modulation of the band profile. These results demonstrate that spectral polarity inversion is deterministic, electrically resolvable, and tunable through static material parameters, without the need for external gating or dynamic modulation.

Room temperature infrared performance of Ge/BP detector

To evaluate the practical impact of spectral polarity engineering under realistic operating conditions, we characterized the room-temperature performance of the Ge/BP heterojunction under blackbody illumination. Figure 4a shows the net blackbody-induced short-circuit current after subtracting the dark baseline measured with the blackbody radiation blocked. As the blackbody temperature was varied from 250 to 1150 K, the measured current agrees well with the polarity-weighted background photocurrent calculated from the measured signed EQE profile (solid line; Supplementary Note 1), confirming that the mean blackbody-induced response is governed by signed spectral integration of the incident photon flux. The noise spectral density measured at 300 K under zero bias is shown in Fig. 4b. According to inoise(f,B)=∫ff+BSn(f~)df~, the noise current is ~15.2 fA (near 1 Hz bandwidth)18. This low value, close to the measurement baseline, indicates that the device does not introduce pronounced excess current noise under the measurement conditions. We next examine the mean background-induced current offset under 300 K blackbody illumination in the absence of signal light. As shown in Fig. 4c, the measured current density (~1.8 × 10−6 A cm−2) is substantially lower than that expected for conventional integration-type detectors evaluated under the same cutoff wavelength (λcut = 4 μm). By engineering three devices with different polarity-inversion wavelengths λR (~2, 2.5 and 3 μm) via Ge doping control, we observe a systematic reduction of the background operating current as λR red-shifts. The lowest offset is nearly two orders of magnitude below that of the integration case. This trend directly reflects redistribution of background photocurrent enabled by spectral polarity inversion, rather than any reduction of the intrinsic photon-number fluctuation noise benchmark.

Figure 4d compares the specific detectivity D* of the Ge/BP device with three references: the conventional unipolar BLIP reference, the residual photon-number fluctuation noise benchmark, and the offset-equivalent polarity-engineered reference. Despite an external quantum efficiency of only ~10%, the measured D* approaches the benchmark envelope defined for idealized integration-type detectors with unity quantum efficiency, differing by a factor of 4.11 under identical background and optical collection conditions. This proximity arises from reshaping the mean background operating point at the detector, rather than from suppression of stochastic photon fluctuations. The detailed derivation of the Law 19 benchmark curve can be found in Supplementary Note 1. The moderate EQE indicates that the present device is not yet optimized for absorption and carrier extraction, owing to limited effective absorption/collection volume, interfacial recombination losses, and the absence of dedicated optical enhancement structures. This leaves substantial room for further improvement through optimized junction geometry, interface quality, optical-field coupling, and carrier-transport design, without changing the underlying background-offset-cancellation mechanism. We further examined the device performance under finite bias in the polarity-reversed regime (Supplementary Fig. 21). Although the responsivity increases under nonzero bias, the corresponding specific detectivity reaches its maximum near zero bias and decreases at finite bias, indicating that zero-bias operation provides the most favorable low-current and low-noise condition for the present device.

Figure 4e benchmarks the peak D* and EQE of the Ge/BP device against representative uncooled infrared detectors, including III–V, HgCdTe, and van der Waals heterostructures. Although the EQE is comparatively modest, the achieved D* is competitive, underscoring the effectiveness of spectral polarity engineering in reducing background-induced offset without relying solely on high absorption efficiency. Supplementary Fig. 22 provides a broader comparison with representative room temperature infrared photodetectors (partially low-temperature). Figure 4f further illustrates the relationship between noise current and EQE across different detector architectures. In contrast to conventional integration-type detectors, where increased EQE directly increases background-induced current and noise, the Ge/BP device redistributes the background contribution through polarity-engineered response, enabling high signal-to-background contrast even at low EQE.

System-level spectral discrimination

To illustrate the system-level implication of spectral polarity engineering, we build a single-pixel scanning imaging prototype based on the Ge/BP photodetector. Figure 5 is not intended to serve as a direct experimental demonstration of weak-signal detection under a true broadband thermal background. Monochromatic laser excitation is used here to provide a controlled optical condition, allowing the deterministic redistribution of signal and interference contributions to be visualized without the additional complexity of a continuous thermal spectrum. This experiment is therefore not aimed at canceling stochastic photon fluctuations, but at demonstrating polarity-enabled spectral discrimination and deterministic interference rejection at the photoelectric-conversion stage.

a Schematic of the single-pixel scanning imaging setup used to demonstrate spectral discrimination. A heat pipe serves as the mid-infrared target. A visible laser (520 nm) is introduced as a deterministic optical interference, while a mid-infrared laser (4000 nm) provides the target illumination. b Imaging comparison under different illumination conditions. Left group: reference images acquired by a commercial CCD without (left) and with (right) visible interference. Right group: images acquired by the Ge/BP detector without (left) and with (right) visible interference, showing preserved target contrast enabled by polarity-engineered response.

As schematically shown in Fig. 5a, a heat pipe is used as the mid-infrared target. A visible laser (520 nm) is introduced as a controlled optical interferer, while a mid-infrared laser (4000 nm) provides target illumination. Both channels are simultaneously delivered to the detector, enabling direct interrogation of how the device converts spectrally distinct inputs into an electrical output through its intrinsic, polarity-dependent responsivity.

Representative images are shown in Fig. 5b. A commercial CCD resolves the target in the absence of the visible interferer, but the target contrast collapses once the visible illumination is applied, consistent with additive (unipolar) spectral integration. In contrast, the Ge/BP detector preserves target visibility even under strong visible illumination. This behavior arises because the device generates opposite-polarity photocurrents for the visible and mid-infrared channels, so that the interferer contribution is intrinsically counterweighted at the detector, rather than being removed by filtering or downstream subtraction.

We further quantify this spectral discrimination by varying the relative optical power between the visible interference (520 nm) and the mid-infrared illumination (4000 nm) (Fig. 6a(i)). Because the two channels contribute with opposite polarity, increasing the mid-infrared power continuously redistributes the net output (Fig. 6a(ii–iii)). For statistical analysis, five representative images are selected. The fifth image is used to define the signal and background regions, with a threshold set to 5% of its signal intensity; the same spatial masks are then applied to the remaining images for consistency. As shown in Fig. 6a(iv), the signal and background output distributions evolve from strong overlap to clear separation as the mid-infrared power increases. The extracted mean values are summarized in Fig. 6b: the signal mean increases while the background mean decreases, indicating progressive contrast recovery. We note that the measurements are acquired using a lock-in amplifier; the reported values therefore represent the demodulated output referenced to the modulation channel, which can manifest as an apparent enhancement of target contrast even when the target emissivity remains unchanged.

(ii) Optical power ratio between the interfering visible source and the mid-infrared illumination for each image in (i). (iii) Corresponding variation of the detectivity of the detector as the mid-infrared power increases. To verify that the improvement is not dominated by gain or saturation artifacts, we examine the background fluctuations. The background standard deviation decreases monotonically with increasing mid-infrared power (Fig. 6c), providing direct evidence that the interference burden is reduced at the image level. Based on these statistics, the image-level SNR is calculated as SNR=20log(Vsignal,mean−Vbackground,meanσbackground). As shown in Fig. 6d, the SNR increases from −4.15 dB (signal submerged) to 37.72 dB as the mid-infrared contribution dominates. Together, these results demonstrate that spectral polarity engineering enables deterministic redistribution of signal and interference contributions and achieves contrast recovery without optical filtering, temporal modulation, balanced readout, or computational post-processing, transforming the detector from a passive broadband integrator into an active spectral discriminator.

Discussion

In summary, we have demonstrated a spectral polarity engineering strategy that reshapes how broadband background radiation is transduced into electrical signals in infrared photodetectors. By exploiting light-induced internal electric-field inversion in a mixed-dimensional Ge/BP heterojunction, we realize a deterministic polarity-inverted spectral responsivity, enabling intrinsic redistribution and suppression of the mean background-induced photocurrent offset at the photoelectric conversion stage. This reweighting mechanism departs fundamentally from conventional integration-type detection, balanced readout, or post-processing approaches, which operate downstream of photo-carrier generation. As a result, the Ge/BP device achieves room-temperature detectivity approaching the background-limited benchmark despite a modest external quantum efficiency, underscoring that detector performance can be improved not only by enhancing absorption, but also by engineering how background photons are electrically transduced.

Beyond the present device-level performance, our results establish spectral polarity as an additional degree of freedom for infrared detector design. Rather than replacing existing dark-current-suppression strategies, this mechanism is better viewed as complementary to them, because it directly targets the mean background-induced offset under broadband thermal illumination instead of the dark current itself19,20. Because the cancellation occurs within a single device, without requiring dual-channel subtraction or external differential circuitry, the concept is in principle compatible with extension to other heterojunction platforms, wider spectral ranges, focal-plane arrays, and on-chip imaging architectures. The system-level demonstration further illustrates how such polarity-engineered detectors can directly discriminate spectrally distinct signals under strong interference, transforming photodetectors from passive broadband integrators into active spectral discriminators. Looking forward, realizing this potential in practical systems will require further progress in heterojunction uniformity, control of the spectral zero-crossing position, long-term interface stability, and co-optimization with readout circuitry and integrated photonic structures. We therefore envision spectral polarity engineering as a general framework for future infrared sensing, offering opportunities for background-robust detection, compact multispectral imaging, and adaptive optoelectronic systems operating under realistic ambient conditions.

Methods

Device fabrication

Ge/BP heterostructures were fabricated using a dry-transfer process. Few-layer BP flakes were mechanically exfoliated from bulk crystals and transferred onto p-doped Ge substrates to form van der Waals heterojunctions. Prior to BP transfer, the Ge substrates were patterned by laser direct writing and coated with a 60-nm-thick Al2O3 layer deposited by atomic layer deposition (200 °C), serving as an insulating layer between BP and metal contacts. The Ge surface was then exposed by etching in 1% diluted HF for 2 min and immediately transferred into a nitrogen-filled glove box to prevent oxidation. Electrode patterns were defined by electron-beam lithography (FEI F50 SEM equipped with an NPGS system), followed by electron-beam evaporation of Cr/Au (10/50 nm). To minimize degradation from ambient exposure, the completed devices were encapsulated with a thin PMMA layer prior to characterization.

Electrical and optoelectronic measurements

All electrical and optoelectronic measurements were performed at room temperature in a vacuum probe station (~10−4 Pa). Current–voltage characteristics and photoresponse measurements were carried out using a Keysight B2912B source-measure unit. A supercontinuum laser source (Leukos, 900–4800 nm) was used for wavelength-dependent photoresponse measurements, while a blackbody source (Newport Oriel 67000) provided controlled thermal background illumination. Time-resolved photocurrent responses were recorded using a Tektronix MDO3014 oscilloscope. Atomic force microscopy (Asylum Cypher) and Raman spectroscopy (Horiba HR800, 514 nm excitation) were employed to characterize surface morphology and material quality. Photocurrent spectra and noise measurements were calibrated using custom-built optical setups; a schematic of the spectral photocurrent measurement configuration is provided in Supplementary Fig. 23. Noise measurements were performed at room temperature under dark environmental conditions using the same electrical readout configuration. No additional cold shielding or background-suppression optics were introduced during the measurements, so that the low-frequency noise behavior was evaluated under practical room-temperature conditions.

TCAD simulations

Technology computer-aided design (TCAD) simulations were performed to analyze the band alignment, electrostatic potential distribution, carrier redistribution and internal electric-field modulation in the Ge/BP heterojunction. The simulations used a drift–diffusion framework coupled with Poisson’s equation and carrier continuity equations. The input material parameters, including layer thickness, doping concentration, bandgap, electron affinity, relative permittivity and carrier effective masses, were determined from experimental measurements where available or taken from standard material parameters and literature values, as summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The simulations were used to capture the equilibrium band profile and the qualitative trend of light-induced carrier redistribution and field modulation in the Ge/BP heterojunction.

Data analysis

The responsivity, external quantum efficiency, noise current and specific detectivity were extracted from the measured electrical, optical and noise data. The background-induced current was calculated by integrating the measured signed spectral response with the blackbody photon flux. The conventional unipolar background-current reference, photon-number-fluctuation noise benchmark and offset-equivalent polarity-engineered reference were evaluated using the framework described in Supplementary Note 1. The specific detectivity was calculated from the measured responsivity, active detector area and noise current. For the polarity-engineered case, the signed mean background-induced current was used to evaluate background-offset suppression, whereas the photon-number fluctuation benchmark was calculated from the unsigned absorbed background photon flux.
