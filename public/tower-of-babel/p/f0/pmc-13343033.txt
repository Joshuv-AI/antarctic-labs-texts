Interdisciplinary strategies to reduce surgical site infections in operating rooms: a scoping review

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are among the most common healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) and are associated with substantial clinical and economic burden.13 In high-income countries, SSIs affect an estimated 2–4% of surgical patients and account for 20–40% of all HAIs. These infections are associated with considerable morbidity, including an average prolongation of hospital stay by 10 days, readmission rates increasing up to 78% and a 2-fold to 11-fold increase in mortality risk factor.4 5 In the USA, SSIs are considered the costliest form of HAIs, with estimated annual expenditures ranging from US$3.5 to US$10 billion. Each SSI is associated with an increase of over US$20 000 in hospitalisation costs per patient admission.6

In Europe, the incidence of SSIs varies from 0.6% to 9.6% depending on the surgical procedure performed.7 In Switzerland, five national point prevalence surveys conducted by Swissnoso between 2017 and 2023 reported that SSIs accounted for between 20% and 30% of all HAIs.

Bundles of evidence-based perioperative practices (Figure 1) have been shown to reduce SSI rates and improve adherence to prevention standards.8

SSI prevention relies on evidence-based bundles implemented throughout the perioperative pathway (figure 1). Effective application requires consistent teamwork, supported by clear communication, coordination and the use of tools such as checklists and standard operating procedures. Although focusing on a few high-impact practices before incision is effective, sustained SSI reduction remains a challenge despite technical and research advances.9 Swissnoso estimates that 90% adherence to selected perioperative practices could prevent up to 50% of SSIs.10 Key measures include hand hygiene, proper hair removal, skin antisepsis Staphylococcus aureus decolonisation, guideline-based antimicrobial prophylaxis, normothermia and glycaemic control.10 Despite clear international guidelines, adherence remains low due to practical, organisational and cognitive barriers. These barriers often emerge at the interfaces between professional groups. The complexity of the OR environment and the diversity of roles require structured communication and coordination. Globally, only 29% of tertiary hospitals report fully structured infection prevention and control (IPC) programmes in Switzerland; for instance, a 2022 Swissmedic survey revealed persistent gaps in compliance, education, access and interdisciplinary coordination.11 Interdisciplinary collaboration is therefore a critical determinant of effective IPC. Although organisations such as Swissnoso, the WHO, Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America and others offer strong theoretical frameworks, operational guidance for interdisciplinary implementation in surgical settings remains limited. Given the short turnover times and high environmental risk, maintaining adherence remains challenging.12 13 Addressing this issue requires the implementation of efficient interdisciplinary collaboration through structured, team-based strategies integrating evidence-based bundles and communication tools.

However, while perioperative bundles are widely promoted, the extent to which they incorporate explicit coordination mechanisms, task-sharing principles and structural features associated with reliable interdisciplinary implementation remains unclear. High-reliability organisations are characterised by predefined escalation pathways, redundancy mechanisms, shared situational awareness and structured coordination triggers that enable consistent performance under conditions of uncertainty and time pressure.14 15 In such systems, reliability is not achieved solely through technical checklists, but through clearly articulated interaction architectures and anticipatory coordination processes.16

A preliminary review of the literature highlights the absence of recent systematic syntheses addressing this gap, underscoring the need to explore, map and analyse interdisciplinary strategies through an implementation-focused lens to better understand their structural properties and potential contribution to SSI reduction.

Study objective

This scoping review examined the existing body of literature on interdisciplinary strategies to reduce SSIs. It aimed to map and analyse the structural characteristics, coordination mechanisms and implementation features of these strategies across contexts, identify evidence gaps and explore their potential contribution to reliable and sustainable SSI prevention.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and complies with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting guidelines. The protocol was developed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) checklist, reviewed by the research team and published in the Journal of Medical Internet Research.17

Eligibility criteria

This review included studies meeting the following Participants–Concept–Context criteria.

Participants

Surgical team members involved in patient preparation before incision, including surgeons, anaesthesiologists, nurse anaesthetists, registered nurses, operating room (OR) nurses, instrument technicians and aides. Only studies including at least three distinct disciplines were considered, requiring an interdisciplinary approach.

Concept

Interdisciplinary IPC strategies defined a coordinated framework of joint action rather than the simple presence of multiple roles.18 Such strategies included standardised operating procedures, shared mental models, explicit communication tools and visual cues for team alignment. Any study describing at least one of these elements was included.

Context

Adult surgical procedures in the operating theatre, focusing on the ‘turnover’ time between wound closure and the next incision. Studies limited to paediatric surgery were excluded due to the deliberately heterogeneous bundles application linked to age.

Types of sources

We included experimental and quasi-experimental studies (randomised and non-randomised controlled trials, before–and–after studies and interrupted time series), as well as analytical observational studies (prospective or retrospective cohorts, case–control studies and cross-sectional studies). Editorials, commentaries, letters, conference abstracts and grey literature were excluded.

We limited retrieval to studies published between 2016 and 2026—reflecting the period following WHO’s designation of SSI as a global priority. Only articles in English or French were included. A detailed search strategy is provided in ‘CINAHL Search strategy from Dec 2016 to Feb 2026’ and ‘Embase Search strategy from Dec 2016 to Feb 2026’, ‘MEDLINE Search strategy from Dec 2016 to Feb 2026’ and ‘Cochrane Library Search strategy from Dec 2016 to Feb 2026’ online supplemental appendix 1. The search strategy was developed in collaboration with an academic librarian experienced in JBI scoping review methodology. Database searches were subsequently executed and verified in collaboration with a health sciences librarian (Geneva School of Health Sciences) to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Selection sources of evidence

All citations were imported into Rayyan from EndNote, and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were then screened independently by two reviewers, with potentially eligible full texts assessed against the predefined criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, when needed, by a third reviewer. Data extraction and critical appraisal were subsequently performed using JBI checklists to ensure methodological consistency. Five reviewers assessed the full texts independently, systematically documenting the reasons for exclusion. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Data charting process and data items

Five reviewers conducted critical appraisal and data extraction, with the lead author independently reviewing all studies for consistency. A customised tool ‘Data extraction instrument’ online supplemental appendix 2 was developed to capture study characteristics (eg, methods, participants, context, key findings, evidence level). It also charted interdisciplinary collaboration in the OR, guided by the WHO Framework for Action on Interprofessional Education and Collaborative Practice (2010). The tool was tested in a limited number of studies and iteratively refined, for instance, by clarifying the definition and scope of ‘visual’ or ‘verbal’ triggers. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and one study author was contacted for missing information.

Critical appraisal

The methodological quality of included studies was appraised using JBI critical appraisal tools. Various checklists were applied according to study design, with varying maximum scores (eg, 9 or 12 items); a score ≥9 was generally considered indicative of high quality. Scores were not aggregated but used descriptively to inform interpretation. These tools assess validity and risk of bias but do not assign evidence levels.20 This classification was used descriptively to contextualise the strength of evidence and did not imply hierarchical comparison or effect estimation: randomised controlled trials as high, quasi-experimental and cohort studies as moderate, descriptive/observational studies generally low (though some quality improvement (QI) projects may reach moderate depending on quality). This distinction underscores the uneven strength of evidence across designs in SSI prevention research.

Results

A total of 2058 records were identified. After removal of 341 duplicates, 1717 unique records were screened. Following title and abstract screening, 79 full texts were assessed for eligibility and 22 studies were included in the final analysis (figure 2).

Characteristic of sources

This scoping review included 22 studies published between 2016 and 2024, conducted across nine countries. Most originated from high-income settings, with the USA contributing the largest proportion (n=12), followed by Switzerland (n=2) and the UK (n=2).

Few studies explicitly reported formal theoretical or conceptual frameworks underpinning interdisciplinary coordination in SSI prevention. Summary of key findings

Of the 22 studies reviewed, most showed that structured, interdisciplinary surgical site infection prevention programmes improved treatment adherence and reduced SSI rates by up to 50%. However, not all interventions reached statistical significance, with varying impact across specialties, such as colorectal surgery and orthopaedic surgery. Differences were also noted in staff adherence, patient engagement and sustainability. These results suggest that effectiveness depends on contextual adaptation and consistent implementation. Organisational commitment and collaborative models remain key to sustaining SSI prevention.

Discussion

This scoping review mapped interdisciplinary strategies for SSI prevention and found that interdisciplinary interventions, especially perioperative bundles and QI initiatives, improve adherence to infection prevention protocols and often reduce SSI rates. Successful implementation depends on strong leadership, team engagement and system-level strategies, whereas patient involvement and contextually adapted bundles add complementary benefits.

Evidence from both high-income and low-resource settings demonstrates that locally adapted bundles, combined with training and a culture of safety, can achieve significant results. This supports the development of context-sensitive models adaptable to specific surgical environments.

Although the bundles often included practices that aligned with WHO and CDC recommendations (eg, timely administration of antibiotic prophylaxis, skin preparation and normothermia), the composition of the bundles varied considerably. Few studies clearly explained why certain elements were prioritised, suggesting that local teams targeted the practices they deemed to be the most urgent or feasible. This variability reflects the pragmatic adaptation typical of QI initiatives but limits reproducibility.

Our analysis suggests that institutional or locally driven QI programmes more frequently reported measurable reductions in SSI rates, whereas initiatives explicitly aligned with WHO/CDC/national guidelines tended to focus on process-oriented outcomes such as compliance and adherence. This highlights a complementary relationship: international/national policies provide a standardised framework and promote safety culture, whereas local institutional projects often achieve direct, tangible impacts on SSI reduction.

The majority of the 22 included studies originated from the USA. It is important to interpret the variability in bundle effectiveness in light of the financial penalties associated with reimbursement under the Hospital-Acquired Condition Reduction Programme.44

These penalties target hospitals rather than individual surgeons and result in reduced reimbursement if specific performance thresholds are not met, which may influence the reported outcomes in US hospitals.45 Such policy-driven effects highlight the importance of contextual factors when comparing outcomes internationally.

Crucial elements such as task sequencing and synchronisation, essential for prophylaxis, skin antisepsis, environmental cleaning and thermal management—as well as hair removal, which is no longer recommended—were often overlooked. While improvements in bundle adherence and SSI rates were frequently reported, the literature offers limited specification of how interdisciplinary coordination was structurally enacted. Bundles and checklists were described, but the interactional architecture required for reliable performance, such as task sequencing, synchronisation mechanisms and escalation triggers, was seldom articulated. Cognitive aids were common yet rarely analysed as tools for real-time interdisciplinary alignment. Overall, implementation appears predominantly technical rather than explicitly systemic.

Nursing care concepts were rarely addressed despite nurses’ central role in change, while patient partnership remained largely limited to education rather than formal integration into bundles. Standardisation often relied on mandatory checklists, which did not necessarily ensure effective team synchronisation. In contrast, successful interventions were supported by strong leadership, multidisciplinary collaboration and systematic audits, whereas evidence from resource-limited settings showed that adapted bundles, training and safety culture can still yield meaningful results. Key challenges include bundle heterogeneity, compliance gaps and sustainability, pointing to the need for future models that explicitly integrate cognitive aids, interdisciplinary triggers and task-sharing principles inspired by aviation safety frameworks.

Limitations

A major limitation is the language restriction to English and French, which may have introduced selection bias and contributed to the over-representation of US studies. The evidence base remains predominantly composed of quasi-experimental and observational designs, limiting causal inference, whereas the absence of studies from low-income countries limits global applicability. Grey literature was not included in this review. Although scoping reviews often consider grey literature to capture quality improvement initiatives, preliminary exploration showed substantial heterogeneity and limited methodological reporting in these sources. The review therefore focused on peer-reviewed studies to ensure sufficient methodological detail for structured data extraction and analysis.

Despite standardised definitions, outcome heterogeneity and variation in implementation fidelity reduce comparability across studies. Nursing roles are often described in operational terms, patient engagement remains mostly informational and standardisation through mandates or checklists does not foster genuine coordination. Even though no validated interdisciplinary framework has yet demonstrated consistent improvements in adherence and outcomes, the overall body of evidence shows encouraging and convergent trends across specialties and settings. These trends reinforce the value of interdisciplinary collaboration as a cornerstone of effective SSI prevention.

Conclusion

This scoping review shows that interdisciplinary, bundle-based interventions are generally associated with improved adherence to infection-prevention practices and measurable reductions in SSI rates. However, effectiveness remains variable across specialties and contexts, and no validated interdisciplinary framework has yet demonstrated consistent improvements in both adherence and patient outcomes.

Despite this, convergent positive trends across health systems reinforce the central role of interdisciplinary collaboration in SSI prevention.

Critical components such as task sequencing, synchronisation and team coordination triggers, which are well established in other high-reliability domains like aviation, are rarely operationalised in surgical settings.

Future research should therefore focus on developing and testing structured, context-sensitive frameworks that integrate synchronisation mechanisms, task-sharing principles, cognitive aids and patient partnership.

Such frameworks may offer an essential basis for developing sustainable, team-based strategies for SSI prevention, potentially contributing to more consistent and system-level improvements in patient safety.
