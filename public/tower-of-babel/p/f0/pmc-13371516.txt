The diagnostic value of MRE in patients with decompensated cirrhosis: comparing it to Gd-EOB-DTPA–enhanced MRI
Authors: Mengjiao Duan, Ziqing Sun, Qiqi Chu, Mengyue Huang, Feifei Yao, Yingyu Che, Yong Zhang, Kaiyu Wang, Jingjing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEstablishing an early and accurate diagnostic approach for identifying decompensation in cirrhosis patients is essential. This study compares the diagnostic value of liver shear stiffness (LSS) measurements from magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) with relative liver enhancement (RLE) measurements from hepatobiliary phase images of gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI in patients with decompensated cirrhosis.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled 79 cirrhotic patients who underwent gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI and MRE, dividing them into two groups: compensated cirrhosis (n = 35) and decompensated cirrhosis (n = 44). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation of the LSS and RLE with the Child-Pugh scoring system and the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scoring system in cirrhotic patients. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis assessed the diagnostic performance of LSS in detecting decompensated cirrhosis, comparing it with RLE. The diagnostic performance of the combined parameters of LSS and RLE was also assessed. Multivariate logistic regression identified factors associated with decompensation.ResultsSpearman’s rank correlation analysis identified that LSS and RLE were both significantly correlated with Child-Pugh and MELD scores (all p values < 0.001). LSS demonstrated significantly higher diagnostic performance than RLE for identifying decompensated cirrhosis (area under the ROC curve [AUROC]: 0.90 [95% CI: 0.83–0.97] vs. 0.77 [95% CI: 0.66–0.87], p = 0.015). However, adding RLE to LSS did not significantly improve diagnostic performance (AUROC: 0.90 [95% CI: 0.83–0.97], p = 0.947). The optimal LSS threshold for distinguishing between compensated and decompensated cirrhosis was 4.57 kPa. LSS was identified as an independent risk factor for decompensated cirrhosis, both unadjusted and after controlling for albumin levels, platelet count, AST, and RLE (OR = 4.31, 95% CI: 1.78–10.41).ConclusionLSS measured by MRE is an independent risk factor for decompensated cirrhosis and demonstrates superior diagnostic performance compared to RLE. Additionally, compared to MRE alone, there is no significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy for decompensated cirrhosis when combining RLE.Keywords: Decompensated cirrhosis, Liver shear stiffness, Magnetic resonance elastography, Relative liver enhancement, Diagnostic performance

Introduction

Cirrhosis is the final pathological outcome of various chronic liver diseases. It is currently one of the most common causes of death globally, contributing significantly to economic and health burdens worldwide [1]. There are two distinct stages of cirrhosis: compensated and decompensated cirrhosis [2]. Most deaths and poor prognoses in patients with compensated cirrhosis result from progression to a decompensated state and its subsequent complications. The probability of transitioning from compensated to decompensated cirrhosis varies between 4% and 12% per year [3–5]. Decompensated cirrhosis is diagnosed based on clinical events such as ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, or esophageal variceal bleeding, which lead to unfavorable outcomes. Therefore, establishing an early and accurate diagnostic approach for identifying decompensation in cirrhosis patients is essential. While liver biopsy has been the gold standard for diagnosing cirrhosis, it is often impractical for monitoring disease status due to its invasive nature and associated risks.

In light of the limitations of liver biopsy, several non-invasive tools have been developed, including serum markers, gadoxetic acid-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), transient elastography, and magnetic resonance elastography (MRE). MRE is a non-invasive, quantitative imaging technique that indirectly measures tissue stiffness by assessing its mechanical properties. Recent studies have shown that MRE is considered the most robust and accurate non-invasive method for estimating liver fibrosis and predicting the future development of cirrhosis and liver-related outcomes [6–8]. Gadolinium ethoxybenzyl-diethylenetriamine penta acetic acid (Gd-EOB-DTPA) is a paramagnetic, hepatocyte-specific contrast agent, with approximately 50% uptake by hepatocytes before being excreted into biliary canaliculi [9]. The elimination pathways of Gd-EOB-DTPA are linked to hepatocyte function integrity, and suboptimal uptake indicates impaired liver function [10]. Several studies have demonstrated the role of gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI in assessing liver function and predicting liver cirrhosis and related outcomes [11–14]. Our study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic value of LSS measurements based on MRE and relative liver enhancement (RLE) measurements on hepatobiliary phase images of gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI for detecting decompensated cirrhosis.

Methods

Study participants

A total of 107 subjects who underwent liver examinations using gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI and MRE from August 2022 to December 2024 were included in this study. This prospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The exclusion criteria were: (1) technical failure of the MRE sequence, (2) presence of liver tumors or prior liver transplantation, and (3) laboratory examinations not performed within one month of the MRI examination.

Decompensated cirrhosis was defined as the presence of at least one decompensation event, including esophageal variceal bleeding, ascites, or hepatic encephalopathy. The diagnosis of decompensation was established through clinical assessment rather than solely on radiological findings (e.g., minimal ascites on imaging). Patients with cirrhosis who did not experience any of these events were classified as having compensated cirrhosis.

The causes of liver cirrhosis included hepatitis B virus (n = 49), hepatitis C virus (n = 4), alcoholic liver disease (n = 2), primary biliary cirrhosis (n = 4), non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (n = 1), autoimmune hepatitis (n = 3), and unknown causes (n = 16).

Relevant laboratory data, including albumin (Alb), total bilirubin, platelet count, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), were collected, the Child-Pugh score and the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score were calculated within 30 days of the MRE exam.

The serum levels of fibrosis markers were measured: the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index, the AST-to-platelet ratio index (APRI), and the AST/ALT ratio (AAR). The FIB-4 index was calculated as follows: age (years) × AST (U/L) / (platelets [109 /L] × [ALT (U/L)]1/2). APRI was calculated using the following formula: [(AST (U/L) / upper normal limit) × 100] / platelets (109 /L). AAR was calculated by AST (U/L) / ALT (U/L).

Magnetic resonance examination

MRI scans were performed on a 3T clinical MR scanner (Discovery 750, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI) equipped with an eight-channel phased-array body coil. Patients fasted for 4–6 h before MRI scans, and imaging was performed in the supine position.

MR elastography imaging and data processing

MRE examination was performed before the routine liver MRI. A passive driver was secured above the right lower ribcage, over the liver, using a circumferential elastic band. Continuous sound pressure waves were generated by the active driver assembly outside the scanner and transmitted to the passive driver via a long plastic tube. The mechanical vibration frequency was 60 Hz. The propagating shear waves were encoded using motion-encoding gradients (MEGs) synchronized with the mechanical vibrations, with MEG timing set to enable phase-sensitive encoding of shear wave motion. Imaging was performed using an axial spin-echo echo-planar imaging (SE-EPI) sequence, with four phase offsets and three alternating orthogonal motion-encoding gradients. Scanning was performed during an end-expiratory breath hold, with each hold lasting 19 s, and the process was repeated six times. The parameters for the 3D SE-EPI MRE were as follows: repetition time (TR) / echo time (TE) = 1375 ms / 39.4 ms; matrix = 96 × 96; number of excitations (NEX) = 1; slice thickness = 3.5 mm; 32 slices. After MRE image acquisition, wave data were post-processed immediately by the system, with processed images generated within 1–3 min after scanning. The composite shear modulus was calculated using the Helmholtz equation for direct inversion, allowing for the extraction of mechanical parameters. LSS was measured on MRE images using MRE-Q2 software by two experienced radiologists (10 and 5 years of abdominal imaging experience, respectively) who were blinded to clinical and biochemical data. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually drawn on the liver parenchyma across 28 slices, as large as possible, while avoiding areas with a confidence index of less than 95%, large vessels, liver edges, artifacts, and fissures. Representative images are shown in Fig. 1. The mean LSS was calculated from all 28 slices.

Gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI and data processing

The routine liver imaging protocol comprised axial T2-weighted imaging, axial T1-weighted imaging, axial diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) T1-weighted imaging following the administration of gadoxetic acid. A 3D T1-weighted breath-hold fat-suppressed spoiled 3D gradient-recalled echo sequence, known as Liver Acquisition with Volume Acceleration (LAVA) flex, was obtained both before and after the intravenous administration of gadoxetic acid at a rate of 1 mL/sec, corresponding to a dose of 0.025 mmol/kg, followed by a 20 mL saline flush with a fixed delay of 20 s. DCE-T1-weighted imaging was performed, including unenhanced phase, hepatic arterial phase, portal venous phase, transitional phase, and hepatobiliary phase (HBP) imaging over 20 min.

The mean signal intensity (SI) was measured on T1-weighted images before and after gadoxetic acid administration by two experienced radiologists (10 and 5 years of abdominal imaging experience, respectively) who were blinded to clinical and biochemical data. ROIs were manually drawn on pre-contrast T1-weighted images (T1w-PRE) and 20 min after contrast administration during the hepatobiliary phase (T1w-HBP). The size and location of the ROIs in pre- and post-contrast images were kept consistent, and the average SI was used for analysis. Efforts were made to place the ROIs in the same position in the liver for each patient, with four ROIs in each of the four liver lobes, while avoiding large vessels, liver lesions, and artifacts. The RLE was calculated using the formula: RLE=(SIHBP-SIPRE)/SIPRE [15], where SIHBP represents the SI on the hepatobiliary-phase images and SIPRE represents the SI on the pre-contrast T1-weighted images.

Statistical analysis

The Chi-square test was used to compare dichotomous variables. Continuous variables were compared using either the independent t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Interobserver agreement for LSS and RLE was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted to identify the correlation of LSS and RLE with Child-Pugh and MELD scores of liver cirrhosis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of LSS, RLE, the combined parameters of LSS and RLE, MELD, and serum fibrosis markers in detecting decompensated cirrhosis. The optimal threshold value of LSS was determined using the Youden index based on ROC analysis. The areas under the curves (AUCs) for the MRE and all other indices were compared using the DeLong nonparametric method. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with decompensation. All data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics v27.0, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between 2022 and 2024, 79 patients with cirrhosis underwent gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI and MRE. Among these, 35 patients (44.3%) had compensated cirrhosis, while 44 patients (55.7%) had decompensated cirrhosis. The study cohort had a mean age of 51.1 ± 11.9 years, with 57 males (72.2%) and 22 females (27.8%). Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among the subjects with hepatic decompensation, 10 patients (22.7%) had a current or previous history of variceal bleeding, 40 patients (90.9%) had ascites, and 9 patients (20.5%) had hepatic encephalopathy. The two groups differed in Alb levels, total bilirubin, platelet count, AST, Child-Pugh and MELD scores (Table 1). No significant differences were found in age, sex, or ALT levels. Patients with decompensated cirrhosis had a significantly higher median LSS of 5.2 kPa (interquartile range [IQR] 4.7–7.4) compared to 3.7 kPa (IQR 2.7–4.4) in the compensated cirrhosis group (Table 1; Fig. 2A). Additionally, patients with compensated cirrhosis had a significantly higher median RLE% of 70.0 (IQR 59.5–88.4) compared to 50.2 (IQR 36.5–64.4) in the decompensated cirrhosis group (Table 1; Fig. 2B).

Correlation of LSS and RLE with Child-Pugh and MELD scores of liver cirrhosis

LSS and RLE were both significantly correlated with Child-Pugh and MELD scores (all p values < 0.05), as identified by Spearman’s rank correlation analysis (Table 2). RLE was significantly negatively correlated with Child-Pugh and MELD scores (r = -0.62; p < 0.001 and r = -0.51; p < 0.001, respectively), while LSS was significantly positively correlated with Child-Pugh and MELD scores (r = 0.59; p < 0.001 and r = 0.40; p < 0.001, respectively).

Diagnostic accuracy of MRE, RLE, MELD, and serum fibrosis markers in diagnosing decompensated cirrhosis

The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) values for diagnosing decompensated cirrhosis using LSS, RLE, MELD, FIB-4 index, APRI, and AAR were 0.90 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.83–0.97), 0.77 (95% CI: 0.66–0.87), 0.76 (95% CI: 0.65–0.86), 0.82 (95% CI: 0.73–0.92), 0.77 (95% CI: 0.66–0.87), and 0.68 (95% CI: 0.57–0.80), respectively (Table 3). The diagnostic accuracy of MRE was significantly better than RLE (p = 0.015), MELD (p = 0.019), APRI (p = 0.014), and AAR (p = 0.003), and was not significantly different from the FIB-4 index (p = 0.188). To evaluate the incremental value of imaging metrics, we constructed combined models. The combination of LSS and FIB-4 yielded an AUC of 0.94, which was significantly higher than FIB-4 alone (p = 0.006), but not significantly different from LSS alone (p = 0.094). Similarly, the combination of LSS and MELD improved diagnostic performance compared with MELD alone (AUC = 0.90 vs. AUC = 0.76, p = 0.003), but this improvement was not statistically significant when compared to LSS alone (AUC = 0.90 vs. AUC = 0.90, p = 0.736). When RLE was added to FIB‑4 or MELD, the combined models showed no significant improvement in AUC compared with individual metrics (all p > 0.05).

Comparison and combination of the accuracy of RLE and LSS in diagnosing decompensated cirrhosis

Interobserver reproducibility was excellent for both LSS and RLE, with ICC values of 0.956 (95% CI: 0.931–0.971) and 0.917 (95% CI: 0.862–0.949), respectively. We evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of the LSS, RLE, and the combination of the two parameters in predicting decompensated cirrhosis, yielding AUROC curves of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.83–0.97) for LSS, 0.77 (95% CI: 0.66–0.87) for RLE, and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.83–0.97) for the combination (Fig. 3). The optimal threshold of LSS for distinguishing between the two conditions was 4.57 kPa, with a sensitivity of 90.9% and a specificity of 82.9%. According to DeLong nonparametric method, the AUCs of these two parameters were significantly different (p < 0.05). The combination of the two parameters does not result in a statistically significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy compared to MRE (p = 0.947).

Independent risk factors for decompensated cirrhosis

Logistic regression analysis identified only LSS as a significant independent risk factor associated with decompensation (Table 4). After adjusting for AST, albumin, platelet count, total bilirubin, and RLE, mean LSS (OR = 4.31, 95% CI: 1.78–10.41) was independently associated with decompensated cirrhosis.

Discussion

In this study, we found that both MRE and gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy for diagnosing decompensated cirrhosis (AUROC = 0.90 [95% CI: 0.83–0.97] for LSS vs. AUROC = 0.77 [95% CI: 0.66–0.87] for RLE) (Fig. 3). Besides, LSS measured by MRE shows superior accuracy compared to RLE based on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI. These findings may be attributed to the fact that liver parenchyma stiffness values obtained through MRE more directly represent architectural distortion in cirrhosis. The elimination pathways of Gd-EOB-DTPA are linked to hepatocyte functional integrity, and suboptimal uptake indicates impaired liver function [10]. Therefore, it primarily reflects hepatocyte function rather than architectural distortion in patients with cirrhosis.

In patients with cirrhosis, chronic hepatic injury from various factors leads to complex inflammatory processes and fibrosis. Damage and repair reactions occur simultaneously, activating hepatic stellate cells to proliferate and produce substantial extracellular matrix, resulting in scar formation and the development of liver fibrosis [16]. Ultimately, liver cirrhosis develops as a result of various pathological processes, leading to structural changes in the liver that alter the stiffness of the liver parenchyma. The decreased liver SI observed in patients with severe liver disease during the hepatobiliary phase may be attributed to reduced uptake of Gd-EOB-DTPA. Patients with advanced cirrhosis often experience delayed uptake of Gd-EOB-DTPA due to increased fibrosis, which collectively contributes to a diminished enhancement effect of Gd-EOB-DTPA on the hepatobiliary phase of the liver parenchyma [17]. A study has shown that the uptake of Gd-EOB-DTPA in liver parenchyma primarily depends on preserved hepatocyte function rather than the severity of morphological changes in liver cirrhosis [18]. Consequently, gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI provides a more indirect method for detecting decompensated cirrhosis compared to MRE. Further analysis in our study identified LSS as an independent risk factor for decompensated cirrhosis, while RLE was not. Furthermore, although serum fibrosis markers can effectively suggest the decompensated condition of liver cirrhosis, MRE has higher diagnostic efficacy, with the advantages of not requiring repeated blood collection or contrast injection. To further evaluate the incremental value of imaging metrics beyond conventional clinical scores, we constructed combined predictive models. Adding LSS to MELD or FIB‑4 significantly improved AUROC compared with the clinical scores alone. However, neither combined model achieved a statistically significant improvement over LSS alone. These findings indicate that LSS alone provides excellent discriminatory ability for identifying decompensated cirrhosis. In contrast, RLE showed relatively lower diagnostic performance and did not offer meaningful incremental value when combined with LSS or standard clinical scores.

Several previous studies have investigated the role of MRE as a significant predictor of future decompensation, related clinical events, or death in patients with liver cirrhosis [6, 19–22]. A study demonstrated that LSS measured by MRE has high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy at a cutoff value of 6.48 kPa for diagnosing decompensated cirrhosis in a cohort of patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [19]. Asrani et al. [6] found that LSS value greater than 5.8 kPa was associated with future decompensation, indicating that elevated LSS can help identify patients at risk for future hepatic decompensation. Other studies have also shown that LSS is a noninvasive method for accurately stratifying the risk of future decompensation in patients with cirrhosis, regardless of the underlying cause, including primary sclerosing cholangitis and NAFLD [20–22]. Our study results further demonstrated that LSS has excellent diagnostic performance in detecting decompensated cirrhosis, with sensitivity of 90.9% and specificity of 82.9% at a cutoff value of 4.57 kPa. However, this cutoff value differs from the 6.48 kPa observed by Han et al. [19] in differentiating between compensated and decompensated cirrhosis. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in the underlying causes of liver disease among the study subjects. Additionally, methodological differences in MRE acquisition (such as the use of 2D MRE versus 3D MRE) may also contribute to variations. Prior studies have shown that LSS may differ between 2D and 3D acquisition techniques, although the diagnostic performance was equivalent for liver fibrosis staging [23, 24].

Several previous studies have demonstrated the value of gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI in evaluating liver function [25–29]. Additionally, strong associations have been found between gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI and the stage of liver fibrosis, as well as between gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI and the severity of liver cirrhosis determined by pathological results [17, 30, 31]. Our results also indicated that RLE could effectively distinguish between compensated and decompensated states in patients with cirrhosis, with an AUROC of 0.77. However, the diagnostic performance of our study is not as robust as that reported by Lu et al. [32], where the liver lobe-based hepatic uptake index (HUI) on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI distinguished between compensatory and non-compensatory states in patients with hepatitis B-related cirrhosis, achieving an AUROC of 0.87. We believe this discrepancy may be attributed to two factors. First, the study populations varied: our cohort included patients with liver disease from multiple causes, while the previous study focused solely on those with hepatitis B. Second, the calculation formulas for the indicators differed; our study did not account for volumetric changes in the liver lobes.

Given the close relationship between the risk and speed of progression from compensated to decompensated liver cirrhosis and patient prognosis, early detection and prediction of disease progression in liver cirrhosis patients using non-invasive diagnostic tools are crucial. Currently, several non-invasive methods for liver disease detection have been extensively evaluated and validated. These include various serum markers [33–35], ultrasound-based imaging techniques such as transient elastography (TE) and two-dimensional shear wave elastography [36, 37], as well as MR-based imaging methods like gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI [25–29] and MRE [6–8, 20–22]. Among these, MRI-based methods are particularly advantageous for liver disease evaluation due to their multiparametric assessment capabilities. Recently, MRE has emerged as the most promising tool for non-invasive detection and staging of liver fibrosis [38] and for predicting the future progression of liver cirrhosis [6]. The fundamental principle of MRE involves measuring shear wave propagation; as liver stiffness increases, both the wave propagation velocity and wavelength increase [39]. This measurement of liver stiffness changes aids in evaluating disease severity [40, 41]. Additionally, MRE offers several advantages over ultrasound-based elastography: it demonstrates superior repeatability and reproducibility with reduced operator dependence [42–46], assesses the stiffness of the entire liver rather than just localized areas as TE does [47], and is unaffected by the presence of ascites or obesity.

Our study has several limitations. First, including patients with chronic liver disease from multiple causes could skew the results, as the measured values may vary based on disease type and be influenced by inflammation. Second, our sample size was small, necessitating validation of our findings with a larger patient population. Third, our study did not include 2D MRE acquisition, further investigations are warranted to directly compare 2D and 3D MRE for assessing liver stiffness. Finally, we did not conduct follow-up assessments, so the ability of LSS to predict cirrhosis decompensation requires further evaluation.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that LSS measured by MRE and RLE from gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI demonstrate good diagnostic performance in identifying decompensated cirrhosis. LSS outperforms RLE in diagnostic accuracy and serves as an independent risk factor for decompensated cirrhosis. Additionally, compared to MRE alone, there is no significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy for decompensated cirrhosis when combining RLE.
