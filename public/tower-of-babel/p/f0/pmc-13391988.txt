ATOH1 and NPAS1 are immune related prognostic biomarkers in colorectal cancer
Authors: Zeyang Fang, Gaichao Hong, Guandi Wu, Zhiqiang Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBasic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors are implicated in tumorigenesis, but their comprehensive role in colorectal cancer (CRC) remains unclear. This study aims to systematically characterize the bHLH family in CRC.MethodsTranscriptomic and clinical data from TCGA COAD/READ cohorts were analyzed as a discovery set and validated with GEO datasets (GSE39582, GSE17538). Differential expression, Kaplan-Meier survival, and Cox regression analyses identified prognostic bHLH genes. A prognostic risk model was constructed, and immune infiltration was assessed via TIMER2.0. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks and methylation associations were explored using STRING and MEXPRESS.ResultsWe identified 36 and 32 differentially expressed bHLH genes in colon and rectal adenocarcinoma, respectively. ATOH1 was an independent protective factor (HR = 0.467, P = 0.005), while NPAS1 was an independent risk factor (HR = 2.327, P = 0.001) for overall survival. ATOH1 expression positively correlated with CD8 + T-cell infiltration, whereas NPAS1 showed a negative correlation. A prognostic model constructed using ATOH1 and NPAS1, which remained significant in the multivariate regression analysis, together with T stage, N/M stage, and MSI status demonstrated moderate predictive efficacy (AUC = 0.796). PPI network analysis suggested ATOH1 involvement in WNT/β-catenin signaling and NPAS1 in hypoxia-response pathways.ConclusionsThe first systematic analysis of the expression, prognosis and immune correlation of bHLH family in CRC. bHLH family members, particularly ATOH1 and NPAS1, represent potential biomarkers for prognosis and immune microenvironment evaluation in CRC. This integrated analysis provides a foundation for developing bHLH-targeted therapies and informs future immunotherapy strategies.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, BHLH transcription factors, ATOH1, NPAS1, Tumor-infiltrating immune cells, Prognostic biomarker

Introduction

Basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors constitute a large protein family characterized by a conserved helix-loop-helix (HLH) domain essential for protein-protein interactions. Typically, these factors possess an HLH domain adjacent to a basic region required for DNA binding. They recognize and bind E-BOX DNA sequences through homodimer or heterodimer formation, regulating critical processes including cell cycle progression, differentiation, and proliferation [1, 2]. Based on structural features, the bHLH family is subdivided into seven classes [1–3]: Class I (widely expressed, e.g., E-proteins), Class II (tissue-specific expression, e.g., myogenic regulatory factors), Class III (bHLH proteins with a C-terminal leucine zipper[LZ] motif, such as the MYC family), Class IV (bHLH proteins also containing a leucine zipper, typically antagonistic to Class III, such as the MAD family), Class V (lacking a functional basic region and inhibiting DNA binding of other bHLH proteins via heterodimerization), Class VI(basic region containing proline residues), and Class VII (containing a C-terminal PAS domain).

BHLH family members exhibit diverse functions and play significant roles in tumorigenesis and progression. Studies demonstrate that conditional HIF-1α (a bHLH-PAS protein) knockout in mammary epithelium suppresses tumor cell proliferation, delays tumor growth, and reduces lung metastasis [4]. TCF21 acts as a tumor suppressor in various cancers, primarily by downregulating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway [5, 6]; however, its specific role in CRC remains to be fully elucidated. The Class I member E2A inhibits epithelial-mesenchymal transition, proliferation, and migration in colorectal cancer (CRC) cells by downregulating YAP (Yes-Associated Protein) [7]. In breast cancer, Id4 protein induces tumor angiogenesis by binding to and stabilizing mRNA of pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., IL-8, GRO-α), thereby promoting their mRNA translation [8]. Overexpression of the bHLH-LZ member MYCN in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, glioblastoma multiforme, and breast cancer correlates with poor prognosis [9]. However, the expression profile, prognostic significance, biological functions, and underlying mechanisms of bHLH family members in CRC require systematic elucidation.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically characterize bHLH gene family expression patterns in CRC, assess their prognostic relevance and association with immune infiltration using integrated bioinformatics approaches, and thereby establish a theoretical foundation for developing novel diagnostic markers and targeted therapeutic strategies for CRC.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The datasets analyzed in this study are publicly available. Transcriptomic and corresponding clinical data for colorectal cancer were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) on February 2025, including COAD and READ cohorts. Gene expression datasets GSE39582 and GSE17538 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) on February 2025.

All data preprocessing, normalization, differential expression analysis, survival analysis, immune infiltration analysis, and figure generation were performed using R (version 4.5.2). The data preprocessing procedures and analysis scripts supporting the conclusions of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Expression analysis of BHLH family genes in colorectal cancer and normal tissues

We analyzed expression differences of BHLH family genes between normal colorectal tissues (from matched TCGA or GEO controls) and colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) or rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) samples using the unpaired Wilcoxon test. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with the thresholds of |log2(fold change)| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.001.

Association of BHLH family genes with colorectal cancer patient survival

We employed the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test to assess the association between BHLH family gene expression and overall survival (OS) in CRC patients. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to determine independent prognostic value, estimating hazard ratios(HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. External validation was performed with the independent GEO datasets GSE39582 (n = 585) and GSE17538 (n = 244).

Construction of a BHLH family gene prognostic risk model for CRC

A prognostic risk model was constructed using independent prognostic variables identified by multivariate Cox regression-including significant BHLH DEGs, TNM stage (Stage III + IV vs. I + II), age (70 years vs. ≥ 70 years), and maximum tumor diameter (< 2 cm vs. ≥ 2 cm). `The model was visualized with Receiver Operating Characteristic.

Pan-cancer expression analysis of BHLH family genes

Pan-cancer expression analysis for the six candidate DEGs (ATOH1, NPAS1, CLOCK, SREBF2, TCF15, MLXIPL) was performed in TCGA cohorts, evaluating their expression across 21 additional tumor types as listed in the Supplementary Appendix.

Immune infiltration analysis of BHLH family genes

Tumor-infiltrating immune cell abundance was quantified using TIMER2.0 [10] based on the CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm. Spearman’s correlation analysis was applied to evaluate the associations between BHLH gene expression (including ATOH1) and the infiltration levels of CD8+T cells, CD4+T cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and neutrophils. Tumor purity was adjusted for in all analyses using partial Spearman’s correlation as implemented in TIMER2.0.

Protein-protein interaction and methylation analysis

PPI networks were constructed for BHLH family genes using STRING [11]. Gene methylation status as well as its association with clinical characteristics were analyzed using MEXPRESS [12], integrating TCGA methylation and clinical data.

In READ, elevated expression of TFAP4, SREBF2, and TCF24 was correlated with improved overall survival (OS) (Fig. 2A and C). Specifically, in the COAD cohort, univariate analysis indicated that eight genes (ATOH1, NPAS1, NPAS4, HES4, HES7, HEY1, SREBF1, USF2) were significantly associated with OS. Performance evaluation of the BHLH prognostic model

According to multivariate Cox regression analyses in CRC cohort (Table 1), we constructed a prognostic model incorporating six CRC-associated BHLH genes (ATOH1, NPAS1, CLOCK, SREBF2, TCF15, MLXIPL). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.636, with sensitivity and specificity of 56.56% and 70.61%, respectively (Fig. 6B), reflecting only modest predictive capacity for overall survival. These results emphasize the need for further model optimization or integration of additional biomarkers to enhance prognostic performance.

Validation using GSE39582 and GSE17538 datasets

The prognostic value of candidate genes (ATOH1, NPAS1, CLOCK, SREBF2, TCF15, MLXIPL) was further validated using univariate survival analyses in the independent datasets GSE39582 and GSE17538 (Table 2). In GSE39582, higher ATOH1 expression was significantly associated with improved overall survival in CRC patients (P < 0.05, Fig. 6A), consistent with results from the TCGA cohort. In GSE17538, elevated MLXIPL expression exhibited a protective effect on patient survival (P < 0.05, Fig. 6B). Furthermore, considering that the integrated prognostic model showed better predictive performance than single‑gene survival analysis, we conducted ROC curve analyses in the GSE39582 and GSE17538 cohorts using a risk score model that combined ATOH1 and NPAS1 and incorporated T stage, N/M stage, and MSI status. ATOH1 and NPAS1 were selected because they remained significant in multivariate Cox regression analysis. The results (Fig. 7A-B) showed that the AUC values were 0.726 in GSE39582 and 0.682 in GSE17538, indicating that the model provides moderate discriminative ability for predicting overall survival.

ATOH1 was significantly downregulated in COAD and READ tumors relative to normal tissues, suggesting a tumor-suppressive function in colorectal malignancies. No significant expression difference was apparent in ESCA or PRAD.

CLOCK showed cancer-type-dependent regulation-upregulated in COAD yet downregulated in READ, with further notable overexpression in CHOL, LIHC, SARC, and SKCM.

MLXIPL presented tissue-specific bidirectional expression: downregulated in CHOL, PCPG, and KICH, implying a tumor-suppressive role, whereas upregulated in COAD, READ, STAD, and THCA, supporting a potential oncogenic effect.

NPAS1 was consistently upregulated in GBM, KICH, PRAD, READ, and STAD, but downregulated in most other tumor types, highlighting a possible dual and context-dependent function.

SREBF2 exhibited cancer-specific suppression, with significant downregulation in COAD, KIRC, and GBM and descending trends in LIHC and SKCM; no significant differential expression was seen in READ.

TCF15 displayed contextual expression roles, being upregulated in CHOL and ESCA but without significant change in PRAD, READ, or COAD.

Correlation of BHLH genes with immune cell infiltration in CRC

Given the critical role of the immune microenvironment in colorectal cancer, we assessed the correlation between expression levels of six key BHLH genes and tumor-infiltrating immune cells using the CIBERSORT abs.mode (Fig. 9). Correlation analysis revealed a spectrum of association strengths, ranging from robust signatures to more subtle patterns that warrant cautious interpretation.

Comprehensive immune infiltration profiling in CRC cohorts from TCGA revealed significant associations between ATOH1, NPAS1, CLOCK, SREBF2, TCF15, and MLXIPL expression levels and tumor microenvironment composition. Blue indicates negative correlation, red indicates positive correlation, with color depth proportional to the strength of association

MLXIPL and TCF15 exhibited the most pronounced and distinct immunological landscapes. MLXIPL expression was strongly and broadly negatively correlated with a vast array of immune populations, including B cells, macrophages (M0/M2), NK cells, and T-cell subsets, strongly suggesting a role in fostering an immune-excluded or suppressed milieu. In contrast, TCF15 showed a striking positive correlation with pro-inflammatory macrophages (M1) and macrophages M0/M2, while exhibiting predominantly negative correlations with plasma B cells and neutrophils, consistent with a role in modulating myeloid polarization.

However, interpreting the landscape for other factors requires caution due to generally lower correlation coefficients. As noted in the heatmap, CLOCK presented a largely subdued profile with weak associations across most immune subsets; while faint positive trends were observed with resting memory CD4 + T cells and macrophages M1, these signals were modest, suggesting that CLOCK may exert a limited or highly context-dependent influence on immune infiltration rather than a dominant regulatory role. Similarly, SREBF2 demonstrated scattered and weak interactions, with only marginal positive associations with Tregs and M0 macrophages, indicating a less defined involvement in immune modulation.

NPAS1 and ATOH1 displayed selective but notable patterns amidst generally low background correlations. NPAS1 showed context-specific negative associations with myeloid cells, whereas ATOH1 was distinguished by a distinct positive correlation with resting memory CD4 + T cells and a negative association with M0 macrophages, hinting at specific navigational roles in adaptive versus innate immunity.

These findings highlight gene-specific immune association signatures of varying intensities. While MLXIPL and TCF15 appear to be potent modulators of the immune landscape, the weaker correlations observed for CLOCK and SREBF2 suggest that their contributions may be subtle or indirect. These patterns underscore the need for further mechanistic validation to delineate true biological regulation from background variations in the tumor microenvironment.

Discussion

Members of the bHLH protein family are implicated in the occurrence and progression of diverse tumors, with dysregulated bHLH gene expression closely associated with key malignant phenotypes and predictive biomarker value in various cancers [1, 24–27]. Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide and imposes a substantial global health burden. Although widespread early screening programs have contributed to a decrease in overall CRC incidence, the incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC; diagnosis before 50 years of age) has increased steadily over the past three decades [28, 29], posing unique clinical and biological challenges. Against this backdrop, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC pathogenesis, identifying effective biomarkers, and developing robust risk prediction models are critical for improving therapeutic decision-making and prognostic assessment.

In this study, utilizing the GDC TCGA database, we systematically integrated clinical data and mRNA expression profiles from colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) cohorts to identify differentially expressed genes between tumor tissues and adjacent normal mucosa. As a result, we identified 36 differentially expressed bHLH family genes in COAD and 32 in READ.

Univariate Cox survival analysis revealed that the expression levels of ATOH1, NPAS1, CLOCK, SREBF2, TCF15, and MLXIPL were significantly associated with overall survival (OS) in CRC patients. However, in the multivariate Cox regression analysis, only ATOH1 and NPAS1 remained as independent prognostic factors for OS, which may be attributable to potential collinearity among the included variables. Notably, ATOH1 exhibited a hazard ratio (HR) less than 1, suggesting a protective effect that may be attributed to its role in promoting the differentiation of intestinal secretory cells (such as goblet and Paneth cells) [30] and mediating tumor cell apoptosis via the JNK-p21 signaling pathway [31]. Conversely, elevated NPAS1 expression was identified as a risk factor associated with poorer prognosis in CRC. While existing literature on NPAS1 primarily concerns its function in the nervous system, its role in tumor biology - particularly in CRC - remains largely unexplored. Available data indicates no significant differential expression in breast cancer compared to normal tissue [32], and there are currently no reports detailing its specific function in CRC cell proliferation, migration, or apoptosis. Our pan-cancer analysis further corroborated these findings: ATOH1 expression was significantly downregulated in both COAD and READ tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal mucosa, whereas NPAS1 was upregulated specifically in COAD, with no significant difference observed in READ.

Furthermore, the prognostic model integrating ATOH1 and NPAS1 with T stage, N/M stage, and MSI status demonstrated improved predictive relevance, as ATOH1 and NPAS1 remained significantly associated with overall survival in the multivariate regression analysis. The relatively suboptimal predictive performance observed in GSE17538 is likely attributable to the limited number of samples with available MSI status in the original dataset, which may have compromised the robustness of the model.

In CRC, persistent infiltration of T cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME) is positively associated with patient prognosis [33]. Therapeutically, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) enhance antitumor immunity by blocking pathways that lead to T-cell exhaustion, thereby promoting T-cell activation and augmenting cytotoxic tumor clearance [34].Our analysis of immune cell infiltration patterns revealed that neither ATOH1 nor NPAS1 expression demonstrated significant associations with the infiltration levels of CD4⁺ T-cell subsets, including Th1, Th2, Th9, Th17, Th22, Tfh, and Treg populations. Notably, increased ATOH1 expression was significantly correlated with higher levels of infiltrating cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cells, whereas elevated NPAS1 expression was associated with reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration. These findings suggest that ATOH1 may contribute to the maintenance of a favorable immune landscape within the TME, potentially enhancing antitumor immune surveillance, while elevated NPAS1 expression may facilitate immunosuppressive microenvironmental conditions and promote immune evasion, thereby contributing to CRC progression. This functional dichotomy underscores the potential of both genes as candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets in immunomodulatory strategies for CRC.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis facilitated the identification of potential mechanistic pathways underlying bHLH gene function in CRC. ATOH1 was found to interact with mucins such as MUC6, identified as a suppressor of epithelial tumor invasion [35], and MUC5AC, which has been associated with increased tumor burden in CRC [36]. The functional implications of these interactions in the context of CRC pathogenesis remain unclear and warrant further mechanistic investigation. Additionally, ATOH1’s interaction with TCF family proteins [14, 37] indicates a putative regulatory role within the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway, potentially mediating inhibitory effects on intestinal oncogenesis by promoting secretory cell lineage differentiation.NPAS1 was implicated in hypoxia-response pathways [21, 38]. We hypothesize that NPAS1 may facilitate heterodimer formation between ARNT (HIF-1β) and HIF-2α under hypoxic conditions, thereby activating hypoxia-response elements (HREs) to promote angiogenesis, tumor cell proliferation, and metastasis. These mechanistic insights suggest that NPAS1 inhibition could represent a potential therapeutic strategy in CRC, meriting further validation.

Although this study provides novel insights into the bHLH family’s role in CRC, several constraints warrant consideration. First, regarding clinical applicability, our current prognostic model lacks integration of key clinicopathological features (such as NPAS1/ATOH1 expression, TNM staging, microsatellite instability status, as well as patient age, sex, and treatment regimens), which is necessary to enhance its predictive utility. Second, with respect to analytical depth, although our study utilized comprehensive RNA-seq datasets, proteomic validation was limited to bioinformatic prediction of protein-protein interactions. Therefore, future investigations should include functional enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG pathways) and experimental validation at the protein level to further elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms. Finally, as our current findings are primarily based on publicly available datasets, comprehensive experimental validation using in vitro cell models and in vivo animal studies is required to confirm the biological functions and translational relevance of candidate genes. Collectively, these limitations delineate critical avenues for future research to realize the clinical utility and mechanistic understanding of the bHLH family in CRC.

One of the most revolutionizing breakthroughs was the introduction of the the Internet of things(IoT) concept within the surgical practice [39].The IoT holds considerable promise for advancing early detection and integrated management of high-risk colorectal cancer patients. By employing smart ingestible sensors, IoT enables non-invasive and continuous intestinal surveillance, offering a viable screening alternative for individuals ineligible for conventional colonoscopy. Concurrently, wearable biosensors coupled with remote telemonitoring platforms facilitate real-time tracking of postoperative physiological parameters and early identification of complications, thereby supporting personalized rehabilitation and longitudinal follow up. The convergence of these IoT-driven technologies is anticipated to improve screening accessibility, optimize end to end patient management, and establish a foundational framework for a data-guided intelligent diagnostic and therapeutic ecosystem in CRC. The identification of ATOH1 and NPAS1 as key regulators lays the groundwork for: (1) deeper mechanistic exploration of CRC pathogenesis; (2) development of novel targeted therapies, particularly those involving NPAS1 inhibition; and (3) potential optimization of immunotherapeutic strategies through further elucidation of the ATOH1-NPAS1 immune axis.
