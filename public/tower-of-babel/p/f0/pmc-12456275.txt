Let’s ask the patient: disease prediction based on patients’ symptom descriptions in free text
Authors: Inés Pérez-Sancristóbal, Nils Steinz, Ling Qin, Tjardo Maarseveen, Floor Zegers, Barbara Bislawska Axnäs, Luis Rodríguez-Rodríguez, Rachel Knevel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study evaluates the value self-reported free-text symptom descriptions for supporting diagnostic decisions in osteoarthritis (OA), fibromyalgia (FM) and immune-mediated rheumatic diseases (imRD) using natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning (ML).MethodsFree-text descriptions from 8454 patients were processed using a word-weighting method (TF-IDF vectorization) that reflects how relevant each word is across the dataset, and then classified with support vector machine (SVM) models. OA and FM models were optimized for specificity and the imRD model for sensitivity based on disease context and validated against an independent dataset. Model explainability was explored using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values.ResultsThe SVM models demonstrated moderate diagnostic support potential with Area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC) values of 0.68 for OA, 0.75 for FM and 0.69 for imRD. When optimized for clinical utility, the models achieved high specificity of 0.82 for OA and 0.92 for FM, effectively reducing unnecessary referrals with misdiagnosis rates of only 17% and 8%, respectively. For imRD, the model achieved a sensitivity of 0.92 and negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.77, ensuring minimal missed diagnoses of these potentially serious conditions. Decision curve analysis confirmed clinical utility across varying threshold preferences. SHAP analysis revealed that key linguistic patterns in patient descriptions aligned with clinical reasoning, enhancing the models’ interpretability.ConclusionOur results highlight the value of patient-reported data in augmenting rheumatology decision-making and sets the stage for further development in AI-assisted diagnostics. While not a standalone diagnostic tool, the integration of NLP-driven analysis of free-text symptom descriptions shows promise in reducing diagnostic ambiguity.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal symptoms are among the most frequent reasons for general practice visits, accounting for nearly 30% of all consultations [1, 2]. Yet, diagnosing rheumatic conditions early remains a major challenge, despite the importance of timely detection [3–6] to prevent joint damage, chronic pain and reduced quality of life [7–9]. A key obstacle is distinguishing rheumatic diseases from other causes of musculoskeletal symptoms, especially in primary care, where General Practitioners (GPs) make referral decisions with limited data.

Patient symptom descriptions are central to diagnosis. Studies suggest up to 80% of diagnostic accuracy relies on a thorough history [10, 11]. While symptom-checkers and diagnostic support systems aim to assist [12, 13], their reliance on structured formats (checkboxes, predefined fields) often limits their capacity to capture the nuanced experiences essential for distinguishing complex conditions [9]. Moreover, patients describe symptoms differently from clinicians, adding complexity.

Free-written text allows patients to detail symptoms in their own words, offering richer context than structured inputs [14]. However, analysing such input at scale has been historically difficult [15]. Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning (ML) now make this possible [16–18]. Yet, most applications have focused on fixed questionnaires or electronic health records (EHRs); little is known about leveraging patients’ own descriptions for diagnostic purposes—despite these being central to traditional clinical practice [19].

Distinguishing between immune-mediated rheumatic diseases (imRD), osteoarthritis (OA) and fibromyalgia (FM) is clinically relevant, as imRDs typically require immunosuppressive treatment, while OA and FM are more often managed conservatively. The goal of this paper is to analyse to what extent patients’ self-reported symptoms in free-written text convey sufficient information to be predictive of the diagnosis of imRD, OA and FM. By applying NLP and ML to patient responses, we assess the predictive value of free-text symptom descriptions and explore their potential to support clinical decision-making. We then used these models to assess how patient-written symptom descriptions contribute to diagnostic classification, with the ultimate aim of supporting early triage decisions in clinical settings.

Methods

We used patients’ self-written symptom descriptions as input for machine learning models aimed at predicting whether they would later receive a diagnosis of OA, FM or an imRD. Below, we provide a detailed description of patient selection, text preprocessing, model development and evaluation procedures. The study outline is represented in Supplementary Fig. S1, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online.

Being non-interventional, the study was exempt from complete ethical assessment by the regional Medical Research and Ethics Committee, registration number: N21.22. Patient recruitment, data collection, storage and analysis follow the European General Data Protection Regulation, and was approved by the Leiden University Medical Center privacy officer. The data analysed in this study were obtained from the Rheumatic? questionnaire [12]. All patients provided written informed consent when participating in the questionnaire and no further consent was needed for this study.

Patients

We recruited patients with musculoskeletal complaints who wondered if they had a rheumatic diagnosis. The study was advertised online (June 2021–February 2023). Participants in the Rheumatic study [12, 13] were asked: ‘Can you state in your own words (max 200) the complaints you have?’. Follow-up surveys at 3, 6 and 12 months collected diagnosis data.

Inclusion required at least one completed follow-up survey.

The diagnoses were based on self-reported information in the follow-up surveys. For a subset of patients, these diagnoses were confirmed by rheumatologists as part of the Rheumatic? project [12] validation process with values showing high concordance: 91% for osteoarthritis (OA), 94% for fibromyalgia (FM) and 80% for immune-mediated rheumatic diseases (imRD).

Data preparation

The input data were patients’ free-text descriptions. We removed responses under 20 characters to ensure the quality and consistency of data. We pre-processed the text by applying 5 NLP steps: word segmentation, lowercase conversion, stop word removal, word normalization and vectorization [20].

Outcome definition

We built classification models for 3 disease groups: OA, FM and imRD. Each model differentiated cases (patients diagnosed during the study) from controls (patients without any reported diagnosis). We defined controls as participants who did not report a diagnosis of OA, FM or imRD at any timepoint across the baseline and follow-up surveys (Fig. 1). This approach allowed us to identify a consistent group of patients who remained without a diagnosis throughout the study period, and thus were used as the negative class during model training and validation. We tested the models for: (I) all cases, (II) existing diagnosis (baseline) and (III) new diagnosis (reported only at follow-up). Patients with multiple diagnosis were included in the analysis of each reported disease group.

Flowchart illustrating the pre-processing and sample selection of the whole study. Controls: patients who did not report any diagnosis (true negatives)

Training, model selection and validation

We randomly split 9013 participants into training and validation datasets (Fig. 1). Within the training dataset, we created three group-specific datasets (OA, FM, imRD), each combining controls with the corresponding cases. Each group dataset was further split into 80% training and 20% internal testing subsets.

The final validation dataset was likewise divided into three disease-specific groups, each including controls. Once more, patients were permitted to indicate multiple diagnoses concurrently. In this final validation stage, we run the best-trained models in the previously constructed validation dataset.

Model development

Following the pipeline proposed by Maarseveen et al. [20], we developed ML classifiers to estimate diagnostic likelihood. We evaluated eight algorithms: word matching, naïve Bayes, logistic regression, decision trees, random forest, gradient boosting, multilayer perceptron (neural network) and support vector machine (SVM). We applied TF-IDF vectorization to train all models and evaluated their performance using 2×5 cross-validation.

To correct for class imbalance, we applied class weighting during both training and cross-validation, assigning higher weight to the minority class to ensure equal contribution to the loss function.

We selected the algorithm with the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) for each disease group, which was SVM, and performed hyperparameter tuning to optimize performance (see Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online). This procedure was applied independently to each group—OA, FM and imRD—resulting in three final models. Each optimized model was then retrained on the full training dataset and internally tested.

Exploration of performance

We assessed ROC curves across all, existing and new cases. We tested the differences between these ROC curves using DeLong test [21], considering differences as statistically significant when P<0.05.

Selecting the cut-off

To analyse how symptom phrasing contributes to prediction, we used swarm plots to determine cut-offs. For imRD, we prioritized sensitivity and NPV to ensure patients are referred for evaluation. For OA and FM, we prioritized PPV and specificity to reduce unnecessary referrals.

Decision curve analysis

To evaluate the clinical utility of our models, we performed decision curve analysis (DCA) comparing the net benefit [22] of our models against ‘refer all’ and ‘refer none’ strategies across thresholds. Thresholds reflect physician tolerance for false positives. A good model maintains a net benefit over a broad range of thresholds. Acceptable ranges were benchmarked against standard referral strategies.

In other words, the potential clinical impact was assessed using DCA, which considers the trade-off of avoiding unnecessary patient consultations (false positive) at the expense of omitting relevant medical evaluations (true positives).

Explainability

We used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) scores [23] (Kernel explainer) to determine the impact of individual predictors to the models. Rare words and n-grams (< 1% frequency) were excluded. SHAP values identify each word’s contribution to a given prediction.

Software

We performed all analyses with Python 3.10 and scikit–learn tools [24] and R version 4.1.2. Data are available upon reasonable request. Methods are available at https://github.com/nilssteinz/rheumatic_nlp

From the initial whole dataset, we removed 559 participants after data curation, leaving a total of 8454 participants for further analysis.

Out of these, 6578 (78%) were women and 75% of the population was over 50 years old. Disease prevalence was higher in women across all groups (Table 1).

Algorithm training and model selection

Among eight algorithms, Support Vector Machine (SVM) and neural networks performed best (Supplementary Fig. S2, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online). We selected SVM, due to its better interpretability, for a further training and optimization.

Hyperparameter outcome

Optimal hyperparameters varied by disease: OA performed best with linear SVM and unigram words, while FM and imRD required more complex n-grams and a radial basis function kernel (Supplementary Table S4, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online). The models have different hyperparameters for the different groups of diseases (Supplementary Table S4, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online). The result of the training and testing phase can be found in Supplementary Fig. S3, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online.

Validation performance

After training the models (Supplementary Figs S3 and S4, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online), we obtained the final performance in the hold-out validation dataset to obtain reliable estimates. Notably, the AUC-ROC between validation and test data set were similar.

Classification of OA

In the case of OA, the overall AUC–ROC resulted in an AUC–ROC of 0.68 (0.65–0.70) (Fig. 2A). When we split the data in existing cases (already diagnosed before study inclusion) and new cases (meaning future diagnosis) we obtained an AUC–ROC of 0.69 (0.67–0.72) (Fig. 2A) and 0.65 (0.62–0.68), respectively. The performance of predicting new cases was significantly lower than the existing cases (P = 0.048, df = 2651.4).

Classification of FM

With respect to FM, the overall AUC–ROC was 0.75 (0.72–0.78) (Fig. 2C). For classification of existing cases, we had an AUC–ROC of 0.76 (0.73–0.79) (Fig. 2C), which was slightly decreased in the performance of predicting the new cases, resulting in an AUC–ROC of 0.72 (0.68–0.77). The performance of prediction for existing cases was higher than the new cases (P = 0.16, df = 1707.3).

Classification of imRD

Finally, for imRD, the overall AUC–ROC was 0.69 (0.60–0.69) (Fig. 2E). For existing cases, we obtained these values: AUC–ROC of 0.71 (0.68–0.74) (Fig. 2E) and AUC–ROC of 0.65 (0.60–0.69) in the new cases scenario. The performance of prediction of new cases was significantly lower than the existing cases (P=0.03, df= 1677.4).

Case identification

For case selection, we set cut–offs for the three disease groups for the overall cases in the test set (Supplementary Fig. S4, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online). For OA and FM, we aimed to have a high positive predictive value (PPV) and specificity. For ImRD diagnosis, the objective is different and we want to capture more potential patients and possibly send them to the GP first for some diagnostics. All the metrics for the possible cut-offs are shown in Supplementary Tables S5–S10, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online.

Upon applying the predefined cut–offs to the final independent validation data set, we achieved a comparable performance to that observed in the internal test set (Fig. 2B–D).

For OA, the moderate precise cut-off at a probability of 0.5 (Fig. 2B) gave a PPV and specificity of 0.70 and 0.45 in the validation data. With this cut–off the SVM classifier identified 1018 true positives (75%) and 327 (24%) false negatives. With the more specific cut–off of 0.75, we obtained a PPV of 0.80 and a specificity of 0.82 and the classifier identified 561 (42%) true positives and 140 (17%) false positives.

For FM, we selected the same cut-off values as for OA representing the same confidence in the possible diagnosis. For FM, the 0.5 cut–off (Fig. 2D) resulted in a PPV and specificity of 0.64 and 0.80, respectively. With this cut–off the classifier identified 275 (54%) true positives and 234 (46%) false negatives. With the cut–off of 0.75, we have a PPV of 0.73 and a specificity of 0.92 and in this case, the number of true positives decreased to 167 (33%) and false positives were 63 (8%).

With imRD, the most sensitive cut–off (0.15) resulted in a negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.77 and sensitivity of 0.92. The more specific cut-off (0.75) gave a PPV of 0.73 and specificity of 0.95, respectively.

Ultimately, with the sensitive 0.15 cut-off (Fig. 2F) we identified 554 (92%) true cases, 631 (80%) false positives and 49 (8%) false negatives. For 161 (20%) we could rule out the imRD. With the 0.75 cut-off, the number of true positives decreased to 115 (19%) but the false positives also decreased to 42 (5%) while false negatives increased to 488 (81%).

Explainability

We used SHAP values to facilitate a comprehensive understanding of model predictions [23].

To diagnose these diseases, it is recommended to pay attention to certain expressions and comments made by patients in accordance with current clinical practice. For instance, an explicit reference to ‘arthrosis’ (Dutch: ‘artrose’), ‘pain’ (‘pijn’), ‘neck’ (‘hals’) and ‘wandeling’ (‘walk’) may indicate a potential OA (Fig. 4A), whereas a mention of ‘morning’ (‘ochtend’) or ‘knee’ (‘knie’) may decrease the probability of a potential FM diagnosis (Fig. 4B). Similarly, a mention of ‘wrists’ (‘polsen’) may indicate an imRD diagnosis whereas the presence of the words ‘right side’ (‘rechter’, ‘rechterlijke’) and ‘tingling’ (‘tintelingen’) may also decrease the potential imRD diagnosis (Fig. 4C). In Supplementary Figs S6–S8, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online, the SHAP values are depicted in the original Dutch names.

Therefore, some expressions may have a positive impact on the model output, while the impact of others may be negative. To illustrate both cases as some examples, the term ‘morning’ (‘ochtend’) had a negative impact on the classification of FM (Fig. 4B), as the pain associated with fibromyalgia is generally constant, lacking a discernible time pattern. It is uncommon for patients to describe their pain just in the ‘morning’ in an explicit manner, particularly as they are unlikely to perceive the pain as intermittent. Furthermore, the explicit presence of ‘painful hand’ (‘pijn hand’) had a negative effect on the FM condition and this is reasonable since the pain associated with generalized pain syndromes such as fibromyalgia, lacks an exact location pattern and is not usually localized. Instead, it is typically a generalized pain throughout the body at muscular and myofascial level.

Likewise, the word ‘elbow’ had a negative impact on the classification of OA (Fig. 4A). This is plausible, given that elbow–specific OA is relatively uncommon and being more frequent when it is of post-traumatic aetiology [25]. In contrast, the terms ‘pain’ (‘pijn’) and ‘many’ (‘veel’) had a positive impact on the classification of OA, in accordance with current clinical practice.

Similarly, in the case of imRD (Fig. 4C), the presence of the words ‘right side’ (‘rechter’, ‘rechterlijke’) demonstrated a negative outcome in relation to this disease group. This is consistent with an inflammatory arthropathy like rheumatoid arthritis (RA), given the presence of symmetrical involvement of small joints the localized and alternating pattern observed in other conditions, such as psoriatic arthritis or OA. Similarly, the positive impact of the word ‘wrists’ (‘polsen’) is also consistent with this symmetrical small joint involvement of rheumatoid arthritis. Although psoriatic arthritis is included in the imRD group of diseases, the disease with the highest prevalence in the dataset from which the SHAP values were derived was RA.

On top of that, the algorithm highlights other words that are surprisingly linked to specific diseases such as ‘through’ and ‘question’ for OA (Fig. 4A). Finally, although it could be argued that expressions (e.g. bi/trigrams) may convey more information than words, our analysis showed that this is not the case. Word clusters provide information but some specific individual words are key in conveying such information, as our graphs show.

Discussion

Our study explores whether patients’ free-text symptom descriptions, analysed using natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning (ML), can effectively distinguish between osteoarthritis (OA), fibromyalgia (FM), immune-mediated rheumatic diseases (imRD) and patients without these conditions. Our findings highlight the potential opportunities and some limitations of this approach.

While many studies focus on structured questionnaire data [26–29] our work is the first to explore free-text symptom descriptions in rheumatology. Our models are clinically interpretable, with key indicators in our SHAP analysis, reinforcing known clinical patterns and offering transparency in decision-making. Thereby, this study represents an important paradigm shift—from analysing what clinicians and scientists deem relevant, to directly analysing what patients report themselves. This conceptual innovation has implications for patient-centred care approaches.

Compared with prior NLP-based diagnostic studies in medicine, our models achieved performance levels within a similar range, despite relying on less standardized input. For example, NLP systems applied to radiology reports for pneumonia or fracture detection have reached sensitivities around 0.88 and specificities up to 0.92 [26–29], benefiting from the consistent structure of those texts. In rheumatology, Knevel et al. [12] developed a decision support tool using structured questionnaires, showing promise for early arthritis triage. However, our study differs by focusing entirely on unstructured, patient-generated text. While this introduces linguistic variability, our models still achieved strong results (e.g. FM specificity 0.92), reinforcing the feasibility of leveraging free-written descriptions in clinical triage. This contrast highlights the importance of balancing real-world applicability with model robustness and interpretability.

Using responses to a single open-ended question (max 200 words), our models achieved moderate overall performance with AUC-ROC values of 0.68 for OA, 0.75 for FM and 0.69 for imRD. For OA and FM, where preventing unnecessary specialist referrals is important, our models achieved excellent specificity (0.82 for OA and 0.92 for FM) with strong positive predictive values (0.80 and 0.73, respectively). These results translate to misdiagnosis rates of 17% for OA and 8% for FM. For imRD, where early detection is critical to prevent irreversible joint damage, our model achieved a high sensitivity of 0.92, ensuring that the vast majority of cases would be identified for further evaluation. Though the NPV of 0.77 does underlines that an imRD cannot be ruled out on patients’ self-reported symptoms only.

Our DCA further contextualizes the implication in terms of net benefit. For OA, our model shows net benefit compared with a ‘refer all’ strategy at threshold probabilities above 50%. For FM, the model performs well across a broader range of thresholds (15–70%), aligning with clinical strategies that prioritize specificity. For imRD, while the high sensitivity at low thresholds (15–30%) is valuable for ensuring cases aren’t missed, the net benefit at these thresholds is nearly identical to simply referring all patients, indicating limited added value in highly sensitive screening scenarios.

The overall performance of the models was somewhat better among the patients with existing diagnoses. Possibly, patients are better able to describe their symptoms once they have gained a deeper understanding of their disease. This also underscores the relevance of how rheumatologists explain complex medical information to their patients and the impact on them.

The SHAP values of our analyses highlighted several words that trained rheumatologists easily link to specific diseases. An important consideration is that many patients explicitly use the term ‘artrose’ (osteoarthritis) when describing their symptoms. As a result, this word emerged as one of the most predictive features in the OA model. While this might appear tautological, it reflects real-world phrasing habits and highlights that patients often report prior diagnoses or suspicions in their own words. This may have partially inflated model performance for OA. Other highly predictive terms are also consistent with clinical reasoning. For example, the term ‘neck’ (‘hals’) is frequently associated with OA. This is plausible given that OA is characterized by the absence of frank inflammation and predominantly mechanical involvement, and patients often report cervical symptoms. In contrast, the absence of ‘tingling’ (‘tinteling’) may suggest imRD, since this kind of symptom is more common in mechanical or non-inflammatory conditions such as carpal tunnel syndrome. However, a key limitation is that it was not feasible to ascertain the exact context in which these words were used.

One limitation of our approach is the grouping of various immune-mediated rheumatic diseases (imRD) into a single category. These conditions include both inflammatory arthritis and connective tissue diseases, which differ in clinical presentation and severity. However, our goal was to support early triage by identifying cases more likely to require rheumatologic care, rather than to provide fine-grained diagnostic classification. Given the limited sample size for many individual conditions (e.g. systemic sclerosis, myositis with 30 and 8 patients, respectively), separate models do not provide adequate statistical power or model reliability. Therefore, we opted to combine these conditions into the broader imRD category to ensure a significant development of the model. Nonetheless, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online, when applying the imRD model distinguishing between diseases, the models performed better for their respective disease groups but showed overlapping probability distributions. This suggests that while the model can broadly separate inflammatory from non-inflammatory presentations, distinguishing between specific diseases remains an open challenge. Indeed, the models were not optimized for inter-disease discrimination, and the observed overlap reflects their triage-oriented design. Future studies should explore whether combining these models with other approaches could help distinguish more precisely between specific rheumatic diseases.

An important consideration is how these findings might translate to clinical practice. Given the performance characteristics, these models would be inappropriate as standalone diagnostic tools. For OA and FM, the modest sensitivity at high specificity thresholds suggests they might serve as rule-in tools in specific contexts where minimizing false positives is paramount. For imRD, the high sensitivity but poor specificity suggests potential value as an initial screening tool only if resources allow for the follow-up of many false positives. In both cases, integration would need to be thoughtfully designed to complement rather than replace clinical judgement.

Real-world implementation faces barriers. Interoperability with EHR systems like Spain’s DIRAYA [30] requires Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR)-standard APIs, which are absent in 60% of EU hospitals [26]. Clinician trust may be bolstered by SHAP-driven explainability but demands intuitive interfaces for busy practitioners. Regulatory hurdles, particularly General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliance [31], necessitate on-premise deployment, limiting cloud-based scalability.

In addition to these implementation challenges, another limitation of our study is the absence of metadata such as education level, socioeconomic status or prior treatments. While these factors might influence how people communicate, they are typically unknown to clinicians at the point of first contact. Our objective was to explore how patients describe their symptoms in their own words, without relying on structured or contextual information. In fact, our design reflects this clinical reality: a physician often does not know whether the patient is a university professor or a manual labourer, nor how much prior health knowledge they have. What matters is how the symptoms are communicated. From this perspective, the absence of these variables may be seen not as a limitation, but as part of the study’s translational relevance.

Regarding treatment, patients with new diagnoses were, by definition, untreated at baseline. For those with existing diagnoses, we recognize that prior medical encounters may have influenced their narratives. We accounted for this by analysing performance separately for existing and new cases (see Fig. 2), and the model’s ability to capture these differences supports the idea that phrasing shifts after diagnosis.

A limiting factor of our outcome definition is the reliance on self-reported diagnoses. Although this introduces potential for misclassification, our design aimed to emulate the uncertainty faced in early clinical triage, where initial diagnosis is often provisional and patient-reported. Controls were defined conservatively as patients who never reported a diagnosis at any follow-up point. While this approach likely reduces false negatives, it may also exclude patients with undiagnosed conditions, which reflects a real-world diagnostic challenge.

Nonetheless, a subset of patient-reported diagnoses was validated by rheumatologists as part of the Rheumatic? platform’s internal quality control procedures, showing high concordance rates: 91% for OA, 94% for FM and 80% for imRD. This reinforces the reliability of self-reported diagnoses in this context and supports their use as a proxy gold standard in large-scale, real-world studies.

Future research should pursue several directions. First, exploring more advanced NLP models, particularly those designed for contextual understanding, might help bridge the performance gap between patient-generated and professional text. Chatbots, for example, could assist patients in articulating their symptoms more effectively before seeing a rheumatologist, potentially reducing the burden on healthcare systems. However, the complexity of these models and their GPU usage make them more difficult to integrate into clinical care than our models.

Finally, broadening the study to more diverse populations and languages would enhance generalizability. Collaborations such as ‘Stratification of Patients using advanced Integrative modelling of Data Routinely acquired for diagnosing Rheumatic complaints’ (SPIDeRR) [32] will help broaden the scope of this research by incorporating data from other countries, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of how free-written text can be used in diagnostic models.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates a significant advancement in leveraging patient-generated free-text descriptions for diagnostic support in rheumatology. We have shown that the words patients choose to describe their symptoms contain valuable diagnostic signals that align with clinical reasoning patterns, and these signals can be successfully extracted using NLP and ML approaches. These results represent an important step towards leveraging patient-centred diagnostic approaches that value patients’ own descriptions of their experiences. While further refinement will enhance performance, the current models already demonstrate potential clinical utility in specific contexts and set the foundation for a new generation of AI-assisted diagnostic tools that incorporate the patient’s voice directly into clinical decision-making. As healthcare systems worldwide seek innovative solutions to diagnostic challenges in rheumatology, this approach offers a compelling pathway that honours patients’ experiences while supporting clinicians’ decision-making processes.
