Prevalence of internet addiction and its association with quality of sleep among secondary and higher secondary school students of Jamnagar Gujarat during COVID-19
Authors: Niraj P. Patel, Anusha S. Garg, Deepak S. Tiwari, Viral R. Shah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:The internet is a vital part of present-day society. Internet use for education has increased in recent years. During the COVID pandemic, schools were closed and education was delivered through online methods, which led to a further increase in internet use, especially in adolescents, leading to internet addiction in some.Aim:To estimate the prevalence of internet addiction and poor quality of sleep in adolescent students of Jamnagar.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional study was carried out between January 2022 to March 2022 and included 1778 adolescent students of Jamnagar, Gujarat. The internet addiction test was used to measure the level of internet addiction, and the short Pittsburgh sleep quality index was used to measure the quality of sleep. Data analysis was conducted using the Chi-square test, Mann-Whitney U test, and multiple logistic regression techniques.Results:In our study, 17.8% of participants had mild internet addiction, 3.2% of participants had moderate-to-severe internet addiction, and 79% of participants were average internet users. Associated variables with internet addiction were male gender, residence at home, duration of internet usage in years, daily hours spent online, and monthly expenditure on internet recharge. Furthermore, 27.2% of participants had poor quality of sleep. Female gender, secondary school education, residence at the hostel, and internet addiction were predictors of poor quality of sleep.Conclusion:Students were found to have a high prevalence of internet addiction. The internet usage increased amid the COVID-19 pandemic. Internet addiction was significantly associated with poor quality of sleep in adolescents.

The internet can be described as a “connection of different networks.” The internet started as a way for government researchers to share information.[1] The internet is a vital part of present-day society. The internet is an essential part of education, and it provides tools for communication and easy access to knowledge. Internet use for education has increased in recent years. During the COVID pandemic, schools were closed and education was delivered through online methods, which led to a further increase in internet use, especially in adolescents. Young formulated the concept of internet addiction as a broad impulse control disorder, attributing its fundamental psychopathology to compromised self-regulation.[2] Griffiths characterized internet addiction as a non-chemical behavioral compulsion.[3] Beard categorized internet addiction among the realm of behavioral addictions.[4] The symptoms of internet addiction include preoccupation with the internet, hiding the behavior from others, loss of interest in other activities and relationships, engaging in excessive online activities, experiencing compulsive internet usage with difficulties in controlling the time spent online and hiding time online from others, poor scholastic performance, decreased productivity anticipation of being online, and lack of control over use.[5] A meta-analysis of 9 studies indicated that the collective prevalence of “problematic internet use” in India stood at 21.6%.[6]

During COVID-19, the internet was mostly used for chatting or communication (44.1%) and playing video games (8.8%). Research conducted in China during the pandemic revealed that the general incidence of internet addiction stood at 36.7% (moderate addiction was 33.9% and severe addiction was 2.8%). Approximately half of the participants indicated a significant increase in the severity of internet addiction., with a significant increase in the time spent online during the pandemic compared to the period preceding it.[6] During development, adolescents shift their emotional valence from family to peer group. Maybe due to hectic schedules and decreasing community playgrounds, online presence has become important for social identity.[7] Currently, adolescents use multiple social media accounts on multiple platforms for different activities.

In 2013, Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) was included in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) for further examination.[8] The International Classification of Diseases, 11th edition (ICD-11), has also incorporated gaming disorder, recognizing that it can manifest in both online and offline gaming contexts.[9] Screen time can displace other activities, including physical activity, which is required for good quality sleep, additional screen time compromises, sleep duration, and shortens sleep duration and quality of sleep.[10] Evening-time and night-time bright light or blue light exposure due to screen decreases melatonin production and disrupts circadian rhythm.[11]

The prevalence of internet addiction in India exhibits variation, and the limited number of available studies underscores the need for further investigation. Hence, the principal aim of our investigation is to ascertain the prevalence of internet addiction within the Indian population. In addition, we aim to explore its association with the quality of sleep.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

An institutional-based cross-sectional study was carried out among the student body in secondary and higher secondary schools of the Jamnagar district of Gujarat, during the period between January to March 2022. The District Education Officer and respective principals were informed and their permission was taken. The students were provided with information regarding the study’s purpose and confidentiality, after which they were requested to complete the semi-structured questionnaire. Approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Committee (Date: 07/01/2022; Reference No.: 69/01/2022) of Shri M.P Shah Government Medical College, Jamnagar.

Sample size

The study’s sample size was established at 1768, with a confidence level of 98% and a margin of error of 1.5%. Sample group selection was done by proportionate probability sampling.

Sampling technique

There are 58,995 students (331 schools) enrolled in 9th to 12th standard listed in six talukas of Jamnagar district and city. Fifteen schools were selected by random sampling to reach the sample size. Information was collected and analyzed thereafter.

Tools

Demographic and clinical proforma

Performa includes demographic details such as age, gender, standard of education, father’s education, mother’s education, geographical area, residence (home or hostel), years of internet use, and hours spent on the internet every day.

Internet Addiction Test (IAT)

The IAT comprises a 20-item scale designed to gauge the presence and intensity of internet addiction. The total score is derived by summing the scores of all 20 items, with a maximum score of 100 points. A higher score indicates a more severe issue. Scores falling between 0 and 30 indicate a normal level of internet usage. Scores from 31 to 49 suggest mild addiction, while those between 50 and 79 indicate moderate addiction. Severe dependence.[12] The IAT has been validated for assessment of internet addiction in Indian school students.[13]

Short Pittsburgh sleep quality index

Short Pittsburgh sleep quality index is a self-administered questionnaire to assess sleep quality and disturbance over the last month.[14] The shorter version is good for a larger, younger, population with less survey time; thus, we selected the shorter version for our population.[14] In a study on college students of Bhuvneshwar, Cronbach’s alpha was found to be 0.821.[15]

Statistics analysis

Data analysis was conducted utilizing the SPSS version 22 (IBM SPSS, Armonk, USA). Scores of internet addiction test and short Pittsburgh sleep quality index were expressed as median and interquartile range. The prevalence of internet addiction and the occurrence of poor sleep quality were quantified in terms of percentages. Determinants of internet addiction and poor sleep quality were analyzed using the Chi-square test, calculating crude odds ratios. For variables showing significance, adjusted odds ratios were calculated. The association between the degree of internet addiction and components of the short Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index was assessed using the Chi-square test.

RESULTS

In our study, there were 1778 participants with a mean age of 15.8 years (13–18 years). Table 1 shows the social demographic characteristics and their median ± IQR of total IAT score and sPSQI score. On average, participants in our study spent 1.6 hours per day on the internet, and the participant’s mean duration of internet use was 2.9 years. The average monthly expenditure of participants on internet recharge was 306.27 ± 363.42 Indian rupees, and the average data consumed per day was 1.38 ± 1.62 GB per day. In our study, 373 (21.00%) participants had internet addiction; of them, 57 (3.20%) participants had moderate-to-severe internet addiction. Almost one-fourth of the participants experienced poor quality of sleep.

DISCUSSION

This study estimated the prevalence of internet addiction and poor sleep quality among the adolescent students of Jamnagar during the third wave of COVID-19. A total of 1778 adolescents participated in our study of secondary and higher secondary schools. The sex ratio of the population was 1:1.1; 55.30% of participants were female. Most of the participants were below 16 years (74.60%). In a similar study conducted in Nepal in a peri-urban setting in adolescents of secondary and higher secondary school during COVID-19 (May–September 2019), the majority of participants were male (55.4%), the male: female ratio was almost equal, and 73.6% of participants were below 15 years of age.[16] Most of the study participants in our study were from rural backgrounds (87.7%), while the Nepal study was conducted in a peri-urban setting.[1] In a similar study conducted in Vadodara, 56.8% of participants were male and the mean age of the participants was 14.5 years.[17]

A study in Bangladesh during COVID-19 (September–October 2021) found that adolescents’ prevalence of internet addiction was 88.25%, which was much higher than that in our study. This may be because during the time of their study, schools were closed, and quarantine and social distancing were followed, but during our study period, schools were open and COVID-19 restrictions were diminished in India.[18] In a study conducted in Nepal, 31% of participants had poor quality of sleep; similarly, in our study, the prevalence of poor quality of sleep was 27.2%.[1] In their study, 65% of participants had mild internet addiction, 21% of participants had moderate internet addiction, and 0% of participants had severe internet addiction, while in our study, 17.8% of participants had mild internet addiction, 3.1% of participants had moderate internet addiction, and 0.1% had severe internet addiction. The prevalence of internet addiction was higher in the Nepal study [1]; this may be because the study was conducted in a peri-urban setting, whereas our study was conducted majorly in rural populations. A similar study was conducted in Uttarakhand, in December 2019 in medical college students, 67.4% of students had mild internet addiction, 26.7% had moderate internet addiction and 5.9% had severe internet addiction.[19] The prevalence of internet addiction was higher in this population may be due to the study population being medical college students and they had easy access to the internet compared to our population. A study conducted in Baroda Gujarat in 2016 found the prevalence of internet addiction to be 8.7%, which was lower than our study; this may be due to different periods of the study and different criteria used to define internet addiction.[17]

Male students exhibited a higher prevalence of internet addiction. Similarly, studies on adolescents conducted in Vadodara and Bhavnagar in 2017 found male gender as a predictor of internet addiction.[17,20] However, a study done in Assam on urban adolescents found that females have a higher prevalence of internet addiction.[21] According to our study, students living at home demonstrated a statistically higher prevalence of internet addiction compared to students residing in hostels. This difference could be attributed to the rules and regulations imposed at the hostel. Similarly, a study conducted in Bhavnagar found that students residing at home had higher internet addiction than students residing in hostels.[20]

In our study, the female gender had a significantly higher prevalence of poor quality of sleep compared to their male counterpart. In a study done in Brazil during COVID-19 in adolescents, similar to our study, the female gender had a significantly higher prevalence of poor quality of sleep.[22] In a study done in Delhi in 2019 on adolescents of urban resettlement, poor quality of sleep was highly prevalent in the female gender.[23] However, a study done in Gwalior found no significant difference in the quality of sleep in male and female genders.[24]

The age of participants had no statistically significant association with the prevalence of poor quality of sleep. However, in other studies, poor quality of sleep was significantly higher in higher age groups. In a study done in China during COVID-19 in adolescents of age group 12–20 years, age above 16 years was a predictor of poor quality of sleep.[25] The mean age of their study participants was higher than ours, and our study group contained participants from the age group of 13–18 years.

Students in secondary school had poorer quality of sleep than students in higher secondary school. This is in contrast to other studies, where poor quality of sleep was more prevalent in higher standards of education.[26] In other studies, poor quality of sleep was more prevalent in higher standards of education.[26]

In our study, students residing in hostels had a statistically significant higher prevalence of poor quality of sleep compared to students residing at home. Good social support at home can be a reason for this. One Flemish study found that adolescents who experienced more support from their families had better quality of sleep.[27] A study on sleep patterns of urban adolescents of Delhi found that total sleep time decreased with higher grades, nocturnal awakening, daytime leg pain, and daytime napping, and daytime sleepiness became more frequent in higher grades.[28]

When studying the association between components of the sleep quality index and different levels of internet addiction, the association was significant between all five components of the sleep quality index and the level of internet addiction. A similar study conducted in Turkey during COVID-19 (March–June 2021) found a statistically significant association between the internet addiction scale total score and PSQI total score, sleep latency, sleep duration, and daytime dysfunction, but no association was found between sleep efficiency and sleep disturbance.[29] Association with all the subscales was not found possibly due to different study populations and the use of different scales for the assessment of internet addiction. In a study conducted on adolescents in Turkey before COVID-19 (September–December 2017), a significant association was found between all the subscales of PSQI and YIAT total score.[30] In a similar study conducted in Bhubaneshwar, India during COVID-19 (October–December 2020) among college students, a significant association was found between subscales of PSQI and total score of IAT except for sleep duration and sleep efficiency. In our study too, a weak correlation was found between IAT total score and sleep duration and sleep efficiency.[31]

The prevalence of internet addiction was higher in adolescents. Predictors of internet addiction were male gender, residence at home, monthly cost of internet recharge, hours of internet use per day, years of internet use, and daily data used on the internet in GB. We identified a significant association between the level of internet addiction and the quality of sleep.

Limitation

Our study was a cross-sectional study; as such, the relationship between exposure and outcome was evaluated at the same time. Therefore, evaluating causal relationships was difficult. The studied sample was from a single geographical region, which may have restricted generalization. The sample was collected by self-reporting method rather than objective assessment by mental health professionals; thus, it cannot correspond to the objective method. Furthermore, we did not assess online activities and other factors associated with internet addiction in adolescents.

CONCLUSION

Students were found to have a high prevalence of internet addiction. Internet usage increased amid the COVID-19 pandemic. Internet Addiction was significantly associated with poor quality of sleep in adolescents.
