Cervical neuroendocrine carcinoma with non-neuroendocrine components has a poorer prognosis than pure neuroendocrine carcinoma
Authors: Sun Min Park, Chel Hun Choi, Kidong Kim, Hyojin Kim, Se Ik Kim, Chae Hyeong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate whether neuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix (NECC) with coexisting non-neuroendocrine tumor components (mixed subtype) is associated with a worse prognosis compared to the pure subtype.MethodsA total of 121 patients diagnosed with NECC at 4 participating hospitals in the Republic of Korea between August 1997 and November 2023 were included. All diagnoses were confirmed through surgical specimens obtained via conization or hysterectomy. Clinical and pathological variables were collected through medical record review. The association between tumor heterogeneity (pure vs. mixed subtype) and progression-free survival (PFS) was analyzed using a Cox proportional hazards model.ResultsThe majority of patients was diagnosed at stage I (62%), had small cell histology (63%), and presented with the pure subtype (66%). Most patients (n=106) underwent surgery with or without adjuvant therapy as initial treatment. In univariate analysis, there was no significant difference in PFS between the pure and mixed subtypes (p=0.952). However, after adjusting for covariates, the mixed subtype was significantly associated with worse PFS compared to the pure subtype (adjusted hazard ratio=3.16; 95% confidence interval=1.58–6.35; p=0.001).ConclusionNECC with non-neuroendocrine components is associated with significantly worse prognosis than the pure subtype.Keywords: Uterine Cervical Neoplasms; Neuroendocrine Tumors; Carcinoma, Neuroendocrine; Mixed Tumor, Malignant; Progression-Free Survival

INTRODUCTION

According to the 2022 World Health Organization classification, neuroendocrine neoplasms are categorized into well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas, the latter of which are considered high-grade malignancies [1]. Although rare, neuroendocrine neoplasms can arise in the female genital tract [2]. Neuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix (NECC) is a poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma that accounts for approximately 1.4% of all cervical cancers [3].

NECC is rare variant of cervical cancer associated with a poor prognosis due to its high propensity for lymph node involvement and distant metastasis. For instance, Tempfer et al. [3] reported a mean recurrence-free survival of 16 months and a mean overall survival (OS) of 40 months. Similarly, Salvo et al. [4] reported a 5-year OS rate of 36%, with a median OS ranging from 22 to 25 months. Also, NECC is an aggressive malignancy with higher risk of death compared to squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), regardless of disease stage [5].

Multimodal treatment, including radical surgery combined with adjuvant chemotherapy based on etoposide and cisplatin, with or without radiotherapy, is generally recommended for early-stage disease. For patients with locally advanced or recurrent disease, concurrent chemoradiotherapy or systemic chemotherapy using agents such as etoposide, cisplatin, topotecan, paclitaxel, and bevacizumab are considered appropriate options [3,4,5].

NECC may coexist with non-neuroendocrine tumor components, such as adenocarcinoma or SCC. This histologic heterogeneity poses a unique challenge for optimal treatment selection. Specifically, while etoposide and cisplatin are the standard regimen for NECC, they may be suboptimal for treating non-neuroendocrine components. Conversely, paclitaxel, carboplatin, bevacizumab, and pembrolizumab constitute the standard regimen for adenocarcinoma or SCC of the cervix but may be ineffective against the neuroendocrine component [6,7,8].

We hypothesized that NECC with coexisting non-neuroendocrine components (mixed subtype) is associated with a worse prognosis than pure NECC. Previous studies examining the prognostic impact of tumor heterogeneity (pure vs. mixed subtypes) in NECC have yielded conflicting results [9,10,11,12]. Moreover, to our knowledge, most prior studies lacked clearly defined eligibility criteria. For example, many did not report the method of tissue sampling. We consider that small biopsy specimens (e.g., punch biopsy) may be insufficient to accurately differentiate between pure and mixed subtypes of NECC. Therefore, cases diagnosed solely via small biopsy should be excluded from analyses investigating tumor heterogeneity. Additionally, specimens obtained after chemotherapy or radiotherapy should also be excluded, as such treatments may alter tumor composition by selectively eliminating certain components.

The aim of this study was to investigate whether NECC with coexisting non-neuroendocrine tumor components (mixed subtype) is associated with a worse prognosis than pure NECC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Patients and variables

This was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study conducted at 4 tertiary referral hospitals in the Republic of Korea. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of each participating institution (representative approval No. B-2402-882-102, Seoul National University Bundang Hospital), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

We included patients diagnosed with NECC based on pathological evaluation between August 1997 and November 2023 at the participating hospitals. To ensure accurate classification of tumor heterogeneity, all diagnoses were confirmed using surgical specimens obtained via conization or hysterectomy prior to any chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

Patients were excluded if the diagnosis was made solely from small biopsy specimens such as punch biopsies, as such samples may be insufficient to accurately assess tumor heterogeneity and are susceptible to misclassification. Additionally, patients who received chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy as initial treatment before obtaining an adequately sized biopsy specimen were excluded, since these therapies may alter the tumor composition and confound assessment of tumor heterogeneity.

Clinical, pathological, and treatment-related data were collected through retrospective review of medical records. Variables included age, height, weight, 2018 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage, histologic subtype (small cell, large cell, or mixed), tumor heterogeneity (pure vs. mixed with non-neuroendocrine components), initial treatment modality, and disease progression.

2. Analysis

The primary outcome of this study was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from the date of initial diagnosis to the date of first documented disease progression or recurrence. Patients who had not experienced an event at the time of analysis were censored at the date of their last follow-up.

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups (pure vs. mixed subtypes) were compared using the log-rank test.

For multivariate analysis, a Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to evaluate the association between tumor heterogeneity and PFS, adjusting for potential confounders including age, height, weight, FIGO 2018 stage, histologic subtype, and initial treatment modality.

A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using the lifelines package in Python 3.11 on the Google Colab platform. Patients with missing data were excluded from the multivariate analysis.

The mean age of the cohort was approximately 50 years. Patients with the pure subtype were significantly younger than those with the mixed subtype (p=0.018). Most patients were diagnosed at FIGO stage I (62.0%), and the majority of tumors were of the small cell type. Initial treatment included surgery in 87.6% of patients, chemotherapy in 81.0%, and radiotherapy in 44.6%. Recurrence and death were observed in 61 (50.4%) and 24 (19.8%) patients, respectively.

2. Association of tumor heterogeneity with PFS

In univariate analysis, there was no significant difference in PFS between patients with the pure and mixed subtypes (p=0.952; Fig. 1A). However, after adjusting for covariates, the mixed subtype was significantly associated with worse PFS compared to the pure subtype (adjusted hazard ratio [HR]=3.16; 95% confidence interval [CI]=1.58–6.35; p=0.001), as shown in Table 2 and Fig. 1B.

DISCUSSION

This multicenter study demonstrated that the mixed subtype of NECC is independently associated with worse PFS compared to the pure subtype, after adjusting for relevant clinical and pathological variables.

Previous literature has reported inconsistent results regarding the prognostic impact of tumor heterogeneity in NECC. For instance, Bermúdez et al. [9] retrospectively analyzed 20 patients. They found that the OS rate was 54% in the pure subtype group vs. 19% in the mixed subtype group, suggesting a worse prognosis for the mixed subtype. Chan et al. [10] analyzed 34 patients with small cell NECC diagnosed between 1979 and 2001 at 2 hospitals. In univariate analysis, the pure subtype was paradoxically associated with poorer survival. However, in multivariable analysis, tumor heterogeneity was not significantly associated with survival. In contrast, Lee et al. [11] conducted a retrospective review of 61 patients diagnosed with NECC between 2000 and 2014 across 2 Korean institutions. Their multivariate analysis identified FIGO stage, tumor size, and tumor heterogeneity as independent prognostic factors for OS. Ishikawa et al. [12] reported a multicenter retrospective cohort of 193 patients with NECC. In their multivariable analysis, locally advanced disease, para-aortic lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and fewer than 4 cycles of chemotherapy were significantly associated with poor survival. However, histologic subtype and pelvic node metastasis were not found to have prognostic significance. More recently, Lyu et al. [13] conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of 98 patients with high-grade NECC, all histologically confirmed by cervical biopsy before surgery. Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that mixed histology was a protective factor impacting OS and PFS. Meanwhile, ovarian preservation, tumor size >4 cm, parity >3, age >45 years and perineural invasion were poor prognostic predictors.

Given the rarity of NECC and its morphological overlap with other cervical malignancies such as poorly differentiated SCC and adenocarcinoma, accurate diagnosis is challenging. Initial biopsy specimens often fail to fully represent the tumor’s histologic composition, which can lead to misclassification. Notably, many previous studies did not exclude cases diagnosed solely on small biopsy specimens, raising concerns about the validity of subtype classification. To address this issue, the current study excluded cases diagnosed by small biopsy alone, thereby improving the accuracy of tumor heterogeneity assessment and enhancing the reliability of our findings.

However, this study has several limitations. First, although it was a multicenter study, all patients were Korean women, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Second, variations in surgical approach, resection margin status, and chemotherapy regimens were not fully standardized across centers, which may have influenced clinical outcomes. Future prospective studies with larger, ethnically diverse cohorts and standardized treatment protocols are warranted to validate these findings and to better inform therapeutic strategies for patients with NECC.

In conclusion, mixed subtype NECC is associated with significantly worse PFS compared to the pure subtype. Our findings highlight the prognostic importance of tumor heterogeneity and support the need for individualized treatment approaches that account for histologic composition in future clinical guidelines.
