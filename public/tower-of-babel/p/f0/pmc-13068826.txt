High and low residual feed intake: Associations with meat quality, energy-lipid metabolism, and immune performance in late-laying hens
Authors: Zhouyang Gao, Yuhui Qin, Jing Yang, Yuchen Jie, Jiangxia Zheng, Guiyun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResidual feed intake (RFI) is considered a more accurate indicator of feed efficiency and is widely applied to determine the production performance of poultry. However, limited research has been conducted on the effects of RFI on the meat quality of hens. This study explored how different RFIs regulate meat quality in late-laying hens, dividing them into lowest (LRFI) and highest (HRFI) RFI groups. Regarding production performance, two groups showed no significant differences in body weight and egg production; however, the LRFI group exhibited reduced accumulation of liver and abdominal fat and more tender with a high intramuscular fat content. Mechanistic analysis demonstrated that the elevated levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in the LRFI group activated FFAR2/3 signaling, which subsequently triggered the NRF1/AMPK/mTOR axis. Activation of this axis accelerated central carbon metabolism and enhanced mitochondrial oxidative metabolism as well as fatty acid β-oxidation. This coordinated metabolic reprogramming improved antioxidant capacity and maintained immune homeostasis, ultimately promoting a more favorable lipid deposition pattern and enhancing meat quality.Keywords: Residual feed intake, Meat quality, Lipid and energy metabolism, Immune performance, Laying hen

Introduction

Eggs and chicken meat constitute major components of the human diet due to their affordability and high nutritional value (Ederer et al., 2023; Walker and Baum, 2022). To improve production efficiency, the rearing cycle of commercial laying hens has been extended from approximately 72 weeks to 80–100 weeks. However, prolonged production is accompanied by age-associated declines in egg yield, egg quality, and meat quality (Janczak and Riber, 2015). These phenotypic changes are frequently coupled with impaired antioxidant capacity, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and disruptions in lipid metabolism (Yang et al., 2023). Despite these observations, the molecular mechanisms underlying metabolic alterations in aged laying hens remain insufficiently defined. In particular, the integration of gut microbiota dynamics with host lipid metabolic regulation during the late laying period has not been systematically elucidated. Therefore, this study aims to clarify the molecular mechanisms governing lipid metabolism in aged hens, with a focus on identifying key regulatory pathways and their functional implications for production performance and product quality.

Lipid metabolism is a central determinant of physiological function and production performance in laying hens, particularly during the late laying period. In poultry, lipids are predominantly synthesized in the liver and subsequently transported to peripheral tissues, making hepatic lipid homeostasis critical for maintaining metabolic balance (Xiao et al., 2020). Under intensive production conditions, factors such as high-energy diets, nutrient imbalance, unrestricted feeding, and genetic predisposition can promote excessive hepatic lipid accumulation (Guo et al., 2025). This dysregulation often culminates in fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS), a disorder with increasing incidence in aged hens. Mechanistically, FLHS is characterized by enhanced lipogenesis, impaired β-oxidation, elevated triglyceride accumulation, and reduced very low-density lipoprotein secretion, reflecting a systemic disruption of hepatic lipid turnover (Anene et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2019).

Lipid metabolism is tightly coupled with energy metabolism through coordinated hepatic regulatory networks. The liver integrates mitochondrial function, central carbon metabolism, and gene expression programs to govern lipid synthesis, oxidation, and transport. Under conditions of energy surplus, excess substrates are converted into triglycerides via de novo lipogenesis, whereas energy demand promotes lipolysis and fatty acid β-oxidation, generating acetyl-CoA for entry into the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Hardy et al., 2016). Disruption of mitochondrial dynamics, particularly the balance between fusion and fission, impairs oxidative capacity and contributes to lipid accumulation (Li et al., 2024). In addition, extrahepatic factors—including gut microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and circulating appetite-regulating hormones—modulate hepatic metabolic pathways by influencing energy intake, receptor-mediated signaling, and transcriptional regulation of lipid metabolism (Beldowska et al., 2023; He et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2025).

Alterations in lipid metabolism during the late laying period have direct consequences for meat quality, a key determinant of poultry product value. The composition of intramuscular fat, amino acids, and fatty acids governs both nutritional properties and sensory attributes such as flavor and tenderness (Mir et al., 2017; Xu and Yin, 2024). Hepatic lipid metabolic imbalance can drive ectopic fat deposition in muscle tissues, thereby disrupting the protein-to-fat ratio and compromising meat quality (Wang et al., 2017). Moreover, lipid metabolic disorders can alter fatty acid profiles, including reductions in unsaturated fatty acids, leading to diminished flavor and oxidative stability (Han et al., 2023; He et al., 2021). Excess energy intake and abdominal fat accumulation further exacerbate these effects, negatively impacting carcass traits, meat appearance, and shelf life, ultimately reducing consumer acceptance and economic value (Fouad and El-Senousey, 2014).

Residual feed intake (RFI) is a widely accepted indicator of feed efficiency, defined as the deviation between actual and expected feed intake based on maintenance and production requirements (Liu et al., 2024). In poultry, RFI has been increasingly applied to investigate the metabolic basis of feed efficiency, providing insights into nutritional regulation during growth and the physiological adaptations of laying hens in the late production stage (Chang et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024). Emerging evidence indicates that variation in RFI is closely associated with hepatic metabolic reprogramming in aged hens. Specifically, high-RFI individuals tend to exhibit impaired metabolic efficiency, characterized by enhanced oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and disruptions in lipid metabolism (Gao et al., 2025).

However, the mechanistic links between RFI variation and hepatic lipid metabolic pathways remain insufficiently defined, particularly regarding their downstream effects on meat quality. The lack of integrated analysis connecting feed efficiency, metabolic regulation, and product quality limits the development of targeted strategies for late-phase production management. To address this gap, the present study systematically evaluated meat quality traits, including amino acid composition, fatty acid profiles, crude protein, and crude fat content, in late-laying hens with divergent RFI phenotypes. Concurrently, key indicators of hepatic lipid and energy metabolism were assessed to elucidate the metabolic differences between high- and low-RFI groups. The primary objective was to clarify the molecular mechanisms linking RFI to metabolic phenotypes, with an emphasis on hepatic lipid metabolism (Fig. 1). These findings aim to provide a theoretical basis for optimizing nutritional management and improving product quality in late laying hens, while also contributing to the development of breeding strategies for enhanced feed efficiency.

Sample collection

In the present study, we employed a subset of animals and corresponding data obtained from an extensive long-term longitudinal investigation detailed in our prior work (Gao et al., 2025). Based on the calculated RFI values, six hens with the lowest RFI (LRFI group) and six hens with the highest RFI (HRFI group) were selected and assigned to two groups for subsequent analyses. At the end of the measurement period (70 weeks of age), all hens in both groups were humanely euthanized. After slaughter, the liver and abdominal fat weights of each hen were measured and recorded to calculate the liver index and abdominal fat percentage. Cecal contents and liver tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80°C; cecal content samples were used for short-chain fatty acid determination, whereas liver samples were used for subsequent physicochemical analyses. Fresh whole muscle samples were collected from the right pectoralis major and longitudinally divided into upper and lower portions along the muscle fiber direction. The upper portion was used for meat quality evaluation, while the lower portion was used for amino acid and fatty acid analyses and stored at −20°C until further analysis.

Meat quality measurement

Determination of shear force in pectoral muscle: Each cooked meat sample was trimmed into columns measuring 2.0 cm × 1.0 cm × 1.0 cm along the direction of the muscle fibers. The center of each column was placed under the wedge blade of a texture analyzer, ensuring that the direction of the muscle fibers was perpendicular to the blade. The TA-XTplus Texture Analyzer was activated, and the shear force measuring blade was installed and calibrated. Each meat column was tested once, and the shear force values were recorded.

Determination of crude fat in pectoral muscle: The first method (Soxhlet extraction method) of the national standard “GB/T 5009.6-2016 National Food Safety Standard for the Determination of Fat in Food” was followed for estimating crude fat. The intramuscular fat (IMF) content (%) was calculated as follows: (fat weight / dry weight of muscle) × 100.

Determination of crude protein in pectoral muscle: Crude protein was quantified in accordance with the national standard “GB/T 6432-2018 Determination of Crude Protein in Feed.”

Fatty acid and amino acid profile analysis

Amino acid profile analysis: Briefly, 100 mg breast muscle samples were homogenized in liquid nitrogen, diluted 10-fold with 0.9 mL ultrapure water, and subsequently diluted to 50-fold. Next, 50 µL of the diluted sample was mixed with 200 µL acetonitrile/CH₃OH (1:1, v/v) containing isotopic internal standards, vortexed, incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was analyzed by ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS; ExionLC™ AD UHPLC-QTRAP 6500, AB SCIEX).

Fatty acid profile analysis: Briefly, 100 mg breast muscle samples were placed in 2 mL grinding tubes with steel beads, mixed with 1 mL dichloromethane: CH₃OH (1:1), ground at 50 Hz for 3 min, ultrasonically treated at low temperature for 15 min, and incubated at −20°C for 15 min. After centrifugation (13,000 × g, 4°C, 10 min), 500 µL supernatant was dried under nitrogen. The residue was dissolved in 0.5 mL methylation reagent (0.5 mol/L NaOH—CH₃OH), vortexed, sonicated at 4°C for 10 min, and placed in a water bath at 60°C for 30 min. Post-cooling, 0.5 mL n-hexane was added, and the mixture was vortexed and centrifuged (13,000 × g, 4°C, 10 min). Next, 100 µL of the upper n-hexane layer was analyzed by the Agilent 8890B-5977B GC/MS system.

Breast lipidomics analysis

Briefly, 80 mg breast samples were weighed, spiked with internal lipid standards, and homogenized (MP homogenizer, 6.0 m/s, 60 s × 2) with 200 μL water and 240 μL methanol. After adding 800 μL MTBE, the mixture was sonicated at 4°C for 20 min, incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and centrifuged (14,000 × g, 15 min, 10°C). The supernatant was dried under nitrogen, reconstituted in 200 μL of 90 % isopropanol/acetonitrile, recentrifuged, and injected (3 μL) into the system. QC samples (pooled from 10 μL of each sample) were analyzed at the start and at every 10 injections by using a UHPLC Nexera LC-30A/Q-Exactive Plus system (Shanghai Applied Protein Technology).

Determination of liver physiological indicators

A 0.5 g liver sample was used to prepare a liver homogenate. After mixing, the sample was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. A reagent kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) was used to measure TG, total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels. All tests were conducted strictly according to the operating procedures outlined in the instruction manual.

Determination of ATPase series indices in the liver

Mitochondria were isolated from hen liver by using Beyotime kits (C3606 and C3601). Fresh liver tissue was homogenized, and the homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 11,000 × g for 10 min to pellet mitochondria. Activities of ATPase enzymes (Na+/K+, Ca2+/Mg2+, Ca2+, and Mg2+) were measured using kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Determination of central carbon cycle metabolites in the liver

Liver samples (100 mg) were homogenized in 500 μL methanol/water (4:1, v/v) mixed with internal standards, incubated for 5 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Next, 2 μL of the supernatant was analyzed by UHPLC-MS/MS (ExionLC™ AD-QTRAP® 6500+, AB SCIEX).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis of liver tissues

RNA was extracted from 50 to 100 mg liver tissue samples by using TRIzol reagent (ZF Biology), and RNA quality/concentration was evaluated by the Epoch 2 microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek). Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA by using the BeyoRT™ II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, and the obtained cDNA was stored at −20°C. qPCR was performed with BeyoFast™ SYBR Green qPCR Mix on the QuantStudio 7500 PCR system by using β-actin as the internal control. The primers (Sangon Biotech) are listed in Table 2. The relative mRNA expression level was calculated by the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Determination of SCFAs content in cecal contents

Cecal contents (0.5 g) were homogenized in 400 μL of 80 % methanol and centrifuged to remove proteins. The supernatant was derivatized with 150 μL of reagent at 40°C for 40 min. Next, 125 μL of the derivatized supernatant was mixed with 875 μL of internal standard solution and analyzed by UHPLC-MS/MS (Vanquish™ Flex-TSQ Altis™, Thermo Scientific).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 23.0. Normality tests were conducted for the data. Student’s t-test was used for data with normal distribution, while Kruskal-Wallis test was used for data with non-normal distribution. Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001, with trends at 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.

Results

Growth performance and meat quality

First, we measured the production performance and meat quality-related indices of hens. Regarding production performance, the HRFI group exhibited a significantly higher RFI67-69 than the LRFI group (P < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed between the two groups in egg production at 70 weeks of age and body weight (P > 0.05) (Fig. S1). In addition, the liver index and abdominal fat percentage were significantly higher in the HRFI group than in the LRFI group (P < 0.05). Regarding meat quality indices, the IMF content in the breast muscle of the LRFI group was significantly higher than that of the HRFI group, while the shear force was significantly lower in the LRFI group than in the HRFI group (P < 0.05). The two groups showed no significant differences in crude protein content (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Fatty acid profile of the breast muscle

The fatty acid composition of the breast muscle was analyzed in the LRFI and HRFI groups (Table 4). A total of 50 fatty acids were identified, including 15 saturated fatty acids (SFAs), 21 monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and 14 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). Among the SFAs, the C11:0 and C13:0 fatty acid contents in the breast muscle were significantly higher in the HRFI group than in the LRFI group (P < 0.05). Among the MUFAs, the C14:1T, C24:1, and total MUFA (ƩMUFA) contents in the breast muscle were significantly lower in the HRFI group than in the LRFI group (P < 0.05). Regarding PUFAs, the C18:3(n-6) and C20:3(n-6) contents in the breast muscle were significantly higher in the HRFI group than in the LRFI group, while the C22:5(n-3) and C22:6 contents in the breast muscle were significantly lower in the HRFI group (P < 0.05). Notably, in the analysis of total n-3 and n-6 fatty acids, the LRFI group showed a significant increase in the content of n-3 series fatty acids and a significant decrease in the content of n-6 series fatty acids compared to the HRFI group (P < 0.05), leading to a decreasing trend in the Ʃn-6/Ʃn-3 ratio in the LRFI group (P = 0.066).

Lipidomics analysis of the breast muscle

By using UHPLC-MS/MS, we identified 3028 lipid molecules in the breast muscle samples of both LRFI and HRFI groups. The detected lipids mainly included 10 major classes, such as TG, phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), ceramide (Cer), cardiolipin (CL), diglyceride (DG), hexosylceramides (Hex1Cer and Hex2Cer), phosphatidylserine (PS), and sphingomyelin (SM) (Fig. 3A). Among these, TG, PC, and PE had relatively high abundance, with 478, 373, and 358 lipid species, accounting for 15.79 %, 12.32 %, and 11.82 %, respectively. Six primary lipid classes and 44 lipid subclasses were detected in the breast muscle of the LRFI and HRFI groups. In the breast muscle of the LRFI group, the identified primary lipid classes included glycerophospholipids (GP; 44.82 %), glycerolipids (GL; 36.90 %), sphingolipids (SP; 16.03 %), fatty acids (FA; 1.56 %), phospholipids (PL; 0.68 %), and sterol lipids (ST (0.02 %). The dominant lipid subclasses were TG (34.14 %), PC (21.48 %), PE (16.18 %), and Cer (8.80 %). In the breast muscle of the HRFI group, the detected primary lipid classes were GP (47.59 %), GL (33.93 %), SP (16.10 %), FA (1.69 %), PL (0.67 %), and ST (0.01 %), and the primary lipid subclasses were TG (31.07 %), PC (22.53 %), PE (17.83 %), and Cer (9.93 %) (Fig. 3B). Following data preprocessing, principal component analysis and partial least squares discriminant analysis were performed, along with validation using the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis model (Fig. 3F-H). The results showed clear clustering and adequate separation between the LRFI and HRFI groups, indicating significant differences in the lipid profiles of the breast muscle between the two groups. To identify differential lipids between LRFI and HRFI hens, we used the following parameters as screening criteria: P < 0.05, variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, and fold change (FC) > 2 or FC < 0.5. The volcano plot revealed 81 differentially expressed lipids, with 64 upregulated and 17 downregulated lipids in the LRFI group (Fig. 3C). These differentially expressed lipid types primarily included GP (53.09 %), GL (30.86 %), SP (14.81 %), and ST (1.23 %); the main lipid subclasses were PE (23.46 %), TG (18.52 %), CL (13.58 %), DG (12.35 %), PC (9.88 %), and Cer (6.17 %) (Fig. 3D, E). To better illustrate the differences in lipid distribution, a heatmap of the 81 differentially expressed lipids was generated based on their VIP values (Fig. 3I). The following lipids showed notable differences in their distribution (Fig. 3J-L): TG(18:1_18:1_18:2), TG(18:1_18:1_18:1), TG(18:0_18:1_18:1), TG(18:1_18:1_18:3), PC(16:0_18:2), PC(11:0_22:1), PE(36:1), PE(16:0_22:4)-H, PE(18:0_18:2)-H, PE(18:2_18:2)-H, PE(16:1e_18:1)-H, PE(36:0), PC(20:1e_18:1), and PE(16:1e_20:4)-H.

Hepatic lipid metabolism

To evaluate differences in lipid metabolism between the LRFI and HRFI group hens in the late egg-laying stage, we determined the lipid content in the livers of the experimental hens. The TG, TC, and LDL-C levels in the livers of HRFI hens were significantly higher than those in the livers of LRFI hens, while the HDL-C levels were significantly lower in HRFI hens than in LRFI hens (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A-D). To further investigate the differences in lipid metabolism between the LRFI and HRFI groups, we measured the expression of genes associated with fatty acid metabolism in the liver. The results revealed that, in the LRFI group, genes associated with fatty acid oxidation, such as CPT1 and ACOX1, were significantly upregulated, while genes related to fatty acid synthesis, such as FAS and SCD1, were significantly downregulated (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4E, F).

(C) Low-Density lipoprotein cholesterol in liver. (D) High-Density lipoprotein cholesterol in liver. (E) Relative expression of genes:lipid biosynthesis in liver. (F) Relative expression of genes: lipid oxidation in liver. Error bars indicate SEM (n = 6), * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01 and *** P < 0.001.

Hepatic energy metabolism

To investigate the mechanisms underlying mitochondrial dysfunction in the livers of HRFI group hens, we measured the activity of ATP-related enzymes and the expression of genes associated with mitochondrial dynamics (Fig. 5A). The activities of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺-ATPase were significantly higher in the livers of LRFI group hens than in the livers of HRFI group hens (P < 0.05); however, the two groups showed no significant differences in the activities of Ca²⁺-ATPase and Mg²⁺-ATPase (P > 0.05). A quantitative fluorescence expression analysis showed that, compared to the HRFI group, the TFAM gene associated with mitochondrial function regulation was significantly upregulated in the LRFI group (P < 0.05). Additionally, the genes Nrf1, AMPK, and Sirt1 associated with energy metabolism regulation were also significantly upregulated in the LRFI group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B, C).

Hepatic carbon metabolic cycle

Central carbon metabolism is a pivotal hub for energy metabolism and provides intermediates and energy for liver metabolic processes. In the present study, we used GC-MS to determine the levels of key intermediate metabolites related to glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in the livers of LRFI and HRFI hens. As shown in Fig. 5D, the levels of critical intermediates involved in the TCA cycle, such as acetyl-CoA, malic acid, pyruvic acid, succinic acid, and citric acid, were significantly higher in the LRFI group than in the HRFI group (P < 0.05). The two groups, however, showed no significant differences in the levels of other metabolites (P > 0.05) (Table S1).

Hepatic antioxidant defense and immunity

The immune status and antioxidant capacity of poultry significantly influence their lipid metabolism by regulating lipid synthesis, degradation, and transport pathways. Therefore, we assessed the expression of genes related to antioxidant enzymes in the livers of LRFI and HRFI group hens (Fig. 6A, B). Regarding antioxidant indicators, the mRNA levels of genes encoding GPX1 and GPX2 were significantly higher in the livers of LRFI group hens than in the livers of HRFI group hens (P < 0.05). Additionally, the mRNA level of the gene encoding SOD1 was also significantly elevated in the LRFI group compared to that in the HRFI group (P < 0.05); however, the two groups showed no significant differences in the gene expression levels of SOD2, SOD3, and GPX3 (P > 0.05). Regarding immune inflammation indicators, we measured the mRNA expression levels of proinflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) and anti-inflammatory factors (IL-10 and IL-4) in the liver tissues of HRFI and LRFI group hens. The results indicated that the mRNA expression levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in the livers of the HRFI group were significantly higher than those in the livers of the LRFI group, while the mRNA expression levels of IL-10 and IL-4 were significantly lower in the HRFI group than in the LRFI group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6C).

Analysis of cecal content SCFAs

We quantified the contents of acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, isovaleric acid, hexanoic acid, and valeric acid in both LRFI and HRFI groups. Regarding major SCFAs, the levels of acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid in the cecal contents of the LRFI group were significantly higher than those in the cecal contents of the HRFI group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7A). For minor SCFAs, isobutyric acid, valeric acid, and hexanoic acid levels were also significantly higher in the LRFI group than in the HRFI group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7B). Additionally, the total content of SCFAs in the cecal contents of the LRFI group was significantly increased compared to that in the cecal contents of the HRFI group (P < 0.05). As targets of SCFAs, the SCFA receptors FFAR2 and FFAR3 must be activated to exert their physiological effects. Therefore, we measured the gene expression levels of these SCFA receptors in the livers of LRFI and HRFI group hens. The expression of both FFAR2 and FFAR3 was significantly upregulated in the livers of LRFI group hens (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7C, D).

Discussion

Residual feed intake (RFI) is widely recognized as a robust indicator of feed efficiency in poultry production (Ramankevich et al., 2025). Previous studies have shown that selection for low RFI can reduce fat deposition without impairing meat quality, although inconsistent associations with carcass traits and intramuscular fat (IMF) have also been reported (Emamgholi Begli et al., 2017; Wen et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2020). These discrepancies highlight that the relationship between RFI and meat quality remains unclear, particularly in late-laying hens, where metabolic status is substantially altered. In the present study, we demonstrate that divergent RFI phenotypes do not affect body weight or egg production but are associated with marked differences in lipid deposition and meat quality. Specifically, LRFI hens exhibited reduced hepatic and abdominal fat accumulation alongside improved meat tenderness and higher IMF content. These findings indicate that improved feed efficiency can be decoupled from production performance while favorably modulating lipid partitioning and meat quality traits.

Body weight and egg production are critical economic traits in poultry production. Consistent with previous studies, RFI as a selection criterion can reduce feed intake without compromising weight gain or egg production, thereby improving feed efficiency (Fathi et al., 2021; Ramankevich et al., 2025). The present results support this conclusion, as no significant differences in body weight or egg production were observed between LRFI and HRFI hens. However, clear differences emerged in carcass traits, with HRFI hens exhibiting significantly higher liver indices and abdominal fat deposition than LRFI hens. This finding is consistent with previous reports in low-RFI poultry, where improved feed efficiency is associated with reduced fat accumulation and enhanced carcass composition (Bai et al., 2022). These results indicate that RFI divergence primarily affects energy partitioning rather than growth performance. In HRFI hens, excessive fat deposition and increased liver index suggest impaired lipid metabolic homeostasis, which may be exacerbated during the late laying stage due to age-related declines in basal metabolic rate and imbalances between energy intake and expenditure. Such metabolic disturbances are closely associated with pathological conditions, including fatty liver syndrome, inflammatory polarization, and ectopic lipid deposition (Van Eck et al., 2023). Furthermore, disrupted hepatic lipid metabolism—characterized by imbalances in fatty acid synthesis and oxidation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory activation—may alter muscle lipid composition, increasing saturated fatty acids while reducing unsaturated fatty acids. These changes can impair myofibrillar structure, thereby reducing meat tenderness and water-holding capacity. Mechanistically, these effects are likely mediated through dysfunction of the hepatic–muscle metabolic axis, mitochondrial impairment, and metabolic reprogramming, ultimately leading to deterioration in meat quality attributes (Fang et al., 2014; Van Every and Schmidt, 2021).

Meat quality is primarily determined by tenderness, intramuscular fat (IMF), and protein content (Li et al., 2016). In the present study, LRFI hens exhibited significantly higher IMF content and lower shear force compared with HRFI hens, indicating improved meat tenderness. As IMF is a key determinant of sensory quality, its moderate accumulation is positively associated with enhanced flavor, juiciness, and overall palatability (Elmasry et al., 2012; Mir et al., 2017). These differences in meat quality are closely linked to alterations in lipid composition and metabolism. Fatty acid profiles, including SFAs, MUFAs, and PUFAs, play critical roles in determining both nutritional value and sensory attributes (Wood and Scollan, 2022). In this study, LRFI hens exhibited a distinct fatty acid pattern characterized by relatively higher n-3 and lower n-6 fatty acid levels, along with reduced MUFA content, which may contribute to improved metabolic and inflammatory status (Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2021). Lipidomics analysis further revealed increased triglyceride (TG) accumulation and reduced phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) levels in LRFI hens. Enhanced TG deposition promotes IMF formation, whereas reduced PC and PE levels may facilitate membrane remodeling and postmortem tenderization, thereby contributing to improved meat texture (Huff Lonergan et al., 2010; Zhao et al., 2020). Collectively, these results indicate that LRFI hens exhibit coordinated changes in lipid deposition and composition, leading to enhanced IMF accumulation and improved meat tenderness.

Following advancements in lifestyle and spending levels, consumers are increasingly demanding high-quality meat products with health benefits based on the nutritional value, flavor, and texture of poultry meat. Tenderness, IMF, and crude protein content are the main parameters to assess poultry meat quality (Li et al., 2016). As a physical property of meat, tenderness is commonly measured by shear force, which is influenced by factors such as muscle protein, fat, and muscle fiber thickness. Finer muscle fibers and a higher fat content lead to lower shear force, resulting in more tender meat (Mir et al., 2017). IMF is a key indicator to determine meat quality and nutritional health; moderate fat deposition can enhance the sensory experience of meat and improve its flavor, tenderness, and juiciness (Elmasry et al., 2012). The present study found that the IMF content in the breast muscle of LRFI group hens was significantly higher than that of HRFI group hens, with a marked reduction in shear force. Fats and fatty acid composition are crucial factors that affect meat quality. Fatty acids can be classified into SFAs, MUFAs, n-3 PUFAs, and n-6 PUFAs (Wood and Scollan, 2022). The MUFAs content in the breast muscle of the LRFI group was significantly lower than that in the breast muscle of the HRFI group; this might be related to the higher total n-3 and lower total n-6 levels in the LRFI group. MUFAs and n-3 PUFAs play roles in regulating lipid metabolism and exert anti-inflammatory effects. MUFAs are positively correlated with meat aroma and acceptability, while excessively high levels of PUFAs may cause oxidative issues in muscles(Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2021). Lipidomics analysis showed that the TG content in the breast muscle of LRFI group hens was higher than that in HRFI group hens, with lower PC and PE levels in the former group. TG accumulation in fat droplets can improve meat tenderness and increase fat content. A reduction in PC and PE levels disrupts the integrity of muscle cell membranes, promoting postmortem tenderization and providing precursors for TG synthesis, thereby enhancing IMF deposition (Huff Lonergan et al., 2010; Zhao et al., 2020). In conclusion, the breast muscle of the LRFI group exhibited enhanced lipid deposition capacity and a more reasonable fatty acid composition, leading to improved IMF content and greater tenderness.

Energy metabolism is closely integrated with lipid metabolism and plays a central role in regulating metabolic homeostasis (Schirrmacher, 2020; Soliman and Safwat, 2020). In the present study, LRFI hens exhibited significantly elevated levels of key tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates, including acetyl-CoA, pyruvate, citric acid, succinic acid, and malic acid, indicating enhanced oxidative energy metabolism compared with HRFI hens. Given that TCA cycle activity reflects cellular energy status and is closely associated with metabolic disorders such as fatty liver syndrome (Fu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024), these results suggest improved metabolic efficiency in LRFI hens. Enhanced energy metabolism in LRFI hens was further supported by improved mitochondrial function. In addition, accompanied by upregulated expression of genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis and energy regulation, including Nrf1, mTOR, AMPK, and TFAM in LRFI groups (Deleyto-Seldas and Efeyan, 2021; Garcia and Shaw, 2017; Lou et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024). Consistently, the activities of ATP-related enzymes (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺-ATPase) were significantly elevated, indicating enhanced mitochondrial energy turnover. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LRFI hens exhibit coordinated enhancement of TCA cycle activity and mitochondrial function, which promotes efficient energy utilization and fatty acid oxidation, thereby reducing lipid accumulation and contributing to improved meat quality (Martínez-Reyes and Chandel, 2020; Zhang et al., 2025).

Immune and antioxidant status are critical regulators of metabolic homeostasis and meat quality in late-laying hens (Ding et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2019). In the present study, LRFI hens exhibited significantly enhanced antioxidant and anti-inflammatory profiles, as evidenced by upregulated expression of SOD1, GPX1, GPX2, IL-10, and IL-4, alongside reduced expression of proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α. These changes suggest an improved systemic metabolic environment in LRFI hens. Elevated proinflammatory factors are known to activate NF-κB signaling, suppress fatty acid oxidation, and promote hepatic lipid accumulation, thereby impairing energy utilization efficiency (Li et al., 2015). In contrast, anti-inflammatory cytokines contribute to maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and preventing chronic inflammation-induced metabolic disorders (Di Vincenzo et al., 2024). Moreover, enhanced antioxidant capacity mitigates mitochondrial oxidative damage and preserves fatty acid β-oxidation efficiency, thereby limiting lipid accumulation (Włodarski et al., 2020). Collectively, these findings indicate that LRFI hens maintain a more balanced immune and redox state, which supports efficient lipid and energy metabolism and contributes to reduced fat deposition and improved meat quality.

The gut–liver axis plays a central role in regulating host lipid metabolism and meat quality in laying hens (Beldowska et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024). In the present study, LRFI hens exhibited significantly higher concentrations of total and individual SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, compared with HRFI hens, consistent with previous reports of enriched SCFA-producing microbiota in low-RFI phenotypes (Gao et al., et al., 2025). Given that SCFAs are key modulators of lipid and energy metabolism (Frampton et al., 2020), these findings suggest enhanced metabolic regulation in LRFI hens. SCFAs exert coordinated regulatory effects on host metabolism through multiple pathways. Propionate promotes fatty acid oxidation and inhibits lipogenesis via activation of the AMPK pathway, while butyrate enhances intestinal barrier function, reduces inflammation, and stimulates lipid catabolism through PPAR signaling. Acetate contributes to systemic energy supply and metabolic flexibility, although its effects on lipid synthesis are dose-dependent (Wang et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2025). In addition, SCFAs improve energy homeostasis by supporting gluconeogenesis, mitochondrial function, and peripheral energy utilization (Chapp et al., 2024; Heimann et al., 2015; Li et al., 2022). These metabolic effects are further linked to improvements in meat quality, including optimized fatty acid composition, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and improved muscle structural properties (Fushimi et al., 2001; Ma et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023). Consistently, the expression of SCFA receptors FFAR2 and FFAR3 was significantly upregulated in LRFI hens, indicating enhanced SCFA signaling. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that increased SCFA production and signaling along the gut–liver axis could potentially promote metabolic homeostasis, reduce lipid deposition, and contribute to improved meat quality in LRFI hens. It is worth noting that, although SCFAs and FFAR2/FFAR3 are closely associated with improved metabolic phenotypes in LRFI hens, the evidence remains correlative. Future studies employing SCFAs supplementation and receptor-targeted interventions are required to establish causality and clarify underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

In summary, this study analyzed the growth performance, meat quality, lipid metabolism, and energy metabolism indicators of HRFI and LRFI hens in the late egg-laying period. Compared to HRFI group hens, LRFI group hens exhibited a significant improvement in breast muscle tenderness, showed an optimal composition of beneficial fatty acids in the muscle, and displayed reduced abdominal and liver fat deposition without affecting production performance. The underlying mechanism might be related to the significantly higher levels of SCFAs in LRFI group hens. These compounds accelerate the body’s energy metabolism rate, balance lipid metabolism, regulate fat deposition rate, enhance immune performance, and improve muscle lipid composition, eventually resulting in better meat quality. This study provides a theoretical basis for balancing breeding efficiency and livestock product quality in poultry production and offers new insights for subsequent breeding practices and the analysis of mechanisms for RFI-based regulation of meat quality.
