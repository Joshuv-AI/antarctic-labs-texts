How Growth Cones Sense Extracellular Cues and Drive Neuronal Migration
Authors: Takahiro Kanzawa, Chikako Nakajima, Masato Sawada, Kazunobu Sawamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMigrating neurons form a growth cone at the tip of their leading process. This specialized structure shares striking anatomical and functional similarities with axonal growth cones. We hypothesize that both cones respond to common extracellular cues and direct neuronal migration and axon extension, respectively, through analogous mechanisms. Guidance cues provide growth cones with attractive or repulsive signals to direct them towards their targets. By binding to specific receptors on growth cones, these cues trigger intracellular signaling pathways that reorganize the cytoskeleton and propel neurons or axons in precise directions. Notably, many of the receptors that mediate axon guidance are also present in the growth cones of migrating neurons, reinforcing the idea of a conserved molecular machinery. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying growth cone dynamics in migrating neurons promises to deepen our understanding of neuronal development, and to pave the way for new regenerative therapies aimed at promoting neuronal migration.

Introduction

The axonal growth cone is a crucial structure located at the tip of the axon that governs both the direction and growth of the axon during neural circuit formation. By interpreting signals from the surrounding environment, the growth cone steers the axon toward its appropriate target [1, 2, 3]. In this process, molecules known as guidance cues play a central role.

Recent studies have identified growth cone‐like structures at the tips of the leading process of migrating neurons [4, 5], and we have further demonstrated that elongating axons and migrating neurons share common morphologies and molecular components at their growth cones, underscoring their functional significance in regulating neuronal migration [6]. Here, we extend the original use of the term ‘growth cone,’ first coined by Ramón y Cajal to describe axonal projections [7], to also describe the leading processes of migrating newborn neurons, given their structural and molecular similarities.

During forebrain development, neural stem cells reside near the ventricles, where they generate new neurons that migrate to their destinations via specific pathways. After birth, most neural stem cells are depleted, but some persist in specialized regions, such as the ventricular–subventricular zone (V‐SVZ) and the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus in the hippocampus. In these regions, neural stem cells continue to produce new neurons, known as neuroblasts, throughout life [8, 9, 10]. For instance, neuroblasts generated in the V‐SVZ migrate to the olfactory bulb (the primary center for olfaction) [11]. However, in response to brain injury, a subset of these neurons can also migrate from the V‐SVZ to the lesion site. Some of these neurons integrate into the damaged area, replacing lost neurons and contributing to functional improvements [12, 13, 14, 15]. Intriguingly, similar to axonal projections, neuronal migration is also guided by a combination of attractive and repulsive guidance cues.

In setting out our hypothesis, we first briefly introduce the current understanding of the structure of the growth cone, and then discuss key factors involved in axon guidance and their functions in neuronal migration because many of the guidance cues that regulate axonal projection also direct neuronal migration [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. While it is now evident that growth cones are present in migrating neurons, the mechanisms by which these growth cones drive migration are only beginning to be elucidated.

It is also important to recognize that the spatial contexts in which axon elongation and neuronal migration occur are fundamentally different. Axon growth is widely observed during the fetal period across various brain regions, including in corpus callosum formation, in projections from the cerebral cortex to the striatum and from the striatum to the globus pallidus, and in corticospinal projections. These axon‐extending neurons are broadly distributed, and their projections span multiple and diverse brain areas.

In contrast, neurons generated in the V‐SVZ migrate to the olfactory bulb. Such neurons originate from a restricted developmental source and migrate along clearly defined pathways. Therefore, axon elongation and neuronal migration occur within distinct environmental and anatomical landscapes, which likely influence both the behavior and molecular regulation of growth cones in each context. Building on these insights, we highlight the recently characterized features common to both elongating axons and migrating neurons, underscoring how the growth cone at the tip of the leading process controls neuronal migration through mechanisms shared with the axonal growth cone.

We also examine distinctions between elongating axons and migrating neurons, including how differences in the extracellular environment may differentially influence the behavior and function of growth cones in each setting.

We propose that the growth cone of migrating neurons acts as a crucial regulator of migration by responding to extracellular factors, such as axon guidance cues. Understanding these processes may inform strategies to restore brain function after injury by modulating the extracellular environment.

Guidance Cues for Axonal Growth Cones and Their Function in Neuronal Migration

Structure of the Axonal Growth Cone

The growth cone is a fan‐shaped structure comprising three domains: the central domain (C domain), the peripheral domain (P domain), and the transition zone (T zone), which lies between the C and P domains (Figure 1a,b). The P domain occupies the outer edge of the growth cone and contains filopodia, long, thin protrusions formed by bundled actin filaments (F‐actin bundles), and lamellipodia, which are broad, sheet‐like structures supported by a branched F‐actin network [23, 24]. The C‐domain, situated at the center of the growth cone, contains numerous organelles, vesicles, a central actin bundle, and stable bundled microtubules that extend from the axonal shaft. The T‐zone features an actomyosin contractile structure called the actin arc. This arc forms a half‐ring within the T‐zone [25] that is oriented perpendicular to the F‐actin bundles [25]. Actin dynamics in these regions play a critical role in growth cone motility.

Axon elongation occurs when the axon encounters an attractive and adhesive substrate. This process begins as the distal end of the growth cone contacts the substrate. This interaction activates the intracellular signaling cascade through receptor binding, forming a “molecular clutch” that links the substrate to the actin cytoskeleton [26]. F‐actin bundles typically polymerize towards the leading edge of the growth cone, generating a pushing force on the surrounding membrane. Simultaneously, under the contractile force of myosin II, F‐actin undergoes retrograde flow towards the T zone, where it is severed and degraded [27]. When F‐actin polymerization continues at the leading edge in the clutch region, the lamellipodia and filopodia extend, enabling the growth cone to advance and propel the axon forward [28] (Figure 1c). A recent three‐dimensional culture study revealed a distinct mode of axonal growth in which growth cones extend axons independently of substrate adhesion and traction forces. This finding contrasts with conventional two‐dimensional culture systems and highlights fundamental differences in the mechanisms of axon elongation under different spatial contexts [29].

Guidance Cues in the Axonal Growth Cone and Their Function in Neuronal Migration

Axon guidance cues provide either attractive or repulsive signals that direct axons to their correct targets. Once they bind to receptors on the growth cone, intracellular signaling cascades are triggered [30], thereby altering cytoskeletal dynamics and ultimately steering axonal movement (Figure 2). Key guidance cues include netrins (Figure 3a), slits (Figure 3b), semaphorins (Figure 3c), sonic hedgehog (Shh, Figure 3d), bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs, Figure 3e), Wnts (Figure 3f), glycosaminoglycans (CSPGs/HSPGs/KSPGs, Figures 1, 3), Nogo/myelin‐associated glycoprotein (MAG)/myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG, Figure 3h), and ephrins (Figure 3i). These molecules work together to create a finely tuned system that orchestrates precise neuronal connections [31]. Netrins

Netrin is a secreted protein that was initially identified as a guidance cue and that directs both neuronal migration and axon outgrowth during neural development. In mammals, there are three secreted Netrins, Netrin 1, 3, and 4, and two membrane‐bound Netrins, Netrin G1, and G2 [32]. All netrin receptors are single‐pass transmembrane proteins belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily. They include deleted in colorectal cancer (DCC), neogenin (a paralog of DCC in vertebrates), members of the uncoordinated 5 (UNC5) homolog family, Down syndrome cell adhesion molecule (DSCAM), and Netrin G ligand (NGL). The specific receptor combination on the growth cone determines whether the netrin signaling is attractive or repulsive. For instance, netrin binding to a DCC homodimer or a DCC/neogenin heterodimer triggers an attractive response via Rac1 activation, whereas binding to a DCC/UNC5 or UNC5/DSCAM heterodimer induces repulsion through RhoA activation [33] (Figure 3a).

Beyond its role in axon guidance, Netrin 1 also plays critical roles in neuronal migration. In vivo evidence from genetic models has shown that during cerebellar and hindbrain development, Netrin 1 attracts migrating neuronal precursors originating from the lower rhombic lip towards the ventral midline to form the pontine nuclei [34]. This migration requires DCC expression in the progenitor cells and Netrin 1 expression by midline cells. Further mechanistic studies have demonstrated that this process depends on RhoA‐mediated nucleokinesis [35]. Mice deficient in α3β1 integrin or Netrin 1 exhibit distinct abnormalities in interneuron migration along the developing cortical plate, indicating that Netrin 1 promotes their migration via α3β1 integrin [36]. In the postnatal brain of Netrin 1 knockout mice, the olfactory bulb is significantly reduced in size during the early postnatal period, and the number of interneurons derived from the V‐SVZ is markedly decreased, suggesting that Netrin 1 is critical for efficient neuronal migration toward the olfactory bulb [37] (Figure 4a,b).

Slits

Slits are large extracellular matrix proteins comprising Slit1–3, while their receptors, the Roundabout receptor family (Robo1–4), are single‐pass transmembrane proteins without intrinsic enzymatic or autocatalytic activity [38]. When Slit binds to Robo, it facilitates the binding of the Rho‐GTPase‐activating protein (srGAP) to the intracellular domain of Robo, which either inactivates the small GTPase, cdc42, [39] or stimulates Rac activity [40]. This leads to decreased actin polymerization and the induction of a repulsive response (Figure 3b). Slit‐Robo signaling in spinal commissural axons is conserved across vertebrates and directs axons to cross the midline by switching from attraction to repulsion during axonal elongation [41, 42]. Furthermore, triple knockout of Slit1–3 in mice leads to severe axonal recrossing and stalling of spinal commissural neurons at the midline [41].

Slits also function as repellents for migrating neurons. When Slit‐expressing cells were grafted onto fetal brain slice cultures, neurons migrating from the lateral ganglionic eminence to the neocortex exhibited a repulsive response [43]. Similarly, neuronal migration from the V‐SVZ to the olfactory bulb was inhibited by grafting Slit‐expressing cells into the rostral migratory stream [39, 44] (Figure 4a,b). Supporting these observations, in vivo assays have confirmed that Slit signaling repels migrating neurons in various mammalian systems [45, 46]. Robo receptors are therefore crucial for neuronal migration; Robo1 governs the migration of cortical interneurons [47, 48], whereas Robo4 controls the migration of cortical projection neurons [49].

Semaphorins

In vertebrates, semaphorins consist of five subfamilies, Semaphorin 3–7 [50], while their receptors are members of the PlexinA‐D families and Neuropilin 1/2 [31]. Semaphorins are typically known for their actions as repulsive cues, although they can also promote attraction in certain contexts [51]. For example, Semaphorin 3A (Sema3A) binds with high affinity to Neuropilin 1 at the tips of the growth cone filopodia. This Sema3A/Neuropilin 1 complex then interacts with PlexinA1 on the surface of the growth cone, inducing its collapse through intracellular signaling cascades that negatively regulate the actin and microtubule cytoskeletons [52, 53, 54] (Figure 3c). In vitro experiments using dorsal root ganglion neurons have demonstrated that Sema3A induces axonal repulsion through the Plexin – Neuropilin complex, triggering growth cone collapse – a critical event in axon pathfinding [55].

Semaphorins also guide various migrating neurons during embryonic and postnatal development [50]. Studies of the fetal brain using knockout models have shown that migrating interneurons expressing Neuropilins are repelled by Sema3A/3F expressed in the striatum and are thereby directed toward the cerebral cortex [56]. Conversely, in vivo studies have demonstrated that conditional knockout of Neuropilin 1 impairs radial migration, whereas Transwell chemotaxis assays indicate that newborn neurons are attracted to Sema3A gradients [57]. Beyond simple repulsion or attraction, in vivo experiments using knockout animals have revealed that interactions between Sema6A and Plexin A2/A4 in radial glial cells regulate the termination of superficial‐layer cortical neuron migration [58]. In addition, Sema3E–PlexinD1 signaling contributes to the cessation of migration of both cortical projection neurons and olfactory interneurons [59] (Figure 4a,b). These findings indicate that specific combinations of Sema, Plexin, and Neuropilin subtypes coordinate not only neuronal repulsion and attraction but also migration initiation and termination. As a potential clinical application, implantation of a hydrogel device containing a defined Sema3A gradient promoted neural progenitor cell migration and differentiation, ultimately leading to cortical tissue regeneration after brain injury [60].

Shh

Shh is a morphogen secreted from the basal plate that plays a key role in neural development. It initially regulates the formation of different ventral neuronal subtypes along the anteroposterior axis and determines the cell fate of spinal cord progenitor cells through transcriptional regulation by the Gli family of transcription factors [31]. Once cell fate is specified, the Shh gradient functions as a chemoattractant, acting independently of transcription to guide commissural neuron axons to the ventral midline. Shh signaling is mediated by Src family kinases in the growth cone, which regulate the cytoskeletal remodeling necessary for growth cone turning [61]. In addition, when Shh binds to the receptors Boc and Ptch1, it stimulates local translation of actin mRNA within the growth cone, thereby driving polarized actin polymerization and enabling the growth cone to migrate along the Shh gradient [61, 62, 63] (Figure 3d).

In addition to its established role in axon guidance, overexpression of Shh in the developing chick optic tectum alters the fate of neural precursor cells via Gli‐dependent transcriptional activity, thereby inhibiting the migration of tectal neurons [64]. In contrast, the Shh receptor, Ptch1, is expressed on neurons derived from the V‐SVZ, and Shh promotes the migration of these V‐SVZ‐derived neurons both in vitro and in vivo through a Smoothened‐dependent signaling pathway [65, 66] (Figure 4b). These contrasting effects on cell migration may be attributed to differences in the stage‐specific responsiveness to Shh signaling, depending on whether the signaling acts through transcription‐dependent or transcription‐independent mechanisms, and whether it occurs before or after cell fate has been specified.

BMPs

The BMP family consists of more than 20 members, grouped into subfamilies conserved across vertebrates and invertebrates [67]. The canonical BMP receptor (BMPR) complex is formed by type I and type II serine/threonine kinases. When BMP ligands bind to this heterocomplex, Smad transcription factors are phosphorylated and act to determine cell fate during early neural development [67]. Evidence from knockout animal models has demonstrated that BMP7/GDF7 heterodimers act as diffusible repellent factors, steering commissural axons away from the roof plate by modulating local cytoskeletal reorganization [68, 69]. Moreover, studies using cultured Xenopus laevis neurons have shown that BMP7 induces axonal attraction in early‐stage neurons via LIMK‐mediated Cofilin phosphorylation, whereas in mature neurons, it elicits repulsion through TRPC1–Ca2⁺–calcineurin–Slingshot signaling, resulting in Cofilin dephosphorylation [70] (Figure 3e).

The BMP‐mediated molecular mechanisms that regulate axonal growth cones also contribute to neuronal migration. In vivo knockout experiments have demonstrated that BMP7, secreted by the meninges, influences the migration and polarity of cortical neurons by modulating actin cytoskeletal remodeling through the Smad4–Cofilin 1 pathway [71] (Figure 4a). These findings indicate that differences in BMP receptor expression and downstream signaling cascades modulate the phosphorylation state of Cofilin to determine whether the response is attractive or repulsive, depending on the developmental stage of the neuron.

Wnts

Wnts are a family of morphogens that serve as guidance cues for axons via the activation of multiple downstream signaling pathways through the Frizzled receptor: the canonical Wnt/β‐catenin pathway, non‐canonical Wnt/Ca2+ pathway, and non‐canonical Wnt/planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway [72]. In the developing nervous system, Wnt‐Frizzled interactions [73] and their downstream β‐catenin [74, 75], Ca2+ [76, 77], and PCP signaling cascades [74] can function as either attractive or repulsive cues in axon guidance (Figure 3f).

Beyond axonal guidance, Wnt signaling also regulates neuronal migration. For instance, a transient downregulation of canonical Wnt/β‐catenin signaling during the multipolar stage is required for proper polarization and radial migration of cortical pyramidal neurons in vivo, mediated by the temporally regulated expression of Wnt5A [78]. Consistently, Wnt/C‐Kit signaling controls glia‐guided radial migration of callosal projection neurons in the cortex by regulating adhesion between neurons and radial glia [79](Figure 4a). In the postnatal mouse brain, disruption of key components of the Wnt/PCP pathway, such as Dishevelled‐2 and the four‐pass transmembrane protein, Van Gogh like‐2, alters the final positioning of interneurons within the olfactory bulb, highlighting Wnt's role in neuronal migration [80](Figure 4b). Together, these findings highlight how Wnt signaling pathways regulate neuronal migration through precise control of neuronal polarity transitions and neuron–glia adhesion dynamics.

Glycosaminoglycans (CSPGs/HSPGs/KSPGs)

Proteoglycans containing sulfated glycosaminoglycans, such as chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs), heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs), and keratan sulfate proteoglycans (KSPGs), are major components of the extracellular matrix. Among them, CSPGs [81] and KSPGs are known inhibitory matrix proteins that inhibit axon elongation in injured neural tissues. KSPGs interact with and modulate the activity of guidance cues from the Robo, Slit, Ephrin, Eph receptor, Semaphorin, and Growth factor receptor families [82]. Receptors for CSPGs include the protein tyrosine phosphatase (PTPσ) [83, 84], Nogo receptors (NgRs) [85], and Contactin 1 [86]. Notably, Contactin 1 and NgRs also function as receptors for KSPGs [87]. When CSPGs bind to and activates PTPσ, which is concentrated in the growth cone, this enhances the dephosphorylation of downstream substrates such as cortactin. This disrupts autophagy flux at the autophagosome–lysosome fusion step, leading to growth cone collapse and the formation of dystrophic endballs, which are hallmark structures that inhibit axonal regeneration after spinal cord injury [88] (Figure 3g).

PTPσ is also concentrated in the growth cones of migrating neurons, where it mediates CSPG‐induced growth cone collapse and inhibits migration in the injured cortex, while HSPGs promote the re‐extension of growth cones through filopodial interactions, enabling migration to resume [6] (Figure 4c). Furthermore, cortactin, a substrate of PTPσ [88], functions in neuronal migration, indicating a shared molecular framework for growth cone regulation in both axonal guidance and neuronal migration [6]. KSPGs also form transient chemical templates that define both radial and tangential neuronal migration pathways to the cortex before neuronal migration begins [89]. Although the molecular mechanisms of these actions remain unclear, differences in the receptor composition on growth cones or in their downstream signaling pathways may underlie the distinct responses of axons and migrating neurons to KSPGs.

Nogo/MAG/MOG

Nogo, MAG, and MOG are myelin‐enriched proteins expressed in the spinal cord and at CNS lesion sites, where they function as inhibitors of axonal outgrowth [90, 91, 92]. These proteins bind to their receptors, such as NgR1–3, which form complexes with co‐receptors LINGO1/p75NTR/TROY and PirB, leading to growth cone collapse through the activation of RhoA signaling [93, 94]. Inhibitory Nogo‐NgR signaling is antagonized by LOTUS binding to NgR1, which suppresses Nogo‐driven growth cone collapse [95] (Figure 3h).

Nogo also regulates the migration of projection neurons in the developing cortex. In vitro neurosphere cultures have shown that inhibition of Nogo‐A and its receptors enhances cell motility, and in vivo studies using Nogo‐A knockout mice have further demonstrated defective neuronal positioning in the upper cortical layers [96] (Figure 4a). As observed in axons – where MAG promotes the growth of embryonic neurons but inhibits axonal extension in postnatal neurons [93] – it is possible that the responsiveness of migrating neurons to Nogo may also vary depending on the developmental stage. MAG and MOG are expressed mainly during the postnatal myelination stage; therefore, when most neuronal migration has already ceased, their roles in neuronal migration remain unclear.

Ephrins

Ephrins are divided into two classes: Ephrin A, which is anchored to the membrane via glycosylphosphatidylinositol, and Ephrin B, which has a transmembrane domain and a cytoplasmic domain [97]. Eph receptors are similarly categorized into EphA and EphB [98]. Both EphA and EphB regulate actin and microtubule dynamics in the growth cone through the Rho family of small GTPases [99, 100, 101]. When ephrin binds to an Eph receptor, it triggers forward signaling in Eph‐expressing cells and reverse signaling in ephrin‐expressing cells (Figure 3i). In spinal motor neurons that co‐express Ephrin A and EphA, stimulating clustered Ephrin A induces growth cone repulsion through EphA forward signaling, while stimulating clustered EphA drives growth cone attraction through Ephrin A reverse signaling. These findings highlight context‐dependent opposing actions on growth cone behavior [102].

Ephrins are not only involved in axon guidance, but also in neuronal migration. In cortical development, in vivo studies using Ephrin B1/B2/B3 triple knockout mice and compound mutants with Reelin deficiency have demonstrated that Ephrin B1 and B3 facilitate the radial migration of cortical projection neurons by forming complexes with Reelin receptors, very‐low‐density lipoprotein receptor, and Apolipoprotein E receptor 2. This interaction leads to the activation of effector proteins, such as Disabled 1 (Dab1), and the promotion of neuronal migration [103, 104]. Experiments using EphA4 mutant mice and Ephrin A2 knockdown models have demonstrated delayed interneuron migration during embryonic development. These findings indicate that EphA4‐mediated reverse signaling, via Ephrin A2 ligands expressed on interneurons, promotes their migration. Moreover, EphA4 forward signaling in interneurons functions to repel these cells from regions expressing Ephrin A3, A5, and B3, thereby preventing their invasion into inappropriate brain areas [105] (Figure 4a). Furthermore, in vivo studies using EphA4 knockout and kinase‐inactive mutant mice have shown that the spatial organization of neurons and surrounding astrocytes within the V‐SVZ and rostral migratory stream is disrupted in the absence of EphA4 signaling. As a consequence, the number of neurons successfully reaching and integrating into the periglomerular layer of the olfactory bulb was markedly reduced [106] (Figure 4b). Therefore, differences in the expression patterns of ephrins and Eph receptors contribute to the proper positioning of migrating neurons by coordinating forward and reverse signaling mechanisms.

Shared Growth Cone Morphology and Common Molecules Between Elongating Axons and Migrating Neurons and Their Significance in Neuronal Migration

Santiago Ramón y Cajal first described a lamellar structure at the distal edge of an elongating axon, referring to it as the growth cone [7]. His observations and terminology were primarily focused on axons, so for many years, growth cones were assumed to be exclusive to axonal extensions during neural circuit formation or regeneration. However, recent studies demonstrate that in migrating neurons, such as cortical interneurons [107], cerebellar granule neurons [108], and postnatal olfactory interneurons [6, 109], a growth cone‐like structure appears at the tip of the leading process. Functional analyses examining F‐actin, microtubules, and the molecular‐clutch indicate that this structure is involved in neuronal migration [110, 111, 112, 113]. While some differences in the functions of cytoskeletal molecules have been reported between axonal growth cones and growth cone‐like structures in migrating neurons [114], their structural and molecular parallels remain largely unexplored.

Our recent findings [6] provide direct evidence that neurons undergoing migration possess a bona fide growth cone at the tip of their leading process, functionally equivalent to axonal growth cones. Using time‐lapse super‐resolution imaging with membrane labeling, we observed that growth cones form in the leading process during the elongation phase in migrating neurons derived from the postnatal V‐SVZ. These structures morphologically resemble axonal growth cones of differentiating cortical neurons in the embryonic cerebral cortex (Figure 1). Immunocytochemistry combined with machine learning‐based segmentation revealed that key axonal growth cone molecules, including PTPσ, Liprin‐α, and Destrin, are significantly enriched in the growth cones of both leading processes and axons. Notably, PTPσ colocalizes with F‐actin in filopodia and lamellipodia, and Liprin‐α knockdown reduces PTPσ concentration, indicating shared molecular mechanisms. Live imaging of F‐actin and tyrosinated tubulin (dynamic microtubules) showed similar cytoskeletal dynamics, including retrograde F‐actin flow, in the filopodia and lamellipodia of both types of growth cones [6, 113]. Therefore, the growth cone at the tip of the leading process in migrating neurons exhibits both morphological and dynamic similarities to axonal growth cones, supporting its equivalence in structure and function. Furthermore, three‐dimensional cultures in Matrigel have demonstrated that the growth cone structures of axons and migrating neurons are also morphologically similar in three‐dimensional space.

Recent proteomic studies have dramatically advanced our understanding of axonal growth cones, identifying a wide range of growth cone‐enriched proteins [115, 116, 117, 118]. Given the morphological and dynamic parallels between the leading process and axonal growth cones, it is plausible that many of these proteins are also concentrated in the growth cone of the leading process. Indeed, the CSPG/ HSPG‐responsive receptor, PTPσ, the scaffolding protein, Liprin‐α, and the actin depolymerizing factor, Destrin, all appear in both types of growth cone (Figure 1). In growth cones that encounter CSPG, filopodia contract and the growth cone collapses, but application of the chondroitin sulfate‐degrading enzyme, ChABC, suppresses this collapse. Conversely, when filopodia interact with heparan sulfate in the presence of chondroitin sulfate, they extend once again, allowing neuronal migration to resume. This phenomenon aligns with the idea that chondroitin sulfate binding to PTPσ inhibits axon elongation [119], but that heparan sulfate competes with chondroitin sulfate, enabling PTPσ oligomerization, thereby attenuating chondroitin sulfate‐mediated inhibition [83].

In the injured postnatal brain, growth cones of migrating neurons located far from the lesion repeatedly extend and retract, whereas growth cone extension and migration of neurons are suppressed at and around the lesion site, which is enriched in chondroitin sulfate. Recent in vivo studies show that administrating a PTPσ inhibitor [120] or implanting an HSPG‐enriched biomaterial [6] mitigates the repulsive effects of chondroitin sulfate and promotes neuronal migration after injury. In particular, when radial glial fiber‐mimetic artificial scaffolds containing heparan sulfate are implanted into the chondroitin sulfate‐rich injured cerebral cortex, growth cone extension is promoted, and the migration and regeneration of neurons are improved, leading to functional recovery of gait behaviors. These artificial scaffolds, referred to as gelatin‐fiber nonwoven fabric with enriched HSPG, therefore, effectively enhance the probing and driving functions of the growth cone in neuronal migration. Collectively, these findings indicate that the growth cone of migrating neurons responds to extracellular cues and facilitates neuronal migration via mechanisms analogous to those driving axonal growth cones.

Conclusions and Prospects: Possible Growth Cone Involvement in Guidance Cue‐Directed Neuronal Migration

The growth cone acts as a critical interpreter of environmental cues, using its specialized morphology and dynamic properties to direct axon extension. This process is regulated by multiple molecules, including semaphorins, ephrins, netrins, slits, Shh, Wnts, BMPs, glycosaminoglycans, and Nogo/MAG/MOG, which together orchestrate axonal pathfinding. The growth cone can exhibit distinct responses – either attraction or repulsion – even to the same guidance cue. These differential responses are influenced by several factors, including variations in guidance cue subtypes, the repertoire of receptors expressed on the growth cone, and differences in downstream signaling pathways. For instance, when the growth cone of an axon reaches a choice point or intermediate target, it must precisely regulate surface receptor expression to shift its responsiveness from attraction to repulsion and to prepare for the next stage of its journey [2]. Notably, many of these same guidance cues also influence neuronal migration. Some cues elicit similar responses in both axons and migrating neurons, whereas others induce distinct effects depending on the cellular context. These differences in responsiveness may result from variations in receptor expression and intracellular signaling cascades between axons and migrating neurons – differences that are, in turn, shaped by the degree of cellular differentiation and the surrounding extracellular environment, including during development and after brain injury. We therefore propose that migrating neurons actively engage with extracellular cues as they travel to their destinations.

Recent evidence has shown that migrating neurons possess a growth cone at the tip of their leading process, where it functions as both a probe and a driver of migration [6]. In addition to PTPσ [6] and Robo [45], which have been identified in the growth cones of migrating neurons, receptors for various other axonal guidance cues may also localize to these structures. Moreover, neuronal migration is not static; it is modulated by dynamic changes in the extracellular environment during development and after brain injury. Receptors at the growth cones of migrating neurons may undergo surface expression changes in response to these environmental cues with regulation at multiple levels, including transcription, translation, and post‐translational modification. Such regulation may result in distinct migratory behaviors.

Collectively, these findings underscore that the roles of growth cones in neuronal migration are as critical to neural circuit formation and regeneration as they are to axonal guidance. Future studies that elucidate the molecular mechanisms promoting growth cone extension in migrating neurons will significantly advance our understanding of the mechanisms governing both neuronal migration and neural repair. Moreover, applying these insights to develop biomaterials incorporating chemoattractants for migrating neurons [60] or to manipulate the expression of guidance cues in migrating neurons themselves, holds great promise for innovative therapeutic strategies in neural regeneration.
