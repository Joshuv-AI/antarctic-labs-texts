Post-resuscitation left ventricular dysfunction in a rat model of ventricular fibrillation and extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation
Authors: Wolfgang Weihs, Matthias Mueller, Ingrid Anna Maria Magnet, Alexander Franz Szinovatz, Ouafa Hamza, Laurenz Wolner, Petra Kodajova, Benjamin Ullram, Roman Brock, Michael Holzer, Bruno K. Podesser, Attila Kiss, Alexandra-Maria Stommel, Sandra Högler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While the majority of rodent resuscitation studies prioritize neurologic outcomes, research into the long-term effects of global ischemia and reperfusion on cardiac function is scarce, generally limited to morphologic assessments. Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) is a promising strategy for highly selected patients with refractory cardiac arrest (CA). We aimed to investigate the impact of ventricular fibrillation CA (VFCA) and subsequent ECPR on cardiac recovery in rats. Adult male Sprague-Dawley rats were subjected to either 6–8 min VFCA, followed by ECPR and compared to sham animals. The primary outcome parameter was cardiac function assessment at 14 days after CA. The hearts of rats surviving for 14 days were isolated and mounted onto an erythrocyte-perfused, isolated working heart (WH) system. Cardiac output (CO), left ventricular systolic pressure (LVSP), and coronary flow were measured. To assess the heart adaptation to hemodynamic stress, the afterload was gradually increased in 10 mmHg increments while CO and LVSP were monitored. Additionally, the hearts from all animals surviving at least 36 h were assessed histologically. Of 15 rats that achieved ROSC after 6 min of CA, 7 could be evaluated in the WH setup 14 days after CA. In the 8 min CA group, 15 animals achieved ROSC, of which 2 were investigated in WH at 14 days after CA. Compared to the hearts of 7 sham animals, no significant differences in cardiac hemodynamics were observed at a set afterload (60 mm Hg; baseline) in the 6 min CA group. However, the two investigated 8 min CA animals exhibited a trend towards reduced CO and LVSP levels. Notably, both CA groups showed impaired hemodynamic performance to hemodynamic stress. Survivors at 14 days consistently showed significant myocardial pathology, with both 6 min and 8 min CA groups exhibiting fibrosis, inflammation, and edema most pronounced in the interventricular septum and right ventricle of the 8 min CA group. Animals that died prematurely displayed time-dependent acute changes, progressing from hypereosinophilic degeneration (36 h survivors) to myocardial necrosis, calcification, and the formation of cell-rich granulation tissue (48–108 h survivors). VFCA led to impaired left ventricular hemodynamic function in 8 min CA rats resuscitated with ECPR at rest and with increasing afterload. The isolated WH system may offer a valuable tool for assessing long-term cardiac function and performance after resuscitation.

Introduction

Sudden out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (CA) is still one of the leading causes of death and thus an unsolved public health problem, with increasing incidence and decreasing survival rates in industrialized countries1–3. Despite significant advancements in emergency medicine and intensive care, survival outcomes continue to be disappointing4–6 with only approximately 10% of patients emerging without lasting impairments3. In response, a range of animal models have been employed to develop innovative therapeutic approaches aimed at reducing the detrimental effects of extended, untreated CA7,8.

Experimental animal models primarily evaluate the consequences of CA, global ischemia, and reperfusion injury (RI) on the central nervous system (CNS); howevertheir impact on cardiac function alterations remains scarce in rat models. This is despite evidence that CA survivors are at an increased risk of developing acute coronary syndrome and heart failure9. Research typically focuses on the immediate effects of CA, resuscitation and therapeutic interventions on cardiac function and hemodynamics. These aspects are usually investigated during the initial hours of the experiment, while the animals are still catheterized and monitored10–13.

For patients experiencing CA who are unresponsive to conventional resuscitation techniques, the use of extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) has emerged as a promising therapeutic option in selected patients14. Although the majority of experimental resuscitation research has concentrated on standard cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) methods, the application of rodent models for resuscitation, particularly those involving ventricular fibrillation CA (VFCA) and ECPR, remains exceedingly uncommon. To align with these advancements in resuscitation techniques, particularly the use of ECPR in highly selected adult patients, we have developed a VFCA ECPR rat model15,16.

The primary aim of this sub-study was to elucidate the effects of CA and global ischemia on heart function in a VFCA ECPR rat model, utilizing the isolated working heart (WH) setup. Additionally, this study sought to link functional impairments with more comprehensive pathohistological observations associated with the duration of CA.

This sub-study follows our recent publication, in which we evaluated the neurological outcomes of 6 min and 8 min VFCA durations, followed by ECPR, assessing long-term survival over a two-week period to establish a reproducible model that yields both a reasonable survival rate and consistent brain damage17. The findings revealed that extending the duration of CA to 8 min resulted in a lower survival rate compared to the 6 min group. Moreover, histological examination of the hippocampal CA1 region indicated that the additional 2 min of CA did not exacerbate neuronal damage in animals that survived for 14 days. However, collagen fibers in the myocardium were significantly increased in the 8 min CA group compared to shams, while the 6 min CA group showed no significant difference to sham animals. Thus, the lower survival rate in the 8 min CA group might have been caused by the failure of organ systems other than the CNS, such as the heart.

To date, research on cardiac function following extended survival periods in VFCA rat models has not been reported. Consequently, our goal was to assess cardiac function after long-term survival in our VFCA ECPR model, while avoiding the need for re-catheterization of the animals, which could be complicated by the healing of initial surgical sites and potentially result in the loss of valuable experimental subjects. It is important to highlight that the ex vivo WH setup offers significant benefits by allowing the assessment of ventricular performance under precise loading and pressure conditions, devoid of neurohormonal influences. This approach yields critical insights into myocardial dysfunction development18,19.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental protocol

The experimental protocol and animal welfare procedures were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee for Laboratory Animal Experiments, Medical University of Vienna and the Federal Ministry of Education, Science and Research, Republic of Austria (GZ: BMBWF-66.009/0315-V/3b/2018). The study was conducted in strict accordance with the Austrian Federal Law on the Experimentation of Live Animals (§ 16 Tierversuchsgesetz, TVG 2012) and the European Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. Furthermore, the study was designed and reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines20. The experimental setup and protocol were published in detail previously17.

Anesthesia and preparation phase

Male Sprague–Dawley rats, aged 10–12 weeks and weighing 450–550 g, obtained from the Division of Laboratory Animal Science and Genetics, Himberg, Austria, were anesthetized with 6% inhaled sevoflurane and given piritramide for pain relief. Following intubation, anesthesia was maintained with 3.5% sevoflurane, and the rats were mechanically ventilated. Throughout the experiment, vital parameters were closely monitored and a body temperature of 37 ± 0.2 °C was maintained.

Using sterile techniques, the arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) cannula, along with venous and arterial catheters for drug administration, hemodynamic monitoring, and arterial blood gas analysis, were placed in the femoral vessels. The venous ECMO drainage cannula was inserted through the right jugular vein with its tip positioned in the inferior vena cava. All catheters and cannulas were treated with heparin to prevent clotting. A heparin bolus was administered intravenously (i.v.) following the insertion of the venous ECMO cannula. The ECMO system, which was custom-made by Dipl.-Ing. Martin Humbs, Valley, Germany, was primed with a crystalloid solution (15 ml) and connected to the arterial ECMO cannula (Fig. 1). Inside the venous ECMO drainage cannula, a pacing catheter was placed to induce VFCA. Defibrillation electrodes, tailored to fit, were sutured to the left and right side of the thorax.

Inducing cardiac arrest and resuscitation

Sevoflurane administration was ceased 90 s prior to terminating ventilation, at which point VF was induced through an electrical alternating current of 12 V/50 Hz, not exceeding 8 mA. Following the removal of the pacing catheter, the ECMO circuit was connected to the venous drainage cannula, and the initial medications, comprising heparin (200 I.U./kg body weight), epinephrine (20 µg/kg body weight, and sodium bicarbonate (1 mmol/kg body weight), were drained into the ECMO´s open reservoir.

CA was then allowed to persist untreated for either 6–8 min, after which ventilation was reinstated and ECPR commenced. Two biphasic defibrillation shocks of 5 Joules each were delivered at 2-minute intervals during resuscitation whenever a shockable rhythm was detected. Concurrently, mechanical ventilation was maintained, and epinephrine (10 µg/kg body weight) was administered i.v. at 2-minute intervals throughout ECPR. Resuscitation efforts continued until return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved or ceased after a maximum of 10 min of ECPR. The experimental protocol is depicted in Fig. 2.

ROSC, weaning, explantation phase and intensive care phase

Upon achieving ROSC, the ECMO system was deactivated, and the blood within the ECMO circuit was reinfused into the rat through the arterial catheter. Mechanical ventilation settings were promptly adjusted to accommodate the rat’s restored cardiac output (CO) and circulation. The venous ECMO cannula was immediately removed following ROSC, and the incision site was closed using sterile techniques.

The next phase involved rapidly weaning the rat off mechanical ventilation to enable extubation. The sham group, which underwent identical surgical procedures without being subjected to CA and ECPR, received comparable durations of treatment and medications, except for those specifically related to resuscitation efforts.

Sampling and assessment of left ventricular cardiac function using isolated working heart system

At the end of the study, after planned survival for 14 ± 1 days following the CA experiment, all surviving animals were deeply sedated and analgetized (sevoflurane, piritramide), endotracheally reintubated and mechanically ventilated. A thoracotomy was performed, the rats were given heparin (200 IU i.v.) and hearts were rapidly excised from the thorax, immersed into ice-cold Krebs-Henseleit buffer and mounted on an erythrocyte-perfused, WH system (Hugo Sachs Elektronik, Freiburg, Germany) as described elsewhere18. The perfusate consisted of a Krebs-Henseleit buffer based suspension of purified bovine erythrocytes (hematocrit 30%). Oxygenation was performed with low gas (75% N2, 20% O2, 5% CO2) to provide a constant pO2 of 100 ± 10 mm Hg. Composition of the buffer was as follows (in mmol/L): NaCl, 118; KCl, 4.7; CaCl2, 2.5; MgSO4, 1.2; KH2PO4, 1.2; NaEDTA, 0.5; NaHCO3, 25; glucose: 11.1; insulin, 2.5 IU/L; bovine albumin, 2 g/L. During the stabilization period, the hearts were in Langendorff mode (LD) with a constant coronary perfusion pressure at 60 mm Hg. After 15 min of stabilization in LD, the left atrium was cannulated via a pulmonary vein and anterograde perfusion of the left atrium and left ventricle (LV) was performed. Afterwards, the heart had to eject against a fixed afterload of 60 mmHg for 20 min (Working heart mode). Briefly, hemodynamic parameters were recorded at predefined time points: WH20 (baseline, Table 1). All hemodynamic data were registered as mean values derived from the respective pressure and flow tracings. The following parameters were recorded during the WH mode: coronary flow (CF, ml/min) was measured as the difference between left atrial flow and aortic flow as well as had been checked by collecting drops of blood solution from the hearts at different time points during the experiment. As the mean aortic pressure is set at 60 mmHg and maintained throughout the experiment, alterations in coronary flow primarily reflect alterations in coronary resistance, allowing evaluation of microvasculature function. In addition, the following parameters were used for the assessment of cardiac function. CO is identical to left atrial flow and was measured continuously with a flow probe (mL/min, Flowmeter Narcomatic RT-500, Narco Biosystems). LV systolic pressure (LVSP) was measured by a high-fidelity MicroTip catheter (Millar SP-407; Millar Instruments, Inc., Houston, TX, USA) inserted retrograde via the aortic valve into the LV. External heart work (EHW) was calculated according to the following formula: CO × LVSP (g × m/min)*0.0136 normalized to heart weight. In addition, pressure-volume (P-V) relationships were performed by increasing the afterload in 10 mmHg increments while CO and LVSP were monitored in order to determine cardiac pump function. The WH system experimental lab team was blinded to the group assignment.

Histological analysis

For the histological assessment of myocardial lesions, hearts of all animals that survived to 14 days were fixed in 4% buffered formalin. A coronary section at the major circumference of the heart was embedded in paraffin wax and cut at a thickness of 2 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) for descriptive evaluation and a semiquantitative assessment using a modified histologic damage score (HDS)21. The wall of LV, right ventricle (RV), and interventricular septum (IVS) were examined separately by a pathologist (SH) blinded to treatment regimens. For the HDS, defined lesions (presence of inflammatory cells, hemosiderophages, fibroblasts, interstitial edema, fibrosis, myocardial vacuolation, and variable myofiber size) were graded on a five-point scale (0 = not present, 1 = mild, focal, 2 = mild, multifocal, 3 = moderate, multifocal, 4 = marked, coalescing) and scores summarized for each region of interest. Sections were also stained with Azan staining after Heidenhain to detect collagen fibers. For the assessment of myocardial fibrosis formation, three images of LV, RV, and IVS each were taken at a magnification of 400x with an Olympus DP26 camera (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) attached to an Olympus BX53 light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Images for fibrosis assessment were taken in a lesion-targeted manner, focusing on regions where lesions were most extensive. Images were analyzed with Fiji to quantify the extent of collagen fibers stained blue as described previously22.

Additionally, the hearts of animals that survived for at least 36 h but not until WH evaluation were assessed histologically with HE-staining for general assessment and descriptive evaluation. If expedient, Azan staining for detection of collagen fibers and von Kossa staining for the detection of calcifications were performed. Animals were grouped according to their survival time in three groups: 36 h, 48–60 h, 72–108 h. The histopathologist (SH) was blinded to the group assignments.

Statistics and data analysis

Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Not normally distributed data are presented as median and interquartile range. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post hoc tests were used for comparisons of hemodynamic parameters ex vivo. In addition, to compare EHW and CO, the area under the curve (AUC) was estimated for each dependent variable for each experimental condition separately as described previously18. Resulting estimates with standard errors were compared between experimental conditions using one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc tests corrected by Sidak’s method. Histological data were analyzed with the Kruskal-Wallis-Test with Dunn´s test as correction for multiple comparisons, because HDS scores are no metric data and data of Azan-staining were not normally distributed as tested by the Shapiro-Wilk Test. Analyses were performed using Prism™ 9 software (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Animals and survival outcomes

Sixteen rats could be examined in the WH model after 14 days of survival (7 in the 6 min CA group, 2 in the 8 min CA group, 7 in the sham group). Detailed findings concerning the model and the histopathological examination of the hippocampus have been documented in a separate publication17. Due to scheduling constraints, the independent operation of the research teams, and one technical failure, not all animals surviving to 14 days could be evaluated with the WH model. The selection of animals for WH examination was based purely on logistical considerations; colleagues conducting the working heart examination (AK, OH, LW) were completely blinded to the group assignment and neurological status of the animals. Histological investigations were conducted on heart tissue from all animals surviving for at least 36 h and compared to heart tissue of 10 sham animals. Isolated Working heart

The primary outcome parameter was the hemodynamic performance. The data of the 8 min CA group was not used for statistical analysis, due to the limited sample size (n = 2). However, the values of these animals are included as pilot data and are presented to illustrate the potential impact of prolonged cardiac arrest duration.

At baseline there was no difference in heart rate between the 6 min CA and sham groups, but we observed a reduction in LVSP, CO and EHW levels in the 8 min CA group (Table 1). We performed a pump function protocol involving an increase of the afterload by 10 mmHg per minute until aortic flow cessation. The dynamic responses to increased afterload were quantified using the area under the curve (AUC). CO AUC showed a modest reduction in the 6 min CA group compared with sham (sham: 4490 ± 257; 6 min CA: 4018 ± 265), while LVSP and EHW AUC values remained comparable between these two groups, indicating preservation of overall cardiac function at this time point. In contrast, the values of both animals after 8 min CA exhibited a pronounced decrease in CO, LVSP, and EHW AUC, reflecting marked impairment of cardiac performance. Histological analyses of the myocardium

Animals surviving for 14 days (6 min CA n = 10, 8 min CA n = 5, sham n = 10): In HE-stained sections lesions were evident in all animals of both CA groups but not in the sham animals. The predominant alterations were foci of infiltrating lymphocytes, often accompanied by interstitial edema or foci of fibroblast proliferation with fibrosis, which encompassed myofibers of different calibers. Vacuolated myofibers and hemosiderin-containing macrophages were also detectable adjacent to these lesions. Representative images of the IVS in HE-staining are shown in Fig. 5A-C.

When assessed with the HDS in HE-staining CA animals had significantly higher scores compared to sham in all regions evaluated (LV: 6 min CA vs. sham p < 0.05, 8 min CA vs. sham p < 0.01, IVS: 6 min CA vs. sham p < 0.01, 8 min CA vs. sham p < 0.001, RV: 6 min CA vs. sham p < 0.05, 8 min CA vs. sham p < 0.01) (Table 2). Lesions were most prominent in the IVS and RV of animals subjected to 8 min of CA (Fig. 6A-C).

Results of 8 min CA animals were corroborated in Azan-stained sections used to quantify the extent of collagen fibers in images of LV, IVS, and RV. Myocardium of 8 min CA animals showed significantly more collagen fibers in all regions examined compared to sham animals (LV and RV: p < 0.01, IVS: p < 0.001) with largest areas in IVS and RV. Animals subjected to 6 min of CA showed significantly more collagen fibers only in IVS compared to sham (p < 0.05), but no differences were detectable in the left and right ventricular myocardium. Animals not surviving to 14 days: Lesions in these animals differed according to their survival time (Table 3). All animals surviving for 36 h (n = 4) were subjected to 6 min CA and showed myocardial degeneration with hypereosinophilic myofibers and loss of striation (Fig. 7A). Three animals presented with interstitial edema and two of them showed myocardial necrosis and few infiltrating neutrophils. Animals surviving for 48 to 60 h (n = 6) were from the 8 min CA group. Myocardial degeneration was present in all animals and myocardial necrosis in four of six. Five rats showed multifocal activated fibroblasts and formation of cell-rich granulation tissue. Multifocal mild to extensive calcification of myofibers was present in two animals and confirmed by von Kossastaining (Fig. 7B). Animals surviving for 72 to 108 h (n = 4) were also from the 8 min CA group. All showed myocardial degeneration and formation of granulation tissue (Fig. 7C). Three of four presented with myocardial necrosis and within the granulation tissue few small collagen fibers were detectable with Azan-staining.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the effects of global ischemia and subsequent resuscitation with ECPR, following 6 and 8 min of VFCA, observing both functional and morphological alterations of the heart at 14 days of survival. Our findings indicate that – although only two animals were assessed – 8 min CA followed by ECPR impaired cardiac performance in our rat model, while a 6 min CA did not yet show the same effect. Increased myocardial fibrosis in animals subjected to 8 min of VFCA supports these findings and may be causative for the impaired cardiac function.

In the group subjected to 6 min of CA, 34% of the resuscitated animals did not survive until the 14-day follow-up, a finding that aligns with previous studies23,24. It is crucial to highlight that 8 min CA reduced long-term survival with a 75% mortality rate before the study endpoint. These results imply that the additional 2 min of CA diminished the probability of survival, suggesting a compound effect on severity. While the exact mechanisms underlying this increased mortality remain to be fully elucidated, our results underscore the significant role of myocardial injury in determining long-term survival and the necessity of monitoring cardiac function post-CA and resuscitation.

Assessing whether early animal mortality before endpoint was due to more severe cardiac damage is challenging based on histological findings alone. Due to the highly dynamic and time-dependent evolution of pathohistological events including the cascade of cellular damage, inflammation, debris clearance, and scar tissue formation, it is inappropriate to compare histological findings in animals that died at varying time points. Consequently, a common severity scale cannot be applied across different time points. A meaningful comparison of damage can only be made between animals with the same time interval since the ischemic event, though individual differences may still influence the results. The results of the histological analyses of the animals that died before the end point, as presented in this study, clearly illustrate this pathohistological evolution over time. Although brain and heart histology provided insight, a critical question remains unanswered. Animals subjected to a 6 min CA primarily died early (before 48 h), whereas most animals from the 8 min CA group died after 48 h (Table 1). This observation remains to be elucidated. Animals that survived less than 36 h were not histologically analyzed, as the visible establishment of damage requires a certain period to become evident. However, these unanalyzed animals were mainly from the 6 min CA group, where early mortality was most frequent and prominent.

This leads to a paradox: why would more severe damage caused by prolonged standstill lead to a later onset of the phase in which animals die? A potential, albeit highly speculative, explanation is that the severely compromised circulation following a prolonged arrest may cause pathological processes to unfold at a slower rate due to reduced delivery of inflammatory mediators and cellular components necessary for the progression of delayed cell death.

We did not assess acute cardiac injury biomarkers such as troponin in the immediate post-resuscitation phase (e.g., within 24–48 h), nor did we evaluate markers of cardiac stress and remodeling such as ANP or BNP at later time points (e.g., 1–2 weeks). These results could have provided important mechanistic insights into myocardial injury and post-arrest remodeling. However, clinical studies have demonstrated that hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP did not provide independent prognostic information following out of hospital cardiac arrest25. In contrast, Nagao et al. demonstrated that BNP levels provide valuable predictive information for survival to hospital discharge in patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest26. Notably, we identified structural cardiac alterations, including fibrosis, which could plausibly increase susceptibility to arrhythmias and adverse outcomes, and decrease survival rate. However, these associations do not establish causality and warrant further experimental studies.

In the current scientific discourse regarding resuscitation after CA or global ischemia, the emphasis lies predominantly on the ischemic and reperfusion-induced injuries to the brain, as evidenced by the extensive body of literature in this area. Cell damage in the vulnerable brain regions and influence on behavior or learning are reported15,27–29. Our histological results regarding neuronal damage in animals surviving to 14 days show no significant difference in hippocampal CA1 injury, regardless of CA duration17 In both groups we found distinct neuronal damage as well as microglial and astrocyte activation without differences between the two groups. Therefore, the results in neuronal damage in the surviving animals does not explain the differences in survival between our 6 and 8 min CA groups. Of course, it can be assumed that the damage would have been greater in the animals that died. However, this cannot be determined histologically, as the animals died at different time points, which again means that the severity of the damage cannot be directly compared.

Influence of CA, resuscitation and subsequent therapeutic interventions on cardiac function and hemodynamics in rodent models is primarily observed during the initial hours post-event when animals remain under invasive monitoring conditions10–13. He et al. focused on cardiac functional alterations in the first 6 h after 6 min VFCA and CPR, and on survival and neurologic deficits after 1–3 days in a rat model13. Kamohara et al. explored LV function for 4 h post-resuscitation in a rat model finding significantly impaired myocardial function after 4-minute VFCA compared to asphyxial CA and also reported neurologic deficits 1–3 days after CA10. In a rat study on 4 min VFCA and CPR, Tang et al. compared myocardial function on perfused isolated hearts, harvested from animals 2 and 20 min after CA and successful resuscitation to hearts of a control group harvested before CA. They found a decreased myocardial contractility and left ventricular pressure 2 and 20 min post ROSC30. These results are in line with our findings. However, the pathomechanistic link between short- and long-term cardiac dysfunction has yet to be elucidated.

Tsai et al. reported in their 5 min VFCA rat model CO after 4 h of survival and histological myocardial injury after 4 and 72 h survival12. A critical challenge associated with this methodological approach is the potential discrepancy between histological findings and cardiac functional assessments due to the progressive nature of myocardial damage over time. Despite these methodological concerns, the long-term impact of CA on cardiac function and structure remains a crucial area of investigation, underpinned by increasing evidence of post-CA myocardial dysfunction as a common complication among CA survivors31. The etiology of myocardial damage post-CA is multifaceted, encompassing the original cause of the CA, global ischemia, the specifics of the resuscitation process, the duration of ischemia/reperfusion injury, and the post-resuscitation care. The pathophysiology underlying post-cardiac arrest syndrome is complex, characterized by a cascade of adverse events including energy depletion, compromised myocardial contractility, and inflammation within the myocardium32,33.

ECPR rat models, particularly those involving VFCA, are exceedingly uncommon in the field. Janata et al28. implemented 6 min VFCA in their ECPR rat model and reported ROSC and survival rates in their normothermic groups that were comparable to our results. However, cardiac function after CA was not assessed in this study. In patients resuscitated from refractory CA with ECPR minimization of LV wall tension or LV distension and increased LV afterload can impair cardiac recovery34. However, in a rat model of acute myocardial infarction animals received venoarterial (VA) ECMO support 30 min after ligation of the coronary vessel for 2.5 h. They used VA-ECMO in this vulnerable phase of cardiogenic shock and showed favorable outcome regarding cardiac function and myocardial fibrosis 15 days after myocardial infarction compared to controls without VA-ECMO support35. This VA-ECMO support rat model is not fully comparable with a CA ECPR model. Therefore, it is crucial to further investigate whether ECPR used as resuscitation method in our study aggravated cardiac dysfunction and contributed to myocardial damage after 8 min of VFCA or whether it might have had a beneficial effect on animals subjected to 6 min CA.

Our study presents novel insights into the impact of CA duration on survival rates and the progressive cardiac dysfunction in association with the morphological changes of the myocardium in ECPR rat models with long term survival of 14 days. Notably, we observed a marked decrease in CO and LVSP in the 8-minute CA group compared to the sham group under resting conditions. This decline was further accentuated under conditions of increased afterload, suggesting these hearts are unable to tolerate hemodynamic stress at certain levels. This observation is similar to post myocardial infarction associated left ventricular dysfunction as previously described18. Therefore, it is tempting to speculate that the significant reduction in survival rate in the 8 min CA group may primarily be due to the increased myocardial damage. In addition, recent clinical data also demonstrate that post cardiac arrest myocardial dysfunction is a relevant complication faced during post-resuscitation care that adversely impacts survival and neurological outcome31,32.

While acute phase cardiac troponin levels were not assessed in our study, which focused on the long-term cardiac outcomes 14 days post-CA, we measured collagen deposition as a consequence of cardiomyocyte loss and subsequent scar formation. Interestingly, fibrosis was predominantly present in the IVS and RV, although the LV of 8 min CA animals also exhibited significantly increased fibrotic areas compared to sham. While the present study was primarily designed to characterize the spatial distribution of fibrotic remodeling, several pathophysiological considerations may underlie this pattern. Regional susceptibility of the RV and IVS to fibrosis may reflect differences in wall stress, perfusion, and myocyte vulnerability. The RV, despite lower pressures, is particularly sensitive to pressure or volume overload and ischemic stress, while the IVS serves as a mechanical and electrical interface between the ventricles, potentially rendering it more susceptible to local strain, altered neurohumoral signaling, or microvascular dysfunction. Nevertheless, further studies are required to determine whether this pattern represents a unique signature of fibrosis following cardiac arrest.

The myocardial lesion distribution after CA is different compared to models of myocardial infarction, in which fibrosis is confined to the LV free wall supplied by the according artery35. Patients undergoing ECPR after refractory CA showed delayed hyperenhancement in non-contrast computed tomography following coronary angiography even in areas perfused by non-obstructive coronary arteries. Furthermore, delayed hyperenhancement was found in patients without coronary artery disease and it was associated with in-hospital death36. This exacerbation of myocardial damage is a critical determinant of scar development, and the ensuing left ventricular contractile dysfunction following CA.

These collective observations underscore the need for targeted strategies to mitigate the adverse cardiac consequences of prolonged CA emphasizing a critical window for intervention to preserve cardiac function and potentially improve survival outcomes.

Limitations

Our study faced several limitations that merit consideration.

Firstly, due to the independent schedules of the collaborating research groups, not all animals were amenable to evaluation using the isolated WH apparatus after two-week survival period. This limitation, though regrettable, was an unavoidable aspect of coordinating across multiple independent research schedules. The selection of animals for examination was based purely on logistical considerations; investigators conducting the working heart examination (AK, OH, LW) were completely blinded to the group assignment and neurological status of the animals. Additionally, within the 8 min CA cohort, one animal was excluded from analysis due to technical complications encountered during the working heart experiment.

A limitation of the WH apparatus is the lack of systemic neurohormonal regulation, which may influence microvascular function and resistance in the heart. Nevertheless, our data on coronary flow reflected the actual changes in microvascular function observed under standardized isolated heart experimental conditions. However, the absence of in vivo functional assessments, such as echocardiography or invasive haemodynamic measurements, limits direct translational inference.

The histological assessments, however, were successfully completed for all animals that survived until the designated endpoint. The diminished survival rate observed in the 8 min CA group resulted in a reduced sample size for end-point investigations, particularly in comparisons with the 6 min CA group. Consequently, our study was limited to evaluating only two animals from the 8 min CA group on the isolated WH apparatus, thus imparting a pilot study nature to our research. Despite this limitation, the findings from these two hearts indicated significant functional impairments, aligning well with the histological observations across all examined 8 min CA animals.

An additional limitation of the study is the use of sevoflurane, epinephrine, and sodium bicarbonate in the experimental model. Sevoflurane has been shown to reduce infarct size, epinephrine may aggravate post-resuscitation myocardial dysfunction, and sodium bicarbonate may worsen intracellular acidosis37, epinephrine may aggravate post-resuscitation myocardial dysfunction38, and sodium bicarbonate may worsen intracellular acidosis. These pharmacologic effects could potentially have influenced the observed outcomes. However, to minimize this potential confounding, all experimental groups (except the sham group) were treated identically. In addition, we attempted to minimize the influence of sevoflurane in our protocol by pausing the administration of sevoflurane before CA induction, at the latest possible point, at which point the animal has not yet shown any signs of awakening.

Another limitation of this study is the fact that the long periods of cardiac arrest, which were aimed at maximizing damage after two weeks of survival, resulted in a bias due to the loss of severely damaged animals. We accepted this because the aim was to map the damage depending on the cardiac arrest durations in terms of cardiac function, survival, and neurology17.

Another potential confounder that cannot be entirely dismissed is the possibility of myocardial damage attributable to the procedure employed for inducing VFCA using a fibrillation catheter. However, in the 6 min CA group, only minor damage comparable to the to the sham group was observed, and no damage was found in the sham animals exposed to the fibrillation catheter without current. This indicates that the CA procedure itself is probably not the main cause of the observed myocardial damage and has only minor effects on the heart tissue. The procedure of harvesting the hearts and attaching them to the isolated heart apparatus could also be suspected to lead to potential damage to the hearts. However, the low variation in the results in the sham and 6 min CA and the significant difference to the 8 min CA animals rule out a possible bias as unlikely. Nevertheless, further studies are warranted to clarify this issue.

Another limitation is the use of exclusively male animals. This is due to the tradition of the model and the corresponding literature. We will update this practice in the future to reflect current standards and acknowledge this as a limitation. Our study design does not account for sex-specific differences in myocardial dysfunction following cardiac arrest, which naturally affects the translational interpretation of our results.

A further limitation for the clinical relevance of our results is, of course, that we worked with healthy animals and thus healthy hearts without previous damage, whereas human patients affected by CA to a large extent have a previously damaged heart. However, this can also be seen as an advantage, as we provide comparable conditions in our study groups.

The application of ECPR in clinical settings is generally reserved for a highly selected group of witnessed CA patients who do not respond to standard CPR measures. In real-world clinical settings, ECPR is only applied if standard CPR is ongoing. This contrasts with our animal model, where ECMO is pre-emptively set up and activated immediately following either a 6- or 8-minute CA, bypassing the low-flow phase experienced by humans undergoing conventional CPR. Consequently, our rat ECPR model presents inherent limitations when directly compared to human clinical scenarios, where ECPR is performed only after standard CPR is already ongoing.

In our experimental setup, rats were swiftly resuscitated through ECPR within minutes of initiation and were rapidly weaned off ECMO, followed by prompt removal of all ECMO cannulas. This procedure starkly contrasts with the clinical practice where ECMO provides circulatory support for several days, with weaning and decannulation processes typically occurring 3–4 days post-resuscitation39.

Conclusion

VFCA was found to impair left ventricular hemodynamic function in rats subjected to 8 min CA, followed by resuscitation via ECPR. Due to the low survival rate and technical and logistical challenges, a clear difference from the other study groups could be observed in only two pilot animals; nevertheless, this finding is still informative. Post-resuscitation, these hearts exhibited heightened vulnerability to hemodynamic stress, aligning with histological findings. Our observations lend support to existing clinical evidence indicating persistent post-CA myocardial dysfunction over the long term. Thus, therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing cardiomyocyte necrosis during the (sub)acute phase post-resuscitation could be critical for enhancing long-term survival prospects. However, validating this hypothesis necessitates further research. Moreover, the isolated WH experimental condition employed in our study represents a robust platform for assessing cardiac function under resting conditions as well as during hemodynamic and pharmacological challenges, thereby underscoring its relevance and utility in resuscitation research.
