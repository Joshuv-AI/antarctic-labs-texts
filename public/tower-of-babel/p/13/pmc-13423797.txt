Protein stability is determined by single-site bias rather than pairwise covariance
Authors: Matt Sternke, Katherine W Tripp, Soumya Prakash Behera, Justin P Nguyen, Doug Barrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe biases revealed in protein sequence alignments have been shown to provide information related to protein structure, stability and function. Recent studies using Potts models show that single-site biases and pair correlations can improve predictions of protein fitness, activity and stability compared to simpler models. Here we use a Potts model to design groups of protein sequences with different amounts of single-site biases and pair correlations and determine the stabilities of sequences from each group. Surprisingly, sequences excluding pair correlations maximize stability compared to sequences that maximize pair correlations, suggesting that pair correlations contribute to other aspects of protein fitness. Consistent with this interpretation, we find that for three enzyme families, activity is greatly increased by maximizing pair correlations. The finding that elimination of covariant residue pairs increases protein stability suggests a route to enhance stability of designed proteins, although this stability may be offset by reduced enzyme activity. Subject terms: Biophysics, Protein folding, Biophysical chemistry, Protein design

Main

Most proteins fold into stable, well-defined structures and perform specific functions. Amino acid substitutions resulting from random mutation are subject to physiochemical constraints that retain native structure and biological function. Because protein structure and function are determined by the many cooperative interactions among their residues1, these interactions constrain protein sequence evolution and lead to statistical correlations among residue pairs.

Residue correlations have been shown to be determinants of structure and function. An approach called statistical coupling analysis showed residue covariances present in multiple-sequence alignments (MSAs) to be important for folding2 and activity3,4. A complementary approach called direct coupling analysis (DCA) has shown promise in predicting protein structure and function. DCA uses a Potts formalism from statistical mechanics to infer single-site and pairwise coupling biases from MSAs5–7. Including both single-site biases and pair correlations has been shown to improve predictions of protein structure6 and effects of mutations on stability and function8,9 and has been used to design new proteins that retain biological function10,11.

Although correlations between residue pairs have been shown to contribute to protein structure and function, design strategies that do not explicitly include these correlations have been quite successful. These strategies include ancestral reconstruction and consensus design, which infer residues independently at each site12,13. Both strategies produce folded, biologically active proteins14–18 with stabilities that often exceed those of natural proteins19,20.

To better understand the contributions of sequence covariance to protein stability and function and to explore why single-site models achieve high levels of success in protein design while ignoring covariance, we used Potts models to generate and analyze a large set of sequences from the DNA-binding homeodomain (HD) family and three enzyme families. For each family, we designed sequences that differ in the relative amounts of pairwise coupling and single-site bias, allowing us to compare the effects of these two biases to stability and activity.

Fitting this site-independent model produces single-site energy coefficients (Ii(a)) that are determined solely by the marginal residue frequencies while not separating single-site biases from pairwise couplings.

To generate sequences that capture different amounts of the single-site and pairwise coupling biases, we constructed various energy functions ε(seq)i that incorporate different sets of the inferred Potts coefficients to use for energy minimization during sequence generation. These included an energy function with all of the pairwise coupling energies and single-site energies (ε(seq)HJ; equation (7)) and an energy function that omits pairwise terms, using only single-site energies (ε(seq)H; equation (8)). Additionally, we constructed an energy function from the coefficients determined by the site-independent model (equation (5)). Although the energy functions ε(seq)H and ε(seq)I are both composed of single-site terms, a key difference is that, whereas the single-site hi(a) terms in ε(seq)H are separated from the pairwise coupling terms jik(a,b), the coupling and single-site terms end up convoluted into the Ii(a) terms. Thus, sequences generated using ε(seq)I, which are closely related to consensus sequences (indeed, the designed HD sequence with the lowest I(seq) score is identical to the XID-reweighted consensus sequence for the MSA used here), are influenced (albeit indirectly) by pairwise coupling, whereas sequences generated using ε(seq)H are free from pairwise bias.

For each of these energy functions, we used a Monte Carlo search to generate 1,000 independent low-energy HD sequences following a similar protocol to that of Shakhnovich and Gutin21. Resulting sequences are consistent with the energy functions used to create them; sequences designed with ε(seq)H, referred to as H-optimized sequences, have low (favorable) H(seq) values but high J(seq) values (green, Fig. 2), whereas sequences designed with ε(seq)HJ, referred to as HJ-optimized sequences, have low (favorable) J(seq) values but high H(seq) values. Although hi(a) and ji,k(a,b) are equally weighted in ε(seq)HJ, there are many more ji,k(a,b) terms in J(seq) than there are hi(a) terms in H(seq) (212×L(L−1)/2 versus 21L for a sequence of length L). Thus, HJ-optimization is dominated by the ji,k(a,b) couplings (for the HJ-optimized HD sequences, the total J(seq) values are about seven times larger than the total H(seq) values; Fig. 2). As a result, HJ-optimized sequences are quite similar to sequences optimized with ji,k(a,b) terms alone.

Interestingly, HJ-optimized HD sequences have a bimodal distribution of H(seq) and J(seq) values (Fig. 2), suggesting that designing sequences with pairwise coupling energies captures higher-order sequence correlations across three or more sites. Consistent with this, pairwise identities among the 1,000 HJ-optimized sequences have a bimodal distribution resulting from two distinct clusters of sequences of roughly equal size (designated A and B and colored orange and blue in Fig. 2 and subsequent figures). Within clusters A and B, sequences have average identities of 89% and 88%, respectively, whereas, between clusters, sequences have average identities of 55% (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, sequences designed using the ε(seq)H energy function have unimodal H(seq), J(seq) and sequence identity distributions (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Sequences generated using the independent model (with the ε(seq)I energy function) have intermediate levels of single-site and pairwise bias compared to sequences generated from the Potts model. Although the H(seq) values of the I-optimized sequences are lower than the values of the HJ-optimized sequences, as might be expected for a design strategy based on single-site frequencies, they are not as low as H(seq) values of the H-optimized sequences (Fig. 2a). Likewise, although the J(seq) values of the I-optimized sequences are higher than those of HJ-optimized sequences, they are not as high as those of the H-optimized sequences (Fig. 2b). These differences are reflected in sequence identities: the I-optimized sequences are closer to both the H-optimized sequences and HJ-optimized sequences than the H-optimized and HJ-optimized sequences are to one another (Supplementary Fig. 1). The finding that I-optimized sequences have J(seq) values midway between the H-optimized and HJ-optimized sequences confirms that I-optimization inadvertently introduces pairs of residues that are positively covariant, as might be expected for strongly conserved covariant pairs. These observations also indicate that sequences designed using ε(seq)H are purged of pairs of correlated residues. Thus, comparing the H-optimized and HJ-optimized sequences provides a stringent test of the relative effects of single-site biases and pair correlations on stability and function, achieving H(seq) and J(seq) values outside the range spanned by consensus-designed and extant proteins.

Stabilities of designed and extant HD proteins

To examine how single-site and pairwise coupling energies influence stability and function, we selected proteins from each Monte Carlo optimization to express, purify and characterize (Supplementary Table 1). Because each Monte Carlo sequence optimization generates some sequence variation (Supplementary Fig. 1), we characterized multiple proteins for each optimization. For each optimization, we picked the sequence with lowest ε(seq) value (referred to as Seq1), the sequence with the mean ε(seq) value (Seq3) and sequences with an ε(seq) value 1 standard deviation below and above the mean (Seq2 and Seq4, respectively). Thus, for each optimization, ε(seq) values increase from Seq1 to Seq4. For the HJ-optimized sequences, we attempted to characterize four such proteins each from cluster A and cluster B. The sequences selected from each Monte Carlo sequence optimization have in-group identities ranging from 81% to 96% (Supplementary Fig. 2). To attempt to provide an unbiased representation of the biophysical properties of extant HD proteins, we characterized four diverse extant proteins from the MSA and the Drosophila melanogaster Engrailed HD used in our previous study (Supplementary Table 1)16.

Most of the HD proteins designed using Monte Carlo expressed and were soluble. However, three of four sequences from HJ cluster B (HJB_Seq1, HJB_Seq2 and HJB_Seq4) and one sequence from H-optimized sequences (H_Seq3) had limited solubilities and could not be characterized. For H_Seq3, we characterized the two sequences closest in ε(seq) score, H_Seq3a and H_Seq3b. For all proteins, far-ultraviolet (UV) circular dichroism (CD) spectra are similar in magnitude to those of extant HDs, indicating stable α-helical structures (Supplementary Fig. 3a). To examine how the single-site and pairwise coupling energies contribute to stability, we collected GdnHCl-induced unfolding transitions (Fig. 3a). All proteins show sigmoidal unfolding transitions with similar slopes, indicating that the folding cooperativity of the designed HDs is similar to the extant HDs. To quantify the free energies of folding, we fitted the unfolding transitions with a two-state model in which free energy depends linearly on GdnHCl concentration (equations (18) and (19)). Extant HDs have folding free energies ranging from −2.38 to −5.45 kcal mol−1 with a mean of −3.91 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 3). HD sequences from the ε(seq)HJ optimization are more stable than the extant HDs, with sequences from cluster A having folding free energies ranging from −6.84 to −9.36 kcal mol−1 with a mean of −8.20 kcal mol−1; the single sequence we were able to purify from cluster B (HJB_Seq1) has a folding free energy of −6.45 kcal mol−1. Thus, including bothe single-site and pairwise coupling information from the MSA increases stability compared to the extant sequences (by an average ΔΔG of −4.29 and −2.54 kcal mol−1 for cluster A and HJB_Seq3; Supplementary Table 3). This result is consistent with previous studies that show correlation between protein stability and Potts energies determined by equally weighting single-site and pairwise couplings.

To determine the relative contributions of the single-site and pairwise coupling energies to protein stability, we measured GdnHCl-induced unfolding transitions of HD sequences generated by optimizing ε(seq)H. To our surprise, these H-optimized sequences are much more stable than those generated by HJ-optimization. Indeed, the sampled H-optimized sequences were so stable that the unfolded baselines were not resolved at 25 °C (Fig. 3a). For these proteins, stabilities at 25 °C were determined using a global analysis including unfolding transitions at elevated temperatures (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 4). Folding free energies of H-optimized sequences at 25 °C ranged from −12.0 to −15.2 kcal mol−1, with a mean of −14.0 kcal mol−1. This observation does not support the idea that increases in stability of Potts-designed sequences result from optimizing pairwise sequence energies (the HJ-optimized sequences have much lower (that is, more negative) J(seq) values than the H-optimized sequences; Fig. 2). Rather, it suggests that the stability increase results from optimizing single-site energies (the H-optimized sequences have much lower (more negative) H(seq) values than the HJ-optimized sequences); indeed, a positive correlation is seen between stability and H(seq) values for all of the HD sequences examined here (Fig. 3c). Alternatively, it is possible that positively covariant residue pairs are destabilizing, although analysis with a larger set of sequences (see below) does not support this mechanism.

The magnitude of the increase in protein stability for the H-optimized sequences is large, especially considering the small size of the HD (57 residues). The average folding free energy of the H-optimized sequences increases by −10.1 kcal mol−1 compared to extant HD sequences (−13.4 versus −3.9 kcal mol−1; Fig. 3b). This is a considerably larger stability increase than we saw previously for consensus HDs. To compare to stabilities of consensus-like sequences designed from the same alignment and Monte Carlo design methods used here for Potts analysis, we measured stabilities of sequences designed using the ε(seq)I energy function. These ε(seq)I-optimized HDs have folding free energies ranging from −8.49 to −12.5 kcal mol−1, with a mean of −10.5 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 3). On average, the H-optimized sequences are more stable than the ε(seq)I-optimized sequences by −3.5 kcal mol−1. A similar stability increment (ΔΔG° = −2.7 kcal mol−1) is seen between the most stable H-optimized sequence (H_Seq2) and the most stable I-optimized sequence (the consensus, Ind_Seq1). Thus, at least for HD sequences, designing sequences using H-optimized coefficients can substantially increase stability beyond that obtained by consensus design.

In contrast, the I-optimized sequences are more stable than the HJ-optimized sequences by an average ΔΔG° of −2.2 kcal mol−1 (cluster A) and −4.0 kcal mol−1 (cluster B HJB_Seq3). This increase in stability may be because of the larger H(seq) energies for the I-optimized sequences than the HJ-optimized sequences (Fig. 2a).

Stabilities of HD sequences with limited pairwise couplings

Our finding that HD sequences designed using pairwise coupling information are less stable than sequences designed using single-site bias (either through H-optimization or consensus-like I-optimization) is unexpected, given previous work demonstrating the importance of residue couplings in promoting folding and predicting structural contacts2,6. One possible explanation for this finding is that optimization using pair coupling terms is dominated by a large number of noisy ji,k(a,b) terms from uncoupled sites. To mitigate the effects of potential noise that may be introduced by small coupling terms, we designed sequences using energy functions in which small coupling terms are omitted. In one design, we optimized sequences using an energy function (ε(seq)S, equation (9)) in which coupling terms are restricted to the 10% of residue pairs that have the strongest couplings (Methods). In another design, we optimized sequences using an energy function (ε(seq)C, equation (10)) in which coupling terms are restricted to the 10% of residue pairs that are closest in the D. melanogaster Engrailed HD structure. These two subsets of pair positions are similar (Supplementary Fig. 9a, right), consistent with findings that strongly coupled residue pairs are close in three-dimensional structure22.

Using the ε(seq)S and ε(seq)C energy functions, we designed HJS-optimized and HJC-optimized HD sequences as described above (Supplementary Table 1). These sequences have H(seq) and J(seq) values midway between HJ-optimized and H-optimized sequences (Fig. 2). HJS-optimized and HJC-optimized proteins have similar stabilities (Fig. 3; mean ΔG° values of −11.1 and −10.2 kcal mol−1, respectively). The HJS-optimized and HJC-optimized sequences are more stable than the HJ-optimized sequences (mean ΔΔG° values of −2.9 and −2.0 kcal mol−1) but are less stable than H-optimized sequences (mean ΔΔG° values are +2.9 and +3.8 kcal mol−1). These results suggest that the lower stabilities of the HJ-optimized sequences compared to the H-optimized sequences are not the result of noise introduced from weak coupling coefficients, but from the stabilizing effects of single-site hi(a) energy terms.

Sensitivity of HD stabilities to global fitting parameters

Fitted values of Potts hi(a) and ji,k(a,b) terms are affected by three global parameters: two regularization parameters (λh and λj) and a sequence reweighting parameter (XID). The regularization parameters mitigate overfitting of the hi(a) and the many ji,k(a,b) terms (~700,000 for HD), most of which correspond to unobserved or infrequently observed residue pairs and are poorly defined by the MSA. The sequence reweighting parameter downweights highly similar sequences in the MSA to reduce effects from taxonomic sequencing biases. For the HD sequences described above, we used the parameters λh = λj = 0.01 and XID = 0.8, which have been used in previous Potts analyses23. To test whether our findings are robust to these global optimization parameters, we generated a set of HD sequences comparable to the set described above using the parameters λh = λj = 0.1 and XID = 0.2. Increasing the regularization parameters by a factor of 10 should decrease potential noise in the fitted hi(a) and ji,k(a,b) coefficients but may bias these coefficients to small values. Decreasing the sequence reweighting parameter should result in a more uniform weighting, as nearly all MSA sequences are identical to one another at the 20% threshold (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Using this new set of global parameters, Potts coefficients were inferred from the same HD MSA used above. Fitted hi(a) and ji,k(a,b) coefficients are similar to those determined above (Supplementary Fig. 5), with Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.94 and 0.65, respectively. As above, these Potts coefficients were combined in different proportions into energy functions for Monte Carlo sequence generation (Supplementary Table 2). These included an energy function using only H(seq) values (εseqH, equation (8)), a function equally weighting H(seq) and J(seq) values (εseqHJ, which again generated two clusters of sequences), and a function that downweights J(seq) by a factor of 20 so that it is equal in magnitude to H(seq) (εseqH+0.05J).

Overall, the HD sequences designed with the global parameters XID = 0.2, λh = λj = 0.1 show the same stability patterns as those described above (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7 and 9 and Supplementary Table 4). The HJ sequences optimized with the global parameters XID = 0.2, λh = λj = 0.1 are more stable than extant sequences (by an average ΔΔG of −2.4 and −3.6 kcal mol−1 for cluster A and B), although they are considerably less stable than H-optimized sequences (by an average ΔΔG of +5.3 and +4.1 kcal mol−1). On average, the H sequences optimized with XID = 0.2, λh = λj = 0.1 are more stable than the consensus (I-optimized) sequence by an average ΔΔG of −1.3 kcal mol−1; the most stable H-optimized sequence is more stable than consensus by −4.2 kcal mol−1. These trends are consistent with the findings above (Fig. 3) that stability is maximized by optimizing single-site H(seq) scores rather than pairwise J(seq) scores, demonstrating this finding to be robust to optimization details.

Correlation of HD stability with H(seq) and J(seq)

Together, the HD sequences from the various Potts design strategies (62 sequences total including extant sequences; Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and 5) provide a large dataset to evaluate how stability is related to the H(seq) and J(seq) scores. We find a moderate positive correlation between ΔG∘H2O values and H(seq) values (calculated with the Potts coefficients obtained using global parameters XID = 0.8, λh = λj = 0.01), with a Pearson correlation coefficient of ρGH=+0.80 (P = 1.49 × 10−13; Fig. 4a). In contrast, we find a weaker negative correlation between ΔG∘H2O values and J(seq) values, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of ρGH=−0.44 (P = 3.17 × 10−4; Fig. 4b). Although this negative correlation may be taken as evidence that positively covariant residues are destabilizing, it may alternatively reflect an underlying negative correlation between H(seq) and J(seq) values (ρGH=−0.61, P = 1.34 × 10−7; Fig. 4c). To control for indirect correlation between H(seq) and J(seq), we calculated partial correlation coefficients between ΔG∘H2O and H(seq) (ρGH•J; equation (31)) and between ΔG∘H2O and J(seq) (ρGJ•H). The value of ρGH•J is nearly the same as ρGH (0.75 versus 0.80), whereas the value of ρGJ•H is much smaller than ρGJ (+0.10 versus −0.44). The extant sequences (blue points in Fig. 4) have an important role in resolving this correlation; they are outliers in the H(seq) versus J(seq) plot (Fig. 4c) and in the ΔG∘H2O versus J(seq) plot (Fig. 4b) but retain correlation in the ΔG∘H2O versus H(seq) plot (Fig. 4a). The partial correlation coefficients suggest that H(seq) is the main determinant of stability for Potts-designed HD sequences, whereas the correlation to J(seq) is an indirect effect of correlation between H(seq) and J(seq).

Stabilities of Potts-designed enzymes

To test whether our findings from the HD family that the hi(a) coefficients are the primary determinants of protein stability are general, we performed Potts analysis on three unrelated enzyme families: adenylate kinase (AK), dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), and phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK). For AK, Potts coefficients were determined using regulation parameters λh = λj = 0.1, whereas, for DHFR and PGK, coefficients were determined using λh = λj = 0.01. We used the resulting Potts coefficients in the ε(seq)H, ε(seq)HJ and ε(seq)I energy functions (equations (8), (7) and (16)) to design sequences as described above (Supplementary Tables 7–9) and expressed and purified sequences with the lowest energy with the exception of the H-optimized PGK sequence, where we expressed and purified the sequence with the median ε(seq)H − 1 standard deviation (the lowest energy H-optimized PGK sequences expressed poorly and showed limited solubility). In addition, we generated a DHFR sequence where we optimized only the ji,k(a,b) coefficients (equation (11)); the lowest energy sequence from this optimization was 98% identical to the HJ-optimized DHFR sequence.

As with the designed HD proteins, the Potts-designed AK, DHFR and PGK proteins have far-UV CD spectra consistent with their expected secondary structures (Supplementary Fig. 3b–d), although there is some variation in shape for DHFR and in magnitude for PGK. For all three enzyme families, CD-monitored unfolding transitions show the same trends as seen for the designed HD proteins; the HJ-optimized (and, for DHFR, J-optimized) proteins denature at the lowest guanidine concentrations, the I-optimized (consensus) proteins denature at intermediate guanidine concentrations and the H-optimized proteins denature at the highest guanidine concentrations (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 10).

To estimate folding free energies, guanidine unfolding curves for AK and DHFR designs were fitted with a two-state model for unfolding. Fitted ΔG°H2O values for H-optimized AK and DHFR proteins are lower (more negative) than for HJ-optimized (and, for DHFR, J-optimized) proteins by −6.7 and −1.7 kcal mol−1, respectively (Supplementary Table 10), reflecting greater stability for H-optimized proteins compared to HJ-optimized proteins. Likewise, fitted ΔG°H2O values for I-optimized AK and DHFR proteins are lower than for HJ-optimized proteins by −6.7 and −1.2 kcal mol−1, respectively.

In contrast to what was seen with HD designs, the fitted ΔG°H2O values for the H-optimized and consensus proteins are within margins of uncertainty for both AK and DHFR. This observation appears to be at odds with the much greater denaturation midpoints (Cm values) for the H-optimized proteins than the I-optimized (consensus) proteins (Supplementary Table 10). This apparent discrepancy is a result of decreased m values for the H-optimized AK and DHFR proteins. Because ΔG°H2O values are extrapolated from the unfolding transition (at Cm) to zero GdnHCl concentration, a low m value will decrease the magnitude of the extrapolated ΔG°H2O value. If the lower m values for the H-optimized proteins result from populated partly folded states in the transition region, the estimated ΔG°H2O values are likely to underestimate the stabilities of the corresponding native states.

Unlike the sigmoidal transitions of AK and DHFR, the GdnHCl-induced folding transitions of PGK show clear evidence of multistate unfolding. This is particularly pronounced for the HJ-optimized PGK protein, which shows two separate unfolding transitions that bracket a partly folded intermediate at about 1.2 M GdnHCl. In addition, the unfolding transition of the H-optimized PGK protein is very broad, consistent with a multistate transition. Multistate equilibrium unfolding has been reported previously for extant PGK proteins24–27, consistent with the two-domain structure of PGK. To estimate the folding free energies of our PGK designs, we fitted the GdnHCl-induced unfolding transitions with multistate models (Methods). For I-optimized (consensus) and HJ-optimized PGKs, multistate fitting provides stability estimates for the N-terminal and C-terminal domains within the context of full-length PGK (ΔG°n and ΔG°c, Supplementary Table 11). As free energy is a state function, the stability of fully folded PGK is the sum of ΔG°n and ΔG°c. This summation gives folding free energies of −16.2 and −19.6 kcal mol−1 for HJ-optimized and I-optimized PGKs, again indicating that I-optimization (consensus) design achieves higher stability than HJ-optimization. For H-optimized PGK, the breadth of the single unfolding transition precluded fitting with a multistate model; although a two-state model adequately fits the unfolding transition, the resulting parameters are not likely to yield a meaningful estimate of folding free energy.

Overall, the unfolding studies of AK, DHFR and PGK indicate that, as was seen with HD, stability is maximized by optimizing H(seq), whereas optimizing J(seq) along with H(seq) (or, for DHFR, optimizing J(seq) alone) decreases stability compared to consensus and H(seq) optimized sequences. Testing whether the destabilization resulting from J(seq) optimization results from a concomitant increase in H(seq) within these enzyme families will require much more data.

Functional properties of Potts-designed proteins

The results above show that, for four protein families, positively covariant residue pairs do not increase protein stability and, to the extent that they conflict with single-site preferences, they may decrease stability. As protein sequence alignments show clear statistical covariation patterns, it seems that covariance must contribute to some other aspect of protein fitness. Thus, we sought to determine how optimizing covariance impacts biological function.

The main function of HDs is to site-specifically bind to DNA to activate transcription28. Accordingly, we determined DNA-binding affinities for H-optimized and HJ-optimized sequences. On the basis of sequence features, both proteins are predicted to bind to the 5′-TAATTA-3′ binding site typical for many HD families29. Whereas all of these proteins retain the ability to bind DNA, the H-optimized sequences bind with much higher affinity (Kd = 8.9 nM) than the HJ-optimized sequences (Kd = 343 nM) (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 11). This tighter binding affinity is achieved through a more favorable binding enthalpy and is slightly offset by a less favorable binding entropy. The H-optimized HD binds with similar affinity as a consensus HD characterized previously (Kd = 8.1 nM; Supplementary Table 12) and is similar to the binding affinity of Ind1 (4.2 nM). Both the H-optimized and HJ-optimized proteins bind with higher affinity than the extant D. melanogaster Engrailed HD (Kd = 787 nM; Supplementary Table 12)16.

AK, PGK and DHFR are bisubstrate enzymes; the first two catalyze phosphotransfer reactions and the third catalyzes hydride transfer. To explore how pairwise covariance influences catalysis, we determined turnover numbers (kcat values) under saturating substrate conditions (Methods). For all three enzyme families, we find kcat values to be much higher for HJ-optimized sequences (and, for DHFR, J-optimized sequences) than for H-optimized sequences (Fig. 6d–f and Supplementary Table 13). For AK and DHFR, the kcat values of the HJ-optimized sequences are similar to extant AK and DHFR from Escherichia coli. For PGK, the kcat value of the HJ-optimized sequence is about fourfold lower than that from Escherichia coli PGK, although it is similar to kcat values from thermophilic PGK sequences (Supplementary Table 13). In a previous study, we found consensus sequences of these three families to have lower kcat values than extant sequences; here, we find that HJ-optimized sequences have much higher kcat values than consensus (I-optimized) sequences for all three families (Fig. 6). Whereas H-optimized AK has a higher kcat value than the consensus sequence, H-optimized sequences of DHFR and PGK have much lower kcat values than consensus sequences (Supplementary Table 13).

Discussion

The above results indicate that, to the extent that Potts design increases protein stability, it does so by including single-site biases rather than pairwise coupling. Consensus design, which has been shown to generate stable proteins20,30, also generates sequences that capture single-site biases; however, because positively covariant residues tend to occur frequently, consensus design inadvertently includes pairwise coupling. The Potts model provides a way to separate single-site biases from pair correlations, revealing that sequences optimizing H(seq) are more stable than those optimizing J(seq).

The observation that pairwise couplings do not contribute to protein stability is surprising. Pairwise couplings have been shown to correlate with structural contacts6,22 and are important for protein structure prediction31. As most protein structures are free energy minima1, pairwise couplings might be expected to help define such minima. Moreover, analyses of deep mutational screens show a positive correlation between protein fitness and Potts scores9,32,33, and designs using Potts scores have been shown to generate stable folded proteins34. One explanation for this apparent discrepancy is that these studies combined hi(a) and ji,k(a,b) scores and compared variants to extant proteins. Indeed, when we compare stabilities of extant proteins with our HJ-optimized sequences, the HJ-optimized sequences have increased stability. This stabilization may be the result of the modest increases in H(seq) values that result from HJ-optimization (Fig. 2). Although our designed sequences that maximize couplings are more stable than extant sequences, they are less stable than sequences in which H(seq) values are maximized at the expense of J(seq) values. Previous studies on WW domains showed that sequences that preserve only the single-site residue frequencies were not folded, whereas sequences that preserve covariance were more often folded2. Although these results seem at odds with our findings, the designed WW sequences maintained average single-site biases of extant proteins, whereas our strategy maximizes single-site biases.

Our finding that single-site hi(a) values determine thermodynamic stability is consistent with a recent high-throughput study that showed that protein abundance in cells could be explained by a thermodynamic model that included only single-site biases35. However, in that study, including pairwise couplings improved the accuracy of the thermodynamic model, consistent with deep mutational studies9,32,33 but at at odds with the results here.

Our finding that single-site hi(a) values determine thermodynamic stability is also consistent with a recent study36 analyzing high-throughput stability data37 that found that single-site independent models predicted ΔG° values, as did pairwise correlation models36. However, we find that ΔG° values for HD correlate better with a single-site bias model than with a model that includes covariance (ρGI=+0.71, ρGJ=−0.44). Another notable distinction is that we find a negative correlation between ΔG°H2O and total Potts score (ρGH+J = −0.33, P = 3 × 10−6), whereas the high-throughput study study revealed positive correlations. Similar correlations of ΔG° to scores from independent and pairwise correlation models might be because of high noise in the latter36. Indeed, this noise problem may be particularly acute in analyses of random mutational studies, where the bulk of the mutations introduce residues that are poorly (or not at all) represented in the MSAs used to calculate independent and Potts scores. An advantage of the strategy used here, where sequences are designed to optimize H(seq), J(seq) and I(seq) scores, is that mutations are introduced that are well represented in the MSAs. As such, substitutions in our design strategy favor statistically significant changes to H(seq), J(seq) and I(seq) scores. A second advantage of our design approach compared to deep mutational studies is that simultaneously optimizing all positions in a protein sequence leads to much larger overall changes in H(seq), J(seq) and I(seq) scores than individual random point substitutions, providing a more robust test of potential correlations.

Our observation of low m values of H-optimized AK, DHFR and PGK proteins suggests that pairwise coupling may contribute to folding cooperativity. Decreased m values often result from partly folded states in the unfolding transition but may also result from disruption structure in the native state in the absence of denaturant, resulting in a smaller cooperative unit. For AK, where the CD spectra of all constructs are nearly identical (Supplementary Fig. 3b), partial disruption of structure seems unlikely, suggesting a multistate unfolding transition for the H-optimized construct. In contrast, the CD-spectrum of the H-optimized PGK construct shows decreased ellipticity in the α-helical region, suggesting that the decreased m value may result, in part, from structural disruption in the native state (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Either way, the observed m value decreases suggest that H-optimization may be insufficient to maintain high cooperativity, especially in larger proteins (for HD, H-optimized proteins retain high m values; Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

Although pairwise coupling information does not appear to contribute to protein stability, it appears to be important for enzyme catalysis. For the three enzymes studied here, HJ-optimized proteins have the highest turnover numbers of all the Potts designs. For DHFR and PGK, H-optimized proteins have the lowest turnover numbers, falling below values for consensus proteins. These findings are consistent with studies linking Potts scores to enzyme activity9. For DHFR and PGK, the high activities of HJ constructs are also consistent with a stability–activity trade-off38; however, the H-optimized AK is both more stable (based on Cm) and more active than consensus AK, suggesting that differences in activity among HJ and H designs are not merely a consequence of stability differences. For HD, where DNA binding is critical for function, we find that although HJ-optimized HD proteins have higher DNA-binding affinity than the extant D. melanogaster Engrailed HD, H-optimized HDs have even higher affinity (Supplementary Table 12). Here again, the variations in DNA-binding affinities and stabilities of designed HD proteins are inconsistent with a stability–activity trade-off.

The observation that stability can be increased by maximizing single-site Potts energies has implications for protein design. Optimizing stability using single-site H energies may provide a route to further enhance stabilities beyond the stabilization typically afforded by consensus design30 and may provide a route to stabilization in what seems to be the minority of cases where consensus sequences do not increase stability39. For binding domains such as HD, H-optimization may also enhance binding affinity. Although our results with DHFR and PGK show that the stability increase afforded by H-optimization may come at the expense of decreased enzyme activity, the result with AK suggests that, in some cases, this decrease may be modest. It is possible that, by introducing limited sets of pair-correlated residues into H-optimized sequences, enzymes can be found that maintain high stability and high activity.

Obtaining MSAs

For the HD family, we obtained the ‘full’ alignment from Pfam (PF00046, accessed on October 23, 2018)40. Positions with gap frequencies greater than 50% were removed, resulting in an alignment length of L = 57. Sequences that contained greater than 50% gap characters were removed from the alignment, as were identical sequences, resulting in an alignment contained m = 19,221 sequences.

For the AK family, we obtained sequences from the InterPro database (IPR007862, accessed on May 13, 2019)41. Sequences containing nonstandard amino acids and sequences shorter than 172 residues or longer than 258 residues were removed. Sequences were then aligned using MAFFT42. Positions with gap frequencies greater than 50% were removed, resulting in a sequence length of L = 214 for all sequences. Sequences that contained greater than 10% gap characters were removed from the alignment, as were identical sequences. The resulting alignment contained m = 14,090 sequences.

For the DHFR family, we obtained sequences from the InterPro database (IPR001796, accessed on August 23, 2024). Sequences containing nonstandard amino acids and sequences shorter than 133 residues or longer than 199 residues were removed from the sequence set. Sequences were then aligned using MAFFT. Positions with gap frequencies greater than 50% were removed, resulting in a sequence length of L = 161 for all sequences. Resulting sequences that contained greater than 50% gap characters were removed from the alignment, as were identical sequences. The resulting alignment contained m = 18,903 sequences.

For the PGK family, we obtained sequences from the InterPro database (IPR001576, accessed on January 19, 2024). Sequences containing nonstandard amino acids and sequences shorter than 318 residues or longer than 476 residues were removed from the sequence set. Sequences were then aligned using MAFFT. Positions with gap frequencies greater than 50% were removed, resulting in a sequence length of L = 393 for all sequences. Sequences that contained greater than 10% gap characters were removed from the alignment. Sequences were clustered at 90% identity using CD-HIT43 and only one representative from each cluster was chosen). The resulting alignment contained m = 19,161 sequences.

Inference of single-site and coupling energy coefficients using the Potts model

To infer the L × 21 (for the 20 amino acids plus a gap) single-site energy coefficients and the 212×L(L−1)2 coupling energy coefficients, we used the pseudolikelihood optimization procedure of Aurell and coworkers7,23. Because of the finite sampling of sequences in the MSA combined with the large number of ji,k(a,b) coefficients, the Potts model is prone to overfitting. In separate fits of the HD MSA, we applied values of λh = λj = 0.1 and λh = λj = 0.01 to explore the effects of the magnitude of regularization on sequences generated from the Potts formalism and their properties. For DHFR and PGK, we used regularization values of λh = λj = 0.01. For AK, we used regularization values of λh = λj = 0.1.

To decrease the effect of phylogenetic biases on the sequence composition of the MSAs, the contribution of each sequence to the model objective function was weighted on the basis of sequence identities. Sequence identities were calculated as the percentage of matching residues or gaps between two sequences over the L positions in the processed MSAs outlined above. A threshold sequence identity (XID) was chosen to identify sequences with high similarities. For a sequence b in the MSA, we determined the number of sequences in the MSA, mb, with identities to sequence b greater than the threshold XID, and weighted the contribution of sequence b in the pseudolikelihood function by wb=1/mb. Thus, a sequence with identity above XID to many other sequences in the MSA is given low weight. For HD, we used thresholds of XID = 0.2 and XID = 0.8 to examine the effects from sequence weighting on the model fit. For the AK, DHFR and PGK, we used a threshold of XID = 0.8.

Gene synthesis, protein expression and preparation

Genes were synthesized by Twist Bioscience. Genes encoding HD, AK and DHFR were cloned into the pET28a(+) expression vector between the NcoI and XhoI restriction sites. PGK-encoding genes were cloned into pET29b(+) using the same restriction sites. HD and DHFR constructs included an N-terminal Met-Gly-Ser sequence for translation initiation and cloning. AK constructs included an N-terminal Trp (that is, Met-Gly-Ser-Trp) to facilitate concentration determination. Consensus PGK included an N-terminal Met-Gly sequence. All constructs included a C-terminal His6-tag for purification.

Proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. Cells were grown at 37 °C to an optical density at 600 nm of 0.6–0.8 and induced with IPTG at a final concentration of 1 mM. HDs and PGKs were expressed overnight at 20 °C, whereas AKs and DHFRs were expressed for 4–6 h at 37 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in buffer containing 50 mM Tris pH 8.0 for HDs and DHFRs, 50 mM Tris and 1 mM TCEP pH 8.0 for AKs and 50 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl and 1 mM TCEP pH 8.0 for PGKs. For HDs, AKs and PGKs, buffers were supplemented with a cocktail of protease inhibitors (Roche cOmplete EDTA-free). For DHFRs, buffers were supplemented with pepstatin A and bestatin. Cells were lysed by sonication and the cell lysate was cleared by centrifugation. Supernatants were collected, supplemented with MgCl2 and CaCl2 to a final concentration of 2 mM each and incubated with 1 mg of DNase I and 250 U of Benzonase nuclease for 1–3 h at 25 °C. PGKs were supplemented with NaCl to 500 mM. Proteins were then purified by Ni-NTA chromatography, dialyzed and further purified with ion-exchange chromatography. For H-optimized and HJ-optimized PGK, ion exchange was omitted but all PGK proteins were further purified with a Cytiva HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75 pg gel filtration column. Purified HDs and DHFRs were dialyzed into buffer containing 25 mM NaPO4 and 150 mM NaCl pH 7.0. Purified AKs were dialyzed into 25 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl and 1 mM TCEP pH 8.0. Purified PGKs were dialyzed into 25 mM HEPES–KOH, 120 mM KCl and 3 mM TCEP pH 7.2.

Two of the AK proteins (the single-site optimized protein and the consensus protein) and the DHFR proteins were found to retain endogenous substrate following purification. These proteins were loaded back onto the Ni-NTA column, washed with buffer containing 25 mM Tris (or, for DHFR proteins, 50 mM Tris), 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP pH 8.0 and 6–8 M GdnHCl to unfold the proteins, thereby dissociating and removing bound substrate. Nickel-bound proteins were refolded by washing with 25 mM Tris (or, for DHFR proteins, 50 mM Tris), 50 mM NaCl and 1 mM TCEP pH 8.0 and were subsequently eluted under native conditions. Purified AKs and DHFR proteins were dialyzed into 25 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl and 1 mM TCEP pH 8.0 and into 25 mM NaPO4 and 150 mM NaCl pH 7, respectively. Proteins were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. The purity of all proteins was assessed by SDS–PAGE. For DHFRs, molecular weights were further verified using intact mass spectrometry using a Waters UPLC-MS Acquity H-Class chromatography system interfaced to a Xevo-G2 QToF mass spectrometer. In addition to showing the ion at the expected mass for intact DHFRs, the HJ-optimized and J-optimized samples showed additional ions at lower molecular weight, although these ions were not detected by SDS–PAGE. Protein concentrations were determined by UV absorbance at 280 nm using extinction coefficients from tryptophan and tyrosine residues44.

Correlation analysis of HD stability, H(seq) and J(seq)

As shown in Fig. 4, the correlations between ΔG∘H2O and the H(seq) and J(seq) scores may be the indirect result of anticorrelation between H(seq) and J(seq). To isolate the effects of H(seq) and J(seq) on ΔG∘H2O from the potentially confounding effects of the other variable, we calculated partial correlation coefficients, ρGH•J and ρGJ•H. Numerically, ρGJ•H is the correlation coefficient between the residuals from linear regression of ΔG∘H2O on the H(seq) scores (ri, GH; Fig. 4a) and the residuals from the regression of the J(seq) scores on the H(seq) scores (ri, JH; Fig. 4c):

The residuals in linear regression represent the variation in the dependent variable that cannot be accounted for from variation of the independent variable. Thus, a large value of ρGJ•H indicates that the variations in ΔG∘H2O that are independent of the H(seq) scores (represented by the ri, GH values) are correlated with the variations in the J(seq) scores that are independent of the H(seq) scores (represented by ri,JH values). Conversely, a small value of ρGJ•H indicates that the variations in ΔG∘H2O that are independent of H(seq) are also independent of the variation in J(seq) values that are independent of H(seq). That is, removing the correlation of J(seq) with H(seq) also removes the correlation of ΔG∘H2O with J(seq), implying that the apparent correlation of ΔG∘H2O with J(seq) is an indirect result of correlation of ΔG∘H2O with H(seq).

Steady-state enzyme kinetics

Steady-state enzyme kinetics was measured spectrophotometrically, following the oxidation or reduction of NAD(P)H/NAD(P)+, either as a direct reaction for DHFR or using coupled enzyme assays for AK and PGK. For AK, ADP produced by starting the reaction with AMP and ATP is used to convert phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate in the presence of excess pyruvate kinase, which is subsequently used to reduce NADH in the presence of excess lactate dehydrogenase. For PGK, 1,3-bisophosophygycerate produced by starting the reaction with ATP and 3-phosphoglycerate is monitored by reaction with NADH, catalyzed by GAPDH.

For AK, steady-state initial velocities were determined from the linear portion of plots of A340 versus time under saturating concentrations of both AMP and ATP and were converted to kcat values using total AK concentration along with extinction coefficients for NADH and NAD+ (Supplementary Fig. 11)47. Reactions were carried out in 50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 20 mM MgCl2, 0. 5 mM TCEP, 10 mM phosphoenolpyruvate, 0.1 mM NADH, 7.4 mM AMP, 9.2 mM ADP, 27–42 U per ml lactate dehydrogenase and 18–30 U pyruvate kinase. All reactions were collected at 25 °C. Reactions were initiated by addition of AK to final concentrations of 0.9 nM for H-optimized AK, 0.4 nM for HJ-optimized AK and 122 nM for I-optimized AK.

For DHFR, steady-state initial velocities were determined from the linear portion of A340 versus time plots under saturating DHF concentrations and increasing concentrations of NADPH using in-house Python scripts and ICEKAT48,49. For H-optimized DHFR, this linear decrease in absorbance was preceded by a rapid increase in absorbance. These steady-state initial velocity measurements were converted to changes in concentrations using the combined NADPH/DHF extinction coefficient of 11,800 cm−1 M−1 (ref. 50) (Supplementary Fig. 12a). All reactions were collected at 25 °C and were initiated by addition of NADPH to a mixture of enzyme and DHF (90–105 μM) that had been preincubated on ice for at least 20 min. Final enzyme concentrations were 400 nM for H-optimized DHFR, 30 nM for the HJ-optimized DHFR, 50 nM for J-optimized DHFR and 150 nM for I-optimized DHFR. Reactions were carried out in 25 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl and 3–5 mM DTT pH 7.5.

We thank members of the Barrick lab for discussions of experimental design, data analysis and interpretations presented here. We thank the Johns Hopkins Center for Molecular Biophysics for access to instrumentation used in this work.

Author contributions

M.S. conceptualized the Potts analysis here and was responsible, along with D.B., for many of the design features used in this study, including selection of multiple variants from each design class with a range of design scores. M.S. calculated the Potts coefficients and designed the sequences for HD and AK. M.S. and K.T. performed the experiments with HD sequences and analyzed the data. M.S. performed the experiments with AK and analyzed the data. S.B. calculated the Potts coefficients and designed the sequences for PGK, performed the experiments and analyzed the data. J.N. calculated the Potts coefficients and designed the sequences for DHFR, performed the experiments and analyzed the data. M.S. and D.B. wrote the manuscript with sections on PGK and DHFR contributed by S.B. and J.N., respectively. All authors edited the manuscript.
