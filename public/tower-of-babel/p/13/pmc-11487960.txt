An expert consensus on the most effective components of integrated motivational interviewing and cognitive behavioural therapy for lifestyle behaviour change: protocol for an online modified Delphi study
Authors: Stephen Barrett, Stephen Begg, Ashley Dunford, Paul O'Halloran, Kane Rodda, Emily Denniss, Jeff Breckon, Michael Kingsley
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionBehaviour change interventions are advocated as a key approach to manage behavioural risk factors such as insufficient physical activity, poor diet and smoking. Integrated motivational interviewing and cognitive behavioural therapy (MI-CBT) interventions have become increasingly popular to promote behaviour change; however, there is a lack of agreement as to what constitutes necessary components of MI-CBT interventions for lifestyle behaviour change. The primary objective of this study is to use a consensus method to develop guidelines to design and deliver MI-CBT interventions for lifestyle behaviour change.MethodsA three-round modified Delphi study will be conducted with an expert, international panel of clinicians and researchers. An online survey will be developed from assessments of key MI-CBT literature and practice guides to identify commonly used components of individually delivered MI and CBT interventions. In each round, participants will rate the extent to which they agree with each component using a Likert scale. Responses from Rounds 1 and 2 will be presented to participants in subsequent rounds. Responses will be represented using bar graphs and include the median and IQR of participants’ responses. To encourage consensus, participants will be asked to consider the group responses before finalising their opinion to the statements. If at least 80% of the experts agree to a statement in Rounds 2 or 3, it will be included in a final list of necessary statements.DiscussionThis modified Delphi process will help transparency in the design and implementation of MI-CBT interventions. The consensus statement will also help reporting and comparability among effectiveness studies for MI-CBT intervention studies, and help inform research, policy and practice.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been granted by the La Trobe University Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number HEC24066). The results will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publications, conferences and international professional associations.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, Behavior, PREVENTIVE MEDICINE

Background

Chronic diseases such as diabetes, heart disease and some cancers are leading causes of ill health and morbidity worldwide.1 2 Modifiable risk factors such as insufficient physical activity (PA), suboptimal diet quality and smoking are associated with an increased risk for chronic disease3; behaviour change is required to mitigate the impact of preventable chronic diseases and to slow disease progression.4 As a result, behaviour change interventions are advocated as a first-line approach to behavioural risk factor management.5

Motivational interviewing (MI) is an evidence-based, effective intervention to promote behaviour change by promoting an individual’s own intrinsic motivation for change and in doing so, overcoming ambivalence for behaviour change.6 MI has well-established efficacy for initiating change in health-related behaviours. As MI was developed to promote initiation of change, many MI interventions have not incorporated maintenance strategies, which can lead to behaviour change relapse.6 MI interventions have been demonstrated to be more effective and longer lasting when integrated with other active treatments, for example, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), rather than delivered alone.7 CBT is most commonly used to undertake and maintain behaviour change, employing prominent skills focusing on self-regulation and relapse prevention.8 Although MI and CBT have been increasingly integrated together,9,13 there are differences in the styles that need to be considered when designing behaviour change interventions.14 15

In MI, therapists use a communication style that is person-centred, empathetic and in accordance with ‘MI spirit’. This spirit refers to the core values of partnership, acceptance, evocation and compassion.6 These relational qualities have been theorised to be significant factors for the effectiveness of MI.16 Four overlapping central processes support the MI practitioner to navigate the MI sessions: engaging (establishing a trusting relationship), focusing (concentrating on the change goal), evoking (helping the patient to voice his/her own motives for change) and planning (developing a commitment to change and creating a specific action plan).6 The technical effectiveness of MI is achieved through a set of micro-skills such as open-ended questions, affirming, reflective listening and summarising to highlight and selectively reinforce patients’ self-motivational ‘change talk’.6

CBT is also characterised by a core set of components, which are given different emphasis depending on the preferred approach of the therapist and the needs of the client.17 For example, increasing PA can be seen as an uptake (or adoption) behaviour; whereas, quitting smoking can be seen as a cessation behaviour. The CBT components or skills needed to promote changes in these behaviours might be different. It is likely that some CBT components are more important to the success of therapy for some lifestyle behaviour change interventions over others. However, there is currently insufficient evidence to pinpoint which components these might be and how they might differ depending on the behaviour to be modified.18 To address this gap, a crucial step is gaining insight into the necessary components of integrated motivational interviewing and cognitive behavioural therapy (MI-CBT) interventions.

One approach to achieve this is to examine which components are considered most important by experienced MI-CBT practitioners and researchers. Such consensus among experts can be ascertained using a modified Delphi study methodology, which involves an iterative approach to measure expert consensus based on a series of statements.19 Delphi studies have been used to identify what experts consider are the key components of CBT for people with psychosis20 and depression.21 This approach has not previously been used to identify the clinically important aspects of MI-CBT for lifestyle behaviour change.

Rationale

The research team conducted two systematic reviews to evaluate the effect of MI-CBT interventions for changes in PA and anthropometrics.9 10 This process identified that the MI-CBT interventions were poorly described, and lacked detail regarding the MI-CBT components and how they were delivered. Indeed, many of the included studies were delivered with no measurement of fidelity. A lack of fidelity adds uncertainty about the extent to which individuals were receiving MI-CBT techniques as intended. These results highlight the lack of consistency in this field and with MI-CBT interventions, as well as the need for improvement with a focused effort on collaboration to gain consensus on design and implementation of MI-CBT interventions.

The aim of this study, therefore, is to use a modified Delphi approach to establish an expert consensus on what constitutes the essential components of MI-CBT interventions for lifestyle behaviour change. The results of this study will help guide the design, training (of those delivering) and implementation of behaviour change interventions into the future.

Methods

Study design

The Delphi technique is an approach to establishing expert consensus through an iterative process of repeated surveys to converge individual opinion into a group consensus.19 Study participants will anonymously rate items in a survey and will be provided with feedback on the group response. Study participants will be asked to re-rate their initial responses in subsequent surveys, considering the group’s response to the preceding survey. This approach allows measurement of the range of opinions as well as the convergence on a consensus opinion among geographically dispersed participants. The independent, anonymously rating of items ensures that all opinions are equally weighted in Delphi studies.

In a modified Delphi approach, the first round uses closed rather than open questions; this method has been chosen for the present study because information on commonly used components of both MI and CBT is readily available.21,25 The MI-CBT components listed in this study were derived from the Revised Cognitive Therapy Scale23 and research that has identified content-based, relational and process techniques of MI and CBT delivered as single interventions.21 24 26 The definition of consensus and the choice to stop data collection after three rounds were defined a priori following the quality criteria proposed by Diamond and colleagues.19

Participants

A literature search will be carried out using the PubMed database to identify national and international academics with expertise in MI-CBT for lifestyle behaviour changes.27 Relevant search terms related to MI-CBT for lifestyle behaviour change will be identified from previous systematic reviews.9 10 A search will be carried out to identify books, book chapters, practice guidelines and grey literature on MI-CBT for lifestyle behaviour changes. A search will be undertaken examining keynote speakers from international conferences relating to behaviour change, MI and CBT. A list of potential participants will be provided by the MI-CBT experts among the study team (SB, PO’H, JB, SB and MK). From these searches, the research team will develop a comprehensive list with the names and contact information of potential participants.

Potential participants will include academics/researchers and published authors in the MI-CBT field, academics/researchers/healthcare professionals involved in the design and implementation of MI-CBT interventions and/or clinicians that provide MI-CBT interventions. Consistent with published Delphi studies, participants will need to demonstrate a minimum of 3 years of direct experience in MI-CBT to be eligible.28 There is a lack of standard guidelines and agreement in the literature as to the appropriate size of the expert panel for modified Delphi studies. The recommended minimum sample size is 20 participants.29 To account for attrition, which is common in Delphi studies due to their iterative nature, the aim is to recruit a minimum of 25 participants.

Recruitment

A registration survey will be developed and administered through REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture, Vanderbilt University). The registration survey will contain detailed information about the study. Sociodemographic data, including name, email address, country, job title, organisation and years of experience with MI-CBT, will be collected through the registration survey.

During targeted recruitment, the research team will use the comprehensive list of potential participants and send out invitations to participate via email. The email information will be obtained from public sources (eg, corresponding author details). The email will contain the link to the registration survey.

During open recruitment, the research team will advertise open invitations to potential experts via relevant social media platforms including X (formally Twitter) and LinkedIn. Consistent with snowball sampling, experts will be asked to disseminate the call to relevant contacts. Throughout the recruitment process, the acquisition of an international, multidisciplinary panel of experts will be prioritised. The research team will use a matrix to try to promote diversity among experts in terms of country, profession and expertise in MI-CBT. The registration survey will be closely monitored, and invitations will be sent out to experts from groups that are underrepresented.

Once the required number of participants have completed the registration survey, the consensus surveys will begin. Participants will receive an email 1 week prior to the launch of the first round to notify them and provide them with necessary information. Participants will receive an email with a link to the survey, which will be administered through REDCap. The study is expected to run from July 2024 through November 2024, with the final outputs delivered in mid-2025.

Consensus

While there is a lack of standard guidelines and agreement in the literature as to what constitutes consensus for Delphi studies, most Delphi studies define consensus as a certain percentage of participants being in agreement, with the level of agreement ranging from 51%–100%.30 Consensus will be defined a priori as ≥80% agreement for this study. This threshold was chosen as an appropriate cut-off given that most health science researchers who have conducted Delphi studies to establish agreed definitions have defined consensus as either ≥70%, ≥75% or ≥80% agreement with ≥80% being the most stringent level of agreement.19 29

Generation of the list of components

The MI-CBT experts within the research team will compile a ‘long list’ of MI-CBT components. The components will be grouped into three categories: (1) relational components, which includes listening styles and skills such as open questions, affirmation, reflections and summaries which will be presented as separate components; (2) content components, which are the technical components of MI-CBT interventions expected to facilitate behavioural change such as activity scheduling, identifying past successes, identifying and exploring unhelpful thinking styles; and (3) process components, which are procedures for the delivery of therapy such as who provides therapy, the number and frequency of sessions, support between sessions, and the mode of delivery.2123,26 There is no a priori limit on the number of components to be included in the long list.

Rounds

Modified Delphi studies can consist of any number of rounds, but they commonly involve 3–5 rounds.31 32 Trevelyan and colleagues put forward that three rounds are considered optimal in Delphi studies.32 There will be three rounds in this study.

Data collection and analysis

Round 1 survey

The survey will be used to collect data on the participant’s country of residence and their professional background including whether they were involved in MI-CBT teaching, supervision, research and practice. Due to differences in what behaviour changes interventions are designed to change, for example, uptake of PA or cessation of smoking behaviours, participants will be asked to answer the statements based on uptake or cessation interventions only. The a priori plan is to conduct analysis for both uptake and cessation combined, and for each individually.

In Round 1, participants will be asked to rate all the long list of MI-CBT components on a 5-point Likert type scale. Participants will be asked to respond to statements on each component, with following wording: ‘Please select whether you think the following components are either: 1. Necessary (absolute core features); 2. Desirable (helpful and effective); 3. Moderately desirable (somewhat helpful but not essential); 4. Undesirable (unlikely to be helpful and effective); or 5. Unnecessary (unnecessary)’.

Participants will be provided with the opportunity to offer a better description for each individual component if they think this is appropriate. Participants will be provided with open-ended options to suggest other components they might consider necessary or desirable for all three categories (relational, content, process). Descriptive statistics (proportions) will be used to describe participants’ responses to each statement within the surveys. Where free text responses are provided in the Round 1 survey, these will be read by the research team and discussed in relation to the overall responses to the survey statements. It is not necessary for multiple participants to make similar recommendations for a component to be modified or added, recommendations made by multiple participants may be weighted more heavily by the team when evaluating the recommendations made and finalising any changes to items in Round 2

Round 2

For the second round, participants will be provided with the responses to each statement from Round 1. No statement from Round 1 will be removed, regardless of level of agreement. As recommended, responses from Round 1 will be presented in a bar graph and include the median and IQR of participants’ responses.32 33 Where required, participants will be presented with the updated component statement and any added statements from the Round 1 analysis. Participants will rate their agreement with the conceptual and operational definitions of MI-CBT for lifestyle behaviour changes. Consistent with the first round, ratings will be given on a 5-point Likert scale. To encourage consensus, participants will be asked to consider the group responses before finalising their opinion to the statements.33

Descriptive statistics (proportions) will be used to describe participants’ responses to each statement within the surveys. Statements that reach the predetermined consensus of 80% as either necessary or desirable in analysis of Round 2 will be considered ‘consensus in’. Statements that reach the predetermined consensus of 80% as either unnecessary or undesirable in analysis of Round 2 will be considered ‘consensus out’; these statements will not go through to Round 3 to minimise participant burden.

Round 3

In Round 3, participants will be presented with results for statements from the Round 2 analysis. These will be presented in a bar graph and with the median and IQR provided. The presentation of results will include highlighting statements that were deemed ‘consensus in’ and not included in Round 3. Participants will rate their agreement with the conceptual and operational definitions of MI-CBT for lifestyle behaviour changes. Consistent with the first two rounds, ratings will be given on a 5-point Likert scale. To encourage consensus, participants will be asked to consider the group responses before finalising their opinion to the statements.

Descriptive statistics (proportions) will be used to describe participants’ demographic characteristics and responses to each statement within the surveys. Statements that reach the predetermined consensus of 80% as either necessary or desirable in analysis will be considered ‘consensus in’; statements that reach the predetermined of 80% as either unnecessary or undesirable in analysis of Round 2 will be considered ‘consensus out’.

Ethics

Ethical approval has been granted by the La Trobe University Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number HEC24066). Potential participants will be informed that responding to the questionnaires implies consent to participate in the initiative, and having their de-identified responses included in future iterations and any analyses. All anonymised data will be stored on a password-protected computer on a secure network and in a locked office, in accordance with local guidelines. These data will only be accessible to the research team.

Manuscript involvement

In recognition of participation and to promote future collaboration opportunities, all participants who complete the three rounds of this modified Delphi will be offered the opportunity to contribute to authorship of the final manuscript. As one of the strengths of the Delphi process is the anonymity of responses and that all opinions are equal, participants will not have access to the raw data. Contributions to the final manuscript will include background, methods and discussion sections. Authorship will be given following significant contribution to the manuscript, consistent with the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research guidelines for Authorship.34

Discussion

The modified Delphi study outlined in this protocol will be conducted to develop internationally accepted conceptual and operational definitions of MI-CBT for lifestyle modifications with an international, multidisciplinary panel of experts. The application of consensus methods is important where unanimity of opinion does not exist due to a lack of evidence on a specific topic.

Clinicians and academics designing and implementing MI-CBT interventions for lifestyle risk behaviours have limited guidance or evidence on which to base decisions. As an example, in MI, therapists use a communication style that is person-centred. This is one of the core characteristics that sets MI apart from CBT; this needs to be considered when integrating the methods. In the absence of evidence for integration, interventions are often implemented using a wide variety of treatment approaches and practices. Consensus on necessary MI-CBT interventions might assist in planning for, and assessment of fidelity. Fidelity provides important information regarding why an intervention may or may not have achieved its intended outcome.35 Participants who have received MI-CBT interventions with measured fidelity11 describe the value they derived from relational, content and process of the intervention.36

There are several strengths associated with the modified Delphi technique, namely: (1) participant anonymity and equal weight of all opinions, (2) multiple rounds of questioning and (3) provision of feedback between each round.37 These characteristics can help reduce bias in the process of gaining consensus.37 The online format is well-suited to gathering the opinions of experts who are geographically dispersed, which is particularly relevant in acquiring an international panel of experts to develop internationally accepted definitions

There are also some limitations of the Delphi technique. The most significant limitation is that even though consensus is achieved on some or all opinions, this approach does not guarantee all statements that have reached consensus are correct; consensus relates to level of agreement and does not necessarily imply truth per se. Therefore, this work may be best viewed as a well-structured sampling of expert opinions on best practice at a point in time. Nevertheless, this convergence of expert opinion can help improve behaviour change science going forward. Another important limitation is the risk of panel attrition.29 37 This issue will be mitigated by determining the size of the expert panel, and the inclusion of retention strategies (eg, sending out reminder emails to non-responders and providing quick turnaround times between rounds of questioning).

Practical implications

Completing a consensus statement on the effective components of MI-CBT for lifestyle behaviour change has practical implications for clinical practice and research. For practitioners, it will provide standardised guidance on which components are most effective, enabling more consistent and evidence-based MI-CBT interventions. This standardisation can improve training programmes and lead to more targeted approaches, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes by focusing on strategies that have the greatest impact. Moreover, standardisation through consensus can permit incorporating fidelity measures to ensure that these interventions are delivered as intended, maintaining the integrity and quality of the intervention and allowing for consistent monitoring and improvement.25 In research, the consensus statement provides a foundation for future studies, helping to design new studies, develop assessment tools and innovate interventions based on the identified effective components, with fidelity measures further supporting the evaluation of intervention delivery.

Dissemination plan

The expected guidelines that emerge from this modified Delphi study will be published in a peer-reviewed journal. The final statement will be disseminated to regional, national and international audiences through presentations at regional, national and international conferences. Dissemination of the consensus will also occur through national and international networks, facilitated by members of the research team and participants. Finally, we will look to disseminate the findings to clinicians through clinical networks; three of the study team are embedded health service clinicians, which will support dissemination.

Conclusion

It is anticipated that this research will have extensive and enduring impacts in the field of behaviour change science. The internationally accepted conceptual and operational definitions of MI-CBT will highlight to clinicians, policy makers and academics the necessary and desirable components that need to be included in interventions labelled as MI-CBT. This should help promote transparency in intervention reporting and comparability across effectiveness studies for MI-CBT intervention studies and help inform research, policy and practice.
