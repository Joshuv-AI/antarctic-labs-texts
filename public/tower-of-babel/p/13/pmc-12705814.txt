Impact of adjuvant endocrine therapy utilization in male patients with early breast cancer: results from a large German real-world claims data analysis
Authors: Dominik Dannehl, Tjeerd Maarten Hein Dijkstra, Alexandra von Au, Anna Sophie Scholz, Léa Louise Volmer, Markus Hahn, Sabine Hawighorst-Knapstein, Ariane Chaudhuri, Florin-Andrei Taran, Markus Wallwiener, Armin Bauer, Diethelm Wallwiener, Sara Yvonne Brucker, Stephanie Wallwiener, Andreas Hartkopf, Tobias Engler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMale breast cancer (BC) is a rare entity and represents a significant clinical challenge due to its late diagnosis and resulting poorer prognosis compared to female BC. As shown by prior studies, approximately 99% of male BC patients are hormone receptor-positive (HR +). This study aimed to evaluate the utilization and impact of adjuvant endocrine therapy (ET) in male early BC patients using a large German real-world claims dataset.Patients and methodsData were collected from a major German statutory health insurance provider (AOK Baden-Wuerttemberg) covering the period between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2020. Male patients diagnosed with early BC who underwent breast surgery were included. ET use was determined by prescription records. The impact of ET on overall survival (OS) was assessed using univariable (log-rank) and multivariable (cox regression) analysis.ResultsAmong 128 male early BC patients, 16% (n = 21) did not utilize adjuvant ET. On univariable analysis, ET was significantly associated with 5-year OS (74% with ET vs. 37% without ET; p < 0.0001). Multivariable analysis revealed ET use (Hazard Ratio [HR]: 0.31, 95% CI 0.14–0.69, p = 0.004) having a positive effect on OS, whereas age (HR: 1.04, 95% CI 1.00–1.09) and comorbidities (HR: 1.18, 95% CI 1.05–1.34, p = 0.007) were identified as negative predictive factors for OS.ConclusionA significant proportion of male patients with early BC does not use ET. However, the use of ET plays a key role in improving survival outcomes for male patients with early BC. Efforts to improve the use of ET, including patient education and management of side effects, are essential to optimize treatment outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00404-025-08198-x.

Introduction

Male breast cancer (BC) is a rare disease, accounting for approximately 1% of all breast cancer cases [1, 2]. Unlike its female counterpart, male BC is almost exclusively hormone receptor-positive (HR +), with up to 99% of tumors expressing hormone receptors [1, 3, 4]. In addition, male BC is associated with a higher prevalence of BRCA2 germline mutations and expression of the androgen receptor, further distinguishing its biology from breast cancer in women [1, 5]. Despite these differences, diagnostic approaches and treatment strategies for male BC are typically extrapolated from female data due to the limited research conducted specifically on men [2]. However, compared to their female counterparts, male breast cancer patients had a higher mortality rate after cancer diagnosis [6].

Treatment for male BC often involves a combination of surgical tumor excision, radiation therapy, chemotherapy when appropriate, and adjuvant endocrine therapy (ET) [7]. Standard endocrine treatment includes tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors (AIs: letrozole, anastrozole and exemestane), and gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogs [7–11]. However, ET is often accompanied by a range of side effects, including fatigue, joint pain, hot flashes, decreased libido, and mood fluctuations [12], which can negatively impact adherence to therapy [13]. Previous studies have demonstrated a decline in adherence to ET over time, comparable to patterns observed in female patients [14, 15].

The survivorship experience for men with BC encompasses not only the physical side effects of endocrine therapies but also unique psychosocial challenges associated with this rare diagnosis [2]. Importantly, failure to appropriate medication use has been linked to poorer clinical outcomes and higher healthcare expenditures due to increased diagnostics, unnecessary therapy escalations, and reliance on more expensive treatments [16].

Despite the known importance of ET in male BC, only few studies have examined its real-world utilization or the impact of non-use on overall survival (OS) [17, 18]. This study seeks to enhance the growing body of knowledge by characterizing the proportion of male BC patients who utilize adjuvant ET in a real-world setting and assessing the impact of ET non-use on overall survival (OS).

Materials and methods

This retrospective study claims data analysis was conducted using data from AOK Baden-Wuerttemberg, one of Germany's largest statutory health insurance providers. The study adhered to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tuebingen University (380/2020BO). Data reporting follows the ESMO-GROW guideline [19].

The dataset used in this study has been described in detail previously [20–23]. In summary, AOK Baden-Wuerttemberg provided a dataset comprising 1,622 male patients with a documented breast cancer diagnosis (ICD-10 code C50) between 2010 and 2020, along with 1,592 age-matched control patients without a breast cancer diagnosis (ICD-10 code C50). The same exclusion criteria outlined earlier were applied to select for non-metastatic breast cancer patients who underwent breast cancer surgery [20]. In addition, patients were matched as previously described, but in this study, a 1:3 matching ratio was used [20]. Follow-up data for all patients were available up to December 31, 2020.

The use of adjuvant ET was determined by analyzing prescription records with Anatomic Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes [23]. These included codes starting with L02BA (anti-estrogens such as tamoxifen or fulvestrant), L02BG (aromatase inhibitors such as letrozole or anastrozole), and L02AE (gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogs [GnRH analogs] such as goserelin and leuprorelin). Each prescription record included the ATC code, fill date, package size, and the prescribing physician’s specialty. The dataset does not provide information on tumor biology, including the expression of hormonal receptors. However, given that 99% of tumors in male BC are HR-positive, we assumed 100% of the patients to be HR-positive and defined all patients without a recorded filled prescription for adjuvant ET as non-users of adjuvant ET [3].

Data processing and statistical analysis were performed using R version 4.3.3 and RStudio (version 2024.04.0 + 735, Posit PBC, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) with the tidyverse packages dplyr 1.1.4, readr 2.1.5, forcats 1.0.0, stringr 1.5.1, ggplot2 3.5.1, tibble 3.2.1, lubridate 1.9.3, tidyr 1.3.1, and purrr 1.0.2. For generating tables, we used packages janitor 2.2.0, gt 0.11.0, and gtsummary 2.0.0. To calculate comorbidities, the R extension package comorbidity 1.1.0 was used. In detail, we used function comorbidity() with parameters map = "charlson_icd10_quan" and assign0 = TRUE followed by function score() with parameters weights = "quan" and assign0 = TRUE. Kaplan–Meier methodology was performed using packages survival 3.7–0 and ggsurvfit 1.1.0 to estimate OS together with the associated 95% confidence intervals (CI). Cox proportional hazards model was used to compute hazard ratios (HR). All p values were nominal and significance level was alpha = 0.05.

Results

In total, 128 male BC patients and 384 control patients that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were included into this analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Of the 128 male BC patients, 107 used adjuvant ET (84%) and 21 patients did not (16%) (Table 1). Mean age at the time of the breast cancer diagnosis was 68.7 (± 11.7) years for patients using ET and 75.6 (± 14.8) years for patients not using ET, which was not statistically significant (p = 0.055). Regarding age at breast cancer diagnosis, patients aged ≥ 80 years constituted 48% (10/21) of the non-ET group, compared to 15% (16/107) in the ET group. Patients in their 70s accounted for 19% (4/21) in the non-ET group and 38% (41/107) in the ET group. Those in their 60s made up 14% (3/21) and 25% (27/107), respectively, while patients under 60 years represented 19% (4/21) in the non-ET group and 21% (23/107) in the ET group (p = 0.014). Place of residence showed no significant differences between groups, with rural areas comprising 38% (8/21) of the non-ET group and 25% (27/107) of the ET group, suburban areas comprising 29% (6/21) and 34% (36/107), and urban areas comprising 33% (7/21) and 41% (44/107) (p = 0.5). Axillary lymph node involvement was observed in 14% (3/21) of patients without ET use and 33% (35/107) of those with ET use (p = 0.091). Among patients who did not use adjuvant ET, none (0/21) received HER2-targeted therapy compared to 5% (5/107) in the adjuvant ET group (p = 0.6). Radiation therapy was utilized in 10% (2/21) of patients without ET use compared to 47% (50/107) of patients with ET use (p = 0.002). Chemotherapy was administered to 5% (1/21) of patients in the non-ET group and 39% (42/107) of those in the ET group (p = 0.002). After a median follow-up of 3.7 years, a total of 42 deaths were observed in the breast cancer patients and a total of 108 deaths in the control patients. Patients who did not utilize adjuvant ET demonstrated significantly worse overall survival (OS) compared to those who received ET (HR 0.31, 95% CI 0.14–0.69, p = 0.004, Cox regression), with 5-year OS rates of 37% for non-ET users versus 79% for ET users (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 3). In addition, patients without ET use had significantly lower 5-year OS rates compared to their age-matched control group (37% vs. 58%, p = 0.008, log-rank test). Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the critical role of adjuvant ET in improving OS for male BC patients. Male patients who did not initiate adjuvant ET exhibited significantly worse OS compared to those who began ET. Furthermore, patients using adjuvant ET demonstrated OS comparable to their age-matched control group, highlighting the therapy’s efficacy in reducing mortality risk.

Unfortunately, German claims data do not include information on estrogen, progesterone, or HER2 receptor status. However, a recently conducted study using data from the cancer registry of the state of Baden-Wuerttemberg in Germany (2015–2021) demonstrated that 99% of all male patients with early breast cancer exhibit a positive hormone receptor status and are, thus, potential candidates for adjuvant ET [3]. Given the substantial overlap between the patient populations (all individuals in the claims dataset are also included in the Baden-Wuerttemberg cancer registry, although not all residents of Baden-Wuerttemberg are insured by AOK), we assume only a minimal bias due to potential differences in tumor biology. In addition, further publications have reported that between 95 and 97% of male breast cancer patients are hormone receptor-positive [18, 24, 25].

Male BC patients who did not use adjuvant ET were generally older and less likely to undergo radiation therapy or chemotherapy. However, factors such as age, axillary lymph node involvement, use of chemotherapy, and comorbidities did not significantly influence the likelihood of initiating adjuvant ET (Supplementary Table 5). The only notable predictor for initiating ET was undergoing radiation therapy. An explanation to this finding might be that patients who initiated radiation therapy were more likely to have received breast cancer treatment according to current guidelines, since previous studies report that men as compared to women are less likely to receive guideline conform breast cancer treatment [26, 27]. This finding highlights the importance of identifying and addressing barriers to guideline compliant treatment including ET use among specific patient populations, particularly older individuals who may encounter unique challenges or harbor misconceptions about treatment. These challenges might often be related to sexual and hormonal side effects, as well as the psychosocial impacts of the disease [2, 28, 29].

In addition to the positive effect of adjuvant ET on OS, higher age and a greater number of comorbidities were significantly associated with reduced OS. Although patients who did not receive adjuvant ET exhibited a higher overall number of comorbidities, no specific condition was exclusively associated with the absence of ET use compared to those who initiated ET (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 6). However, claims data only capture the presence of additional diseases beyond breast cancer and do not reflect their severity. Therefore, based on these data, it cannot be excluded that patients in the non-ET group were more severely affected by comorbid conditions than those in the ET group. Nonetheless, patients in the non-ET group showed a substantially higher risk of death compared to their age-matched controls, suggesting an impact of ET use on breast cancer-specific mortality. Interestingly, tamoxifen, prescribed in 90% of cases, has been suggested to exhibit cardioprotective effects, potentially mitigating some risks associated with comorbidities [30]. Conversely, aromatase inhibitors, though used by a minority of patients, have been linked to long-term cardiac toxicity in prior studies [30]. Notably, only one patient was prescribed concurrent gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogs, despite achieving higher treatment effectiveness when used in combination with tamoxifen and AIs [8, 31].

A significant observation is the distinct role of general practitioners (GPs) in managing male BC patients. GPs issued 59% of adjuvant ET prescriptions, compared to 29% by gynecologists and 10% by oncologists (Supplementary Table 4). This differs from female BC patients, for whom gynecologists are primarily responsible for prescribing ET in Germany (32). This remarkable observation highlights the critical need for targeted educational initiatives aimed at GPs to enhance the quality of care for male BC patients. Strengthening interdisciplinary collaboration between breast centers, office-based gynecologists, and GPs is essential to ensure a more integrated and continuous management approach throughout the course of treatment. Breast centers, as specialized institutions, can play a central role in providing structured information, training opportunities, and clear treatment pathways, facilitating seamless transitions between inpatient and outpatient care. Office-based gynecologists, who often serve as key coordinators of oncological care, should be actively involved in follow-up management and serve as intermediaries between the hospital-based specialists and GPs. Concurrently, GPs, who maintain long-term relationships with patients and often manage comorbid conditions, should receive focused training particularly in the recognition, management, and mitigation of side effects related to adjuvant ET. This would not only enable them to provide timely interventions but also to counsel patients effectively, thereby reducing treatment discontinuations. By intensifying communication and cooperation across these specialties and offering standardized, easily accessible educational resources, it is possible to significantly improve therapy adherence. Furthermore, the implementation of shared care models, regular interdisciplinary case discussions, and structured survivorship care plans could further contribute to maintaining high adherence rates, ultimately leading to improved outcomes for male BC patients.

This study has several limitations. The small number of male BC patients, the low event rates and the relatively short median follow-up of 3.7 years limited the ability to accurately calculate adherence metrics, the duration of adjuvant ET use, and distant relapse-free survival. Moreover, breast cancer-specific survival could not be directly assessed, as this information is not captured in the analyzed claims dataset. Comparisons between male BC patients and their respective age-matched control group allow only for the estimation of assumptions and trends regarding breast cancer-specific survival, but not for direct calculation. In addition, comorbidities beyond BC are reported solely as counts, without information on disease severity. Consequently, the association between the absence of adjuvant ET use and reduced OS could not be further elucidated. It remains unclear whether patients with limited prognosis are less likely to initiate adjuvant ET, or whether the omission of adjuvant ET itself contributes to poorer prognosis. A direct impact of adjuvant ET on OS after a median follow-up of 3.7 years should indeed be discussed. However, multivariable Cox regression analysis revealed that comorbidities had no significant impact on adjuvant ET use (Supplementary Table 5), while ET use consistently emerged as an independent factor positively influencing OS. Furthermore, although patients who did not receive ET were generally older than those who did, their OS was worse compared to age-matched controls. As a secondary claims data analysis, the study was also limited by the lack of subjective patient insights regarding reasons for not starting ET. Finally, it is not possible to exclude the potential scenario in which prescriptions were filled but the medication was not actually consumed by patients. Therefore, future studies with larger patient cohorts and longer follow-up periods should incorporate adherence measures to directly correlate them with outcome parameters such as overall survival, breast cancer-specific survival, and distant disease-free survival, to further elucidate this important issue.

In conclusion, 16% of male BC patients did not utilize adjuvant ET despite its demonstrated benefits for OS. Efforts to improve ET use must include identifying and addressing the specific needs of this patient population. As general practitioners play a pivotal role in the treatment of male BC, it is of high importance to target educational initiatives and interdisciplinary collaborations between inpatient and outpatient providers. Moreover, integrating cost bearers into clinical program development is essential to sustainably enhance ET use and overall care quality in this unique patient population. Future real-world studies should aim to further characterize these patients and explore tailored interventions to increase ET utilization.
