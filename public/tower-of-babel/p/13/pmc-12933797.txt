Generative artificial intelligence in dementia care: a scoping review protocol on nursing roles, experiences and ethical perspectives
Authors: Yong-Chuan Chen, Hsiao-Chi Nieh, Hsiao-Chu Chen, Jung-Chih Chen, Mei-Yu Chang, Pi-O Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionGenerative artificial intelligence (GAI), including large language models and multimodal generative systems, is rapidly emerging in healthcare with growing interest in its potential applications for dementia care. These technologies offer new possibilities for communication support, cognitive engagement and personalised interaction, yet they also introduce complex ethical, relational and practical challenges. Nurses—who hold central, sustained roles across dementia care settings—are key mediators of technology adoption and are positioned to assess the appropriateness, safety and ethical implications of GAI use. However, existing literature remains fragmented and largely focused on technological development or patient-facing outcomes, with limited synthesis of nurses’ roles, experiences and ethical perspectives. This scoping review aims to map the current evidence regarding how nurses engage with GAI in dementia care and to identify gaps that may inform practice, policy and future research.Methods and analysisThis review will follow the methodological framework of Arksey and O’Malley, refined by Levac et al and guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis. Reporting will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews checklist. The review will be conducted between March 2026 and October 2026, encompassing database searching, screening, data charting, synthesis and reporting. A comprehensive search will be conducted across MEDLINE (Ovid), CINAHL, PsycINFO, Scopus and Web of Science using controlled vocabulary and keywords related to nursing, generative artificial intelligence and dementia. Eligible sources will include empirical studies, reviews, conceptual papers and policy analyses that report nursing roles, experiences or ethical considerations related to GAI in dementia care. Two reviewers will independently screen titles/abstracts and full texts and extract data using a structured charting form. Findings will be synthesised through descriptive statistics and inductive thematic analysis, supported by conceptual mapping to illustrate relationships among GAI types, nursing roles, ethical concerns and care settings. Critical appraisal will not be undertaken, as it is optional in scoping reviews and is not aligned with the primary mapping objectives of this review.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required as the review synthesises publicly available literature. Should the optional interest-holders consultation be undertaken, ethical clearance will be obtained from an appropriate institutional review board prior to participant engagement. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication, conference presentations and knowledge-translation outputs targeted at clinicians, educators, policymakers and AI developers. The review will support informed, ethically grounded integration of GAI in dementia care.Keywords: Nursing research, Dementia, Artificial Intelligence

Introduction

Dementia is a progressive neurocognitive syndrome that affects more than 55 million people worldwide, with prevalence projected to nearly triple by 2050.1,3 Beyond impairments in memory, communication and executive function, dementia presents complex psychosocial and ethical challenges that require sustained, person-centred and relationally grounded care.4 5 Nurses play a critical and multifaceted role in this context. Across hospital settings, long-term care facilities and home-based or community care environments, nurses function not only as professional caregivers but also as coordinators of the nursing and caregiving process, interpreters of behavioural change, therapeutic communicators and ethical stewards in decision-making processes.5 6 Their continual presence positions them as key actors in shaping dementia care practices and mediating emerging care technologies.

Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence (GAI)—including large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT and other foundation models—are transforming possibilities for communication, cognitive support, simulation and assistive technologies in healthcare.7 8 Unlike earlier rule-based or deterministic AI systems, GAI can produce novel and contextually adaptive outputs, enabling more naturalistic conversation, personalised content generation and dynamic emotional expression.8,10 Although applications specifically targeting dementia care remain emergent, these capabilities underpin tools such as conversational agents, voice-based assistants and digital companions that could, in principle, support cognitive stimulation, orientation, communication and social connection for people living with dementia.3 7

While these technologies offer promising opportunities to enhance communication, alleviate caregiver burden and support non-pharmacological interventions, their use with people living with dementia also raises significant concerns. The relational vulnerability of cognitively impaired individuals heightens ethical risks such as digital deception, simulated empathy, anthropomorphism, unclear consent and potential emotional manipulation.11 12 Broader issues—including algorithmic bias, privacy, data governance and dependence on automated systems—further complicate the responsible introduction of GAI into dementia care settings.8 9 13

Within this context, the role of nurses is particularly pivotal. Nurses frequently serve as frontline implementers, assessors and mediators of new technologies, making real-time judgments about appropriateness, safety, relational impact and alignment with person-centred care principles.6 7 They are responsible for integrating technology into care routines, communicating with families, navigating emotional responses to AI tools and addressing practical challenges such as training, workload and system compatibility. Their experiential insights—grounded in clinical expertise, relational knowledge and ethical responsibilities—are essential for determining whether GAI tools support or undermine quality dementia care.

Despite this, the emerging literature on GAI in dementia care remains predominantly technology-driven or patient-focused, with limited exploration of nursing perspectives. Ethical and policy analyses of AI often remain conceptual and do not systematically incorporate the experiential knowledge of frontline nursing practice.11,13 Without synthesising how nurses experience, evaluate and negotiate GAI in dementia care, technological development risks becoming disconnected from clinical realities, relational considerations and the ethical standards that underpin nursing practice. Given the rapid evolution of GAI systems and their increasing integration into healthcare, a clear mapping of existing knowledge is needed to inform education, implementation strategies, workforce development and policy.7,9

A scoping review is therefore methodologically appropriate. The literature on GAI in dementia care is diverse, emergent and interdisciplinary, spanning nursing, gerontology, informatics, ethics and technology studies. Evidence ranges from conceptual analyses and policy discussions to early empirical studies and practice commentaries. A scoping review enables comprehensive mapping of this heterogeneous field, clarification of conceptual boundaries and synthesis of reported roles, experiences and ethical considerations. This approach aligns with established guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework for examining complex, underexplored or rapidly developing topics.14,16

Accordingly, this scoping review aims to systematically map what is known about the roles, experiences and ethical perspectives of nurses regarding the use of GAI in dementia care. Specifically, it will (1): identify GAI technologies that have been applied or proposed in dementia care involving nurses (2); examine nursing roles in adopting, mediating, delivering or evaluating GAI tools (3); synthesise reported benefits, challenges and experiential insights and (4) explore ethical and relational concerns raised within the nursing literature or practice contexts. Through this synthesis, the review will highlight gaps, tensions and emerging priorities to inform future research, workforce training, ethical guidelines and policy development for the responsible integration of GAI in dementia care.

Eligibility criteria

Types of sources

This scoping review will include peer-reviewed empirical studies, including qualitative, quantitative research and mixed-methods studies. In addition, peer-reviewed reviews, conceptual or theoretical papers, and policy analyses that address nursing roles, experiences or ethical considerations related to GAI in dementia care will be included. Conference abstracts will be considered only when sufficient methodological or conceptual detail is provided. Editorials, news media, dissertations and non-peer-reviewed opinion pieces will be excluded, except for Letters to the Editor that directly respond to an included empirical study and contain substantive analytical or ethical content.

Participants

Studies will be eligible if they include nurses in any role relevant to dementia care, including registered nurses, nurse practitioners, licensed practical/vocational nurses, specialist dementia nurses, gerontological nurses and nursing students when they are engaged in dementia-related care activities. Studies focusing solely on people living with dementia, family caregivers or other healthcare professionals will be included only if the experiences, perceptions or roles of nurses are explicitly reported. Studies with no reference to nursing involvement will be excluded.

Phenomenon of interest

The primary phenomenon of interest is the use, adoption, perception, experience or ethical evaluation of GAI in the context of dementia care. GAI is defined as AI systems capable of producing novel outputs (eg, text, speech, images, avatars, conversation) such as LLMs, multimodal generative models, synthetic voice systems, conversational agents, digital companions or adaptive cognitive-support tools. Studies examining non-generative or rule-based technologies, general digital health tools, electronic health records or non-AI assistive technologies will be excluded unless they explicitly incorporate a generative component.

Context

Eligible studies must focus on dementia care delivered in hospital settings, long-term care (including both institutional facilities and home-based or community care environments), or other residential care contexts. Studies conducted in laboratory or simulation environments will be included if they explore technology intended for eventual application to dementia care. Studies addressing general ageing, cognitive decline or neurodegenerative conditions will be included only if they explicitly analyse implications for dementia care. Studies conducted outside a dementia-relevant context will be excluded.

Outcomes and concepts

This review will include studies that report at least one of the following (1): nursing roles in implementing, mediating or evaluating GAI (2); nurses’ experiences, attitudes or competencies related to GAI (3); perceived or observed benefits, risks or challenges associated with GAI or (4) ethical, relational or practice-based considerations raised by nurses in relation to GAI in dementia care. Studies without any nursing-relevant outcomes will be excluded.

Language and time frame

Studies published in English with no restriction on publication year will be included to capture the full evolution of GAI technologies. Articles published in languages other than English will be excluded due to resource and quality-assurance constraints, despite increasing availability of machine-assisted translation tools.

Study exclusion criteria

Studies will be excluded if they (1): do not address GAI (2); do not involve nurses in any capacity (3); focus exclusively on computational performance, model optimisation or technical development without implications for care practice (4); examine general AI ethics without reference to dementia or nursing or (5) lack sufficient methodological or conceptual detail to contribute meaningfully to the review objectives.

Methods and analysis

The planned scoping review will be conducted between March 2026 and October 2026, following protocol acceptance. This scoping review will follow the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley, enhanced by the refinements of Levac et al, and guided by the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis.14 15 17 Reporting will adhere to the PRISMA-ScR checklist16; accordingly, the TARCiS (Terminology, Application and Reporting of Citation Searching) reporting framework is not applicable. Consistent with these guidelines, the review will follow six iterative stages (1): identifying the research questions (2); identifying relevant studies (3); selecting studies (4); charting the data (5); collating, summarising and reporting results and (6) optional consultation. The protocol will be registered with PROSPERO prior to commencing full screening to enhance transparency and avoid duplication.

The research questions were developed iteratively through preliminary literature scanning, clinical relevance assessment and consultation with experts in dementia care, nursing and AI ethics. A scoping review was chosen due to the novelty, interdisciplinarity and conceptual heterogeneity of GAI research in dementia care.

Discrepancies at any stage will be resolved through discussion, and if necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer. A PRISMA-ScR flow diagram will be used to document the number of records identified, screened, included and excluded, along with reasons for exclusion at the full-text level.

In addition, backward and forward citation tracking will be conducted for all included studies to identify potentially relevant literature not captured through database searches.

Stage 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results

The synthesis of results will combine quantitative descriptive and qualitative thematic approaches to provide a comprehensive understanding of how GAI is situated within the context of nursing in dementia care. This stage will not only summarise the existing evidence base but also identify patterns, conceptual relationships and areas of knowledge concentration or paucity.

The process will begin with a descriptive numerical summary to quantify the distribution of included studies across key dimensions, including publication year, country of origin, study design, type of GAI technology and care setting. Frequencies will also be reported for the nursing roles described (eg, user, evaluator, co-developer) and the functions of GAI applications (eg, communication aid, memory prompt, cognitive engagement tool).

Next, a thematic synthesis will be undertaken using an inductive coding approach informed by the framework of Thomas and Harden.18 Key thematic domains are anticipated to include (1): the nature and depth of nursing engagement with GAI (2); practical and relational challenges in integration (3); ethical and professional considerations, such as consent, authenticity and algorithmic transparency and (4) contextual enablers or barriers affecting feasibility and acceptability across settings.

To ensure nuanced insights, thematic coding will be stratified by both technology type and care context. Findings will be organised according to GAI technology category (eg, conversational agents, multimodal avatars, generative image tools, adaptive cognitive stimulation systems) and primary application (eg, communication support, memory prompts, emotional engagement, simulation training). Ethical considerations—such as informed consent, authenticity of interaction, algorithmic bias, emotional manipulation and data privacy—will be examined both as stand-alone themes and in relation to specific technologies and care environments (eg, hospital/acute care, long-term care and home-based or community care). This dual-level coding will facilitate comparative analysis of how ethical and relational issues are experienced and addressed in diverse technological and contextual conditions, generating practice-relevant and policy-relevant conclusions.

Where appropriate, conceptual mapping techniques—such as evidence matrices or visual frameworks—will be used to illustrate relationships between GAI types, nursing roles, ethical concerns and care contexts. These visual tools will help to identify thematic intersections and gaps, supporting the formulation of actionable recommendations.

In accordance with scoping review methodology as outlined by Peters et al,17 no critical appraisal of included studies will be undertaken. The synthesis will focus on producing a narrative and conceptual overview that reflects the diversity and complexity of the literature, while remaining grounded in the values, judgements and responsibilities that underpin nursing practice in dementia care.

Stage 6: consultation (optional but recommended)

An optional consultation phase may be undertaken to enhance the interpretive validity and practical relevance of findings, in line with Levac et al.15

Interest holders may include dementia care nurses, nurse educators, informatics specialists and AI ethics and policy experts. Engagement methods may include online surveys, focus groups or semistructured interviews. Ethics approval will be sought if required. The consultation will aim to (1) validate thematic findings, (2) identify practice-based insights absent in the literature, (3) explore acceptability and feasibility of GAI in clinical settings and (4) refine recommendations for practice, education and policy. Consultation findings will be transparently reported, including how feedback informed the final synthesis.

Ethics and dissemination

If the optional interest-holders consultation phase is undertaken, all consultation activities will adhere to established ethical standards for research involving human participants, including informed consent, confidentiality, data protection and voluntary participation.

The findings of this scoping review will be disseminated through multiple channels to maximise accessibility and impact across academic, clinical and policy contexts. The primary dissemination route will be a manuscript submitted to a peer-reviewed open-access journal (BMJ Open). Results will also be shared through presentations at conferences related to nursing, dementia care and digital health, as well as through professional networks and interest-holders.

To support knowledge translation, additional materials such as policy briefs, infographics and educational presentations may be developed to facilitate the application of findings in clinical practice, education and policy development. These outputs will be tailored to key user groups—including nurse educators, frontline clinicians, health system leaders, AI developers and policymakers—to inform ethically grounded, practice-informed and person-centred integration of GAI technologies in dementia care. The synthesised evidence is expected to guide future research directions, support implementation and workforce training, and contribute to broader discourse on the responsible use of AI in healthcare.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or initial development of this scoping review protocol, as the study focuses on the synthesis of existing literature and does not involve direct recruitment or data collection from human participants. However, if the optional consultation phase is implemented, people with lived experience—including family caregivers of individuals living with dementia and frontline dementia care nurses—may be invited to provide feedback on the preliminary findings. Their perspectives would help assess the practical relevance, clarity and applicability of the synthesised evidence. Any future involvement of patients or the public will be conducted in alignment with ethical guidance and will be transparently reported in the final review manuscript.
