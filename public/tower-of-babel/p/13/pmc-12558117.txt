Tracking changes in anxiety, depression, and stress among Ukrainian university students: a three-wave study from the COVID-19 pandemic to two years of ongoing full-scale war
Authors: Lyudmyla Kolisnyk, Mimi Yung Mehlsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Since 2020, modern youth in Ukraine have faced an exceptionally challenging period, first enduring the burdens of the COVID-19 pandemic and then being thrust into the full-scale war, characterized by persistent traumatic exposure. Given the profound and lasting psychological consequences of such crises, it is essential to monitor the long-term impact of war on youth mental health. However, despite the urgency of this issue, longitudinal studies with large, diverse samples remain scarce.Objective: This study aims to track changes in anxiety, depression, and stress and explore their associations with demographic and educational variables among university students in Ukraine, from the COVID-19 pandemic through two years of full-scale war.Method: Data were collected online at three time points over four years from independent samples of students from 27 higher education institutions across 12 Ukrainian cities. A total of 757 students participated in 2020, 2,592 in 2023, and 838 in 2024, all completing anonymous questionnaires. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and a General Linear Model were used to analyse the results.Results: The findings revealed a significant increase in anxiety, depression, and stress levels among university students during the transition from the pandemic to wartime conditions. Women reported higher levels of anxiety, depression, and stress, while students aged 15–25 exhibited poorer mental health than those aged 26–55. Although students across all fields of study experienced heightened distress in 2023, those in technical disciplines were the only group to show a decline in 2024.Conclusions: Ukrainian youth have been living under high levels of distress for more than four years, with little indication of improvement. The results highlight the urgent need for targeted strategies in prevention, intervention, and treatment of anxiety, depression, and stress among university students and other vulnerable young adults.

Introduction

The World Mental Health Organization’s Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2030 emphasizes the explicit inclusion of youth mental health within general and priority health policies (WHO, 2022). Even in contexts characterized by social, political and economic stability, the WHO identified adolescents and young adults as high-risk groups for mental disorders, including depression, anxiety, and behavioural conditions. According to the American College Health Association and numerous studies, anxiety and depression are among the most commonly reported mental health issues in students and significantly affect their academic performance (Liyanage et al., 2021).

The recent COVID-19 pandemic intensified the mental health challenges faced by youth, contributing to a further deterioration in their psychological well-being. Numerous studies show that the pandemic had a profound negative effect on children, adolescents and young adults (Balakrishnan et al., 2023; Ravens-Sieberer et al., 2022; Sakai et al., 2022; Thorisdottir et al., 2023). Many students experienced a switch to online learning, closed campuses and libraries, disrupted social environments, altered communication channels for teachers and administrative support, no meetings with friends, university colleagues, or relatives, no parties, no travelling, new assessment methods, different workloads, and performance levels, being trapped abroad (Elmer et al., 2020; Hamaideh et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2020; Pan, 2020). These factors made higher education students particularly prone to mental health disorders during this pandemic, being the high-risk age group for developing mental illness (Bogardus et al., 2022; Gonzalez et al., 2020; Solmi et al., 2021; Webber et al., 2023). For example, Balakrishnan et al. found that the prevalence of stress, anxiety, and depression among students doubled in 2020 (2023), and according to the findings of Hamaideh et al., the majority of university students experienced moderate levels of depression, anxiety, and stress during the COVID-19 pandemic (Hamaideh et al., 2022).

Unlike in many countries where life gradually returned to normal life after the pandemic, young people in Ukraine were confronted with a new and even more traumatic challenge – Russia's war of aggression against their country. The ongoing war has had, and continues to have, an unprecedented and profoundly negative impact on the mental health of the population, particularly among young people. In addition to the social isolation and health-related anxiety brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic – factors that persist during wartime – Ukrainian adolescents are now exposed to a wide range of war-related stressors. These include armed attacks, military operations, atrocities, organized violence (including physical and sexual violence), severe human suffering, displacement, the breakdown of social networks, and the graphic portrayal of war in the media. Such events occur during critical stages of physical, emotional, social, and cognitive development, posing serious risks to the psychological and physical well-being of young people (Osokina et al., 2023). The impact may vary depending on geographical location. For instance, Osokina et al. (2016–2017) compared the mental health of adolescents from a war-affected region of Ukraine with those from a more stable central region and found significantly higher rates of PTSD, severe anxiety, and moderately severe depression among adolescents living in the conflict zone (Osokina et al., 2023).

After more than two years of full-scale war, young people all over Ukraine have been affected by secondary stressors, such as family separation and loss, fear for personal and family safety, death of peers, property damage, poverty, and school closures, all of which could have had an additive effect. Disruptions to the usual life course, bereavement, distress from shelling, challenging economic conditions, and the necessity of adhering almost entirely to online education for nearly five years have contributed to increased anxiety disorders among the Ukrainian population (Kurapov et al., 2023a).

Data suggests that youth are particularly vulnerable to psychological trauma (Goto et al., 2024), and exposure to war directly heightens the risk of psychological trauma in adolescents (Osokina et al., 2023), children and adolescents had higher mental health morbidity (Somasundaram & Sivayokan, 1994).

Given these concerns, it is crucial to track the long-term impact of war on youth mental health. To our knowledge, some studies focused on Ukrainian youth mental health have appeared in recent times, but many of them had notable limitations, such as limited sample sizes, limited geographic scope (often confined to one or a few Ukrainian regions or even single universities), insufficient information about the samples or measures used (Goto et al., 2024; Kurapov et al., 2023b; Mykhaylyshyn et al., 2024; Pavlova et al., 2024; Stadnik et al., 2022).

Existing research literature shows that war has a significantly negative impact on the civilian population, leading to increased anxiety (Musisi & Kinyanda, 2020), stress disorders (Kashdan et al., 2009), depression (Frounfelker et al., 2018), and PTSD (Morina et al., 2018). Studies conducted during wartime – both in Ukraine after 2014 (Goto et al., 2024; Kurapov et al., 2023a; Stadnik et al., 2022) and in other countries such as communities in Africa, Kosovo, Afghanistan (Cardozo et al., 2004; Kashdan et al., 2009; Musisi & Kinyanda, 2020) – showed a similar tendency toward the deterioration of mental health in the population due to the impact of war. According to the study by Kurapov et al. declines in financial and employment conditions during wartime in Ukraine were associated with heightened anxiety; direct exposure to trauma was linked to increased levels of both anxiety and depression, while exposure to other war-related stressors was associated with elevated acute stress (2023a). The same study also found a statistically significant association between reported trauma exposure and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress among youth both residing in Ukraine and those living abroad (Kurapov et al., 2023a). Extending these insights from studies on the psychological impact of war, the grave sequence of events in Ukraine – where the pandemic was followed by a full-scale invasion – provides a unique opportunity to examine the development of mental health indicators among young people across two qualitatively distinct types of crises.

The present study aims to examine the development of mental health indicators through a generational lens, capturing changes in a cohort of young people exposed to two major societal crises: a global pandemic and the national trauma of an ongoing full-scale invasion. Using data from 2020, 2023, and 2024 (N = 4,187 students across 27 institutions in 12 cities), it addresses the following research question: How have levels of anxiety, depression, and stress among Ukrainian university students changed over time, and how do these changes vary by age, gender, and field of study?

Methods and materials

Design

The repeated cross-sectional study was conducted in three phases: Sample 1, Sample 2 and Sample 3. The first phase of the survey-based study (Sample 1) was conducted in November-December 2020 to evaluate the mental health of Ukrainian university students during the COVID-19 pandemic. The second phase of the study (Sample 2) took place in February–March 2023, one year after the Russian invasion, and the third phase (Sample 3) in March-April 2024 aimed at assessing the mental health of Ukrainian university students exposed to the ongoing war.

Measures

The survey consisted mainly of two parts: demographic (age, gender) and educational (field of study) variables, and the questionnaire package included Ukrainian versions of three standard questionnaires: the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, HADS (14 items), Beck's Depression Inventory (21 items) and Perceived Stress Scale (10 items). We selected these scales due to their high reliability as supported by existing literature (Beck & Beck, 1972; Veldbrekht & Tavrovetska, 2022; Zigmond & Snaith, 1983). The gender of the respondents was assessed as male or female in the 2020 questionnaire. In the 2023 and 2024 questionnaires the option ‘other gender' was added.

Data collection on depression and anxiety was conducted using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, HADS (14 items) and Beck's Depression Inventory (BDI, 21 items).

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) – a scale used to screen for emotional disorders, such as adjustment disorders, major depression, and anxiety disorders (Zigmond & Snaith, 1983). It consists of 14 self-reported items rated on a four-point scale, with seven items focusing on anxiety (HADS-A) and seven on depression (HADS-D). Scores of 0–7 indicate no symptoms of anxiety or depression, 8–10 suggest a possible diagnosis, and scores above 11 indicate a confirmed diagnosis of anxiety or depression. The HADS was selected for this study due to its brevity, validation in the Ukrainian context, and established use in both general and student populations (Zigmond & Snaith, 1983).

Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) consists of 21 items in total and each term is scored between 0 and 3. The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) was created through the clinical observation of behaviours and symptoms typical of individuals with depression. The questionnaire encompasses a broad spectrum of depressive symptoms, addressing emotional, cognitive, motivational, and physical aspects of the disorder. The BDI consists of 21 groups of items that assess both cognitive/affective and neurovegetative symptoms of depression. Respondents are asked to select the statement in each item group that best describes how they have been feeling over the past week, including today. The following scores correspond to the different severity of depressive symptoms: minimal depression = 0–10, mild depression = 11–16, borderline clinical depression = 17–20, moderate depression = 21–30, severe depression = 31–40, and a score above 40 points indicates extreme depression (Beck & Beck, 1972).

To measure stress, we used the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) developed by Cohen et al. (Veldbrekht & Tavrovetska, 2022). It is one of the most widely utilized tools for assessing stress levels in different sick and healthy populations. The questionnaire consists of 10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, where higher scores reflect either a greater frequency of stressful experiences or more effective coping strategies. After recording the reverse-scored items (0 = 4; 1 = 3; 2 = 2; 3 = 1; 4 = 0), the total score is calculated by summing the scores of all the responses on the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). This total provides a global measure of perceived stress. In the 10-item version of the questionnaire used in our study, items 1, 2, 3, 7, 9, and 10 are straightforward, while items 4, 5, 6, and 8 are reverse-scored. The internal consistency of the selected scales was measured in the present study using Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient. All scales demonstrated high reliability: HADS (α = 0.816), BDI (α = 0.902), and PSS (α = 0.860).

Sample

The target population for this study consisted of Ukrainian university students. The three cohorts of students were sampled, including 4187 university students from Ukraine, aged between 15 and 55 (M = 19.29; SD = 3.51), 26.9% men and 72.1% women. A small proportion of respondents (0.98%) identified as ‘other gender' and were excluded from the analysis due to the low-number group. Participants were enrolled in bachelor’s, master’s and PhD programmes across various faculties. All mentioned fields of study were categorized into three groups: (1) social sciences and humanities (63.17%), (2) natural sciences (8.74%), (3) technical sciences (28.09%).

Sample 1 (2020). Students from 22 Ukrainian higher educational establishments participated in this phase by completing a web-based questionnaire designed using Google-forms. A total of 757 students agreed to participate and responded to all survey questions and were included in the analyses. This sample consisted of 545 females, 212 males with an average age of 18.86 (SD = 3.3; age range: 16–47 years).

Sample 2 (2023). Students from 23 Ukrainian higher educational establishments participated in this phase by completing a web-based questionnaire designed using the REDCap platform. A total of 2592 students agreed to participate and responded to all questions of the survey and were included in the analyses. The sample consisted of 1895 females, 667 males, and 30 other-identified participants, with an average age of 19.26 (SD = 3.40; age range: 15–55 years).

Sample 3 (2024). Students from 27 Ukrainian higher educational establishments participated in this phase by completing a web-based questionnaire designed using the REDCap platform. A total of 838 students agreed to participate and responded to all questions of the survey and were included in the analyses. This sample consisted of 579 females, 248 males, and 11 other-identified participants with an average age of 17.94 (SD = 3.94; age range: 16–55 years).

All participants were divided into two age groups: 15–25 years (97.11%) and 26–55 years (2.89%) to investigate differences in the study variables between these age groups. Data collection

Data was collected anonymously via web-based surveys using Google Forms (2020) and the RedCap platform (2023, 2024). Invitations to participate were distributed by university counselling psychologists, who are members of the Association of Psychologists of High School of Ukraine. Participants got the link to the questionnaire via social media platforms and their university accounts. Based on the snowball sampling strategy, participants were also encouraged to invite new respondents from their personal contacts and networks. Participants who registered for the study but did not complete all questionnaires were excluded from the analyses. Consequently, the final dataset contained no missing questionnaire data.

Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Association of Psychologists of High School of Ukraine (decision no. APHSU2020/10/15-01). In the introduction section of the survey, researchers explained the purpose of the study and informed the participants that their participation was voluntary and anonymous, that their confidentiality would be protected, and their data would be kept confidential. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were allowed to terminate their involvement at any time. No incentives were offered to participants.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (IBM-SPSS v 24) programme. Descriptive statistics were used to describe sample characteristics and summarize qualitative variables using frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Continuous variables were given as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare mean scores for anxiety, depression, and stress. General Linear Model analyses were used to assess differences in HADS, BDI and PSS scores across demographic and educational groups. The level of statistical significance was set at p < .05. Internal consistency was assessed by means of Cronbach’s alpha. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared.

As shown in Table 2, during the COVID-19 pandemic, 7% to 18% of students reported anxiety and depression levels indicative of mental health issues, and 6% experiencing severe stress. After the Russian invasion, these percentages surged to two or three times higher, highlighting a marked escalation in mental health problems among students.

In a series of one-way ANOVA’s we tested the differences in symptoms of anxiety and depression and perceived stress in the different cohorts of students.

Anxiety

According to the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), 56.8% of respondents reported a non-clinical level of anxiety in 2020, 41.51% in 2023 and 42.79% in 2024 (Table 2). A one-way ANOVA was conducted to assess the differences in anxiety scores between the three samples (Table 3). The analysis revealed a significant difference between years. Post hoc analyses showed a significant difference in anxiety between 2020 and 2023 (p < .001) and between 2020 and 2024 (p < .001) but no difference between 2023 and 2024 (p = .793). This indicated that the level of anxiety increased in 2023 compared to 2020 (the COVID-19 period) and remained at nearly the same level in 2024. Depression

Depressive symptoms were measured both by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI).

HADS scores revealed that 75.69% of participants had a normal level of depression in 2020, 60.53% in 2023 and 61.38% in 2024 (Table 2). One-way ANOVA results showed significant differences in depression between these three samples (Table 3). Post hoc analyses showed a significant difference in depression between 2020 and 2023 (p < .001) and between 2020 and 2024 (p < .001) but no difference between 2023 and 2024 (p = .680); indicating the level of depression increased in 2023 compared to 2020 and remained stable in 2024.

A one-way ANOVA analysis of the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) scores also revealed significant differences in depression between the three samples (Table 3). Post hoc analysis showed a near significant difference in depression measured by BDI between 2020 and 2023 (p = .056) and between 2023 and 2024 (p = .004), but no significant difference between 2020 and 2024 (p = .800). So, after an increase in depression levels in 2023, it decreased in 2024, nearly reaching the 2020 level.

Perceived Stress

As shown in Table 2, the results of the Perceived Stress Scale indicated that 6.34% of students reported high stress in 2020, but this number increased to 16.67% in 2023 and only slightly decreased to 15.14% in 2024. One-way ANOVA analysis confirmed a significant difference between the three samples (Table 3). Post hoc analyses showed a significant difference between 2020 and 2023 (p < .001) and between 2020 and 2024 (p < .001), but no significant difference between 2023 and 2024 (p = .997).

Gender

The results of the General linear model showed statistically significant differences between males and females in anxiety, stress and depression measured by the BDI (F: 17.9–137.1, p < .05); female participants had higher scores. However, we found no statistically significant difference between males and females according to the HADS (F = 1.2, p = .349). Interactions between gender and sampling-year did not have effects on anxiety, depression, nor stress (p: .11–.73); indicating that gender differences in symptom level did not vary over time.

Age group

Differences between the 15–25 and the 26–55 age groups in anxiety, depression, and stress were observed (F: 8.6 −16.2, p < .05); younger respondents demonstrated a higher level of all types of distress. The results of the tests of interaction between sampling-year and age showed no significant interaction effects on anxiety, depression, nor stress (p: .281–.724); suggesting that age as well as gender differences were stable across the sampling years.

Field of study

No statistically significant difference in symptoms of anxiety and depression or perceived stress was found between students from different fields of study: social and humanities, natural sciences, technical sciences (F: .01–2.0, NS). However, the tests of interaction between sampling-year and demographic variables showed significant interaction effects on depression, anxiety, and stress (F: 2.5–6.8, p < .05). This indicates that symptoms levels developed differently in students from different fields of study (See Figure 2).

Discussion

The aim of this study was primarily to provide a descriptive report of changes in mental health indicators in a unique Ukrainian generation exposed to two qualitatively different, but all-encompassing societal crises. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, 6–18% of Ukrainian university students reported levels of anxiety, depression, and stress that indicated mental health problems. These symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress among students at Ukrainian higher education institutions significantly increased in 2023, marking one year since the start of the full-scale war in Ukraine. In 2024, there were almost no significant changes in these indicators. Especially, women and younger adults reported higher levels of distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, Ukrainian university students already exhibited relatively high levels of anxiety, depression, and stress compared to the period before the pandemic. Osokina et al. reported data from 2016 to 2017 survey conducted among adolescents in a peaceful region of Ukraine: 23.2% of students had mild/moderate anxiety (compared to 25.3% with borderline anxiety in our 2020 study), and 1.5% had severe anxiety (vs. 17.97% with abnormal anxiety in 2020 in our study) (Osokina et al. 2023). Regarding depression, 2.9% had moderately severe or severe symptoms in 2016–2017 (vs. 6.87% with abnormal depression or 5.68% with severe/extreme depression in 2020 in our study). It is however worth noting that although Ukrainian university students showed relatively elevated mental health concerns in 2020, their levels of psychological distress were generally lower compared to those reported among youth in other countries during the pandemic (Aslan et al., 2020; Biswas & Biswas, 2023; Rogowska et al., 2020; Son et al., 2020). For instance, only 5.59% of students in Turkey reported low level of stress in 2020 (PSS) (Aslan et al., 2020) while 26.95% students reported low stress in Ukraine. The mean score of stress (PSS) in Polish students was 20.32 ± 8.38 in 2020, and in US college students it was 18.8 ± 4.9 (Rogowska et al., 2020; Son et al., 2020), while this indicator in Ukraine was 17.33 ± 6.35 at the same time. This difference could be attributed to the higher adaptability of Ukrainian youth to crisis situations, such as periods of political and economic instability in the country, as well as their experience living under conditions of change and uncertainty. These factors may have enhanced their stress-coping and adaptation skills. Additionally, this difference could be linked to the absence of strict quarantine measures in certain regions of the country and the shorter duration of these measures.

The present results, showing a marked increase in stress, anxiety, and depression after COVID-19, indicate that the full-scale war was a significant factor contributing to poor mental health among Ukrainian students.

After the initial increase in mental health symptoms, the levels of anxiety, depression, and stress among Ukrainian students did not change significantly in 2024, at the two-year mark of the full-scale war in Ukraine, compared to 2023. The BDI even showed a significant decrease in the level of depression. The current data do not permit further exploration of this somewhat contradictory finding, which may be attributed to differences in how the depression construct is represented in each questionnaire.

Cross-national comparisons of anxiety, depression, and stress levels among Ukrainian students show both higher and lower values relative to other countries in conflict (Cardozo et al., 2004; Mollica et al., 1993; Somasundaram & Jamunanatha, 2002). Even within the same country, such as Afghanistan (Cardozo et al., 2004), findings vary widely – similar to Ukraine-based studies from 2023 to 2024. For example, Goto et al. (2024) found moderate or severe depression in 42.3% of 15–17-year-olds, aligning with our HADS results but lower than our BDI findings. In contrast, Stadnik et al. (2022) reported no depression symptoms in 79–93% of university students, differing markedly from our 38 to 54%. General Ukrainian population studies also show divergence: Kurapov et al. (2023a) found 84.3% with depressive symptoms, while Chudzicka-Czupala et al. (2023) reported 46.5%, closer to our range and that of Kurapov et al. –42.3% (2023b).

The observed anxiety levels in this study exceed those in several Ukrainian studies (Chudzicka-Czupala et al., 2023; Goto et al., 2024; Kurapov et al., 2023a; Osokina et al., 2023; Stadnik et al., 2022). For instance, Goto et al. (2024) reported 24.8% of 15–17-year-old students with moderate or severe anxiety –well below our findings of 58.53% in 2023 and 57.21% in 2024. Stadnik et al. (2022) found 76–92% of students with no anxiety symptoms, compared to just 41.51% (2023) and 42.79% (2024) in our sample.

Stress levels in our data are consistent with some Ukrainian studies (2023–2024) (Kurapov et al., 2023a; Lushchak et al., 2023; Pavlova et al., 2024), though others report far lower levels. Stadnik et al. (2022) found 78–94% of students with no stress symptoms (Stadnik et al., 2022), and Chudzicka-Czupala et al. (2023) reported only 28.6% of the general population experiencing stress (Chudzicka-Czupala et al., 2023). Lushchak et al. (2023) found a comparably high stress level among non-displaced persons (15.5%) but much higher rates among internally displaced individuals and refugees (21.6% and 25.2%, respectively) (Lushchak et al., 2023).

These discrepancies underline the importance of further research, particularly studies with broader, more diverse samples. Many existing Ukrainian studies rely on limited samples from single institutions or regions (Osokina et al., 2023; Pavlova et al., 2024; Stadnik et al., 2022), which may not accurately reflect national trends.

We found significant differences between men and women in anxiety (HADS), depression (BDI) and stress (PSS) throughout the entire study period – women demonstrated higher levels of measured indicators. These findings are consistent with other studies conducted both during the COVID-19 period among students (Aslan et al., 2020; Biswas & Biswas, 2023) and during wartime in different countries (Cardozo et al., 2004; Riad et al., 2022; Skwirczynska et al., 2022), including Ukraine (Kurapov et al., 2023b; Stadnik et al., 2022). However, it is worth noting that some studies reported higher levels of stress among men (Mykhaylyshyn et al., 2024), which may be related to mandatory military service (required for men over the age of 25 in Ukraine).

Younger respondents (15–25 years) displayed higher levels of distress and reported more symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress. These results are in line with findings in other studies (Biswas & Biswas, 2023; Skwirczynska et al., 2022). Also, it is consistent with the notion that younger individuals may be more vulnerable to wartime trauma (Osokina et al., 2023). There is a risk that these reactions will remain observable among these young Ukrainians for many years to come as lasting psychological consequences of the war.

Students from technical study programmes showed a significant increase in all measures of distress in 2023, but these levels declined again in 2024 (See Supplementary materials). The data do not provide any explanation for this, and further studies are needed to illuminate whether this finding reflects specific characteristics of this student population or it is a chance finding. A possible explanation for this tendency could be related to specific personality traits of these categories of students (for example, higher hardiness or resilience) and their reactions to crisis events. A variety of universities across different years of study, especially in terms of their location and specialization, could also influence the results obtained.

Future research in this area should focus on monitoring mental health indicators – such as levels of anxiety, depression, and stress – among Ukrainian students and youth more broadly. It should also aim to identify the factors that influence these indicators and the coping mechanisms that affect psychological well-being. Potential factors to be studied include direct or indirect involvement in hostilities or military volunteer activities, proximity of residence to active conflict zones, strength of social connections, and level of financial stability. Another important research perspective should explore the forms of psychological support services that are not only preferred by this population but also feasible and accessible under wartime conditions.

Based on the findings, it is recommended that relevant authorities, specialized organizations, and mental health centres allocate greater resources toward providing psychological support for Ukrainian university students. Particular attention should be given to university-based psychological services, which should be accessible and delivered in formats that correspond to students’ actual needs and preferences. This population group warrants enhanced attention not only due to the concerning levels of mental health difficulties but also because these young adults are expected to play a crucial role in sustaining the country's functioning and contributing to its recovery and reconstruction in the future.

Limitation

Although our findings shed light on changes in anxiety, depression and stress among university students in Ukraine from the COVID-19 period to the ongoing full-scale war, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we did not account for other potentially influential characteristics, such as experiences of severe trauma, displacement conditions, or the involvement of close relatives or acquaintances in active military combat. These factors may have significantly affected the results and could help to explain the elevated levels of the measured mental health outcomes. Second, the sample is not representative of the Ukrainian student population. Although recruitment covers all national regions, the representation of the student groups does not reflect the actual distribution across fields of study. In addition, our sample includes a higher proportion of women, which may result in certain subgroups of male students being underrepresented or not represented at all. Third, the study only includes students of higher educational institutions. Including other social groups could offer a more comprehensive understanding of the mental health situation in the country. Despite these limitations, the current sample has notable strengths of the current sample, particularly its large size and broad geographical coverage.

Conclusion

This research provides a unique insight into a country currently experiencing the third year of a full-scale invasion by Russia after the COVID-19 pandemic crisis. The study results offer an opportunity to examine students’ psychological reactions to a large traumatic event (full-scale war) preceded by an earlier traumatic experience (pandemic). Our findings shed light onto the changes that occurred after the onset of the full-scale war and allow for monitoring the mental health of students during the war period. We found that in spite of the restricted conditions Ukrainian students experienced during the pandemic, their symptoms of anxiety, depression and stress significantly increased after the Russian invasion and did not decline in the second year of war. Ukrainian youth have been living under a high level of distress for more than four years, with almost no tendency to decrease over time. The results of this study highlight the importance of implementing strategies for the prevention, intervention, and treatment of anxiety, depression and stress among university students and other young adults, who are a particularly vulnerable group.
