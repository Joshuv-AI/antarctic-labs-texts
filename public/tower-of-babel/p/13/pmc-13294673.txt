Tetrahedral DNA nanostructure-delivered suppressor tRNA ameliorates nephropathy in COL4A5 nonsense mutation-mediated Alport syndrome mice
Authors: Jingru Lv, Ying Li, Dean Hong, Yuxuan Shen, Zeyuan Mi, Qing Liang, Zhetao Zheng, Bangyan Kong, Wankai Gao, Zhiyang Dou, Songlin Tian, Qing Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonsense mutations introduce premature termination codons (PTCs) that trigger mRNA degradation and underlie genetic diseases, including Alport syndrome (AS). Suppressor tRNAs (sup-tRNAs) are potential therapies for such disorders, as they can precisely readthrough PTCs and restore full-length protein synthesis. For AS caused by COL4A5 nonsense-mediated deficiency, we developed a TDN-sup-tRNA delivery strategy using tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDNs) characterized by excellent biocompatibility and renal targeting. In a Col4a5-R471X mouse model, TDN-sup-tRNA achieved high readthrough efficiency, restoring COL4A5 protein expression and reconstituting glomerular basement membrane integrity. Notably, proteinuria was reduced to 35% of baseline within 4 weeks. Compared with AAV2/9-mediated delivery, this system exhibited enhanced renal targeting and superior therapeutic efficacy over a one-month treatment period, without detectable toxicity. Collectively, this strategy represents a safe and effective non-viral RNA therapeutic approach for precise correction of nonsense mutation–associated renal diseases.

Introduction

Alport syndrome (AS) comprises a group of hereditary nephropathies characterized by structural defects in the type IV collagen network, with COL4A5 mutations being the most prevalent cause due to their X-linked inheritance pattern [1, 2]. Epidemiological data indicate that ~88% of COL4A5-associated cases manifest as the typical AS phenotype. Although nonsense mutations account for only 7% of these genetic variants, they are frequently correlated with the most severe clinical outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S1A). These mutations introduce a premature termination codon within the coding sequence (CDS), which triggers premature translation arrest and yields truncated proteins that are often non-functional or even proteotoxic. Furthermore, cells employ nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD)—a stringent mRNA surveillance mechanism—to degrade PTC-containing transcripts. This pathway further diminishes the abundance of mutant mRNA and restricts the expression of the encoded protein, thereby precipitating various diseases rooted in a loss-of-function (LOF) mechanism [3–5].

According to the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) [6], arginine-to-TGA nonsense mutations account for only a small fraction of TGA-related AS variant records (5/28 records). Conversely, clinical data from a local hospital reveal a strikingly higher prevalence, with mutations at arginine-encoding sites reaching 13/19 AS case records (Supplementary Fig. S1A). This discrepancy highlights a critical, unmet clinical need for therapeutic interventions capable of suppressing TGA codons while specifically restoring the wild-type arginine at the mutation site. Multiple strategies have been explored to address nonsense mutations, including approaches targeting mRNA processing, translation, and genome editing. Among these, translational readthrough-inducing drugs (TRIDs) and gene replacement therapy represent the principal strategies that have advanced toward clinical application and hold the potential to restore full-length protein expression [7]. However, TRIDs are frequently hindered by the risk of stochastic amino acid insertion at the PTC and the potential toxicity resulting from the readthrough of natural termination codons [8, 9].

In recent years, suppressor tRNAs (sup-tRNAs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic modality for nonsense mutations [10, 11]. By utilizing anticodons that form canonical Watson–Crick base pairs with PTCs, sup-tRNAs effectively mimic the behavior of elongator tRNAs, enabling the precise readthrough and site-specific insertion of their cognate amino acids [12, 13]. Consequently, the ability of sup-tRNAs to restore full-length protein synthesis with high fidelity has positioned them as a potentially disease-agnostic therapy for correcting nonsense mutations.

Several delivery strategies for sup-tRNA have been documented to date, including AAV-mediated delivery of sup-tRNA genes (AAV-sup-tRNA) [14, 15], prime editing-mediated endogenous tRNA reprogramming (PERT) [16], and lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-encapsulated engineered sup-tRNA molecules (LNP-sup-tRNA) [17]. These approaches have successfully demonstrated PTC suppression and phenotypic rescue in various models, such as MPS I mice (AAV-sup-tRNA [14, 15], PERT [16]), hereditary retinal disease models (AAV-sup-tRNA [18]), and primary cells from patients with cystic fibrosis (LNP-sup-tRNA [17]). Notably, however, there have been no reported attempts to use sup-tRNAs for the in vivo treatment of nonsense mutation-associated diseases specifically involving the kidney. Furthermore, existing delivery platforms have failed to exhibit significant accumulation or specificity within renal tissues [19, 20], suggesting that current delivery strategies are suboptimal for addressing renal-involved genetic pathologies.

To address the challenges of achieving specific renal targeting—a feat that remains elusive for AAV and LNP vectors—we propose a targeted delivery strategy utilizing tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDN) as carriers for sup-tRNAs [19, 21, 22]. Hereafter, the TDN loaded with sup-tRNA is referred to as TDN-sup-tRNA. TDNs are self-assembled nanostructures driven by the precise base-pairing of four to six DNA strands. Characterized by their well-defined and rigid geometry, excellent biocompatibility, and high programmability, TDNs have been successfully employed for the in vivo delivery of functional nucleic acids, such as siRNA and mRNA [23–25]. However, the application of TDNs for sup-tRNA delivery has not been previously explored.

Leveraging the intrinsic renal-accumulating properties of TDNs, we facilitated kidney targeting by covalently conjugating the sup-tRNA to the 5′ terminus of a cargo DNA strand. In this study, we identified a lead TGA sup-tRNAArg candidate (R-TCT [DNA] and R-UCU3 [RNA], respectively), which demonstrated robust readthrough activity across diverse PTC (TGA) sequence contexts in COL4A5. Covalent coupling of R-UCU3 to the TDN core significantly enhanced its metabolic stability. In a Col4a5-R471X mouse model of AS, TDN-sup-tRNA treatment surpassed AAV-mediated delivery in therapeutic efficacy, effectively restoring COL4A5 expression and significantly ameliorating the nephropathic phenotype.

Materials and methods

Design and preparation of sup-tRNAs

The tRNA gene sequences were obtained from the genomic tRNA database (http://gtrnadb.ucsc.edu/). Design of tRNA-Arg-derived sup-tRNAs (sup-tRNA-Arg) was carried out based on the scaffold of human cytoplasmic sup-tRNA-Arg, selected for its high natural abundance and structural stability. The native anticodon was replaced with the complementary sequence 3′-UCA-5′ to recognize the TGA premature termination codon. The sup-tRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Translation readthrough efficiency assay

To quantitatively evaluate the ability of sup-tRNAs to mediate translational readthrough of disease-associated nonsense mutations, a fluorescence-based COL4A5-T2A-EGFP reporter system was established. The full-length human COL4A5 coding sequence was cloned upstream of an enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) coding sequence, separated by a Thosea asigna virus 2A self-cleaving peptide. This design allows the production of two discrete polypeptides from a single transcript, with downstream EGFP expression strictly dependent on successful translation through the upstream COL4A5 coding region. The resulting construct was designated COL4A5-WT. Site-directed mutagenesis was subsequently performed to introduce individual TGA nonsense codons at four evolutionarily conserved arginine residues within the COL4A5 coding sequence (R266TGA, R373TGA, R1569TGA, and R1683TGA), generating four independent nonsense mutant reporter constructs. All experiments were performed in HEK293T cells maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Excell) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells were transfected at ~70%–80% confluence using Lipofectamine 3000, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with each reporter plasmid co-transfected together with the corresponding sup-tRNA expression vector. The wild-type reporter and nonsense mutant reporters transfected in the absence of sup-tRNAs were included as controls. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were harvested and analyzed to quantify EGFP fluorescence intensity. Translational readthrough efficiency was calculated by normalizing the EGFP fluorescence of each nonsense mutant reporter to that of the wild-type reporter. In this system, EGFP expression serves as a quantitative surrogate for nonsense suppression, as downstream EGFP translation occurs only when the introduced TGA codon is efficiently suppressed; failure of readthrough results in premature translation termination upstream of the T2A sequence and absence of EGFP signal.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a Hifair® AdvanceFast One-step RT-gDNA Digestion SuperMix for qPCR Kit (Yeasen) to remove genomic DNA contamination. Each reaction was performed in triplicate under the following cycling conditions: 95°C for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method and normalized to the internal GAPDH.

Mass spectrometry

Protein purification was performed using the Flag-tagged fusion protein purification kit (Elabscience). The purified protein was loaded into sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) using 5× loading buffer (Applygen, B1034) after boiling at 95°C for 10 min. The Col4a5-Flag band was visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining, and the gel was excised for mass spectrometry analysis. For LC-MS/MS analysis, a Thermo Ultimate 3000 nano-UPLC system was coupled to a Thermo Fusion LUMOS mass spectrometer. An Acclaim PepMap RSLC column (75 µm ID, 250 mm length, C18) was employed, with data analyzed using Proteome Discoverer software.

Western blot

Total cellular protein was extracted from transfected cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentration was quantified using the Bradford assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE under reducing conditions and subsequently transferred onto methanol-activated PVDF membranes (Millipore). After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies. The membranes were then washed three times (10 min each) with TBST and incubated with corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Biodragon, 926-68070; 1:10 000) for 1 h at room temperature. Following further washes, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and imaged with a Tanon imaging system. Gray value analysis of protein bands was performed using ImageJ software, and target protein expression levels were normalized to vinculin as an internal loading control.

In vitro transcription of sup-tRNA

Sup-tRNA used in stability assays (R-UCU3) were generated by in vitro transcription. Briefly, DNA templates encoding the engineered tRNA-Arg-TCT-3 scaffold with an anticodon modified to recognize the UGA stop codon were synthesized (Biolino, Shanghai, China) and amplified by PCR to include a T7 promoter sequence upstream. In vitro transcription was performed using T7 RNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transcription reaction was carried out at 37°C for 4 h, followed by DNase I treatment to remove the DNA template. The resulting RNA products were purified by denaturing PAGE and eluted from the gel. Purified sup-tRNAs were quantified by NanoDrop spectrophotometry and verified by denaturing PAGE to confirm integrity and expected size.

Synthesis of TDN-sup-tRNA

DNA tetrahedral nanostructures were self-assembled by mixing four single-stranded oligonucleotides at equimolar ratios through a thermal annealing process. The DNA oligonucleotides were chemically synthesized and purified by Biolino (Shanghai, China). Briefly, oligonucleotides dissolved in sterile PBS buffer (final concentration of 2 μM for each strand) were first denatured at 95°C for 5 min and then gradually cooled from 95°C to 4°C over 4 h to allow stepwise folding of the tetrahedron. During assembly, arginine-derived sup-tRNAs were integrated into the DNA tetrahedron via electrostatic interactions, yielding TDN-R-UCU3 complexes. The assembly products were analyzed by native PAGE (6% gel, 4°C, 100 V, 1.5 h), with successful tetrahedron assembly and tRNA incorporation confirmed by shifts in band mobility. All assembly procedures were performed under sterile conditions using DEPC-treated, nuclease-free materials to minimize RNA. DNA origami sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Serum stability assay

To evaluate the serum stability of free R-UCU3 and TDN-R-UCU3 complexes, FBS (excell) was used in two experimental designs. In the concentration-dependent experiment, equal concentrations of TDN-R-UCU3 and R-TCT3 were incubated with a gradient of FBS concentrations (0, 1%, 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10%) at 37°C for 24 h. In the time-course experiment, samples were incubated with 10% FBS at 37°C for various time intervals (0 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 10 h, and 12 h). After incubation, equal volumes of each reaction mixture were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel run at 130 V for 30 min. Gel images were captured under UV illumination, and band intensities were quantified to assess the integrity of R-UCU3.

Cellular uptake analysis

Fluorescently labeled TDN-R-UCU3 complexes were incubated with 293T and MPC-5 cells (Procell), respectively. Prior to the experiment, cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37°C under 5% CO₂ for 24 h until reaching 50%–60% confluence. After removing the original medium, fresh medium containing fluorescently labeled TDN-R-UCU3 complexes was added to each well, and cells were incubated at 37°C for various time points (10 min, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h). Following each incubation period, cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS to remove unbound complexes. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with DAPI or 10 min to label nuclei, and then stained with phalloidin-488 to visualize F-actin cytoskeletons in green. Cellular uptake was observed under a fluorescence microscope. Images were captured in the corresponding fluorescence channel for the labeled complex (Cy5), the DAPI channel for nuclear staining, and the F-actin channel (488 nm).

Recombinant AAV production

Recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 2/9 (rAAV2/9) carrying the UGA sup-tRNA R-TCT3 was generated via the standard triple-plasmid transfection protocol (Obiosh, Shanghai). The expression cassette was engineered as hU6-(R-TCT3)-Spacer-CMV-mCherry-Flag-WPRE. Within this construct, the mCherry reporter was utilized to characterize the tissue tropism and transduction efficiency of the AAV vectors in vivo.

Mice

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Peking University Health Science Center under animal protocol no. DLASBF0028 and conducted in strict accordance with the committee’s guidelines as well as the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines for animal use. The Col4a5-R471X mouse model (purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms) was used in the experiments. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free barrier facility at the Department of Laboratory Animal Science of Peking University Health Science Center under controlled environmental conditions: temperature 18–23°C, humidity 40%–60%, 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water, and 3–5 mice per cage. Four-week-old male littermates were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and interventions were initiated at 10 weeks of age. The mice were divided into three groups: the TDN-R-UCU3 intervention group received tail vein injections of TDN-R-UCU3 complexes at a dose of 0.4 mg/kg every 3 days for 4 weeks; the AAV-R-TCT3 intervention group received tail vein injections of AAV-R-TCT3 at a total dose of 2.5 × 1011 vg per mouse over a one-month period; and the control group received equal volumes of normal saline via tail vein injection following the same schedule and frequency as the intervention groups. All administrations were performed at a fixed time of day to minimize circadian influences. Body weight and general behavioral status were recorded weekly throughout the intervention period. After the experiment, serum and tissue samples were collected for subsequent analysis.

In vivo biodistribution

To investigate the distribution and pharmacokinetics of Cy5-labeled TDN-R-UCU3 complexes in vivo, the labeled complexes were administered to experimental mice via tail vein injection. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 30 min, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h post-injection using a small-animal in vivo imaging system (IVIS). Whole-body and regional fluorescence signals were acquired to analyze the overall distribution profile of the complexes over time. At each time point, animals were euthanized, and major organs (including heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and brain) were collected for ex vivo organ fluorescence imaging to further quantify the accumulation of the complexes in different tissues. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using the accompanying software and expressed as relative fluorescence units per unit tissue weight or area. Ultimately, a time–tissue distribution profile of the TDN-R-UCU3 complexes was obtained.

Multiplex immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded kidney tissue sections were sequentially dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a pressurized decloaking chamber for 15 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 15 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. After permeabilization with TBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min, non-specific binding sites were blocked by incubation with a blocking solution consisting of 5% bovine serum albumin in TBS for 1 h. Multiplex staining was carried out using a sequential labeling method. First, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting COL4A5 (abcam, ab231957) and Wilms tumor protein (abcam, ab89901). Then, the sections were incubated at room temperature with appropriate species-specific secondary antibodies for 1 h. To enable multiple rounds of labeling, antibody–antigen complexes were dissociated after each staining step by treatment with 0.1 M glycine-HCl buffer for 10 min, followed by thorough washing with TBS. After labeling all targets, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma) at room temperature for 8 min. Sections were stored at 4°C until imaging.

Transmission electron microscopy

Renal tissue samples were collected within 3 min after excision. A culture dish containing electron microscopy fixative (ServiceBio) was prepared in advance. Using a sharp surgical blade, the tissue was rapidly cut into small cuboid blocks measuring 1 mm × 1 mm × 3 mm directly in the fixative. Immediately after collection, the tissue blocks were fully immersed in fresh electron microscopy fixative and fixed at room temperature in the dark for 2 h, then transferred to 4°C for storage. Subsequent processing included rinsing with 0.1 M phosphate buffer, post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydration through a graded ethanol series, and infiltration and embedding with epoxy resin. The embedded blocks were polymerized in a 60°C oven for 48 h to achieve full curing. Ultrathin section with a thickness of 50–70 nm was cut from the polymerized blocks using an ultramicrotome and collected on copper grids coated with a formvar support film. The sections were double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, dehydrated with absolute ethanol to remove excess stain, and dried at room temperature. The prepared sections were observed and imaged under a transmission electron microscope (TEM) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

Serum and urine samples

Serum creatinine (Cre), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and urinary creatinine (U-Cre) levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer via enzymatic colorimetric assays. All samples were analyzed following the instrument’s standard operating procedures, and quality control samples were included to ensure assay accuracy. Urinary albumin (U-Alb) was quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Nanjing Jiancheng). After collection of 24-h urine samples, appropriate volumes of urine samples and standards were added to pre-coated wells, followed by incubation at 37°C, washing, sequential addition of detection antibody, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody, and substrate. All samples were analyzed within 24 h after collection.

Histological and ultrastructural analysis

Renal tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by paraffin embedding and serial sectioning at a thickness of 4 μm. Tissue sections were subjected to conventional hematoxylin and eosin staining to evaluate the basic renal histoarchitecture and morphological changes. Masson’s trichrome staining was used to specifically examine collagen deposition and the extent of interstitial fibrosis in the kidney. Periodic acid-Schiff staining was performed to assess glycogen deposition in the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) and mesangial regions. All stained sections were observed under a light microscope.

ELISA assay

Whole blood samples were allowed to stand at room temperature for 1 h, then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was carefully collected as serum, which was either used immediately for subsequent assays or aliquoted and stored at −80°C for future use. The levels of inflammatory and immune-related cytokines in serum, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), were quantitatively analyzed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (Invitrogen). All procedures were performed strictly according to the instructions of the respective ELISA kits: serum samples and standards were added to pre-coated wells, incubated at 37°C, washed, and then sequentially treated with biotin-labeled detection antibodies, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin, and TMB substrate. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves. Each sample was assayed in duplicate, and all experiments included negative controls and standard controls to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the results.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical significance was determined using appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests as specified in the figure legends. A P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening and identification of sup-tRNAs for the suppression of COL4A5 TGA nonsense mutations

Given the clinical prevalence of COL4A5 R-to-X (X = TGA) mutations (Supplementary Fig. S1A), we aimed to develop sup-tRNAs capable of reading through the TGA while specifically incorporating the wild-type Arg. A search of the GtRNAdb database [26] identified two high-confidence human tRNA-Arg isoacceptor families—tRNA-Arg-TCT and tRNA-Arg-TCG—that could be converted into TGA-recognizing sup-tRNAs via a single-nucleotide substitution in the anticodon. These families consist of five and six isodecoders, respectively (Fig. 1A). Accordingly, we generated 11 sup-tRNA constructs by modifying the anticodons of selected arginine tRNAs. According to the standard tRNA nomenclature, these sequences are designated as tRNA-Arg-TCT-1 to -5 and tRNA-Arg-TCG-1 to -6. For simplicity, the corresponding constructs are hereafter referred to as R-TCT-1 to -5 and R-TCG-1 to -6, respectively (for detailed sequences, see Supplementary Table S1). Given that both promoter architecture and transcriptional termination elements can influence sup-tRNA expression and PTC suppression efficiency, we employed the 7SK promoter—a well-characterized RNA polymerase III promoter known to support robust transcription of small non-coding RNAs, including engineered tRNAs. This system has been successfully utilized in previous studies and provides consistent transcription with defined initiation and termination properties [13, 16, 27]. The complete sequence of the expression cassette, including promoter, tRNA, and termination elements, is provided in Supplementary Table S2. To evaluate readthrough efficiency, we established a reporter system using a COL4A5-R266TGA-T2A-EGFP construct [hereafter referred to as the COL4A5-R266TGA reporter (for detailed sequence, see Supplementary Table S3)]. Successful TGA suppression allows for the downstream expression of EGFP, serving as a fluorescent proxy for sup-tRNA activity. Following the co-transfection of the COL4A5-R266TGA reporter and sup-tRNA candidates into HEK293T cells for 48 h, readthrough levels were assessed via flow cytometry and western blot (Fig. 1B).

Fluorescence imaging revealed distinct variations in suppression efficiency among the candidates, with R-TCT-3 exhibiting the most robust readthrough activity (Fig. 1C). Quantitative analysis showed that R-TCT-3-mediated EGFP expression exceeded 50% of the COL4A5-WT reporter (Fig. 1D), a finding corroborated by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. S1B and C). To assess the specificity of the observed readthrough, we included a scrambled tRNA control lacking a functional anticodon–codon pairing capability. As shown in (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B), the scrambled tRNA exhibited minimal EGFP signal and negligible readthrough activity, indicating that suppression is dependent on the specific sequence and decoding function of the engineered sup-tRNAs rather than nonspecific effects. To exclude potential confounding effects from variability in transfection efficiency, a CMV-driven mCherry plasmid was co-transfected as an internal reference. No significant differences in mCherry fluorescence intensity were observed across groups (Supplementary Fig. S2C and D), confirming comparable transfection efficiency among different conditions. Consistent with these findings, Western blot analysis confirmed that R-TCT-3 was the most effective candidate for suppressing the TGA of COL4A5-R266TGA reporter (Fig. 1E and F). Sequence alignment revealed only minor nucleotide differences among the tested sup-tRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Notably, R-TCT-3 differs from R-TCT-2 by a single G-to-A substitution at position 59 and from R-TCT-5 by a single base-pair change at positions 50:64. Despite these minimal variations, substantial differences in readthrough efficiency were observed, suggesting that even subtle sequence features can significantly influence sup-tRNA performance. It is likely that multiple factors—including tRNA folding, stability, aminoacylation efficiency, and interactions with the translational machinery—collectively contribute to these functional differences, although the precise mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Based on these results, R-TCT-3 was selected as the lead sup-tRNA candidate for subsequent studies.

To further validate the fidelity of amino acid incorporation during readthrough, we performed mass spectrometry analysis using a COL4A5-R373TGA reporter. As shown in (Supplementary Fig. S3), arginine was exclusively detected at the position corresponding to the TGA codon, with no evidence of alternative amino acid incorporation. These findings demonstrate that R-TCT-3 mediates high-fidelity suppression of TGA codons, enabling precise restoration of the wild-type arginine residue.

R-TCT-3 exhibits effective readthrough across diverse COL4A5 TGA sequence contexts

Previous studies have reported that the readthrough efficiency of a given sup-tRNA is highly sensitive to the specific sequence context surrounding a PTC [28, 29]. In addition, prior work has examined readthrough efficiency across different COL4A5 nonsense variants and demonstrated context-dependent variability [30], and sup-tRNA-mediated correction of PTC of COL4A5 mRNA has also been previously reported [31]. These studies primarily established the feasibility of sup-tRNA–mediated readthrough in vitro. In contrast, the present study focuses on overcoming a major translational barrier—efficient in vivo delivery—by developing a TDN-based delivery system and systematically evaluating its therapeutic potential in comparison with viral approaches.

To evaluate the versatility of R-TCT-3, we engineered a series of COL4A5 reporter constructs—R266TGA, R373TGA, R1569TGA, and R1683TGA—each corresponding to a clinically reported pathogenic variant in AS patients [32–34] (Fig. 2A). Following co-transfection of these reporters with R-TCT-3 constructs into HEK293T cells, fluorescence imaging and western blot analysis revealed that R-TCT-3 mediated robust readthrough across all four TGA sites (Fig. 2B–D). Furthermore, R-TCT-3 treatment consistently increased COL4A5 mRNA abundance in all four reporter groups (Fig. 2E). Interestingly, we observed relatively consistent readthrough efficiencies across these COL4A5 PTC variants, with all sites reaching ∼50% of wild-type levels.

While the initial screening utilized EGFP as a surrogate marker for readthrough, we performed IF analysis to verify the restoration of the COL4A5 protein itself. For this validation, we selected the COL4A5-R373TGA reporter, which is proximal to the mutation site in the Col4a5-R471X AS mouse model. Prominent and co-localized fluorescence signals for both EGFP and COL4A5 were observed in the R-TCT-3-treated group, whereas these signals were nearly undetectable in the COL4A5-R373X control group (Fig. 2F). These findings demonstrate that R-TCT-3 effectively restores the functional expression of the COL4A5 protein in vitro. Collectively, these data establish R-TCT-3 as a highly robust sup-tRNA capable of suppressing TGA across diverse COL4A5 sequence contexts.

Construction and characterization of the TDN-sup-tRNA

Following the identification of the high-efficiency candidate R-TCT-3, we sought to develop a renal-targeted delivery formulation, designated TDN-sup-tRNA. The TDN platform employed in this study was adapted from previously reported designs [22], which have been widely used for nucleic acid delivery. Given the use of a non-viral TDN platform, the sup-tRNA was incorporated in its RNA form (R-UCU3). The TDN scaffold was assembled from four structural strands (S1–S4) forming the tetrahedral framework, together with a cargo strand (S5) for sup-tRNA loading and an optional labeling strand for downstream tracking (Fig. 3A). Regarding formulation parameters, we did not perform extensive de novo optimization of TDN sequences, as the structural design was based on previously validated configurations [22]. Similarly, the dosing regimen used in this study was selected based on prior reports demonstrating effective in vivo delivery of nucleic acid cargos using comparable TDN systems [35]. The results demonstrated a distinctive stepwise retardation in electrophoretic mobility as each successive structural or cargo strand was added to the reaction (Fig. 3B).

Given that effective renal targeting of sup-tRNAs relies on systemic circulation [36, 37], we evaluated the metabolic stability of TDN-R-UCU3 in vitro. Using in vitro transcribed (IVT) naked R-UCU3 as a control, we incubated the TDN-R-UCU3 complex at 37°C with varying concentrations of FBS for 24 h to simulate the circulatory environment. While this system does not fully recapitulate the complexity of in vivo blood circulation, it provides a commonly used and controlled model to assess the relative nuclease resistance of nucleic acid formulations. We first assessed the stability of TDN-R-UCU3 under different serum concentrations by incubating the complexes at 37°C for 24 h. Under these conditions, naked R-UCU3 was completely degraded across all tested FBS concentrations, whereas TDN-R-UCU3 exhibited a concentration-dependent degradation profile with significantly enhanced stability (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S4A). To further characterize this protection, we investigated the time-course degradation kinetics in 10% FBS. Notably, naked R-UCU3 was fully degraded within only 30 min. In striking contrast, the TDN-R-UCU3 complex retained ~12% integrity even after 12 h of incubation (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S4B). Notably, the degradation profile of TDN-R-UCU3 exhibited a biphasic pattern, characterized by an initial rapid decay followed by a slower degradation phase, which may reflect differential accessibility of exposed versus structurally protected regions within the nanostructure. These findings indicate that the TDN framework provides substantial structural protection to the sup-tRNA cargo, thereby enhancing its stability under serum conditions and potentially supporting its eventual accumulation in renal tissues.

The therapeutic efficacy of sup-tRNA is fundamentally dependent on the ability of the delivery vehicle to be internalized by target cells, particularly those within the renal system. To evaluate the uptake kinetics of TDN-R-UCU3, we incubated the complex with HEK293T cells for varying durations. Confocal microscopy revealed distinct intracellular co-localization signals at 12 h post-incubation, confirming the successful internalization of the TDN-sup-tRNA complex (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. S4C). To more closely recapitulate the in vivo renal environment, we further assessed the uptake efficiency in a mouse glomerular podocyte cell line MPC5 (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S4D). Consistent with the observations in HEK293T cells, robust cellular internalization of TDN-R-UCU3 was documented at the 12-h mark. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the TDN platform not only provides effective loading and structural protection for R-UCU3 but also facilitates its efficient entry into target renal cells, laying a critical foundation for its in vivo application.

TDN-sup-tRNA ameliorates glomerular pathologies in Col4a5-R471X AS mice

To evaluate the in vivo renal-targeting efficiency of TDN-R-UCU3, we administered Cy5-labeled TDN-R-UCU3 via tail-vein injection to characterize its biodistribution (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs revealed that Cy5-TDN-UCU3 initially localized in the liver within the first 3 h post-administration, followed by rapid and preferential accumulation in the kidneys. By 24 h, Cy5-TDN-UCU3 exhibited highly specific renal enrichment, with signal intensities significantly surpassing those in other tissues (Supplementary Fig. 5B and C).

Having confirmed the renal targeting of the TDN platform, we compared its efficacy with an AAV-based delivery system, as AAV is a conventional vector for sup-tRNA delivery [14]. We generated an AAV2/9-sup-tRNA formulation (AAV-R-TCT3) as a control (Supplementary Fig. S5D). For in vivo evaluation, 10-week-old Col4a5-R471X (X = TGA) AS mice were treated with TDN-R-UCU3 (10 µg/mouse) via tail-vein injection every 3 days for 1 month. In parallel, the AAV-R-TCT3 group received a single tail-vein injection at a dose of 2.5 × 1011 vg/mouse. Therapeutic efficacy was assessed 1 month after treatment initiation (Fig. 4A). Quantitative mCherry analysis of AAV-R-TCT3-treated mice showed no significant difference in viral distribution between the liver, heart, and kidneys (Supplementary Fig. S5E). These results further highlight the pronounced renal-targeting advantage of the TDN platform over traditional viral vectors. To exclude the possibility that the observed readthrough activity is attributable to the delivery vehicles themselves, we included vector-only controls for both TDN and AAV systems. Neither TDN-vector nor AAV-vector treatments elicited detectable readthrough activity in the reporter assay, as evidenced by minimal fluorescence signals and quantitative analysis (Supplementary Fig. S5F and G). These findings confirm that the observed suppression is specifically mediated by the delivered sup-tRNA rather than nonspecific effects of the delivery platforms. To further evaluate whether the therapeutic effects observed in vivo are specifically attributable to sup-tRNA activity, we assessed renal function in mice treated with vector-only controls. Neither TDN-vector nor AAV-vector treatment resulted in improvement of renal function, as indicated by unchanged urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (U-ALB/U-CRE) compared to untreated AS mice (Supplementary Fig. S5H). These results further support that the observed therapeutic benefit is dependent on the functional activity of the delivered sup-tRNA rather than nonspecific effects of the delivery vehicles.

At the molecular level, IF analysis revealed robust and continuous expression of the COL4A5 protein within the glomeruli of TDN-R-UCU3-treated AS mice. In stark contrast, AAV-R-TCT3-treated mice exhibited negligible protein signals, similar to the untreated AS group (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 6A). Quantitatively, Col4a5 mRNA levels in the TDN-R-UCU3 group were approximately two-fold higher than those in untreated AS mice, reaching ∼40% of WT levels (Supplementary Fig. 6B). These findings indicate that TDN-mediated delivery of sup-tRNA not only restores COL4A5 protein expression but is also associated with increased transcript abundance. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining further corroborated the superior restorative effect of TDN-R-UCU3 compared to the AAV-R-TCT-3 group, which showed no significant improvement over untreated controls (Supplementary Fig. 6C).

We then investigated whether the re-expression of COL4A5 translated into structural and functional recovery of the GBM, the hallmark site of AS pathology [38]. TEM revealed that while untreated and AAV-treated AS mice exhibited characteristic GBM anomalies—including lamellation, fragmentation, and irregular thickening—the GBM of TDN-R-UCU3-treated mice remained remarkably intact and uniform, closely resembling that of WT mice. Quantitative morphometric analysis confirmed these observations: TDN-R-UCU3 treatment significantly reduced GBM thickness (Fig. 4C and D), restored the FPN per unit length (μm) (Fig. 4E), and significantly decreased the FPW (Fig. 4F). No statistically significant improvements in these parameters were observed in the AAV-R-TCT3 group.

Finally, we assessed the recovery of the glomerular filtration barrier function. After one month of treatment, urine samples were collected for protein analysis via Coomassie brilliant blue staining. Notably, the massive proteinuria observed in untreated and AAV-treated AS mice was nearly abolished in the TDN-R-UCU3 treatment group, which displayed protein profiles comparable to WT mice (Fig. 4G). This indicates that TDN-R-UCU3 treatment effectively reconstitutes the glomerular filtration barrier and restores renal function, whereas AAV-mediated delivery at the tested dose fails to provide therapeutic benefit in this context.

TDN-sup-tRNA treatment comprehensively ameliorates renal dysfunction and mitigates pathological damage in Col4a5-R471X AS mice

To fully evaluate the therapeutic impact of the TDN-sup-tRNA strategy on nonsense mutation-mediated AS, we conducted a systematic assessment of renal function across the experimental cohorts [39, 40]. Analysis of core physiological parameters revealed that TDN-R-UCU3 treatment significantly reduced the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (Alb/Cre) (Fig. 5A), indicating effective mitigation of glomerular filtration barrier hyperpermeability and a substantial decrease in proteinuria. Concurrently, serum biochemical markers reflecting glomerular filtration and metabolic waste clearance showed synchronized improvement. Compared to untreated AS mice, the TDN-R-UCU3 group exhibited significantly lower levels of serum creatinine (SCr) and BUN (Fig. 5B and C), while serum total protein (TP) levels exhibited a recovery trend (Fig. 5D). These results demonstrate that TDN-R-UCU3 treatment comprehensively restores the filtration capacity and metabolic homeostasis of AS mice, whereas AAV-R-TCT3 treatment failed to yield statistically significant improvements.

Further histological examination revealed that untreated AS glomeruli exhibited characteristic structural disorganization, including capillary loop collapse, mesangial expansion, and glomerular size heterogeneity. In contrast, glomeruli in the TDN-R-UCU3-treated group displayed enhanced structural integrity, regular morphology, and a more uniform distribution pattern, closely approximating the histological features of WT mice (Fig. 5E). Quantitative analysis of the glomerular area confirmed that TDN-R-UCU3 treatment corrected the abnormal area distribution typical of AS pathology (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, semi-quantitative kidney injury scoring based on H&E staining demonstrated that TDN-R-UCU3 significantly alleviated both glomerular damage and inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 5G).

Given the pivotal role of fibrosis in AS progression, we evaluated extracellular matrix deposition. Masson’s trichrome staining showed prominent collagen accumulation in both the glomerular and interstitial regions of untreated AS mice, which was significantly attenuated following TDN-R-UCU3 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Quantitative analysis of the CVF further confirmed the anti-fibrotic efficacy of the therapy (Fig. 5H). Additionally, PAS staining revealed a significant reduction in mesangial matrix expansion (Supplementary Fig. S7B and Fig. 5I), providing further evidence of structural stabilization. Collectively, these functional and histological findings corroborate the molecular restoration of COL4A5 and the ultrastructural improvements observed via TEM, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of the TDN-sup-tRNA platform. Notably, the AAV-R-TCT3 group consistently failed to show significant differences compared to the untreated AS controls across all evaluated parameters.

TDN-sup-tRNA exhibits favorable biocompatibility and no observable systemic toxicity

To evaluate the feasibility of the TDN-sup-tRNA platform as a long-term therapeutic regimen, we systematically assessed its safety profile and potential immunogenicity under repeated in vivo administration. Histopathological analysis via H&E staining of major organs—including the heart, liver, spleen, and lungs—revealed no evidence of inflammatory cell infiltration, tissue necrosis, structural destruction, or abnormal remodeling in TDN-R-UCU3-treated AS mice compared to WT controls (Supplementary Fig. S8). Furthermore, no significant differences in body weight were observed across all experimental cohorts throughout the dosing cycle (Fig. 5J), suggesting that neither repeated intravenous injections of TDN-sup-tRNA nor single administration of AAV-sup-tRNA adversely affected the general physiological state of the animals.

Recognizing the pivotal role of chronic inflammation in AS progression, we further examined the impact of our therapy on disease-associated inflammatory states. Notably, TDN-R-UCU3 treatment led to a downward trend in renal injury-related pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and TNF-α (Fig. 5K and L). This anti-inflammatory effect is highly consistent with the observed improvements in renal architecture and function. To exclude the possibility of non-specific immune activation induced by the TDN scaffold itself, we measured IL-6 levels as a marker for both innate and adaptive immune responses. Serum IL-6 levels in TDN-R-UCU3-treated mice showed no statistical difference compared to WT mice (Fig. 5M), indicating that the TDN-R-UCU3 complex does not trigger a significant systemic immune response.

In summary, comprehensive evaluations spanning renal function, histological integrity, fibrotic progression, multi-organ safety, and systemic immunogenicity collectively demonstrate that TDN-R-UCU3 possesses excellent biocompatibility and a robust safety profile. By achieving substantial structural and functional rescue under repeated dosing, these findings provide compelling evidence for TDN-sup-tRNA as a viable and safe non-viral RNA therapeutic strategy for genetic disorders.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a TDN-based delivery strategy for sup-tRNAs aimed at treating nonsense mutation-mediated diseases with renal involvement. Through a systematic in vitro screening, we identified a lead sup-tRNA candidate (R-TCT3 [DNA] and R-UCU3 [RNA], respectively) that exhibits robust readthrough efficiency across diverse PTC sequence contexts. Previous studies have demonstrated that the efficiency of nonsense suppression is strongly influenced by the nucleotide context surrounding premature termination codons [41]. In contrast, we observed relatively consistent readthrough efficiencies across multiple COL4A5 UGA variants, with all sites reaching ~50% of wild-type levels. Several factors may contribute to this observation. First, all mutations examined in this study share the same stop codon (UGA) and are evaluated within a common COL4A5 reporter framework, which may limit variability in local sequence context. Second, the sup-tRNA identified in this study (R-TCT-3) exhibits relatively strong activity, which may impose a ceiling effect under high-expression conditions, thereby masking more subtle context-dependent differences. Third, the use of a CMV-driven cDNA reporter system, which lacks intron-dependent regulatory features such as exon junction complexes and is expressed at high levels, may reduce the influence of endogenous regulatory mechanisms on translation termination. Together, these factors may contribute to the relatively uniform readthrough observed in our system.

Curiously, the biphasic degradation behavior observed for TDN-R-UCU3 may reflect differential accessibility of structural components, where initially exposed or unprotected regions are more susceptible to nuclease digestion, followed by a slower degradation phase likely due to the partial protection conferred by the tetrahedral framework. Similar biphasic degradation patterns have been reported for structured nucleic acid nanomaterials [42, 43].

When formulated as TDN-R-UCU3 and administered to Col4a5-R471X AS mice over a 1-month period, the therapy successfully restored COL4A5 expression, significantly ameliorated renal dysfunction, reduced pathological injury, and demonstrated a favorable systemic safety profile.

Significant efforts in the field of therapeutical sup-tRNA have focused on enhancing the readthrough efficiency of sup-tRNAs. Given that a sup-tRNA undergoes a complex journey—from aminoacylation by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (aaRS) to its recruitment to the ribosomal A site via interactions with elongation factor 1-alpha (eEF1A)—its performance is governed by a multitude of biochemical checkpoints [13, 44]. The high readthrough efficiency observed in our reporter assays represents an apparent readout; however, the precise molecular determinants underlying these percentages remain partially elusive. Variations in readthrough efficiency may stem from differential affinities for aaRS, varied stability of the eEF1A-GTP-tRNA ternary complex [17], or the kinetics of codon-anticodon interaction within the ribosome [45, 46]. Nevertheless, it is evident that these functional “secrets” are encoded within the tRNA primary sequence and secondary structure [13, 44, 47]. For instance, R-TCT3 and R-TCT2 differ by only a single nucleotide at position 59 (Fig. 1C), yet their readthrough efficiencies differ two-fold (Fig. 1D and F). Whether this disparity arises from altered eEF1A interaction or other structural constraints remains an intriguing question for further structural biology investigations.

The clinical translation of sup-tRNA is inseparable from effective delivery. Although AAV2/9 possesses some renal transduction capacity [48], AAV-R-TCT3 failed to produce significant therapeutic benefits in AS model. In contrast, TDN-R-UCU3, which specifically accumulates in the kidney, demonstrated marked efficacy. This discrepancy underscores that the therapeutic success of sup-tRNA is highly context-dependent, predicated on both the target disease and the delivery modality. While recent advancements often frame sup-tRNA as a disease-agnostic therapeutic [10, 11, 15–17, 49–51], this remains an idealized vision that assumes the sup-tRNA can readily access the affected cells. Our findings suggest that the translational potential of sup-tRNA therapy is fundamentally constrained by the current state of delivery technology.

Finally, as this study utilized the RNA form of sup-tRNA, the relatively short half-life remains a limitation. Since this work serves as a proof-of-concept for delivery and treatment, future optimizations—such as the incorporation of chemical modifications into both the TDN scaffold [52, 53] and the sup-tRNA cargo—could significantly enhance metabolic stability and translational potential [54]. Importantly, the safety profile of engineered sup-tRNAs has been increasingly supported by recent studies. Multiple independent investigations using high-resolution ribosome profiling (Ribo-seq) and quantitative proteomics have shown that sup-tRNAs exhibit minimal off-target readthrough at natural termination codons and do not substantially perturb global translational fidelity or proteome integrity [13, 14, 16–18, 49, 55]. This high selectivity is thought to arise from the strong competition between release factors and sup-tRNAs at authentic stop codons, thereby limiting unintended readthrough under physiological conditions. Although our focus was limited to AS, the TDN-sup-tRNA strategy established here provides a versatile framework for combining DNA nanotechnology with sup-tRNA to treat a broader spectrum of genetic disorders.
