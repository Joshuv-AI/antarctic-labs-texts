Functional validation of driver mutation‐specific uveal melanoma biomarkers: role of COL9A3 in cancer cell plasticity
Authors: QCC van den Bosch, RM Verdijk, H Fadlelseed, TPP van den Bosch, S Owens, S Kennedy, E Kilic, E Brosens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUveal melanoma (UM) is a deadly ocular malignancy with well‐described genetic alterations that predict disease outcome. However, our current understanding of the biological underpinnings of high‐risk uveal melanoma progression remains relatively limited. Using RNA expression profiles from 250 patients with UM, we identified 12 novel biomarkers associated with high‐risk UM, with protein expression level validation of the top two candidates: the transcriptional regulator RBFOX2 and matrix protein COL9A3. Moreover, we investigated the functional contribution of COL9A3 via its overexpression in EIF1AX mut and BAP1 mut UM cell lines. Our data suggest that COL9A3 enhances cell motility and cell proliferation and alters morphology specifically in BAP1 mut UM cells. Furthermore, zebrafish xenograft studies revealed increased cell dissemination in a EIF1AX mut UM cell line upon overexpression of COL9A3, whereas a BAP1 mut UM cell line remained unchanged under wild‐type conditions. Interestingly, RNA sequencing revealed a high‐stress profile by upregulated metabolic activity and loss of protein translation due to COL9A3 overexpression. BAP1 mut UM appears to adapt to this stress by upregulation of plasticity markers, such as CD44, Nestin, EZH2, ABCB5, PAX3 and CD166, as a coping mechanism. These markers are known as differentiation markers during the development of melanocyte biogenesis and are implicated in plasticity in other high‐risk cancers. The relationship between COL9A3 and plasticity was validated by multiplex immunohistochemistry of formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue, which demonstrated colocalization of COL9A3, CD44 and Nestin. BAP1 mut‐UM samples presented higher levels of COL9A3, CD44 and Nestin expression than EIF1AX mut or SF3B1 mut UM samples based on the mean fluorescent intensity of each marker. A positive correlation between COL9A3 and Nestin expression in triple‐positive cells, irrespective of the UM subtype, was observed. Collectively, our findings suggest that COL9A3 is a novel high‐risk biomarker that may contribute to UM cell plasticity and is most prevalent in BAP1 mut UM. © 2026 The Author(s). Introduction

Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most common primary intraocular malignancy, with established driver and secondary mutations that predict disease progression [1]. UM is a deadly malignancy with a high probability of metastatic disease [2], and clinical care has limited treatment options available. Despite excellent control of primary tumors, once metastatic disease occurs, only a subset of patients are eligible for immunotherapy. The main eligibility criterion is human leukocyte antigen (HLA) compatibility with respect to treatment with the immunotherapeutic agent tebentafusp [3]. For over 40 years, in vivo models have failed to improve therapeutic options for metastatic disease, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of UM biology [4].

The key elements in tumor progression are proliferation at the primary site followed by dedifferentiation toward a more stem‐like cell state to allow for self‐renewal and migratory and invasive behavior [5]. This is a crucial process necessary for metastatic disease and is generally understudied in UM. UM is a well‐defined genetically characterized tumor, with driver mutations typically arising in GNAQ or GNA11 or at a lower frequency in PLCB4 or CYSLTR2. Driver mutations are found in nevi [6, 7], suggesting that for UM to flourish, secondary mutations are key to unlocking phenotypic plasticity. The core secondary mutations in UM are generally point mutations in EIF1AX, hotspot mutations in R625 in SF3B1 or loss‐of‐function mutations in BAP1 [8, 9]. Secondary mutations are strongly associated with disease progression, and patients with UM are subdivided into low‐risk (EIF1AX mut), intermediate‐risk (SF3B1 mut), and high‐risk (BAP1 mut) patients [10, 11]. As secondary mutations are generally mutually exclusive and result in distinct disease progression, we hypothesize that key elements involved in early tumor progression and local invasion are dependent on mutational subtype. This hypothesis is supported by our previous work illustrating methylation‐specific profiles that cluster to their representative secondary mutations [12]. Additionally, metastatic primary UM and matched UM metastases present similar epigenetic profiles, suggesting that metastatic capacity develops early in primary UM. However, epigenetic alterations unique to metastasis are patient specific [12].

Cancer is a heterogeneous disease in which tumor cells exist in different transcriptional states [13]. We hypothesized that the dedifferentiation of cancer cells toward a more stem‐cell‐like state is dependent on the cell of origin, whereby intermediate states of embryonic‐like pluripotency may mimic early differentiation subtypes of the originating cell lineage. To identify regulatory genes involved in the early development of uveal melanocytes, we previously identified a panel of genes involved in the early development of ocular pigmentation using zebrafish larvae as a model system [14]. More recently, comparative analysis between normal uveal melanocytes and two aggressive PDX models demonstrated upregulation of PRAME, EPHA4, and EZH2 [15]. Of particular interest was the upregulation of EZH2, which has been implicated in the development of cancer stem cells [16]. Owing to mutation‐specific disease progression, we hypothesized that early uveal melanocytic developmental genes upregulated in high‐risk UM may reveal mutation‐specific dedifferentiation processes through functional analysis of novel biomarkers and may subsequently serve as potential therapeutic targets. Here, we evaluated 480 genes implicated in early ocular pigmentation in a mutation‐specific manner in three independent RNA‐seq cohorts and validated protein expression of the top candidate markers in two patient cohorts. Finally, we performed functional analysis of the validated biomarker COL9A3 and validated the experimental findings in patient tissue.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and patient consent

Patients provided consent for the use of their clinical data and tumor tissue for research purposes. All clinical data presented were anonymized. All zebrafish experiments were performed in accordance with the European Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. According to this directive, experiments utilizing larval zebrafish up to 120 h post‐fertilization (hpf) do not constitute animal procedures and do not require formal legal or institutional animal ethics permit approvals, as the larvae are not yet independently feeding. The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee at Erasmus MC, Rotterdam, The Netherlands.

Discovery of novel biomarkers and identification of altered pathways using RNA‐seq

Novel biomarkers were identified through reanalysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) uveal melanoma cohort and previously published RNA‐seq data from two independent cohorts of the Rotterdam Ocular Melanoma Studygroup (ROMS) [17]. A total of 250 samples were studied comprising RNA‐seq cohorts (ROMS1, n = 26; ROMS2, n = 41; TCGA, n = 80) and validation cohorts derived from RNA‐microarray datasets (ROMS‐array, n = 50; GSE22138, n = 53 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE22138)). The gene expression of previously discovered genes implicated in early vertebrate ocular pigmentation development [14] was studied by comparing the gene expression of 480 genes (supplementary material, File S1) between molecularly distinct subgroups.

Total RNA profiling of primary tumors and UM cell lines

Two independent total RNA sequencing experiments were performed. First, in the discovery cohort (ROMS1), we compared BAP1‐mutated primary tumors (n = 14), SF3B1‐mutated primary tumors (n = 11) and EIF1AX‐mutated primary tumors (n = 9) (supplementary material, Files S2 and S3). Differential expression analysis was performed on primary tumor samples with a known secondary driver mutation or negative BAP1 immunostaining. We replicated the signals from this experiment with an independent second cohort (ROMS2) of BAP1‐mutated primary tumors (n = 7), SF3B1‐mutated tumors (n = 12), and EIF1AX‐mutated primary tumors (n = 7) (supplementary material, Files S4 and S5 [17]). The replication cohort has been previously described, and the data were reanalyzed using the same analysis settings as the discovery cohort. Replicated differentially expressed molecules in the BAP1, SF3B1, and EIF1AX groups were validated in the corresponding groups of the TCGA cohort BAP1 (n = 23), SF3B1 (n = 15), and EIF1AX (n = 9) with a known secondary driver mutation (supplementary material, Files S6 and S7 [18]). These replicated and validated RNA biomarkers were considered in downstream analysis. Clinical data and RNA expression values can be found in the Supplementary Materials and Methods. For the human UM cell lines 92‐1‐GFP, 92‐1‐COL9A3‐GFP, MP46‐GFP, and MP46‐COL9A3‐GFP, total RNA sequencing experiments were performed in triplicate, with variation in their culture passage, and separated by at least 1 week of culture between each replicate. Following data normalization and differential gene expression between the GFP and COL9A3‐GFP groups, significantly differentially expressed genes were selected for downstream pathway analysis. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (QIAGEN, Aarhus, Denmark) was used for downstream pathway analysis of UM cell lines. All analyses were performed using the same settings, applying a minimal fold‐change threshold of −2 or 2 and a false discovery rate of p < 0.05.

Reanalysis of the mRNA microarray validation cohort

Two datasets were included in this analysis. The first, previously described by Laurent et al, included 53 patients with UM [19] (supplementary material, Files S8 and S9). Data (GSE22138) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [20]. The second dataset (ROMS‐array), previously generated by our group, included 50 patients [21] (supplementary material, Files S10 and S11). The downloaded raw data files (CELs) were processed using brb‐array tools v4 6.2 [22] and the Just GCRMA (GC content–Robust Multi‐Array Average) algorithm to adjust for background intensities. The data were normalized by quantile normalization, variance stabilization, and log2 transformation. Genes and markers whose expression differed by at least 1.5‐fold from the median in at least 20% of the arrays were retained. In contrast to our in‐house dataset, the metadata for GSE22138 did not include BAP1, EIF1AX, or SF3B1 mutation status. To compare datasets, we applied the 15‐gene expression panel described by Onken et al [23] to predict prognostic class. Classification into gene expression profile (GEP) class 1 or GEP class 2 was performed using five models: compound covariate predictor [24], diagonal linear discriminant analysis [25], nearest neighbor classification [25], support vector machines with a linear kernel [26], and the Bayesian compound covariate predictor. Clinical data and RNA expression values are provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Patient inclusion criteria for protein analysis

Protein validation of mutation‐specific RNA biomarkers was initially tested on a set of patients from the ROMS cohort between 2010 and 2023 (n = 52). The criteria for patient inclusion were a known mutation status or chromosomal profile, sufficient formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tumor material, and an appropriate follow‐up time. Following initial immunohistochemical analysis of the top five markers, additional UM samples meeting the same inclusion criteria from the Dublin‐based cohort (n = 119) were included. Additionally, all samples were stained for BAP1 and scored by both Rotterdam (QB, RM) and Dublin (HF, SO, SK); only samples with concordant scoring were used in this study.

Immunohistochemistry of PTP4A3, SLC11A2, DHX16, RBFOX2, and COL9A3

Immunohistochemistry was carried out using an automated, validated, and accredited staining system (Ventana Benchmark ULTRA, Roche Tissue Diagnostics, Tucson, AZ, USA) combined with an ultraview Universal Alkaline Phosphatase Red Detection Kit (Ventana Cat. No. 760‐501, Roche Tissue Diagnostics). Following deparaffinization and heat‐induced antigen retrieval (CC1 for 32 min), the tissue samples were incubated for 32 min with primary antibodies against PTP4A3 (1:3,200 Abcam, Cambridge, UK, Cat. No. ab50276), SLC11A2 (1:1,600, Abcam, Cat. No. ab140977), DHX16 (1:200, ThermoFisher Scientific, Bleiswijk, The Netherlands, Cat. No. PA5‐62411), RBFOX2 (1:400, ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat. No. PA5‐52268), or COL9A3 (1:100, Sigma‐Aldrich, Amsterdam, The Netherlands Cat. No. HPA040125). Counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin II stain for 12 min and blue coloring reagent for 8 min following the manufacturer's protocol (Ventana Benchmark ULTRA, Roche Tissue Diagnostics). For each antibody, an appropriate control tissue sample was used as a positive control.

Chromogenic multiplex immunohistochemistry of COL9A3, SOX10, and PU.1

Multiplex immunohistochemistry was carried out via an automated staining system (Ventana Discovery, Roche Tissue Diagnostics). Following deparaffinization and heat‐induced antigen retrieval (CC1, for 32 min), the tissue samples were incubated with COL9A3 (1:100, Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat. No. HPA040125) and detected using a Discovery Purple kit (Ventana Medical Systems, Roche Tissue Diagnostics, Cat. No. 760‐229). The COL9A3 antibody was subsequently stripped by incubation with CC2 for 8 min at 100°C. The next antibody used was PU.1 (1:800, Abcam, Cat. No. ab76543), was incubated for 32 min and detected via a Discovery Yellow kit (Ventana, Roche Tissue Diagnostics, Cat. No. 760‐239). Finally, after an additional antibody stripping step, SOX10 (ready‐to‐use, Cell Marque, Rocklin, CA, USA, Cat. No. 760‐4968) was incubated for 32 min and detected via a Discovery Teal kit (Ventana, Roche Tissue Diagnostics, Cat. No. 760‐247). The samples were not counterstained to prevent interpretation artifacts between blue‐colored hematoxylin nuclei and teal‐colored SOX10‐positive nuclei.

Fluorescent multiplex immunohistochemistry of COL9A3, CD44, and Nestin

Multiplex immunohistochemistry was carried out using an automated staining system (Ventana Discovery, Ventana Medical Systems). Following deparaffinization and heat‐induced antigen retrieval (CC1, for 32 min), the tissue samples were incubated with COL9A3 (1:100, Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat. No. HPA040125) and detected using a Discovery Red 610 kit (Ventana, Roche Tissue Diagnostics, Cat. No. 760‐245). The COL9A3 antibody was subsequently stripped by incubation with CC2 for 8 min at 100°C. The next antibody, Nestin (1:25,600; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA, Cat. No. NB300‐265), was incubated for 32 min and detected using a Discovery Cy5 kit (Ventana, Roche Tissue Diagnostics, Cat. No. 760‐238). Finally, after an additional antibody stripping step, CD44 (1:4,000, Novus Biologicals, Cat. No. NBP1‐47390) was incubated for 32 min and detected via a Discovery FAM kit (Ventana, Roche Tissue Diagnostics, Cat. No. 760‐243). The slides were mounted with mounting medium containing DAPI (Abcam, ab104139) to visualize the nucleus.

Generation of COL9A3‐overexpressing UM cell lines

Synthetic COL9A3 cDNA from pGEM‐COL9A3 (Sino Biological Europe GmbH, Eschborn, Germany, Cat. No. HG13717‐G) was amplified and cloned and inserted into pTFORF2004 (Addgene, Watertown MA, USA Cat. No. 143330) by replacing the original cDNA insert via HiFi assembly (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA, Cat. No. E2621S). Additionally, the pCOL9A3 plasmid was adapted to express enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) for selection and imaging purposes by removing the puromycin coding region with a CMV‐GFP insert from pCMV‐GFP (Addgene, Cat. No. 11153). Lentiviral production was achieved by transfecting HEK293T cells with pMDLg/RRE (Addgene, Cat. No. 122511), pMD2. G (Addgene, Cat. No. 12259), and pRSV‐Rev (Addgene, Cat. No. 12253) together with pCOL9A3‐eGFP for 48 h at 37 °C in DMEM (Gibco/ThermoFisher Scientific, Grand Island, NY, USA, Cat. No. 41965047) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Gibco/ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat. No. A384001) and 1% non‐essential amino acids (Gibco/ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat. No. 11140035). Media containing viral particles were filtered to remove cell debris and sequentially used to transduce 92‐1 cells and MP46 cells. After 24 h, transduction was validated using fluorescence microscopy, and eGFP+ cells were isolated by fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) (Aria) to obtain stable cell lines overexpressing COL9A3 and eGFP.

Cell culture conditions

The melanoma cell lines 92‐1 (established at the Leiden University Medical Center, Leiden, The Netherlands [27]) and MP46 (established at the Institute Curie, Paris, France [28]) were used. The chromosomal aberrations and mutation status and research resource identifiers of all the cell lines can be found in the supplementary material, Table S1. Melanoma cell lines were authenticated previously by single nuclear polymorphism (SNP) analysis and the AmpFLSTR Identifiler Plus PCR Amplification Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific), followed by sequencing. All uveal melanocytes were propagated in RPMI supplemented with 20% heat‐inactivated fetal calf serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2‐enriched atmosphere and regularly checked for mycoplasma. For protein analysis of the cell cultures, the cells were centrifuged, fixed in 10% buffered formalin and mixed in 3% low‐melting agarose (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat. No. 16520100). Agar blocks were sequentially processed to obtain FFPE blocks for standardized immunohistochemistry as described above.

Cellular architecture and attachment analysis

A total of 2.5 × 105 cells were seeded in 48‐well plates and imaged on an Olympus IX‐70 (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at the same coordinates at 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 20, and 48 h post‐seeding. A total of five wells per cell line were investigated. The obtained images were stacked and analyzed in FIJI (Open‐source platform; https://imagej.github.io/software/fiji/ [29]) to manually count the number of attached and unattached cells. Two additional images per well were subsequently obtained at 48 h post‐seeding to analyze the shapes of 40 attached cells per cell line. Cell detection was performed manually in FIJI to measure the cell perimeter, Ferret diameter, and circularity. The resulting values were processed in GraphPad Prism V9 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) applying the Mann–Whitney U test to compare knockout cells with wildtype (WT) cells.

Cell proliferation

Total cell proliferation was quantified using the Opera Phenix high‐content screening system (Revvity, Groningen, The Netherlands). A total of 2,000 cells per cell line were seeded per well five times in a 96‐well plate. Automated detection of the cell surface obtained at 0 h served as the starting point for quantification. Serial images were obtained every 12 h for a total of 72 h. Growth was determined by normalizing the data on the basis of the overall surface coverage at 0 h and plotting the data in GraphPad V9 (GraphPad Software) to determine the duplication time points of each cell line.

Wound healing assay

Migration capacity was investigated using a 2D invasion model by seeding UM cells and fibroblasts in a culture insert (Ibidi, Gräfelfing, Germany, Cat. No. 80366). After 24 h, the insert was removed and the cells were imaged for 24 h in a temperature, humidity, and CO2 controlled environment via a Nikon Eclipse Ti‐E inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon Instruments, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Every 10 min, images were captured at multiple locations along the cell‐free gap between UM cells and fibroblasts. Time‐lapse images were analyzed in FIJI [29] to quantify the migratory paths of 16 cells per cell line. Finally, the acquired data were statistically analyzed in GraphPad Prism V9 (GraphPad Software) using one‐way ANOVA with post hoc Dunnett's test.

Zebrafish xenograft models

Wild‐type AB zebrafish were maintained under standard conditions with a 14 h light and 10 h dark cycle. In this study, only larval zebrafish (no older than 120 h post‐fertilization) were used. Zebrafish embryos were raised in E3 medium supplemented with 0.003% phenylthiourea in a Petri dish at 28 °C. At 24 h post‐fertilization, the medium was changed following dichlorination of the larvae. At 48 h post‐fertilization, zebrafish larvae were anesthetized with 0.016% tricane and used for injections. A total of 2.5 × 106 cells were harvested and stained with 2.5 μm CellTracker CM‐Dil dye (Invitrogen/ThermoFisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 5 min at 37 °C and then for an additional 15 min at 4°C. After staining, the CM‐Dil dye was removed by centrifugation. The cells were then washed with PBS and resuspended in 2% PVP‐40/PBS. A total of approximately 200–300 cells were injected into the perivitelline space. Successfully injected larvae were selected 1 h post‐injection under Leica M165 FC fluorescence stereomicroscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany), placed in E3 medium supplemented with PTU and maintained at 34°C. At 3 days post‐injection, xenograft larvae were anesthetized and embedded in 1% low‐melting agarose for live imaging.

Confocal microscopy of zebrafish xenografts

Zebrafish xenograft larvae were imaged using a Leica SP5 (Leica Camera, Wetzlar, Germany) under standard conditions (561 nm, 35% laser power with additional bright field images). Tile scans of three images were generated to obtain full‐body images for analysis. The number of disseminated cells, the distance of dissemination and the total tumor volume were quantified in FIJI using publicly available scripts, as previously described [30]. The resulting values were processed in GraphPad Prism V9 (GraphPad Software). Comparisons of the number of detected spots, distance of dissemination, and tumor volume between the different cell lines were performed using the Mann–Whitney U‐test to compare WT versus overexpression cells.

Results

Identification of SF3B1 ‐specific and BAP1 ‐associated RNA biomarkers

Raw RNA‐seq data were extracted from the TCGA cohort, ROMS‐1, and ROMS‐2 cohort to be aligned and normalized via CLC Bio to make the datasets comparable with each other. Initially, the gene expression (TPM values) of 482 genes previously identified [14] were compared between molecular subtypes in the ROMS1 cohort and those validated in the TCGA cohort (supplementary material, Figure S1). Among the 482 genes, a total of 13 significantly discriminated biomarkers were identified. We identified one upregulated SF3B1‐specific RNA biomarker, DHX16 (Figure 1A–D), and multiple high‐risk biomarkers associated with BAP1 mut UM (the top 12 genes are plotted in Figure 1E). Most genes were clinically correlated with overall survival on the basis of RNA expression (supplementary material, Figure S2). For further investigations, the top‐ranking genes were selected on the basis of disease‐free survival (DFS) significance and the availability of FFPE‐validated antibodies for protein validation, resulting in a total of four biomarkers eligible for further investigation (supplementary material, Figure S1). COL9A3, PTP4A3, and RBFOX2 expression was first investigated in the RNA‐array dataset GSE22138 and our in‐house generated dataset [21]. Overall, a similar trend was observed, with the exception of SLC11A2 expression where higher expression was associated with better survival in the ROMS‐array cohort (supplementary material, Figure S3). Second, additional technical validation using microarray‐based datasets (GSE22138 and ROMS‐array) showed that the top four RNA biomarkers (COL9A3, PTP4A3, SLC11A2, and RBFOX2) were significantly associated with mutation status or GEP classification (supplementary material, Figures S3 and S4). The top four RNA biomarkers were also correlated with poor prognosis (Figure 1F–I) and were selected for further investigation together with one SF3B1‐specific RNA biomarker (DHX16).

Protein validation of COL9A3, RBFOX2, DHX16, and SLC11A2

Protein expression was determined by immunohistochemistry on FFPE tissue slides in a subset of the ROMS cohort as a discovery set and validated in the Dublin cohort. Initially, protein expression was validated in samples with known RNA data and available FFPE tissue. Protein expression was assessed using either a 0, 1+, 2+, and 3+ scoring system or absence/presence of staining by an ophthalmic pathologist. Immunohistochemical staining for DHX16, COL9A3, SLC11A2, and RBFOX2 confirmed higher protein expression in samples with known elevated RNA expression (supplementary material, Figure S5), whereas PTP4A3 staining showed elevated protein levels in only one out of three samples (supplementary material, Figure S6). Owing to the RNA–protein interaction of PTP4A3 and differences between its expression locations (nucleus or cytoplasm), this marker was not evaluated further in the large ROMS and Dublin cohorts. A previous study on PTP4A3 in UM also demonstrated variability in the staining pattern, with unclear localization and intensity scores. Subsequent functional studies were performed in OCM cell lines [19], which are derived from cutaneous melanoma, making translatability questionable regarding UM biology. Additionally, OCM cell lines have been identified as problematic cell lines on Cellosaurus. (https://www.cellosaurus.org/CVCL_6934). The COL9A3 staining pattern suggested the presence of potentially positive macrophages instead of cancer cells due to the foamy‐like appearance of positive cells (Figure 2A). To validate its expression in either macrophages or cancer cells, we utilized chromogenic multiplex staining with COL9A3 (in red), macrophage marker PU.1 (in yellow), and melanocyte marker SOX10 (in blue). Although the COL9A3‐positive cells appeared foamy, multiplex validation confirmed that expression was restricted to cancer cells (Figure 2B). The ROMS cohort was subsequently used as a discovery set comprising 52 FFPE samples to assess clinical relevance on the basis of protein expression. In the discovery cohort, RBFOX2 and COL9A3 protein expression were the only two markers that correlated with poor prognosis (supplementary material, Figure S6). Given that COL9A3 is a type of collagen, we investigated whether its expression correlated with extracellular matrix patterns, such as closed vascular loops. Fisher's exact test revealed a significant correlation between positive COL9A3 staining and the presence of closed vascular loops in 47 samples (p = 0.0031, supplementary material, Table S2). Additionally, positive COL9A3 staining was significantly correlated with the presence of epithelioid cells (p < 0.0001, supplementary material, Table S2), a known histological parameter for aggressive UM. DHX16 expression was not correlated with DFS; however, increased expression appeared to be elevated in both BAP1 mut and SF3B1 mut UM (supplementary material, Figure S7).

To validate our initial findings, a larger external cohort from Dublin (n = 119) was additionally stained for RBFOX2 and COL9A3. All tissue microarray (TMA) cores were independently scored by three pathologists and two researchers with extensive experience in ophthalmic histopathology. The obtained scores were compared and discordant scorings were discussed between the scorers to gain concordance between all the samples. Elevated RBFOX2 protein levels were significantly correlated with poor clinical outcome in both the ROMS cohort (Figure 2C) and the Dublin cohort (Figure 2D) and were significantly increased after combining both cohorts (Figure 2E). Similarly, COL9A3 protein expression was significantly correlated with clinical outcome in the ROMS cohort (Figure 2F) and the Dublin cohort (Figure 2G) and significantly increased after combining both cohorts (Figure 2H). The samples analyzed additionally presented typical clinical outcomes on the basis of BAP1 staining (Figure 2I).

Validation of COL9A3 overexpression and in vitro characterization in UM cell lines

To elucidate the role of COL9A3 in UM progression, stable cell lines overexpressing COL9A3 were established via lentiviral integration. Owing to the strong association between COL9A3 expression and BAP1 status, we investigated its role in both the BAP1 wild‐type (92‐1 cells) and a BAP1 mutant (MP46) UM cell line. Following FACS of GFP‐positive cells, immunocytochemical staining was carried out to validate the overexpression of COL9A3 in 92‐1 cells (Figure 3A,B) and MP46 cells (Figure 3C,D). Subsequent analysis of brightfield images for standard morphometrics revealed few alterations in 92‐1 cells compared with COL9A3‐overexpression cells (Figure 3E–H). Interestingly, overexpression of COL9A3 in 92‐1 cells resulted in significantly slower cell proliferation, with best fit doubling times of 26.07 h versus 64.70 h (Figure 3I). Conversely, overexpression of COL9A3 altered the morphology of MP46 cells, as the overexpression cells were significantly larger (Figure 3J–L).

To evaluate whether COL9A3 influences migration, a wound healing assay revealed a significant increase in the motility of MP46 cells overexpressing COL9A3 (Figure 3M; supplementary material, Figure S8C,D), whereas this increase was not observed in 92‐1 cells (Figure 3L; supplementary material, Figure S8A,B). Moreover, overexpression of COL9A3 significantly altered the doubling time of MP46 cells, with best fit doubling times of 182.8 versus 108.2 h (Figure 3N).

In vivo characterization of COL9A3 overexpression in zebrafish xenografts

To further explore the oncogenic ability of COL9A3, we utilized our standardized zebrafish xenograft model [30] to evaluate the tumor burden and cell dissemination capacity in vivo. 92‐1‐COL9A3‐GFP cells did not increase the tumor burden, cell dissemination distance, or size of disseminated cell clusters; however, the number of disseminated cells was significantly greater than that of 92‐1‐GFP cells (Figure 4A–F). Interestingly, MP46‐COL9A3‐GFP xenografts did not increase the tumor burden, cell dissemination distance, number of disseminated cells, or size of disseminated cell clusters (Figure 4G–L). These findings suggest that there may be a threshold for the extent to which COL9A3 influences UM progression, as 92‐1‐GFP cells naturally exhibit lower COL9A3 expression, whereas MP46‐GFP cells exhibit higher expression (Figure 3A,C).

RNA sequencing reveals cellular stress in MP46 cells and suggests that plasticity is a coping mechanism

To understand the influence of COL9A3 in UM, we compared the transcriptomes of stable COL9A3 overexpression UM cell lines with those of GFP controls. Differential gene expression revealed a strong effect of overexpressing COL9A3 in MP46 cells, with a total of 2,895 differentially expressed genes with an FDR p value > 0.05 and a fold‐change > 2 or < −2, whereas the effect on 92‐1 cells was minimal, with a total of 91 differentially expressed genes (Figure 5A,B). Pathway analysis using ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) revealed an increase in protein kinase A signaling, the most significantly affected pathway, in 92‐1 cells (supplementary material, Figure S9A). However, owing to the large number of differentially expressed genes in MP46‐COL9A3‐GFP cells, many pathways were significantly altered. Overall, MP46‐COL9A3‐GFP cells exhibited a global loss of RNA translation and processing with additional mitochondrial dysfunction due to the loss of oxidative phosphorylation (supplementary material, Figure S9B). Additionally, significant alterations in cytoskeletal and cytoplasm organization were observed (supplementary material, Figure S9C), in line with our morphometric analysis and immunohistochemical staining patterns. A total of 24 overlapping differentially expressed genes were identified (supplementary material, Table S3) between 92‐1‐COL9A3‐GFP and MP46‐COL9A3‐GFP. Most genes were small nuclear RNAs involved in RNA biogenesis and translation [31], however pathway analysis did not suggest translation impairment in 92‐1‐COL9A3‐GFP cells, unlike in MP46‐COL9A3‐GFP cells (supplementary material, Figure S9A,B). These finding suggests high cellular stress specifically in MP46‐COL9A3‐GFP cells; however, our previous results demonstrated altered morphology and increased in vitro motility and proliferation, whereas in vivo behavior was comparable with that of MP46‐GFP cells. We hypothesized that plasticity markers – genes involved in stem cell processes that can drive dedifferentiation – may provide a mechanism by which MP46‐COL9A3‐GFP cells overcome cellular stress. Investigation of plasticity markers based on the current literature [32] suggests that MP46‐COL9A3‐GFP indeed upregulated multiple plasticity markers (Figure 5C–H).

Expression of COL9A3 is correlated with plasticity markers in patients with UM

Owing to the plasticity association of COL9A3, we investigated whether COL9A3 is involved in dedifferentiation or is a marker for plasticity. Therefore, we investigated the same ROMS‐TMA samples used in this study, which enabled analysis of 36 FFPE samples (n = 25 BAP1 mut, n = 7 SF3B1 mut, n = 4 EIF1AX mut) using multiplex immunofluorescence staining for COL9A3, CD44, and Nestin (Figure 6A). Cell classifiers on the basis of protein expression levels were developed via QuPath to identify single‐, double‐, and triple‐positive cells. Overall, the BAP1 samples presented the highest percentage of cells positive for COL9A3 alone (Figure 6B) and double positive for COL9A3 with CD44 (Figure 6C) or COL9A3 with Nestin (Figure 6D). Interestingly, the percentage of triple‐positive cells did not differ across mutation classes (Figure 6E). Given the greater sensitivity of immunofluorescence compared with chromogenic staining, we investigated protein expression levels at the single‐cell level across mutation subtypes. Expression of all markers was highest in the BAP1mut tumor cells (Figure 6F–I). Finally, given that our RNA data suggest a relationship between elevated COL9A3 expression and upregulated plasticity markers, we assessed whether triple‐positive cells show similar coexpression at the protein level. All triple‐positive cells were plotted per individual to obtain Spearman's correlations, which were sequentially plotted together on the basis of their mutational subtype (Figure 6I,J). Interestingly, we observed a generally weak–moderate correlation between COL9A3 and Nestin in all subgroups (Figure 6I), whereas on average, only a weak correlation between COL9A3 and CD44 was observed in SF3B1 mut tumor cells (Figure 6J).

Discussion

In this study, we describe novel biomarkers associated with disease progression in UM at both the RNA and protein levels. From a list of 480 genes, we identified the top 12 RNA markers that correlate with poor prognosis. All 12 markers were upregulated in BAP1 mut UM, suggesting that they may make functional contributions to aggressive behavior, particularly in a BAP1‐related context. Furthermore, two genes, RBFOX2 and COL9A3, were upregulated at the protein level. To evaluate functional contributions, this study investigated COL9A3 by manipulating UM cell lines and performing in vivo analysis of zebrafish xenografts, in a manner similar to our previously study demonstrating that RBFOX2 is involved in metastatic behavior [33]. Our results suggest that COL9A3 is involved in the plasticity of UM cancer cells, which was validated by multiplex immunohistochemistry.

COL9A3 encodes one of the alpha chains of the type IX collagen heterotrimeric molecule [34], which is part of fibril‐associated collagen [35]. Heterozygous autosomal dominant inheritance of an exon skipping variant or amino acid substitution may be associated with peripheral vitreoretinal degeneration, retinal detachment [36], whereas recessive loss of function causes Stickler syndrome [37]. In both cases, COL9A3 is unable to function properly; whereas no mutations have been described in UM to our knowledge. In UM, we describe overexpression of COL9A3, suggesting there is no loss of functionality but rather a biological contribution. The functional mechanisms of COL9A3 in cancer are currently poorly understood, although its association with high‐risk cancer is well established in several cancers. For example, elevated COL9A3 expression is associated with poor prognosis in colorectal cancer [38, 39, 40], gastric cancer [41], osteosarcoma [42], esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [43], salivary adenoid cystic carcinoma [44], and breast cancer [45]. Investigation of the ability of curcumin to suppress LGR5+ colorectal cancer stem cells revealed downregulation of COL9A3. Interestingly, curcumin was able to induce autophagy and inhibit the TFAP2A‐mediated ECM pathway, which in turn was shown to be involved in regulating TEAD2 and COL9A3. Moreover, TEAD2 alone has been implicated as a regulator of COL9A3 [40]. While this study supports our findings, in which COL9A3 is involved in cancer cell plasticity, it also suggests an additional relationship between COL9A3 and TEAD2. The interplay between COL9A3 and TEAD2 may warrant further investigation, as TEAD2 is a known binding partner of YAP [46], which is a key gene involved in UM etiology [1, 7]. Additionally, the extracellular matrix is known to be an important factor for stem cells, providing structural support and signals that regulate interactions between cells and extracellular matrix components [47]. Although our COL9A3 staining results clearly revealed intracellular expression, a correlation was also observed between COL9A3 expression and the presence of closed vascular loops, which is a known histological parameter associated with poor prognosis [48]. Therefore, the role of COL9A3 as a structural collagen and interaction protein is of particular interest because of its involvement in the plasticity of UM cells. Indeed, COL9A3 has been identified together with COL6A5 as a downstream extracellular matrix interaction molecule in gastric cancer. The role of COL9A3 was identified following studies on USP3 in gastric cancer. USP3 is a deubiquitylase that promotes cell migration and invasion [49], mediated through stabilization of COL9A3 and COL6A5 in a deubiquitinating‐dependent manner [41]. Furthermore, USP3 is a known chromatin modifier that regulates H2A and H2B [50], which are also targets of BAP1 [51]. This link between COL9A3 and a deubiquitylase is of particular interest, as our study suggests that COL9A3 is specifically relevant for BAP1 mut UM. Although little is known about COL9A3 at metastatic sites or its intracellular role in metastatic tumors, extracellular matrix proteins are key elements in creating, adapting, and actively driving tumor microenvironments for metastasis [52]. Future research on metastatic sites of UM may reveal whether COL9A3 is involved only in the initial phases of aggressive behavior, or whether it plays a more prominent role in seeding and establishing micro‐niches for UM metastasis.

As COL9A3 overexpression is associated with BAP1 UM, epithelioid cell type and closed vascular loops; future studies are needed to investigate whether COL9A3 can serve as a biomarker for metastatic disease using liquid biopsies. Collagen peptides have been described as predictors for carcinoma recurrence [53] and prognosis in ovarian cancer [54]. In fact, extracellular matrix‐derived biomarkers have been shown to play an important role in tumor diagnosis, prognosis, and personalized therapy [55]. Circulating tumor cells may lyse in the bloodstream, releasing intracellular COL9A3; however this needs to be studied in patient serum to provide clinical evidence and value.

In summary, our results suggest a role for COL9A3 in the plasticity of UM cells, which is supported by the current literature associating it with UM‐related genes. However, our validation in patient samples remains limited, as only two plasticity markers have been evaluated. We acknowledge that the absence of loss‐of‐function experiments represents a notable limitation of our study. While our overexpression data in two UM cell lines indicate that COL9A3 can modulate gene expression and influence cellular plasticity, complementary knockdown or knockout studies are essential to confirm whether COL9A3 is necessary for these effects, thereby establishing a causal relationship. However, there are practical challenges associated with performing such experiments in BAP1mut UM cells, including their slow doubling times and inefficient transfection rates. Nevertheless, incorporating loss‐of‐function approaches would allow us to directly test whether COL9A3 is essential for the aggressive behaviors observed, potentially clarifying its mechanistic role in cell plasticity, metastasis initiation, and interactions with extracellular matrix components. Such experiments could also validate whether targeting COL9A3 represents a viable therapeutic strategy, particularly in the context of BAP1mut tumors.

Additionally, investigations of the interactions of COL9A3 with TEAD2 and deubiquitylation are interesting and may provide novel insights into the plasticity and extracellular matrix interactions of BAP1 mut UM cells. Finally, a deeper understanding of the extracellular matrix and its interactions with UM cancer cells has therapeutic potential for drug development [56].
