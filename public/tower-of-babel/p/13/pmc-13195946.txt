The impact of social support on quality of life among older adults in times of COVID-19 pandemic in Dongguan, China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tingxian Wang, Shuqi Chen, Ziyue Fan, Juxiao Li, Yanwei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe study examined the impact of social support (SS) on quality of life (QoL) among older adults and whether these impacts varied across different levels of QoL during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsA cluster-random-sampling survey was adopted within two towns in Dongguan, China for two months during the epidemic lockdown. Demographic characteristics, SS and QoL were measured. The number of types of services provided by the community, and the frequency and benefits of participation in community activities were used to assess formal social support (FSS), and the Social Support Appraisals (SS-A) scale for informal social support (ISS). The 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36) was used to measure QoL. Multiple quantile regression adjusting for demographic characteristics variables was employed to explore the impact of SS on QoL among older adults.ResultsOne thousand one hundred thirty older adults (≥ 60 years) were included. The mean age was 78.97 years (± 8.99), over half of (53.45%) received one or two types of community-provided services. 49.29% participated in community activities occasionally. 60.80% perceived these activities as beneficial to their health. In terms of ISS, the mean (SD) scores for social support from family, friends, and others were 23.68 (3.27), 20.14 (2.81), and 15.27 (1.72). The mean PCS-T and MCS-T scores were 50.00 (SD: 6.87) and 50.00 (SD: 9.04), respectively. When controlling for confounders, the results showed that “often” participating in community activities demonstrated a significant positive association with PCS-T, with the associated attenuation at higher quartiles. SS from others exhibited a significant negative correlation only at the lowest quartile of the PCS-T(β= -0.61, p = 0.005). “Always” participating in community activities demonstrated a significant negative association with MCS-T at the highest quantile. SS from family exhibited a significant positive association with MCS-T at both the 5th (β = 0.77, p < 0.001) and 95th (β = 0.8, p < 0.001) quantiles, with SS from others showing a positive association specifically at the highest quantile (β = 0.51, p = 0.022).ConclusionsFormal community-based social support called for greater attention to the needs of older adults with a poorer quality of life, expanding its coverage, and enhancing the effectiveness of their participation in community activities. Fostering a harmonious and caring social and community environment proved to be a pathway to improving the quality of life for older adults during the pandemic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27248-w.

Introduction

China has experienced a faster population aging process than other countries [1]. 75% of older adults aged 60 years and above suffer from more than one chronic disease, while the number of disabled and partially disabled older adults exceeded 40 million [2, 3]. Additionally, the prevalence rate of depressive symptoms among older adults was 20.0% in China [4]. Previous study showed that healthy life expectancy was 77.4 years [5] and the quality of life (QoL) of older adults in China became an undeniable public health problem, which was exacerbated during Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, lockdown and quarantine measures led to increased social isolation, depressive symptoms and cognitive decline [6–9]. Additionally, restrictions on going outside reduced older adults’ mobility, which might contribute to greater frailty [10]. More importantly, older adults who were often frail with comorbidities were particularly vulnerable to the pandemic, which resulted in devastating morbidity and mortality rates [9]. In summary, frailty in older adults involved not only physical health but also mental and social aspects [11].

Quality of life (QoL) is a comprehensive indicator of health in later life, and has attracted increasing attention in recent epidemiological study [12]. Generally, QoL refers to an individual’s perception of their own physical and mental health in life within the context of their culture and value systems [13]. A previous study showed older adults’ QoL declined during the COVID-19 pandemic compared to pre-pandemic [14], and QoL was considered a significant predictor of mortality [15]. QoL decline was associated with multiple factors, including individual, social, and environmental factors [16]. Evidence demonstrated that social support (SS) was positively associated with QoL, as it can act as a stress buffer for older adults’ psychosocial well-being. Previous studies in China distinguished SS between formal social support (FSS) and informal social support (ISS) [17]. ISS included resources accessible from family members, friends, and others personal connections [18], while FSS was provided primarily by government and communities [18]. Based on the definition of FSS, China’s contextual characteristics, and with reference to similar studies [19–21], this study constructed a self-developed questionnaire to assess FSS. As found in previous studies, low levels SS was associated with QoL decline [22], loneliness [23], depression [24], and mortality [25]. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, older adults’ social interaction activities were further restricted, highlighting the need to reassess social support networks. However, recent studies on this issue remained limited. Studies showed that stronger neighborhood networks played an important role in mitigating poor mental health outcomes among vulnerable older adults [26]. Enhancing social support might help alleviate the negative impact of COVID-19 pandemic on older adults’ QoL.

Previous studies reporting an association between SS and QoL were mostly relied on ordinary least square (OLS) models. This model assume that the impact of SS was uniform across all individuals, regardless of the QoL distribution [27, 28]. However, assessing the impact of SS on QoL by calculating mean values might fail to capture the heterogeneity in the SS’s influence. Although many studies examined the impact of SS on QoL, few studies explored disparities beyond the mean QoL level. Compared to traditional linear regression, quantile regression (QR) model enabled the analysis of different levels (or the full distribution) of a dependent variable [29–32]. Additionally, QR models could capture differential associations that may be overlooked by conventional mean-based regression [33]. However, previous study on the relationships between SS and QoL using QR models was limited in China, particularly among older adults. We aimed to use QR to illuminate potential nonlinear effects of the explanatory variables and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of SS on QoL [34] during the COVID-19 pandemic. This study also provided evidence for developing targeted strategies in a pandemic.

Referring to the 2010 Sixth National Census data of Dongguan, 2.26% of the population was aged 65 years and above. Most residential districts in Dongguan had population between 3,000 and 25,000, meaning approximately 68–565 potential subjects in each community met the inclusion criteria. Data collection

The survey was conducted between September and December 2020, during a period of strict COVID-19 management policies in the region. In accordance with local health protocols, all participants accessible for interviews were confirmed to have no previous history of COVID-19 infection at the time of the survey.

A multi-stage sampling approach was used for participant selection. The first stage involved the convenience sampling of two towns in Dongguan. Cluster sampling was implemented within each town, with six communities purposively selected to construct the sampling frame. The purposive selection of communities was guided by two key criteria: (1) population size and (2) cooperation feasibility—to enhance the practicality of data collection.

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained and assessed proficient interviewers. Two interviews’ methods were adopted: (1) interviews at community activity centers, and (2) household interviews led by older adult. Interviewer read questions one by one according to the questionnaire and recorded the corresponding answer, EpiData was used for data entry and storage. All questionnaires were completed by participants who had provided informed consent (Supplementary file Questionnaires). All procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Review Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University (PJ2019-064).

A quality control protocol was implemented to prevent and address missing data. Firstly, immediately after each interview, field supervisors cross-checked the questionnaires. If any missing or ambiguous items were identified, interviewer re-approached the participant on-site for clarification. Secondly, a dedicated staff member reviewed all questionnaires again on the day of collection. For any remaining issues discovered during this review, participants were contacted by phone or re-visited for follow-up interview to complete the information.

Measurement

Demographic characteristics

Participants’ demographic characteristics included gender, age, educational attainment, marital status, average monthly income, living arrangement, and presence of chronic diseases.

Social support

Two types of social support were assessed: formal social support (FSS) and informal social support (ISS). FSS was defined as the resources accessible to individuals from their residential community, including three variables measured in the questionnaire as follows:

Number of community service types in 2020

Community services included eight categories. The questionnaire asked, “Which of the following services are available to your the community?” Respondents could select one or more options: “Daily care (e.g., grocery shopping, cleaning), recreational and cultural activities for older adults, emergency assistance, senior activity venues, free physical examinations, professional social work services, others, and none of the above.” This variable reflected community service availability, calculated as the count of accessible services types for each participant.

Frequency of community activity participation in 2020

The questionnaire asked, “Do you participate in activities organized by government or community, or other social groups?” Responses were categorized as “never, occasionally, often, always.” covering participants’ experiences from early 2020 to the interviews date.

Benefits of community activity participation in 2020

The questionnaire asked, “Have you benefited from participating in community activities?” Responses were rated on a 5-point scale: “low, lower-middle, middle, upper-middle, and high,” reflecting participants’ subjective perceptions after service participation.

ISS was measured using the Social Support Appraisals (SS-A) scale [36]. This 20-item questionnaire contained eight items on family, seven on friends, and five on support from others. Each item was measured on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 4 = very much agree), with higher total scores indicating higher levels of SS. The SS scores from family, friends, and others range from 8 to 32, 7 to 28, and 5 to 20, respectively. The internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) was 0.927 and construct validity (factor loadings) was over 0.4 in this study.

Quality of life

The 36-item Short-Form (SF-36, Version 1.0) comprises 36 items grouped into eight dimensions: bodily pain (BP), physical function (PF), role limitations due to physical health problems (RP), general health (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), role limitations due to emotional problems (RE), and emotional well-being/mental health (MH). These eight dimensions were aggregated into two summary measures: the physical component summary (PCS) and mental component summary (MCS). The RAND-36 scoring algorithm was adopted [37, 38]. Statistical analyses

First, descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means with standard deviations (SDs) or median with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables.

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicated that the PCS-T and MCS-T distribution were skewed. Differences in the PCS-T and MCS-T across the classification of potential associated factors (FSS and ISS) were examined using the Spearman correlation analysis and the Kruskal-Wallis test.

Finally, to explore the differential impacts of predictors across different points of the outcome distribution, quantile regression (QR) was utilized which revealed how relationships may vary across subpopulations. Multiple QR models were applied to explore the impact of SS on QoL among older adults. SS served as the independent variable, with QoL (PCS-T and MCS-T) as the dependent variables. Models were estimated at quantiles τ = {0.05,0.25,0.50,0.75,0.95}. All regression analyses adjusted for demographic and health-related covariates including age, gender, education attainment, marital status, living arrangement, income, and the number of chronic diseases [40–44].

To address the potential clustering effect at the community level which was a key feature of our data structure, cluster-robust standard errors were used in all regression analyses. To mitigate Type I error inflation from multiple quantiles test, the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction via the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to the P-values of FSS and ISS across all five quantiles.

For readability and to highlight the primary findings, only coefficients for the SS variables were presented. All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0, R 4.4.0 and Stata 16.0. Cluster-robust standard errors were computed using the “qreg2” extended commands [45] and FDR correction was implemented via the “stats” package (version 4.4.0) in R. The priori alpha level (α) was set at 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 1,146 questionnaires were collected in two towns, Machong and Dalang, in Dongguan. After rigorous validation, 16 were identified as invalid and excluded. 1,130 older adults (≥ 60 years, 98.6%) were included. 72.21% of them were female. The mean age was 78.97 years (± 8.99), and 52.00% were aged over 70 years. 44.34% were single, and 39.56% lived alone. Most participants (85.00%) had education attainment of primary school or below. 76.90% suffered from chronic diseases, with 35% suffering two or more. 50.18% of the participants had an average monthly income of less than ࿥1,000; the median average monthly income was ࿥925 (IQR: 500–1300) (Table 1).

Social support and quality of life

Social support

Only 3.98% of participants did not receive any FSS from their communities, while over half of (53.45%) received one or two types of community-provided services. 49.29% participated in community activities occasionally. 60.80% perceived these activities as beneficial to their health. In terms of ISS, the mean and standard deviation of SS from family, friends, and others were 23.68 (3.27), 20.14 (2.81), and 15.27 (1.72), respectively. Over half of the participants reported receiving better SS from friends (55.66%) and family (56.55%), while more than 60% indicated poor SS from other individuals (Fig. 1).

The mean PCS-T and MCS-T were 50.00 (SD: 6.87) and 50.00 (SD: 9.04), respectively. The PCS-T was lower in females, participants aged over 90 years, those who were single, and those living alone. Regarding FSS, compared with participants who never participated in community activities, those who participated often had significantly a positive association with PCS-T across the 5th to 95th quantiles(Q5:β = 4.7, p < 0.001, Q25:β = 2.46, p = 0.011, Q50:β = 2.36, p < 0.001, Q75:β = 3.73, p < 0.001, Q95:β = 2.04, p < 0.001). Discussion

This study revealed the association between SS and QoL among older adults, providing essential evidence for prevention and control measures during the pandemic. Although previous research has suggested that both formal and informal social support positively enhance QoL among older adults [46–48], our findings indicate that this positive association is not consistently present. Frequent participation in community activities was positively associated with older adults’ physical health, and this was more pronounced among those with a higher QoL. This finding was consistent with previous studies. The study further clarified that older adults with a high quality of life were the primary beneficiaries in community support during the pandemic. This underscored the need to further extend support in community to reach a broader older adult. At the same time, SS from family and others played a significant protective role on the mental health to the older adults with higher QoL also, and no significant association was found between SS and health among those with lower QoL. This phenomenon might be attributed to insufficient ISS available to this group during the pandemic, limited ISS from family, friends and others might reduce their sensitivity. These results suggested that older adults with lower QoL might have been overlooked in terms of SS during pandemic. Our findings provided evidence to inform social containment strategies during the pandemic.

There was a significant and complex association between FSS and QoL among older adults during pandemic. The findings of this study added to previous results [49], as it was observed that “occasionally” and “often” participation in community activities was associated with better physical health among older adults, regardless of their baseline physical health status. The older adults with better physical health perceived more benefits from participating in community activities, which was consistent with previous studies that showed that not all older adults benefited from increased social activities during the pandemic [50]. The reverse causality might result in the negative association between perceived community activity benefits and physical health, and motivated by the desire to counteract early physical decline, individuals might proactively engage in community activities to prevent further deterioration. Furthermore, the correlation between lower-middle levels of perceived benefit and poorer physical status likely indicated that health-limited individuals could only take part in lower-intensity activities, resulting in limited perceived returns. Additionally, there was an overall reduction in physical activities during the pandemic lockdown, which might have contributed to broader physical health declines among older adults [51, 52]. The community public health strategies should not only prioritize vulnerable groups but also design tailored activity programs to address the diverse needs of frail older adults with disabilities and health challenges during pandemic.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, participating in community activities inherently increased older adults’ risk of infection, potentially altering the emotional consequences of social engagement. In terms of emotional well-being, the study showed that always participate in community activities was negatively associated with older adults’ emotional well-being, especially among those with better emotional well-being. Although most previous studies showed that more community support could be linked to improved older adults’ mental health [53, 54], some studies suggested that older adults who engaged in a greater numbers of social activities experience more mental distress [55]. Additionally, some studies showed that SS services dropped since pandemic outbreak. Meanwhile, cases of depression have increased among older adults [56]. Our study added to the previous results that the negative associated between community support and emotional well-being was just among older adults with better mental health who always participation in community activities. The observed negative associated might be explained by pandemic-specific mechanisms: excessive participation during this period could induced fatigue or social burnout, while also heightening anxiety related to infection risk. It was recommended that older adults participate in community activities in moderation, cautioning against excessive volunteer engagement by those with favorable self-rated health to prevent potential risks during pandemic.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, strict social distancing measures may have constrained the ability of family and friends to provide direct support. Previous studies shown that older adults in China tended to rely on family and friends for physical needs and daily living assistance [57, 58]. However, this study revealed that such support from family and friends showed a weaker association with well-being during pandemic, on the other hand, the social support provided by others gained significance, which might be attributed to social distancing. This highlighted that the harmonious social environment fostered by others during the pandemic might be positively associated with mental and physical health of older adults. Previous studies showed that support from family was considered to mitigate the mental problems during the time of the pandemic [59]. This phenomenon was observed across older adult groups with both lower and higher levels of mental health during pandemic.

Limitations

This study provided the first evidence on the impact of formal and informal social support on the quality of life of older adults during the pandemic. The findings might inform the development of social policies in times of public health crises. However, it was important to note the limitations of this research. Firstly, as a city in the Pearl River Delta, Dongguan had a pleasant climate. Approximately two-thirds of its young population were migrants, while the majority of its older adults were long-lived local residents. The composition of our sample reflected this demographic profile of the city’s older population, which might limit the generalizability of the findings to other contexts [60]. Secondly, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships examined. Moreover, participants were recruited from only two towns using a convenience sampling approach, which limits the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the self-reported data from older adults might introduce the recall bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, there was heterogeneity in the associations between social support and quality of life among older adults during the pandemic. These findings call for greater attention to the needs of older adults with a poorer quality of life, particularly through expanding coverage of formal community-based support and enhancing the effectiveness of their participation in community activities. Fostering a harmonious and caring social and community environment appeared to be a pathway associated with the quality of life for older adults during the pandemic, with interventions tailored to both the source of support and the targeted dimension of QoL.
