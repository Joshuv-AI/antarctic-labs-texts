Comprehensive profiling of the competitive interplay among carbamylation, glycation, and enzymatic cross-links in human bone
Authors: Shoutaro Arakawa, Shota Katayama, Hikari Sugawa, Taiki Neyatani, Yu Yamashita, Daisuke Kubota, Tomohiro Kayama, Hiroteru Hayashi, Teruyuki Miyasaka, Toshiyuki Omori, Ryoji Nagai, Mitsuru Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbamylation-derived products (CDPs) are implicated in protein dysfunction but remain understudied in human bone. We profiled homocitrulline (HCit, the predominant CDP), advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), and enzymatic cross-links in human cancellous bone specimens (n = 211). Homocitrulline accumulated substantially, surpassing carboxymethyl-lysine. Multivariable regression identified male sex and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) as independent determinants of HCit. This suggests men face a “dual burden” of renal-dependent accumulation and physiological urea loading driven by greater muscle mass. We observed a shift in the modification balance where higher BUN increased the HCit fraction while decreasing the AGEs-HCit ratio. Functionally, HCit correlated negatively with immature cross-links. Crucially, partial coefficient of determination (R2) analysis revealed that carbamylation and glycation act as concurrent determinants of cross-link impairment, with HCit exerting a stronger inhibitory effect than AGEs specifically in patients with mild BUN elevation (≥21 mg/dL). These findings demonstrate that carbamylation is a pervasive modification that actively interferes with collagen maturation, posing a significant threat to bone quality even in non-dialysis populations. Monitoring the carbamylation–glycation balance may offer new insights into bone fragility, especially in aging men and early-stage CKD.Keywords: homocitrulline (HCit), carbamylation, advanced glycation endproducts (AGEs), enzymatic collagen cross-links, bone quality, CKD

Introduction

Bone strength depends not only on bone mass but also on bone quality, which is largely governed by the structural and biochemical properties of type I collagen.1,2 Among these properties, collagen cross-linking is a major determinant of post-yield behavior, toughness, and resistance to microdamage, with collagen network connectivity explaining up to 36% of cortical bone fracture toughness variance.3,4 Cross-links are broadly categorized into enzymatic cross-links, regulated by lysyl hydroxylases and lysyl oxidase, and non-enzymatic post-translational modifications (NEPTMs), such as glycation- and oxidation-derived products. Alterations in either pathway can compromise the material properties of bone and contribute to fragility in aging, osteoporosis, and diabetes mellitus.1,5 Indeed, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis highlighted that patients with type 2 diabetes face an increased risk of fracture despite having normal or even elevated BMD, underscoring the critical role of altered bone quality.6

Glycation is one of the most extensively studied NEPTMs. Reducing sugars react with the ε-amino groups of lysine residues to form Amadori products and advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). Advanced glycation end-products accumulate in long-lived matrix proteins, stiffening collagen fibrils and potentially interfering with enzymatic cross-link formation. Site-specific analyses have demonstrated that glycation targets the same helical lysine residues required for lysyl oxidase-mediated cross-linking.7

Carbamylation is another NEPTM of increasing interest. Driven by urea-derived isocyanic acid or myeloperoxidase-dependent thiocyanate oxidation, carbamylation converts lysine to homocitrulline (HCit), altering protein charge and conformation. Carbamylated collagen exhibits reduced fibril polymerization and structural disorganization.8,9 Although carbamylation is known to increase with aging, CKD, and inflammatory states, its impact on human bone quality remains less characterized compared to glycation.

As illustrated in Figure 1, enzymatic cross-linking, glycation, and carbamylation all target the ε-amino groups of lysine residues, leading to a competitive interplay for the same modification sites.7,10,11 Reciprocal inhibition has been demonstrated in vitro and in vivo, and combined diabetes-CKD animal models suggest that carbamylation can prevail over glycation under high uremic stress.10,11 However, the competitive interplay among glycation, carbamylation, and enzymatic cross-links has never been examined in human bone tissue. Furthermore, traditional bone quality models focused primarily on AGEs may overlook the significant contribution of carbamylation, particularly in patients with mild-to-moderate renal dysfunction.

Schematic illustration of the competitive modification of lysine ε-amino groups in bone collagen. Lysine residues in collagen can undergo one of three major modifications: formation of enzymatic immature cross-links (eg, DHLNL, HLNL, and LNL), non-enzymatic glycation leading to AGEs (eg, CML, CEL, and pentosidine), or non-enzymatic carbamylation yielding carbamylation-derived products (CDPs, eg, HCit). These processes compete for the same ε-amino groups, thereby influencing the balance of collagen cross-linking and non-enzymatic modifications. We previously established a liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) platform capable of quantifying AGEs and enzymatic cross-links in human bone.12 While our initial work in cancellous bone suggested competitive inhibition between AGEs and enzymatic cross-links,12 our recent collaborative study extended this finding to human cortical bone. We demonstrated that abundant non-crosslinking AGE adducts (eg, CML, CEL, and MG-H1) correlate strongly with reduced immature enzymatic cross-links and are a significant determinant of impaired fracture resistance.13 Building upon this foundation, the present study comprehensively evaluates: (1) the accumulation of HCit in human cancellous bone, (2) its clinical determinants, (3) the competitive balance between carbamylation and glycation, and (4) the combined impact of these NEPTMs on enzymatic collagen cross-link profiles.

Materials and methods

Participants and study design

This study included patients with knee osteoarthritis (OA) or RA who underwent total knee arthroplasty (TKA) at the Jikei University Hospital between 2018 and 2020. Patients with secondary OA due to trauma or infection were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Jikei University School of Medicine (No. 26-312). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical and biochemical measurements

Fasting blood samples were collected preoperatively. Complete blood count parameters, including white blood cell (WBC) count, red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct), and platelet count, were measured.

Routine biochemical parameters were assessed using standard automated laboratory methods, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total protein (TP), albumin, total bilirubin (T.Bil), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (Cr), and C-reactive protein (CRP).

To comprehensively evaluate glycemic status, fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), glycoalbumin (GA), and 1,5-anhydroglucitol (1,5-AG) were measured. Glycoalbumin and 1,5-AG were assessed as markers of intermediate-term glycemic control and postprandial glucose excursions, respectively.

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the equation for Japanese citizens recommended by the Japanese Society of Nephrology. As markers of bone turnover, serum total procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (total P1NP) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase-5b (TRACP-5b) were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) and enzyme immunoassay (EIA), respectively.

Bone tissue preparation

Cancellous bone specimens were harvested from the central lateral tibia (10 mm depth from the cartilage surface) and pulverized using a Multi-beads Shocker (Yasui Kikai). Basic pretreatment, including washing, delipidation, and demineralization, was performed as previously described.12 The demineralized bone powder was aliquoted for 3 separate analytical procedures. First, for the quantification of enzymatic cross-links and specific AGEs (CML, CEL, and MG-H1), approximately 0.1 mg of bone powder was reduced with sodium borohydride as described previously,12 followed by hydrolysis in 6 M HCl at 110 °C for 18 h. Second, for HCit and furosine, another aliquot of approximately 0.1 mg was hydrolyzed directly in 6 M HCl at 110 °C for 18 h without prior reduction. Third, for pentosidine, approximately 5 mg of bone powder was reduced and hydrolyzed as described above, and the hydrolysate was subsequently purified using a Bond Elut-Cellulose SPE column (Agilent Technologies).

Prior to hydrolysis, samples were spiked with appropriate isotope-labeled internal standards. Isotope-labeled internal standards were obtained from the following sources: [2H2]-CML, [2H4]-CEL, and [2H3]-MG-H1 were purchased from PolyPeptide Laboratories. [2H3]-L-Hyp was purchased from C/D/N Isotopes Inc. [2H4]-Pentosidine was obtained from Alsachim. [2H3]-HCit, [2H4]-Furosine, [2H6]-PYD, and [2H4]-DHLNL were purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals. [2H4]-DPD was purchased from Iris Biotech GmbH. Deuterated enzymatic immature cross-links ([2H4]-LNL and [2H4]-HLNL) were custom-synthesized by Shinsei Chemical Company Ltd. The hydrolysates were dried and re-suspended in the mobile phase for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Quantification of HCit, AGEs, and enzymatic cross-links by LC-MS/MS

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed following the method previously described.12 The system consisted of a Shimadzu UFLC Prominence (Shimadzu) coupled to a micrOTOF-QII quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (QqTOF-MS) by Bruker Daltonics.

Chromatographic separation was performed at 40 °C with a flow rate of 200 μL/min.

For Hyp, HCit, AGEs (CML, CEL, and MG-H1), furosine, and enzymatic cross-links: Analytes were separated on a SeQuant ZIC-HILIC column (5 μm, 150 × 2.1 mm; Merck Millipore) using a linear gradient of water and acetonitrile (MeCN) containing 0.1% formic acid.

For Hyp, HCit, AGEs (CML, CEL, and MG-H1), furosine, and enzymatic cross-links: Analytes were separated on a SeQuant ZIC-HILIC column (5 μm, 150 × 2.1 mm; Merck Millipore) using a linear gradient of water and acetonitrile (MeCN) containing 0.1% formic acid.

For pentosidine: Separation was performed on a SunShell RP-AQUA column (2.6 μm, 150 × 2.1 mm; ChromaNik Technologies) in isocratic mode (0% MeCN with 0.1% formic acid).

For pentosidine: Separation was performed on a SunShell RP-AQUA column (2.6 μm, 150 × 2.1 mm; ChromaNik Technologies) in isocratic mode (0% MeCN with 0.1% formic acid).

The QqTOF-MS was operated in positive ion mode with an electrospray ionization source (capillary voltage: 4500 V; dry gas: 200 °C). Post-run calibration was performed using ESI-L Low Concentration Tuning Mix.

Data acquisition and processing were performed using the Bruker Compass software suite (HyStar 3.2, otofControl 4.0, Data Analysis 4.3, and QuantAnalysis 4.3). Target analytes and their internal standards (ISTDs) are listed in Table S4. The levels of AGEs, HCit, furosine, and cross-links were normalized to Hyp content and expressed as μmol/mol Hyp.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± SD or as the median and IQR, depending on the data presentation. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Differences between groups were examined using the Student’s t-test. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rs) was used to determine the strength and direction of associations between variables.

To identify independent determinants of AGEs, HCit, and their derived indices (AGEs–HCit ratio and fHCit), multiple linear regression analyses were performed using the forced-entry method. The dependent variables included individual NEPTMs (CML, CEL, MG-H1, pentosidine, and HCit) and their derived indices. The independent variables were selected based on clinical relevance and the results of single regression analyses (Table S2). These variables, entered simultaneously into the model, included age, sex, eGFR, BUN, HbA1c, total P1NP, and TRACP-5b. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were checked to confirm the absence of significant multicollinearity (VIF < 10).

To quantify the relative contribution of HCit and AGEs (defined as the sum of CML and CEL) to the variance of enzymatic immature cross-links, partial coefficient of determination (R2) values were calculated. The partial R2 was defined as the reduction in the R2 when the specific variable of interest was removed from the full model containing all other covariates. To assess the robustness of these estimates, 95% CIs for the partial R2 values were generated using a bootstrap method with 2000 iterations.

The p-values of <.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS University Edition ver. 3.8 (SAS Institute Inc.) and JMP Pro 17 (SAS Institute Inc.).

Specifically, the concentration of HCit was notably higher than that of CML and CEL, and substantially higher (approximately 2 orders of magnitude) than that of pentosidine. Although pentosidine abundance was low, it was reliably quantified above the detection limit by using a specific enrichment protocol with cellulose solid-phase extraction (SPE) and RP-AQUA column separation, as previously validated.12 Regarding enzymatic cross-links, physiological immature (HLNL and DHLNL) and mature (DPD and PYD) cross-links were detected at physiological levels.

Correlational analyses (Table S1) revealed distinct relationships among NEPTMs.

Homocitrulline showed a clear inverse association with furosine, suggesting competition between carbamylation and early glycation. CML and CEL were moderately correlated with each other, while MG-H1 showed weaker relationships with other AGEs, consistent with its methylglyoxal-driven formation pathway.

Associations of NEPTMs with clinical parameters

In single-regression analyses (Table S2), HCit correlated positively with age (rs = 0.158) and BUN (rs = 0.278) and negatively with TRACP-5b (rs = −0.169). HCit was not associated with eGFR, indicating that its accumulation reflects urea exposure rather than renal clearance. Advanced glycation end-products showed weaker associations with renal and glycemic indices, whereas furosine correlated strongly with HbA1c, glycoalbumin, and 1,5-AG.

In multivariable models (Table 2), male sex, older age, higher BUN, and lower TRACP-5b were independent determinants of higher HCit. Neither eGFR nor HbA1c remained significant after adjustment.

Relationships between NEPTMs and enzymatic immature cross-links

As shown in Table S3, CML, CEL, and HCit exhibited comparably strong negative correlations with immature enzymatic cross-links (LNL, HLNL, and DHLNL), with CEL generally showing the largest absolute coefficients. These findings indicate that both glycation- and carbamylation-derived NEPTMs are closely related to reduced immature cross-link levels. However, considering that both NEPTM accumulation and enzymatic cross-link profiles are age-dependent, we performed multiple linear regression analyses to rule out the potential confounding effect of aging. As shown in Table 3, HCit remained significantly and negatively associated with immature cross-links (LNL, HLNL, and DHLNL) even after adjusting for age, gender, and BUN. Notably, age itself was not identified as an independent determinant in these models. These results indicate that the negative association between HCit and enzymatic immature cross-links is not merely a reflection of age-related accumulation but represents a robust and independent link, suggesting that carbamylation contributes to cross-link deterioration alongside glycation.

Contribution of NEPTMs to impaired enzymatic cross-link formation

To quantify the relative influence of NEPTMs on enzymatic cross-link formation, partial R2 values were calculated for LNL, HLNL, and DHLNL in multivariable models (Figure 4A). Partial R2 analysis revealed that HCit showed the greatest contribution to the variance of LNL. For HLNL and DHLNL, the contributions of HCit and AGEs (CML+CEL) were comparable, suggesting that carbamylation and glycation act as concurrent determinants of enzymatic cross-link deterioration. The bootstrap-derived 95% CIs (2000 iterations) supported the robustness of this pattern, indicating that carbamylation plays a dominant role in explaining the variance in immature cross-link levels.

When the models were stratified by BUN quartiles (Figure 4B-D), the partial R2 attributable to HCit increased progressively from Q1 to the highest quartile (Q4; BUN ≥21 mg/dL), whereas the contribution of CML+CEL gradually decreased across the same categories. This reciprocal shift was particularly evident for LNL and HLNL, demonstrating that higher systemic urea exposure augments the impact of carbamylation while diminishing the influence of glycation on impaired immature cross-link formation. For DHLNL, although the reciprocal pattern was less distinct across the quartiles, HCit remained a substantial contributor to cross-link variance consistent with the overall model.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive profiling of CDPs, AGEs, and enzymatic cross-links in human cancellous bone. Our primary findings challenge the conventional dogma that glycation is the sole dominant NEPTM driving bone aging.1,14 We demonstrated that HCit accumulates in human bone collagen at levels surpassing well-known AGEs, such as CML and pentosidine. Furthermore, our data suggest a “competitive interaction” where urea-derived carbamylation progressively outcompetes glycation at lysine residues, independently contributing to the impairment of physiological enzymatic cross-link formation. These results position carbamylation alongside glycation as a critical determinant of collagen integrity in both aging and renal dysfunction, necessitating a re-evaluation of bone matrix quality.15 Underscoring the clinical importance of this concept, recent clinical evidence from the LIMB-Q trial further emphasizes that improving bone quality—beyond mere density—is crucial for reducing fracture risk in older adults.16

Although carbamylation is known to be driven by cyanate derived from urea dissociation or the myeloperoxidase (MPO)–thiocyanate pathway during inflammation,17,18 evidence of its accumulation in bone has previously been limited to in vitro models and rodent studies.19 Here, we confirmed that HCit is a ubiquitous modification in human cancellous bone. Notably, HCit accumulation was strongly defined by BUN levels rather than eGFR. This distinction is clinically pivotal; it suggests that even mild elevations in uremic burden—common in early-stage CKD (G2–G3a) or with aging—are sufficient to drive cumulative chemical modifications in long-lived matrix proteins, even without dialysis-dependency. Unlike rapidly turning-over serum proteins, bone collagen acts as a reservoir, integrating and recording long-term exposure to uremic stress.20

In our multiple regression analysis, BUN was identified as an independent determinant of HCit accumulation; however, interestingly, male sex remained a significant and potent predictor even after adjusting for BUN levels (Table 2). Generally, men have greater skeletal muscle mass and a higher physiological baseline of urea production derived from protein catabolism compared to women—consistent with our data showing significantly higher Cre and BUN levels in men. The fact that both sex and BUN remained independent factors (with VIF < 2 indicating no multicollinearity) suggests that HCit accumulation is not solely defined by the spot BUN value at a single time point. Rather, it likely reflects the long-term physiological background specific to men, such as constitutively higher protein catabolism and a larger urea pool. This implies that male bone collagen is exposed to a “dual burden,” that is, the risk of impaired urea excretion due to renal function fluctuations, and a physiological susceptibility to higher urea loads. This likely explains why men are fundamentally more prone to HCit accumulation than women.

A core novelty of this study is the demonstration of a competitive interplay between carbamylation and glycation in human tissue. Since both cyanate (carbamylation) and reducing sugars (glycation) target the ϵ-amino group of lysine residues, they potentially compete for the same binding sites. Our analysis by BUN quartiles revealed a distinct reciprocal shift: as systemic urea load increased, the fraction of HCit (fHCit) within total lysine modifications rose, while the AGEs–HCit ratio declined. This validates the “competitive inhibition” model proposed by Nicolas et al. in skin and aortic tissues,10 confirming its applicability to the human skeletal system. The functional implication of this shift lies in the inhibition of enzymatic cross-linking. Our data show that both HCit and AGEs are negatively correlated with immature enzymatic cross-links (HLNL). Importantly, partial R2 analysis indicated that under conditions of high uremic stress (highest BUN quartile), HCit exerts a more potent inhibitory effect on cross-link formation than AGEs. This finding is consistent with previous observations showing strong negative correlations between AGE adducts and HLNL in bone.13

The functional implication of this biochemical competition is the inhibition of enzymatic cross-linking. Our data demonstrated that immature enzymatic cross-links generally declined as uremic burden increased. While this negative association was statistically more robust for HLNL, a similar downward trajectory was observed for DHLNL. The larger variance in DHLNL levels, particularly in high-BUN subgroups, likely reflects the inherently high metabolic turnover of cancellous bone,21,22 which can introduce heterogeneity that masks the inhibitory effect of carbamylation. Nevertheless, the coherent pattern across these markers reinforces the conclusion that carbamylation actively interferes with the physiological enzymatic processing of collagen, preventing the proper maturation of the bone matrix.

This study has several limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional design, we cannot directly establish a causal relationship between NEPTM patterns and fracture risk. We inferred mechanical compromise based on the reduction of enzymatic cross-links—an established surrogate for bone toughness—but did not perform direct biomechanical testing. Second, as we analyzed tibial cancellous bone, whether these profiles extend to cortical bone or other skeletal sites requires verification. Third, although BUN was the primary factor, we did not quantify MPO activity in the bone marrow. Finally, we did not quantify hydroxyhomocitrulline (HHCit), another carbamylation product found in collagen. Although Taga et al. highlighted HHCit as a collagen-specific carbamylation product, their data also indicated that the absolute abundance of HCit was higher than that of HHCit in collagenous tissues.23 Consistent with their findings, our targeted analysis of human bone collagen revealed that the signal intensity of HCit was substantially higher (approximately 38-fold) than that of the putative HHCit peak (Figure S1). Furthermore, the lack of a commercially available stable isotope-labeled internal standard for HHCit precluded definitive identification and accurate quantification. However, given the remarkably strong positive correlation observed between HCit and this putative HHCit peak (Spearman’s rs = 0.945, Figure S1), we conclude that HCit levels effectively reflect the total carbamylation burden in bone, justifying our focus on HCit.

Conclusions

In conclusion, carbamylation is a pervasive and competitive modification in human bone collagen, comparable in both quantity and impact to glycation. HCit accumulation, driven by mild elevations in BUN, actively interferes with the formation of physiological enzymatic cross-links. While the pathophysiology of CKD-Mineral and Bone Disorder (CKD-MBD) has traditionally been viewed through the lens of mineral and endocrine abnormalities, our findings extend the understanding of “uremic osteopathy” to the material level of the collagen matrix. Monitoring the balance between carbamylation and glycation may offer new insights into bone fragility risk, suggesting that interventions aimed at reducing systemic urea load or scavenging cyanate could be essential for maintaining bone quality in aging and renal dysfunction.
