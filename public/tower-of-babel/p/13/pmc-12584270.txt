A novel compound heterozygous variant in LAMA2 gene in a family with merosin-deficient congenital muscular dystrophy
Authors: Parham Nejati, Nafiseh Falsafi, Elham Alimoradi, Teymoor Khosravi, Mohana Kamari, Morteza Oladnabi, Reza Alibakhshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLAMA2 encodes the alpha-2 subunit of a protein called Laminin. It consists of three subunits Y; alpha, beta and gamma. Alpha2 subunit from LAMA2 gene along with beta-2 and gamma-2 forms laminin-2 protein. This protein is necessary for assembly of basement membrane in skeletal muscle cells, Schwann cells, astrocytes and pericytes. More than 100 LAMA2 variant identified so far which cause recessive form of muscular dystrophy (MD) either a severe form, congenital (CMD) or mild form, limb-girdle (LGMD). Patient with LAMA2 MD suffered from muscle weakness, elevated creatine kinase, facial dysmorphism, peripheral motor neuropathy, epilepsy/seizure, developmental delay, and white matter changes in brain MRI. In this study, we conducted whole exome sequencing (WES) to investigate molecular etiology of patients with CMD in one family with non-consanguineous marriage from Iran. WES has identified a novel compound heterozygous variant, [c.2049_2050del (p.Arg683Serfs*21)]; [c.2857–2 A > G (p.?)], in the proband. The identified variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and its segregation within the family was verified. Subsequently, in-silico analysis was performed to map the protein–protein interaction network between LAMA2 and proteins implicated in CMD pathogenesis. Our findings may be considered valuable molecular and clinical insights for improving our understanding of CMD, particularly regarding LAMA2 variants. Furthermore, this finding gives new insights to laboratorians, genetic counselors and clinicians for determining at-risk couples in the prenatal diagnosis (PND) program.Keywords: LAMA2, Congenital muscular dystrophy, Whole-exome sequencing, Genetic variant, Iran

Introduction

LAMA2-related Muscular dystrophy (LAMA2 MD), an autosomal recessive muscular dystrophy is caused by mutations in LAMA2 gene. It is characterized by muscle weakness ranging from severe to mild and is divided into two subgroups: severe early-onset LAMA2 congenital muscular dystrophy (LAMA2-CMD) and mild late-onset limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (LAMA2-LGMD) [1]. The majority of patients with LAMA2 mutations have a congenital muscular dystrophy (CMD) phenotype classified as type 1 A (MDC1A; MIM# 607855) which presents as neonatal hypotonia or muscle weakness during the first months of life [2]. It is estimated that up to 48% of all patients have CMD and most of them are from European countries (Asians are rare) [3].

More than 100 LAMA2 gene mutations have been identified in individuals with LAMA2 MD. Patients with LAMA2 MD suffer from muscle weakness, elevated creatine kinase, facial dysmorphism, peripheral motor neuropathy, epilepsy and seizure. Moreover, developmental delay, and white matter anomalies in brain MRI have been observed previously [4]. In some cases, cardiomyopathy, and autism-like behavior was also an obvious behavior [5]. LAMA2 gene is located on chromosome 6 and includes 65 exons that encode laminin α2 subunit.

Laminin proteins are made of three subunits called alpha, beta, and gamma. Each subunit has several isoforms, with each isoform encoded by a distinct gene. The LAMA2 gene provides instruction for the alpha-2 subunit. This subunit together with beta-1 and gamma-1, forms the laminin-2 protein also known as merosonin or laminin-211, which is necessary for assembly of basement membrane [6]. This protein is predominantly expressed in skeletal muscle cells, astrocytes and pericytes of brain capillaries, as well as Schwann cells of peripheral nerves [7].

For the early diagnosis of MD patient, the concentration of CPK in serum, a biopsy of skin or muscle, and MRI to examine white matter changes would be helpful [8]. Recent reports showed that whole exome sequencing (WES) can expand our knowledge about the molecular aspects of CMD and might identify novel mutations in CMD patients [9, 10].

Although most reports on LAMA2-related muscular dystrophy have come from European cohorts, data from Middle Eastern populations are limited. Our proband, from Iran, represents an underrepresented group in genomic databases. In this study, we clinically characterized the patient and identified a novel compound heterozygous LAMA2 mutation by WES, confirmed through Sanger sequencing, and further explored its protein network using in-silico analyses. Such cases broaden the mutational spectrum of LAMA2 and enhance understanding of CMD genetic diversity across different populations.

Materials and methods

Clinical investigation

In this study, we investigated a eleven-year-old female patient born to non-consanguineous parents with no prior family history of genetic disorders or neurodevelopmental conditions. Despite normal cognitive development, the proband exhibited congenital hypotonia, severe muscle weakness, physical abnormalities, and inability to walk. At 12 days of age, she experienced seizures and presented with marked scoliosis and secondary joint contractures. Initial biochemical evaluation revealed elevated levels of creatine phosphokinase (CPK = 464 U/L) and aldolase (10.7 U/L), suggestive of muscle damage. Electrophysiological studies, including nerve conduction studies and electromyography (EMG), revealed normal nerve conduction and myopathic patterns, respectively—findings consistent with congenital myopathy. A muscle biopsy was subsequently obtained using an open surgical technique. The tissue was rapidly frozen in isopentane cooled in liquid nitrogen to preserve morphology. Histopathological examination confirmed features of muscular dystrophy. Interestingly, a paternal first cousin had displayed similar symptoms and had died in his forties; however, no genetic data were available for this individual. The proband’s family sought genetic consultation at a laboratory in Kermanshah, Iran, where whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed.

Muscle biopsy and staining procedures

A muscle biopsy was obtained from the patient’s left deltoid muscle using an open surgical technique. The specimen was immediately snap-frozen in isopentane cooled by liquid nitrogen to preserve tissue morphology. Two types of tissue blocks were prepared: one frozen block for histochemical analysis and one paraffin-embedded block for additional evaluation. Histochemical and enzymatic stains were performed on cryosections from the frozen tissue and included the following: 1. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E): To assess overall muscle architecture and identify variations in fiber size and connective tissue changes. 2. Modified Gomori Trichrome: To detect ragged red fibers, vacuoles, or other mitochondrial abnormalities. 3. Congo Red: To evaluate for congophilic inclusions indicative of amyloid deposition. 4. Oil Red O (ORO): To assess lipid accumulation within muscle fibers. 5. Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS): To evaluate glycogen content in muscle fibers. 6. NADH-Tetrazolium Reductase (NADH-TR): To visualize the intermyofibrillar network and oxidative enzyme activity. 7. Succinate Dehydrogenase (SDH) and Cytochrome c Oxidase (COX): To assess mitochondrial function and detect COX-deficient fibers or mitochondrial proliferation. 8. ATPase staining at pH 9.4, 4.63, and 4.35: To differentiate muscle fiber types and evaluate for fiber type predominance or grouping.

Procedure based on standard protocol

Cryosections (8–10 μm) were fixed in cold acetone for 10 min. Non-specific binding was blocked using 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with species-specific fluorescent or HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI or hematoxylin. Labeling patterns were visualized under a fluorescence or light microscope, and images were captured using standardized exposure settings. Negative controls (omission of primary antibody) were included to confirm staining specificity.Beta-spectrin served as a positive control to ensure proper sarcolemmal labeling and rule out technical artifacts.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples obtained from the proband and her parents using the Qiagen FlexiGene™ DNA Kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a Thermo Scientific™ NanoDrop™ One Microvolume UV–Vis spectrophotometer, and the integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Whole exome sequencing pipeline

DNA library Preparation

The extracted genomic DNA was sheared into 150–300 bp fragments using ultrasonication. Target enrichment was performed with the SureSelect Human All Exon V7 kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Library preparation and capture were carried out at Macrogen Europe (Germany).

Sequencing and data processing

Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform, achieving an average coverage depth of 175×. Raw reads were evaluated using FastQC and IlluQC for quality control. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 (hg38) human reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA). Post-alignment processing included base quality recalibration and duplicate removal using SAMtools, Picard, and Base Quality Score Recalibration (BQSR) tools.

Variant calling followed the GATK Best Practices workflow. To ensure data reliability, variants were subjected to stringent quality control filters: variants with a Phred-scaled quality score < 30, read depth (DP) < 10, or mapping quality (MQ) < 40 were excluded. Additionally, variants with strand bias (FS > 60.0) or quality by depth (QD < 2.0) were filtered out. Only variants that passed these thresholds were retained for downstream analyses.

Variant calling and annotation

Variant calling was performed using the HaplotypeCaller algorithm from GATK v4.0. Annotated variants were generated using ANNOVAR, incorporating multiple public and population-specific databases including 1000 Genomes Project, ExAC, gnomAD, dbSNP, and Iranome. Variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1% located in exonic regions were prioritized. Given the suspected autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, homozygous and compound heterozygous variants were specifically assessed.

In silico interpretation of variants

To predict the pathogenicity and protein impact of the identified LAMA2 variant, multiple in silico prediction tools were applied, including CADD (v1.6), MutationTaster2025 (accessed March 2024), SpliceAI (v1.3), and AlphaMissense (release 2024). Clinical interpretation of variants was performed according to the ACMG guidelines using the Franklin (https://franklin.genoox.com/clinical-db/home) platform, which integrates computational prediction algorithms, population frequency databases, and clinical evidence to generate standardized classifications. Population and clinical databases were also interrogated to support variant classification, including gnomAD (v3.1.2, accessed June 2024), ClinVar (release February 2024), dbSNP (build 155), and OMIM (accessed May 2024). The corresponding protein changes were documented accordingly.

Protein-protein interaction analysis

An interaction network of the functional proteins associated with the identified variants was analyzed using the online STRING server. In-silico analysis was conducted to investigate the functional network of the LAMA2 protein.

Variant confirmation and segregation

For validation of identified variants, trio-based Sanger sequencing was performed on proband and his parents. Primers were designed using Primer3 software and verified with NCBI Primer-BLAST. PCR amplification was carried out using HotStarTaq DNA Polymerase (Qiagen) under standard cycling conditions. Bidirectional sequencing was performed using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit on an ABI 3500xl Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Sequence analysis was conducted by alignment with the reference sequence (GRCh38/hg38) using BioEdit software. Appropriate positive and negative controls were included in all experiments.

Evolutionary conservation study

The evolutionary conservation of the LAMA2 protein sequence was analyzed using the ConSurf web server [11], a tool designed to identify evolutionary patterns in amino acid and nucleotide sequences. ConSurf predicts both functional and structural regions by assessing conservation across species. Conservation scores of amino acids range from 1 to 9, where 1 represents highly variable regions, 5 represents moderately conserved areas, and 9 signifies regions that are highly conserved. Notably, exposed residues with high conservation scores are inferred to be functional, while highly conserved buried residues are associated with structural roles.

Modeling the 3D structure of wild and mutant types

To model the 3D structures of LAMA2 variants, the I-TASSER server (available at https://zhanglab.ccmb.med.umich.edu/I-TASSER/) was used. This tool enables the generation of three-dimensional protein models, including wild-type, mutant, and superimposed structures. The server operates by identifying homologous sequences from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) that align with various segments of the input sequence. These sequences are then assembled using sophisticated algorithms focused on energy optimization and structural stability. The resulting models are visualized with UCSF Chimera (available at https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimera/), a powerful platform for detailed examination and analysis of protein structures.

Result

Clinical finding

The eleven-year-old female patient (Fig. 1a, Ⅴ−2) was referred to the genetic lab. She was from a non-consanguineous marriage in Kermanshah, Iran. In the family history, her father’s first cousin (Ⅲ−1) had similar symptoms and died in his forties, unfortunately there was no additional information supporting his genetic variant. About the proband, although she was born normally, her brain capillary ruptured due to birth pressure. She had convulsions and fever in twelve days old and after one month she completely lost the movement in her arms and legs. Moreover, she had another seizure and fever at 11 years old. She suffered from a variety of symptoms including inability to walk or hold something with hands. She needs support for sitting and doing her homework (Fig. 1b). Also, she had severe respiratory infection which decreased with medication prescribed by her doctor and physiotherapy. Surprisingly, her brain MRI illustrated normal areas (Fig. 1c), however her muscle biopsy showed a few round hypertrophic and degenerative fibers. From H&E staining and NADH-TR reaction revealed poor differentiation of muscle fibers, moreover, nonspecific intermyofibrillar network abnormalities were seen, which collectively showed compatibility with muscular dystrophy. For the next step, we performed whole exome sequencing (WES) to identify the variant. Then, to confirm the identified variant, Sanger sequencing was performed for the proband, siblings and their parents.

Genetic testing

After WES analysis, we identified a novel compound heterozygous variant in LAMA2, consisting of a frameshift deletion (c.2049_2050del [p.Arg683Serfs*21]) and a splice site variant (c.2857–2 A > G) (Fig. 1d). LAMA2 encodes the α subunit of the laminin protein, crucial for cell attachment and migration, and these alterations likely destabilize it. The variants are classified as pathogenic per ACMG guidelines, with c.2049_2050del supported by PVS1 (loss-of-function mechanism), PM2 (absence in gnomAD, dbSNP, ExAC, Iranome), PM3 (compound heterozygosity), and PP5 (clinical reports of similar frameshifts), while c.2857–2 A > G is supported by PVS1 (aberrant splicing), PM2 (absence in population databases), PM3 (compound heterozygosity), and PP5 (reports of splice site variants). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation, with the father carrying c.2857–2 A > G and the mother carrying c.2049_2050del, and evolutionary conservation (Fig. 1e) reinforces their impact. In silico tools were initially considered but deemed unnecessary given the strong PVS1 evidence.

Muscle biopsy and staining findings

Muscle-biopsy examination revealed the classic portrait of merosin-deficient congenital muscular dystrophy. Haematoxylin–eosin showed marked fibre-size variation, rounded contours, scattered atrophic and occasional hypertrophied fibres, rare degenerative/regenerative foci, increased endomysial connective tissue and adipose replacement; no inflammation was present. Oxidative enzyme reactions demonstrated poor fibre-type differentiation, nonspecific intermyofibrillar disarray and enlarged mitochondria, whereas mitochondrial proliferation or cytochrome-c-oxidase-negative fibres were absent. ATPase reactions at three pH levels disclosed a conspicuous type-I fibre predominance without grouping.

Immunohistochemistry confirmed intact sarcolemmal expression of dystrophin, α- and γ-sarcoglycan, dysferlin and β-spectrin; in contrast, merosin (LAMA2) was completely absent in every fibre and in intramuscular nerve bundles, validating the genetic finding of biallelic LAMA2 variants. Protein association network

The STRING database [12] employs multiple criteria, including gene neighborhood, gene fusion, and gene co-occurrence, to predict the protein interaction network of LAMA2. Figure 2a highlights the primary and secondary interactors of LAMA2, which are anticipated to exhibit the most significant associations with this protein. The primary interactors of LAMA2 include FKRP, SGCA, DMD, DAG1, CD44, LAMB2, LAMB1, LAMC1, LAMC3, and LAMA1. Several of these proteins are key components of the dystrophin–glycoprotein complex (e.g., SGCA, DMD, and DAG1), underscoring the critical role of LAMA2 in maintaining sarcolemmal integrity and muscle stability. FKRP, involved in glycosylation, further highlights the contribution of LAMA2 to the proper processing of extracellular matrix proteins, while interactions with laminin subunits such as LAMB1, LAMB2, LAMC1, and LAMA1 reflect the necessity of heterotrimeric laminin assembly for basement membrane organization.

Beyond these primary interactors, a broad spectrum of secondary interactors—including POMGNT1, SGCG, SGCB, UTRN, SGCD, AGRN, NID1, HSPG2, NID2, LAMA4, LAMA3, LAMC2, SV2C, ITGA11, LAMB4, LAMB3, LAMA5, ITGA7, NTN4, and ITGA10—are predicted to have comparatively weaker associations with LAMA2. Despite their weaker strength, these interactions may still play functional roles. For instance, POMGNT1 participates in O-mannosyl glycosylation of α-dystroglycan, suggesting potential modulation of laminin–dystroglycan binding. Secondary sarcoglycans (SGCG, SGCB, SGCD) and utrophin (UTRN) are known to stabilize membrane complexes, indicating that even peripheral interactors could influence LAMA2-related signaling pathways. Moreover, extracellular matrix proteins such as AGRN, NID1, NID2, and HSPG2 contribute to basement membrane assembly and neuromuscular junction integrity, further supporting the notion that LAMA2 is embedded in a larger network regulating muscle function and stability. Collectively, the primary and secondary interactions emphasize that LAMA2 functions not in isolation but as part of an extended protein network whose disruption could have profound implications for the pathogenesis of congenital muscular dystrophies.

Discussion

Owing to the scarcity of CMD in Asian origin especially Iranians, we conducted a study to evaluate genetic background of CMD. In the current investigation, we found a novel compound heterozygous variant of LAMA2 (c.2049_2050del; c.2857–2 A >G). In terms of variants spectrum, frequently reported genotypes are those in which premature termination codon with truncated protein were created, in contrast, missense mutations are present in small number of patients. Also, large proportion of LAMA2 mutations are deletions with/without frameshift (Fig. 2c). Some of them are located in splice sites (donor/acceptor) and due to limitation of laboratory studies, impact of them mostly evaluated via bioinformatic tools to find deleterious and pathogenic effects. For an instance, c.819 + 2 T >C, located in the donor splice site of intron 5 was an aberrant transcript [13]. In our data, we had both deletion and substitution which cause frameshift and splice site variant, respectively. Our patient was from a non-consanguineous marriage of heterozygous parents for each variant. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first report of this variant, so far [5, 10, 13–17]. According to previous studies of LAMA2 gene variants database (LOVD), 886 unique public variants have been reported in 1706 individuals proposing heterogeneity of LAMA2-CMD. According to LOVD, only 6 LAMA2 variants in Iranians showed pathogenic rare origin as previously reported for Asians, while LAMA2 involvement in CMD in Iranian families are rare [13]. Most of CMD patients have a dystrophy in their muscles classified as type 1 (MDC1A) and patients mostly have muscle weakness in early stage of their lives and inability to sit without support which we observed as well. Other manifestations including creatine kinase (CK) level elevation in muscle biopsies [18], chest infections, and respiratory failures were also reported in our patient which is a hallmark of CMD patients [19]. However, we could not observe any white matter changes in her MRI or motor nerve conduction declining as previously reported in studies [20, 21]. For a genetically heterogenous disease like CMD, NGS (both WES and WGS) can be the best diagnostic strategy [21–24]. Recent studies confirmed the effectiveness of NGS on clinical studies of CMD, especially in those patients where only a heterozygous variant was found in exonic regions of the LAMA2 gene and need deep sequencing of intronic variants that affect splicing or in promotor regions. The precise function of LAMA2 protein is still unknown but it is thought to mediate organization of cells into tissues, migration, differentiation and cell adhesion. Martins et al. showed LAMA2 deficiency affects proliferation and cell cycle of myoblast cells in vitro. Indeed, in LAMA2-deficient cells the level of ROS elevated which affects mitochondrial integrity and may linked to incomplete proliferation. Due to failure of cytoskeletal organization the differentiation of myoblasts into myotubes might fail [25]. LAMA2 absence may alter signaling pathways related to communication between intra- and extra-cellular regions like JAK/STAT and FAK and could alter ECM rigidity [26]. Further investigations, such as Western blotting and cell-based protein expression assays, could be conducted to assess the protein-level impact of these variants, providing deeper insights into their functional consequences. The LAMA2 is a conserved protein during evolution. Any changes of the conserved position could be pathogenic and, in our report, the variant showed damaging effect and pathogenic role. According to prediction of computational tools, it is the first time that this variant has been reported in Iranian population. The observed clinical features were remarkably similar to previous studies, nevertheless there were some differences. For instance, Nouri et al. reported cardiomyopathy, and autism-like behavior in a patient with CMD phenotype with LAMA2 p.Tyr1313LeufsTer4 [5]. In some cases, a LAMA2 mutation was associated with dependent ambulant and need wheel chair. Other clinical features of our proband including feeding help have been observed as well in British patients [27]. Moreover, brain MRI displayed overlapp of our findings with some cases. However, in others, white matter changes have been observed as well. These included cortical abnormalities (pachygyria, and agyria mainly in occipital lobes, and mild cortical atrophy), atypical white matter change, and ventricular dilation white matter changes in signal intensity.

Conclusion

This study identified a novel compound heterozygous variant in the LAMA2 gene (c.2049_2050del; c.2857–2 A > G), expanding the genetic spectrum of CMD in rare cases from Iran. Our findings emphasize the critical role of genetic testing, particularly whole-exome sequencing (WES), in diagnosing rare diseases with genetic heterogeneity. WES provides valuable insights into both exonic and intronic mutations, enabling accurate diagnosis in complex cases and guiding further research into pathogenic mechanisms.
