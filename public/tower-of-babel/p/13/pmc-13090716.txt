Two-step bioconversion of pyridoxal 5′-phosphate from pyridoxine through cofactor regeneration and pyridoxine 5′-phosphate oxidase evolution
Authors: Xinwei Yang, Beisi Lu, Meiling Wang, Yuchen Chen, Xiyue Lu, Yong Tao, Jianzhong Huang, Chongrong Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP), a vital cofactor in cellular metabolism, is a high-value compound widely used in the food and pharmaceutical industries. However, the efficient biosynthesis of PLP remains a significant challenge. In this study, we established a salvage pathway for the synthesis of PLP from pyridoxine (PN) using PN kinase (PdxK) from Salmonella enterica and pyridoxine 5′-phosphate oxidase (PdxH) from Saccharomyces cerevisiae as the key enzymes. To increase economic viability, an ATP regeneration system was integrated, enabling the use of AMP and polyP as substrates instead of expensive ATP. Additionally, an O2 circulating system was incorporated to provide sufficient oxygen supply and a H2O2-free environment. PdxH was identified as the rate-limiting enzyme, so we performed directed evolution of ScPdxH through random mutagenesis coupled with high-throughput screening. The optimized mutant, ScPdxH2−3E, exhibited a 3.4-fold increase in kcat/Km compared to the wild-type enzyme. Using a two-step bioconversion process, we achieved the production of 35.2 g/L pyridoxine 5′-phosphate (PNP) with a molar conversion of 94.7%, and 21.3 g/L PLP, corresponding to a final molar conversion yield of approximately 57.5% from PN in a 1-L bioreactor. This work demonstrates an efficient, sustainable and scalable strategy for the industrial production of PLP.

Introduction

Vitamin B6 is a water-soluble vitamin that contains six convertible pyridine derivatives, including pyridoxine (PN), pyridoxamine (PM), pyridoxal (PL), pyridoxine 5′-phosphate (PNP), pyridoxamine 5′-phosphate (PMP), and pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP) [1]. Among them, PN hydrochloride represents the predominant commercial form [2], while PLP serves as the biologically active form that functions as a cofactor for more than 160 enzymes [3]. To date, all sequenced bacterial genomes encode PLP-dependent enzymes, which are related to diverse biochemical pathways, including transamination, decarboxylation, racemization, and various elimination reactions [4]. Two de novo synthesis routes of PLP in organisms are the 1-deoxy-d-xylulose-5-phosphate (DXP)-dependent pathway and the DXP-independent pathway [5]. The DXP-independent pathway exists in most vitamin B6 autotrophic organisms and is the dominant PLP synthesis route, whereas the DXP-dependent pathway is found primarily in α- and γ-proteobacteria [6]. Most organisms can acquire PLP through direct uptake of extracellular vitamers (PL, PN, and PM) coupled with an endogenous salvage pathway that enzymatically converts these vitamers into their phosphorylated forms via kinase-mediated (PN/PL/PM kinase, PdxK) phosphorylation, followed by oxidase-catalyzed (PNP/PMP oxidase, PdxH) oxidation.

Currently, PN (PLP precursor) is produced primarily through traditional chemical synthesis, which relies heavily on organic reagents and high-temperature processes and often involves chlorination reactions and the generation of toxic byproducts and intermediates [7]. Thus, the microbial synthesis of vitamin B6 has emerged as a promising area of research. Large-scale screening studies revealed that the gram-negative bacterium Ensifer meliloti (formerly Sinorhizobium meliloti or Rhizobium meliloti) is the best producer of vitamin B6, and its highest production reached 1.30 g/L PN when the E. coli epd (encoding 4-phosphoerythronate dehydrogenase) gene and the native dxs (encoding DXP synthase) gene were expressed after cultivation for 7 days [8]. Additionally, E. coli and B. subtilis were chosen as efficient workhorses to produce vitamin B6. Through systematic engineering of the DXP-dependent pathway, PN production in E. coli was increased to 1.4 g/L by decoupling cell growth from biosynthesis and incorporating engineered variants of PdxA (4-phosphohydroxy-l-threonine dehydrogenase) and PdxJ (PNP synthase) as well as high-performing Epd and Dxs [9]. While omics-guided fermentation optimization increased the PN titer to 1.95 g/L in 70 h of fed-batch fermentation [10], a subsequent strategy employing restricted mixed-carbon feeding and pressure-controlled fermentation in a 5 L bioreactor further elevated the titer to 3.33 g/L [11]. Mutation of Epd and overexpression of the glycerol-pentose phosphate pathway subsequently resulted in a PN yield of 2.12 g/L by batch fermentation [12]. Notably, co-expression of PLP phosphatase (YbhA) and PdxH could produce 1.58 g/L PLP, which was then converted to 2.30 g/L PN by a second strain expressing membrane-anchored YbhA and PdxI (pyridoxal reductase) [13]. Meanwhile, B. subtilis has also been engineered to produce 65 mg/L PN by hetero-expressing the pdxA and pdxJ genes from E. coli when supplied with the precursor 4-hydroxy-l-threonine (4HT) and an amino acid cocktail of Asp, Lys, Thr, Met and Ile [14]. Despite extensive engineering efforts (Table S1), none of the genetically engineered microorganisms have achieved industrial production target to compete with chemical synthesis process [15].

As a highly reactive cofactor prone to spontaneous chemical reactions, PLP molecule are kept low in the cell through strict cellular regulation [3]. The PdxST (PLP synthase PdxS and glutamine aminotransferase PdxT) enzyme complex consisting of 12 PdxS and 12 PdxT subunits in the DXP-independent pathway demonstrates limited catalytic efficiency and poor tractability for rational design [16]. Similarly, the DXP-dependent pathway contains a series of “sluggish” enzymes that exhibit low turnover numbers and high Km values and are also difficult to engineer [9]. The utilization of the salvage pathway offers a superior strategy for enhancing PLP production at the industrial scale. The engineered acid phosphatase from Salmonella typhi and pyridoxine oxidase from E. coli were coupled as pathway enzymes to synthesize PLP from PN and pyrophosphate (PPi), and ∼14.5 g/L PLP was produced from 0.2 mol/L PN HCl and 0.28 mol/L PPi, with a molar conversion rate of ∼29% from PN to PLP [17] (Table S1).

Therefore, our study leveraged the salvage pathway to circumvent the de novo biosynthesis route, establishing an efficient dual-enzyme cascade (PdxK/PdxH) for direct PLP synthesis from low-cost PN (Fig. 1). However, two critical limitations were identified: 1) potent product inhibition through tight binding of PLP to residue Lys229 of PdxK [16] and the requirement for expensive adenosine triphosphate (ATP) as an essential phosphate donor; 2) the production of H2O2 as a ROS during PdxH catalysis, and PLP tightly binds to an allosteric site of oxidase, causing the inhibition of the enzyme and thereby limiting its activity. To address this problem, four modifications were implemented: 1) the bioconversion process was divided into two steps to prevent PLP-mediated inhibition of PdxK; 2) an ATP regeneration system was introduced to supply self-sufficient ATP by utilizing inexpensive phosphate donors; 3) the O2 circulating system was incorporated to ensure an adequate oxygen supply for PLP biosynthesis from PNP and cell integrity against peroxide; and 4) an HTS platform was established to identify PdxH variants with enhanced catalytic activity. Finally, the optimal mutant of PdxH was isolated, and 21.3 g/L (86.2 ± 2.2 mM) PLP was obtained via two-step bioconversion, resulting in a molar conversion rate of ∼57.5% from PN to PLP (Table S1). Genome manipulation

Six pdxK genes, two ppk genes, five pdxH genes and two catalase genes were artificially synthesized via codon optimization by GenScript Biotech Corporation (Shanghai, China) (Table S2). The EcpdxK and EcpdxH genes were amplified from the genome of E. coli BW25113. The purified DNA fragments were subsequently inserted into the pYB1s via a ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) with the primers P1–P52 (Table S3). Next, the verified recombinant plasmids were transformed into competent cells of the corresponding strains to obtain whole-cell biocatalysts. The genes involved in the degradation of PLP/PNP (ybhA or yigL) were knocked out via the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing system as previously described with the primers P53–P65 (Table S3). The mutant strains were confirmed via PCR and then verified via gene sequencing (SUNYA Co., Fuzhou, China).

Bioconversion conditions

The engineered strains were cultured in ZYM media supplemented with 50 μg/mL streptomycin and 0.2% (w/v) l-arabinose as an inducer at 30 °C and 200 rpm. After induction for 19 h, the cells were harvested via centrifugation and subsequently washed twice with ice-cold 0.9% NaCl solution. The two-step bioconversion system proceeded as follows: in the first step, a cell lysate prepared from catalytic cells (20 OD600/mL, ∼15 g/L wet cell weight) via ultrasonication was used to convert PN to PNP in 100 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 5.0) containing 50 mM PN, 10 mM AMP, and 11 mM polyphosphate (polyP), following the introduction of an ATP-regeneration system. In the second step, permeabilized whole cells (treated with repeated freeze-thaw cycles at −80 °C and adjusted to 20 OD600/mL) were resuspended in 50 mM PNP supernatant from the first step, supplemented with 5 mM FMN as the essential cofactor, and the pH was adjusted to 7.6. For scale-up in a 1 L bioreactor (700 mL working volume), a fed-batch strategy was applied in the first step: starting with 50 mM PN, an additional 25 mM PN was supplemented at 1, 3, 5, and 7 h (total PN input 150 mM), with PN and polyP co-fed at a 3:1 molar ratio while maintaining the pH at 5.0. In the second step, the PNP supernatant was adjusted to pH 7.6, supplemented with 10 mM FMN, and the pH was maintained at 7.6. All reactions were performed at 30 °C with shaking or stirring at 200 rpm for 6–24 h. Samples were taken at intervals, quenched with 5 mM H2SO4, centrifuged, diluted, filtered (0.22 μm), and analyzed by HPLC using an external PLP standard.

Mutant library construction and high-throughput screening

Random mutagenesis of PdxH3 was performed via error-prone PCR (EP-PCR) via primers PH-F (5′-gctaacaggaggaattaaccatgggtactaagcaagctgaagaga-3′) and PH-R (5′-tcaccgaattcaccactagtttacggagcgaggcgaacc-3′) with the QuickMutationTM Random Mutagenesis Kit (Beyotime, China). The resulting PCR products were ligated into the pYB1s expression vector via Gibson Assembly and subsequently transformed into electrocompetent E. coli BW25113 cells to generate a mutant library.

The engineered strains harboring PdxH3 variants were cultured in 96-deep-well microtiter plates (MTPs) containing 1000 μL of ZYM media with 50 μg/mL streptomycin at 30 °C for 22 h. After OD600 measurement, 900 μL of cell suspension from each well was transferred to a fresh 96-well plate, then pelleted by centrifugation, and resuspended in 300 μL of reaction buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 20 mM PNP, 2 mM FMN, pH 7.6). The reactions proceeded at 30 °C with vigorous shaking (615 rpm) for 3 h in the dark, and PLP formation was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 414 nm [18] using a SpectraMax microplate reader (Molecular Devices, CA, USA). PdxH3 variants were identified by their relatively high absorbance values (OD414/OD600), and variants exhibiting enhanced PdxH activity were further validated via whole-cell bioconversion in shake flasks over 12 h. The top three performers were selected for subsequent protein purification and enzymatic characterization.

Expression, purification, and characterization of the PdxH3 variants

The original pdxH3 gene and its variant genes were inserted into pET28a for recombinant expression. Proteins were purified from cell lysates via Ni-NTA affinity chromatography using an ÄKTA pure system (Cytiva, USA). After purification, the imidazole was removed by overnight dialysis against 50 mM PBS (pH 7.6) via Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (Merck Millipore, USA). Protein concentrations were determined via a Bradford assay kit (Sangon Biotech, China). The enzymatic activity of purified PdxH3 was determined via a modified literature-based assay system [19]. One unit (U) of enzymatic activity was defined as 1.0 μmol of PLP produced in 1 min at 30 °C at a pH of 7.6. To determine the Km and kcat values of PdxH3 and its variants, the initial rate of the reaction was measured at a PNP concentration range of 0–4 mM at pH 7.6 and 30 °C. Kinetic parameters were calculated by nonlinear regression analysis of the Michaelis-Menten equation using ORIGIN PRO 2021 (OriginLab Co., USA).

Analytical methods

The concentrations of PN, PNP, PLP, ATP, ADP, and AMP were measured via HPLC (Thermo UltiMate 3000 system) via a YMC-Triart C18 (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) column. For PN, PL, PNP and PLP, detection was performed at 319 nm with a 5 μL injection volume and a 0.5 mL/min flow rate at 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) methanol and (B) 4.5 mM sodium heptanesulfonate (pH 3.0, adjusted with H3PO4). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0 min (5% A), 10 min (10% A), 15 min (80% A), 20 min (80% A), 25 min (5% A), and 30 min (5% A). An isocratic elution method was employed for ATP, ADP, and AMP analysis, in which 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (K2HPO4/KH2PO4, pH ∼6.8) was used as the mobile phase, with UV detection at 235 nm and a column temperature of 35 °C. The injection volume was 5 μL, and the flow rate of the mobile phase was 0.5 mL/min. The optical density of the cells was determined by a UV-visible spectrophotometer.

Phylogenetic tree and molecular modeling

PdxH sequences were retrieved from UniProt using E. coli PdxH (UniProt ID: P0AFI7) as the probe. Redundant sequences were removed with CD-HIT. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA11 with 1000 bootstrap replicates and subsequently visualized using the Itol web tool (https://itol.embl.de/). The tertiary structure of S. cerevisiae PdxH (ScPdxH; PDB ID: 1CI0) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank. Molecular docking of PLP with ScPdxH was performed via AutoDock Vina (version 1.5.7), and ScPdxH variant structures were predicted via AlphaFold3. All structural visualization, graphical representation, and molecular interaction analyses were conducted in PyMOL (version 3.1.4.1).

Statistics and reproducibility

All the experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results are presented as the means ± SDs. The data analysis was performed primarily by ORIGIN PRO 2021 (OriginLab Co., USA).

Results

Construction of the PNP biosynthetic pathway from PN

A heterologous B6 salvage pathway from PN to PNP was constructed in E. coli for PN biosynthesis by overexpressing PN kinase (PdxK). On the basis of the enzymatic activity data from the BRENDA database, six PdxKs were identified in the UniProt database using the sequence of PdxK from E. coli (UniProt ID: A8A2R4) as a probe. The codon-optimized PdxKs from Homo sapiens (PdxK1, UniProt ID: O00764), Plasmodium falciparum (PdxK2, UniProt ID: W7F4Z2), Salmonella enterica (PdxK3, UniProt ID: P40192), Bordetella pertussis (PdxK4, UniProt ID: Q7VYK4), Enterococcus faecalis (PdxK5, UniProt ID: A0A1Y0LX27) and Staphylococcus aureus (PdxK6, UniProt ID: Q5HI96) were inserted into the pYB1s plasmid, while the native PdxK (PdxK7) overexpression strain served as a control. The resulting E. coli strain BW25113 containing pYB1s-pdxK1–7 (designated as BK1–7) were used as the biocatalysts, and 0.5-36.6 mM PNP was obtained from 50 mM PN in a 12 h bioconversion with the addition of 50 mM ATP. Among them, the engineered BK-3 strain (expressing PdxK from S. enterica) achieved maximal PNP production (36.6 ± 0.7 mM) with nearly 1:1 ATP stoichiometry, significantly outperforming both heterologous and native PdxK-expressing strains (Fig. 2a).Fig. 2Screening of PdxK enzymes for PNP production using PN as the substrate. During the reaction, PdxK requires an equivalent amount of ATP as a phosphate donor; therefore, an ATP regeneration system was introduced to reduce the addition of ATP. The Class III polyphosphate kinase 2 enzymes from Meiothermus ruber (Ppk1) [20] and from Cytophaga hutchinsonii (Ppk2) [21] were inserted into pYB1s-pdxK3 in different orders to construct the ATP regeneration module. Specifically, four plasmids (pYB1s-pdxK3-ppk1, pYB1s-ppk1-pdxK3, pYB1s-pdxK3-ppk2 and pYB1s-ppk2-pdxK3) were transformed into the chassis BW25113 (designated as BAK1–4). After 6 h of bioconversion, approximately 98% of the PN was converted to PNP (49 mM) in the BAK-4 strain, and the productivity of PNP biosynthesis was 8.2 mM/h, which was faster than that in the other strains (Fig. 2b). Notably, the bioconversion required only 10 mM AMP across all four strains, indicating efficient PPK2-mediated ATP regeneration during PNP biosynthesis. The optimized BAK-4 strain was subsequently employed for all further studies.

Construction of the PLP biosynthetic pathway from PNP

During PLP biosynthesis, oxygen serves as the co-substrate of the salvage pathway from PNP to PLP, whereas H2O2 is the co-product of PLP. Thus, the catalases that regenerated O2 from H2O2 were selected from Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Cat1) [22] and Bacillus pumilus (Cat2) [23] and then inserted into pYB1s-pdxH3 in different orders (pYB1s-pdxH3-cat1, pYB1s-cat1-pdxH3, pYB1s-pdxH3-cat2 and pYB1s-cat2-pdxH3) to provide sufficient oxygen supply and a H2O2-free environment for PLP biosynthesis (designated as BHO1–4 strains). When catalase from M. tuberculosis was functionally co-expressed with PdxH3 in the BHO2 strain, the highest PLP production (27.2 ± 0.6 mM, ∼6.7 g/L) was achieved within 12 h of whole-cell bioconversion (Fig. 3b). The productivity improved to 2.3 mM/h, an increase of 156% compared with that without O2 cycling, indicating that the introduction of an endogenous O2 circulating system was beneficial for PLP biosynthesis. However, the conversion from PNP to PLP reached a yield of only 54.4%, with 2–5 mM PN produced during bioconversion, and the initial rate of this reaction was only ∼5–6 mM/h. In sharp contrast, the first step (PN to PNP) achieved a yield of ∼98% and a much higher initial rate of ∼26–28 mM/h. The lower conversion efficiency and reaction rate of the second step clearly indicate that PdxH acts as the rate-limiting enzyme in this two-step pathway and that the PNP degradation needs to be considered.

High-throughput screening of PdxH variants

To improve the efficiency of PdxH, a random mutant library was constructed by cloning the mutant pdxH3ep genes into the pYB1s plasmid and subsequently transformed into E. coli BW25113. In general, there is a linear relationship between PLP production and the OD414 reading values (Fig. S2), so a high-throughput screening approach based on random mutagenesis and a microplate reader was established. By employing this screening procedure, approximately 1504 clones were screened after EP-PCR of the pdxH3 gene (Fig. S3), and 104 clones exhibiting higher OD414/OD600 values than those of the original strain were selected as candidate positive mutants. After the value of the fold change was set to 2, 23 clones were cultivated for whole-cell bioconversion, and PLP production was detected after 12 h. Blocking the competitive pathways and scale-up

The ybhA and yigL genes encode phosphatases that catalyze the dephosphorylation of PLP and PNP [24]. To avoid the degradation of PLP and the direct precursor PNP in the second step, the ybhA and yigL genes were separately knocked out from the locus of the genome in the original E. coli BW25113 chassis to construct the N001 and N002 strains. As a result, disruption of YbhA in strain YH1O2 increased PLP production to 35.5 ± 1.8 mM, a 6.9% improvement over that of the original BH1O2 strain. Similarly, deletion of YigL in strain LH1O2 yielded 34.8 ± 1.4 mM PLP, corresponding to a 4.8% increase. The recombinant strain NH1O2 with both ybhA and yigL double deletion achieved a PLP titer of 36.2 ± 1.8 mM after 12 h of whole-cell bioconversion with stable PN levels and no detectable PL throughout the reaction, resulting in a molar conversion ratio of 72.4% from PNP to PLP.

To scale up the process and maximize PNP yield, the engineered strain NAK4 (E. coli BW25113△ybhA△yigL with pYB1s-ppk2-pdxK3) was employed for PNP production via a fed-batch of the direct substrate PN in a 1-L bioreactor with a 700 mL working volume. When the concentration of PN was increased to 150 mM, approximately 142.1 ± 2.9 mM (∼35.2 g/L) PNP was produced within 14 h, the conversion ratio reached 94.7%, and the productivity was 10.15 mM/h (Fig. 5a). After that, 142.1 mM PNP was completely used as the direct substrate for PLP production using the NH1O2 strain (E. coli BW25113△ybhA△yigL with pYB1s-cat1-PdxH32−3E) as the whole-cell biocatalyst in a 1-L bioreactor, 86.2 ± 2.2 mM (∼21.3 g/L) PLP was achieved after 12 h of bioconversion, attaining a molar conversion ratio of 60.7% from PNP to PLP, which was 35.7% greater than that of the control strain BHO2 (E. coli BW25113 with pYB1s-cat1-pdxH3) with the original PdxH3 in the E. coli BW25113 chassis (63.5 ± 2.3 mM) (Fig. 5b). Discussion

In the field of the biosynthesis of vitamin B6, most current research has focused predominantly on de novo DXP-dependent synthetic pathways, with PN remaining the primary product due to its superior stability compared with that of other vitamers. Through metabolic engineering, rational design of key enzymes and fermentation optimization, the ability of native or recombinant strains to produce PN has been enhanced to approximately 1.9–3.3 g/L by fed-batch fermentation [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]] (Table S1). Despite extensive engineering efforts, none of the genetically engineered microorganisms achieved the industrial production target of 10 g/L PN in 48 h required to compete with the chemical synthesis process 14. Here, we focused on the biosynthesis of PLP, the active form of vitamin B6, which serves as an essential cofactor for approximately 4% of all classified enzymatic reactions [25].

Among the PLP-dependent enzymes, aldolase, decarboxylase and transaminase have emerged as particularly valuable biocatalysts for stereoselective organic synthesis [26,27]. Some enzymes have been widely used in industrial biocatalysis, including threonine aldolase (TA) for the synthesis of valuable α-amino, β-hydroxy-building blocks [28]; lysine decarboxylase or glutamate decarboxylase for the sustainable production of cadaverine or γ-aminobutyrate (GABA) [29,30]; transaminase, which enables the asymmetric synthesis of L‐PPT from the keto acid substrate [31]; and tyrosine phenol-lyase, which catalyzes the one-pot synthesis of l-DOPA from pyruvate, ammonia, and catechol [32]. In reactions catalyzed by a PLP-dependent enzyme, the addition of exogenous PLP could increase the catalytic efficiency, increase the reaction rate and prolong the duration of biotransformation. However, the necessity for stoichiometric PLP supplementation elevates production costs by 15–30% (based on current cofactor market prices), constituting a major bottleneck for the industrial-scale implementation of PLP-dependent biocatalysis. Therefore, we targeted a salvage pathway using low-cost PN as the substrate to efficiently produce the biologically active form of PLP to meet industrial requirements.

Owing to the reactivity and potential hepatotoxicity of PLP, cells maintain free PLP at approximately 1 μM through multiple regulatory mechanisms [33]. Therefore, product inhibition of PdxK/PdxH serves as the key bottleneck in PLP biosynthesis from PN. To circumvent this limitation, a two-step bioconversion system was applied over a one-pot system in this study, effectively decoupling PLP production from the PdxK-catalyzed step. Under comparable conditions, the one-pot system at its optimum pH (pH 7.6) generated only ∼12 mM PLP after 12 h of bioconversion, whereas the two-step system achieved approximately 26.5 mM PLP (Fig. S4). When the ATP regeneration system was introduced to replace the expensive ATP with the phosphate donor polyP and fed-batch of the direct substrate PN in the first step, the PNP titer reached 35.2 g/L in a 1-L bioreactor within 14 h of bioconversion. Nevertheless, the two-step system presents several limitations: 1) a prolonged total processing time of 26 h; 2) increased operational complexity, such as separate catalyst preparations, pH adjustments for each step, and the need for PNP separation and transfer; 3) a moderate overall conversion yield from PN to PLP (∼57% in a 1-L bioreactor). Therefore, critical efforts should focus on enhancing industrial practicality in the future: 1) continuously screening and evolving of PdxH to relieve PLP-mediated inhibition; 2) optimizing the fed-batch strategy for the first step to alleviate the inhibition by PN and PolyP at high concentrations; 3) developing a high-efficiency one-pot system for efficient PN-to-PLP bioconversion.

To increase the conversion yield of the second step from PNP to PLP, the catalytic efficiency of PdxH was improved in our study through directed evolution employing error-prone PCR coupled with high-throughput screening. The PdxH3 sequence screened in our study was from S. cerevisiae (ScPdxH) and shares 41.6% sequence similarity with PdxH6 from E. coli (EcPdxH), which contains an N-terminal 'arginine cage' motif (Arg23, Arg24 and Arg215) that can effectively trap PLP at its allosteric site [34,35]. According to the structural simulation, the conserved Arg225 residue, the Arg160 residue at the N-terminus of the longest helix and the Arg202 residue situated in the first protomer in a long loop like a lid, compose an 'arginine cage' of ScPdxH [33]. Random mutagenesis introduced five mutations of the best performer (ScPdxH2−3E variant), comprising two silent mutations (positions136/202) and three functional substitutions (E122V, L206S, K222Q), which collectively increased catalytic efficiency 3.4-fold relative to that of the wild-type. Furthermore, the pH tolerance and thermostability of the evolved ScPdxH2−3E variant were detected, and the variant exhibited optimal activity in a slightly alkaline range (pH 7.5–8.0) and maintained functionality from 20 °C to 50 °C, indicating its potential for industrial application (Fig. S5).

Among the three mutated residues, both the L206 and K222 sites are close to the "arginine cage", and their mutations may cause damage to the arginine cage, making PLP binding to the allosteric site unstable. In particular, L206 forms a hydrogen bond with PLP, and mutation from an aliphatic leucine with a hydrophobic side chain to a polar serine with a neutral side chain disrupts the hydrogen bond and native hydrophobic microenvironment around the allosteric site, reduces the steric hindrance near the allosteric site and relieves the allosteric feedback of PLP due to structural changes [34] (Fig. 6a,b). To verify this hypothesis, we determined the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of PLP for both wild-type ScPdxH and the evolved ScPdxH2−3E variant, using 4 mM PNP and 0.4 mM FMN as substrates. A measurable increase in the IC50 of PLP from 2.4 mM (wild-type ScPdxH) to 2.7 mM (ScPdxH2−3E variant) was observed, and this higher IC50 value indicates that the variant exhibited reduced sensitivity to allosteric inhibition, supporting the speculation that damage to the “arginine cage” impairs PLP binding, thereby alleviating feedback inhibition. The third mutation at the E122 site is adjacent to the FMN-binding site Arg120, engaging it through one hydrogen bond, whereas Arg120 in turn coordinates FMN via two hydrogen bonds. When glutamic acid (E122) is mutated to valine, the loss of H-bond interaction with Arg120 is predicted to decrease the stability of FMN binding, increasing the cofactor dissociation rate. The resulting change in FMN affinity increased the enzyme's catalytic turnover (0.65 ± 0.01 min−1 for wild-type ScPdxH vs. 0.97 ± 0.03 min−1 for the ScPdxH2−3E variant), thereby enhancing its catalytic efficiency (Fig. 6c,d).Fig. 6Analysis of the catalytic conformation of wild-type ScPdxH and its mutant ScPdxH2−3E on the basis of autodocking results and molecular dynamics analysis. In conclusion, an efficient two-step bioconversion system for PLP synthesis from PN was established. This system was achieved by integrating an ATP regeneration system to drive PdxK-catalyzed PN phosphorylation with an O2 circulating system to support an evolved ScPdxH variant obtained through random mutagenesis and high-throughput screening. This two-step bioconversion operates under aqueous conditions at ambient temperature and near-neutral pH, utilizes AMP and PolyP in place of costly ATP or toxic reagents, and employs atom-economical cofactor recycling throughout the entire bioconversion process. Ultimately, this system achieved a PLP titer of 21.3 g/L (86.2 ± 2.2 mM), corresponding to an ∼57.5% molar conversion yield from PN to PLP in a 1-L bioreactor. This study demonstrates a viable strategy and proposes an effective and sustainable approach to PLP biomanufacturing at an industrial scale.
