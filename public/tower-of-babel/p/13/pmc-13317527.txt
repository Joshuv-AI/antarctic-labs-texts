Renal cell carcinoma intraspinal metastases: a peripheral nerve sheath tumor mimic. Illustrative case
Authors: Sean O’Leary, Aiyana Adams, Christian Ogasawara, Marc Prablek, Rishi Lall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDRenal cell carcinoma (RCC) metastases to the spinal cord, cauda equina, nerve root, and dorsal root ganglion (DRG) are rare and can mimic benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors.OBSERVATIONSA PRISMA-guided review of the PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, and Google Scholar databases identified 47 studies consisting of 57 patients with intradural extramedullary (IDEM), intramedullary spinal cord, or nerve root/DRG RCC metastases. The mean age was 59.4 ± 12.4 years, 71.9% were male, and clear cell RCC was the most common subtype (59.6%). Lesions involved intramedullary spinal cord metastasis in 57.9%, IDEM disease in 31.6%, and primary nerve root/DRG metastases in 10.5%. IDEM and nerve root lesions most often caused pain and radiculopathy, whereas intramedullary lesions uniformly produced myelopathy or motor weakness and more frequent sphincter dysfunction. Surgery was performed in 71.9% of patients, with gross-total resection in 80.5% of operative cases. The illustrative case was a symptomatic left C6–7 foraminal mass with focal Ga-68 DOTATATE uptake and progressive radiculopathy with mild left upper extremity weakness that mimicked schwannoma but was confirmed intraoperatively as metastatic RCC; resection and stabilization achieved durable symptom resolution.LESSONSNew foraminal or dumbbell-shaped lesions in patients with malignancy warrant tissue diagnosis to guide appropriate metastasis-directed management. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26244 Keywords: renal cell carcinoma, nerve root, dorsal root ganglion, intradural extramedullary, intramedullary spinal cord metastasis, peripheral nerve sheath tumor, schwannoma mimicABBREVIATIONS: DRG = dorsal root ganglion, IDEM = intradural extramedullary, ISCM = intramedullary spinal cord metastasis, MPNST = malignant PNST, NET = neuroendocrine tumor, PNST = peripheral nerve sheath tumor, RCC = renal cell carcinoma

Metastatic involvement of the peripheral nervous system by renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is exceedingly rare, with most peripheral nerve tumors being benign and arising from nerve sheath elements such as schwannomas or neurofibromas. When metastasis to the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) or nerve root occurs, it can closely mimic primary peripheral nerve sheath tumors (PNSTs), including malignant PNSTs (MPNSTs), both clinically and radiographically.1,2 This diagnostic overlap is particularly significant given the aggressive nature and poor prognosis associated with MPNSTs, which account for 5%–10% of soft tissue sarcomas and are often associated with neurofibromatosis type 1, but can also arise sporadically or after radiation exposure.3–7

The clinical presentation of both metastatic RCC and MPNSTs in the nerve root region may include progressive pain, radiculopathy, and neurological deficits, while imaging features such as avid enhancement on MRI and increased uptake on PET/CT further complicate differentiation.1,2,3,5 Histopathological evaluation remains the gold standard for diagnosis, as radiological findings alone are often insufficient to distinguish between metastatic carcinoma and primary nerve sheath malignancies.2–,4,8 Notably, rare cases have been reported where metastatic RCC to the cauda equina or DRG was initially presumed to be a nerve sheath tumor based on imaging and intraoperative appearance, only to be reclassified following pathological analysis.2,9

Given the increasing survival of patients with metastatic RCC, awareness of this diagnostic pitfall is crucial for neurosurgeons, oncologists, and pathologists. Accurate diagnosis impacts management strategies, as treatment paradigms for metastatic carcinoma differ significantly from those for primary MPNSTs, which typically require aggressive resection and may involve adjuvant therapies.3,6,7 Here, we present a systematic literature review on RCC metastasis to the nerve root and DRGs, along with a case illustration underscoring the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis for nerve root lesions, especially in patients with a history of malignancy, and highlight the need for tissue diagnosis to guide appropriate therapy.2,3,9

Illustrative Case

A 58-year-old woman with a history of RCC after left radical nephrectomy, retroperitoneal lymph node dissection, and partial right nephrectomy, as well as pancreatic lesions concerning for a neuroendocrine tumor (NET), presented with progressive neck pain described as soreness with movement. The pain radiated from the neck into the posterior arm and forearm in a C7 dermatomal distribution and was associated with mild triceps weakness (4+/5), consistent with a left C7 radiculopathy. Ga-68 DOTATATE PET imaging demonstrated diffuse uptake in the pancreatic body and tail, suggestive of a possible NET (Krenning score 2), as well as abnormal radiotracer uptake along the left C6–7 neural foramen corresponding to a 1.4-cm soft tissue lesion (Fig. 1). Prior surveillance imaging showed no evidence of residual or recurrent disease at the renal primary site and no evidence of disseminated RCC. Subsequent MRI revealed an avidly enhancing lesion within the left C6–7 neural foramen, corresponding to the area of radiotracer uptake on PET/CT, suspicious for RCC metastasis or PNST (Fig. 2).

Given the concerning clinical and radiographic findings, management options were discussed extensively with the patient. In the absence of progressive radiculopathy or weakness, short-interval surveillance imaging may have been considered; however, due to worsening symptoms, surgical intervention for tissue diagnosis and resection was deemed appropriate. The patient was counseled that the procedure would likely require resection of the left C6–7 facet joint to access the lesion, necessitating posterior instrumented fusion to address the resultant instability. She was further informed that if intraoperative frozen-section analysis suggested an MPNST, en bloc resection might be required, potentially resulting in postoperative weakness and sensory deficits.

The patient was taken to the operating room, intubated, and positioned prone with multimodal neuromonitoring. Following standard sterile preparation and a time-out, a midline posterior cervical incision was made, and subperiosteal exposure was carried from C4 to T2. Intraoperative O-arm navigation guided placement of pedicle screws at C4, C5, T1, and T2 bilaterally, with omission of left-sided C6 and C7 screws to allow for tumor exposure. After decompression with removal of the C6–7 spinous processes and left C6–7 facet, an abnormal, enlarged nerve root was identified within the left C6–7 foramen. On opening the root sleeve, a friable, vascular, tan tumor was encountered without identifiable normal fascicles. Stimulation of the lesion revealed no motor activity. Frozen-section analysis demonstrated metastatic RCC. Careful microsurgical debulking was performed using the SONOPET ultrasonic aspirator (Stryker) with continuous neurostimulation, stopping once proximity responses were noted. The vertebral artery was visualized and preserved. After hemostasis, a dural onlay (DuraGen) and sealant (DuraSeal) were applied, and a medium Hemovac drain was placed. Instrumented fusion was completed with rods secured to the existing screws. The wound was closed in a layered fashion. There were no intraoperative complications, and the patient awoke with a stable neurological examination.

At the 1-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated continued improvement in her preoperative neck pain and left upper extremity radiculopathy, with complete resolution of her preoperative weakness. Postoperative Ga-68 DOTATATE PET/CT showed no evidence of residual or metastatic disease (Fig. 3), and follow-up MRI confirmed gross-total resection with no residual enhancing lesion within the left C6–7 (Fig. 4). She was subsequently referred for adjuvant radiotherapy given the tumor’s malignant potential and underlying metastatic profile. At the most recent follow-up, she remained neurologically stable without recurrent symptoms.

Systematic Review

A systematic review was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, and Google Scholar following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines. The following search terms were used, with no time frame limit: “renal cell carcinoma” AND (“intradural” OR “intradural extramedullary” OR “spinal canal” OR “foraminal” OR “neural foramen” OR “paraspinal” OR “nerve root sleeve” OR “mimicking schwannoma” OR “peripheral nerve sheath tumor”). We included original case reports and series presenting primary information such as risk factors, clinical presentation, management, or outcomes of spinal cord or nerve root metastases from a primary RCC. Literature reviews, correspondence, commentaries, letters to the editor, book chapters, animal studies, opinion pieces, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses were excluded.

Three authors (S.O., A.A., and C.O.) independently conducted the initial screening process based on titles and abstracts to determine eligibility. These three authors then reviewed the full texts to finalize the inclusion and extract relevant data. The bibliographies of included articles were also examined for additional relevant studies. Disagreements were resolved through consensus meetings. For each case, we extracted 1) patient demographics; 2) RCC histological subtype and relevant past medical/oncological history; 3) presenting signs and symptoms; 4) imaging characteristics; 5) management strategies, including surgical approach and adjunctive therapies; and 6) outcomes at last follow-up, including neurological and pain-related status with follow-up duration when reported (months) or documented as not reported. We also documented study limitations, conclusions, and bibliometric details such as study design, publication year, and country.

Descriptive statistics are used to categorize demographics, presentation, management, and outcome measures across the included cases. Continuous variables are presented as mean values and standard deviations, while categorical variables are summarized using frequencies and percentages.

Our systematic literature review identified 227 citations through database searches and 23 through other peer-reviewed sources (Fig. 5). Thirty-eight citations were removed due to being duplicates, leaving 212 citations for full eligibility screening, of which 165 were excluded. Ultimately, 47 studies met inclusion criteria, consisting of 44 case reports1,2,10–51 and 3 case series52–54 consisting of 57 patients (Supplemental Table 1).

A total of 57 patients were identified across 47 studies: 44 individual case reports and 3 case series. The mean age at presentation was 59.4 ± 12.4 years (range 36–86 years). The cohort was predominantly male (41/57, 71.9%), with 15 females (15/57, 26.3%), and sex not reported in 1 patient (1/57, 1.8%). Histopathological subtype was most commonly clear cell RCC, reported in 34 patients (34/57, 59.6%). Less common subtypes included papillary RCC (3/57, 5.3%), renal small cell carcinoma (1/57, 1.8%), and RCC metastasis arising within a hemangioblastoma (1/57, 1.8%); histological subtype was not specified in 18 patients (18/57, 31.6%).

A history of prior nephrectomy was documented in 34 patients (34/57, 59.6%), whereas 23 patients (23/57, 40.4%) presented without prior surgical management of their primary renal malignancy. At the time of spinal presentation, 27 patients (27/57, 47.4%) had no known extracranial/systemic metastases, while 30 patients (30/57, 52.6%) had evidence of metastatic disease elsewhere. The most frequent metastatic site was the lung (13/57, 22.8%), followed by bone (tibial, femoral, or rib; 3/57, 5.3%), brain (3/57, 5.3%), colon (1/57, 1.8%), genital tract (1/57, 1.8%), and multiple metastatic sites (10/57, 17.5%).

Relevant comorbidities were infrequently reported and included hypertension (4/57, 7.0%), atrial fibrillation (1/57, 1.8%), remote middle cerebral artery infarct (1/57, 1.8%), and prostate cancer (1/57, 1.8%). Von Hippel–Lindau disease was present in 3 patients (3/57, 5.3%), one of whom also had a prior cerebellar hemangioblastoma (1/57, 1.8%).

Lesions involved the intradural extramedullary (IDEM) compartment in 18 of 57 patients (31.6%), most often affecting the cauda equina/conus region. A distinct subgroup of 6 patients (6/57, 10.5%) presented with primary nerve root or DRG involvement. Radiographically, these lesions were frequently described as dumbbell shaped or foraminal/intraneural, mimicking benign PNSTs. Intramedullary spinal cord metastasis (ISCM) accounted for the remaining 33 of 57 cases (57.9%), with lesions reported throughout the cervical and thoracolumbar cord, including the conus medullaris. Most patients had solitary lesions (54/57, 94.7%), some of which demonstrated multilevel IDEM or ISCM involvement, while multifocal disease was uncommon (3/57, 5.3%).

Clinical presentation varied by anatomical compartment. Among patients with IDEM and primary nerve root/DRG involvement (24/57, 42.1%), localized spinal pain and radiculopathy were the most common presenting symptoms (22/24, 91.7%). Motor deficits (10/24, 41.7%) and sphincter dysfunction (8/24, 33.3%) were less frequent and generally associated with more advanced disease. In contrast, ISCM uniformly manifested with myelopathy or motor weakness (33/33, 100%), often progressing rapidly to severe neurological decline, including paraparesis, quadriparesis, or Brown-Séquard–type syndromes. Sphincter dysfunction was also more common in intramedullary disease (15/33, 45.5%). Radiographically, disease was overwhelmingly solitary (94.7%), with multifocal lesions being rare. Enhancement patterns varied by compartment: homogeneous enhancement predominated in IDEM and nerve root lesions, whereas heterogeneous or hemorrhagic features were more frequently observed in cauda equina and intramedullary presentations.

Overall, surgical intervention was performed in 41 of 57 patients (71.9%), most commonly via posterior decompression with intradural exploration and resection. Among surgically treated patients, gross-total resection was reported in 33 of 41 cases (80.5%), whereas subtotal resection was performed in 7 of 41 cases (17.1%); extent of resection was not reported in 1 of 41 cases (2.4%). Surgical decision-making frequently depended on anatomical involvement (IDEM/nerve root vs ISCM), neurological status, and feasibility of safe resection. Nonsurgical management occurred in 16 of 57 patients (28.1%), most commonly in ISCM cases treated with corticosteroids and/or radiotherapy.

Adjuvant or adjunctive therapies were variably reported and included radiotherapy, systemic targeted therapy (e.g., tyrosine kinase inhibitors), immunotherapy, and historical regimens such as cytokine-based treatments in older series. In general, surgically treated IDEM and nerve root lesions most often demonstrated rapid pain relief and neurological improvement when outcome was reported, whereas ISCM cases showed more heterogeneous neurological recovery and were more frequently associated with limited survival due to systemic disease burden. Follow-up duration was inconsistently reported across studies, ranging from short-interval postoperative assessments to extended surveillance in select long-term survivors.

Limitations

This review is limited by the nature of the available evidence, which is dominated by single-patient case reports and small case series that are prone to publication/selection bias and are not representative of true incidence or outcome distributions. Reporting was inconsistent across studies, with frequent missing data on histology, systemic disease burden, imaging features, extent of resection, adjuvant therapies, follow-up duration, and standardized neurological outcome measures, which limits comparability across cases and precludes meaningful pooled quantitative analysis. In addition, substantial heterogeneity in lesion compartment, neurological severity, management strategies, and treatment era, given evolving imaging and modern RCC systemic therapies, reduces generalizability to contemporary practice, and database indexing/terminology variability may have resulted in missed eligible cases.

Discussion

Observations

This case emphasizes a high-stakes diagnostic trap: a foraminal, nerve root–based lesion in a patient with a malignancy history can present with the classic clinical-radiographic profile of a PNST, yet represent metastatic RCC. RCC metastasis to nerve root–adjacent structures is uncommon, representing only 10.5% of RCC metastases to the spinal cord and nerve root in our analysis, but is repeatedly reported to mimic schwannoma-like lesions on imaging and during operative exposure, including lesions described as DRG-based, intraneural, or dumbbell configurations within the neural foramen.1,2,21,22,29 In this context, the patient’s progressive neck pain and radiculopathy with mild focal weakness, together with an avidly enhancing foraminal mass, align closely with the symptom pattern reported in other RCC nerve root/DRG metastases, in which pain and radiculopathy predominate and frank myelopathy is typically absent unless there is cord involvement.1,15,21,22

A key point in this case is that functional imaging supported suspicion for an active lesion at the radiculopathy level. Although Ga-68 DOTATATE PET/CT is typically leveraged for NET characterization, focal uptake localized to a symptomatic neural foramen served as a decisive trigger for targeted MRI characterization and operative planning. More broadly, case-level literature indicates that PET-based imaging can identify metabolically active or receptor-avid lesions along nerve roots and guide anatomical correlation when conventional imaging is equivocal or when foraminal anatomy complicates assessment.20 While DOTATATE uptake is not specific for RCC, the clinical lesson is that focal PET signal along a nerve root in a cancer patient should be treated as actionable, prompting definitive imaging correlation and tissue diagnosis rather than reassurance based on morphology alone.20

Operative findings and intraoperative pathology in this case reinforce an additional pattern seen in reported RCC nerve root/DRG lesions: lesions may appear friable and vascular and may lack the distinct encapsulation and well-formed fascicular displacement that often characterizes benign schwannoma. In our patient, the abnormal enlarged nerve root sleeve contained tan, friable tumor with no identifiable normal fascicles and absent motor response on stimulation, and frozen-section analysis immediately established metastatic RCC. This intraoperative confirmation is clinically consequential because it shifts the operative objective from sarcoma-style oncological margins toward maximal safe decompression/cytoreduction and preservation of function, followed by adjuvant management tailored to systemic disease context.22,53

The need for stabilization in this case, driven by required facetectomy to access a foraminal lesion, also reflects a practical reality of these PNST-mimicking metastases. Foraminal/dumbbell lesions may require destabilizing bony resection for adequate exposure, making preoperative planning for fixation essential. Similar operative decision-making has been described in nerve root RCC metastases where adequate access necessitated aggressive foraminal exploration and where resection strategy was tailored to the degree of intraneural involvement and proximity to critical neurovascular structures.1,15,21 The excellent neurological recovery and durable symptom relief in this case are also consistent with the dominant outcome theme reported for surgically managed IDEM and nerve root/DRG metastases from RCC. Rapid reduction in pain/radiculopathy and stabilization or improvement in focal deficits follow when decompression is achieved.2,10,14,19,53

Lessons

A new foraminal or dumbbell-shaped lesion in a patient with a history of RCC should not be presumed to be a benign schwannoma based on location or enhancement alone. Across published cases, RCC metastases involving the nerve root/DRG can closely resemble PNSTs on MRI and even at surgery, which is exactly why they are vulnerable to misclassification early in the workup.1,2,21,22,29 The practical takeaway is to keep metastatic disease in the differential whenever a symptomatic nerve root lesion arises in a patient with a current or prior malignancy, even when the imaging phenotype appears benign.

This diagnostic overlap makes tissue confirmation the pivotal step that aligns subsequent management with the true biology. Imaging and PET avidity can identify the lesion and localize it to the symptomatic level, but they do not establish whether the process is metastatic carcinoma, benign PNST, or MPNST. Intraoperative frozen-section analysis can therefore function as a real-time decision point, immediately determining whether the operative goal should prioritize maximal safe decompression/cytoreduction and functional preservation, typical for metastasis, versus oncological margin considerations that may be relevant when MPNST is suspected.11, 12, 16, 17, 19, 20, 23–25, 27, 30–32, 34–36, 40, 41, 43, 45, 47, 49, 51–53 In this case, frozen-section analysis enabled an appropriate metastasis-directed strategy rather than an unnecessarily morbid margin-driven resection that would be contemplated for high-grade PNST malignancy.7

Because these lesions frequently occupy the neural foramen and may require aggressive bony removal for adequate access, preoperative planning must incorporate stability and approach-related risk as part of the primary operative plan. Facetectomy or extensive foraminotomy may be necessary to achieve exposure, and surgeons should anticipate the potential need for fixation when destabilizing elements are removed. Reports of nerve root RCC metastases underscore that extent of intraneural involvement, vascularity, and proximity to critical structures can complicate resection, making neuromonitoring, meticulous microsurgical technique, and intraoperative adaptability especially important.9,15,21

Finally, durable control is typically achieved through multidisciplinary integration rather than surgery alone. Even after gross-total resection, metastatic RCC biology supports consideration of adjuvant local therapy and systemic management tailored to the patient’s overall disease status. Contemporary systemic options, including targeted agents and immunotherapy, have changed survival trajectories for many patients with metastatic RCC, and longer survivorship may increase the frequency with which rare metastatic patterns such as nerve root/DRG involvement are encountered.27 Taken together, the broader case literature and the present experience reinforce that early recognition, tissue diagnosis, maximal safe resection for symptom relief, and coordinated adjuvant planning can produce excellent functional outcomes even in uncommon metastatic presentations.19,22,27
