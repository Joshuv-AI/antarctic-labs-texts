Harnessing an endophytic Bacillus strain closely related to Bacillus velezensis for biocontrol of twig blight in bayberry caused by Pestalotiopsis versicolor
Authors: Ezzeldin Ibrahim, Raghda Nasser, Doaa A Kafsheer, Hequan Zhu, Nada A Alzunaidy, Khaled S Allemailem, Wafa Abdullah I Al-Megrin, Dan Wang, Haiping Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTwig blight disease caused by Pestalotiopsis versicolor threatens bayberry (Myrica rubra) cultivation in China, prompting extensive reliance on chemical fungicides with limited long-term efficacy. This study investigated the potential of endophytic bacteria isolated from bayberry leaves as sustainable biocontrol agents against this pathogenic fungus.ResultsAn endophytic bacterium designated strain Bay9 was isolated from healthy bayberry leaves and identified as a Bacillus sp. closely related to Bacillus velezensis based on 16 S rRNA gene sequence analysis. Bay9 exhibited strong antifungal activity against P. versicolor in vitro and showed disease-suppressive effects under semi-controlled greenhouse conditions. On potato dextrose agar (PDA) and in potato dextrose broth (PDB), inhibition rates reached 74.44% and 87.65%, respectively, accompanied by severe ultrastructural damage to fungal hyphae observed by electron microscopy. Cell-free supernatants (CFSs) of Bay9 exhibited concentration-dependent antifungal activity, with inhibition rates of 15.77%, 34.99%, 66.74%, and 98.82% at concentrations of 2.5%, 5%, 10%, and 20% (v/v), respectively. CFSs at EC50 and EC90 concentrations (7.30% and 17.43%, respectively) significantly reduced spore production and germination, induced leakage of intracellular nucleic acids and proteins consistent with membrane perturbation and leakage, but not direct evidence of a defined molecular mechanism and substantially reduced colony formation. In detached leaf assays, EC50 and EC90 treatments reduced lesion development by 65.31% and 94.58%, respectively. Under semi-controlled greenhouse conditions, Bay9 treatment significantly reduced disease incidence (from 97% to 49.33%) and disease severity index (from 82.19% to 38.69%) compared with pathogen-inoculated plants (p < 0.05), without causing phytotoxic effects on bayberry saplings.ConclusionsBay9 exhibited strong antifungal activity associated with multiple putative mechanisms, potentially involving siderophore production, hydrolytic enzyme activities (protease, amylase, and lipase), and lipopeptide-related metabolites, as suggested by biochemical assays and MALDI-TOF-MS-based tentative identification of surfactin-, iturin-, and fengycin-like compounds. Collectively, these findings suggest that Bay9 has potential as a biocontrol agent against P. versicolor under laboratory, detached-leaf, and semi-controlled greenhouse conditions. However, further studies are required to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and to validate its efficacy under field conditions before practical application can be recommended.Keywords: Myrica rubra, Pestalotiopsis versicolor, Bacillus sp, Lipopeptides, Siderophores

Introduction

The bayberry fruit tree (Myrica rubra) is native to East Asia and is widely grown in tropical and subtropical regions around the world [1–3]. Its vibrant red color and sweet flavor make it a beloved ingredient in many traditional dishes. In China, in addition to being eaten fresh or cooked due to their pleasant taste, berries also contain a high concentration of bioactive compounds, especially flavanols and anthocyanins, which have powerful antioxidant properties [1, 4–6]. Furthermore, some research has shown that bayberry juice contains a variety of amino acids, highlighting its nutritional value [6]. This resulted in its widespread cultivation throughout China, with the provinces of Zhejiang, Guizhou, Fujian, Sichuan, and Yunnan being the most significant [7, 8]. It has since grown into a crop of significant economic importance, with many mountain farmers depending on it as a reliable source of yearly income [8]. Unfortunately, bayberry trees have been affected by several diseases, among which twig blight has caused significant damage to orchardss. It had attacked more than 6,000 hectares in the Zhejiang region by 2011 [9]. According to Ren et al. [9], the genus Pestalotiopsis is one of the most important genera causing this disease. P. mangiferae, P. vismiae, P. versicolor, P. microspore, and P. myricae are pathogens of bayberry twig blight [9], along with other fungal pathogens such as Epicoccum sorghinum, Lasiodiplodia theobromae, Nigrospora oryzae and Neofusicoccum parvu [10]. In this province, the most common was P. versicolor, which caused severe damage and high mortality within a single year [11]. Some researchers have tried to combat and eliminate this pest by using fungicides such as pyraclostrobin, mancozeb, prochloraz, difenoconazole, and propiconazole [8, 12]. They did, however, notice the emergence of new strains that are more harmful and resistant to these pesticides [13, 14]. In addition, many researchers have warned of the toxicity of pesticides to other beneficial organisms [15–17].

Recently, the use of phytopathogen biocontrol has gained popularity as a solution to this problem [18, 19]. Researchers are actively seeking effective, safe, and environmentally friendly alternatives for controlling plant pathogens [20, 21]. Enhancing biological measures could be an alternative to chemical control strategies for disease management. One of the most promising biological control agents is endophytic bacteria, due to their plant growth-promoting properties through nitrogen fixation, increased nutrient intake, solubilization of inorganic phosphate, and secretion of phytohormones [22–24]. In addition, it can reduce pathogen infection in plants, which ultimately promotes plant growth indirectly [25, 26]. Therefore, this kind of bacteria can be used as a substitute for chemical fungicides to combat a variety of fungal diseases [27, 28]. Numerous endophyte bacteria that have the ability to suppress pathogens in addition to their unique properties that enhance plant growth, such as the genera Streptomyces [29, 30], Pseudomonas [31, 32], and Bacillus [33], have been reported. Among these, Bacillus species have attracted considerable attention as biocontrol agents due to their ability to produce a wide range of bioactive metabolites, including lipopeptides, hydrolytic enzymes, siderophores, and antibiotics, as well as their capacity to form durable endospores and efficiently colonize plant tissues [21, 22]. Consequently, Bacillus has become one of the most widely studied and frequently applied genera in biological control programs [34], Several species, including B. amyloliquefaciens [35], B. siamensis [36], B. licheniformis [37], B. subtilis [38], and B. tequilensis have demonstrated strong biocontrol potential against a variety of plant pathogens. These beneficial effects are primarily attributed to the direct inhibition of pathogens through the production of antifungal compounds and cell wall-degrading enzymes, as well as other antagonistic metabolites [25]. To date, no endophytic bacteria have been tested for the management of bayberry twig blight caused by P. versicolor. Most previous studies have focused primarily on rhizosphere-associated Bacillus species [8], whereas the present study investigates an endophytic Bacillus strain (Bay9) closely related to B. velezensis isolated directly from internal bayberry leaf tissues. This distinct ecological origin may provide advantages in host colonization and interaction with the pathogen. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the biocontrol potential of strain Bay9 against P. versicolor under laboratory, detached leaf, and greenhouse conditions. In addition, several potential antagonistic traits of the strain were investigated, including the production of hydrolytic enzymes, siderophores, and lipopeptide-associated metabolites. Collectively, these findings provide new insights into the potential role of endophytic Bacillus strains in the sustainable biological management of bayberry twig blight.

Materials and methods

Fungal pathogen sources and growth mediums

The fungal pathogen P. versicolor strain XJ27 causing bayberry twig blight was obtained from the Institute of Horticulture, Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Hangzhou, China. This strain was previously identified and characterized by Ren et al. based on morphological characteristics, ITS sequence analysis, β-tubulin gene sequencing, phylogenetic analyses, and pathogenicity tests [9]. The GenBank accession numbers associated with strain XJ27 are JN861773 (ITS) and JN861768 (β-tubulin). To activate the pathogen, it was grown for 6 d at 27 °C on PDA medium, which is made up 200 g of potato infusion, 20 g of glucose, and 20 g of agar per liter.

Isolation of endophytic bacteria

Intact leaves of M. rubra plants from Zhejiang Province, China, were carefully collected, and any attached dirt was removed by washing them under running tap water. For surface sterilization, the leaves were rinsed four times in sterile distilled water after immersing them in the following sterilization solutions: 70% ethanol (4 min), 6% NaClO (5 min), and 70% ethanol (1 min). Using a sterile knife, sterile leaves were chopped into 1-cm pieces. After that, one gram of chopped leaves was crushed in nine milliliters of 0.85% saline and serially diluted to a 10− 4 dilution [39]. Separately, 50 µL of each dilution was added to Petri dishes holding 10 mL of nutrient agar (NA), and the plates were incubated for three days at 30 °C. Bacterial colonies grown on the plates were individually transferred to new NA plates to confirm their purification. Purified isolates were grown on nutrient broth (NB) medium for two days at 30 °C, after which they were mixed with 30% (v/v) glycerol separately and stored at − 80 °C until evaluation of their antifungal activity against P. versicolor.

Assessment of the efficacy of bayberry-isolated endophyte bacteria against p. versicolor on PDA and PDB medium

On PDA

A 7-day-old P. versicolor disc (5 mm in diameter) was picked up and placed near the edge of the dishes holding the PDA media. On the edge of the other plates were placed 5 µL of endophyte bacteria strains (equivalent to 1 × 108 CFU/mL), which were obtained from M. rubra and grown on NB medium for 48 h separately. PDA inoculated with P. versicolor discs without strain cultures was used as a control. After seven days of incubation at 27 °C, mycelium development was measured on each PDA plate, and the percentage of inhibition was calculated according to Puei et al. [40].

On PDB

Conical flasks containing 100 mL of PDB were inoculated with 1 mL of bacterial suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) and incubated for 48 h cultures. Five 5-mm-diameter P. versicolor mycelial plugs were added to each flask. The flasks were incubated at 28 °C for 7 d. The experiment included a control consisting of PDB inoculated with P. versicolor only, under identical conditions. Fungal biomass was harvested, dried to constant weight, and measured under standardized conditions. All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates, each consisting of three technical replicates. Biomass values were normalized to the untreated control before statistical analysis [41].

Identification of potential endophytic bacteria

Molecular identification of endophytic bacteria that showed antagonistic potential against P. versicolor was performed. Isolates were identified using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. 1492R (5-GGTTACCCTTGTTACGACT-3′) and 27 F (5-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) were the primers used to amplify the 16 S rRNA gene [25]. Using 50 µL of 2× TSINGKE Master Mix (TsingKe Biological Technology, Beijing, China), a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed according to the instructions. PCR products were analyzed on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel to see amplified bands. TsingKe Biological Technology was then commissioned to sequence the PCR results. BLAST analysis was performed on the resulting sequences [42], and based on sequence similarity, the closest lineages were identified, and phylogenetic trees were constructed using MEGA 5.0 software [43].

Morphological changes in P. versicolor mycelium due to endophytic bacteria treatment

According to Gao et al. [44], the morphological alterations of P. versicolor mycelium extracted from the inhibitory zone produced by endophytic strain Bay9 were examined using optical microscopy and scanning/transmission electron microscopy (SEM/TEM). 5-mm mycelial pieces were collected from the P. versicolor plate that had been treated with and without Bay9. The mycelial pieces were immediately seen under the optical microscope following two PBS cleanings. Conversely, the mycelial was fixed in 2.5% v/v glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PBS for an entire night at room temperature. For an hour at room temperature, the samples were postfixed in osmium tetroxide (1% (w/v) in 0.1 M PBS). The specimens were dried using a graded sequence of ethanol concentrations (30–100%) until the fungal samples were totally dry. Next, the alterations in the hyphal morphology of P. versicolor were examined using TEM (JEM-1230, JEOL, Akishima, Japan) and SEM (TM-1000, Hitachi, Japan).

Antifungal efficacy of CFSs against P. versicolor

The antifungal activity of Bay9 CFSs was evaluated based on the inhibition of mycelial growth of P. versicolor on PDA plates, following Ohike et al. [45] with minor modifications. Briefly, the bacterial strain was cultured in 100 mL of nutrient broth (NB) medium at 30 °C for 48 h under shaking conditions. The culture was then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore-size membrane filter to obtain sterile CFSs. The absence of viable bacterial cells in the filtrate was confirmed by plating 100 µL of the CFSs onto nutrient agar plates and incubating at 30 °C for 48 h; no bacterial growth was observed. For antifungal assays, PDA medium was supplemented with CFSs at final concentrations of 2.5%, 5%, 10%, and 20% (v/v). As a control, sterile NB medium processed under identical conditions (centrifugation and filtration) was incorporated into PDA. A 5-mm-diameter mycelial plug of P. versicolor was placed at the center of each plate. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 7 d, after which fungal colony diameter was measured and the percentage of growth inhibition was calculated. The EC50 and EC90 values, defined as the concentrations causing 50% and 90% inhibition of mycelial growth, respectively, were determined using dose–response analysis.

Impact of EC50 and EC90 on the viability of P. versicolor spores

Spore production

P. versicolor was cultivated on PDA plates supplemented with CFSs at EC50 and EC90 concentrations and incubated for 21 d at 27 °C to evaluate their effect on spore production. PDA plates inoculated with P. versicolor without CFSs served as the control. After incubation, fungal mycelia were gently scraped from the surface of each Petri dish into 10 mL of sterile distilled water. The suspension was filtered through sterile cheesecloth to remove hyphal fragments. Spore concentration was determined using a hemocytometer (Neubauer chamber), and counts were performed in triplicate for each treatment. The percentage inhibition of spore production was calculated relative to the control.

Spore germination

Spore germination was evaluated following Ibrahim et al. [46] with minor modifications. A conidial suspension (1 × 106 spores/mL), prepared as described by Wu et al. [47], was mixed with EC50 and EC90 solutions to obtain a final volume of 600 µL. Control treatments consisted of conidial suspension mixed with sterile distilled water. The mixtures were incubated at 28 °C for 6 h. After incubation, 25 µL of each treatment was placed on a glass slide and examined under an optical microscope. A spore was considered germinated when the germ tube length was equal to or greater than half of the spore length. At least 200 spores per replicate were evaluated, and three independent biological replicates were performed for each treatment.

DNA and protein leakage

The effect of CFSs at EC50 and EC90 concentrations on DNA and protein leakage in P. versicolor spores was assessed using the method described by Khalil et al. [48]. The released DNA and protein molecules were quantified at 260 and 280 nm, respectively, using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. Briefly, spore suspensions (1 × 106 spores/mL) were grown for 24 h at 27 °C and 180 rpm on a PDB medium supplemented with CFSs EC50 and EC90. The control was a PDB medium without CFSs. P. versicolor culture filtrate (CF) was obtained to quantify the amount of protein and DNA leakage.

Colony formation and spore survival assay

The method described by Kheiri et al. [49], with slight modifications, was employed to examine the effect of EC50 and EC90 on P. versicolor colony formation. A 300 µL of conidia suspension with EC50 and EC90 were combined separately to obtain a final volume of 1 mL. As controls, conidia suspension tubes mixed with water were employed. At 28 °C, each tube was incubated for 6 h. Then, 25-µL of each sample was spread out on PDA plates and stored at 28 °C for three days. The number of colonies that developed on the plates was counted to determine the extent to which the number of colonies in the treatment was inhibited. Additionally, 10 µL of 1.5 mM propidium iodide (PI) was used to stain conidia from the EC50 and EC90 treatments in the dark for 15 min to quantify the number of PI-positive conidia, which denotes spore death. Green fluorescence microscopy was used to count the number of PI-positive conidia in the samples after they had been cleaned in PBS twice.

Antagonists assay of EC50 and EC90 CFSs against P. versicolor on a detached leaf

Antagonistic assays of EC50 and EC90 CFSs against P. versicolor were conducted on detached bayberry leaves following the method of Li et al. [10], with minor modifications. Briefly, healthy bayberry leaves (approximately 6 cm in length) were surface-sterilized with 5% NaClO for 1 min, rinsed several times with ddH₂O, and placed in square plastic boxes (10 × 10 cm) containing 0.6% agar medium to maintain a humid environment. Each leaf was wounded at the center using a sterile 7-mm-diameter cork borer. The experiment included four treatments: (1) non-inoculated control consisting of PDA plugs without P. versicolor or CFSs treatment; (2) pathogen control consisting of PDA plugs inoculated with P. versicolor; (3) P. versicolor inoculum treated with EC50; and (4) P. versicolor inoculum treated with EC90. For each biological replicate, five detached leaves were used per treatment, and the entire experiment was performed using three independent biological replicates (total = 15 leaves per treatment). The inoculated leaves were incubated in a growth chamber at 28 °C with 75% relative humidity under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod for 7 d. Lesion diameters were measured after incubation, and the inhibition rates of EC50 and EC90 treatments were calculated relative to the pathogen control.

Antimicrobial properties of endophytic bacterium strain Bay9

Assay of hydrolytic enzymes

The protease output of Bay9 was tested, according to Vianayarani et al. [41]. Fresh Bay9 cultures were added to Petri dishes containing 15 g of skim milk, 0.5 g of yeast extract, and 9.5 g of agar per liter. After three days of incubation at 30 °C, a distinct zone appears around the colonies, indicating protease formation. Amylase output was tested in Bay9, according to Ntabo et al. [50]. New cultures of Bay9 were spotted in Petri dishes filled with starch agar medium and incubated at 30 °C for 2d. Next, the infected dishes were immersed in a 1% iodine solution for 10 min. Amylase production is seen in the visible zone formed around the colonies. According to Bjelić et al. [51], the ability of Bay9 to produce lipase was examined. For one week at 30 °C, Petri dishes containing basal agar medium provided with Tween 80 were left infected with Bay9. Dark, opaque areas surrounding the colonies indicate positive lipase production.

Assay for producing siderophores

The method described in [52], with some adjustments, was used to evaluate the Bay9 strain’s capacity to generate siderophores. First, the CFSs of Bay9 were collected after they had been cultured for two days at 30 °C in an MSA medium. Then, an equivalent volume of CAS (Chrome Azurol S) solution was combined with these CFSs. The generation of siderophores was indicated by a color shift from blue to purple, yellow, or orange when the combination was incubated at room temperature for three hours in the dark. Using a SPECTRAmax PLAUS384 microplate spectrophotometer, the absorbance at 630 nm was measured to determine the total siderophore units. In addition, a colony of Bay9 was grown on the surface of CAS agar medium and incubated for five days at room temperature. A color shift around the colony from blue to purple, yellow, or orange indicated the presence of siderophore production. Three duplicates of each treatment were carried out to guarantee accurate outcomes.

Detection of lipopeptides

Using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS), the Bay9 strain was examined for lipopeptide detection according to Masum et al. [52]. After cultivating the Bay9 strain for 72 h at 30 °C on NA agar plates, colonies were meticulously suspended in an Eppendorf tube filled with a matrix solution (10 mg of cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid per milliliter in 70% water; 30% acetonitrile; and 0.1% TFA). Next, 1 µL of the sample solution that had been homogenized was placed onto a MALDI-TOF MTP 384 target plate, which was provided by Bruker Daltonik GmbH located in Leipzig, Germany. The ultra-extreme equipment, MALDI-TOF (Bruker, Bremen, Germany), fitted with a smart beam laser, was used to record MALDI-TOF mass spectra following natural drying.

Greenhouse evaluation of the biocontrol efficacy of strain Bay9 against P. versicolor

To evaluate the biocontrol efficacy of Bay9 against P. versicolor under greenhouse conditions, healthy M. rubra saplings (2 years old, 35–55 cm in height) were transplanted into 2 L plastic pots (approximately 22 cm in diameter) containing a sterile substrate composed of peat moss, vermiculite, and autoclaved loamy soil (3:1:1, v/v/v). Plants were maintained in a controlled greenhouse at 25–28 °C/18–20 °C (day/night), 70 ± 5% relative humidity, and a 16 h photoperiod for one week prior to inoculation. P. versicolor was cultured on PDA at 27 °C for 21 days, and spores were harvested in sterile distilled water (SDW) containing 0.01% Tween 80 and adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ spores mL⁻¹ using a Neubauer hemocytometer. Bay9 was cultured in nutrient broth at 30 °C for 48 h, and bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with SDW, and adjusted to 1 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻¹. The experiment followed a completely randomized design with four treatments: (G1) SDW control, (G2) P. versicolor only, (G3) Bay9 + P. versicolor, and (G4) Bay9 only. Each treatment consisted of three biological replicates with ten plants per replicate. Plants were first sprayed with Bay9 suspension or SDW until runoff. After 24 h, plants were inoculated with P. versicolor spore suspension or SDW according to the treatment design. Following inoculation, plants were covered with transparent polyethylene bags for 48 h to maintain high humidity and promote infection. Disease progression was monitored daily, and final disease assessment was performed 10 d after inoculation. Disease severity was evaluated using a 0–5 disease rating scale based on the percentage of symptomatic leaf area and the extent of wilting symptoms, where 0 = no visible symptoms; 1 = small brown spots covering less than 10% of the leaf area; 2 = distinct lesions covering 10–25% of the leaf area; 3 = extensive lesions covering 26–50% of the leaf area; 4 = severe lesions covering 51–75% of the leaf area accompanied by partial wilting; and 5 = complete leaf necrosis or whole-plant wilting. Disease ratings were assigned on a per-plant basis according to the overall severity of symptoms observed on each plant. Lesion length was measured using a digital caliper, and the number of visible lesions per plant was recorded. Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using three independent biological replicates, with the definition of the biological unit varying depending on the experimental system. For in vitro assays, each Petri dish was considered one biological replicate. For detached leaf assays, each plant was considered one biological replicate, and five detached leaves from that plant were collected per treatment and treated as subsamples. For greenhouse experiments, each treatment consisted of three biological replicates, with ten plants per replicate. Each individual plant was considered the experimental unit, and plants within the same replicate were treated as subsamples. In all cases, three technical replicates were performed within each biological replicate. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test at p < 0.05 using SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The EC50 and EC90 values were estimated using nonlinear regression analysis based on a four-parameter logistic (4PL) dose–response model in GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The fitted equation was . Based on seven tested concentrations of Bay9 CFSs (5%, 7.5%, 10%, 12.5%, 15%, 17.5%, and 20%, v/v), the estimated EC50 and EC90 values were 7.30% (95% confidence interval: 6.52–8.15%) and 17.43% (95% confidence interval: 15.66–19.38%), respectively. The goodness of fit of the model was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R² = 0.98), indicating an excellent fit between the experimental data and the model.

Results

Endophytic bacterium’s antagonistic effect and molecular identification

Out of five endophytic bacterial isolates, only one, called Bay9, showed an effective effect in suppressing the pathogen P. versicolor, which causes twig blight in bayberry plants. When Bay9 and P. versicolor were tested against each other on PDA and PDB media, the results showed that Bay9 significantly inhibited the growth of P. versicolor (Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 1A, B, the P. versicolor inhibition rate on PDA was about 74.44%, and 87.65% on PDB. Optical microscopy, SEM, and TEM analyses were used to observe the morphological changes of P. versicolor mycelium in response to treatment with strain Bay9 (Fig. 1C). In the treated samples, optical microscopy and SEM images revealed noticeable alterations in hyphal morphology, including deformation, shrinkage, and partial fragmentation compared with the untreated control. TEM observations indicated apparent structural alterations in fungal cells, including disruption of cell wall integrity and reduction of intracellular contents in some hyphal segments. In contrast, untreated P. versicolor hyphae exhibited regular morphology under optical microscopy and SEM, while TEM images showed intact cell wall structure and normal cellular organization. These microscopic observations are presented as qualitative visual evidence supporting the antifungal activity of strain Bay9, in agreement with the quantitative inhibition data obtained from growth and viability assays. This led to Bay9’s molecular identification by 16 S rRNA gene sequence analysis. After the sequences were matched using BLAST, it was discovered that the Bay9 16 S rRNA sequence and the Bacillus species sequence are extremely similar. Based on 16 S rRNA gene sequence analysis and phylogenetic inference, strain Bay9 was identified as a Bacillus sp. closely related to Bacillus velezensis (Fig. 2).

Calculation of EC50 and EC90 values and evaluation of CFS-mediated Inhibition of P. versicolor fungal development

The antifungal activity of CFSs from strain Bay9 against P. versicolor fungal development was investigated. As depicted in Fig. 3A, B, the inhibitory effect of Bay9 CFSs on P. versicolor growth in the PDA medium was significant. The inhibitory percentages were observed to increase with increasing concentrations of Bay9 CFSs. Specifically, at concentrations of 2.5%, 5%, 10%, and 20% (v/v) of Bay9 CFSs, the fungal growth was inhibited by 15.77%, 34.99%, 66.74%, and 98.82%, respectively. To obtain more precise values for the effective concentration that inhibits fungal growth by 50% (EC50) and 90% (EC90), P. versicolor was exposed to seven different concentrations ranging from 5% to 20% (v/v) of Bay9 CFSs. Based on the data presented in Fig. 3C, D, the calculated EC50 and EC90 values were approximately 7.30% and 17.43%, respectively. These concentrations, derived from mycelial growth inhibition, were subsequently used as standardized reference concentrations in the spore production, spore germination, colony formation, detached leaf, and greenhouse assays. They were selected to facilitate comparison across different biological endpoints and do not imply equivalent quantitative effects for all measured responses.

Assessment of EC50 and EC90 efficacy on P. versicolor spore viability

The efficacy of EC50 and EC90 concentrations from CFSs on P. versicolor spore viability was evaluated in terms of their ability to suppress spore production, spore germination, spore DNA and protein damage, colony formation, and spore survival. Treatment with EC50 and EC90 concentrations resulted in a reduction in spore production, spore germination, spore survival, and colony formation, while also inducing damage to spore DNA and proteins. The effect of EC50 and EC90 treatments on the production of sporulating conidiomata structures on PDA plates was observed. Figure 4A, B illustrate a substantial abundance of sporulating conidiomata in the control group, while their production was significantly reduced in dishes treated with EC50 and almost non-existent in dishes treated with EC90. Further examination through optical microscopy revealed the impact of EC50 and EC90 on spore production in P. versicolor, as depicted in Fig. 4C. Treatment with EC50 and EC90 resulted in inhibition rates of 65.45% and 95.36%, respectively, indicating a significant reduction in spore production compared to the control group. Additionally, the application of EC50 and EC90 doses resulted in a considerable inhibition of spore germination (Fig. 4D). The germination rate in the control group was 98.48%, whereas the germination rates at EC50 and EC90 concentrations were 31.66% and 2.33%, respectively (Fig. 4E). This suggests that EC50 and EC90 treatments significantly reduce spore germination. Furthermore, EC50 and EC90 treatments caused a significant increase in OD260 and OD280 values, indicating enhanced leakage of intracellular nucleic acids and proteins. In the treatment with EC50 and EC90, the OD260 values were measured as 1.95 and 2.97, respectively. Similarly, the OD280 values for the EC50 and EC90 treatments were 2.42 and 3.53, respectively (Fig. 4F). In contrast, the control treatment exhibited OD260 and OD280 values of 0.52 and 0.91, respectively. To correct for any background absorbance, the OD260 and OD280 values of the CFSs alone (without spores) were measured and subtracted from the values obtained from spore‑containing samples. The OD values shown in Fig. 4F already reflect this subtraction, confirming that the increased readings result from spore damage rather than CFSs interference. These findings suggest increased membrane permeability and cellular leakage in treated samples.

Additionally, after incubating 25 µL of P. versicolor spores for four days, the colonies that developed on a PDA medium enriched with EC50 and EC90 concentrations were counted. According to the findings, EC50 and EC90 were equally successful in dramatically lowering the number of P. versicolor colonies that formed on PDA plates (Fig. 5A, B). In particular, EC50-treated PDA plate colonies demonstrated a 66.25% inhibition rate in comparison to the control. In contrast, the results revealed that the spore slices in the control group showed no uptake of PI dye, indicating their viability. The inhibitory effect of Bay9 against P. versicolor on bayberry leaves

Following the demonstrated efficacy of the endophytic bacterium strain Bay9 and its CFSs in inhibiting the disease in vitro, it’s potential to suppress P. versicolor on bayberry leaves was investigated (Fig. 6). Examination of separated leaves revealed that the application of Bay9 CFSs at EC50 and EC90 concentrations effectively halted the spread of the disease and prevented its penetration into the bayberry plants. The size of the lesions on leaves inoculated with discs from fungus dishes treated with EC50 and EC90 reached 6.00 mm and 0.75 mm, respectively. In contrast, the lesion size on leaves inoculated with discs from untreated fungus plates reached 17.25 mm, while no symptoms were observed on control leaves treated with PDA discs without fungus inoculation and untreated with EC50 and EC90. This reduction in lesion size, achieved through the application of Ec50 and EC90, resulted in P. versicolor inhibition rates of 65.31% and 94.58%, respectively.

Antifungal properties of Bay9

The antifungal properties of Bay9 were assessed by evaluating its production of siderophores, hydrolytic enzymes, and lipopeptides. The Bay9 strain showed the ability to produce siderophores, as observed by a color change in the CAS solid and liquid medium from blue to pink (Fig. 7A, B). In the solid CAS medium, a zone with a diameter of 19.32 mm was observed around the Bay9 colony, while in the liquid CSA medium, Bay9 demonstrated a siderophore production rate of 87.32%. These findings indicate the ability of Bay9 to produce siderophores. The testing of hydrolytic enzymes also showed that the Bay9 strain can make lipase, amylase, and protease enzymes (Fig. 7C–E). When Bay9 was inoculated on basal medium supplemented with Tween 80 (1% v/v), starch agar medium, and skim milk agar medium, noticeable clear zones were observed surrounding the Bay9 colonies. The zone sizes measured were 18.43 mm, 23.37 mm, and 31.23 mm, respectively, indicating the production of lipase, amylase, and protease enzymes by Bay9. These results demonstrate that Bay9 possesses enzymatic activities, which may be involved in its possible antifungal qualities. Figure 7F displays the series of mass spectra that were produced from the MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry investigation of the Bay9 strain. In the Bay9 strain, the major mass peaks were detected within the m/z range of 616–1540. Mass signals within the m/z range of 678–972 were consistent with members of the fusaricidin family [53]. In addition, peaks observed in the m/z 1023–1193 region were tentatively assigned to mycosubtilin, iturin, and surfactin [54–56]. Signals detected between m/z 1293 and 1358 were consistent with the polymyxin family [53, 57], whereas peaks in the m/z 1471–1540 range corresponded to the fengycin family [53]. These results suggest the potential presence of multiple lipopeptide families that may contribute to the observed antifungal activity.

Greenhouse evaluation of the biocontrol efficacy of strain Bay9 against P. versicolor

The biocontrol efficacy of strain Bay9 against P. versicolor was further evaluated under greenhouse conditions using intact M. rubra saplings. Typical disease symptoms, including leaf necrosis, wilting, and lesion development, were clearly observed in plants inoculated with P. versicolor, whereas Bay9-treated plants inoculated with the pathogen exhibited markedly reduced symptoms (Fig. 8A). No visible disease symptoms were observed in the SDW control or Bay9-only treatment, indicating that Bay9 did not cause phytotoxic effects on bayberry saplings. Disease incidence in pathogen-inoculated plants reached 97%, whereas Bay9 treatment significantly reduced disease incidence to 49.33% (Fig. 8B). Similarly, the disease severity index was significantly reduced in Bay9-treated plants compared with pathogen-inoculated plants, decreasing from 82.19% to 38.69% (Fig. 8C). In addition, the number of lesions per plant decreased significantly from 65.33 to 22.67 following Bay9 treatment (Fig. 8D). Lesion length was also significantly reduced following Bay9 treatment, with average lesion lengths decreasing from 16.33 mm in pathogen-inoculated plants to 5.35 mm in the Bay9-treated group (Fig. 8E).

Discussion

Twig blight caused by P. versicolor is a destructive disease affecting bayberry cultivation and causing substantial economic losses in China [9, 10, 58, 59]. The extensive use of chemical fungicides has contributed to the emergence of resistant pathogen strains, further complicating disease management and raising environmental concerns [60–62]. Consequently, there is an increasing demand for sustainable and environmentally friendly alternatives. Among the potential biological control agents, endophytic bacteria, particularly Bacillus species, have attracted considerable attention because of their ability to colonize plant tissues, suppress phytopathogens through multiple antagonistic mechanisms, and promote plant health [23, 25, 26, 32–34].

The present study shows that strain Bay9 exhibits strong antifungal activity against P. versicolor across multiple experimental systems, ranging from in vitro assays to whole-plant greenhouse experiments. In vitro assays demonstrated that Bay9 significantly inhibited mycelial growth of P. versicolor by 74.44% on PDA medium and 87.65% in PDB culture (Fig. 1A, B). In addition, optical microscopy, SEM, and TEM observations revealed severe ultrastructural abnormalities in fungal hyphae following treatment, including shrinkage, deformation, cell wall disruption, and cytoplasmic leakage (Fig. 1C). Although these observations are primarily descriptive, they provide visual evidence that complements the quantitative inhibition assays. Furthermore, the concentration-dependent antifungal activity of Bay9 CFSs, reflected by EC50 and EC90 values of 7.30% and 17.43%, respectively (Fig. 3), provides quantitative support for antifungal potency. Detached leaf assays further confirmed the protective effect of Bay9 CFSs, which reduced lesion development by 65.31% and 94.58% at EC50 and EC90, respectively (Fig. 6).

The greenhouse experiment provided preliminary evidence that the antifungal activity observed under laboratory conditions may translate into whole-plant protection under semi-controlled conditions; however, further validation under field conditions and different application regimes is required. Bay9 treatment significantly reduced disease incidence from 97% in pathogen-inoculated plants to 49.33%, while the disease severity index decreased from 82.19% to 38.69% (p < 0.05). Similarly, lesion length and lesion number per plant were markedly reduced following Bay9 treatment compared with pathogen-inoculated controls (Fig. 8). In addition, no visible phytotoxic symptoms were observed in Bay9-treated plants under the conditions tested. These findings are particularly important because previous studies investigating biological control of P. versicolor have largely remained limited to laboratory-based evaluations without greenhouse validation. Ali et al. (2020) previously reported the antifungal activity of rhizosphere-associated Bacillus species against P. versicolor using only in vitro assays, with inhibition rates ranging from 58% to 71% [8]. In comparison, the endophytic strain Bay9 exhibited comparatively higher inhibition under the conditions tested and additionally provided preliminary greenhouse evidence supporting the potential of Bacillus-mediated biological control against this pathogen. The enhanced performance of Bay9 may be associated with its endophytic origin, which could facilitate closer interaction with host tissues and improved pathogen suppression compared with rhizosphere-derived isolates [33, 34, 59, 63–65]. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential advantages of endophytic Bacillus strains for sustainable management of bayberry twig blight [25, 66–71].

The antifungal activity of strain Bay9 may involve multiple putative and complementary factors. The strain produced high levels of siderophores (87.32% in the liquid CAS assay) as well as several extracellular hydrolytic enzymes, including protease (31.23 mm halo), amylase (23.37 mm), and lipase (18.43 mm) (Fig. 7). Siderophore production may reduce iron availability to the pathogen, whereas hydrolytic enzymes may contribute to degradation of fungal structural components [26, 72, 73]. In addition, MALDI-TOF-MS analysis showed mass peaks tentatively consistent with lipopeptide-related compounds, potentially corresponding to surfactin-, iturin-, and fengycin-like molecules; however, confirmation using LC–MS/MS is still required [74, 75]. These compounds are widely recognized for their ability to disrupt membrane integrity and inhibit fungal growth [21, 47]. The increased OD260 and OD280 values observed in CFS-treated spores suggest leakage of intracellular nucleic acids and proteins. These findings are consistent with membrane perturbation and leakage, although they do not provide direct evidence of a specific molecular mechanism. Moreover, the reductions observed in spore germination, colony formation, and PI-based spore survival collectively support the observation that Bay9 metabolites are associated with reduced fungal viability.

Despite the promising findings obtained in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although greenhouse experiments provide conditions that are more representative than in vitro systems, they still cannot fully reproduce the complexity of field environments, including fluctuating temperatures, UV radiation, rainfall, and microbial competition. Therefore, field trials are necessary to evaluate the long-term stability and efficacy of strain Bay9 under practical agricultural conditions. In addition, the colony formation assay has inherent limitations, including the use of a relatively small sample volume (25 µL) and potential counting bias. However, the consistency of the results across multiple independent assays, including spore germination and PI-based survival analyses, supports the reliability of the conclusions. Furthermore, although MALDI-TOF-MS analysis suggested the presence of several lipopeptide families, quantitative metabolite profiling and purification of individual compounds using approaches such as HPLC-MS/MS are still required to fully elucidate the structure–activity relationships underlying the observed antifungal effects. In conclusion, strain Bay9 exhibited strong and consistent antifungal activity against P. versicolor under laboratory, detached leaf, and greenhouse conditions without causing phytotoxic effects on bayberry plants. These findings suggest that Bay9 has potential as a biocontrol agent under semi-controlled conditions, warranting further investigation in field settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study showed that the endophytic bacterium strain Bay9, isolated from bayberry leaves, exhibits strong antifungal activity against P. versicolor under laboratory, detached-leaf, and semi-controlled greenhouse conditions. The strain produces multiple putative antifungal-related traits, including siderophore production, hydrolytic enzyme activities, and lipopeptide-related compounds. Collectively, these findings suggest that strain Bay9 has potential as a biocontrol agent against P. versicolor. However, further studies are required to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and to validate its efficacy under field conditions before practical application can be recommended.
