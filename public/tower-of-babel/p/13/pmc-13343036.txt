NAD+ metabolic collapse and arrest of epithelial development in Hirschsprung disease
Authors: Yitong Zhao, Jielin Yang, Bo Li, Dorothy Lee, Yi Xiong, Agostino Pierro, Qian Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHirschsprung disease (HSCR) is characterized by distal aganglionosis. Persistent postoperative dysmotility and enterocolitis suggest that intrinsic epithelial vulnerabilities may remain within the ganglionic bowel. We here aimed to define both the state of the epithelium and its metabolism in HSCR.MethodsDistal colonic tissues from patients with HSCR and non-HSCR controls (HSCR allied disorders) were examined by single-cell RNA sequencing. Particular attention was given to nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation across epithelial subsets. Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry for γH2A.X, PARP1, TOM20, KRT20, and poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) validated transcriptional signatures of cellular stress, metabolic remodeling, and impaired epithelial maturation.ResultsCompared with control, HSCR epithelium showed marked transcriptional remodeling, with accumulation of stem and transit-amplifying cells, and depletion of differentiated epithelial lineages, indicating impaired epithelial maturation. These changes were most pronounced in the distal ganglionic colon with a reduced epithelial KRT20 signal, where oxidative phosphorylation, ATP synthesis, and lipid and steroid metabolic pathways were broadly suppressed. Key NAD+ biosynthetic enzymes were downregulated, while NAD+-consuming sirtuins and PAR polymerases (PARP) were upregulated, particularly in HSCR ganglionic colon crypt populations. Similarly, HSCR tissues exhibited increased crypt γH2A.X and nuclear PARP1, as well as elevated TOM20 and poly/mono-ADP-ribose staining, consistent with metabolic and genomic stress.ConclusionHSCR epithelium displays metabolic fragility characterized by NAD+ biosynthetic insufficiency, increased NAD+ consumption, mitochondrial remodeling, DNA damage, and impaired epithelial maturation. These epithelial alterations complement enteric nervous system (ENS)-centered disease models and suggest that NAD+-related pathways may represent modifiable programs relevant to postoperative mucosal resilience and susceptibility to Hirschsprung disease-associated enterocolitis (HAEC).

Introduction

Hirschsprung disease (HSCR) is a congenital disorder caused by failed migration and differentiation of enteric neural crest cells, resulting in distal colonic aganglionosis and functional obstruction.1 Although surgical resection of the aganglionic segment restores bowel continuity, many children continue to experience postoperative morbidity, including persistent bowel dysfunction and HSCR-associated enterocolitis (HAEC), a potentially life-threatening complication.2 3 These postoperative sequelae suggest that factors beyond aganglionosis—such as ongoing dysmotility and luminal stasis with altered microbial exposure, together with impaired epithelial barrier function and regenerative capacity—contribute to compromised mucosal integrity and increased susceptibility to HAEC.2 We therefore hypothesize that epithelial vulnerability in HSCR reflects not only enteric nervous system (ENS)-driven enteric dysfunction but also epithelial-intrinsic defects shaped by an altered intestinal microenvironment.

The intestinal epithelium functions as a dynamic barrier that integrates proliferative renewal, lineage differentiation, and mucosal defense.46 Accumulating evidence links impaired epithelial regeneration, barrier integrity, and stress responses to postoperative mucosal dysfunction and the development of HAEC in HSCR.23 711 Consistent with these observations, our recent study demonstrated that aberrant stromal remodeling and disrupted epithelial-stromal crosstalk in HSCR lead to impaired epithelial regeneration and increased susceptibility to inflammation.12 However, the epithelial-intrinsic programs that underlie this postoperative vulnerability remain poorly defined.

The intestinal epithelium relies on tightly regulated metabolic networks to sustain continuous stem cell-driven renewal, lineage differentiation, and barrier defense.46 Oxidative phosphorylation, biosynthetic pathways, and lipid-related and steroid-related metabolism collectively support energy production and cell fate transitions. In this study, we used single-cell RNA sequencing of distal colonic tissues from patients with HSCR and non-HSCR controls to define epithelial differentiation states and metabolic programs, with a specific focus on nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) biosynthesis and consumption pathways. By integrating transcriptional profiling with immunofluorescence markers of DNA damage, poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) activation, and mitochondrial remodeling, we sought to identify epithelial-intrinsic defects that complement and extend our previous findings on stromal remodeling. This may provide an expanded mechanistic framework for postoperative epithelial vulnerability and susceptibility to HAEC in HSCR.

Methods

Tissue collection

HSCR was diagnosed by intraoperative frozen-section analysis and confirmed by postoperative histopathological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, supplemented with immunohistochemical staining to assess ganglion cell status. Specimens were divided into three regions: the aganglionic segment, the distal ganglionic colon, and the proximal ganglionic colon, as described in detail in our previous study.12 In this study, the distal ganglionic colon was defined as the ganglionated bowel immediately proximal to the aganglionic rectum, corresponding mainly to the distal sigmoid colon and including the transition zone, rather than representing the more proximal normally innervated colon. This region could show variable degrees of gross dilation depending on the extent of bowel obstruction and individual specimen anatomy. None of the patients included in this study had clinically documented episodes of HAEC before pull-through surgical management.

Non-HSCR control tissues, including rectum and/or distal colon, were obtained from pediatric patients undergoing bowel resection for indications unrelated to HSCR. These controls exhibited dysfunctional gut motility but retained ganglion cells on histopathological evaluation and required surgical intervention following failure of medical management. For analyses, tissues from defined HSCR segments and anatomically equivalent control regions were collected and processed as previously described.12

Epithelial subset extraction and pseudo-time analysis

Single-cell data acquisition and preprocessing were performed using our established pipeline.12 Briefly, low-quality cells were excluded according to detected gene number, unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, and mitochondrial transcript percentage. Potential doublets were removed before normalization, integration, and batch correction across samples. Epithelial populations were isolated based on canonical marker expression and annotated into lineage-defined subsets. Pseudo-time trajectories were inferred using Monocle3 as previously described, with modifications only to incorporate the extended NAD+ gene set analyzed in this study.

Differential gene expression and pathway enrichment

Differential gene expression between HSCR and non-HSCR controls was assessed using Seurat’s Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Pathway and functional enrichment analyses were performed using ClusterProfiler with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) databases. NAD+ biosynthesis and consumption pathways were curated from KEGG, Reactome, and published reference sets, followed by manual review to retain genes directly related to NAD+ biosynthesis, salvage, degradation, or NAD+-consuming enzymatic activity.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded colonic tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, antigen retrieval was performed, and sections were then blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 1 hour. Primary antibodies against γH2A.X (1:1000), PARP1 (1:50), TOM20 (1:300), and KRT20 (1:50) were applied (table 1) and incubated overnight at 4°C. After PBS washing, sections were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200). Nuclei were counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI).1214 For each marker, positively stained cells were quantified by three blinded investigators from at least ten well-oriented crypts and five randomly selected fields per subject.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded human colon sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval using standard protocols.12 Sections were blocked with 30% bovine serum albumin for 2 hours at room temperature and then incubated with a mouse anti-poly/mono-ADP-ribose primary antibody (1:100; detailed antibody information is provided in table 1). After washing, sections were incubated with an horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated horse anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:200). Signal detection was performed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine for 5 min, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 2 min. Slides were subsequently dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for bright-field imaging.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. All analyses were performed in R (V.4.5.0). Comparisons of pathway activity scores between HSCR and non-HSCR controls used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Disrupted epithelial metabolic programs in HSCR

Integrated single-cell uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embedding of distal colonic specimens from HSCR and non-HSCR controls delineated major epithelial, mesenchymal, immune, and neural cell populations (online supplemental figure 1A). To identify functional alterations beyond cellular composition, we assessed metabolic pathway activities across all major lineages. Compared with controls, HSCR samples exhibited broad suppression of energy-producing and biosynthetic pathways, particularly within the epithelium of the distal ganglionic colon and immune compartments (online supplemental file 1B). Oxidative phosphorylation, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and fatty acid metabolic programs were markedly reduced in the distal ganglionic colon epithelium. By comparison, rectal epithelial populations showed a more limited and less consistent pattern of pathway alteration, supporting the observation that metabolic dysfunction in HSCR was more evident in the distal ganglionic colon than in the rectum.

Pseudo-time analysis reveals impaired epithelial maturation in HSCR

To examine whether these metabolic abnormalities coincide with impaired epithelial maturation, we reintegrated all epithelial cells and identified canonical colonocyte, goblet, tuft, enteroendocrine, and stem/transit-amplifying (TA) lineages (figure 1A). Pseudo-time reconstruction demonstrated continuous differentiation trajectories in non-HSCR controls across the rectum and distal colon, with stem/TA cells progressing toward terminal epithelial fates. In contrast, HSCR samples from both regions exhibited a pronounced accumulation of cells in early pseudo-time states, accompanied by a marked depletion of cells along differentiated branches (figure 1B). Analysis of pseudo-time distributions showed a substantial shift toward early epithelial states, accompanied by depletion of late-stage differentiated cells in HSCR in both rectal and distal ganglionic colon tissues (figure 1C). Dot-plot profiling of epithelial lineage programs further supported impaired terminal maturation in HSCR, with reduced expression of late-stage differentiation markers. These included mature absorptive/colonocyte (KRT20, CA1/CA2, SLC26A3, VIL1) and goblet cell-associated markers (MUC2, TFF3) (figure 1D). To validate the loss of terminally differentiated epithelial programs, we performed immunofluorescence staining for KRT20, a canonical marker of mature colonic epithelial differentiation. Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, HSCR tissues exhibited a markedly reduced epithelial KRT20 signal compared with controls (figure 1E), accompanied by a significantly decreased KRT20+ fractional area on quantification (figure 1F; p=0.0182). Collectively, these findings indicate an immature/progenitor-like epithelial state with impaired maturation that mirrors the broadly observed metabolic suppression.

Epithelium with impaired maturation exhibits suppressed energy programs and focal DNA damage

To characterize the transcriptional consequences of developmental arrest in the HSCR colonic epithelium, we performed GO enrichment on genes downregulated in HSCR epithelium relative to controls. Terms associated with oxidative metabolism, ATP generation, and electron transport chain activity were significantly suppressed (figure 2A). Given the central role of mitochondria in these pathways, we evaluated mitochondrial content using TOM20 immunostaining. HSCR tissues exhibited a markedly increased TOM20 signal in both crypt and surface epithelium relative to controls (figure 2B,C), suggesting stress-associated mitochondrial remodeling rather than functional enhancement.

We next assessed whether metabolic repression was accompanied by genomic stress. γH2A.X immunostaining revealed focal clusters of DNA damage-positive nuclei within HSCR crypt stem/TA cells, whereas control tissues showed minimal staining (figure 2B and C). These findings indicate that HSCR epithelial progenitors with impaired maturation accumulate DNA damage in parallel with suppression of energy-generating pathways.

HSCR distal ganglionic colon epithelium shows NAD+ biosynthetic insufficiency and increased NAD+ consumption

To identify metabolic nodes central to the observed dysfunction, we examined cofactor-centered pathways. Reporter-metabolite analysis in HSCR distal ganglionic colon highlighted a cluster of significantly affected redox cofactors, with NAD+, NADH, and NADPH emerging as top downregulated nodes, together with glutathione (online supplemental figure 2A). Heatmap analysis across epithelial subsets revealed systematic downregulation of NAD+ biosynthetic enzymes, including NAMPT, NAPRT, NADSYN1, NMNAT1, and NMNAT3 in HSCR distal ganglionic colon colonocytes and stem/TA cell populations compared with controls (online supplemental figure 2B); In contrast, rectal samples showed a more limited pattern of alteration, with only partial reductions observed across epithelial subsets.

Conversely, multiple NAD+-consuming enzymes were upregulated in HSCR distal ganglionic colon epithelium. Sirtuins (notably SIRT6 and SIRT7) and PARP family members (PARP1–4) demonstrated increased expression in stem/TA cell and colonocyte subsets. Collectively, these patterns indicate a transcriptional imbalance between NAD+ biosynthesis and consumption in HSCR, particularly within progenitor-rich compartments of the distal ganglionic colon.

PARP1 activation in HSCR epithelial progenitors with impaired maturation

Because PARP1 activation is strongly induced by DNA damage and is a major consumer of NAD+, we next examined PARP1 protein levels by immunofluorescence. HSCR distal ganglionic colon tissues showed markedly increased nuclear PARP1 staining in crypt epithelium relative to controls, with the signal predominantly confined to the crypt compartment (figure 3A). Quantification of nuclear PARP1 puncta revealed a significantly higher number of PARP1 foci per nucleus in HSCR crypt epithelium relative to controls (figure 3B; p=0.0382). Collectively, these data indicate enhanced nuclear PARP1 accumulation in HSCR crypt progenitors, consistent with activation of DNA damage-associated stress responses.

To obtain an orthogonal readout beyond protein abundance, we also performed immunohistochemical staining for PAR, the catalytic product of PARP-mediated ADP-ribosylation. The PAR signal was increased in HSCR tissues compared with controls (figure 3C), with a significantly higher PAR-positive fractional area on quantification (figure 3D; p=0.0190). Together, these findings are consistent with enhanced PARP-associated ADP-ribosylation and increased NAD+ consumption in HSCR epithelium, although direct metabolite measurements will be required to determine whether intracellular NAD+ pools are depleted.

Discussion

In this study, we provide a comprehensive single-cell characterization of epithelial metabolic and differentiation states in HSCR, with particular attention to the distal ganglionic colon. This is a region that often shows impaired regenerative behavior despite its histologically normal appearance and the presence of enteric ganglia. Importantly, because our analyses were performed on ganglionic bowel, the epithelial stress and metabolic signatures observed cannot be attributed solely to aganglionosis or complete loss of enteric innervation. We identify a convergent epithelial-intrinsic program characterized by metabolic suppression, developmental arrest, mitochondrial remodeling, focal DNA damage, and altered NAD+ biosynthetic and consumption pathways. These findings expand our previous work showing that stromal remodeling and disrupted epithelial-stromal interactions contribute to epithelial vulnerability in HSCR12 and further support the presence of intrinsic epithelial susceptibilities within ganglionic bowel that may underlie postoperative mucosal fragility and increased susceptibility to HAEC.

A prominent feature of the HSCR epithelium is a broad downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, and fatty acid metabolism, particularly within the distal ganglionic segment. This reduced metabolic activity is accompanied by an accumulation of cells in early pseudo-time positions and a corresponding depletion of mature colonocyte and secretory lineages. These observations indicate that impaired metabolic capacity and delayed epithelial maturation occur together in HSCR.1519 This impaired maturation resembles patterns described in other conditions with intestinal injury in which progenitor populations fail to transition into absorptive or secretory cell types. As a complement to these transcriptional patterns, immunostaining reveals increased TOM20 expression and focal γH2A.X accumulation within the stem and TA zones. This combination suggests that mitochondrial stress and DNA damage converge specifically within progenitor compartments. Because mitochondrial dysfunction and genomic instability can impair cell cycle progression, lineage specification, and barrier maintenance, these alterations provide a plausible upstream mechanism for the metabolic suppression observed at the transcriptomic level.

Concurrent suppression of bioenergetic pathways and enrichment of DNA damage-associated signatures in HSCR progenitors point to a shared mechanistic node linking cellular metabolism, genome maintenance, and stress signaling. We therefore asked whether disrupted NAD+ homeostasis might constitute such a node and thereby modulate epithelial regenerative capacity in HSCR. NAD+ plays a central role both as a redox cofactor and as a substrate for sirtuins and PARPs, thus coupling energy metabolism to DNA repair and stress responses.20 Modulation of NAD+ availability has been associated with impaired intestinal regeneration and increased susceptibility to injury.2123 However, whether NAD+ dysregulation contributes to epithelial defects in HSCR remains unexplored, particularly at single-cell resolution.

In our dataset, enzymes required for NAD+ biosynthesis, including NAMPT, NAPRT, NADSYN1, and members of the NMNAT family, were consistently reduced in HSCR, whereas several NAD+-consuming enzymes, including PARP1 through PARP4 and members of the sirtuin family, were increased. These opposing changes are consistent with diminished NAD+ biosynthetic capacity alongside enhanced NAD+-consuming programs, potentially limiting NAD+ availability and downstream signaling in the HSCR epithelium. Studies in other systems have shown that NAD+ depletion impairs intestinal stem cell activity and regeneration; restoration of NAD+ improves mitochondrial fitness and reduces the severity of colitis.15 16 24 In HSCR, the agreement of DNA damage-associated signatures, reduced expression of NAD+ biosynthetic genes, and increased nuclear PARP1 staining supports a model in which NAD+-related stress pathways may be engaged in crypt progenitors. However, PARP enzymatic activity and NAD+ pool depletion were not directly assessed in this study, and data should therefore be interpreted with caution pending direct measurements; this might include ADP-ribosylation assays and quantitative NAD+/NADH profiling.5 15 25 This framework is consistent with models in which excessive PARP activation drives metabolic collapse under conditions of unresolved genomic stress. At the tissue level, these molecular changes align with the structural and functional abnormalities characteristic of HSCR. Although aganglionosis and stromal remodeling remain defining components of the disease, our findings show that metabolic and genomic stress signatures are also evident in epithelial cells from the ganglionic colon, indicating that these vulnerabilities are not confined to aganglionic tissue. Such epithelial stress may exacerbate mucosal fragility and susceptibility to inflammation in the context of persistent dysmotility and luminal stasis. This contributes to continued HAEC risk even after anatomically successful pull-through surgery. Accordingly, the distal ganglionic colon may represent a zone of heightened metabolic vulnerability that predisposes the mucosa to impaired barrier maintenance and dysregulated inflammatory responses.

The translational implications of these findings are significant. First, they highlight NAD+ metabolism and related bioenergetic programs as candidate pathways for future investigations of postoperative mucosal resilience and potential HAEC susceptibility in HSCR. Approaches that increase NAD+ availability, including nicotinamide riboside, nicotinamide mononucleotide, or pharmacologic modulation of NAD+-consuming enzymes, have been shown to promote epithelial repair, preserve mitochondrial function, and strengthen barrier integrity in models of intestinal injury and aging.15 16 26 Whether similar approaches can support epithelial regeneration or reduce the risk of HAEC requires evaluation in organoid and in vivo systems. Second, the NAD+-centered transcriptional signature identified here suggests that metabolic markers, including expression of NAD+ biosynthetic genes or circulating NAD+-related metabolites, may assist in identifying patients at increased risk of mucosal dysfunction. Integrating such metabolic indicators with established clinical parameters, as shown in inflammatory bowel disease, may ultimately improve risk prediction and support individualized postoperative management for children with HSCR.27 28

Despite the clear strengths of this work, several limitations should be acknowledged. The non-HSCR controls were obtained from pediatric patients requiring bowel surgery and therefore do not represent completely healthy colon; they do provide a clinically relevant comparator for ganglionated bowel with dysfunctional motility. In addition, our analyses focused on the postnatal distal ganglionic colon, which lies adjacent to the aganglionic rectum and may include the transition zone. Therefore, the observed epithelial phenotypes should be interpreted as region-specific changes within the diseased HSCR bowel rather than uniform features of ganglionated colon. Of note, we did not perform single-cell transcriptomic analysis, immunostaining, or metabolite-level assessment of NAD+-related pathways in the proximal ganglionic colon. Finally, although transcriptomic data and PARP1/PAR immunostaining suggest NAD+-related metabolic dysregulation, we did not directly measure NAD+/NADH levels or perform functional rescue experiments. Future multi-segment profiling, metabolite-evel validation, patient-derived organoid studies, and correlation with matched HAEC outcomes will be required to establish causality and clinical relevance.

In summary, this study identifies a previously unrecognized epithelial metabolic state in the ganglionic colon of patients with HSCR, characterized by transcriptional signatures of reduced NAD+ biosynthesis, increased NAD+ consumption, focal DNA damage, enhanced PARP1/PAR immunoreactivity, and impaired epithelial maturation. These findings extend current models of postoperative HSCR morbidity and HAEC susceptibility beyond aganglionosis and stromal remodeling, highlighting NAD+-centered pathways as potentially modifiable determinants of mucosal resilience. Future studies integrating organoid models, metabolic rescue approaches, and multiomics profiling will be essential to translate these findings into targeted therapeutic strategies for HSCR.
