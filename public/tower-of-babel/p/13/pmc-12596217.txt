Effect of vortex fluidic device and its combined treatment with ultrasonication on the conformational changes of soy protein isolate
Authors: Ankita Kumari, Abdul Rahaman, Ibrahim Khalifa, Muhammad Adil Farooq, Xin-An Zeng, Narpinder Singh, Shan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnhancing the functional properties of Soy Protein Isolate (SPI) is critical for its effective application in the food industry. The present study explored SPI modification through twomethods: (1) varying the rotational speed of a Vortex Fluidic Device (VFD) between 2000 and 8000 rpm, and (2) integrating Ultrasound (US) treatment (40 kHz, 220 V; 10–50 min) in combination with VFD operation at 8000 rpm. VFD treatment reduced substantial particle size (from 1732 nm to 591.6 nm) and increased the denaturation temperature (Tp) to 109.56 °C, indicating enhanced thermal stability. While the secondary structure was largely stable, a specific conformational shift occurred at VFD6000, marked by a ∼6.7 % decrease in β-turns and a ∼2.9 % increase in β-sheets. In contrast, the synergistic USVFD treatment induced more pronounced structural changes; the US30VFD8000 condition increased β-sheet content by ∼7.4 % and promoted re-aggregation (particle size ∼761.5 nm vs. 623.1 nm for VFD8000), albeit with a slight reduction in Tp to 107.79 °C. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) confirmed that USVFD accelerated degradation, increasing mass loss to ∼36.7 % (vs. ∼29.4 % for native SPI) and reducing final residue to ∼21.2 % (vs. ∼25.3 % for VFD8000). SEM imaging clearly illustrated these trends, revealing fragmented structures after VFD and uniform aggregates with clusters following USVFD treatment. These findings demonstrate that VFD treatment primarily unfolds and fragments SPI, while USVFD promotes re-aggregation into a modified structure. This provides a strategic basis for tailoring SPI functionality, VFD for thermal stability and USVFD for improved hydration, enabling targeted applications in food design.Keywords: Soy protein isolate, Vortex fluidic device, Ultrasonication, Secondary structure, and Aggregation

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a growing demand for protein sources that are highly nutritious, functional, affordable, and suitable for use as functional ingredients in the food industry. Utilising agro-industrial by-products for protein production presents a promising solution, enhancing their commercial value while meeting market needs [1]. Protein isolates are increasingly popular food ingredients because of their important functional properties in food processing and product formulation. Among plant proteins, soy protein isolate (SPI) has emerged as the largest commercially available legume protein due to its high nutritional value, affordability, and excellent functional properties, such as gelation and emulsification, which are valuable in food processing [2]. With advancements in food processing technology and evolving consumer awareness regarding health benefits, SPI is being utilised more extensively as a raw material in food production [3].

However, the native, globular structure of SPI, primarily composed of glycinin and β-conglycinin, buries critical functional groups (e.g., hydrophobic areas, sulfhydryl groups). This compact conformation results in suboptimal functional properties, including poor solubility and gelation, and can impair digestibility, reducing nutritional value [[4], [5], [6]]. Furthermore, environmental factors such as pH near the isoelectric point, heat treatment, and salt ions can induce denaturation and aggregation, limiting SPI's applications [7]. Consequently, structural modification is essential to expand its industrial use. While various chemical, enzymatic, and physical methods exist, physical modifications are increasingly favoured for their simplicity and avoidance of chemical residues, making them a safer alternative [8].

Ultrasound (US) is a safe, non-thermal, prominent physical technology widely used for protein modification. Its mechanism hinges on acoustic energy, where the formation and collapse of bubbles in a liquid–solid system generate intense local shear forces and turbulence. This physical action effectively disrupts non-covalent interactions, such as hydrophobic forces and hydrogen bonds, within and between protein molecules, leading to structural changes that enhance functionality [9]. The efficacy of US is highly dependent on its operational parameters. For example, high-intensity probe sonication (20 kHz) has been shown to significantly improve protein properties, but higher frequencies (e.g., 500 kHz bath treatment) often yield negligible results, underscoring the critical importance of appropriate parameter selection [10]. This principle is evident across various applications: HIU (20 kHz, 400 W) can reduce the particle size of a soy protein-pectin complex to 451.85 nm and boost its solubility to 89.04 % [11], while simultaneously enhancing the gelation and rheological properties of SPI [12]. The impact is also protein-specific; HIU treatment (20 kHz at 400 W) more profoundly alters the tertiary structure and functionality of soybean β-conglycinin (7S) than glycinin (11S) [13]. Furthermore, targeted US application can inhibit undesirable reactions, such as lysinoalanine formation in rice protein, by breaking disulfide bonds and altering protein conformation [14]. Given the sensitivity of outcomes to ultrasonic parameters, a powerful strategy has emerged: combining US with complementary techniques to achieve synergistic effects. This approach is clearly demonstrated in soy protein modification using dual-frequency US (20/28 kHz, 100 W/L, 40 min) with ionic liquids (ILs). This combination significantly alters protein structure, with [BMIM][PF6] increasing solubility by 38.33 % [15] and [BDMIM]Cl boosting it by 71.8 % [16]. The choice of IL even enables targeted functionality, tailoring hydrolysates for improved foaming or enhanced emulsifying and antioxidant activities [17]. The synergistic potential of US extends beyond ILs, as shown by its use in an US-assisted Maillard reaction (400 W, 70 °C, 20 min) with xylo-oligosaccharides, which markedly improved the solubility and emulsifying properties of pea protein across a broad pH range. range [18]. Collectively, the literature confirms that US is a versatile tool whose impact can be amplified through strategic combination with other methods.

The Vortex Fluidic Device (VFD) is a continuous flow microfluidic platform that excels in generating high shear forces, intense mixing, and efficient mass transfer, overcoming limitations of traditional batch processing [19]. Its applications are expanding rapidly, including small-molecule synthesis, drug delivery, and manipulation of single-cell organisms [20]. The VFD can accelerate enzymatic reactions by an average of sevenfold, depending on parameters like rotational speed of the glass tube [21]. Additionally, it facilitates biodiesel production from sunflower oil at room temperature without saponification or complex catalysts [22], and it enables the direct transesterification of microalgae [23]. Besides, the specific rotational speed of VFD causes the sample tube to vibrate harmonically, producing Faraday or pressure waves, which can accelerate protein folding [24,25]. In food applications, VFD processing effectively reduces starch particle size more than conventional homogenization, enhancing the gel strength of kamaboko when combined with fish gelatin [26]. It also transforms kombucha cellulose into a more compact and crystalline form [27].

Despite these diverse applications, no comprehensive study has detailed the VFD's potential for modifying food proteins. Therefore, to establish its industrial relevance in food processing, the effects of VFD pretreatment on plant protein structure and function must be investigated. This study aims to examine the impact of VFD treatment, both independently and combined with US (USVFD), on the structural and aggregation characteristics of SPI. The investigation encompasses particle size distribution, zeta potential, and structural analysis using FTIR, UV–visible spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, DSC, TGA, and DLS. Morphology is characterised by SEM imaging. Ultimately, this research seeks to expand the understanding of how VFD and combined USVFD treatments modulate SPI's functional properties through a comprehensive analysis of its structural and interfacial characteristics.

Experimental materials and methods

Materials

SPI (purity ≥ 85 %) was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All reagents utilised in this study were of analytical grade. Aqueous solutions were prepared using deionised water to ensure purity and minimise contamination.

SPI pretreatment with VFD and combined USVFD

A suspension of SPI was prepared by dispersing 3 g of SPI powder in 200 ml of deionised water, resulting in a concentration of 15 mg/ml. The mixture was stirred at ambient temperature for 2 h to ensure thorough hydration. This solution was then treated with VFD by transferring into the VFD quartz tube (17.5 mm ID, 20 mm OD, 18.5 cm length) in continuous flow mode at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and an inclination angle of 45°, with the rotational speed set to 2000, 4000, 6000, and 8000 rpm, respectively. The processed samples were collected and labelled as VFD2000, VFD4000, VFD6000, and VFD8000, respectively, and freeze-dried for further analysis. For combined USVFD treatment, 15 mg/ml of SPI aqueous solution was subjected to ultrasonication in a water bath (bath-type US) at 25 ± 2 °C in an ultrasonic device SB25-12DT (Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co. Ltd., China) at a frequency of 40 kHz and an ultrasonic power of 220 V for varying durations of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 min. Following ultrasonication, the samples underwent VFD treatment at a rotation speed of 8000 rpm as mentioned above. The resulting processed samples were freeze-dried for further characterization and designated as US10VFD8000, US20VFD8000, US30VFD8000, US40VFD8000, and US50VFD8000 [28].

Characterization of SPI treated with ultrasonication and VFD

X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis

XRD characterization was conducted using a SM-7001F (JEOL Ltd., Japan) instrument. An appropriate quantity of samples was loaded into the sample holder. The instrument was configured with the following parameters: a wavelength (λ) of 0.154 nm, an operating tube voltage of 40 kV, and a current of 40 mA using a copper target. The data were collected at a scanning rate of 5°/min over a range of 5° to 80°, with a scanning frequency of 0.02°/step for increased resolution [29]. UV–Visible Absorption analysis

The absorbance of the sample was tested by a UV–visible spectrophotometer (VIS-7220 N, Beijing Beifen-Ruili Analytical Instrument (Group) Co. Ltd., China). A total of 20 mg of freeze-dried samples was dissolved in 30 ml of deionised water to form the corresponding solution. Spectroscopic measurements were conducted across a wavelength range of 200 to 600 cm−1 at a temperature of 25 °C [30].

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

Functional group characterization conducted by FTIR (Tensor27, Bruker AG, Germany). The crushed samples were mixed with potassium bromide (KBr) powder (1:250, mg/mg) and ground using an agate mortar. Spectra were collected within the beam range of 400 to 4000 cm−1, with a wave number accuracy of 0.01 cm−1 and a resolution of 4 cm−1 [31]. The amide I region, spanning 1600 to 1700 cm−1, was analysed by a Gaussian curve fitting procedure for detailed analysis.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) analysis

The thermal behavior of SPI was studied using DSC (TA DSC250, USA). To investigate the pyrolysis behavior of SPI, 7 mg samples were placed into aluminium crucibles, and the experiment was initiated at a temperature of 30 °C and terminated at 200 °C. The heating rate was set at 10 °C/min, and an empty crucible was used as a blank. The entire detection process was carried out under nitrogen protection, with a nitrogen flow rate of 40 ml/min [32].

Thermogravimetric Analyser (TGA) analysis

To conduct a thermogravimetric analysis of freeze-dried samples, a TGA analyser from TA5500, Germany, was used. The sample weighing 3.0∼5.0 mg was accurately measured and placed into the sample holder before inserting it into the analyser. Experimental parameters were set with a starting temperature of room temperature and a thermal decomposition end temperature of 800 °C. The heating rate was 10 °C/min, and a blank crucible was used for comparison. During the entire measurement process, a nitrogen purge flow rate of 25 ml/min was maintained to ensure an inert atmosphere [32].

Zeta Potential and Particle Size analysis

The dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments, UK) was utilised for particle size and zeta potential analysis. The procedure involved weighing 20 mg of freeze-dried samples and dissolving them in 30 ml of deionised water to form a solution. The Malvern Zeta sizer instrument quantitatively analyses the time-dependent fluctuations in scattered light to determine particle size distributions. [33].

Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) analysis

Structural analysis of the processed SPI was carried out using a SEM (TESCAN MIRA LMS, Czechia). The freeze-dried SPI was pasted on a copper table with black double-sided adhesive tape. After gold spraying, it was placed in the instrument for vacuuming, and parameters such as focal length, contrast, and brightness were adjusted to observe the sample at different magnifications. The acceleration voltage was 15 kV, and the working distance (WD) was 10 mm [34].

Data Processing and Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are expressed as the mean ± SD. Significant differences between means were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's post-hoc test, using a 95 % confidence interval. SPSS 19.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for all statistical calculations, and graphs were created with Origin 2018 and Microsoft Excel.

Results and discussion

XRD analysis

XRD is a powerful technique used to analyze the crystal structure of SPI, particularly its sheet-like properties. The XRD pattern of the combined USVFD-treated SPI (Fig. 1B) was characterized by a strong diffraction peak at 2θ = 19.5° and a smaller peak at 2θ = 9°. These features are indicative of a highly crystalline structure and correspond to the β-sheet (crystalline region II) and α-helical (crystalline region I) conformations, respectively [29,35,36].

A. and B. represent the X-ray diffraction pattern, while C. and D. show the UV–visible spectra of SPI pretreated with VFD and combined US-VFD.

The observed patterns are consistent with previous studies on soybean 7S and 11S globulins, which reported characteristic reflections at approximately 2θ = 10° and 22° [36,37]. A comparison of native SPI with SPI treated by VFD at different rotational speeds (rpm) revealed a gradual shift of the secondary diffraction peak (from ∼19.2° to 19.74° for VFD8000) toward higher 2θ values (Fig. 1A). Whereas, combined USVFD treatment resulted in a slight shift of this peak back toward lower 2θ values compared to VFD8000 treatment alone (Fig. 1B). These peak shifts suggest that the applied treatments induce measurable alterations in the protein's secondary structure [38].

Additionally, results showed that the diffraction peak intensity was significantly affected by the treatment parameters. An increase in US treatment duration within the combined USVFD groups correlated with a marked increase in peak intensity, suggesting enhanced sample crystallinity [39]. In contrast, VFD treatment alone caused a reduction in diffraction peak intensity. The XRD analysis indicated that the synergistic effect of the combined USVFD treatment ultimately led to a net increase in SPI crystallinity compared to native or singly-treated samples. This enhancement suggests the formation of a more ordered molecular structure, which may have likely contributed to improved structural stability of SPI [40].

These findings align with previous work on ultrasonicated SPI coatings, which also reported increased crystallinity [39]. The positive correlation between crystallinity and diffraction intensity observed herein is further supported by the general principle that more crystalline materials yield sharper and more intense XRD peaks [41].

UV–visible absorption spectra analysis

The ultraviolet absorption profile (absorption maxima: λmax) of a protein is a direct indicator of its molecular structure, primarily governed by its constituent aromatic amino acids, tryptophan (λmax ∼ 280 nm), tyrosine (λmax ∼ 275 nm), and phenylalanine (λmax ∼ 260 nm), and the peptide backbone itself (absorbing between 180–230 nm). Consequently, shifts in these characteristic absorption peaks provide a sensitive means to monitor protein behavior and structure in various biochemical contexts [42].

As illustrated in Fig. 1C, VFD treatment induced significant changes in SPI's UV absorption spectrum. Treatments at 2000, 4000, and 6000 rpm yielded higher absorption intensities than both native SPI and the VFD8000-treated sample, indicating that intermediate shear forces prompted substantial protein unfolding and exposure of chromophores. These findings are further supported by a study that reported protein unfolding by shear forces [43].

This effect was further amplified by combined USVFD pretreatment (Fig. 1D). All USVFD-treated samples exhibited a notable increase in absorbance compared to VFD treatment alone, with the most pronounced enhancement observed for the US10VFD8000 sample. This suggests that the effect of ultrasonication was optimal at the 10-minute mark, maximally altering the SPI structure to expose aromatic residues to the solvent and thereby increasing UV absorption [44,45]. A slight absorption maximum at 260 nm across samples can be attributed to a change in the microenvironment of phenylalanine (Phe) residues, aligning with previous findings where US-assisted ionic liquids pretreatment altered SPI Structure [15]. Interestingly, a decrease in absorbance was observed for the US20VFD8000 sample. This reduction could be attributed to factors such as excessive aggregation, pH variations, or steric hindrance from protein conjugation, which can mask chromophores and alter the protein's absorption properties despite underlying structural changes [46].

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectra of SPI were recorded to illuminate the chemical bonds and secondary structural changes of SPI following VFD and combined USVFD treatments. Fig. 2, Fig. 2 show the FTIR spectra of SPI in the range from 400 to 4000 cm−1. The main characteristic spectrum consists of three intense IR bands in the amide regions, which were evaluated in terms of peak shifts and changes in transmittance. Analysis focused on the primary amide bands: Amide I (1700–1600 cm−1, C=O stretch), Amide II (1590–1500 cm−1, N–H bend and C–N stretch), and Amide III (1300–1000 cm−1), alongside the broad O–H stretching region (3500–3000 cm−1) [47].

A. and B. are the FTIR spectra, and C. and D. are the absorbance vs. wavenumber spectra for the amide I region (1700–1600 cm−1), while E and F are the secondary structure contents of SPI pretreated with VFD and combined US-VFD.

Comparative analysis revealed no peak shifts in VFD-treated samples compared to native SPI, indicating that the fundamental chemical groups remained intact. However, significant changes in transmittance intensity were observed. For VFD-treated samples (Fig. 2A), the most substantial decrease in transmittance occurred with VFD6000, while VFD2000 showed an increase compared to native SPI. The SPI samples treated with different rpm of VFD and combined USVFD treatments showed characteristic peaks at the following wavelengths: C-O stretching at 1075 cm−1, O–H stretching at 3300 cm−1, amide I band at 1662 cm−1, amide II band at 1530 cm−1, and amide III band at 1237 cm−1. Similarly, no peak shift was observed in the case of combined USVFD. However, this synergy (Fig. 2B) induced transmittance changes relative to VFD8000 alone, with US10VFD8000 and US20VFD8000 showing increased transmittance and US30VFD8000 to US50VFD8000 exhibiting a progressive decrease. These intensity alterations suggest initial conformational changes in the protein secondary structure.

To quantify secondary structural changes, the Amide I region (1600–1700 cm−1) was subjected to second-derivative analysis and deconvolution using a Gaussian curve-fitting method (Fig. 2, Fig. 2) [48]. Peaks were assigned as follows: α-helix (1664–1646 cm−1), β-sheet (1637–1615 cm−1 and 1700–1682 cm−1), β-turn (1681–1664 cm−1), and random coil (1645–1637 cm−1). The results are summarised in Fig. 2, Fig. 2.

The impact of VFD treatment on the secondary structure of SPI was found to be highly dependent on the rotational speed. While the secondary structures remained largely stable at 2000, 4000, and 8000 rpm, with only minor fluctuations in α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil content compared to the native SPI. This indicates a general resilience of the protein's architecture to the shear forces induced at these conditions. However, a significant and specific conformational shift was observed exclusively at 6000 rpm (Fig. 2E). This was characterised by a ∼6.7 % decrease in β-turn content (18.82 %) and a ∼2.9 % increase in β-sheets (40.62 %) compared to native SPI. This specific conversion of β-turns to β-sheets suggests a partial unfolding and reorganization into a more ordered, aggregated state, indicating a distinct shear energy threshold that triggers this structural transition [16]. The observed shift from disordered elements (random coils) to more ordered structures (β-sheets) is a classic indicator of protein folding or aggregation [49].

The combined USVFD treatments produced more pronounced secondary structural changes than VFD treatment alone, exhibiting a clear dependence on US duration (Fig. 2F). The most significant structural reorganization was observed at the US30VFD8000, resulting in a ∼7.4 % increase in β-sheets (42.50 %) and a ∼11.5 % decrease in α-helices (17.43 %) relative to the VFD8000 baseline. This notable shift suggests that sonomechanical forces synergistically promote the unfolding of α-helices into an extended, aggregation-prone β-sheet conformation. Beyond this 30-minute optimum (US30VFD8000), the structural response changed; β-sheet content remained elevated while α-helix content recovered, suggesting a shift in aggregation pathways or partial refolding at higher energy inputs. These findings demonstrate that USVFD treatment has a synergistic effect, disrupting intermolecular interactions and altering intramolecular hydrogen bonding, thereby significantly modulating SPI's secondary structure. Notably, the β-sheet structure exhibits greater flexibility and openness than the α-helix structure. Thus, the shift towards more flexible β-sheet structures at specific treatment parameters (e.g., US30VFD8000) could enhance protein malleability [15,50]. Overall, the data align with studies reporting that ultrasonic treatment can enhance protein stability by inducing controlled structural rearrangements [45].

DSC analysis

In this study, DSC was used to evaluate the degree of thermal denaturation behavior of SPI following VFD and combined USVFD treatments. This technique measures heat flow changes associated with protein unfolding, providing key parameters such as the onset (To), peak (Tp), and conclusion (Tc) temperatures of denaturation, as well as the enthalpy change (ΔH), which reflects the total energy required for the transition [51,52].

All samples exhibited a notable endothermic peak (Fig. 3, Fig. 3), corresponding to the denaturation of the major protein components in SPI. The data for native and VFD-treated SPI reveal a clear trend: increasing the rotational speed of the VFD from 2000 to 8000 rpm progressively enhanced the thermal stability of the proteins. This is evidenced by a significant increase in the peak denaturation temperature (Tp) from 102.9 °C (Native) to 109.56 °C (VFD8000). The onset temperature (To) also increased, reaching a maximum of 75.71 °C for VFD6000, indicating that a higher energy input was required to initiate unfolding after VFD treatment. These findings suggest that the high-shear environment of the VFD induces structural changes, such as the formation of more stable protein aggregates that are more resistant to thermal denaturation [53,54].

A. and B. are the DSC, and C. and D. are the TGA thermograms of SPI pretreated with VFD and combined US-VFD.

In combined USVFD processing, the VFD8000 sample served as the baseline; US pretreatment modified the thermal properties in a complex, duration-dependent manner. The Tp values for the combined treatments followed the order: US50VFD8000 (109.69 °C) > VFD8000 (109.56 °C) > US40VFD8000 (108.57 °C) > US20VFD8000 (107.86 °C) > US30VFD8000 (107.79 °C) > US10VFD8000 (106.76 °C). This indicates that for most US durations (10–40 min), the pretreatment resulted in a slight decrease in thermal stability compared to VFD8000 alone. This can be attributed to the effects of US, which cause partial unfolding and disaggregation [18], potentially leading to protein structures that are slightly less thermally robust upon subsequent VFD processing. However, the extended 50-minute US treatment (US50VFD8000) yielded a Tp that marginally exceeded that of VFD8000. This suggests that prolonged US exposure may facilitate a more extensive and uniform rearrangement of proteins, enabling the formation of aggregates with superior thermal stability during the subsequent VFD step [54].

Further insights can be drawn from the ΔH. The native SPI had a ΔH of −198.1 J/g. The variations observed in treated samples (e.g., −216.4 J/g for VFD2000 and −197.9 J/g for US50VFD8000) suggest alterations in the cooperativity of the unfolding process and the integrity of the native protein structure [53].

A notable observation was the significant broadening of the denaturation peak in USVFD samples, particularly US30VFD8000 and US40VFD8000, as indicated by their high Tc (160.19 °C and 159.44 °C, respectively) compared to VFD8000 (Tc = 145.25 °C). A wider denaturation temperature range (Tc to To) often signifies a less cooperative unfolding process, pointing to increased structural heterogeneity within the protein population after combined treatment [53].

These findings suggest that VFD processing effectively enhances the thermal stability of SPI, with higher rotational speeds providing greater stability. Its combination with US (USVFD) does not simply amplify this effect but modulates it, primarily by altering the denaturation pathway. The duration of US application is critical, with shorter durations generally reducing stability, and a longer duration (50 min: US50VFD8000) potentially leading to a uniquely stable configuration. The restoration of high thermal stability at US50VFD8000 may be due to the formation of new, stable aggregates or a more extensive structural reorganization that enhances heat resistance. Moreover, the changes in peak broadening further reveal that USVFD treatments increase structural heterogeneity [18,55].

TGA analysis

TGA was employed to assess the thermal degradation profile of SPI subjected to VFD and combined USVFD treatments. The weight loss curves for all samples, ranging from 25 °C to 800 °C, exhibited a similar two-stage degradation pattern (Fig. 3C and 3D), consistent with reports on other plant protein isolates (e.g., sunflower and potato) [[56], [57], [58]]. The initial stage (up to ∼150 °C) involved minimal weight loss, corresponding primarily to dehydration of protein. The second major stage of degradation was attributed to the thermal breakdown of protein polypeptide chains and the volatilization of the resulting fragments.

Despite consistent overall degradation patterns, derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) revealed critical differences in degradation kinetics, particularly in the extent of mass loss and final residue. VFD treatment alone, across all rpm levels (2000, 4000, 6000, and 8000), did not significantly alter the onset temperature of the main protein degradation compared to native SPI (Fig. 3C), indicating that the processing did not enhance the intrinsic thermal stability of the protein molecules. However, in the primary degradation step, VFD-processed samples exhibited greater mass loss (∼32.85 %) than native SPI (∼29.36 %). This suggests VFD processing reduced the thermal stability of the protein's primary structure, likely by inducing conformational changes that made it more susceptible to decomposition. In contrast, analysis of the final residue at 700 °C revealed a speed-dependent trend. The VFD8000 sample yielded a residue (25.88 %) comparable to native SPI (26.87 %), whereas lower speeds (e.g., VFD4000 at 24.38 %) resulted in slightly less residue. This indicates that higher VFD speeds promote a minor stabilising effect at high temperatures.

The combination of USVFD produced more significant alterations, providing key mechanistic insights into the degradation process. Notably, USVFD treatments accelerated the primary degradation step instead of stabilising the protein. This was evidenced by a greater mass loss in the 260–340 °C range compared to the VFD8000 (e.g., ∼36.7 % loss for US40VFD8000 vs. ∼ 33.7 % for VFD8000). This key observation suggests that the intense shear forces and acoustic energy from ultrasonication synergized with VFD to further fragment the protein structure. The resulting smaller, less ordered aggregates consequently exhibited reduced thermal stability, leading to more rapid and complete volatilization during heating.

A more striking effect was observed in the final residue content at 750 °C. All USVFD-treated samples exhibited a significantly lower final residue than the VFD8000 sample (Fig. 3D). For instance, the residue for US10VFD8000 and US30VFD8000 was approximately 21.2 % and 21.6 %, respectively, compared to 25.3 % for VFD8000. This indicates that USVFD treatment effectively reduced the ash (final residue) content of the SPI. The trend in residue reduction was non-linear (US10VFD8000 ≈ US30VFD8000 > US50VFD8000 ≈ US20VFD8000 > US40VFD8000), indicating that the underlying mechanism depends on ultrasonication intensity in a complex, non-monotonic manner.

The non-linear relationship between US treatment and the observed effects suggests a balance between beneficial physical disruption and competing processes, such as excessive denaturation or reaggregation. These findings indicate that the synergy between VFD-induced shear and ultrasonication efficiently fragments protein aggregates and dissociates protein–mineral complexes. This explains the key findings: enhanced organic matrix degradation due to increased surface area and reduced ash content from the liberation of inorganic constituents. The optimal effects at intermediate powers (e.g., US40 for primary degradation; US10/US30 for ash reduction) likely represent an energy threshold that maximises this disruption. This interpretation, that ultrasonication promotes breakdown, is consistent with studies on other proteins (e.g., whey and sunflower protein isolates), where it decreases molecular weight and alters functionality [57,[59], [60], [61]]. Beyond 670 °C, weight loss plateaued, indicating complete volatilization and stable ash formation.

In conclusion, the combined USVFD treatment is a far more effective modifier of SPI's thermal properties than VFD alone. The changes stem not from increased stability, but from extensive physical breakdown of the protein matrix and a reduction in inorganic content. This underscores USVFD's efficacy as a powerful tool for tailoring the composition and structure of plant proteins.

In contrast, VFD treatment alone caused a substantial reduction in particle size, with mean diameters decreasing to between 591.6 nm (VFD6000) and 670.5 nm (VFD4000). This demonstrates the efficacy of shear forces in disrupting native SPI aggregates. The high Span values (>1.5) for VFD2000 and VFD4000 indicate the formation of a polydisperse mixture of smaller particles, while the lower Span for VFD6000 (0.77) suggests a more controlled breakdown or reorganization.

The combination of ultrasonication with VFD8000 led to more complex and duration-dependent effects. Compared to VFD8000 alone (Mean = 623.1 nm), shorter US treatments (US10-, US20-, US30VFD8000) resulted in similar or slightly larger mean sizes. However, US40VFD8000 induced a significant shift towards larger aggregates (Mean = 816.2 nm, D90 = 1441.1 nm) and high polydispersity (Span = 1.87). This suggests that extended ultrasonication promotes extensive aggregation, likely through enhanced protein unfolding and exposure of hydrophobic groups, facilitating intermolecular interactions. The subsequent decrease in mean size for US50VFD8000 (747.2 nm) may indicate a fragmentation of these large aggregates or a shift towards a different equilibrium.

The intensity-based size distributions (Fig. 4, Fig. 4) corroborate these findings. The prominent large-size peak observed for US50VFD8000, along with the high-intensity signal from larger particles in US40VFD8000, confirms the presence of high-molecular-weight aggregates [62,63]. These findings are consistent with the known bimodal distribution of SPI, where the observed peaks can be attributed to the formation of soluble aggregates driven by non-covalent and covalent interactions induced by the mechanical and acoustic energy input [64,65].

Zeta potential was measured to evaluate the colloidal stability of SPI dispersions following VFD and USVFD treatments (Table 2). A high absolute zeta potential value (typically > |±30| mV) indicates strong electrostatic repulsion between particles, promoting stability, whereas lower values suggest a tendency towards aggregation [66].

The native SPI exhibited a high zeta potential of −35.5 mV, denoting a stable colloidal system. VFD treatment alone caused a slight reduction in the absolute zeta potential across all rpm levels (ranging from −31.1 to –33.9 mV), though all values remained within a stable range. This suggests that shear forces partially alter the surface characteristics without critically compromising stability.

The combination of ultrasonication with VFD8000 produced more significant and non-linear effects. Most combined treatments (US10VFD8000, US20VFD8000, US30VFD8000, and US50VFD8000) further decreased the absolute zeta potential compared to VFD8000 alone, with US10VFD8000 showing the lowest value (−30.7 mV). This reduction is likely due to protein aggregation induced by ultrasonication, which can mask surface charges and reduce net charge [67]. In contrast, the US40VFD8000 treatment resulted in the highest zeta potential of all treated samples (−34.5 mV), even exceeding that of native SPI. This indicates that a specific ultrasonication duration (40 min) synergizes significantly with VFD8000 to enhance colloidal stability.

We propose that the mechanical and physical forces of ultrasonication in US40VFD8000 optimally unfold the protein structure, exposing additional internal polar and charged groups (e.g., carboxylate and amine groups) to the particle surface, thereby increasing the net charge [68]. This enhanced electrostatic repulsion would effectively prevent particle aggregation [69]. The subsequent drop in zeta potential for US50VFD8000 (−31.2 mV) implies that excessive ultrasonication leads to over-processing, favoring aggregation over stabilization.

SEM imaging

SEM clearly reflected the impact of different VFD and USVFD treatments on the microstructure of SPI (Fig. 5) at a magnification factor of 4.4kx. The microstructure of native SPI (Fig. 5A) exhibited a characteristic globular yet shriveled morphology, consistent with previous reports [47,70]. Distinct differences were observed between the treatment types. The SEM micrographs of combined USVFD-treated SPI (Fig. 5B) were more uniform in particle size distribution than in VFD treatment alone, where more heterogeneous structures were found. The surface of the USVFD-treated fragmented SPI granules appeared folded or wrinkled with clusters. Some particles were hollow and showed a more porous, albeit indented exterior as the US treatment increased. VFD-treated SPI showed a smooth surface. The structure of the SPI particles in different treatment groups likely results from dissolved protein in the water [70]. The distorted spheres seemed to result from the uneven shrinkage of protein-water particles [71]. The SEM image of USVFD (US20VFD8000, US30VFD8000, and US50VFD8000) indicated the emergence of larger aggregates, suggesting that extending the ultrasonic treatment duration led to the formation of aggregated structures. This phenomenon was due to the unfolding of SPI molecules by the US's acoustic energy-induced shear processing, exposing more free SH groups and hydrophobic regions, enhancing their interactions, and fostering larger aggregates [72]. These SEM micrographs provide direct visual evidence that USVFD treatment, particularly at longer durations, fundamentally alters the supramolecular architecture of SPI, creating a more aggregated and porous matrix compared to the native state or VFD treatment alone.

Mechanism of VFD and combined USVFD treatment

VFD treatment

The application of VFD induces significant structural reorganization in SPI through intense hydrodynamic shear forces (Fig. 6). These forces mechanically disrupt the native protein structure, breaking down large, native aggregates into a population of smaller particles, as evidenced by the drastic reduction in mean particle size from 1732 nm to ∼623 nm (DLS). This shear-induced deformation causes partial unfolding of the secondary structure (FTIR: decreased β-sheets, increased random coils) and disrupts the native crystalline order (XRD: shift to higher 2θ angles (↑2θ)). The partial unfolding exposes hydrophobic amino acid residues (increased UV absorbance at 280 nm). Notably, at high shear (VFD8000), these changes promote the formation of new, stable intramolecular interactions, leading to significantly enhanced thermal stability (DSC: highest Tp and ΔH). However, these structural changes also reduce the protein's water-holding capacity (decreased bound water content from 38 % to 35 %), likely due to altered surface properties. SEM reveals a heterogeneous morphology with both fragmented and aggregated regions, reflecting the competing effects of shear.

Mechanism of VFD and combined USVFD pretreatment on SPI modification.

Combined USVFD treatment

The combination of USVFD introduces a well-known phenomenon of ultrasonic mechanical and physical forces alongside shear (Fig. 6). Acoustic energy-induced shear forces of US first further fragments the protein particles but then drives their reassembly through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding (DLS: increased mean particle size; FTIR: partial restoration of β-sheet structures). This reassembly results in the formation of more uniform, clustered, and porous aggregates (SEM). This reaggregated structure leads to an increase in bound water content (35 % to 47 %), suggesting improved hydration properties. However, these acoustic energy-induced aggregates are generally less thermally stable than those formed by VFD8000 alone (DSC: lower Tp for most USVFD samples), indicating a less efficient and ordered molecular packing. The structural reorganization is further confirmed by UV absorbance shifts, reflecting changes in both the peptide backbone and the exposure of aromatic residues.

Conclusion

This study is among the first to systematically investigate the effectiveness of VFD treatment for transforming SPI, revealing significant conformational changes and demonstrating its powerful synergy when combined with US. The findings indicate that VFD treatment significantly modifies the structural and functional properties of SPI. The processing disrupts SPI's native conformation, as evidenced by decreased β-sheet content and increased random coils, leading to smaller particle sizes and, at optimal high-shear conditions (VFD8000), significantly improved thermal stability, albeit with a lower water-binding capacity. These changes suggest enhanced solubility and potential applications in emulsification or dispersion-based systems.

In contrast, the combined USVFD treatment induced a distinct structural reorganization, restoring some β-sheet content while promoting particle reaggregation. This resulted in a bimodal size distribution, increased hydration, and generally reduced thermal stability compared to the optimal VFD treatment. The structural modifications proceed through fundamentally different pathways: VFD creates heterogeneous structures through mechanical shear disruption, while USVFD drives reorganization into more clustered architectures via acoustic energy input. These distinct morphological outcomes directly correspond to their differing functional properties, with VFD-treated SPI favoring applications requiring partial dissociation and USVFD-treated SPI being more suitable for systems benefiting from controlled reaggregation and water retention.

These insights provide a foundational rationale for selecting processing techniques based on desired functional outcomes in food and biomaterial applications, aligning with industry trends towards non-thermal, sustainable technologies. Future work should focus on optimizing parameters to fine-tune functionality and elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms involved. Overall, this work advances the understanding of non-thermal protein modification, offering scalable approaches to enhance SPI functionality for sustainable innovations.
