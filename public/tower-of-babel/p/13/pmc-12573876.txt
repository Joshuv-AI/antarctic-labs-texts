Impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the monthly proportion of male births and spontaneous fetal death rates in Japan: a descriptive epidemiological study using national vital statistics
Authors: Mihoko Takahashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA significant decrease in secondary sex ratio in December 2020 in Japan has been reported, suggesting the COVID-19 pandemic was a population-level stressor. However, few studies have investigated detailed trends in secondary sex ratio during the pandemic. This study aimed to describe the proportion of male births in Japan by month, mother’s age and whether it was her first live birth, and suggest potential causes of anomalous variations during the COVID-19 pandemic, including variations in monthly spontaneous fetal deaths.MethodsJapanese national vital statistics data for 2006–2022 were used. Monthly proportion of male births by mother’s age and whether it was her first live birth were analyzed. Spontaneous fetal death rates were also examined. Using a method similar to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{\text{X} }$$\end{document}−R control chart, anomalous variations in each indicator during COVID-19 were identified and the excess proportion of male births and excess spontaneous fetal deaths were estimated.ResultsAbnormal increases in the proportion of male births occurred in March 2020, November 2021 and June 2022, and a decrease in December 2020 and the biggest decrease in September 2021. These abnormal variations did not occur among first live births for mother’s 20–29 and 40 or over, but there was an increase in March 2022 among subsequent live births for mother’s 20–29, and decreases in March or April 2022 among all first live birth groups except women 20–29. Background

The COVID-19 pandemic may have affected pregnancy and birth. It is essential to understand these effects, to develop strategies for future health crises. Secondary sex ratio (SSR) is expressed as the sex ratio at birth (male births/female births) or the proportion of male births (male births/total births). It is a sentinel indicator of population health, with decreases suggesting unfavorable conditions and increases suggesting favorable ones [1–4]. Factors that could affect SSR include birth order, maternal age, coital frequency, and socio-economic conditions [5]; infections [6], lifestyle (e.g. dietary habits, chronic stress) [7], and occupational chemical exposure (e.g. medical disinfectants) [8]. During the pandemic, people experienced changes in living conditions, economic stagnation, poorer employment environments, and a public health crisis. These all caused increased uncertainty about the future, and possibly stress. Exposure to stressful events such as earthquakes [9], terrorist attacks [3], and war [10] are also associated with lower SSR. SSR could influence household structure, number of births, and total population [11, 12]. It is therefore important for public health planning to examine trends and investigate causes of abnormal phenomena.

Two mechanisms can explain SSR changes. One is a change in the probability that the egg is fertilized by sperm with an X or Y chromosome. This results in a biased SSR 8–10 months later. The other is the disproportionate demise of embryos of one sex. Miscarriages are more common for male fetuses than female [13, 14], which could cause a lower male birth proportion.

In England and Wales, the SSR was higher in December 2020, possibly because of increased sexual activity associated with lockdown restrictions in March 2020 [4]. The United States had SSR decreases in twins in three conception cohorts exposed in utero to the onset of the pandemic [16], possibly a result of miscarriages, but no change in the SSR for singleton births [17]. However, there were marked differences by maternal race/ethnicity, age, and education, with a lower SSR in more vulnerable groups [17]. Inoue et al. showed a significant SSR reduction throughout Japan in December 2020 (9 months after COVID-19 was declared a pandemic) using monthly statistics from January 2013 to April 2021 [15]. This suggests that the pandemic was a population-level stressor. They found no other SSR reduction.

Government statistics in Japan provide information about monthly births by sex, maternal age, and live birth order. However, few studies have analyzed those data, and none have analyzed SSR after April 2021. Aggregate SSR comparisons hide shifts in SSR by maternal age and live birth order subgroups. Looking at monthly SSR stratified by mother’s age and whether it was her first live birth (FLB) or a subsequent live birth (SLB) may suggest causes of abnormal variations in SSR during the pandemic in Japan. This could provide valuable insights to prevent abnormal variations in the proportion of male births in future public health crises.

This study therefore aimed to describe the distribution of proportion of male births in Japan by month, mother’s age and FLB/SLB, and generate hypotheses about factors that might have caused anomalous variations during the COVID-19 pandemic, including variations in monthly spontaneous fetal death rate (SFDR).

Materials and methods

Design and setting

This was a descriptive epidemiological study in Japan. The study focused on the years before the COVID-19 pandemic (2006–2019 except 2009–2012) and during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022). The data from 2009 to 2012 were excluded because the A(H1N1) pdm09 pandemic and Great East Japan Earthquake occurred during those years [18, 19]. Monthly proportion of male births was analyzed by group, defined by mother’s age (19 or under, 20–29, 30–39, and 40 or over) and whether she had other children (FLB/SLB). Monthly SFDR was also analyzed. However, no monthly data are published for fetal deaths by mother’s age and whether she has other children.

Definition of SFDR

Japanese national statistics define monthly SFDR as spontaneous fetal deaths (SFD)/total births (live births + fetal deaths). However, the SFDR should be an indicator of the tendency toward fetal deaths. Monthly SFDRmod was therefore defined as:

Data source

Statistics on live births and fetal deaths were obtained from the National Vital Statistics of Japan [20]. For monthly proportion of male births, the study used the annual and monthly data on live births between 2006 and 2022, including sex, mother’s age and live birth order. Earlier data are not published. Missing data was excluded (Those were less than 0.0017% of the total data). For monthly SFDRmod, monthly data were obtained on live births, SFD (miscarriages/stillbirths) and artificial fetal deaths (abortions) between January 2006 and July 2023, plus data on percent distribution of annual live births, spontaneous fetal deaths, and artificial fetal deaths by period of gestation between 2006 and 2023.

The National Vital Statistics is a survey based on birth, death, marriage, divorce, and fetal death records. It is published as an annual report. The use of these data therefore did not require any particular ethical considerations.

Results

Annual live births and proportion of male births

Total live births decreased consistently from about 1.1 million in 2006 to 0.77 million in 2022. Trends differed among the groups. For mother’s 19 or under and 20–29, live births decreased from 2006–2019 among SLB and FLB. Meanwhile, for mother’s 30–39, live births increased from 2006–2008 among SLB and from 2006–2010 among FLB. For mother ‘s 40 or over, live births increased from 2006–2016 among SLB and FLB. After that, all trends declined from 2020–2022. Throughout the observed period, the largest number of live births was among SLB for mother’s 30–39 (365,623 in 2006 and 285,153 in 2022) and the smallest was among SLB for mother’s 19 or under (1,692 in 2006 and 503 in 2022) (Supplementary File 4).

Seasonal indices of proportion of male births before the pandemic

Before the pandemic, overall seasonal indices were significantly higher in April–July and September than March (Mann–Whitney U test, all p < 0.005) (Supplementary File 5). Specifically, they were significantly higher in April than in March among SLB for mother’s 30–39, in June than in March among SLB for mother’s 20–29, in June than in February among FLB for mother's 19 or under and in July than in February among FLB for mother’s 20–29 (Mann–Whitney U test, all p < 0.005). Variances were not significantly different between months in any groups (Levene’s test; all p > 0.05).

Discussion

There were abnormal decreases in the proportion of male births in December 2020 and September 2021, and increases in November 2021 and June 2022. One or more of these changes occurred in all groups except FLB groups, 20–29 and 40 or over. The decreases in March/April and October/November 2022 only occurred in some FLB groups. Increases in June, July, and December 2022 occurred in all SLB groups except 40 or over.

Impact of COVID-19 infection

The same phenomenon has historically occurred. SSR decreases have been noted following the Spanish flu pandemic and outbreaks of tuberculosis/HIV [24–26]. Some research has also identified that having confirmed COVID-19 was associated with negative mental health outcome (i.e. a stressor) [27, 28]. However, the abnormal decreases in this study were mostly in individual months and across different groups. COVID-19 infection was therefore probably not the cause.

Impact of SFDRmod

There was only one abnormal increase in SFDRmod, in August 2022, when SARS-CoV-2 Omicron subvariant BA.5 was prevalent. The decline in proportion of male births in November 2020 and September 2021 therefore cannot be explained by changes in SFDRmod. The decrease in SFDRmod in August 2020 was larger, but also does not explain the increases in proportion of male births in November 2021 and June 2022. However, there were slight decreases in SFDRmod in January 2021 and February 2022 (when B.1.1.214/B.1.1.284 and BA.1/BA.2 variants were prevalent), which may be related to increased proportions of male births in November 2021 (10 months later) and June 2022 (4 months later). (Supplementary file 7).

Policy impact: COVID-19 vaccination

A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that COVID-19 vaccination was not associated with an increased risk of miscarriage or reduced rates of ongoing pregnancy or live births among women of reproductive age [29]. The COVID-19 vaccination program for adults began on 17 June 2021 in Japan (Supplementary File 7). The majority of the population had received one dose of vaccine by 7 March 2022: over 80% of each age group from 12–49 [30]. Abnormal changes in proportion of male births and SFDRmod were observed in all groups after vaccination started. However, there are no obvious relationships.

Policy impact: disinfectant

In April 2020, the World Health Organization recommended improving hand hygiene [31]. The resulting increased use of disinfectant in Japan [32–34] might have affected the proportion of male births. However, this does not explain decreases in specific months because hand hygiene compliance remained high at least until March 2022 [32].

Policy impact: state of emergency declarations

Births in December 2020, September 2021, March/April 2022 and October/November 2022 resulted from pregnancies beginning around March 2020, December 2020, June/July 2021 and January/February 2022. Those months were immediately before Japan’s state of emergency periods (7 April–25 May 2020, 8 January–21 March 2021, 25 April–30 September 2021 and 9 January–21 March 2022) (Supplementary File 7). Research has linked COVID-19-related stressors, i.e. worry about COVID-19 infection, loneliness, and loss of employment, etc., with clinical mental symptoms [35]. Social and economic factors might therefore have affected male conception or earlier miscarriage.

Social impact: coital frequency

Parental coital rates may affect proportion of male births, via parental hormone levels at conception [36, 37]. Proportion of male births from natural insemination changes with the menstrual cycle, being higher for insemination at six or more days before menstruation, and lower around ovulation [38, 39]. High coital rates at conception could therefore be followed by high SSR [36].

One study showed a lower frequency of sexual intercourse from late March–late May 2020 in Japan [40]. Another showed significant decreases in total births between December 2020 and February 2021 in Japan (observed period: December 2011–May 2021), suggesting reduced parental sexual activity throughout the population around February–May 2020 [41]. In Croatia, sex ratio at birth remained constant despite two concurrent episodes of COVID-19 and earthquakes, and total live births were also unchanged in this period [42]. Decreased coital rate in Japan might at least partially explain the decrease in the proportion of male births in December 2020. However, there were no abnormal decreases in the proportion of male births in January and February 2021 during the largest decrease of live births. Reduced parental sexual activity is therefore unlikely to have caused decreases in the proportion of male births.

Social impact: positive events

The FIFA World Cup in South Africa was correlated with increased male births about 9 months later, suggesting reduced stress and/or increased coital activity [43, 44]. In Japan, an increase in SSR was also observed in some prefectures 10 months after the 2019 Rugby World Cup in Japan, which was held in Asia for the first time [15]. Similarly, our results suggest that positive events could be related to increases in the proportion of male births 8–10 months later. Births in November 2021 and June 2022 resulted from pregnancies beginning around February 2021, when Japan started its COVID-19 vaccination program, and September 2021, when the third and longest state of emergency was lifted. Those events restarted socio-economic activity and might have reduced stress. Births in March 2020 resulted from pregnancies beginning around June 2019, when Japan’s new imperial era started, although that was not during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Economic impact of COVID-19

Economic decline may also reduce the proportion of male births [45–47]. To address the economic impact of COVID‑19, the Japanese government provided financial support as a COVID-19 fiscal stimulus program called Special Cash Payments, giving JPY100,000 to every individual around May–July 2020. Virtually all households (99.4%) received benefits [48]. There were no decreases in the proportion of male births in any group 9–10 months later. The Japanese government also provided subsidies for households with children between May 2020 and March 2022. These included an extraordinary special benefit of JPY100,000 per child under 18 years (income-tested) in Nov 2021–Mar 2022. The first month for accepting applications was decided by each municipality. This subsidy was distributed to 17.2 million children (90% of the total population under 18 years old) [49]. The different trends in October–December 2022 in FLB and SLB could be related to this. Financial support programs might therefore have prevented decreases in the proportion of male births during the states of emergency.

In Japan, women with children under the age of 18 had more marked decreases in pre-tax monthly income (around 6%–12% of a normal month) than all women (around 0%–9%) and Males (around 2%–5%) in March–July 2020, especially April–May 2020 [50]. That might partly explain the different trends in November 2020–May 2021 in FLB and SLB. The Japanese government announced the implementation of Special Cash Payments in April 2020, right after the state of emergency area was first enlarged from seven prefectures to nationwide. Those payments were concentrated in June and July 2020 (especially June) [48]. These may be the reasons why abnormal decreases occurred only in December 2020 but not in consecutive months.

Definition of SFDRmod

A new indicator of SFDRmod (per 1,000 pregnancies ≥ 12 weeks of gestation) was defined and used. This has two limitations. First, to estimate the number of pregnancies, it was necessary to assume that the proportion of LB, SFD and AFD by gestation period were constant across months, because of the data limitation. Seasonality in these figures may have led to underestimation or overestimation of the number of pregnancies in some months. There is especially a possibility of underestimation around “April” and overestimation around “October” and “July–September in 2020”, given that the largest number of live births occurred in September, and the smallest number of live births occurred in March, and there were decreases in live births from December 2020–February 2021 [41]. The decline in SFDRmod in August 2020 may be partly due to this. Second, SFDRmod analysis by group was impossible because of limited data availability. Gathering and analyzing monthly SFDRmod details by group may provide new insights.

A previous study showed an apparent increase in SFDR in February 2021 [51], which was not confirmed in this study. The apparent increase in traditional SFDR may have been partly due to the impact of COVID-19. However, a significant decrease in live births in February 2021 has also been reported [41]. The definition of SFDR means that reduced live births could give an apparent increase in SFDR. The definition of SFDRmod could add new findings about the tendency for fetal death to occur that are not apparent when the traditional method of calculating SFDR is used. This can be a substantial strength of SFDRmod.

SFDRmod and COVID-19 variants

Japan experienced eight waves of COVID-19 in the period covered by this study [52]. The decreases in SFDRmod in August 2020, January 2021 and February 2022, and increase in August 2022 were all at the peak of a wave. A survey covering 60% of delivery institutes in Japan showed that only a small number of pregnant women were infected between July 2020 and June 2021 (pre-Delta variant period) and there was no significant association between COVID-19 and stillbirth [53]. Pregnant Japanese women complied with COVID-19 preventive measures [54, 55], and the SFDRmod decreases may have resulted from stronger preventive behaviors at the peak of each wave.

Evidence on how the Omicron subvariant BA.5 affected the risk of SFD in Japan is limited. However, a study in the United States showed that risk of stillbirth remained high during the Omicron period, although lower than in the pre-Delta and Delta periods [56]. COVID-19 cases increased with every wave, including among pregnant women, especially during the Omicron BA.5 period [57]. This could have caused the SFDRmod increase in August 2022, although a previous study using the traditional SFDR definition showed no SFDR increase in August 2022 [51].

Incidental findings

Finally, there were also some incidental findings. The lowest annual proportion of male births was among FLB for mother’s 40 or over and the highest among FLB for mother’s up to 39. SLB for 20–29 and 30–39 had monthly disparities in male birth proportion between March and April/June, and there was a monthly disparity in SFDRmod between June and October. Further research is needed to understand factors that may cause these variations, which is outside the scope of this study.

Study limitations

Some studies have used an Auto Regressive Moving Average (ARMA) model to predict monthly SSR without the pandemic. Inoue et al. used an ARMA model, and predicted SSR from February 2020–April 2021 using data from January 2013–January 2020 (before the pandemic). However, this study focused on the proportion of monthly male births and SFDRmod for 2020–2022. There is a risk in predicting SSR for January 2020–December 2022 from a limited number of years (2013–2019). The reference range of the seasonal indices for monthly proportion of male births and SFDRmod assumed that seasonality did not change during the observed period, which may not be accurate. However, the findings were similar when reference range determination used data from 2013–2019. The study did not directly look at the proportion of males in SFD due to a high proportion of unspecified sex (around 35% of SFD). The Japanese government declared states of emergency for certain prefectures in each case. However, the study did not examine monthly trends by region. Inoue et al. showed that the SSR only decreased in December 2020 in the 13 prefectures under “special alert”, where it was particularly necessary to promote measures to prevent the spread of infection [15]. This supports our hypothesis that socio-economic factors might have affected the proportion of male births. Further analysis by prefecture is needed to clarify the pandemic’s regional impact. The study limitations also include the absence of individual data such as socioeconomic status and demographic. The study therefore did not consider other factors that might have affected the male birth proportion, such as father’s age, lifestyle differences, and occupational exposure to chemicals.

Finally, the number of monthly live births for SLB group for women 19 or under from 2006–2019 was only 55–177, which could be too small to estimate excess seasonal indices accurately. However, months showing changes in the proportion of male births in this group largely matched the overall figures.

Conclusion

There were anomalous variations in the proportion of male births in Japan from 2020–2022. There was also an abnormal increase in the SFDRmod, but this did not explain the abnormal decreases in the proportion of male births. Abnormal variations in the proportion of male births could be related to the timing of the state of emergency declarations, the cancellation of the longest state of emergency, the start of the vaccination program, and financial stimulus payments, as many of the variations occurred 8–10 months after these socio-economic events.

Abnormal decreases in the proportion of male births were not always seen alongside reduced live births. This implies that changes in parental sexual activity were unlikely to have affected the proportion of male births. However, socio-economic events might have affected either the risk of miscarriage before 12 weeks of gestation, or the probability of the egg being fertilized by sperm with an X or Y chromosome. Positive events, such as the start of the vaccination program and financial support programs might have prevented decreases in the proportion of male births during the states of emergency. This might explain why abnormal decreases did not occur in corresponding months, but analytical studies are needed to test this hypothesis.
