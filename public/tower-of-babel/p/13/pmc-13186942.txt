Effects of speed–agility–quickness (SAQ) training on pre-planned change-of-direction speed in adolescent and young adult team ball sport athletes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuyang Ji, Bin Wang, Qingqiong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo systematically review and meta-analyze the effects of speed–agility–quickness (SAQ) training on pre-planned change-of-direction speed (CODS) in adolescent and young adult team-sport athletes and to explore potential moderating factors. Following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, randomized controlled trials published from database inception to 15 November 2025 were searched in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, CNKI, EBSCOhost, and the Cochrane Library. Eligible studies involved basketball, soccer, or handball athletes aged 9–26 years, with the experimental group receiving SAQ-dominant interventions and the control group performing routine training, regular sport-specific practice, no additional training, or other non-SAQ comparison conditions. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using a random-effects model. Subgroup analyses, restricted cubic spline meta-regression, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias assessments were conducted. Twenty-two studies contributing 26 effect sizes were included, comprising 17 effect sizes for pre-planned CODS and 9 for linear sprint performance. Compared with controls, SAQ training significantly improved pre-planned CODS (SMD = − 0.71, 95% CI − 0.92 to − 0.51, P < 0.00001) and also improved linear sprint performance (SMD = − 0.90, 95% CI − 1.18 to − 0.62). For CODS, subgroup analyses revealed no significant moderation by age (≤ 18 vs. > 18 years, P = 0.92), weekly training volume (≤ 120 vs. > 120 min/week, P = 0.19), competitive level (elite/club vs. school/university, P = 0.63), or sport discipline (basketball, soccer, handball). Meta-regression did not identify statistically significant non-linear associations for the examined moderators. Sensitivity analyses supported the stability of the pooled estimates, although potential publication bias should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the effects. SAQ training appears to be an effective strategy for improving pre-planned CODS and linear sprint performance in team-sport athletes aged 9–26 years. The available evidence suggests that these benefits may be observed across different age groups, training volumes, competitive levels, and sports, although variability in intervention design, outcome assessment, and study quality should be acknowledged. These findings support the inclusion of SAQ training within routine physical conditioning programs for this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46144-2.Keywords: Speed-agility-quickness training, Change-of-direction speed, Youth athletes, Team sports, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Pre-planned change-of-direction speed (CODS) is a critical physical determinant of competitive performance and tactical execution in team ball sports1. Time–motion analyses indicate that elite adult soccer players perform approximately 1200–1400 directional changes per match, of which 700–800 involve turns of 0–90°2. In basketball, 20–25% of sprint bouts include at least one sharp change of direction3, whereas a substantial proportion of attacking and defensive actions in handball require rapid deceleration followed by re-acceleration (Karcher and Buchheit 2014). Superior pre-planned CODS is associated not only with enhanced performance during decisive match situations but also with a lower risk of non-contact lower-limb injury4,5. However, the association between CODS and linear sprint speed is only moderate (r = 0.25–0.84), indicating that these capacities involve partially distinct physiological and technical demands and may therefore require partly specific training stimuli6,7.

The period from childhood through young adulthood (approximately 9–26 years) represents a phase of substantial neuromuscular plasticity, during which targeted multidirectional training may induce meaningful performance adaptations8,9. SAQ training, which typically integrates short sprints, multidirectional drills, ladder footwork, cutting tasks, and rapid movement-coordination exercises, is widely used in the physical preparation of team ball sport athletes. Randomized controlled trials have reported that 6–12 weeks of SAQ training can improve performance in tests such as the T-test, Illinois agility test, 505 change-of-direction test, and shuttle-run protocols among adolescent soccer, basketball, and handball players10,11. Recent meta-analyses have also reported moderate-to-large effects of SAQ training on pre-planned CODS, linear sprint performance, and lower-limb explosive power in youth athletes12,13.

Despite these findings, several important limitations remain in the current literature. First, many previous meta-analyses have focused predominantly on a single sport, especially soccer, and have included relatively small pooled samples, with comparatively limited representation from basketball and handball athletes8. Second, potentially important moderating variables, such as age, weekly training volume, competitive level, and sport discipline, have not been examined consistently, which may partly explain the substantial heterogeneity reported across studies14. Third, some studies and previous reviews have not clearly distinguished pre-planned CODS from reactive agility, despite their partially different movement and perceptual-cognitive demands. In addition, dose–response patterns have rarely been explored using flexible approaches such as nonlinear meta-regression8,15. As a result, uncertainty remains regarding the magnitude, generalizability, and possible moderators of SAQ training effects on pre-planned CODS in adolescent and young adult team ball sport athletes.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to systematically review and quantitatively synthesize the available randomized controlled trials published up to November 2025 to determine the effects of SAQ training on pre-planned CODS in basketball, soccer, and handball athletes aged 9–26 years. Linear sprint performance was also examined as a secondary outcome when reported. In addition, subgroup analyses and restricted cubic spline meta-regression were conducted to explore the potential moderating roles of age, weekly training volume, competitive level, and sport discipline, thereby providing a more comprehensive evidence base for physical training practice in team ball sports.

Materials and methods

Registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement. The review protocol was registered prospectively in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration no CRD420251244911).

Search strategy

Literature searches were independently conducted by two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer when necessary. The following electronic databases were searched from inception to 15 November 2025: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, EBSCOhost (including SPORTDiscus and Academic Search Complete), the Cochrane Library, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). An initial search was performed in April 2025 and updated in November 202516.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH) and free-text keywords related to speed–agility–quickness training, change-of-direction performance, youth athletes, team ball sports, and randomized controlled trials17. Separate but conceptually equivalent search strategies were used for English-language databases and CNKI. No restrictions on country of origin or publication language were applied. Chinese-language studies identified through CNKI were eligible for inclusion when they met the predefined criteria. Grey literature sources such as conference abstracts and trial registries were not included in the primary analysis; however, the reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible trials.

The PubMed search strategy was as follows: (“speed-agility-quickness” OR “SAQ training” OR “agility training” OR “change of direction” OR “change-of-direction speed” OR “CODS” OR “pre-planned agility” OR “multidirectional speed” OR “ladder drill” OR “cone drill” OR “agility ladder” OR “shuttle run training”) AND (“adolescent” OR “youth” OR “young adult” OR “teen” OR “junior athlete” OR “child” OR “pediatric” OR “9–26 years”) AND (“soccer” OR “football” OR “basketball” OR “handball” OR “team ball sport”) AND (“randomized controlled trial” OR “randomised controlled trial” OR “RCT” OR “controlled trial” OR “intervention study”).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined a priori according to the PICOS framework18.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Population: adolescent or young adult athletes aged 9–26 years who regularly participated in basketball, soccer, or handball at school, university, club, sub-elite, or elite levels; (2) Intervention: the experimental group received a structured speed–agility–quickness (SAQ) training program as the primary intervention; (3) Comparator: the control group continued routine training, received no additional training, or performed non-SAQ interventions such as linear sprint training, traditional strength/power training, or technical/tactical practice only; (4) Outcomes: the primary outcome was pre-planned change-of-direction speed (CODS), assessed using objective timed field-based tests such as the Illinois agility test, T-test, 505 change-of-direction test, 4 × 10 m shuttle run, or other pre-planned directional-change protocols. Linear sprint performance was considered a secondary outcome when reported; and (5) Study design: parallel-group or cluster-randomized controlled trials. Studies were required to provide sufficient pre- and post-intervention data for effect size calculation.

Studies were excluded if participants were outside the 9–26-year age range, involved sports other than basketball, soccer, or handball, did not use SAQ as the primary intervention stimulus, failed to include a usable concurrent control group, did not report sufficient data for effect size calculation, or were non-randomized studies, reviews, conference abstracts, protocols, or non-peer-reviewed theses.

PICOS framework

The review question was formulated a priori according to the PICOS framework. The population comprised adolescent and young adult team-sport athletes aged 9–26 years participating in basketball, soccer, or handball. The intervention of interest was SAQ-dominant training. Comparators included routine training, no additional training, or other non-SAQ training conditions. The primary outcome was pre-planned CODS assessed using objective field-based timed tests, whereas linear sprint performance was treated as a secondary outcome when available. Eligible study designs were randomized controlled trials. The age range of 9–26 years was selected to capture the developmental continuum from late childhood to early adulthood. To address potential developmental heterogeneity, age was examined a priori in subgroup analyses (≤ 18 vs. > 18 years).

Operational definition of SAQ-dominant training

In this review, SAQ-dominant training was operationally defined as a structured program in which the primary training stimulus targeted rapid acceleration, deceleration, pre-planned directional changes, footwork frequency, and movement quickness through drills such as agility-ladder footwork, cone-based cutting drills, shuttle-run variations, and short multidirectional sprint sequences.

Programs were classified as SAQ-dominant when (1) at least 70% of the intervention content or scheduled session time consisted of SAQ-related drills, or (2) the authors explicitly described SAQ as the principal intervention focus. Mixed programs that included resistance, plyometric, or technical exercises were included only when these components were clearly supplementary and did not represent the primary intervention stimulus. Trials were excluded when the specific contribution of SAQ could not be reasonably isolated.

Study selection and data extraction

All records retrieved from the electronic databases were imported into Zotero (Corporation for Digital Scholarship, Vienna, VA, USA) for deduplication. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were then retrieved and assessed independently by the same two reviewers. Disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer.

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized electronic spreadsheet. The extracted information included study characteristics (first author, publication year, and country), participant characteristics (age, sex, competitive level, sport discipline, and sample size per group), intervention characteristics (training content, duration, frequency, and weekly volume), comparator characteristics, and outcome data for all eligible pre-planned CODS and linear sprint tests, including pre- and post-intervention means, standard deviations or standard errors, and exact sample sizes.

Risk-of-bias assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias of each included randomized controlled trial using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when necessary. Five domains were evaluated: bias arising from the randomization process (D1), bias due to deviations from intended interventions (D2), bias due to missing outcome data (D3), bias in measurement of the outcome (D4), and bias in selection of the reported result (D5). Each domain was judged as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk,” and an overall judgment was assigned according to RoB 2 guidance. Risk-of-bias summary figures are presented in Figs. 2 and 3, following Cochrane RoB 2 guidance18.

Statistical analysis

Effect sizes were calculated as standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals. Negative SMD values indicated better performance in the SAQ group because lower times represented superior CODS or sprint performance. The magnitude of the standardized mean difference (SMD) was interpreted using conventional thresholds: < 0.2 as trivial, 0.2 to 0.49 as small, 0.5 to 0.79 as moderate, and ≥ 0.8 as large. Random-effects models were used to account for expected between-study heterogeneity in participant characteristics, intervention protocols, and outcome assessments. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, the I2 statistic, and the between-study variance (τ2)19. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to age (≤ 18 vs. > 18 years), weekly training volume (≤ 120 vs. > 120 min/week), competitive level (elite/club vs. school/university), and sport discipline (basketball, soccer, handball). The 120 min/week threshold was selected as a pragmatic cutoff because it approximated the central distribution of weekly exposure across the included interventions and allowed comparison between lower- and higher-volume SAQ programs. Restricted cubic spline meta-regression was further performed to explore potential non-linear associations between selected continuous moderators and training effects. Because meta-regression estimates can be unstable when based on a limited number of studies, these analyses were interpreted cautiously18. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using leave-one-out procedures and exclusion of studies at high risk of bias. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test when sufficient studies were available. All analyses were conducted in R using the metafor and rms packages. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Results

Study selection

The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1 following the PRISMA guidelines. A total of 663 records were identified through database searching, and no additional records were obtained from other sources. After removing 312 duplicates, 77 records excluded by automation tools, and 62 records excluded based on other criteria, a total of 212 records remained for title and abstract screening. During the screening phase, 188 records were excluded based on predefined eligibility criteria. Subsequently, 24 full-text articles were sought for retrieval, of which 2 reports could not be retrieved (e.g., due to unavailable full texts). The remaining 22 articles were assessed for eligibility, and no additional studies were excluded at this stage. Finally, 22 studies were included in the systematic review, contributing a total of 26 effect sizes for the meta-analysis.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection.

Detailed characteristics of the included studies and interventions are presented in Table 1. Overall, the included studies involved adolescent and young adult athletes from soccer, basketball, and handball, with participants covering school-, university-, club-, and national-level backgrounds. Sample sizes were generally modest, although several studies included comparatively larger cohorts. Participant ages spanned late childhood to early adulthood, reflecting the predefined age range of 9–26 years used in the present review. The intervention protocols also varied across studies. Program duration ranged from 4 to 12 weeks, with training frequencies of 1–5 sessions per week and weekly training volumes ranging from 30 to 420 min/week when available. Control conditions mainly included routine training, regular sport-specific practice, or no additional training. Risk-of-bias assessment of included studies

The methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, which evaluates five domains of potential bias: bias arising from the randomization process (D1), bias due to deviations from intended interventions (D2), bias due to missing outcome data (D3), bias in measurement of the outcome (D4), and bias in selection of the reported result (D5).

The detailed assessment of each included study across the five domains is presented in Fig. 2.

Overall, most studies were judged as having some concerns regarding risk of bias. A small number of studies were classified as high risk of bias, mainly due to issues related to the randomization process. In contrast, the domains related to missing outcome data (D3) and measurement of outcomes (D4) generally demonstrated a low risk of bias, indicating that outcome assessment and data completeness were adequately addressed in most studies.

The distribution of risk-of-bias judgments across all included studies is shown in Fig. 3. The majority of studies were rated as having some concerns overall, while a smaller proportion were classified as high risk of bias. No study was judged as having a completely low overall risk of bias.

These findings suggest that while the included studies generally demonstrated acceptable methodological quality, certain methodological limitations—particularly related to randomization procedures—should be considered when interpreting the pooled results.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis (CODS)

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the pooled effect for pre-planned change-of-direction speed (CODS). Each study was sequentially removed and the meta-analysis was recalculated to assess whether any single study exerted a disproportionate influence on the overall estimate (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity Analysis for CODS.

The results indicated that the pooled standardized mean difference remained relatively stable after the exclusion of individual studies. The recalculated pooled effect sizes ranged from − 0.64 to − 0.77, compared with the original overall estimate of SMD = − 0.71 (95% CI − 0.92 to − 0.51). In all iterations, the direction and statistical significance of the effect remained unchanged.

These findings suggest that no single study had a dominant influence on the overall result and confirm the robustness and stability of the meta-analytic findings regarding the effect of SAQ training on CODS performance.

Sensitivity analysis (Sprint)

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was also performed for the sprint performance outcome to examine the influence of individual studies on the pooled effect estimate (Fig. 5).

Sensitivity Analysis for Sprint.

After sequentially removing each study, the pooled standardized mean difference ranged from − 0.81 to − 1.00, while the original pooled effect was SMD = − 0.90 (95% CI − 1.18 to − 0.62). The magnitude and direction of the pooled effect remained consistent across all iterations.

This result indicates that the overall effect of SAQ training on sprint performance was not driven by any single study, further supporting the reliability and robustness of the pooled estimate.

Sensitivity analyses excluding studies with high risk of bias yielded similar results, suggesting that the overall findings were robust.

Effects of SAQ training on CODS

Overall meta-analysis

A total of 17 randomized controlled trials examining pre-planned change-of-direction speed (CODS) were included in the meta-analysis. The pooled results obtained from the random-effects model indicated that SAQ training significantly improved CODS performance compared with the control condition (SMD = − 0.71, 95% CI − 0.92 to − 0.51; Fig. 6). The negative standardized mean difference reflects faster completion times following SAQ training.

Forest plot showing the overall effect of SAQ training on change-of-direction speed (CODS). Legend Random-effects meta-analysis comparing SAQ training with control conditions. Squares represent individual study effect sizes weighted by inverse variance, and horizontal lines denote 95% confidence intervals. The diamond indicates the pooled standardized mean difference. Negative values favor SAQ training (faster performance).

Subgroup analysis by competitive level

To explore whether training status influenced the effectiveness of SAQ training, studies were grouped according to athletes’ competitive level (elite/club vs. school/university).

The pooled analysis showed that SAQ training improved CODS performance in both groups. The effect appeared larger among elite or club-level athletes (SMD = − 1.12, 95% CI − 1.75 to − 0.49; I2 = 79.4%) compared with school/university-level athletes (SMD = − 0.64, 95% CI − 0.89 to − 0.38; I2 = 10.7%).

However, the test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (QM = 1.50, p = 0.220), indicating that the observed difference between competitive levels should be interpreted with caution (Fig. 7).

Subgroup analysis of CODS performance according to competitive level. Legend Forest plot comparing the effects of SAQ training between elite/club athletes and school/university athletes. Diamonds represent pooled subgroup effects. The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant.

Subgroup analysis by weekly training volume

A subgroup analysis was also conducted based on weekly training volume, using a threshold of 120 min per week.

Studies with training volumes of ≤ 120 min per week showed a pooled effect of − 0.69 (95% CI − 1.05 to − 0.32; I2 = 45.8%). In studies with volumes greater than 120 min per week, the pooled effect was − 0.74 (95% CI − 1.03 to − 0.46; I2 = 0.0%).

Despite slightly larger effects in the higher-volume group, the difference between subgroups was not statistically significant (QM = 0.16, p = 0.693). These findings suggest that improvements in CODS performance following SAQ training may occur across a range of weekly training volumes (Fig. 8).

Subgroup analysis of CODS performance according to weekly training volume. Legend Studies were categorized into ≤ 120 min/week and > 120 min/week training volumes. Diamonds represent pooled effects for each subgroup.

Subgroup analysis by sport type

To examine whether sport discipline influenced the training response, studies were categorized according to sport type (basketball, soccer, and handball).

The pooled effect size was − 0.58 (95% CI − 0.92 to − 0.24; I2 = 0.0%) for basketball players and − 0.72 (95% CI − 1.01 to − 0.43; I2 = 43.6%) for soccer players. In handball athletes, the estimated effect was larger (SMD = − 1.74, 95% CI − 3.53 to 0.05; I2 = 80.6%), although the wide confidence interval reflected the small number of available studies.

The overall test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (QM = 4.28, p = 0.118), suggesting that the effect of SAQ training on CODS performance was broadly consistent across sports (Fig. 9).

Subgroup analysis of CODS performance according to sport discipline. Legend Forest plot presenting subgroup effects for basketball, soccer, and handball athletes. Diamonds represent pooled subgroup effects.

Meta-regression analysis

A meta-regression analysis was performed to investigate whether participant age influenced the magnitude of the SAQ training effect on CODS performance.

The analysis did not reveal a significant association between mean age and the standardized mean difference (β = − 0.017, p = 0.620; QM = 0.25, p = 0.620). The regression line indicated a slight negative trend, but the relationship was not statistically significant.

These findings suggest that the effectiveness of SAQ training on CODS performance appears relatively consistent across the age range represented in the included studies (Fig. 10).

Weekly training volume meta-regression

To further explore whether the magnitude of training exposure influenced the effectiveness of SAQ training, a meta-regression analysis was conducted using weekly training volume as a continuous moderator.

The analysis did not reveal a significant relationship between weekly training volume and the effect of SAQ training on CODS performance (β = − 0.002, p = 0.195; QM = 1.68, p = 0.195; Fig. 11).

Although the regression line suggested a slight tendency for larger improvements with higher weekly training volumes, the association was not statistically significant. These findings indicate that the beneficial effects of SAQ training on CODS performance were observed across a broad range of weekly training exposures.

Baseline CODS performance meta-regression

A second meta-regression analysis was conducted to examine whether baseline CODS performance influenced the training effect.

The results showed no significant association between baseline CODS performance and the standardized mean difference (β = − 0.010, p = 0.622; QM = 0.24, p = 0.622; Fig. 12).

Although the regression slope indicated a slight negative trend, the relationship was weak and statistically non-significant. This finding suggests that the effectiveness of SAQ training on CODS performance was relatively consistent regardless of athletes’ initial performance levels.

Effects of SAQ training on sprint performance

Overall effect of SAQ training on sprint performance

The pooled analysis demonstrated that SAQ training produced a significant improvement in sprint performance compared with the control condition.

Across the included studies, the overall standardized mean difference (SMD) was − 0.90 (95% CI − 1.18 to − 0.62) under the random-effects model, indicating a large beneficial effect of SAQ training on sprint performance.

Heterogeneity among the studies was low to moderate (I2 = 31%, τ2 = 0.0662, p = 0.17), suggesting that the observed effects were relatively consistent across studies (Fig. 13).

Subgroup analysis based on weekly training volume

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis was conducted based on weekly training volume, categorized as ≤ 120 min per week and > 120 min per week.

For studies with a weekly training volume of ≤ 120 min/week (k = 4), SAQ training showed a significant improvement in sprint performance with a pooled effect size of − 0.70 (95% CI − 1.03 to − 0.37). The heterogeneity within this subgroup was low (I2 = 10.0%).

For studies with a weekly training volume of > 120 min/week (k = 3), the pooled effect size was − 1.27 (95% CI − 1.67 to − 0.87), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%).

The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (QM = 5.69, p = 0.017), indicating that weekly training volume may influence the magnitude of sprint performance improvements. Specifically, higher weekly training volumes appeared to be associated with larger training effects.

Meta-regression analysis based on age

A meta-regression analysis was performed to assess whether participant age moderated the effects of SAQ training on sprint performance.

The results indicated no significant association between participant age and the standardized mean difference (β = − 0.031, p = 0.518). The moderator test was also non-significant (QM = 0.42, p = 0.518) (Fig. 14).

Although the regression slope showed a slight negative trend, suggesting that older participants might experience marginally greater improvements, the relationship was weak and statistically non-significant.

These findings indicate that the effectiveness of SAQ training on sprint performance appears to be relatively consistent across different age groups (Fig. 15).

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test for both CODS and sprint outcomes.

Visual inspection of the funnel plots suggested a degree of asymmetry for both outcomes (Fig. 16). Egger’s regression test further confirmed the presence of potential publication bias, with significant results observed for CODS (p = 0.010) and sprint performance (p = 0.007).

To further evaluate the potential influence of publication bias on the pooled estimates, the trim-and-fill method was applied (Fig. 17).

The trim-and-fill analysis indicated that three studies were imputed for CODS, resulting in an adjusted pooled effect size of − 0.61 compared with the original estimate of − 0.71.

For sprint performance, five studies were imputed, and the adjusted pooled effect size was − 0.64, compared with the original estimate of − 0.90.

Despite these adjustments, the overall effects remained statistically significant, suggesting that the beneficial effects of SAQ training on CODS and sprint performance were robust even after accounting for potential publication bias.

Discussion

This systematic review included 22 eligible studies contributing 26 effect sizes, of which 17 were synthesized for pre-planned change-of-direction speed (CODS) and 9 for linear sprint performance. The pooled results showed that speed–agility–quickness (SAQ) training significantly improved CODS performance compared with control conditions (SMD = − 0.71, 95% CI − 0.92 to − 0.51). In addition, SAQ training also produced a significant improvement in linear sprint performance (SMD = − 0.90, 95% CI − 1.18 to − 0.62). Taken together, these findings indicate that SAQ training is an effective approach for improving both pre-planned multidirectional performance and straight-line speed in adolescent and young adult team-sport athletes. However, the magnitude of these effects should be interpreted in light of the variability in intervention protocols, outcome assessments, and study quality.

The observed beneficial effect on pre-planned CODS is broadly consistent with previous meta-analytic evidence showing that SAQ- or agility-related training can improve directional-change performance in youth athletes and team-sport populations40. In the present review, the pooled CODS effect was of moderate-to-large magnitude and was directionally similar to values reported in previous meta-analyses12,15,41,42. These convergent findings support the practical relevance of SAQ training for sports such as basketball, soccer, and handball, in which repeated braking, cutting, and re-acceleration actions are common.

At the same time, the current estimate should not be interpreted as evidence that all CODS outcomes are fully interchangeable. As shown in Table 1, the included studies used different tests, including the Illinois agility test, T-test, shuttle-run variants, and several study-specific pre-planned directional-change protocols43. These tests differ in movement pattern, turning angle, and braking demand, and such methodological differences may partly influence the magnitude of pooled effects44,45. This point is important because some of the heterogeneity observed across studies may reflect differences in test construction rather than differences in the underlying effectiveness of SAQ training alone46.

Several mechanisms may explain why SAQ training improves pre-planned CODS. First, repeated cutting and deceleration–acceleration drills are likely to enhance eccentric braking capacity and the rapid transition from eccentric to concentric force production, both of which are central to effective directional-change performance7,47. Second, SAQ drills may improve intermuscular and intramuscular coordination, thereby facilitating more efficient sequencing of lower-limb actions during braking and re-acceleration48,49. Third, repeated rehearsal of pre-planned movement patterns may refine technique-related elements such as body lean, foot placement, braking distance, and repositioning strategy40,45. These mechanisms are particularly relevant to pre-planned CODS, in which the athlete knows the movement pattern in advance and performance depends primarily on physical and technical execution.

Accordingly, pre-planned CODS should be distinguished from reactive agility, which additionally requires stimulus perception, response selection, and rapid decision-making under time pressure40,45. This conceptual distinction is important because some studies in the broader literature have used these terms interchangeably, whereas they represent related but distinct performance qualities. The present review therefore focused specifically on pre-planned CODS and should not be interpreted as directly addressing perceptual-cognitive agility performance.

SAQ training also produced a significant beneficial effect on sprint performance (SMD = − 0.90), suggesting that the training stimulus was not limited to multidirectional actions alone. This is plausible because many SAQ programs include short accelerations, repeated starts, footwork drills, and multidirectional sprint patterns that may transfer to early acceleration and short-distance sprint performance1,45. In the present review, the sprint effect was numerically larger than the CODS effect, which is broadly in line with previous reports50,51. However, this should not be overinterpreted as evidence of formal superiority of one outcome over the other, because CODS and sprint were synthesized as separate outcomes and were not directly compared within a unified subgroup model. A more cautious interpretation is that SAQ training appears capable of benefiting both qualities, with meaningful transfer to linear sprint performance when sprint-related elements are included in the training process41.

The subgroup and meta-regression findings suggest that the beneficial effects of SAQ training on CODS were relatively stable across the age range represented in the included studies. Neither the age subgroup comparison nor the age meta-regression was statistically significant for CODS, and sprint age meta-regression was likewise non-significant. This pattern is broadly consistent with previous reviews indicating that SAQ- or neuromuscular-oriented training can be beneficial across youth developmental stages51,52. Although earlier work suggested larger effects in children younger than 12 years53, later evidence has emphasized adolescence as an important period for trainability of neuromuscular performance54,55. The present findings support a cautious interpretation that SAQ training can be effective from late childhood to early adulthood, although the current analyses do not exclude the possibility that maturation, training history, or baseline capacity may influence the magnitude of adaptation in specific contexts.

With respect to training dose, the CODS subgroup analysis showed similar pooled effects in studies using ≤ 120 min/week and > 120 min/week, with no significant subgroup difference. Likewise, the weekly-volume meta-regression for CODS did not reveal a statistically significant association, although the curve suggested a slight tendency toward larger effects at higher exposure levels. These findings indicate that improvements in pre-planned CODS may be achieved across a relatively broad range of weekly SAQ exposure, although training-load management in adolescent athletes requires careful consideration of developmental status, cumulative stress, and recovery capacity56. In contrast, the sprint subgroup analysis showed a statistically significant difference according to weekly training volume, with larger effects in studies exceeding 120 min/week. This pattern suggests that sprint outcomes may be more sensitive than CODS outcomes to total weekly exposure, although this interpretation should remain cautious because the sprint subgroup analysis was based on a relatively small number of effect sizes. Previous discussions of training dose in youth athletic development have also suggested that dose–response relationships may be non-linear rather than strictly linear and that load prescription in young athletes should account for maturation, accumulated fatigue, and recovery demands57–59. Therefore, the current evidence supports the practical usefulness of SAQ training at different weekly volumes, but does not yet define a precise optimal dose.

Competitive level and sport discipline also did not significantly moderate the CODS effect. Although the point estimate was numerically larger in elite/club athletes than in school/university-level athletes, the subgroup difference was not statistically significant and heterogeneity was substantial in the elite/club subgroup60. This finding is broadly consistent with previous meta-analyses suggesting that agility- or SAQ-related training can be effective across different competitive standards12,50,52. Similarly, CODS improvements were observed across basketball, soccer, and handball, but the subgroup difference by sport type was not statistically significant. The point estimate for handball was large, yet the confidence interval was wide, reflecting the limited number of available effect sizes. These findings suggest that SAQ training may have broad applicability across different youth team-sport contexts, while also indicating that subgroup-specific estimates should be interpreted cautiously when based on relatively few studies. This cautious interpretation is further supported by evidence showing that physical performance profiles in youth athletes vary according to competitive standard, maturation, and sport-specific demands61.

An important issue in the present review is publication bias. Visual inspection of the funnel plots suggested asymmetry, and Egger’s regression test was significant for both CODS and sprint outcomes. After trim-and-fill adjustment, the pooled effects were attenuated but remained statistically significant62, indicating that the beneficial effects of SAQ training were not fully eliminated after accounting for potentially missing studies. Nevertheless, the original pooled estimates may have been somewhat inflated. Therefore, the present findings support the effectiveness of SAQ training, but the magnitude of benefit should be interpreted with appropriate caution rather than as a fixed or definitive estimate.

The methodological quality of the included studies should also be considered when interpreting the results. Most studies were judged as having some concerns in the RoB 2 assessment, and a smaller proportion were judged as high risk of bias, particularly in relation to the randomization process63. In contrast, missing outcome data and outcome measurement were generally less problematic. Sensitivity analyses excluding studies with high risk of bias yielded similar results, which supports the stability of the pooled estimates. However, the overall evidence base still includes modest sample sizes, incomplete methodological reporting in several trials, and substantial variability in training design.

Several limitations of this review should be acknowledged. First, although the review was restricted to SAQ-dominant interventions, the specific content, progression, and comparator conditions varied substantially across studies. Second, CODS was assessed using multiple field-based protocols that were treated as reflecting the same broad construct of pre-planned directional-change performance, even though they are not mechanically identical44. Third, the age range of 9–26 years spans markedly different developmental stages, and maturational status was rarely reported in sufficient detail for more refined analysis. Fourth, the sprint synthesis was based on only 9 effect sizes, which limits the precision of moderator findings. Finally, the presence of potential publication bias indicates that the pooled estimates may overstate the true effect size to some extent.

Overall, the present findings suggest that SAQ training appears to be an effective strategy for improving pre-planned CODS and linear sprint performance in adolescent and young adult team-sport athletes. The available evidence indicates that these benefits are observable across different sports and training backgrounds, but the exact magnitude of effect remains influenced by methodological variability, outcome selection, and possible publication bias. Future research should prioritize better-standardized outcome assessment, clearer reporting of intervention content and training load, and larger randomized trials capable of examining developmental stage, sport-specific demands, and dose–response relationships with greater precision.

Conclusion

This systematic review included 22 eligible studies contributing 26 effect sizes, of which 17 were synthesized for pre-planned change-of-direction speed (CODS) and 9 for linear sprint performance. The pooled evidence indicates that speed–agility–quickness (SAQ) training significantly improves pre-planned CODS in adolescent and young adult team ball sport athletes compared with control conditions (SMD = − 0.71, 95% CI − 0.92 to − 0.51). SAQ training also produced a significant beneficial effect on linear sprint performance (SMD = − 0.90, 95% CI − 1.18 to − 0.62).

For CODS, subgroup analyses did not show statistically significant moderation by age, weekly training volume, competitive level, or sport discipline, suggesting that the observed benefits may be applicable across a range of youth team-sport settings. Meta-regression did not identify statistically significant non-linear associations for the examined moderators. Sensitivity analyses supported the stability of the pooled estimates, although potential publication bias and methodological variability should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the effects.

Overall, SAQ training appears to be an effective strategy for improving pre-planned CODS and linear sprint performance in youth team-sport athletes. These findings support the inclusion of SAQ training within routine physical conditioning programs, while acknowledging variability in intervention design, outcome assessment, and study quality. Future research should clarify the optimal weekly dose, long-term retention of training effects, and the extent to which gains in pre-planned CODS transfer to broader agility-related performance.
