Psychological and behavioral mechanisms that drive medical staff’s engagement in kidney palliative care—an empirical analysis from medical staff in mainland China
Authors: Wenbo Zhu, Meijie Wu, Shuo Zhang, Ru Li, Tiejun Liu, Yue Zhou, Yuxia Guan, Jinghua Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe high medical costs of continuous treatment for end-stage renal disease impose significant financial strain on patients and their families, highlighting the need for palliative care. However, the development of renal PC in mainland China remains hindered by substantial challenges.ObjectiveTo explore the psychological and behavioral mechanisms that drive medical staff’s engagement in kidney palliative care in mainland China, and to provide empirical evidence to inform future renal palliative care interventions.MethodsBased on the KAB model and self-efficacy, 719 valid questionnaires were collected from medical staff (doctors and nurses) specializing in renal care in mainland China. Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 for descriptive statistics, Amos 26.0 for model validation, and Process 3.3.1 for mediated effects analysis.ResultsMedical staff’s knowledge was significantly positively associated with attitudes toward palliative care, while self-efficacy was positively associated with both attitudes and behaviors to palliative care. Hypotheses H2, H3, H4, and H5 were supported. Additionally, attitudes mediated the relationship between self-efficacy and palliative care practices.ConclusionPalliative care knowledge, attitude, and self-efficacy of renal medical staff are associated with practice in mainland China. Therefore, when training medical staff, medical institutions should not only focus on the improvement of skills and knowledge, but also emphasize the shaping of emotions and attitudes, in order to achieve better practice results and promote the development of kidney palliative care in China.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14213-x.Keywords: Palliative care, End-stage kidney disease, Chronic kidney disease, Self -effciency, KAB model

Introduction

The incidence and prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) are steadily increasing, posing significant challenges to health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and life expectancy. This growing burden affects not only individual patients but also imposes substantial economic and societal costs, positioning CKD as a critical global public health challenge [1]. Notably, its impact is disproportionately felt in low-income countries (LICs) and lower middle-income countries (LMICs), where limited healthcare resources and infrastructure exacerbate the consequences of the disease [2–4]. An estimated 850 million people worldwide are affected by kidney disease, making it the 12th leading cause of death and accounting for approximately 41.5 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). The incidence and prevalence of CKD have increased by approximately 40% over the past 30 years [5]. In China, according to the latest nationally representative survey (2018–2019), the prevalence of CKD among adults was estimated at 8.2%, affecting approximately 82 million people [6]. Meanwhile, Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study estimates indicate that 196,726 deaths in China were attributable to CKD in 2019. Over the past three decades, alongside accelerated population aging, China’s crude CKD prevalence has increased at an average annual rate of 1.6%, while the crude mortality rate has risen by 1.8% per year, highlighting a persistently escalating disease burden [7]. With the progression of CKD, patients may advance to End-Stage Kidney Disease (ESKD), at which point renal replacement therapies—primarily peritoneal dialysis (PD) and hemodialysis (HD)—constitute the main treatment modalities. By the end of 2023, there were more than 1.06 million registered maintenance dialysis patients in China [8], of which the number of maintenance hemodialysis patients (MHD) was much higher than that of PD patients (916,000 vs. 185,000). The urgency of ongoing treatment for ESKD leads to high healthcare costs, placing considerable financial strain on patients and their family support structures [9].

The World Health Organization(WHO) defines palliative care (PC) as a comprehensive approach that aims to enhance the quality of life of individuals facing life-threatening illnesses and their families. The approach emphasizes the prevention and alleviation of suffering through early identification, accurate assessment, and active treatment of pain and other physical symptoms, while also addressing psychosocial and spiritual distress [10]. As PC continues to evolve, its operational model has increasingly emphasized early integration and demand-driven implementation. Notably, its scope has expanded beyond patients with limited life expectancy to encompass individuals affected by a broad spectrum of chronic diseases, reflecting a shift in focus toward addressing diverse and long-term healthcare needs [11]. Although advances in MHD therapy have substantially prolonged survival among patients with ESKD [12], the high incidence and progression of complications are associated with an annual mortality rate of approximately 23% among ESKD patients receiving MHD therapy [13]. Moreover, lifelong treatment dependence, cumulative psychological distress, and escalating family medical expenditures collectively create a multidimensional burden encompassing physical, psychological, and socioeconomic domains. This burden affects not only patients but also their families, further complicating the management of chronic kidney disease [14–16]. Therefore, consideration of PC is critical for patients receiving MHD [17, 18]. One study confirmed that nearly half of ESKD patients undergoing MHD in China have moderate to severe PC needs [19]. In response, the WHO and multiple scholars have called for the provision of PC regardless of prognosis, emphasizing its role in reducing suffering and addressing the complex needs of patients across all stages of CKD [20, 21].

Despite ongoing efforts of the Chinese government and academics to promote PC, public discussion of death remains culturally sensitive within the unique context of mainland China. Public education on death and dying is limited, further hindering broader awareness and acceptance of end-of-life care [22]. Limited educational and training resources, the absence of a comprehensive legal framework, insufficient funding and investment, inadequate access to essential medications, and pronounced regional economic disparities collectively hinder the development of PC in mainland China [23]. In addition, the misconception that PC is equivalent to hospice care and and to giving up on life persists among the public and even among medical professionals in the Asian region [24]. In recent years, interest in renal PC has increased as more healthcare professionals recognize its importance for patients with CKD. Although nephrology practitioners report positive attitudes toward supporting PC, they also express a lack of confidence in and training for providing this type of care to patients undergoing hemodialysis [25]. Moreover, PC is often not considered a primary goal of renal therapy because most treatment modalities emphasize dialysis preparation. Consequently, referral rates to PC services for patients with ESKD remain low. Even among those who receive PC services, 32% to 84% of MHD patients report only partial remission or no remission at all; among these, the proportion of patients who have received inpatient PC services and home PC services is only 8% and 5%, respectively [26, 27].

As the primary drivers in disseminating the PC concept, the clinical practices of medical staff play a decisive role in advancing the concept [28, 29], and it is therefore crucial to understand the underlying mechanisms shaping their engagement in PC provision for patients undergoing MHD. Accordingly, this study conducted a cross-sectional online survey between October 1 and December 15, 2023, to explore the psychological and behavioral mechanisms that drive renal medical staff’s engagement in palliative care for MHD patients. The findings may help clarify how knowledge, attitudes, and related behavioral factors influence PC provision and may inform the design of targeted educational and organizational strategies. These insights may also support the development and professionalization of renal palliative care services in mainland China.

Methods

Measurements

The questionnaire used in this study was jointly developed by nephrologists, PC specialists, and clinical nurses to ensure alignment with the clinical context and cognitive characteristics of nephrology healthcare professionals. The expert panel first established the overall framework through a comprehensive literature review, defining the questionnaire structure and section domains. Specific items were then formulated according to the research objectives and the predefined framework. Some items were adapted from existing literature and modified appropriately to fit the renal palliative care context in China, with detailed information on item sources, contextual modifications, and pilot testing outcomes provided in Appendix 1.

In the initial draft, a duplicated core item (“I consider myself able to remain calm and provide effective palliative care when dealing with emotionally complex end-stage patients”) was intentionally included across two sections to assess item stability during pilot testing. Before formal administration, a pilot test was conducted among 14 nephrologists and 10 nephrology nurses to assess content validity, linguistic clarity, and technical feasibility. Pilot feedback indicated no profession-specific differences in item comprehension or response patterns, supporting cross-professional applicability. In addition, the pilot test yielded preliminary estimates of internal consistency, with acceptable Cronbach’s α values for each dimension (knowledge = 0.78, attitude = 0.82, self-efficacy = 0.88, and behavior = 0.85), providing initial support for the reliability of the questionnaire. Based on this feedback and considerations of survey efficiency, the redundant item was removed in the final version, and no further modifications to item wording or structure were required. Accordingly, all data used in the formal analyses were derived from the finalized version of the questionnaire.

The questionnaire was divided into two parts. The first part collects respondents’ basic demographic and professional information. The second part consisted of four dimensions of the study hypothesis and 13 items including self-efficacy, knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors toward renal PC. All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly Disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly Agree”). Items with negative phrasing (e.g., “I don’t know what to say …” in Item 12) were reverse-scored (see Appendix 1) to ensure that higher scores consistently represent greater knowledge, more positive attitudes, stronger self-efficacy, or more frequent practice behaviors. Composite scores for each of the four latent constructs were calculated by averaging the scores of their respective items.

Participants and data collection

The formal survey was conducted by distributing the questionnaire as a web link at a national nephrology conference and a convenience sample of healthcare professionals (doctors and nurses) involved in the care of kidney diseases in mainland China. Inclusion criteria were (i) nephrology medical staff with experience in caring for MHD patients and (ii) voluntary consent to participate in the study. The questionnaires were required to be filled out completely and invalid questionnaires were excluded. Therefore, a total of 719 valid questionnaires were collected, with a validity rate of 89.8%, which meets the minimum sample size requirement based on the “10-fold rule,” a guideline recommending that the sample size should be at least ten times the maximum number of structural paths directed toward any latent variable in the model [30]. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (No. K2326), and all participants signed an informed consent form.

Model building

The KAB (Knowledge-Attitude-Behavior) model is an important theoretical model of health education that explains the part of knowledge in behavior change and emphasizes that behavior change is a product of knowledge and attitudes [31, 32]. According to previous studies, lack of knowledge is a significant barrier factor for healthcare professionals to provide PC [33, 34]. Second, medical staff’s understanding of death and their attitudes toward treating patients who have suffered a major shock or are dying can also affect the quality of PC [35–38]. In addition, self-efficacy has been identified as a key factor in the ability to perform PC effectively [39, 40], it refers to an individual’s confidence in his or her ability to perform a specific behavior or skill [41]. One study reports that medical professionals’ PC knowledge is one of the strongest factors in their self-efficacy [42]，self-efficacious health professionals are confident in their ability to face challenges and overcome difficulties, and this confidence helps them to demonstrate a higher level of positive attitudes towards providing support and care, which in turn helps patients and families to accept and understand treatment options [43].

In this study, knowledge was defined as the foundational understanding required to deliver quality PC services to patients across all settings. Attitude was defined as the feelings, perspectives, and level of comfort associated with administering PC to patients and their caregivers. Self-efficacy was defined as medical staff’s perceived capability to effectively provide PC services. In order to understand the influencing mechanisms affecting Chinese medical staff’s provision of PC to MHD patients and to develop improvement strategies for the various influencing factors, this study introduces a novel investigative methodology by integrating the KAB model with relevant literature, thereby developing a more comprehensive and holistic framework, as shown in Fig. 1. And based on the above theory, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

KAB expansion model

Renal medical staff’s knowledge is positively associated with PC self-efficacy.

Renal medical staff’s knowledge is positively associated with PC attitude.

Renal medical staff’s self-efficacy is positively associated with PC attitude.

Renal medical staff’s attitude is positively associated with PC behavior.

Renal medical staff’s self-efficacy is positively associated with PC behavior.

Renal medical staff’s PC attitude serves as a mediator in the relationship between self-efficacy and behavior.

Statistical analysis

In this study, data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software (No missing data was found). Descriptive statistics were calculated for continuous variables and are presented as means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Regression analyses were conducted to examine associations between key variables.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using AMOS version 26.0 to examine the relationships among knowledge, attitudes, self-efficacy, and palliative care (PC) behaviors among renal healthcare professionals. PC provision behaviors were specified as the outcome variable, while knowledge, attitudes, and self-efficacy were modeled as exogenous and mediating constructs as appropriate. Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to assess the reliability and validity of the measurement model prior to structural analysis.

To evaluate the significance of mediation effects, bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals were estimated using 5000 bootstrap resamples. Mediation effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% confidence interval did not include zero. Model fit was assessed using multiple goodness-of-fit indices, including the relative chi-square (χ2/df), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), incremental fit index (IFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and comparative fit index (CFI). After confirming acceptable model fit, hypotheses were tested based on standardized path coefficients and corresponding p-values. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Description of the sample characteristic distribution

In this study, data from 719 validated questionnaires were processed using SPSS 26.0 for statistical description and analysis. The findings revealed that the majority of participants were female (80.5%, n = 579), with a significant proportion aged 30–40 years (44.1%, n = 317). Most participants held a bachelor’s degree (64%), and a considerable number had 11–20 years of professional experience (39.4%, n = 283). Clinically, 95.8% of participants (n = 689) had experience managing advanced dialysis patients. Approximately 43.3% (n = 311) reported handling an average of 1–3 deaths annually, while 29.9% (n = 215) had encountered patient dissatisfaction or disputes related to dialysis withdrawal. Regarding social support, 34.9% of participants indicated a lack of accessible PC team services in their hospital or region. Notably, 95.1% of participants expressed strong support for incorporating PC into routine staff training programs. Detailed participant characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Discussion

Renal medical staff require enhanced PC system training

The findings indicate that higher levels of palliative care (PC) knowledge among renal medical staff are associated with more positive attitudes toward PC, and professionals who have received direct PC education are more likely to demonstrate favorable attitudes. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that structured PC education is associated with more favorable perceptions and acceptance of PC principles and practices among healthcare professionals. These results underscore the close association between PC-related knowledge and attitudes toward PC provision.

However, the education and training of most healthcare professionals in China primarily focus on acute medical care and pathophysiology, with limited systematic training in PC. For example, a systematic review indicates that PC education in China generally lacks formal curricula and practical training opportunities at both undergraduate and graduate levels, resulting in insufficient development of relevant knowledge and skills [44]. Furthermore, in many training programs, PC is often not incorporated as a standalone or standardized component into medical student and resident training programs [45]. As a result, many healthcare workers have limited experience in managing PC-related challenges in practice, particularly in effective communication with patients and families, symptom assessment, and comprehensive symptom management, including pain control [20]. Although some hospitals and organizations have begun to introduce relevant courses, the overall coverage remains limited. This gap is especially pronounced in primary care hospitals, where shortages of professional resources and structured guidance hinder healthcare workers’ ability to acquire and apply PC knowledge and skills in routine practice [46].

In addition, in the Chinese cultural context, which traditionally favors active treatment, PC is often misinterpreted by some clinicians as end-of-life care or abandonment of treatment [47, 48], which may be associated with a lower willingness to actively promote relevant PC measures in practice [49]. Similar misunderstandings have been reported among healthcare professionals, patients, and caregivers across different disease contexts, and are particularly evident among nephrology trainees, who have consistently reported deficiencies in PC education [50, 51]. Notably, even among medical staff who express positive attitudes toward PC, substantial educational needs remain, especially in areas related to resource coordination, interdisciplinary collaboration, and the practical implementation of PC services [52]. Patients with ESKD often bear a heavier symptom burden compared to those with other chronic serious illnesses and frequently undergo intensive care and acute therapeutic measures at the end of life. Currently, there is a significant lack of accessible renal PC, regardless of whether it is provided by trained nephrology professionals or PC specialists.

Study findings suggest that PC training may be particularly important for Chinese medical staff dealing with kidney disease, especially those working with hemodialysis patients. Through systematic training and policy support, such efforts may contribute to improvements in PC quality and support the broader integration and sustainable development of PC practices within renal and hemodialysis care settings.

Increase PC value perceptions and utilize attitude-driven practices

Using the KAB model, we found that the provision of PC is strongly associated with the attitudes of renal medical staff [35], underscoring the importance of attitudinal factors in the translation of knowledge into clinical practice. Previous research has shown that even when healthcare professionals receive PC-related training, negative or ambivalent attitudes may weaken their willingness to apply acquired knowledge [53]. In such cases, clinicians may underestimate patients’ psychosocial needs, which may be associated with delayed PC initiation and reinforce a persistent “knowledge–practice disconnect.” When healthcare professionals do not recognize the value of PC, they may avoid discussions related to symptom management, advance care planning, and end-of-life preferences, limiting patients’ access to timely PC services. For instance, it has been reported that only 13% of CKD patients were actively informed of PC options [53], which has been linked to healthcare professionals’ concerns about “triggering patient anxiety.” In contrast, healthcare professionals who hold more favorable attitudes toward PC tend to be more inclined to integrate available PC resources, facilitate interdisciplinary collaboration, and support gradual shifts away from traditional, treatment-centered decision-making models within their departments [50]. In China, cultural perspectives on life and death may make accepting PC more challenging for patients compared to Western cultures. However, this study suggests that Chinese renal healthcare professionals generally report positive attitudes toward PC, aligning with prior research [54]. This positive outlook provides a foundation for optimism regarding the future development and implementation of PC in China.

Importantly, the findings highlight a context-specific mechanism in which positive professional attitudes may help attenuate the constraining influence of traditional cultural norms on PC practice. This suggests that optimizing PC implementation in developing countries may benefit from fostering supportive attitudinal environments, rather than relying solely on knowledge-based training. Therefore, training programs for renal healthcare professionals should not only focus on PC knowledge and skills but also enhance their recognition of PC’s value. For example, initiating open discussions about ethics could help establish a localized framework for understanding PC, creating new opportunities for its development in Chinese nephrology care.

Self-efficacy significantly positively predicts PC attitudes and behaviors in medical personnel

We also explored the relationship between renal medical staff’s PC self-efficacy and knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. The results revealed a significant positive association between self-efficacy and both PC attitudes and behaviors, aligning with prior research findings [55, 56]. Specifically, PC self-efficacy reflects medical staff’s perceived ability to perform palliative care-related tasks [42]. This perceived competence may be associated with lower anxiety regarding concerns about “triggering patient distress” and with more positive attitudes toward PC [57, 58]. Healthcare professionals with high self-efficacy tend to believe they can alleviate patients’ suffering more effectively, which may be linked to higher work engagement, better coping with complex cases, lower anxiety related to cultural or ethical concerns, and greater patient receptiveness to PC, and may be associated with earlier consideration of PC involvement [59]. In addition, self-efficacy is closely related to professional practice [39], including a greater willingness to engage in training and skill development to improve PC services [60].

Although most of the previous studies have reported an association between self-efficacy and practical knowledge [61, 62], the effect of PC knowledge on self-efficacy was not statistically significant in the present study. These findings suggest that knowledge acquisition alone may not be sufficient to be associated with higher self-efficacy in PC delivery. In healthcare systems that prioritize curative care, medical staff may acquire PC knowledge but have limited opportunities to apply it due to institutional constraints, which may limit the translation of knowledge into perceived self-efficacy.

This finding holds significant practical implications: Future PC training may benefit from not relying solely on knowledge transfer, but from placing greater emphasis on experiential and confidence-building approaches. Methods such as simulation-based training, case-based learning, reflective practice, and supervised clinical experiences can provide medical staff with opportunities for mastery and constructive feedback. These training approaches may be more conducive to higher self-efficacy and to the application of PC principles in clinical settings. To address this, it may be beneficial for healthcare organizations to consider establishing mentorship programs with experienced staff to guide newcomers in PC practice and organize regular PC training, such as workshops and seminars, to enhance the professional skills and self-efficacy of healthcare professionals [63–65]. This study also highlights the importance of supporting the mental well-being of healthcare professionals. A supportive work environment may help alleviate anxiety related to cultural differences, ethical dilemmas, and patient-care demands, thereby fostering self-efficacy. Providing psychological support resources and fostering an empathetic culture may play an important role in reducing stress and supporting healthcare workers’ well-being.

Positive associations of PC attitudes as a mediator between self-efficacy and behavior in healthcare workers

The present study identified attitudes as a statistically significant mediator in the association between medical staff’s PC self-efficacy and PC provision. Specifically, medical staff with higher self-efficacy tended to develop more positive PC attitudes, which were associated with higher levels of PC-related practice behaviors, consistent with previous studies [66]. Positive PC attitudes appear to be associated with a stronger link between self-efficacy and practical clinical actions. Conversely, negative attitudes toward PC may be associated with a weaker expression of self-efficacy in practice, even among medical staff with high confidence, due to apprehension, anxiety, or resistance.

In a cross-sectional correlational study, Kim et al. reported that nurses’ attitudes moderated the relationship between knowledge and self-efficacy [67], such that higher knowledge was associated with greater self-efficacy, particularly among nurses with moderately to highly positive attitudes. These findings suggest that increasing knowledge alone may not be sufficient to be associated with higher self-efficacy. The present study highlights the importance of fostering positive attitudes toward PC among medical staff. Training programs within medical institutions may therefore benefit from extending beyond skill and knowledge enhancement to include the development of emotional and attitudinal competencies. Accordingly, institutions may consider implementing incentive-based approaches that recognize excellence in PC practice, such as performance-based incentives or honorary recognition. These strategies may be associated with higher motivation, job satisfaction, and perceived self-efficacy, and may support a more proactive and compassionate approach to PC, thereby contributing to improvements in the quality of care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the structural equation model was theory-driven; although it empirically supported the applicability of the KAB model among renal healthcare professionals, it could not capture all relevant psychological and behavioral mechanisms underlying PC provision for patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD). Future studies could improve model comprehensiveness by conducting exploratory analyses to identify additional explanatory constructs prior to theory-based model construction. Second, the cross-sectional design limited the ability to examine changes in PC-related behaviors over time. Longitudinal designs with data collected at multiple time points may better capture the dynamic influences on renal healthcare professionals’ PC behaviors. Finally, convenience sampling was used, with participants primarily recruited from medical personnel attending the National Nephrology Conference. This approach may have introduced selection bias, as conference attendees are more likely to have greater professional engagement and more positive attitudes toward palliative care than the general renal healthcare workforce, potentially limiting sample representativeness and the external validity of the findings. Future studies are therefore encouraged to adopt multicenter and more diverse sampling strategies to enhance geographic coverage and demographic diversity.

Conclusion

To develop a context-specific PC program for renal healthcare professionals in China, this study explored the modifiable psychological and behavioral mechanisms underlying medical staff’s engagement in PC using the KAB integration model. This model provides a robust framework for understanding the psychological and behavioral mechanisms that drive medical staff’s engagement in PC. The findings revealed that PC knowledge, attitudes, and self-efficacy play interconnected roles in shaping PC behaviors, with knowledge being associated with more positive attitudes and attitudes serving as a statistically supported mediator in the association between self-efficacy and PC behaviors. These insights offer new directions for future research and provide empirical evidence relevant to PC policy discussions in nephrology. By clarifying the interrelationships among key psychological and behavioral constructs, healthcare policymakers may be better informed when targeting interventions aimed at enhancing PC application in CKD management.
