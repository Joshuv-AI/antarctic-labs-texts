Pediatric NUT carcinoma: a patient series and literature review
Authors: Yalan You, Suying Lu, Feifei Sun, Xiangling He, Juan Wang, Junting Huang, Yi Que, Zijun Zhen, Yizhuo Zhang, Jia Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNuclear protein in testis (NUT) carcinoma (NC) in children is a rare tumor with highly aggressive nature and dismal prognosis.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of case series towards NC-patients aged 0–18 years treated at the Department of Pediatric Oncology, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center between July 2021 and September 2022. The primary end point was to explore the efficacy of therapy. The clinical and pathological characteristics of the patients were also analyzed. The last follow-up date was March 1, 2025.ResultsThe ages of the patients ranged from 8 to 18 years. The tumor sites were head and neck in four patients and vulva in one patient. Three patients underwent upfront surgical resections, followed by chemotherapy(CHT) ± radiotherapy. One patient received neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by delayed resection and one patient with inoperable NC received chemotherapy only. BRD4-NUT fusion was positive in 3 patients. No recurrence occurred in two patients receiving postoperative chemotherapy, whereas the response was poor in two patients who could not be initially operated on. Currently, two patients are alive, while one patient died of postoperative infection.ConclusionsNC is a rare and aggressive disease. Local therapy plays a vital role in disease control. NC seems to be sensitive to alkylating agents, platin compounds, and anthracyclines in combination. Multimodal approach including surgery, radiation, multidrug chemotherapy, and PD1 inhibitors may lead to improved outcomes for patients with NC. Novel therapies such as inhibitors of BET proteins, histone deacetylase, EZH2 warrant further exploration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06278-0.Keywords: NUT carcinoma, Children treatment, BRD4-NUT fusion

Introduction

NC is a rare, poorly differentiated squamous cell epithelial cancer with a highly aggressive nature, arising in the midline of the body. NC has often been misdiagnosed solely on pathological examination. Pathologically, NC is characterized by variable degrees of squamous differentiation with a predominance of the poorly or undifferentiated component. The presence of an intratumoral neutrophilic infiltrate is common and can be very prominent [1]. NC is molecularly characterized by chromosomal rearrangement of the NUT gene to another gene, most commonly the bromodomain and extraterminal (BET) gene BRD4, forming the BRD4-NUT fusion oncogene. Immunohistochemistry staining with the specific anti-NUTM1 antibody is highly diagnostic. NC typically arises from the mediastinum and midline organs and has dismal outcome. Presenting symptoms vary by location such as dyspnea, chest pain, cough, and hemoptysis for pulmonary involvement and epistaxis, headache, and nasal obstruction for the head and/or neck involvement, with short onset times (within 2–4 weeks) in most patients [2]. However, NC lacks specific clinical and histomorphological features. NC may occur at any age but mainly in children and young adults. In addition, males and females are equally affected [3]. The extent of initial resection has essential role in disease control. Neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy may be considered for unresectable or metastatic tumor. The median overall survival was 6.7 months. Children had superior survival compared to adult counterpart. NC of salivary gland had a better prognosis [4, 5]. The prognosis of NC is generally poor despite aggressive multimodal management, which incorporates surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiation [6, 7] Because of its poor response to radiation and standard chemotherapy regimens, and its lack of expression of checkpoint immunotherapy markers, median survival is typically only several months for adults, and slightly longer in children [4, 8]. The European Standard Clinical Practice (ESCP) recommendations for the management of NC have been established in 2022 [9].

Methods

Patients

We searched the database of our hospital from July 2021 to September 2022 for patients of NC. The clinical data of five patients of NC diagnosed at the Department of Pediatric Oncology, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center were retrieved.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic information, the pathological and genetic characteristics. The treatment approaches and the outcomes of five patients diagnosed by pathology -/+ genetic testing were also analyzed. The last follow-up date was March 1, 2025.

Immunophenotypic analysis

Immunohistochemical studies were performed using formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded biopsy or surgery specimen. The antibody used was specific for NUT antibody.

Cytogenetic analysis

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis for the detection of NUT(15q14) breakpoint was performed on interphase nuclei using the dual-color, locus-specific probe according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Treatment

The initial chemotherapy regimen included CAP/IE (cyclophosphamide + pirarubicin + cisplatin/ifosfamide + etoposide) alternately. Surgery and radiotherapy were performed when possible. In refractory and relapsed settings, chemotherapy doses were adjusted in combination with immune and/or targeted therapy, including paclitaxel, carboplatin, fluorouracil, sintilizumab, camrelizumab, chidamide, apatinib, etc.

Results

We present five patients of NC (Table 1), consisting of one male and four females, aged 8 to 18 years, with primary tumors located in the tonsil, pharynx, nasal cavity, parotid gland, and vulva. All patients had lymph node metastasis at diagnosis; among them one had bone metastasis (Patient 5) and one presented with nasal tumor invasion into the ethmoid sinus(Patient 4) (Fig. 1). All patients had positive NUT staining in the IHC, and three of them were further confirmed by genetic investigation (Patient 2,3,5) as illustrated in Fig. 2 exhibiting the NUTM1-BRD4 gene rearrangement.

Patient 1 underwent resection of cervical mass at initial diagnosis. Relapse occurred at the primary site of the tumor one month later. The patient showed a significant reduction in tumor size after chemotherapy comprising cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and cisplatin alternating with etoposide and ifosfamide. The patient received R1 reoperation and radiotherapy, but died of rapidly progressive disease 20 months later. Patient 2 did not progress with postoperative (R1 resection) chemotherapy. Radiotherapy was refused by her guardians. The patient was alive until last follow-up. Patient 3 underwent postoperative chemotherapy and radiotherapy, with the metastatic lymph nodes gradually shrinking and no locoregional recurrence. The patient survived 39 months. Patient 4 had a minimal response during initial treatment, but the tumor progressed. Chemotherapy protocols were then adjusted with a combination of PD1 monoclonal antibody, chidamide, or apatinib, but the response remained poor. The patient died of disease 13 months later. Patient 5 shrank after chemotherapy but later progressed, showing no response to the regimen containing PD1 antibody. The patient succumbed 12 months after initial diagnosis.

Regarding local radiotherapy, Patient 5 did not begin radiotherapy, and Patient 2 refused radiotherapy. The remaining three patients(Patient 3,1,4) completed radiotherapy with doses of 50 Gy, 66 Gy, and 72.8 Gy, respectively.

In refractory and relapsed patients, chemotherapy doses were adjusted in the setting of immune and/or targeted therapy in combination, such as paclitaxel, carboplatin, fluorouracil, sintilizumab, camrelizumab, chidamide, apatinib, etc.

Discussion

Considering rarity of NC, our findings are relevant in several aspects. NC is a chemo-sensitive tumor. The cyclophosphamide - pirarubicin - cisplatin (CAP) alternating with ifosfamide-etoposide (IE) courses of chemotherapy appears to be effective in some patients with NC. As previously shown, the most common site in this study was head and neck, followed by vulva. Although the cell of origin is unknown, it has been speculated that NC may arise from primitive neural crest-derived cells. Genetically, its pathogenesis is related to a chromosomal rearrangement involving the NUTM1 gene. In most patients, the main oncoprotein is BRD4-NUT with a translocation of NUTM1 and BRD4 genes(about 70%−80%), but in a few patients, the fusion type is BRD3-NUT, or NSD3-NUT [10]. Patients with a primary thoracic origin exhibited poorer outcomes compared to their non-thoracic counterparts, as shown in Table 2 [10]. All the three presented cases with available molecular data had the most prevalent BRD4-NUTM1 fusion (Figure 2).

BRD4 belongs to a transcription coactivator and family member of the mammalian, bromodomain and extraterminal (BET), which interacts with acetylated histones and transcription factors to prevent cellular differentiation [11].

Currently, the European Cooperative Study Group on pediatric rare tumors (EXPeRT) developed recommendations. For localized disease, a complete microscopic surgical resection should be achieved whenever and as soon as possible, followed by irradiation of primary tumor and involved lymph nodes area (60 to 70 Gy) [9]. Management approaches have incorporated chemotherapy regiments, surgical resection followed by radiotherapy, and targeted therapy [12]. Complete tumor resection of non-thoracic primary tumor followed by chemotherapy and radiation is associated with long-term survival as Patient 3 in Table 2 [13]. Chemotherapy protocols according to sarcoma regimen were adopted. Various drugs in combination schedules, such as cisplatin, carboplatin, cyclophosphamide, etoposide, doxorubicin, actinomycin D, vinorelbine, vinblastine, paclitaxel, docetaxel, 5-fluorouracil, bleomycin, vincristine, ifosfamide and gemcitabine, were used in NC. Alkylating agents, platin compounds, and anthracyclines in combination exhibited better response in some patients [14]. Upfront complete surgical resection as the mainstay modality contributed to the outcome of our patients. Patient 1, 2 and 3 underwent surgical resection at diagnosis, and radical resection was considered in two patients of response after preoperative chemotherapy.

Three patients received radiotherapy. One patient did not receive radiotherapy due to concerns regarding long-term side effects. Due to tumor progression after chemotherapy, two patients(Patient 4 and Patient 5) did not receive resection as progressive enlargement of the mass after initial shrinkage.

In accordance of the literature, the prognosis is generally dismal. The median overall survival ranged from 6.5 to 10 months (Table 2) [2, 6, 10, 15] In a study with outcome data derived from 124 patients with NC, nonthoracic, BRD3-NUT, or NSD3-NUT were associated with a better prognosis [10]. Age did not appear to affect the outcome [14]. In this study, the primary sites were pharynx, vulva and tonsil, with local lymph node involvement at diagnosis. All tumor sites were nonthoracic. Consistent with ESCP guidelines [9], Patient 4,5 were poorly sensitive to chemotherapy. No consensus has been established regarding the best chemotherapy regimens. “Ewing sarcoma- like protocols” with multiagent chemotherapy combining alkylating agents, anthracycline, cisplatin alternating with ifosfamide, etoposide regimen seemed to be effective in three patients(Patient 1,2,3). Response to chemotherapy could contribute to further local therapy and extending survival.Maintenance treatment could be considered for metastatic disease. There is no harmonized consensus recommendations for maintenance treatment of NC and it has been reported that one patient with NC benefited from radiotherapy combined with anlotinib hydrochloride. Patient 1 received apatinib maintenance therapy after chemotherapy and exhibited stable disease. Targeted therapy and new agents have been explored in NC such as BETi (molibresib (GSK525762), amredobresib (BI 89499)), and the PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab [9, 16]. However, more evidence is needed to confirm the clinical effect of anti-angiogenesis inhibitor.

The major limitation of our study lied in its small sample size. Second, another limitation is the short length of follow-up. Finally, fusion types were not identified in two patients because of limited tissue samples.
