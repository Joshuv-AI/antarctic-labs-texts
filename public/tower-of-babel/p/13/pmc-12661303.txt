Constructing dyadic management behavioral personas for patient-caregiver dyads in ambulatory chemotherapy: A qualitative study
Authors: Tianze Yu, Xiaoju Zhang, Liying Wang, Zhenqi Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop patient-caregiver dyadic personas in ambulatory chemotherapy settings. These personas will enable clinicians to rapidly identify behavioral heterogeneity in dyadic illness management behaviors and deliver targeted support and guidance.MethodsAn interpretive phenomenological study was conducted at a tertiary cancer hospital in mainland China from March to June 2025. Patient-caregiver dyads participating in ambulatory chemotherapy were recruited through purposive sampling. Semi-structured interviews explored their dyadic illness management experiences, adhering to the inductive principles of phenomenological inquiry. The data were first analyzed to identify emergent themes and patterns from the participants' lived experiences. Subsequently, the COM-B framework was employed as a heuristic lens to systematically interpret and characterize the underlying behavioral mechanisms of the identified patterns, leading to the development of dyadic personas.ResultsTwenty-five eligible patient-caregiver dyads were included. Using the COM-B framework, five distinct personas were identified: Full-Collaboration Dyads, Patient-Led Dyads, Caregiver-Led Dyads, Phase-Responsibility Dyads, and Guidance-Dependent Dyads, reflecting heterogeneous dyadic illness management behaviors and needs in ambulatory chemotherapy.ConclusionsUnderstanding management heterogeneity through dyadic personas is valuable for optimizing ambulatory chemotherapy care. This study established five behavioral personas specific to patient-caregiver dyads, offering a structured approach to develop individualized interventions that address diverse needs across the treatment continuum.

Introduction

Persistent global increases in cancer incidence have substantially heightened clinical demand for chemotherapy, a cornerstone of comprehensive cancer treatment. Projections indicate a 53% relative growth in global chemotherapy needs by 2040, affecting approximately 15 million new patients—25% of whom will be in China.1,2 This surge challenges the capacity of traditional inpatient chemotherapy models and accelerates the shift toward more efficient, precise, and patient-centered oncology care.3 In this context, ambulatory chemotherapy has gained widespread adoption due to its advantages in streamlining care delivery, reducing hospitalization, alleviating psychological and financial burdens, and improving quality of life. It has become the dominant model in Western countries,4,5 and recent strong endorsement by China's health authorities has further prioritized the enhancement of day oncology services.6

However, the efficiency of this model introduces inherent challenges that underscore the importance of dyadic care. The brief on-site treatment per visit reduces direct medical supervision compared to inpatient care, shifting the locus of care to the home environment. This creates discontinuities in care and a gap between standardized in-hospital guidance and the complex, heterogeneous needs of out-of-hospital management.7 It also leaves a vacuum in continuous professional oversight, requiring the patient–caregiver dyad to become the primary unit responsible for daily illness management—including side effect recognition, treatment adherence, and problem-solving. Inadequate dyadic management in this setting may lead to missed interventions, reduced compliance, and compromised quality of life, highlighting the critical need to understand and support these collaborative behaviors.

Dyadic illness management during chemotherapy involves coordinated efforts between patients and informal caregivers, encompassing symptom recognition, treatment adherence, lifestyle regulation, and psychological adjustment—each dimension critically influencing treatment safety and clinical outcomes.8,9 Primary caregivers, typically family members providing essential daily support, assume central roles in tasks such as symptom monitoring, medication reminders, emotional support, and care coordination.10 Importantly, illness management functions as a dyadic phenomenon rather than an individual endeavor,11,12 with patients' dependency often intensifying as treatment progresses.13 Evidence confirms that active caregiver involvement enhances treatment confidence, quality of life, and clinical outcomes.14,15 Yet, current research remains largely focused on individual-level behaviors, with limited attention to the collaborative dynamics of patient–caregiver dyads, particularly in ambulatory chemotherapy.

Although nursing interventions can improve dyadic management behaviors, their effectiveness hinges on accurate assessment of service recipients. Comprehensive pre-chemotherapy assessments help prevent complications, optimize outcomes, and enhance quality of life.16 However, in high-volume ambulatory settings, nurses face practical constraints such as rapid patient turnover and heavy workloads, which limit the depth and scope of traditional assessments and hinder a full understanding of dyadic needs.

The persona approach offers a promising solution by synthesizing key characteristics of diverse dyads into distinct behavioral archetypes. This allows clinicians, especially nurses under time pressure, to quickly identify critical dyadic attributes and tailor interventions accordingly.17,18 Unlike assessments focusing on isolated traits, this method captures dyadic behavioral patterns within an actionable framework.19 Significant heterogeneity exists across dyads in terms of management initiative, role clarity, and problem-solving strategies. To systematically analyze these variations, this study adopts the COM-B model,20 which examines behavior through three interrelated dimensions: Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation.21,22 This multi-dimensional lens allows for a nuanced interpretation of dyadic personas and clarifies the underlying logic of their management behaviors.

This study aims to bridge the gap between dyadic needs and existing support frameworks by exploring illness management experiences in ambulatory chemotherapy; characterizing the emergent behavioral patterns; interpreting these patterns to construct personas using the COM-B model as a theoretical lens; and establishing a foundation for developing targeted, actionable clinical interventions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study adopted an interpretive phenomenological design, chosen for its unique suitability in investigating the complex, co-constructed nature of dyadic illness management—an inherently relational process that requires examining how meaning is generated within the patient-caregiver relationship. This choice aligns with the study's core objective: to explore not just dyadic management behaviors, but how and why these behaviors emerge through relational interaction. Descriptive phenomenology focuses on universal experiential structures, which cannot capture the relational essence of dyadic illness management. Interpretive phenomenology centers on context-specific, shared meanings, ensuring methodological alignment with the relational nature of dyadic management and the study's aim to characterize behavioral patterns.23 Unlike descriptive phenomenology, which focuses on essential structures of experience, this approach explores the dynamics of shared meaning-making to understand the emergence of behavioral patterns. Accordingly, the analysis employed a two-phase design: an initial inductive thematic analysis identified emergent patterns from participant's lived experiences, followed by the post-hoc application of the COM-B model as a heuristic lens to systematically interpret the underlying behavioral mechanisms characterizing each pattern.

The research was conducted at an ambulatory chemotherapy center within a tertiary cancer hospital in China between March and June 2025. Consistent with the consensus on management of ambulatory chemotherapy in Shanghai municipal hospitals,24 all patients were required to have good functional status [Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) Performance Status ≤ 1 or Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score ≥ 70] to be eligible for this treatment model, which served as the foundational criterion for our sampling framework. To ensure depth and comprehensiveness of findings, participants were recruited from patient-caregiver dyads undergoing ambulatory chemotherapy using purposive sampling. Sample size determination followed the principle of data saturation, whereby interviews were terminated when no new information emerged in three consecutive interviews and at least ten interviews had been completed.25

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients

Inclusion criteria:

(1) Completed cycles of the current chemotherapy regimen < 50% of the total planned cycles; (2) Age ≥ 18 years; (3) Possesses basic communication skills, normal cognition, clear consciousness, and able to cooperate in completing the interview in Chinese; (4) Informed consent and voluntary participation in this study.

Exclusion criteria:

(1) Comorbid with poorly controlled other chronic diseases or complications; (2) Severely limited self-care ability, unable to perform illness management.

Primary caregivers

Inclusion criteria:

(1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Identified by the patient as the family member or friend providing the primary daily living care and/or medical care assistance; (3) Possesses basic communication skills, normal cognition, clear consciousness, and able to cooperate in completing the interview in Chinese; (4) Informed consent and voluntary participation in this study.

Exclusion criteria:

(1) Has an employment relationship with the patient or receives financial remuneration from the patient, the patient's family, or a third party for caregiving activities (e.g., paid caregivers). The presence of such a relationship also led to the exclusion of the entire dyad; (2) Suffers from severe physical illness or mental illness, which, as determined by the researchers, may significantly impact their ability to provide care for the patient.

Data collection

Researchers initially recruited participants at the reception desk of the ambulatory chemotherapy center. Participants were selected using purposive sampling combined with a maximum variation strategy to ensure diversity in general sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, and education level. This approach aimed to capture a broad spectrum of perspectives related to dyadic illness management behaviors in ambulatory chemotherapy.

Eligible participants were then informed of the study's purpose, significance, and the principle of voluntary participation. Prior to formal interviews, researchers reviewed medical records to confirm patients' tumor diagnosis types, cancer stage, chemotherapy regimens, and whether they had received or were currently receiving other antitumor therapies (e.g., surgery, radiotherapy, immunotherapy). Additionally, general sociodemographic information was collected, including age, gender, marital status, education level, employment status, and religious affiliation. This information was used to explore potential associations between participants' dyadic management behaviors and their sociodemographic backgrounds.

Finally, data were collected through face-to-face in-depth interviews at locations preferred by participants. Each interview was conducted by a primary researcher who engaged with participants, operated audio recording equipment, and documented nonverbal information (e.g., facial expressions, tone of voice) through field notes. Patients and caregivers were interviewed separately, with each session lasting 30–60 minutes. Upon obtaining participant consent, interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim within 24 hours. Recruitment of new participants ceased when information saturation was achieved.

Interview guide

A semi-structured interview guide was developed, with the conceptual focus being dyadic illness management. As a methodological consideration to enhance participant rapport and comprehension, the familiar term “self-management” was adopted within the interview questions. Data analysis validated that the collected data accurately captured the intended dyadic phenomena. Consequently, the manuscript reverts to the precise conceptual term, “dyadic illness management,” for reporting. The guide's development included review by three oncology nursing experts and pretesting with an independent group.

Research team

The research team comprised professionals with clinical nursing and research expertise, including one Primary Researcher (PR) and one Research Assistant (RA). The PR was responsible for study design, interview implementation, and data analysis. All interviews were conducted by the PR, who led the interview process and maintained field records. Post-interview, the RA and PR collaboratively collated and analyzed interview data. Ambiguous findings arising during analysis were resolved through joint deliberation between the PR and RA. Persistent disagreements were adjudicated by consulting external experts until consensus was achieved, ensuring analytical accuracy and reliability. All team members received formal training in qualitative methods and demonstrated expertise through involvement in ≥ 3 prior qualitative studies. Additionally, each possessed clinical experience in nursing management or ambulatory chemotherapy care, thereby safeguarding methodological rigor.

Data analysis

A two-phase analytical design was employed to ground the findings in participant's lived experiences before theoretical interpretation. Guided by a dyadic analysis framework,26 which treats the dyad as the fundamental unit of analysis, the process ensured that emergent patterns were derived inductively from the data before the COM-B model was applied as an interpretive lens.

Phase one: phenomenological-thematic analysis

The first phase involved a phenomenological-thematic analysis of interview transcripts and field notes. Guided by the dyadic analysis framework,26 the PR and RA independently analyzed the data, focusing on the contrasts and overlaps between patient and caregiver narratives within each dyad. This comparative process occurred at both the descriptive level (e.g., recounting of management tasks) and the interpretive level (e.g., meaning ascribed to roles and interactions) to identify shared and divergent experiences.

Following an inductive approach, the researchers identified significant statements and meaning units related to dyadic illness management. Through constant comparative analysis, these units were grouped into initial codes, which were collaboratively discussed and refined into emergent themes and sub-themes. Crucially, this phase was conducted without any pre-existing theoretical framework (such as the COM-B model) to ensure themes emerged organically from the data.

Phase two: theoretical interpretation using the COM-B model

Upon establishing stable thematic patterns, the COM-B model was applied post hoc as a heuristic lens in the second phase. This aligns with the hermeneutic tradition of interpretive phenomenology, where theoretical constructs are used to deepen understanding of empirical findings.23 The emergent themes were systematically mapped onto the COM-B components:•Capability: Knowledge, skills, and information processing.•Opportunity: External factors (e.g., social support, healthcare access).•Motivation: Core drives, goals, and decision-making processes.

Persona development and validation

Personas were developed by synthesizing demographic characteristics and COM-B-defined behavioral features for each distinct pattern. Preliminary personas were shared with a subset of participants (n ​= ​5 dyads) for member checking; feedback was incorporated to refine the descriptions and enhance credibility.

Rigor

To ensure rigor and credibility, the PR conducted face-to-face interviews while operating recording equipment and documenting nonverbal behaviors in field notes. Interview techniques (e.g., active listening, reflective questioning) were flexibly applied to elicit authentic responses. A neutral stance was maintained throughout data collection and analysis to prevent participant induction. Post-interview, the PR and RA debriefed to refine field notes.

Triangulation enhanced reliability through researcher analysis, participant validation, and expert evaluation.27 Data saturation was confirmed by recruiting two additional eligible dyads after three consecutive interviews yielded no new information, with subsequent analysis verifying saturation.

Ethical considerations

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (Approval No. 2503-Exp150-KY). All participants were fully informed of the study's purpose, content, and methodology prior to participation and signed written informed consent forms. Participant privacy and data security were rigorously protected throughout the research. Original recordings were processed using professional voice anonymization software to remove biometric voice characteristics. All transcripts underwent de-identification by redacting personal identifiers beyond coded IDs. Without participant consent, no data was disclosed to external entities or used for non-academic purposes. For analysis and reporting, all identifiable information was anonymized using coded identifiers: patients were denoted as P (Patient) ​+ ​number, caregivers as C (Caregiver) ​+ ​number, and dyadic pairs as F (Family) ​+ ​shared number, ensuring comprehensive privacy protection for all research subjects.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

This study enrolled 25 eligible ambulatory chemotherapy patient-caregiver dyads. Given that the dyad was the primary unit of analysis, data collection focused on demographic variables most relevant to the partnership level (e.g., age, gender, relationship). Thus, individual-level socioeconomic details for caregivers (e.g., specific employment status, education) were not obtained to avoid unnecessary participant burden and an over-emphasis on individual attributes. Among the patients, 5 were male and 20 were female, with ages ranging from 25 to 75 years (mean ​= ​47 years). A total of 56.0% were urban residents, 48.0% had higher education, 76.0% were married, and 44.0% were employed. Spouses were the most common caregiver type (48.0%). Demographic and management personas of the five dyadic personas.

The following subsections provide a detailed narrative interpretation for each persona, integrating the insights from both the conceptual framework and the demographic personas to offer a comprehensive understanding.

Full-Collaboration Dyads

This persona accounted for 12% of dyads (F2, F5, F23). All patients were women aged 35–44, primarily married, employed urban residents with higher education. Both members participated in all management activities—from symptom monitoring to plan implementation—through consensus-based discussions. Their def-ining feature was “shared decision-making with coordinated action".

With strong health literacy, these dyads possessed solid health knowledge and could integrate information, though complex scenarios required further learning. Close collaboration enabled accurate problem identification and timely responses, forming an “early detection–rapid response” cycle. Their shared goal of preventing treatment delays motivated proactive joint management.

While collaboration strengthened during treatment, unclear role boundaries occasionally reduced efficiency. They strictly followed treatment plans, recognizing their importance for recovery. Accessible professional support channels boosted their confidence and kept information synchronized.

We agreed to keep warm first. When it didn't improve the next day, we messaged the doctor's assistant. Hearing back put our minds at ease. We still handle everything through discussion—we're getting more experienced."

Patient-Led Dyads

This persona represented 20% of dyads (F4, F6, F9, F13, F14). Patients were predominantly women aged 25–40 years with diverse marital statuses and urban/rural distribution. Patients maintained strong autonomy in daily management while caregivers provided auxiliary support such as treatment accompaniment and material procurement. Patients demonstrated independent capabilities in dietary management and symptom response, actively handling core responsibilities while rarely expressing needs to caregivers to minimize perceived burden. Caregivers respected patients' leadership roles, offering shopping and meal support without interfering in decisions—adapting to rather than directing management rhythms.

Fragmented external resources were observed: patients accessed health information through diverse channels (books, online sources, instructional videos, and fellow cancer patients) but struggled to verify content reliability; caregivers faced participation constraints due to competing social commitments; and communication barriers with healthcare teams impeded real-time professional consultation during urgent situations.

C6 (Mother): "My daughter rarely requests help. I only assist with laundry and shopping while consciously avoiding other involvements."

Caregiver-Led Dyads

This persona comprised 28% of dyads (F1, F10, F11, F16, F17, F18, F25). Predominantly female patients aged 53–75 years, mostly unemployed with limited educational attainment. Caregivers (primarily spouses or adult children) assumed full management responsibility, independently directing core tasks with absolute decision-making authority. Driven by the goal of preventing errors, they controlled management rhythms and often made decisions on patients' behalf.

Patients demonstrated limited illness management capabilities due to educational barriers and physical constraints, resulting in high dependency. They complied with caregiver directives without resistance, reinforced by deep trust. This dynamic established a unidirectional information flow—caregivers controlled information acquisition and determined sharing with patients, further reducing patient engagement.

Phase-Responsibility Dyads

This persona accounted for 24% of dyads (F3, F7, F12, F19, F20, F24). Patients were predominantly women aged 32–52 years, with most employed and possessing higher education levels, alongside balanced urban/rural residency distribution. The behavioral signature manifested as role leadership shifting across treatment phases: during chemotherapy administration periods, one member assumed primary responsibility for medical coordination such as clinician communication and treatment monitoring, whereas during intermission intervals, the other dominated daily care management including nutrition planning and symptom tracking. Crucially, both partners demonstrated phase-specific competencies, resulting in high adherence to the “leader-supporter” framework within each phase and stable collaboration.

However, inter-phase coordination proved suboptimal. Due to limited awareness of information consolidation, transitions between treatment and recovery periods carried risks of communication breakdowns and task omissions. When moving to a new phase, the prior-phase leader's management motivation diminished significantly—often perceiving their task as “completed"—while the successor lacked initiative to integrate historical information, considering it “beyond assigned responsibilities.” Consequently, despite accessible targeted resources (e.g., treatment-phase clinical guidance and intermission nursing support), the absence of standardized handover mechanisms undermined continuity of care across phases.

Participant Quotes:

C3 (Spouse): "During chemo, she drank well in the hospital—got everything covered treatment-wise. Once home resting, isn't treatment over? I didn't think to remind her about hydration. Didn't pay much attention, then she complained about dry mouth."

I just cooked more greens—didn't dig deeper. Only after his hemoglobin dropped did the doctor say we needed protein."

C19 (Brother): "My sister had everything covered during chemo—no worries at the hospital. Home afterward, I figured treatment's done—she should remember the dietary restrictions. Didn't monitor her. Next thing, she couldn't resist sneaking fried chicken and vomited violently!"

Guidance-Dependent Dyads

This persona represented 16% of dyads (F8, F15, F21, F22). Patients showed balanced gender distribution, aged 33–73 years, predominantly unemployed rural residents with limited education. Both members exhibited low initiative and decision-making capability in dyadic illness management, demonstrating high dependency on professional guidance and executing tasks only through explicit external instruction. While compliant with prescribed routines, their limited behavioral flexibility and inability to anticipate potential issues left them unprepared for scenarios beyond standardized protocols.

Regarding opportunity, they relied on singular channels for medical resources, typically deferring problems until the next clinical visit. This resulted in critically insufficient professional support during intermission periods. Compounded by low health literacy, lack of initiative, and absence of culturally appropriate tools, they lacked capacity to proactively seek information. Consequently, when confronting unanticipated situations, they failed to recognize intervention needs or initiate appropriate actions, remaining in a state of external instruction dependency.

Discussion

Multidimensional differentiation: heterogeneity in dyadic illness management behaviors of ambulatory chemotherapy dyads

This study employs the COM-B model to deconstruct the behavioral ecology of ambulatory chemotherapy dyads, translating rich phenomenological accounts of lived experience into a typology of actionable behavioral patterns. This process directly addresses our research aim of investigating dyadic management practices and characterizing their behavioral signatures. The resulting five personas represent archetypes of adaptation, each defined by a unique configuration of capabilities, opportunities, and motivations derived from the dyads' narratives. This typology provides a functional map of dyadic management, illustrating how common challenges are navigated through distinct behavioral pathways. More than mere categories, these personas reveal the underlying logic—the ‘why’ behind the ‘what'—of dyadic behaviors, demonstrating that effective support must be aligned with each dyad's operational logic rather than generically applied. These personas reflect context-adapted strategies—not hierarchies of value—each constituting a functional response to specific constraints and resources. Crucially, by moving from experiential descriptions to a theoretically-grounded typology, this approach bridges a critical gap between deep qualitative understanding and the needs of clinical practice. By clarifying these patterns, the typology equips clinicians to implement precision interventions, optimizing care through individualized strategies that enhance treatment adherence and quality of life.

Analysis of dyadic personas: underlying mechanisms and clinical implications

The five dyadic personas identified in this study represent more than mere behavioral categories; they constitute distinct adaptive configurations arising from the interplay of individual capacities, relational dynamics, and contextual constraints within ambulatory chemotherapy. Viewed through the COM-B lens, each persona's core challenges and strengths are rooted in specific configurations of Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation, offering a theoretically grounded basis for developing targeted interventions.

Full-Collaboration Dyads exemplify the highly coordinated “shared management” ideal noted in chronic illness,28 achieving notable efficiency through a synergy of strong capabilities and robust opportunities. However, this study reveals the model's conditional nature, as its core strength—interdependence—becomes a liability when the established collaborative pattern is disrupted, exposing a latent rigidity and lack of resilience.29 This finding shifts the clinical focus from optimizing collaboration alone toward building adaptive capacity. Corresponding interventions should thus incorporate scenario simulation training to develop unilateral emergency response skills30 and implement web-based patient-reported outcome tools to maintain information continuity during absence.31 The persona's prevalence among dyads with higher education and urban residence further suggests that socio-ecological factors not only predispose couples toward this collaborative ideal but also equip them to engage these sophisticated, resilience-focused interventions.

In Patient-Led Dyads, a distinctive paradox emerges: the patient's drive for autonomy, often motivated by a desire to avoid burdening the caregiver, paradoxically fosters informational isolation. This pattern arises when moderate health literacy coexists with a tendency to conceal needs, revealing the limitations of purely individual-focused self-management models. While these dyads appear functionally efficient, this efficiency is often achieved by sacrificing shared understanding—creating clinical risks when unreported symptoms or unverified information guide decisions. This suggests that autonomy, when interpreted narrowly as personal responsibility, can inadvertently compromise the collaborative basis of effective illness management. Interventions should therefore reframe autonomy as informed agency within a relational context, integrating validated safeguards such as curated health information with simplified interpretations32 and PRO-CTCAE tools for precise symptom tracking.33,34 Concurrently, fostering dyadic communication is essential to transform protective silence into shared awareness—enhancing systemic resilience while preserving the patient's leadership role.

In contrast, Caregiver-Led Dyads typically operate under a different relational logic: a protective mandate that associates care with assuming full control. While ensuring practical task completion, this well-intentioned model tends to create informational asymmetry that marginalizes the patient, reducing their role to passive compliance. This dynamic poses a two-fold problem: it can lead to patients feeling disconnected from managing their own illness, and it also severely weakens the dyad's ability to handle situations when the caregiver is absent. The key clinical challenge lies in recalibrating this interaction toward partnership without dismissing the caregiver's deep commitment. Goal-oriented health coaching, structured around progressively negotiated tasks, can effectively rebuild patient competence and confidence.35 This strategy maintains the caregiver's vital involvement while creating space for the patient's voice and agency to reemerge—ultimately transforming the dyad from a protector-dependent arrangement into a truly collaborative alliance.

Phase-Responsibility Dyads illustrate how temporal specialization, though efficient within phases, can obscure handover deficits resulting from a perception of each phase as a discrete task rather than part of an integrated process. These dyads exhibit motivational decline and poor information transfer between phases, as seen in cases F3 and F12. The underlying issue is not individual incompetence, but a lack of shared responsibility for continuity—a vulnerability mirroring unstructured care transitions in professional settings.36 Interventions should provide external structure to bridge these gaps, using tools such as “Treatment-Intermission Handoff Checklists” to standardize monitoring,37 complemented by clinician-led follow-ups at key transitions to compensate for coordination deficits.38 This persona may reflect an adaptive strategy to manage competing modern-life demands, underscoring how broader social structures can shape dyadic management styles—sometimes at the cost of care continuity.

Guidance-Dependent Dyads highlight critical equity issues, as their behavior stems from structural constraints—such as low literacy, limited resources, and inadequate support—rather than preference. These dyads experience a convergence of low capability and restricted opportunity, leading to high dependency on explicit external guidance. This state represents an adaptive response to inaccessible knowledge and support, not passive resistance, and often results in reactive care and elevated clinical risks, such as treatment delays for infections or dehydration (F8, F15). Interventions must therefore simultaneously build internal capability and engineer external opportunity, employing low-literacy educational tools39,40 and visual aids alongside integrated care networks, proactive telehealth, and tailored decision aids41,42 to bridge support gaps and reduce delays in seeking help. Addressing the needs of these dyads is both a clinical and ethical imperative for advancing equity in ambulatory cancer care.

The COM-B model as an interpretive lens: enhancing the explanatory power of personas

This study employed the COM-B model as a post-hoc interpretive lens to address a critical gap in persona development: the leap from descriptive classification to explanatory understanding.43 While the five personas derived from inductive thematic analysis effectively catalog the heterogeneity in dyadic management behaviors, their value for designing interventions is limited without understanding the underlying reasons for these differences.

The COM-B model provided this essential explanatory power. By systematically mapping the emergent themes onto the dimensions of Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation, it offered a coherent behavioral science framework to interpret the findings. This process recast the personas from descriptive labels into explanatory personas. For instance, the “Guidance-Dependent” pattern was revealed not as mere passivity, but as the result of a behavioral system where low Capability converges with severely constrained Opportunity. Similarly, the “Phase-Responsibility” pattern was explained by a specific configuration of Motivation (focused on phase-specific tasks) and Opportunity (lack of handover structures) that precipitates coordination failures.

Thus, the COM-B model did not constrain the inductive findings but enriched them, illuminating the “active ingredients” that define each persona's behavior.20 This integration demonstrates the unique value of the model in persona development: it grounds clinically useful categories in a validated theory of behavior. This crucial transformation shifts the typology's focus from what dyads do to why they do it, thereby creating a robust, theory-informed foundation for deriving tailored interventions and bridging the gap between rich qualitative insight and the practical demands of clinical behavior change.

Dynamic personas: behavioral evolution across the chemotherapy trajectory

This study challenges a static view of dyadic personas, recasting them as dynamic adaptations to evolving circumstances. Behavioral shifts in participant accounts—such as a caregiver's move from passive to active intervention after a clinical scare—underscore that personas are fluid configurations, not fixed categories. These transitions often follow critical incidents that disrupt the dyad's equilibrium, serving as catalytic moments that prompt a reassessment of capabilities, opportunities, and motivations. This fluidity confirms that dyadic behaviors evolve meaningfully over time.44 The malleability of COM-B components suggests the persona framework is best used as a dynamic clinical tool for ongoing assessment, supporting dyads through transitions rather than applying a one-time label. This perspective calls for longitudinal, relationship-centered care that anticipates adaptation. Future research should therefore track persona evolution to refine interventions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the qualitative approach to developing dyadic personas lacks standardized methodological guidelines, which may affect cross-study comparability. Second, while theoretical saturation was achieved with 25 interviews, this remains a relative concept, and a more heterogeneous sample might yield additional persona types. Third, the identified personas capture role characteristics at a specific treatment point; their dynamic evolution with disease progression and changing contexts may limit long-term clinical utility. Fourth, as a single-center investigation, the findings may be influenced by institutional protocols and local patient demographics, necessitating future multi-center validation. Fifth, the generalizability and analytical scope are bounded by the study's design. The findings are influenced by the specific clinical context of a sample with a high proportion of gynecological oncology patients (≈70%). Moreover, by design, the analysis operates at the dyadic level and does not incorporate fine-grained individual caregiver characteristics (e.g., specific education, occupation). This focus was necessary to demarcate a clear unit of analysis but precludes examination of how individual-level factors within the partnership modulate the identified patterns. Finally, although cancer stage data were analyzed, they did not differentiate the personas, suggesting that interpersonal and socioecological dynamics may outweigh disease stage in shaping management behaviors within this functionally preserved ambulatory population.

Future research should address these limitations by establishing standardized protocols, expanding sample heterogeneity, implementing longitudinal designs to capture dynamic interactions, and conducting multi-center studies to verify generalizability.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this qualitative study presents the first evidence-based classification of dyadic management behaviors in ambulatory chemotherapy patient-caregiver dyads, using the COM-B framework. Five distinct personas emerged: Full-Collaboration, Patient-Led, Caregiver-Led, Phase-Responsibility, and Guidance-Dependent. These personas systematically capture behavioral heterogeneity across the dyadic dimensions of capability, opportunity, and motivation, reflecting key patterns and underrecognized challenges such as discontinuous care coordination during treatment–intermission transitions.

The typology provides clinicians with a structured, efficient method for identifying dyadic support needs and enabling targeted interventions, thereby advancing the optimization of collaborative dyadic management. To translate these findings into practice, subsequent research should focus on operationalizing the framework—for instance, by developing a rapid assessment tool for clinical profiling and designing corresponding intervention modules. The ultimate value of this persona-based approach should be evaluated based on its ability to improve outcomes critical to the ambulatory chemotherapy experience, such as interphase symptom burden, rates of unplanned care utilization, and the quality of the patient and caregiver experience. Future work should also validate the dynamic evolution of these personas across the treatment trajectory and assess the impact of persona-guided protocols on long-term safety and quality-of-life outcomes.
