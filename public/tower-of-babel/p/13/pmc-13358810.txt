Associations among mild behavioral impairment, cognition, and brain pathology in preclinical autosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe Mild Behavioral Impairment Checklist (MBI‐C) captures neuropsychiatric symptoms in individuals at risk of dementia. In this study, we examined MBI‐C scores in autosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease (ADAD).METHODSWe included 83 cognitively unimpaired presenilin‐1 E280A mutation carriers and 114 non‐carriers with MBI‐C, obfjective cognition, and subjective cognitive decline (SCD) assessments. Study sub‐samples underwent neuroimaging to assess Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology and neurodegeneration.RESULTSMBI‐C total scores were greater in carriers than non‐carriers (r rb = 0.19 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.05, 0.31], P = 0.009), driven by impulse dyscontrol symptoms. Elevated MBI‐C scores were associated with higher study partner–reported SCD scores (β = 0.28 [95% CI 0.07, 0.050], P = 0.008) and with greater neocortical amyloid beta (β = 0.46 [95% CI 0.17, 0.075], P = 0.003) among carriers.DISCUSSIONOur study reveals that mild behavioral impairment symptoms may emerge before cognitive decline in ADAD and supports the MBI‐C as a tool for detecting early behavioral changes linked to AD pathology and identifying at‐risk individuals for clinical trials and early intervention.

BACKGROUND

Over the course of Alzheimer's disease (AD), 9 in 10 individuals exhibit neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPSs), contributing to functional impairment, premature institutionalization, health‐care costs, and overall decrease in quality of life of both patients and caregivers. 1 , 2 These symptoms, primarily depression, apathy, anxiety, irritability, agitation, and hallucinations, may arise prior to the onset of dementia and predict faster progression to mild cognitive impairment (MCI). 3 , 4 De novo emergence of NPSs is core to the construct of mild behavioral impairment (MBI), an increasingly recognized neurobehavioral syndrome. 5 In this framework, behavioral and cognitive symptoms representing MBI and MCI, respectively, may emerge concurrently or independently in preclinical or prodromal disease stages. 5 Indeed, MBI is prevalent in 37% of individuals with subjective cognitive decline (SCD) and in 54% of those with MCI, 6 conferring an increased risk of incident dementia and AD. 7

The Mild Behavioral Impairment Checklist (MBI‐C), developed by the Neuropsychiatric Syndromes Professional Interest Area of the International Society to Advance Alzheimer's Research and Treatment (ISTAART), captures NPS domains that meet MBI criteria. Contrary to other scales, the MBI‐C assesses new‐onset NPS in late life persistent for at least 6 months, disregarding chronic or transient states. 8 By aligning with dementia risk before overt cognitive decline, the MBI‐C detects behavioral and mood changes not attributable to other psychiatric causes. 9 NPSs not meeting MBI criteria confer significantly lower odds of dementia compared to those meeting MBI criteria, underscoring the biological basis of the construct. 10 Thus, understanding the link between MBI and AD progression is critical to further validate MBI as a biological risk state.

Higher MBI‐C scores, indicating presence and severity of NPSs, have been associated with worse cognitive performance among individuals with SCD and MCI. 6 , 11 This link has also been observed in cognitively unimpaired individuals, in line with the idea that behavioral changes may emerge before or independent of cognitive changes. 12 Additionally, a growing body of neuroimaging studies in dementia‐free populations have reported associations between MBI and amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau pathology as well as neurodegeneration. 7 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 While these findings suggest that behavioral changes represent early sequelae of AD pathology, evidence remains relatively scarce. Elucidating the relationship between MBI‐C and underlying pathology may facilitate the identification of individuals at elevated risk for dementia.

Despite the need to further understand MBI prevalence and its clinical and biological correlates, MBI has not been investigated in autosomal‐dominant AD (ADAD). This early‐onset form of AD provides a unique opportunity to study preclinical pathological changes in a well‐characterized population, free from aging confounds. In this study, we leveraged presenilin‐1 (PSEN1) E280A mutation carriers from the Colombian ADAD kindred, who typically develop MCI by age 44. 17 Our primary aim was to compare MBI‐C total and domain scores between cognitively unimpaired PSEN1 E280A carriers and non‐carriers. Additionally, we investigated the association between MBI‐C scores and cognition between groups, focusing on global cognition, objective memory performance, and subjective memory complaints. Finally, in a study sub‐sample, we examined the association between MBI‐C scores and early AD imaging biomarkers, including regional Aβ and tau burden, brain volume, and glucose metabolism, using positron emission tomography (PET) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). We hypothesized that MBI‐C scores would be greater in PSEN1 E280A carriers than non‐carriers and that elevated MBI‐C scores would correlate with worse cognitive function and greater burden of AD pathology among carriers.

METHODS

Study population

We used data from cognitively unimpaired PSEN1 E280A mutation carriers and non‐carrier family members from the Colombian ADAD kindred. 17 All participants who completed neuropsychological and MBI‐C assessments were included. We used the Functional Assessment Staging Tool (FAST; range: 1–7) to determine cognitive status, with FAST ≤ 2 indicating no cognitive impairment. 18 Participants were excluded if they had a significant psychiatric or neurological disorder; history of stroke, seizures, or substance abuse; or FAST > 2. The final sample included 83 cognitively unimpaired PSEN1 E280A carriers and 114 non‐carriers. A sub‐sample of participants additionally underwent neuroimaging, including 16 carriers and 24 non‐carriers with Aβ PET, tau PET, and MRI scans, and 23 carriers and 28 non‐carriers with fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET scans. Neuropsychological assessments were conducted at the Neuroscience Group of Antioquia, University of Antioquia, Colombia, and imaging data were acquired at the Massachusetts General Hospital, USA (Aβ PET, tau PET, and MRI) and Pablo Tobón Uribe Hospital, Colombia (FDG PET). Procedures were approved by the respective local institutional review boards. All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study procedures and were compensated.

Neuropsychological and MBI‐C assessments

Neuropsychological assessments were conducted in Spanish from May 2015 to June 2024. Global objective cognition was assessed via the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE, range: 0–30) 19 and memory performance was assessed using the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) Word List Delayed Recall (range: 0–10). 20 These measures were selected as the earliest indicators of objective cognitive changes in PSEN1 E280A carriers. 21 Self‐reported and study partner–reported subjective memory complaints were assessed using the Memory Complaint Scale (MCS; range: 0–45), 22 as a proxy of SCD. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS; range: 0–15). 23

The 34‐item self‐reported MBI‐C questionnaire was administered to evaluate presence (yes/no) and severity (range: 1–3) of NPSs across 5 domains, assessing persistent NPSs in the past 6 months. 8 MBI‐C domains include: (1) decreased motivation, (2) affective dysregulation, (3) impulse dyscontrol, (4) social inappropriateness, and (5) abnormal perception or thought. The MBI‐C total score was computed as the sum of the severity of all domains (range: 0–102). 8

Image acquisition and processing

Imaging studies were performed within 6 months of neuropsychological assessments. T1‐weighted MRI scans were acquired on a Siemens 3 Tesla Tim Trio scanner (repetition time = 2300 ms, echo time = 2.95 ms, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 1.05 × 1.05 × 1.2 mm). Images were processed using FreeSurfer v6 to parcellate cortical and subcortical regions of interest from the Desikan atlas. 24 To assess early regional atrophy, we used hippocampal volume normalized to the total intracranial volume. 25 Aβ PET and tau PET scans were acquired on a Siemens ECAT HR+ scanner (3D mode; image planes = 63; axial field of view = 15.2 cm; transaxial resolution = 5.6 mm; slice interval = 2.4 mm). 26 Aβ data were acquired with a 60‐minute dynamic protocol after an 8.5 to 15 mCi bolus injection of 11C‐Pittsburgh compound B (PiB; 12 × 15‐s+57 × 60‐second frames) and analyzed by the Logan reference method. Tau data were acquired 80 to 100 minutes after a 9.0 to 11.0 mCi bolus injection of 18F‐flortaucipir (FTP) in 4 × 5‐minute frames. FDG PET scans were acquired on a 64‐section Siemens Biograph mCT PET/computed tomography. 27 Intravenous injection of 5 mCi of FDG was administered 30 minutes before a dynamic emission scan of 6 × 5‐minute frames in ≥ 5‐hour fasting. PET processing included motion correction, frame summation, affine co‐registration to MRI, and image resampling using Desikan parcellations. Distribution volume ratio (DVR) for PiB data and standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) for FTP and FDG data were estimated after intensity normalization using cerebellar gray matter as the reference region and underwent partial volume correction. 28 To assess early neocortical Aβ accumulation, we computed the average PiB DVR in the frontal, lateral temporal, parietal, and retrosplenial cortices. 29 Early tau accumulation was measured as the average FTP SUVR in the medial temporal lobe (MTL), including the entorhinal, amygdala, parahippocampal, fusiform, inferior, and middle temporal regions. 30 Early glucose metabolism was assessed as precuneus FDG SUVR. 27

Statistical analysis

Demographics were compared between cognitively unimpaired PSEN1 E280A carriers and non‐carriers using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables and Pearson χ2 tests for categorical variables. Sub‐samples with imaging data available were compared to each other and to the main sample using standardized mean differences (SMDs). Group differences in MBI‐C total and domain scores were assessed using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests. We report test statistics (W) and rank‐biserial correlations (r rb) as measures of effect size. As a secondary analysis, we further grouped individuals into MBI+ and MBI– based on the MBI‐C total score cutoff of 8, validated in older clinical populations. 31 To examine associations between MBI‐C total scores (outcome) and demographics (predictors) across groups, we used linear regression models which included group interactions with demographic variables (age, years of education, sex, and GDS scores). To examine associations between MBI‐C total scores (predictor) and cognitive performance or imaging biomarkers (outcomes), we used linear regression models which included group by MBI‐C interactions. Individual models were computed for each cognitive measure (MMSE scores, Word List Delayed Recall, self‐reported and study partner–reported SCD scores) and each imaging biomarker (neocortical PiB DVR [Aβ burden], MTL FTP SUVR [tau burden], hippocampal volume, and precuneus FDG SUVR [glucose metabolism]). We report standardized main effects (βmain) of the predictors on the outcomes representing associations across the full sample and group interaction effects (βint) representing slope differences between groups. For statistically significant interactions, we performed post hoc analyses of estimated marginal trends (βtrend) indicating the association between predictor and outcome for each group. All models were adjusted for age, education, sex, and GDS scores, except for imaging biomarker models, whose small sample sizes precluded complex modelling. For each objective, Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple comparisons. Confidence intervals (95% CIs) and adjusted R 2 are reported for all statistics. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Sensitivity analysis of the statistical power attained with imaging sub‐samples was performed (Supplementary Methods in supporting information). All analyses were carried out with R (v4.1.1).

Differences in MBI‐C scores between PSEN1 E280A carriers and non‐carriers

First, we compared MBI‐C total and domain scores between groups. The MBI‐C total score was right‐skewed, with 18 carriers (22%) and 35 non‐carriers (31%) reporting no NPSs across MBI domains. We observed that cognitively unimpaired PSEN1 E280A carriers had higher MBI‐C total scores than non‐carriers (W = 3705, r rb = 0.19 [95% CI 0.05, 0.31], P = 0.009; Figure 1A). The median MBI‐C total score was 4.0 [interquartile range: 1.0, 12.5] in carriers and 2.0 [0.0, 6.0] in non‐carriers. As a secondary analysis, we observed significant differences in MBI positivity prevalence, with 20 (18%) non‐carriers and 28 (34%) carriers being MBI+ (χ2 = 6.83, p = 0.009).

Association between MBI‐C scores and demographic characteristics in PSEN1 E280A carriers and non‐carriers

Next, we explored the associations between MBI‐C total scores and demographics (age, years of education, sex, and GDS scores) in ADAD groups (Figure S1 and Table S1 in supporting information). MBI‐C total scores were not associated with age or years of education in PSEN1 E280A carriers or non‐carriers. Among carriers, MBI‐C total scores were higher in females than in males (W = 1117, r rb = 0.29 [95% CI 0.10, 0.47], P = 0.008), but no sex differences were found among non‐carriers (W = 1694, r rb = 0.206 [95% CI 0, 0.24], P = 0.524). Higher MBI‐C total scores were associated with higher GDS scores across the full sample (βmain = 5.40 [95% CI 3.94, 6.87], P < 0.001), with slope differences between groups (βint = 2.50 [95% CI 0.48, 4.53], P = 0.016, R 2 adj = 0.490). Post hoc analysis revealed that the association between MBI‐C scores and higher burden of depressive symptoms was stronger for carriers (βtrend = 7.91 [95% CI 6.46, 10.84], P < 0.001) than non‐carriers (βtrend = 5.40 [95% CI 3.94, 6.87], P < 0.001).

Association between MBI‐C scores and cognitive function in PSEN1 E280A carriers and non‐carriers

We further examined the associations between MBI‐C total scores and cognitive function in ADAD groups, including global cognition (MMSE scores), objective memory performance (Word List Delayed Recall) as well as self‐reported and study partner–reported SCD (MCS scores; Table S2 in supporting information). We observed no associations between MBI‐C and MMSE scores across the full sample (main effect; βmain = −0.10 [95% CI −0.35, 0.14], P = 0.405) or within groups (interaction effects; βint = 0.11 [95% CI −0.16, 0.39], P = 0.413, R 2 adj = 0.134; Figure 2A). Similarly, MBI‐C total scores were not associated with Word List Delayed Recall (βmain = −0.04 [95% CI −0.27, 0.19], P = 0.716; βint = 0.05 [95% CI −0.20, 0.31], P = 0.683, R 2 adj = 0.253; Figure 2B).

In contrast, self‐reported and study partner–reported MCS scores showed associations with MBI‐C scores across groups. Higher MBI‐C total scores were associated with higher burden of self‐reported SCD across the full sample (main effect; βmain = 0.25 [95% CI 0.02, 0.47], P = 0.030), but slope differences between groups were not significant (interaction effects; βint = −0.05 [95% CI −0.30, 0.20], P = 0.694, R 2 adj = 0.288; Figure 2C). Post hoc analysis revealed a positive correlation between MBI‐C and study partner–reported SCD scores in PSEN1 E280A carriers (βtrend = 0.28 [95% CI 0.07, 0.50], P = 0.008), but not in non‐carriers (βtrend = −0.04 [95% CI −0.28, 0.21], P = 0.774; Figure 2D).

Association between MBI‐C scores and imaging biomarkers in PSEN1 E280A carriers and non‐carriers

The demographic characteristics of the PET and MRI sub‐samples were comparable to each other and to the main sample (SMD < 0.4; Table S3 in supporting information). We finally assessed whether MBI‐C scores and imaging biomarkers of AD pathology and neurodegeneration were associated in these sub‐samples (Table S4 in supporting information). We observed that the association between neocortical PiB DVR and MBI‐C total scores was different between groups (βint = 0.50 [95% CI 0.08, 0.92], P = 0.020, R 2 adj = 0.595), with carriers showing a correlation between greater neocortical Aβ burden and higher MBI‐C total scores (βtrend = 0.46 [95% CI 0.17, 0.75], P = 0.003), but not non‐carriers (βtrend = −0.05 [95% CI −0.35, 0.26], P = 0.764; Figure 3A). Group differences trended toward a greater MTL tau burden in association with higher MBI‐C total scores in carriers, but the group interaction did not reach statistical significance (βint = 0.53 [95% CI −0.06, 1.11], P = 0.077, R 2 adj = 0.208; Figure 3B). Neither hippocampal volume (βint = 0.38 [95% CI −1.04, 0.28], P = 0.256, R 2 adj ≈ 0) nor precuneus metabolism (βint = 0.25 [95% CI −1.12, 0.62], P = 0.559, R 2 adj = 0.020) showed any associations with MBI‐C total scores across the full sample or within groups (Figure 3C, D).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we characterized MBI, as measured by the emerging MBI‐C, in cognitively unimpaired PSEN1 E280A mutation carriers and non‐carriers from the Colombian ADAD kindred. We observed that MBI‐C total scores were higher in mutation carriers compared to non‐carriers, primarily driven by symptoms of impulse dyscontrol. While MBI‐C scores were not linked to objective cognition, higher MBI‐C total scores were associated with greater burden of self‐reported subjective memory complaints across the full sample and with greater burden of study partner–reported complaints among carriers, independent of depressive symptoms. In a study sub‐sample, elevated MBI‐C total scores correlated with greater neocortical Aβ burden, but not with tau burden or markers of neurodegeneration.

Our findings showing higher MBI‐C scores in early‐onset ADAD build upon prior evidence showing that persistent NPSs meeting MBI criteria are associated with progression of cognitive decline. 10 Older individuals with concurrent MCI and MBI have a 3.5‐fold greater risk for incident dementia compared to those without MBI. 7 , 32 This pattern has been particularly observed in AD, 33 suggesting that MBI may serve as a sensitive at‐risk marker for AD. Our study confirms and expands upon this literature, by demonstrating that NPSs meeting MBI criteria manifest along the AD continuum, even in genetically determined forms of the disease. Of note, the MBI‐C was designed for individuals aged ≥ 50 to assess at‐risk states for late‐onset AD. 8 Given that our sample includes preclinical PSEN1 E280A carriers aged 30 (26, 33) years old who develop MCI by age 44 (43, 45), our MBI‐C assessment does not fully comply with the original criteria. Being the first to assess MBI in ADAD, this study calls for a revision of the age criteria in the MBI‐C to consider early‐onset cases. Additionally, an extensive characterization of stressors in early adulthood is necessary to evaluate potential modifications to the MBI‐C questionnaire in early‐onset AD, whose prevalence has been increasing worldwide. 34

Notably, cognitively unimpaired PSEN1 E280A carriers had higher MBI‐C scores compared to non‐carriers, suggesting that NPSs meeting MBI criteria may precede cognitive decline in this population with near‐certain progression to MCI. Differences in MBI‐C scores across groups were primarily driven by impulse dyscontrol, followed by affective dysregulation, social inappropriateness, and abnormal perception or thought. Symptoms of impulse dyscontrol are primarily characterized by agitation, aggression, irritability, frustration, stubbornness, and changes in eating behaviors. These findings are consistent with population‐based studies in older individuals showing that symptoms of impulse dyscontrol and affective dysregulation are the most frequently reported MBI domains in prodromal dementia. 31 , 35 In fact, agitation is present in 70% of patients with late‐onset AD and is under evaluation as a potential target for AD treatment. 36 Decreased motivation (e.g., depression and apathy) did not differ between carriers and non‐carriers, despite affecting 32% of older individuals with MCI. 37 In carriers, the first depressive symptoms begin at age 46, 38 indicating that other domains of the MBI‐C may be more sensitive to early behavioral changes in this population.

Among PSEN1 E280A carriers, MBI‐C scores were not associated with objective global cognition or memory performance, despite having lower MMSE scores and higher GDS scores than non‐carriers. This supports the idea that MBI and MCI reflect different pre‐dementia syndromes, with behavioral and cognitive symptoms emerging independently or sequentially in AD progression. 5 In turn, higher MBI‐C total scores were associated with higher burden of subjective memory complaints, a proxy of SCD, independent of clinical depression. Both groups showed a positive correlation between MBI‐C and self‐reported SCD scores, while the correlation with study partner–reported SCD scores was only observed in PSEN1 E280A carriers. It is important to consider that self‐reports are generally more unreliable than informant‐based reports of cognitive and behavioral data. 39 Even though carriers are cognitively unimpaired, their path toward cognitive decline is typically accompanied by a loss of self‐awareness. 22 , 40 The association observed in non‐carriers may be attributed to other factors such as personality traits or caregiving for a family member, as described previously in this cohort. 22 Contrarily, slope differences between carriers and non‐carriers can be attributed with great confidence to AD‐related mechanisms due to the high penetrance of the PSEN1 E280A mutation. In our cohort, study partner–reported SCD scores were higher in carriers than non‐carriers, ≈ 15 years before the estimated MCI onset. We also found that MBI‐C scores were higher in female than in male carriers, in line with a previous study showing that female carriers had greater SCD scores than male carriers. 40 We may postulate that, at this preclinical stage, MBI‐C scores are linked to subjective rather than objective cognitive decline, in line with literature in dementia‐free populations. 12 , 41 Indeed, 34% of individuals with SCD have been reported to have MBI. 6 MBI+SCD+, MBI+SCD–, and MBI–SCD+ individuals exhibit 8, 5, and 4 times greater odds of cognitive decline compared to MBI–SCD– individuals, respectively. 42 Thus, MBI has been conceptualized as an effect modifier for cognitive risk, conveying that the earlier MBI symptoms manifest, the greater its contribution to disease progression. Sex differences in the prevalence and perception of MBI domains should be carefully evaluated for an effective identification of individuals at risk of cognitive decline.

MBI has also been shown to predict progression to neuropathologically confirmed AD. 32 , 33 In line with this, we showed that higher MBI‐C total scores were associated with greater Aβ burden in regions of early accumulation. The association with MTL tau burden followed a similar trend but was not statistically significant. While these findings should be interpreted with caution due to limited sample sizes and different sub‐samples, they support a link to AD‐specific pathology previously investigated in older individuals. 13 , 15 , 16 In contrast to other studies linking MBI to neurodegeneration in non‐demented adults, 14 , 43 we did not observe correlations with glucose metabolism or hippocampal volume. It is possible that type II errors due to small imaging samples and low statistical power may lead to false negative correlations. Another explanation is that we are capturing an early stage of AD pathology in carriers. Neocortical Aβ typically starts to increase at age 28, whereas precuneus hypometabolism and hippocampal atrophy begin at ages 32 and 38, respectively. 27 , 44 It remains unclear whether MBI symptoms constitute downstream effects of early Aβ and tau deposition, which tend to occur in brain regions involved in behavioral regulation, 45 , 46 or act as risk factors that increase regional vulnerability to disease progression. 47

This study leverages a unique single‐mutation cohort of individuals from the Colombian ADAD kindred with a deterministic course of pathology. It provides valuable insight into the distribution of NPSs meeting MBI criteria in a relatively large sample of preclinical PSEN1 E280A carriers, while minimizing age‐related confounds. Although behavioral differences between early‐onset and late‐onset AD have yet to be systematically characterized, our study points to a potential overlap between pathology and clinical manifestations. However, it remains to be investigated which MBI‐C total score cutoffs are clinically significant and better discriminate risk of cognitive decline within PSEN1 E280A carriers. Longitudinal studies are critical for elucidating the progression of MBI‐C scores and MCI onset in carriers. Integrating complementary behavioral and cognitive assessments in combination with disease biomarkers in clinical practice could hold important implications for identifying at‐risk individuals for trial enrichment and targeted intervention plans. 48 Some limitations should be considered. First, its cross‐sectional design precludes predicting future cognitive and behavioral decline in this cohort, though this is partially offset by the high penetrance of the PSEN1 E280A mutation. Second, the small imaging sub‐samples limit statistical power to ascertain associations between MBI‐C and AD biomarkers. In this exploratory study, most associations did not hold statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. However, we were able to identify specific differences between groups throughout our study that lay the groundwork for future research in this population. Third, while both self‐reported and study partner–reported MBI‐C have been validated in older populations, the use of the self‐reported MBI‐C in individuals at risk of cognitive impairment may introduce response bias. 49 The loss of self‐awareness of cognitive and functional deficits has been linked to disease progression in late‐onset AD, 50 highlighting the need to explore study partner–reported MBI‐C questionnaires in this cohort. Fourth, we did not collect information on medication that could confound our findings, such as antidepressants, anxiolytics, or antipsychotics. Finally, the extent to which our findings generalize to late‐onset AD remains unclear.
