Canine epidermal growth factor-expressing Lactococcus lactis promotes intestinal injury repair and alleviates diarrhea in canine
Authors: Yinghui Gong, Huiqi Cui, Jinxin Zhang, Wen Feng, Yongjie Yuan, Ganzhen Deng, Yucheng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCanine diarrhea with long course and a certain mortality rate depends mainly on supportive treatment. Oral recombinant epidermal growth factor (EGF) can stimulate intestinal development and reduce diarrhea incidence in human and other animals, but few related studies have been done in canine.ResultsHere, we firstly constructed a recombinant lactococcus lactis (L. lactis) expressing bioactive canine EGF (cEGF), and then explored its effect on cell proliferation, intestinal injury repair and inflammatory factor secretion in diarrheal mice and canines. The results showed that cEGF promoted the proliferation of CMT-1211 cells, that cEGF-expressing L. lactis strain recovered the damaged mucous structure and suppressed the inflammatory response, potentially by activating EGF receptor and its downstream AKT and ERK1/2 signals, thereby alleviating diarrhea in mice and canine.ConclusionsWe first generated a cEGF-expressing L. lactis strain and demonstrated its potential to repair intestinal injury and alleviate diarrhea in mice and canines, and thus cEGF-expressing L. lactis could be a new therapy strategy for animal diarrhea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05297-y.Keywords: Lactococcus lactis, cEGF, Diarrhea, Canine

Background

Acute and chronic diarrhea caused by pathogens is one of the most common diseases in canines, but currently there is no effective treatment strategy [1]. Usually, antibiotics are applied to canine diarrhea, but they tend to damage the intestinal microorganism homeostasis and induce indigestion [2, 3]. Although probiotics can effectively treat diarrhea by expressing specific active proteins, they fail to recover the damaged intestinal mucosa. Epidermal growth factor (EGF) can bind to its receptor to activate the downstream Ras/MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, thus promoting the regeneration of epithelial cells after intestinal injury [4, 5]. Therefore, constructing recombinant probiotics expressing EGF may be a new strategy for the treatment of canine diarrhea.

Mouse EGF is a 53-amino acid single-chain polypeptide, and it was first isolated from submandibular glands [6] and later found in kidneys, liver, intestine, and mammary glands [7]. EGF can promote the repair of intestinal injury by enhancing the proliferation of intestinal stem cells and maintaining the intestinal barrier [5, 8]. Recombinant human EGF accelerates recovery from intestinal mucosal damage induced by irradiation or ischemia/reperfusion of mesenteric vessels in mice [9, 10]. Canine EGF (cEGF) is a 52-amino acid single-chain polypeptide, exhibiting a high similarity to feline EGF. EGF can promote the maturation of canine oocytes, and it is associated with the canine mammary tumorigenesis [11, 12]. However, the effects of cEGF on canine diarrhea remain largely unclear.

Lactococcus lactis (L. lactis) widely used in food industry is metabolically active in gastrointestine [13], and it can effectively secrete biologically active proteins [14]. EGF-expressing L. lactis enhances growth performance in newly-weaned piglets and their intestinal development [15, 16]. However, few reports are available on the role of cEGF-expressing L. lactis in repairing intestinal mucosal injury and relieving diarrhea in canine. Here, we first generated a recombinant L. lactis highly expressing cEGF, and then explored its effects on the development and repair of injured intestinal mucosa, as well as its anti-inflammatory effects in diarrheal mice and canines. Overall, this study revealed the clinical value of cEGF- expressing L. lactis in diarrheal canines.

Results

Expression of cEGF protein in L. lactis

The cEGF cDNA was cloned into pNZ8149 plasmid to generate recombinant plasmid pNZ8149-cEGF. After the sequence identity was confirmed, pNZ8149-cEGF was transformed into L. lactis to obtain L. lactis containing pNZ8149-cEGF (LL-cEGF). To determine whether LL-cEGF could secrete cEGF, 10 ng/mL nisin was used to induce cEGF expression in LL-cEGF strain for 12 h. The cEGF protein was detected in LL-cEGF culture supernatant by Western blot (Fig. 1A), indicating that LL-cEGF strain could secrete cEGF protein. To determine the optimal induction concentration and induction time of nisin, the expression of cEGF in the supernatant of the LL-cEGF fermentation product were investigated at different concentrations of nisin (0 ,1,2 ,5, 10, 20, 40 and 80 ng/mL) for 20 h, and different induction durations (5,10,15,20,25 and 30 h) at 5 ng/mL. The Western bolt assay results showed that the expression of cEGF was the highest when the nisin was 10 ng/mL and the induction time was 20 h (Fig. 1B and C). Therefore, 10 ng/mL and 20 h were determined as the optimal induction concentration and induction duration, respectively.

cEGF promotes CMT-1211 cell proliferation

Due to the lack of post-translational modification ability, prokaryotic cells tend to exhibit lower biological activity of EGF expression than eukaryotic, and EGF performs its biological function by promoting cell proliferation [14]. To investigate the biological activity of cEGF secreted by LL-cEGF, cEGF protein obtained from the supernatant of LL-cEGF was purified (Fig. 2A). After being treated with purified cEGF protein, the proliferative ability of CMT-1211 cells was detected. The CCK8 assay demonstrated that the treatments of cEGF at the concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 µg/mL significantly enhanced CMT-1211 cell proliferation, with 4 µg/mL cEGF treatment exhibiting the strongest enhancement effect (Fig. 2B), indicating biological activity of the cEGF secreted by LL-cEGF.

Significant difference was presented at the level of *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01 by two-tailed Student’s t test

cEGF alleviates DSS-induced intestinal damage and diarrhea in mice

EGF plays a crucial role in safeguarding the integrity of the intestinal barrier and fostering the proliferation of intestinal cells [5]. To investigate the effect of cEGF on intestinal injury repair, we induced mouse intestinal injury and diarrhea with DSS, and then treated mice with LL-vector and LL-cEGF fermentation product added with nisin. The results showed that DSS treatment resulted in weight loss in mice, but the following administration of LL-cEGF facilitated weight recovery (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, LL-cEGF treatment mitigated DSS-induced diarrhea in mice (Fig. 3B). Histological observations showed that DSS induced the congestion in the duodenum and colon submucosal layer, which was characterized by mild hemorrhage and inflammatory infiltration of polymorphonuclear and mononuclear leukocytes to varying degrees in hyperemic regions, but LL-cEGF treatment attenuated these symptoms on day 7 (Fig. 3C and D) and day 14 (Fig. 3E and F). Additionally, we also observed that LL-cEGF treatment significantly reduced the levels of serum inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumour necrosis factor α (TNF-α) (Fig. 3G-I). The above results indicated the potential of cEGF to diminish the DSS-induced inflammatory response and intestinal damage in mice.

cEGF alleviates FSE-induced intestinal damage and diarrhea in canines

Folium sennae extracts (FSE) has been documented to induce diarrhea in mice [17]. To further explore the effect of cEGF on canine diarrhea, FSE was used to induce canine diarrhea, and then canines were fed with lyophilized samples containing nisin and LL-vector/LL-cEGF fermentation product. The results showed that FSE successfully induced diarrhea in canines, and LL-cEGF treatment obviously ameliorated diarrhea in canines (Fig. 4A). Histological analysis revealed that FSE led to shorter intestinal villi and inflammation, but LL-cEGF treatment promoted intestinal villus growth and mitigated the inflammatory response (Fig. 4B and C), as evidenced by the reduced levels of serum inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 4D-F). EGF has been reported to activate downstream Ras/MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways by promoting EGFR phosphorylation, thereby facilitating the regeneration of epithelial cells after intestinal injury [4]. In this study, we found that LL-cEGF promoted EGFR phosphorylation, thus enhancing the phosphorylation of its downstream targets AKT and ERK1/2 (Fig. 4G). The above results suggested that cEGF might facilitate intestinal repair and alleviate diarrhea by activating EGFR signaling in canine.

Discussion

Pathogenic microorganisms may induce intestinal damage, thus resulting in diarrhea in canines. EGF plays a pivotal role in promoting intestinal injury repair, meanwhile maintaining intestinal homeostasis [5]. In this study, we used nisin to induce cEGF expression in LL-cEGF strain, and optimized nisin induction concentration and induction duration. We found that with the nisin concentration increasing, the expression of cEGF increased, but it decreased when nisin concentration exceeded 10 ng/mL, which might be due to the antibacterial effect of nisin, namely, inhibitory effect of high-concentration nisin on L. lactis growth [18]. With the extension of induction time, the cEGF expression peaked at 20 h, and then it decreased. The possible reason might be that lactic acid produced in large amount due to L. lactis metabolism inhibited its own growth [19], and that extracellular protease secreted by L. lactis degraded cEGF [20].

Due to the lack of a post-translational modification mechanism in the prokaryotic expression system, some eukaryotic proteins expressed by prokaryotes have been reported to lack biological activity [21]. However, in this study, cEGF secreted by LL-cEGF strain was found to promote the proliferation of CMT-1211 cells, indicating the biological activity of cEGF. The different findings in these two studies might be because the simple structure of EGF has relatively low demand on post-translational modification. Despite differences in the amino acid sequence of EGF among species, the EGF can exert functions across species. For instance, human EGF has been shown to function in rats, and mouse EGF in pigs, which might be due to the highly conserved functional domain of EGF receptor (EGFR) [22]. This study revealed that cEGF alleviated DSS-induced intestinal damage in mice, which might be because cEGF promoted intestinal stem cell proliferation. Additionally, LL-vector also partially alleviated intestinal damage, suggesting that some components of LL-vector fermentation products promoted intestinal injury repair. Our data showed that cEGF reduced inflammation in mice, which might be associated with its ability to maintain intestinal homeostasis and reduce the entry of lipopolysaccharide from the intestine into the bloodstream [23].

Lyophilization emerges as an effective method to maintain bacterium and protein activities. Our results showed that lyophilized LL-cEGF alleviated FSE-induced canine diarrhea and reduced the levels of inflammatory cytokines in diarrheal canines. This alleviation effect might be attributed to intestinal epithelial cell proliferation induced by cEGF. In this study, LL-cEGF was found to activate EGFR, thus promoting the activation of downstream AKT and ERK1/2 signals. This finding is consistent with one previous report that the EGF functions by binding to EGFR, thus leading to autophosphorylation of receptor tyrosine kinase and subsequent activation of downstream signaling pathways including the PI3K/AKT and ERK1/2, eventually maintaining intestinal homeostasis [5].

Conclusion

We constructed a recombinant strain of cEGF-expressing L. lactis, and this strain exhibited a potential to promote intestinal injury repair and alleviate diarrhea in mice and canines. Our findings suggest that administering LL-cEGF may be a novel therapy strategy to mitigate animal diarrhea.

Methods

Bacterial strain culture

L. lactis NZ3900 (NIZO Food Research B.V., Ede, The Netherlands) was incubated in M17 medium (Difco Laboratories, England) supplemented with 0.5% glucose at 30 ℃ without vibration.

Construction of recombinant strain of L. lactis expressing cEGF

The sequence of the mature cEGF peptide was derived by aligning the amino acid sequence of the pro-cEGF (NCBI Reference Sequence: NP_001001292.1). The cEGF and signal peptide usp45 genes were fused, with their codons optimized, by Tsingke Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Fusion gene fragment carrying the NcoI/SacI restriction sites and a 6× His-tag was digested with NcoI/SacI restriction enzymes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and inserted into the plasmid pNZ8149 to construct the recombinant plasmid pNZ8149-cEGF. The obtained pNZ8149-cEGF was transformed into the competent cells of L. lactis NZ3900 by electroporation (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Electroporated bacteria were smeared onto Elliker medium plates and cultured at 30 ℃ for 36 h. Colonies L. lactis carrying pNZ8149-cEGF (LL-cEGF) were selected according to the appearance of surrounding yellow zones (signifying lactose utilization capacity of strain) and sequenced (Tsingke Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Expression and purification of recombinant cEGF protein in Lactococcus lactis

The LL-cEGF strain was incubated in M17 medium until optical density (OD) reached 0.4, and cEGF exocytosis secretion was induced with the nisin (Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd., St Louis, MO, USA) at the indicated concentration for indicated time at 30 ℃ without vibration to determine the optimal induction conditions. The secreted cEGF was verified by western blotting.

The cEGF samples were prepared from the 300 mL of the LL-cEGF culture under the optimized conditions (20 h induction, with 10 ng/mL nisin, at 30 ℃ without vibration), and centrifuged at 7500 g for 20 min at 4 ℃ to obtain supernatants. The supernatants were added with ammonium sulfate (80%, w/v), automatically stirred for 2 h at 4 ℃, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 30 min at 4 ℃. After centrifugation, the precipitates were harvested and dissolved in a binding buffer containing 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Na3PO4, and 30 mM imidazole (pH = 7.4) and dialyzed overnight using a 1.0-kDa cut-off membrane in the renewed buffer at 4 ℃, centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min, and filtered with a 0.45-µm filter to remove insoluble debris. Then, the supernatant was loaded onto a 1-mL HiTrapHP chelating column (Cytiva, 17 524 701) for binding reaction. Afterwards, the protein was eluted from the column with the buffer solution (pH 7.8) containing 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tri-HCl and 250 mM imidazole. Then, the protein was concentrated with Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters (Millipore). The size and purity of the purified proteins were detected by SDS–PAGE with Coomassie blue staining.

Serum cytokines assay

Serum was collected from blood of mice and canines by centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The levels of serum TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were analyzed using ELISA kit (Shanghai Bogoo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blotting

The L. lactis culture solution was centrifuged at 7500 g and 4 °C for 15 min to harvest supernatant, with the cell pellets discarded. To inhibit proteolysis in the supernatant, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride (PMSF, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were added. Protein concentrations of L. lactis supernatant were determined by BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0011) before western blotting to ensure consistent protein concentrations in all the samples. Canine colon tissues were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, P8340) and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, P2850) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lysates were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min and loaded onto SDS–PAGE gel membrane to separate protein. The separated protein was transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the membranes were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (Abbkine, BMU102-CN). The primary antibodies included antibodies against FLAG (#AE092) and GAPDH (#A19056) from Abclonal (Wuhan, China), and those against phospho-EGFR (Tyr1068, #11862), EGFR (#2523), phospho-AKT (Ser473, #4060), AKT (#4685), phospho-Erk1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204, #4370), and Erk1/2 (#4696) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA).

Diarrhea model of mice and canines

All the animal experiments followed the Huazhong Agricultural University Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (HZAUMO-2015-12).

Male ICR mice (6–7 weeks old) were obtained from Huazhong Agricultural University Laboratory Animal Centre, and they were reared in a pathogen-free animal facility under a standard 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, with standard rodent chow and water ad libitum. The mouse diarrhea was induced by dextran sulphate sodium salt (DSS) water (MP biochemicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA). The 5% DSS was dissolved in drinking water and supplied to mice for 3 days, with drinking water containing no DSS as control. The LL-vector and LL-cEGF strain were incubated in M17 medium until OD reached 0.4. Afterwards, 10 ng/mL nisin was used to induce the cEGF expression of LL-cEGF strain at 30 °C without vibration for 20 h, with LL-vector as control. The 24 mice were randomly divided into the following 4 groups (6 mice/group). The control group (healthy mice) was gavaged with only M17 medium; For diarrhea group, diarrhea was first induced by DSS, and then it was gavaged with only M17 medium; LL-vector treatment group was subjected to DSS diarrhea induction and treatment with LL-vector fermentation product; and LL-cEGF treatment group underwent DSS diarrhea induction and treatment with LL-cEGF fermentation product (at 2.41 µg/mL cEGF concentration). All the mice in four groups were orally garaged with 0.1 mL respective treatment agent once a day. The mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 100 uL of pentobarbital sodium, and then sacrificed by the physical method of cervical spine fracture. Half mice (3 mice) in each group were sacrificed on day 7, and another half (the remaining 3 mice) were sacrificed on day 14 after treatment. Their tissue and serum were collected immediately after their sacrifice.

Male beagle canine (12 months) was obtained from Hubei Yizhicheng Biotechnology CO., LTD and reared in a kennel under a standard 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle with standard canine chow and water ad libitum. Canine diarrhea was induced with Folium sennae extracts (FSE) which were prepared by previously reported method [24]. Briefly, 1 kg Folium sennae was washed in cold water, and naturally dried, and soaked in 10 L boiling water for 10 min. The soak solution was filtered through 0.45 μm filter membrane. Then the filtrate was evaporated, concentrated into 1 L (1 g/mL crude drug) in a 70 °C rotary evaporator, and preserved at 4 °C. The 2 mL/kg FSE was gavaged to canines to induce the diarrhea. The 10 ng/mL nisin was used to induce LL-vector or LL-cEGF fermentation, and fermentation products were freeze-dried with skim milk, with the content of L. lactis being 109 CFU/g. The 16 canines were randomly divided into the following 4 groups (4 canines/group) and fed with 10 g lyophilizated samples per day. Specifically, the control group was fed with 10 g lyophilizated samples containing L. lactis-free M17 medium, nisin, and skim milk; For the diarrhea group, diarrhea was first induced FSE, and then the canines were fed with 10 g lyophilizated samples containing L. lactis-free M17 medium, nisin, and skim milk; LL-vector treatment group was subjected to diarrheal induction and treatment with 10 g lyophilizated samples containing LL-vector fermentation products, nisin, and skim milk; LL-cEGF group underwent diarrheal induction and treatment with 10 g lyophilizated samples containing LL-cEGF fermentation product (cEGF 25.3 µg), nisin, and skim milk. The canines are euthanized using high doses of the anesthetic sodium thiopental on day 14 post treatment. Their tissue and serum were collected immediately after canine euthanization.

H&E staining

The duodenum and colon samples of mice and canines were fixed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 4% formalin, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Intestinal sections were stained with H&E (Beyotime, C0105S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell proliferation assay

CMT-1211 cells (canine breast cancer cell line) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Hyclone, sv30010) in 96-well plates in a incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C overnight. When cell confluence reached 60%, cells were cultured in DMEM containing cEGF (0, 2, 4, and 8 µg/mL) for 12 h, with the control group cultured in DMEM containing no cEGF. After 12-h treatment, the cell culture was added with to 10 µL Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8, Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc, Rockville, MD, USA) and incubated for 1 h. The OD450 of the cell culture was measured using a MULTISKAN MK3 (Thermo Electron Corporation, Waltham, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times. Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 6 software. The data were expressed as means ± SD. Student’s two-tailed t-test (unpaired) was used to determine statistical significance differences between groups. Statistical significance was presented at the level of *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.
