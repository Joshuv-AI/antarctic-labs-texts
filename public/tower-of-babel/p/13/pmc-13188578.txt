The effect of augmented feedback with velocity-based training on post-activation performance enhancement in college soccer players
Authors: Ziren Zhao, Xin Zheng, Yuanhang Li, Tao Zhong, Jiawei Tian, Kaixiang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effects of visual augmented feedback with velocity-based training (VBT) on post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) in college soccer players.MethodsEighteen male college soccer players participated in a crossover trial. They were randomly assigned to receive three resistance activation protocols (85% of 1RM barbell squats) to induce PAPE. The three activation protocols consisted of a visual feedback protocol based on 5%VL (VF5%VL), a fixed 12-repetition visual feedback protocol (VF12reps.), and a fixed 12-repetition protocol without visual feedback (NVF12reps.). We assessed the PAPE using 20-meter sprint performance metrics (5-meter and 20-meter sprint times, maximum speed, maximum acceleration) at 4, 8, 12, and 16 min following barbell squat activation in soccer players. We analyzed those metrics using two-way repeated measures ANOVA and calculated the effect size). Introduction

Optimizing athletic performance, particularly in sports that demand repeated sprints, such as soccer, remains a core focus of sports science research and training practice. Post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) is a transient improvement in muscular performance following a high-intensity conditioning activity (e.g., heavy barbell squats). It has emerged as a promising strategy to optimize acute athletic performance, such as sprinting, jumping, and change of direction [1–6]. This phenomenon is attributed to neural potentiation, as well as to factors such as increased muscle temperature, changes in muscle-tendon stiffness, and heightened arousal levels [1]. The conventional PAPE activation protocol involves athletes performing 1–2 sets of heavy resistance exercises (e.g., ≧85% of 1RM squats), followed by a 4-5-minute rest before repeated vertical jump or sprint tests. For example, one study found that 10 single repetitions at 90% 1RM in the squat effectively improved 10- and 30-meter sprint performance in team-sport athletes (basketball, volleyball, handball, and soccer players) [7]. The conventional PAPE activation protocol typically uses fixed-load settings based on percentages of the one-repetition maximum (1RM). However, this protocol does not adequately account for individual differences and variations in daily performance. Athletes can exhibit significant differences in neuromuscular responses even when lifting the same loads, compromising the consistency of activation results [8]. Additionally, the conventional PAPE activation protocol does not allow for real-time monitoring of the training process, failing to reflect the athlete’s daily neuromuscular status dynamically [9]. As a result, some athletes may experience insufficient stimulation, while others may face excessive fatigue. More importantly, repeated activation under high-load conditions could impose considerable physiological stress. This accumulated fatigue may undermine or obscure the expected performance gains when applied before a competition.

Velocity-based training (VBT) offers potential advantages over the conventional PAPE activation protocol by dynamically adjusting load in real time based on movement velocity. It addresses individual differences; controls fatigue through the velocity loss (VL) threshold and provides immediate visual augmented feedback to enhance athletes’ effort levels and neural drive [9–13]. The VL is commonly used in VBT as a metric of neuromuscular fatigue and is defined as the percentage reduction in movement velocity from the fastest to the slowest repetition within a single set of resistance training (e.g., 85%1RM barbell squats) [10, 11, 14]. Studies have shown that a 6-week VBT protocol offers significant advantages in enhancing athletes’ rapid strength and explosive power compared to conventional resistance training [15–18]. A meta-analysis has also shown that lower VL thresholds (e.g., 5% VL) are superior for jumping, sprinting, and velocity against submaximal loads performance [14]. Additionally, a review has suggested that 5–20%VL may be the optimal threshold for developing explosive strength in athletes [9]. Yuan et al. (2023) first compared the effects of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% VL activation protocols on PAPE in sprinters, finding that 5% VL was the optimal threshold for enhancing repeated countermovement jump (CMJ) performance [19]. Despite evidence that VBT enhances load adjustments, controls fatigue, and offers real-time feedback, no studies have directly compared VBT to the conventional PAPE activation protocol. This gap needs to be addressed to assess whether VBT is more effective at enhancing PAPE and optimizing the load threshold.

This study aims to investigate the acute effects of three PAPE activation protocols on the repeated 20-meter sprint performance of soccer players, using a randomized crossover design. The protocols included a visual feedback method based on 5% VL, a fixed 12-repetition visual feedback approach, and a fixed 12-repetition protocol without visual feedback (standard PAPE activation). The hypothesis was that resistance activation with visual feedback would significantly improve players’ repeated 20-meter sprint performance compared to no-feedback conditions.

Methods

This study included 18 male college soccer players with the following characteristics (Mean ± SD): age: 21.7 ± 1.2years; body weight: 76.2 ± 7.5 kg; body height: 183.1 ± 6.8 cm; body fat: 13.0 ± 4.6%; 1RM squat: 135.8 ± 7.2 kg (relative strength ratio: 1.79 ± 0.16). All participants were healthy, medication-free, non-smokers, not taking any dietary supplements, and maintained the habit of performing resistance training at least three times a week. Procedures

This study utilized a randomized crossover design to assess the PAPE effect of VBT on 20-meter repeated sprints performance. Visual augmented feedback was provided in real time via a Chronojump linear position transducer and software on a laptop screen about 1.5–2.0 m in front of the participant at eye level. The feedback included (1) the mean concentric velocity (m/s) for each repetition, shown after each, and (2) the velocity-loss percentage (%) relative to the fastest repetition in the set. VBT consisted of two types of visual feedback (VF), one providing participants with the mean concentric velocity during a 6-repetition (2 sets) barbell squat (protocol 1 = VF12reps.), and the other using the 5% velocity loss (VL) of mean concentric velocity (protocol 2 = VF5%VL) as the visual feedback signal. Participants in the control protocol (protocol 3 = NVF12reps.) performed a conventional barbell squat with no feedback. The experimental protocol consisted of a process familiarization, a one-repetition maximum squat test, and three sessions with a 72-hour washout period. The participants were informed about the experimental procedure and signed an informed consent form before the 1RM squat test. During the experiment, participants were instructed to avoid caffeinated beverages, such as coffee or tea, and other substances that may affect physiological and perceptual outcomes. Participants were also instructed to avoid high-intensity physical activity during the experiment. To avoid environmental and diurnal variations, the intervention and tests were performed in an indoor physical training center (indoor temperature 24◦C) between 13:30 and 16:30. The first session proceeded 48 h after the 1RM test. In this session, participants completed each task scheduled in the experimental research protocol (Fig. 1). The order of the protocols was determined by a random draw in which an equal number of three-color strips (red, yellow, and green) representing VF12reps., VF5%VL, or NVF12reps. were drawn first. Participants drew one strip in a blinded status to determine the type of training. Then, after the first training was completed, the draw was repeated to determine the next training type. Coaches instructed participants to consume the same diet throughout the study without making any dietary changes. Participants ate together in the training center cafeteria to ensure they received the same food.

Anthropometric measurement

Participants were anthropometrically measured after familiarization with the process. Body height and body mass were measured using a digital weighing scale DSZN-M-101 A (Dashu Intelligent Technology Co., Tianjin, China). Percent body fat was determined using bioimpedance analysis (Inbody770, South Korea).

One repetition maximum test (1RM test)

Prior to the 1RM test, participants engaged in a 10-minute warm-up on a cycle ergometer, followed by 10 min of dynamic stretching of the lower limbs, and a set of 8–12 repetitions of a free barbell squat at an anticipated weight equivalent to 50% of their 1RM. After a 3-minute rest, participants performed four repetitions at 70–75% of the anticipated 1RM, followed by another 3-minute rest and 2–3 additional repetitions at 85–90% of the anticipated 1RM. Following a 4-minute rest period, the information gleaned from the third set was utilized to determine the final weight of each individual’s 1RM [20]. Participants were required to perform each barbell squat in a 3/0/x/0 movement tempo following a movement rhythmized 3/0/x/0, which denotes a 3-s eccentric phase, no intentional isometric pauses during the transition phase, a concentric phase as fast as possible, and no pause between the completion of the concentric phase and the beginning (eccentric phase) of the next repetition.

All 1RM testing and squat activation sessions were supervised by two qualified strength coaches. Trials were stopped immediately if any pain, dizziness, abnormal symptoms, or loss of proper technique occurred, or upon participant request. Adverse events (AEs) were defined as any injury or symptom occurring during or after testing or training that required cessation of the session, medical attention, or resulted in missed participation.

Barbell squat activation protocol

Prior to barbell squat activation, all participants performed a 10-minute warm-up on a cycle ergometer at 60 rpm, followed by 10 min of dynamic stretching, and one set of barbell squats (6–8 repetitions at 50%1RM) with a 5-minute rest. After the warm-up, participants completed a 20-meter sprint as a baseline. Following a 5-minute rest, participants completed a set of six barbell squats with a load of 85%1RM, resting one minute between sets [19, 21]. Two strength coaches stood at each end of the barbell to ensure that the participants completed each squat correctly and safely. The barbell squat training was performed in a squat rack (Technogym, Shanghai, China). The concentric velocity and number of repetitions were measured by a linear position transducer (Linear encoder kit, Chronojump, Spanish). The linear position transducer comprises a floor unit with a spring-powered, retractable, wound-on a cylindrical spool, coupled to the shaft of an optical encoder [22]. The floor unit is placed on the floor perpendicular to the right collar of the barbell. The other end of the cable was attached vertically to the barbell (immediately proximal to the right collar) with a Velcro strap for data acquisition [23]. Previous studies have shown the linear position transducer to be reliable for measuring velocity during resistance training [23–26]. Post-activation performance enhancement test

Previous studies have shown that the 20-meter repeated-sprint test is a routine method for assessing PAPE in athletes [19, 27]. In this study, participants performed a 20-meter sprint on the track in a standing position after warm-up (baseline) and at the 4th, 8th, 12th, and 16th minutes after barbell squat activation. To minimize fatigue and preserve the time course of PAPE, only one sprint trial was performed at each time point. The 5-m acceleration time was derived from the same 20-m sprint trial. The 5-meter acceleration time, 20-meter sprint time, maximal speed (Vmax), and maximal acceleration (Amax) during 20-meter repeated sprints were measured using a friction encoder Race Analyzer (Chronojump, Barcelona, Spain), with a distance accuracy of 3 cm and a temporal accuracy of 4 microseconds. The device was located on a 1-meter -high tripod placed behind the participants. Height was regulated with a tripod, which allowed the cable to stay parallel to the ground. Then, participants were positioned into a belt attached to the friction encoder and received standardized verbal encouragement (same investigator, wording, and timing) across all sessions and conditions to ensure maximal effort. Data were analyzed using the manufacturer software (Chronojump-Boscosystem). Previous studies have shown the RaceAnalyzer to be a reliable device for measuring the kinematic parameters of short-distance sprints [28–30] (Table 3).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Data normality was tested by the Shapiro-Wilk test and the visual inspection of the box plot to observe the presence of outliers. The effect of barbell squat activation on PAPE (5-meter acceleration time, 20-meter sprint time, maximal speed, and maximal acceleration) was analyzed using a two-way (time × protocol) repeated-measures ANOVA, with post hoc analyses using the Least Significant Difference test. Repeated measurement data needed to satisfy Mauchly’s test (p > 0.05); when repeated measurement data were violated (p ≤ 0.05), an epsilon (ε)correction was required. If no interaction between time and protocol was observed, the data were analyzed for main effect, and then the effect size [partial eta-squared ()] was calculated, where effect size was categorized as trivial (< 0.01), small (0.01 ≤< 0.06), moderate (0.06 ≤< 0.14), and large (≥ 0.14) effects [31]. The smallest worthwhile change (SWC) for 5-meter time, 20-meter time, maximal speed, and maximal acceleration in five 20-m sprints were calculated for the control protocol (NVF12reps.). SWC is the smallest performance improvement that is practically meaningful and worth pursuing for the experimental protocol (VF5%VL and VF12reps.), as opposed to insignificant changes that may not have a noticeable impact [32, 33]. To compute the SWC, we calculate the between-subject standard deviation (SD) and multiply this number by 0.2 [32]. We defined meaningful performance enhancement as a change value ≥ SWC in the experimental protocol (VF5%VL and VF12reps.). We also calculated the within-protocol coefficient of variation (CV) for assessing the reliability of the 20-meter repeated sprint test. When the CV ≤ 5%, it is considered acceptable; otherwise, the test may exhibit significant systematic bias [32]. Differences in means were also expressed using 95% confidence intervals (CI). For all tests, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using the SPSS statistical package (version 25.0, IBM statistics, Chicago, IL).

Results

No adverse events were reported during 1RM testing, squat activation, or sprint testing. All data were normally distributed, and there was no time × protocol interactions for each of the outcomes (p > 0.05). Additionally, all data satisfied Mauchly’s test of Sphericity or Epsilon correction (p > 0.05). CVs were < 5% in all protocols. The statistical results were shown in Table 2.

5-meter acceleration

The SWC of 5-meter acceleration time in the NVF12reps. protocol was 0.01 s. The time effect showed that the VF5%VL, VF12reps., and NVF12reps. protocols all significantly reduced the 5-meter acceleration times of participants compared to baseline data (F = 11.489, p < 0.001,= 0.403, large effect). The protocol effect showed significant differences between the VF5%VL, VF12reps., and NVF12reps. protocols in reducing 5-meter acceleration times of participants (F = 4.553, p = 0.037,= 0.211, large effect). Pairwise comparisons revealed that there was a mean reduction of 0.04 s(> SWC = 0.01 s) in VF5%VL compared to NVF12reps. (p = 0.019, 95%CI: -0.067~-0.007), and a mean reduction of 0.03 s(> SWC = 0.01 s) in VF12reps. compared to NVF12reps. (p = 0.067, 95%CI: -0.069~0.003); however, there were no significant differences between the VF5%VL and VF12reps. protocols (mean difference = 0.004 s, p = 0.616, 95%CI: -0.019~0.011) (Fig. 2).

20-meter sprint

The SWC of the 20-meter sprint time in the NVF12reps. protocol was 0.01 s. The time effect showed that the VF5%VL, VF12reps., and NVF12reps. protocols all significantly reduced the 20 m sprint times of participants compared to baseline data (F = 24.781, p < 0.001,= 0.59, large effect). The protocol effect showed that there were no significant differences between the VF5%VL, VF12reps., and NVF12reps. protocols in reducing the 20-meter sprint time of participants (F = 1.578, p = 0.221,= 0.09, moderate effect). Pairwise comparisons revealed that there was a mean reduction of 0.03 s(> SWC = 0.01 s) in VF5%VL compared to NVF12reps. (p = 0.086, 95%CI: -0.066~0.005), and a mean reduction of 0.02 s(> SWC = 0.01 s) in VF12reps. compared to NVF12reps. (p = 0.305, 95%CI: -0.063~0.021); and the time in the VF5%VL was marginally lower than that in the VF12reps. protocol (mean difference = 0.01 s, p = 0.555, 95%CI: -0.044~0.024).

Maximum speed

The SWC of Vmax in the NVF12reps. protocol was 0.03 m/s. The time effect showed that there were no significant increases in participant 20-meter sprint maximal speed in the VF5%VL, VF12reps., and NVF12reps. protocols compared to baseline data. (F = 0.644, p = 0.633,= 0.04, small effect). The protocol effect showed that there were no significant differences between the VF5%VL, VF12reps., and NVF12reps. protocols in increasing the 20-meter maximum speed of participants (F = 0.996, p = 0.380,= 0.06, moderate effect). Pairwise comparisons revealed that there was a mean enhancement of 0.05 m/s (> SWC = 0.03 m/s) in VF5%VL compared to NVF12reps. (p = 0.301, 95%CI: -0.046~0.141), and a mean enhancement of 0.05 m/s(> SWC = 0.03 m/s) in VF12reps. compared to NVF12reps. (p = 0.159, 95%CI: -0.021~0.121); but there were no significant differences between the VF5%VL and VF12reps. protocols (mean difference = 0.002 m/s, p = 0.959, 95%CI: -0.087~0.083).

Maximum acceleration

The SWC of maximum acceleration in the NVF12reps. protocol was 0.05 m/s2. The time effect showed that the VF5%VL, VF12reps., and NVF12reps. protocols significantly increased the 20 m sprint acceleration of participants compared to baseline data (F = 8.949, p < 0.001,= 0.35, large effect). The protocol effect showed significant differences between the VF5%VL, VF12reps., and NVF12reps. protocols in increasing maximum acceleration of participants (F = 3.680, p = 0.036,= 0.178, large effect). Pairwise comparisons revealed that there was a mean enhancement of 0.28 m/s2 (> SWC = 0.05 m/s2) in VF5%VL compared to NVF12reps. (p = 0.018, 95%CI: 0.053~0.500), and a mean enhancement of 0.13 m/s2 (> SWC = 0.05 m/s2) in VF12reps. compared to NVF12reps. (p = 0.241, 95%CI: -0.095~0.351); but there were no significant differences between the VF5%VL and VF12reps. protocols (mean difference = 0.15 m/s2, p = 0.133, 95%CI: -0.50 ~ 0.346).

Discussion

The findings of this study offer valuable insights into the role of visually augmented feedback VBT in PAPE for soccer players. The significant improvement observed in 5-meter acceleration and maximum acceleration indicates that visual augmented feedback VBT may enhance the neuromuscular adaptations necessary for explosive initial sprint performance, which is critical in soccer match play, where quick accelerations can be decisive. However, the lack of significant improvement in 20-meter sprint times and maximum speed suggests that the benefits of augmented feedback VBT may be more applicable to sprint start acceleration rather than to maximal sprinting speed or longer sprint performance.

These results are consistent with recent studies indicating that visual stimuli enhance acute athletic performance [34–36]. Several factors could explain this observation. First, visual feedback enhances motor cortex excitability, and increased motor-related cortical oscillations (e.g., µ-rhythms) observed through EEG are directly linked to improved force output and speed [37]. Second, providing visual feedback to participants significantly increased both motor output and neural signal complexity, suggesting enhanced motor adaptation and greater resistance to interference [1, 38]. The benefits of these visual signals may have enabled participants to strengthen nerve impulses, improve motor unit recruitment, and facilitate the transformation of inorganic phosphagen, subsequently influencing post-activation potentiation [39, 40]. Participants in the VF5%VL and VF12reps. protocols completed the barbell squat at a faster concentric contraction velocity than those in the NVF12reps. protocol. An increase in concentric contraction velocity may be more favorable for myosin regulatory light chain (MRLC) phosphorylation, thereby increasing the rate of force development between maximal contraction velocity and maximal load [39]. This finding emphasizes the potential of visual augmented feedback VBT to improve neuromuscular efficiency during the initial phases of sprint acceleration. During this phase, explosive strength, rate of force development, and effective ground force application are crucial. Research indicates that real-time visual augmented feedback can enhance movement execution and training adaptations by reinforcing optimal motor strategies and increasing motivation. For instance, Jiménez-Alonso et al. (2022) showed that providing instantaneous velocity feedback during resistance training significantly improved movement velocity in both countermovement jumps and squats [41]. Similarly, a study involving rugby players found that feedback-based training resulted in greater gains in CMJ power and short-distance sprint performance compared to no-feedback training [42]. The modality and timing of visual augmented feedback are also important factors. Immediate, knowledge-of-results (KR)-focused feedback, such as velocity data, has proven especially effective for explosive tasks that demand rapid force production [43]. From a motor learning perspective, visual augmented feedback not only aids in error correction but also enhances attentional focus and motivation, thereby accelerating neuromuscular adaptations [44]. These insights have significant practical implications for sprint training in soccer players, suggesting that incorporating real-time, velocity-based feedback at key thresholds can improve early acceleration performance. Coaches and practitioners can include visual feedback into VBT at this threshold to maximize explosive-start acceleration performance in soccer players. This finding is a valuable addition to Yuan et al.’s research [19]. It suggests that the effect of resistance activation on PAPE may rely on stimulus intensity rather than stimulus volume. Therefore, visual augmented feedback serves as an additional safety measure, increasing resistance stimulus intensity rather than simply increasing the weight load.

In contrast, the lack of notable improvements in 20-meter sprint times or maximal sprint speed suggests that the advantages of visual augmented feedback may be specific to certain training phases. One possible explanation for improvements in maximum sprint speed results from a combination of factors, including acceleration mechanics, stride frequency, stride length, and the utilization of elastic energy, which may require distinct or complementary training stimuli [45–47]. This study’s activation protocol may induce fatigue, decreasing or delaying PAPE, particularly during longer sprints. Additionally, the absence of step analysis in the test may result in missed observations regarding actual changes during the 0–20 m and maximum speed phases. This discrepancy highlights the importance of customizing training interventions to target the specific sprint performance outcomes of interest. It is recommended that future studies incorporate spatial-temporal and mechanical characteristic analyses, including segmented speed and force-speed indicators, to enhance sensitivity to the adaptations occurring in these specific phases. VBT is becoming more popular due to its capacity to tailor training loads and monitor fatigue levels [48]. This study extends the existing evidence by suggesting that a small velocity loss during visual-augmented feedback resistance training can elicit sufficient neuromuscular activation to enhance acceleration performance without causing excessive fatigue.

Limitations

Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample comprised a small group of trained male college soccer players; therefore, the findings should be cautiously generalized to female players, adolescents, or untrained populations. Second, the conditioning activity (85% 1RM, 2 × 6 repetitions with 1-min rest) may have induced fatigue, which could attenuate or delay PAPE and potentially mask effects on 20-m sprint time and Vmax. Third, only acute responses were examined; the chronic adaptations remain unknown. Future studies should include larger, more diverse samples and evaluate longer training interventions with different feedback modalities (e.g., auditory or combined feedback) and different velocity-loss thresholds.

Conclusions

This study shows that visual augmented feedback velocity-based training (VBT) may be an effective strategy for inducing Post-Activation Potentiation (PAPE) in soccer players. Among the different training protocols, both VF5%VL and VF12reps. demonstrated similar effectiveness in achieving PAPE, but VF5%VL required a lower load. Future studies should explore the underlying physiological mechanisms involved.
