Swallow screening of older adults at hospital admission
Authors: Anna Miles, Amanda Sextus, Becca Hammond, Jacqui Allen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe risk of swallowing difficulties increases in older adults due to a combination of factors such as multiple comorbidities and polypharmacy. Swallowing may be further destabilised by the onset of acute illness. At hospital admission, these factors can be identified and combined with a simple screening tool to select patients for additional assessment and intervention. This prospective observational cross‐sectional cohort study aimed to screen hospitalised adults age 75 years or older (older than 65 years for Māori and Pasifika) for swallowing risks, regardless of the reason for admission.MethodsA total of 644 participants were screened with the Eating Assessment Tool (EAT‐10) self‐report questionnaire from August 2021 to December 2023. Clinical teams were instructed to use the EAT‐10 to guide management. Clinical outcomes were monitored for 30 days postdischarge, and the relationship between EAT‐10 scores and subsequent clinical management was explored.ResultsAge and ethnicity were not correlated with increased EAT‐10 (p > .05), but the number of comorbidities (p < .001) and regular medications on admission were correlated with a higher EAT‐10 score (p < .001). Despite the screening process, there were associations between elevated EAT‐10 scores and readmission (p < .001), pneumonia (p < .001) and mortality (p < .01).ConclusionsThese results suggest that screening for swallowing difficulties in at‐risk older patients is valuable and allows teams to take action to prevent adverse clinical outcomes. Further investigation is required to clarify the timing for screening and to explore optimal clinical pathways for those identified at risk.Keywords: ageing, dysphagia, emergency department, screening

Practice impact

Elevated EAT‐10 scores were associated with an increased number of comorbidities and polypharmacy as well as an increased risk of readmission, pneumonia and mortality. Screening for swallowing difficulties in older patients admitted to the hospital is valuable through allowing teams to take action to prevent adverse clinical outcomes.

Policy impact

Routine screening for swallowing difficulties in older patients should be considered in general emergency departments and general acute admission wards.

INTRODUCTION

Dysphagia and its consequences are a worldwide health concern. 1 In New Zealand, prevalence is projected to grow from 9300 in 2020 to 24,500 people by 2073. 2 A dual presence of malnutrition and dysphagia is a significant risk factor for 6‐month and 1‐year mortality rates. 3 Dysphagia in hospitalised adults leads to worse clinical outcomes and increased costs, with longer hospital stays and higher mortality rates. 4 New Zealand‐based research found the economic cost associated with acute hip/femur fracture admissions was 60% higher, with 10 times higher rates of aspiration pneumonia, longer hospital stays (16 vs. 34 days) and four times greater mortality rate when dysphagia was present (n = 2453). Dysphagia was more frequent in older patients, those with more than five comorbidities and those taking more than five regular medicines. 5

Dysphagia in older age

Despite changes in the ageing swallow, researchers agree that age alone is unlikely to cause dysphagia. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Anatomically, there is loss of elasticity, ossification of bones and cartilages and atrophy of muscles. 10 However, research suggests that, in typical ageing, swallowing compensations such as mastication adaptations, volume modulation and food selection ensure sufficient functional capacity to retain airway protection. 11 , 12 , 13

It is well recognised that some comorbidities, or hospital admissions, have higher dysphagia risk. Poststroke risk of dysphagia is cited at ~50%. 14 , 15 Degenerative diseases, such as Parkinson's and chronic respiratory diseases, all come with high risk of dysphagia. 16 Many less obvious issues may precipitate a decline in an elder's swallowing, including depression, hypertension, diabetes, urinary tract infection and cognitive alterations. 17 A China‐based cross‐sectional study with 770 adults in residential care (75 years+) found 31% self‐reported dysphagia using the Eating Assessment Tool (EAT‐10), and 85% of those with dysphagia had comorbidities. 18 A New Zealand inpatient hospital study found that older adults experienced higher rates of swallowing difficulties, even for unrelated admissions. 7 The authors suggest that a delicate mix of comorbidity, polypharmacy and other inpatient factors, such as respiratory support, delirium and fatigue contributed to swallow decline in the hospital, rather than old age.

Screening for dysphagia

Routine screening for dysphagia is thought to improve outcomes for patients by mitigating risks of readmission, aspiration pneumonia, length of stay and mortality. 19 , 20 Early identification of swallowing risk expedites interventions such as speech therapy, altered medication formulations or dietary support to ensure safe swallowing. While swallow screening is common in stroke pathways, 21 routine screening is not current practice for general hospital admissions, where referrals are often triggered by medical team or nursing observations of overt swallowing difficulty, which may take some time to identify. The vulnerability of older people admitted to hospital is well established. 5 Yet, emergency departments (ED) and acute admission wards are busy environments, and staff are under time pressures. A succinct, reliable screening protocol that can identify swallowing risk is needed. Self‐report measures can be completed by untrained staff or patients themselves and therefore hold merit. 21 The EAT‐10 is a self‐administered, symptom‐specific swallow rating instrument with strong test–retest reproducibility, internal consistency and criterion‐based validity. 22 Recently, EAT‐10 has been used as a one‐off screening tool to understand the risk of dysphagia in individuals in various settings. In their recent meta‐analysis, Zhang et al. 23 concluded that the EAT‐10 was a valid screening tool, surmising that for the best diagnostic accuracy for screening for dysphagia, a cut‐off score of 3 was recommended.

This research aimed to (a) determine the prevalence of dysphagia risk in older adults admitted to hospital, (b) characterise older patients admitted to hospital who are at risk of dysphagia, (c) examine the swallowing management received by these patients and (d) explore the clinical outcomes among this population. This research asked the following questions: What are the demographic associations with older patients who self‐reported dysphagia symptoms? Are there associations between 30‐day health outcomes (aspiration pneumonia, mortality, length of stay and readmission) and EAT‐10 scores at admission?

It was hypothesised that in those over 75 years old, higher EAT‐10 scores would correspond with the presence of increased medications and comorbidities as well as poorer clinical outcomes.

METHODS

This observational cohort study recruited older adults (75 years and older) admitted to a large urban public hospital (663 beds) August 2021 to December 2023. All eligible patients completed EAT‐10 at admission to the hospital regardless of diagnosis. Clinical teams were instructed to use EAT‐10 scores to guide referral for swallow management. A mandatory care pathway was not prescribed, but typical sequelae of action at the research site included referral for a swallow assessment, nurse/health‐care assistant mealtime supervision, dietitian referral, aspiration pneumonia prevention strategies (e.g. oral cares and upright postmeal) and medical team surveillance. Patient medical records were reviewed at 30 days. National ethics approval was sought from Auckland Health Research Ethics Committee (AHREC) (Ethics reference: 21/STH/142). Reporting was guided by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort and cross‐sectional studies. 24

Participants

Inclusion criteria were aged 75 years or older and admitted for any reason, with this age range indicated, based on our previous work at this institution, as a significant predictor of dysphagia in hospitalised patients admitted with hip/femur fracture. 10 Under the recommendation of Māori Research Advisors, inclusion criterion for Māori and Pasifika patients was reduced to 65 years due to evidence of greater prevalence of comorbidities at an earlier age in these populations. 25 Recruitment took place in the ED or the acute admission wards. Patients were excluded if their admission was specifically for swallowing difficulties, or they had severe cognitive difficulties prohibiting them from providing informed consent and/or self‐reporting their swallowing difficulties. To enable participants with mild cognitive or communication difficulties to participate, patients were provided with a simplified participant information sheet, a verbal explanation, and were encouraged to have family present. Support was provided to read and complete the EAT‐10 by family or staff; however, the participant was required to indicate the answers themselves not rely on carer perspective.

Study Protocol

Typically, recruitment occurred 2 days per week. An independent research assistant (RA), with an allied health background but not involved in the patient's care, approached eligible patients for consent. The 10‐question EAT‐10 was used (0 = no problems, 4 = severe problem). The EAT‐10 scores were included in the patient's medical record. Data Collection

At 30 days postadmission, medical data were collected by a second independent researcher, who was not involved in patient care, which included relevant demographic information (age, gender, ethnicity and reason for admission), clinical management while hospitalised and health outcomes (such as length of stay, mortality, readmission and reported chest infections). The number of comorbidities and the number of regular medicines on admission were recorded. Clinical management was categorised based on the speech‐language therapist (SLT) recommendations made after referral. The number of days until the SLT assessment and the total number of SLT contacts was also recorded.

Analysis

Data were explored using descriptive statistics, plots and normality testing using Excel and SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29). Variables were not normally distributed even after log transformation. Associations between demographic data, 30‐day health outcomes and EAT‐10 scores were computed based on data type (Spearman's rho R s and χ 2). Differences in 30‐day health outcomes across EAT‐10 groups (EAT‐10 scores of 0–3, 4–7 and ≥8) were calculated using Kruskal–Wallis analysis. A p‐value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

In total, 652 patients consented to participate in the study; five had incorrect National Health Index (NHI) numbers preventing data collection, two were readmissions and two had incomplete EAT‐10 scores, leaving 644 eligible patients. Patient age ranged from 75 to 101 years (excluding the 16 Māori and Pasifika patients between 65 and 74 years). Mean EAT‐10 across all patients was 3.5 out of 40 (SD = 6.2, range = 0–36); with 156% (n = 98) of patients scoring ≥8 on the EAT‐10. The most frequent admission reasons were ‘Cardiac’ (n = 165, EAT‐10 > 8 n = 25 [26%]) and ‘Other’ (n = 166, EAT‐10 ≥ 8 n = 23 [22%]). Reasons for admission were not associated with higher EAT‐10 score categories (χ 2 [10, n = 644] = 14.75, p = .014). Table 1 shows demographic information for all patients. There was a weak association between EAT‐10 score and number of comorbidities (r s = .157, p ≤ .001), and number of regular medications (r s = .107, p = .007). There was no association between age or ethnicity and EAT‐10 score (r s = .067, p > .05; r s = .17, p > .05, respectively).

Clinical management of elevated EAT‐10 scores

Table 3 shows clinical management provided to patients who were referred to a SLT after elevated EAT‐10 ≥ 8 scores. In total, 100 patients scored ≥8, and 95 were assessed by SLT. Twenty‐six (26%) of these patients had oropharyngeal dysphagia that required further inpatient management including either further assessment or compensatory/rehabilitation interventions to improve swallow safety and efficiency. Nine patients (9%) with elevated EAT‐10 scores (EAT‐10 ≥ 8) were previously diagnosed or had suspected oesophageal dysphagia following multidisciplinary assessment, and were referred to different services. Four SLT referrals were deemed unnecessary (despite elevated EAT‐10 scores) following examination of medical notes: two due to palliative care orders, one was referred back to nursing staff following SLT recommendation of rectification of dentition issues and one had resolved, according to nurse report, before the assessment occurred. Number of days until inpatient SLT assessment (not including those who declined a SLT referral, or were discharged prior to SLT assessment, n = 53) was a mean (M) of 1.95 (SD = 2.02, range = 0–7) with 29 patients (57%) seen within 24 hours of referral. Inpatient assessment included clinical notes checking, interdisciplinary conversations and/or bedside assessments. Nineteen patients required follow‐up phone calls after scoring EAT‐10 ≥ 8, as they were discharged before assessment completion (Table 3). Of these patients, five required further support, two had one‐off education/diet modification conversations, and three were referred to a community SLT. The number of days until phone follow‐up in those who were discharged (n = 19) was highly variable (M = 13.25, SD = 21.39, range = 0–60).

Of the patients requiring repeat EAT‐10 as they scored between 4 and 7 (n = 69, Table 1), 61% (n = 42) were discharged within a 48‐h period, before the second EAT‐10 survey was administered. Six patients requiring repeat EAT‐10 scored ≥4 and were referred to SLT for initial assessment (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This cross‐sectional cohort study used EAT‐10 to screen for dysphagia in older hospitalised adults regardless of the reason for admission. In patients with swallow risk (EAT‐10 ≥ 8), we found significantly increased comorbidities and medications, and worse 30‐day health outcomes, including readmission rate, mortality and pneumonia. This supports our previous work examining 30‐day outcomes exclusively in hip fracture admissions. 5

Age and dysphagia risk

There was no association between age and elevated EAT‐10 scores. Previous research also reported that older age alone is not an independent risk factor for oropharyngeal dysphagia. 7 , 13 , 24 Researchers agree that increased comorbidities are associated with increased dysphagia risk in older people, and the tendency for these conditions to develop as we age can lead to erroneous attribution of age as being causally associated with the onset of swallow dysfunction. 5 , 26 The present study supports the assertion that comorbid disease and medications are drivers but not age alone, and replicates our previous work. 5 In the current study, we screened for swallowing problems at admission to eliminate swallow destabilisation that might be related to hospital‐induced factors (periods nil‐per‐mouth, deconditioning, institutional malnutrition, medications). We also observed an association between the number of regular medications and swallowing risk. Medications can cause xerostomia, drowsiness or tiredness, and may also lower voluntary muscle control. Dry mouth makes bolus control and transfer difficult and alters taste, making food less palatable. 11 As with other intersecting factors that impact swallowing, research confirms that polypharmacy has bi‐directional effects on an elder's risk of developing dysphagia. 5

Elevated EAT‐10 and health outcomes

Higher rates of readmissions, pneumonia and mortality were associated with higher EAT‐10 scores. The association between mortality and dysphagia is well reported. 14 , 17 Prior research has variable consensus on whether dysphagia is a risk factor for readmission. Cabré et al. 27 found that oropharyngeal dysphagia was an independent risk factor for readmission, but only when readmitted with pneumonia. In one cross‐sectional study of 444 acute geriatric patients using EAT‐10, no association was found between readmission and elevated EAT‐10 scores. However, it is relevant that they found associations between elevated EAT‐10 scores and readmission when there was an added comorbidity (e.g. COPD). 20 Our present study did not find associations between reasons for admission and elevated EAT‐10 scores. This emphasises that undiagnosed swallow impairments may exist in older adults across all general medicine admissions. This is also supported by a review by Clavé and Shaker 28 reporting undiagnosed swallowing problems in older patients admitted to general wards 29 and Allen et al. 5 in older people admitted with orthopaedic trauma.

Worse outcomes in patients with EAT‐10 ≥ 8 may be the result of several factors. Hospital admissions may decrease an older adult's functional reserve, with inactivity, polypharmacy, missed meals and possible intubations whittling away the ability to compensate or adapt. Treatments themselves applied in the hospital, such as radiation, surgery or drug therapy, may cause direct swallowing disturbances alongside worsened overall medical status. Poor intake then results in deconditioning, further impairing deglutitive functions. Implications of this for clinicians include a duty to minimise provoking factors, reconcile medications frequently and facilitate early detection and management of swallowing disorders in order to optimise swallow and nutrition.

Optimising care pathways

Early detection (at admission) of dysphagia may help inpatient management, in turn reducing the risk of aspiration pneumonia. Research supports the importance of the multidisciplinary team in swallow screening. Nurses often provide front‐line support to patients, and including them in screening may lead to faster interventions. 30 However, there are limitations to screening in ED. The rapid nature of care in the ED means that clinicians are often under pressure to make quick decisions, leaving little time for comprehensive screening. 15 Screening in this study did not prevent secondary complications (pneumonia, readmissions and death) for some patients. For some, their medical status, rather than their dysphagia, may account for their negative clinical outcomes. However, another possible barrier is the lack of available SLTs to effectively implement the clinical management required. An SLT can comprehensively assess swallowing through clinical swallowing evaluation and instrumental assessment (both radiographic and endoscopic) and, in turn, recommend compensatory and rehabilitative interventions to reduce the risk of secondary complications of swallow deficits in the short and long term where needed. Yet, SLT services and the nurses often tasked to support their dietary recommendations are often stretched, and it is unknown how much this impacts the comprehensive approach to swallowing‐related risk management.

The identification of the association between swallow impairment and polypharmacy also raises an important consideration about the role of pharmacists. Admission to hospital often results in additional medications being prescribed that may interact with regular medications and alter underlying physiological states, such as causing xerostomia or clouded mentation, exacerbating the effects of long‐standing medications. Reconciliation and reformulation of medication regimens for older hospitalised adults may be a pragmatic and powerful way to reduce swallowing risks. Therefore, if elevated EAT‐10 also prompted a pharmacy review, there may be a further opportunity for mitigation of swallow‐related complications.

Limitations

Although management of EAT‐10 referrals in hospital was documented, a mandatory care pathway was not prescribed. Clinical records rely on accurate reporting. The EAT‐10 is a validated screening tool; however, as a patient‐reported tool, it is subjective. Patients may over‐ or under‐report their swallowing concerns. The high number of patients who self‐reported EAT‐10 ≥ 8 but did not require SLT management from ED reflects this. A patient's perception of their swallowing may shift with their current state. Acute symptoms may lead to elevated EAT‐10 scores compared to the ‘well‐state’ in those presenting at hospital. Patients may have cognitive impairments. They may have heightened concerns about their swallowing when in a stressed situation. Conversely, patients may under‐report if distracted with other more pressing health matters. The EAT‐10 flags concerns; however, it may not discern between oropharyngeal dysphagia, which sits in an SLT remit, and oesophageal dysphagia, which needs to be referred to gastroenterology.

The ethnicity data do not reflect the population of New Zealand or internationally, with ~70% reported as New Zealand European. There were only a small number of New Zealand Māori and Pacific Island participants despite the expanded inclusion criterion attempting to pick up patients with comorbidities at an earlier age in these ethnic groups. It may be beneficial to replicate this work in other regions with higher populations of New Zealand Māori, or instigate intentional prioritised recruitment in future studies, to successfully capture swallowing outcomes in these at‐risk groups.

CONCLUSIONS

Older adults admitted to hospital may have underlying swallow impairments that can be destabilised by acute illness and may contribute to worse early outcomes regardless of admission reason. Risk could be detected through use of a self‐report measure such as the EAT‐10, in a non‐invasive and time‐efficient way during the admission process. Individuals aged 75 years or older, and presenting with five or more comorbidities and prescribed five or more medications, have an increased risk of swallowing dysfunction and would most benefit from additional screening. This study confirms that elevated EAT‐10 scores may indicate risk for worse clinical outcomes, such as pneumonia, mortality and readmission to hospital within 30 days. Identification of swallow dysfunction through screening provides the opportunity to act to reduce adverse clinical outcomes. Further investigation is required to optimise timing and tools for screening swallowing in the acute setting and to explore optimal clinical pathways for those identified at risk.
