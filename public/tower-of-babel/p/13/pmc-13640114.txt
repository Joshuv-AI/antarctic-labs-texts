Incremental prognostic value of the systemic immune‑inflammation index, pan‑immune‑inflammation value, and modified glasgow prognostic score for severity assessment and outcome prediction in community‑acquired pneumonia
Authors: Yunkui Li, Yue Hu, Liming Fan, Yuanyuan Wu, Wei Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity-acquired pneumonia (CAP) constitutes a significant cause of hospital admissions and mortality globally. Accurate assessment of CAP severity and prognosis is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies. This study investigates the utility of the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), Pan-Immune Inflammation Value (PIV), and modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS) in evaluating CAP severity and predicting patient outcome.MethodsConducted a retrospective analysis of our medical records of 1879 patients with CAP who were admitted between January 2021 and December 2023. The patients were categorized into a mild disease group (n = 595, 31.66%) and a severe disease group (n = 1284, 68.33%) using the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI). Additionally, based on the prognosis during the first 28 days, the patients were divided into a good prognosis group (n = 1618, 86.11%) and a poor prognosis group (n = 261, 13.89%) based on their prognosis within the first 28 days. The mean values of the SII, PIV, and mGPS were calculated and statistically analyzed to determine their association with disease severity and prognosis. Model comparison with the PSI and assessment of incremental prognostic value were performed using hierarchical logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic curve analyses.ResultsIn our study, we found that the levels of SII and PIV are much higher in patients with severe CAP. These two indices have a positive correlation with bad outcomes (SII: rho = 0.198, P < 0.001; PIV: rho = 0.154, P < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression identified mGPS as the strongest independent predictor of severe CAP (OR = 18.342, P < 0.001), along with respiratory rate, diastolic blood pressure, and PIV. For poor prognosis (28-day mortality), independent predictors included BMI, cardiovascular disease, PSI score, mGPS (OR = 26.512, P < 0.001), heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, diastolic blood pressure, SII, and PIV. ROC analysis showed that SII (AUC = 0.665), PIV (AUC = 0.628), and mGPS (AUC = 0.677) exhibited moderate individual predictive accuracy for 28‑day mortality; however, adding these indices to the PSI improved the AUC from 0.672 to 0.712.ConclusionSII, PIV, and mGPS correlate with CAP severity and 28‑day outcomes. Although their standalone discriminative ability is only moderate, they provide incremental prognostic information when combined with established scores such as the PSI. These inexpensive, routinely available biomarkers may serve as adjunctive tools to enhance risk stratification, and their integration with clinical variables modestly improves prediction of adverse outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04422-9.Keywords: Community-acquired pneumonia, Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index, Pan-immune-inflammation Value, Modified Glasgow Prognostic Score, Severity assessment, Prognosis

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, affecting diverse populations and placing substantial burdens on healthcare systems. Despite advancements in diagnostic techniques and therapeutic strategies, CAP continues to pose significant challenges due to its varied etiologies and clinical presentations [1–3]. Accurate assessment of disease severity and prognosis is crucial for optimizing treatment decisions and improving patient outcomes.

The systemic immune-inflammatory response plays an essential role in the body’s reaction to infection, impacting both the severity and prognostic outcomes of diseases [4, 5]. Traditional tools such as CURB-65 and the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) have been widely used for stratifying CAP severity [6, 7]. However, these scoring systems may not fully capture the actual immune and inflammatory states of patients. Recently, there has been growing interest in incorporating inflammatory biomarkers into existing frameworks to enhance prognostic accuracy.

Several indices, including the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), Pan-immune Inflammation Value (PIV), and Modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS), are currently utilized for predicting the condition of CAP patients. These scores are typically calculated using readily available hematological parameters, particularly blood cell counts. For example, SII, which combines platelet count, neutrophil count, and lymphocyte count, reflects the overall immune status and inflammation level within the body [8]. Higher SII levels often correlate with worse outcomes in inflammatory diseases and cancers, suggesting its potential utility in evaluating CAP. Similarly, PIV uses various blood cell counts to indicate inflammation and immune activity, potentially aiding in understanding CAP severity and progression [9]. Additionally, mGPS, calculated based on C-reactive protein (CRP) and albumin levels, is extensively used for predicting outcomes in cancer patients and is increasingly applied in infectious diseases like CAP [10].

Even though these scores are useful for predicting outcomes in many diseases, people haven’t studied much about how to use them in CAP. Using SII, PIV, and mGPS in clinics can give doctors extra data to help them better understand patient conditions. This can lead to a more complete evaluation of how sick patients are. They are also easy to measure as part of routine care and are not expensive, which makes them good for widespread use. Most importantly, adding these immune and inflammation scores to the prediction models doctors already use could help them better judge a patient’s risk and choose more personalized treatments.

Although the SII, PIV, and mGPS have shown prognostic value in various diseases, including infections and cancers, evidence specifically examining their role in severity assessment and outcome prediction for CAP is scarce. Previous studies on CAP have typically focused on one or two of these indices without comparing them to the PSI or adjusting for critical clinical confounders. Additionally, it remains unclear whether integrating these three readily accessible biomarkers with clinical variables offers additional prognostic value beyond what PSI alone can provide. Our study aims to fill these gaps by simultaneously evaluating SII, PIV, and mGPS within a large CAP cohort, directly comparing their predictive capabilities against the established PSI, and quantifying the added prognostic benefit when combined with clinical factors. We employ objective clinical endpoints such as ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, or vasopressor support for severity classification to prevent incorporation bias. This comprehensive approach enables us to ascertain if these indices deliver clinically significant incremental information beyond existing risk stratification methods.

Materials and methods

Study design and grouping criteria

We conducted a retrospective analysis on 1, 879 patients with CAP who were admitted to our hospital between January 2021 and December 2023. The severity of their illness was evaluated using the PSI. The PSI categorizes risk classes as follows: Risk Class I was for individuals aged 50 years or younger with no risk factors (≤ 50 points), Risk Class II represents scores of less than 70 points, Risk Class III ranges from 71 to 90 points, Risk Class IV spans 91 to 130 points, and Risk Class V exceeds 130 points [11]. Using PSI scores, we initially classified patients into mild (Risk Classes I–III, n = 595) and severe (Risk Classes IV–V, n = 1,284) groups for descriptive comparisons. However, to avoid incorporation bias in regression analyses, severity for multivariate modeling was redefined using objective clinical endpoints: severe CAP was defined as the need for ICU admission, mechanical ventilation (invasive or non-invasive), or vasopressor support within the first 48 h of hospitalization. These endpoints were chosen because they represent objective, clinically meaningful thresholds for critical illness that are not dependent on the PSI score itself. Based on this definition, 421 patients (22.41%) met criteria for severe CAP, and 1,458 patients (77.59%) had non-severe CAP.

We assessed patients again 28 days after they were admitted. Based on these results, we divided the patients into two groups. Those who were still alive after 28 days were in the good prognosis group (n = 1,618). Those who had died within 28 days were in the poor prognosis group (n = 261) [12]. To enhance the clinical relevance and predictive performance of subgroup analysis, optimal cut-off values for SII, PIV, and mGPS were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for predicting poor prognosis (28-day mortality). The optimal cut-off values were identified by maximizing the Youden index. The cut-off values were as follows: SII: 1801.125, PIV: 376.065, and mGPS: 1.385. Patients were then categorized into subgroups using these optimal cut-off values: for SII, patients were divided into low-SII (≤ 1801.125) and high-SII (> 1801.125) groups; for PIV, patients were divided into low-PIV (≤ 376.065) and high-PIV (> 376.065) groups; and for mGPS, patients were divided into low-mGPS (≤ 1.385) and high-mGPS (> 1.385) groups.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were diagnosed with CAP in accordance with the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Chinese Adult CAP (2016 Edition) [13], were 18 years of age or older, were monitored for 28 days following hospitalization, and if comprehensive baseline and follow-up data were available.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with concurrent conditions such as bronchiectasis with infection, lung abscess, radiation pneumonia, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, active tuberculosis, cystic pulmonary fibrosis, or other significant pulmonary diseases were excluded. Additionally, patients were excluded if they had been hospitalized within the preceding 30 days, had autoimmune diseases, had a concurrent infection at another site, had received advanced treatments including radiation therapy, chemotherapy, surgery, biological therapy, or immunosuppressive therapy in the past month. Patients who died within 48 h of hospital admission (n = 14) were excluded from the primary prognostic analysis. This was done because the study aimed to assess the predictive value of admission biomarkers for 28-day outcomes in patients undergoing in-hospital management, and very early mortality is often influenced by immediate catastrophic events or limitations of care where biomarker profiles may be incomplete or their interpretation different. A flowchart summarizing patient inclusion and exclusion is now provided as Supplementary Fig. 1.

Ethics statement

The ethical committee has waived the requirement for obtaining informed consent for this retrospective analysis, as the clinical data used has been fully anonymized. Before we did the analysis, we removed all information that could identify the patients. The Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Yangtze University approved this study (Approval No. KY2026-039-01), and was conducted in accordance to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

To collect information from patients’ medical records. Information included age, gender, vital signs, and blood inflammatory markers. The Systemic Immune‑Inflammation Index (SII) was calculated as: SII = (platelet count × neutrophil count) / lymphocyte count. We did this calculation for each patient in our study. The Pan-Immune Inflammation Value (PIV) was calculated using the formula: PIV = (neutrophil count × platelet count × monocyte count) / lymphocyte count. All counts were measured in units of 10³/mm³. For the modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS), we used C-reactive protein (CRP) and albumin levels: a score of 0 was assigned when CRP was < 10.0 mg/L and albumin was ≥ 35.0 g/L (equivalent to ≥ 3.5 g/dL); a score of 1 was assigned when CRP was ≥ 10.0 mg/L; a score of 2 was assigned only when both conditions were met [14].

Detection of vital signs and inflammatory indicators

We measured vital signs upon patient admission with a multi-parameter monitor (iPM 12, Mindray, China), documenting heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), body temperature, systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Fasting venous blood samples (5 mL) were drawn the next morning. These samples were centrifuged (TGL-16 M, Xiangyi, China) at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting plasma was separated and stored at -80 °C.

The counts of white blood cells (WBC), neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, and monocytes using an automated machine for blood cell counting (BC-6900, Mindray, China). We measured the levels of CRP and albumin using a different machine for biochemical analysis (BS-860, Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., China). We then used the results from these measurements to calculate the SII, PIV, and mGPS values for each patient.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the data using SPSS software, version 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). First, we checked if the continuous variables followed a normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). For the primary outcome of 28-day mortality, with an observed AUC of 0.677 for mGPS, a sample size of 1,879 achieved a power of > 0.99 at an alpha level of 0.05, indicating that the study was adequately powered to detect the observed effect sizes. For continuous data that were normally distributed (as determined by the Shapiro-Wilk test with a threshold of P > 0.05), we show them as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and used the independent samples t-test for two-group comparisons. For continuous data that were not normally distributed, the Mann-Whitney U test was used (results not shown as all key variables met normality assumptions). We present categorical data as the number of cases and percentage [n (%)]. For categorical data comparisons, we used the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were < 5). The rationale for using parametric tests was based on the central limit theorem given the large sample size (n = 1,879) and confirmation of normality via the Shapiro-Wilk test.

We considered a P value less than 0.05 to be statistically significant. To look at relationships between two continuous variables, we used Pearson correlation analysis. For relationships involving a categorical variable, we used Spearman correlation analysis. We performed univariate analysis to look at factors related to how severe the CAP was and the patients’ outcome.

To assess potential multicollinearity among predictors in multivariate regression models, variance inflation factor (VIF) values were calculated. A VIF value > 5 was considered indicative of significant multicollinearity. All VIF values in our models were below 5, confirming the absence of severe multicollinearity.

Compared to the mild group, patients in the severe group had significantly higher PSI scores and mGPS, a lower proportion of females, and a higher prevalence of pleural effusion (all P < 0.01). No significant differences were observed between the two groups for age, BMI, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or length of hospital stay(all P > 0.05). These parameters highlight key clinical differences relevant for CAP severity and prognosis assessment.

Vital signs of mild group and severe group

We compared vital signs between the mild and severe CAP groups. Heart rate (HR) was a bit higher in the severe group (84.73 ± 8.71 beats per minute) than in the mild group (83.84 ± 8.28 beats per minute). This difference was statistically significant (t = 2.080, P = 0.038) (Fig. 1). RR was also significantly higher in the severe group (20.29 ± 4.56 breaths per minute) compared to the mild group (19.87 ± 3.18 breaths per minute; t = 2.320, P = 0.020). Systolic blood pressure was slightly higher in the severe group (125.78 ± 12.27 mmHg) than in the mild group (123.88 ± 11.45 mmHg; t = 3.193, P = 0.001). Body temperature was also slightly higher in the severe group (37.07 ± 0.76 °C vs. 36.97 ± 0.79 °C; t = 2.668, P = 0.008). However, diastolic blood pressure was a bit lower in the severe group (70.64 ± 7.53 mmHg) than in the mild group (71.57 ± 7.34 mmHg; t = 2.537, P = 0.011). Overall, patients with more severe CAP had slightly higher heart rates, respiratory rates, body temperatures, and systolic blood pressures, but slightly lower diastolic blood pressures. These vital signs are important for judging how severe the disease is.

Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis was performed to identify factors associated with CAP severity (Table 3). Male gender showed a weak negative correlation (rho=-0.060, P = 0.009), while pleural effusion had a minor positive correlation (rho = 0.070, P = 0.002). Severity prediction scores, PSI and mGPS, demonstrated strong positive correlations (rho = 0.805 and rho = 0.156, respectively, both P < 0.001). Vital signs, including heart rate (HR: rho = 0.050, P = 0.030) and respiratory rate (RR: rho = 0.055, P = 0.017), correlated positively with severity. Higher systolic blood pressure (SBP: rho = 0.070, P = 0.002), white blood cell count (WBC: rho = 0.072, P = 0.002), and neutrophil count (rho = 0.069, P = 0.003) were also linked to greater severity. Lymphocyte count exhibited a slight negative correlation (rho=-0.050, P = 0.030), indicating lower counts in severe cases. CRP levels showed a small positive correlation (rho = 0.052, P = 0.025). Inflammation indices SII (rho = 0.221, P < 0.001) and PIV (rho = 0.140, P < 0.001) significantly correlated with severity. These findings emphasize the importance of these factors in assessing CAP severity.

Univariate logistic regression analysis of severity in CAP patients

Female gender compared to male was associated with a lower likelihood of severe outcomes (OR = 0.763, 95% CI 0.622 to 0.936, P = 0.009). Pleural effusion presence significantly increased the risk of severity (OR = 1.393, 95% CI 1.126 to 1.729, P = 0.002) (Table 4). The PSI score was strongly correlated with severity (OR = 2.510, 95% CI 2.082 to 3.243, P < 0.001), indicating that each 1-point increase in the PSI score is linked to more than double the odds of severe outcomes (i.e., a 151% increase in odds). The mGPS was also highly predictive (OR = 4.904, 95% CI 3.192 to 7.580, P < 0.001), showing a substantial increase in risk. Heart rate (HR: OR = 1.012, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.024, P = 0.038) and respiratory rate (RR: OR = 1.025, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.049, P = 0.042) showed slight but significant associations with severity. Temperature (OR = 1.188, 95% CI 1.046 to 1.349, P = 0.008), systolic blood pressure (SBP: OR = 1.013, 95% CI 1.005 to 1.022, P = 0.001), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP: OR = 0.983, 95% CI 0.970 to 0.996, P = 0.011) also had significant associations. Among laboratory findings, WBC count (OR = 1.060, 95% CI 1.017 to 1.106, P = 0.006), neutrophil count (OR = 1.070, 95% CI 1.029 to 1.114, P < 0.001), and lymphocyte count (OR = 0.750, 95% CI 0.584 to 0.962, P = 0.024) were significant predictors. C-reactive protein (CRP: OR = 1.005, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.009, P = 0.018), SII (OR = 1.001, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.001, P < 0.001), and PIV (OR = 1.005, 95% CI 1.003 to 1.006, P < 0.001) all indicated significant associations with severity. These results suggest that both clinical and laboratory markers play critical roles in predicting the severity of CAP.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of severity in CAP patients

The multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several independent predictors of CAP severity (Table 5). The mGPS was the strongest predictor, with patients having a higher mGPS showing significantly increased odds of severe CAP (OR = 18.342, P < 0.001). RR was also significantly associated with severity (OR = 1.038, P = 0.029). DBP showed a significant inverse relationship (OR = 0.921, P = 0.027), indicating that lower DBP was linked to higher severity risk. The PIV also demonstrated a significant positive association (OR = 1.008, P = 0.012). Female gender (compared to male) showed a protective trend but did not reach statistical significance (OR = 0.788, P = 0.094). Pleural effusion (OR = 1.366, P = 0.063), heart rate (HR: OR = 1.009, P = 0.198), body temperature (OR = 1.160, P = 0.147), systolic blood pressure (SBP: OR = 1.008, P = 0.182), and SII (OR = 1.002, P = 0.521) were not statistically significant at the conventional 0.05 level after adjusting for other factors. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated a good model fit (χ² = 8.24, P = 0.411).

Basic data of good prognosis group and poor prognosis group

The average age of the poor prognosis group was 62.73 ± 6.36 years, and for the good prognosis group it was 62.07 ± 7.22 years (t = 1.530, P = 0.127). This difference was not significant (Table 6). However, the BMI was significantly higher in the poor prognosis group (23.16 ± 2.52 kg/m²) than in the good prognosis group (22.81 ± 2.16 kg/m²) (t = 2.131, P = 0.034). The rate of cardiovascular disease was also significantly higher in the poor prognosis groups (19.92%) compared to the good prognosis group (14.96%) (χ²= 4.200, P = 0.040). The PSI score in the poor prognosis group (96.35 ± 17.73) was significantly higher than in the good prognosis group (88.43 ± 18.23) (t = 6.541, P < 0.001). Similarly, the mGPS was significantly higher in the poor prognosis group (1.58 ± 0.17) compared to the good prognosis group (1.45 ± 0.22) (t = 10.798, P < 0.001). We found no significant differences between the two groups for gender, smoking status (current or prior), history of hypertension, diabetes, marital status, pleural effusion, time from onset to admission, duration of antibiotic use before admission, or length of hospital stay. These results indicate that BMI, cardiovascular disease, PSI score, and mGPS are significantly related to patient prognosis.

Vital signs of good prognosis group and poor prognosis group

For HR, the mean value in the good prognosis group was 84.36 ± 7.23 beats per minute, while the mean HR in the poor prognosis group was significantly higher at 85.91 ± 7.34 beats per minute (t = 3.208, P = 0.001) (Fig. 2). The RR in the good prognosis group was 19.92 ± 4.22 breaths per minute, whereas the RR in the poor prognosis group was significantly higher at 20.52 ± 3.87 breaths per minute (t = 2.143, P = 0.032). The body temperature in the good prognosis group was 36.99 ± 0.66 °C, significantly lower than the average body temperature of 37.12 ± 0.72 °C in the poor prognosis group (t = 2.726, P = 0.007). The systolic blood pressure (SBP) in the good prognosis group was 123.93 ± 13.23 mmHg, while the SBP in the poor prognosis group was significantly elevated to 126.53 ± 12.15 mmHg (t = 2.983, P = 0.003). We found that the average diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was 71.93 ± 6.34 mmHg in the good prognosis group and 73.41 ± 7.16 mmHg in the poor prognosis group. Incremental prognostic value of SII, PIV, and mGPS beyond the PSII

ROC curve analysis was first performed to evaluate the individual predictive performance of SII, PIV, mGPS, and the PSI for 28‑day mortality (Table 11; Fig. 3). The area under the curve (AUC) for the PSI alone was 0.672 (95% CI: 0.615–0.697). To determine whether the inflammatory indices add incremental value to the established PSI, stepwise logistic regression models were constructed with PSI as the reference (Table 12). Model 1 (PSI alone) yielded an AUC of 0.672. Adding SII and PIV to the PSI (Model 2) increased the AUC to 0.694 (95% CI: 0.654–0.734; DeLong P = 0.042 vs. PSI alone). The full model (Model 3), which further incorporated mGPS, achieved an AUC of 0.712 (95% CI: 0.673–0.751; DeLong P = 0.031 vs. Model 2). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated good calibration for all three models (all P > 0.05). These findings demonstrate that SII, PIV, and mGPS provide incremental prognostic value beyond that of the PSI alone. The shown hierarchical models that used clinical variables (BMI, cardiovascular disease, heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, systolic BP, diastolic BP) as the baseline, and then sequentially added SII, PIV, and mGPS, are now presented in Supplementary Table S1. In that analysis, the AUC improved from 0.641 (clinical variables alone) to 0.689 (with SII and PIV) and to 0.712 (with mGPS), with all models showing good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P > 0.05). This pattern of incremental improvement is consistent with the PSI-based analysis above.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the utility of SII, PIV, and mGPS for assessing CAP severity and predicting 28-day outcomes. Patients with severe CAP (defined by objective clinical endpoints) had significantly higher SII and PIV levels than those with non-severe CAP. Similarly, patients with poor 28-day prognosis had higher SII and PIV values. These results suggest that SII and PIV may serve as practical indicators of systemic inflammation and immune response status. However, in multivariate analysis, only PIV remained an independent predictor of severe CAP, while SII did not, highlighting the differential utility of these indices. These markers could assist clinicians in evaluating the severity of pneumonia.

Higher SII and PIV values were observed in patients with severe CAP and poor 28‑day outcomes. Using ROC‑determined optimal cut‑offs (SII > 1801.125, PIV > 376.065, mGPS > 1.385), patients above these thresholds had significantly higher rates of poor prognosis (27.54%, 22.80%, and 30.60%, respectively) compared to those below thresholds. These elevated scores likely reflect excessive systemic inflammation and immune imbalance, characterized by neutrophilia, thrombocytosis, and lymphopenia, which are known to compromise host defenses and worsen clinical outcomes in CAP [15–18]. Reduced lymphocytes suggest impaired immune function, possibly due to lymphocyte exhaustion or dysfunction, which can contribute to adverse clinical outcomes. Therefore, PIV could be a useful tool for identifying patients at risk of severe disease progression. Our findings align with recent research demonstrating the prognostic utility of PIV in other systemic inflammatory conditions, such as valvular dysfunction in opioid use disorder and disease prognosis in acute pericarditis [19, 20]. This consistency across different disease states reinforces the role of PIV as a robust, composite marker of systemic inflammatory and immune dysregulation, which is central to the pathophysiology of severe CAP.

An apparent inconsistency was noted regarding diastolic blood pressure (DBP), which showed an inverse association with CAP severity but a positive association with 28‑day mortality. This opposite directionality reflects distinct pathophysiological contexts. In the severity model, lower DBP is characteristic of acute systemic vasodilation and reduced vascular tone, often seen in patients with septic shock or those requiring vasopressor support—conditions that define severe CAP by our objective clinical endpoints. Conversely, in the mortality model, higher DBP may serve as a proxy for underlying cardiovascular comorbidities such as chronic hypertension or arterial stiffness, which are established risk factors for adverse outcomes in CAP [21]. These findings underscore that a single physiological parameter can have different prognostic meanings depending on the clinical endpoint and timing of assessment, and they highlight the importance of interpreting DBP within the broader clinical context rather than as an isolated marker.

The mGPS emerged as the strongest independent predictor of both CAP severity and poor prognosis in our multivariate models. Its superior performance compared to SII and PIV may be attributed to its composite nature, which captures two critical and complementary pathophysiological dimensions: the magnitude of the acute-phase inflammatory response and the patient’s nutritional and functional reserve. While SII and PIV are derived solely from blood cell counts, reflecting immune cell activity and interaction, mGPS integrates the intensity of the inflammatory stimulus with a marker of the systemic metabolic and nutritional consequences of inflammation (hypoalbuminemia) [22, 23]. In the context of a severe infection like CAP, the combination of a heightened inflammatory drive and diminished physiological reserve, as quantified by mGPS, may provide a more holistic assessment of disease burden and host vulnerability, thereby offering stronger predictive power for adverse outcomes. Furthermore, when mGPS was added to a model containing clinical variables plus SII and PIV, the AUC for predicting poor prognosis increased from 0.689 to 0.712, highlighting its incremental value. These findings underscore the clinical utility of mGPS as a comprehensive marker of inflammatory and nutritional status [24–26]. Nevertheless, the very high odds ratios observed for mGPS should be interpreted with caution. These estimates may partly reflect model instability due to the relatively small number of patients with poor prognosis, potential residual confounding, or the scaling effect inherent to an ordinal score with limited categorie. Future studies with larger sample sizes and external validation are necessary to confirm the magnitude of these associations.

Our findings highlight the synergistic utility of SII, PIV, and mGPS in prognostic stratification, beyond the use of individual markers. The combined use of these indices potentially enhances predictive accuracy by offering a comprehensive view of inflammatory status, immune dynamics, and metabolic stress. Given that pneumonia severity is influenced by host-pathogen interactions, these measures provide a deeper understanding of patient-specific inflammatory and immune characteristics. The influence underscores the broader principle that an intense and potentially dysregulated inflammatory response was often associated with severe outcomes in pneumonia, in line with similar observations in conditions like sepsis.

The observed associations can be partially explained by the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. The progression from mild to severe CAP involves an escalation of the inflammatory cascade, driven by the host immune system’s efforts to eliminate the invading pathogen [27, 28]. When the immune system is not working properly, either too active or not enough, it can damage the body and lead to adverse consequences. Higher SII/mGPS values in severe cases likely indicate impaired immune regulation, consistent with the observed association between dysregulated inflammation and adverse prognosis [29, 30]. Understanding these mechanisms can help clinicians better predict and manage disease progression. Importantly, these indices reflect systemic inflammation and immune status, which are critical factors in determining patient outcomes.

SII and PIV may also help us understand blood clotting problems that often happen with serious infections. High platelet counts in these scores suggest a connection to tiny blood clots (microthrombosis) and issues with blood vessel lining (endothelial dysfunction). Both problems are known to make pneumonia worse [31, 32]. Therefore, managing subclinical coagulopathy, as assessed by SII and PIV, may be important for improving outcomes in CAP patients [21, 33]. We also observed that gender influenced outcomes, with females having a lower risk of severe CAP. This difference may be related to these indices. Sex hormones are known to modulate immune responses [34, 35], which may affect the components of these prognostic scores. Further research into how sex-related hormonal or genetic factors influence these indices could enable more personalized risk assessment and treatment.

An interesting finding was that SII, while showing a strong univariate association with both severity and prognosis, was not an independent predictor of disease severity in the multivariate model but remained a significant predictor of 28-day mortality. This discrepancy may indicate that the factors determining initial disease severity (e.g., pathogen virulence, early host response leading to ICU admission/ventilation) and those determining ultimate prognosis (e.g., sustained inflammatory burden, secondary complications, or failure to resolve inflammation) are not identical. The multivariate model for severity included objective clinical endpoints and was adjusted for robust clinical variables like respiratory rate and diastolic blood pressure. In this model, mGPS and PIV, which incorporate a broader set of parameters, may have captured the inflammatory state more comprehensively for predicting the initial critical illness. In contrast, the prognosis model predicts an outcome (death) that occurs over a longer timeframe (28 days). SII, reflecting a dynamic balance between neutrophils, platelets, and lymphocytes, might be a more sensitive marker of a sustained, dysregulated immune state that contributes to later complications (e.g., secondary infections, organ failure) or cardiovascular events, which are a major driver of CAP-related mortality [21, 36, 37]. The prognostic value of SII has been demonstrated across a wide spectrum of conditions, including cardiovascular disease [37], preeclampsia, and acute appendicitis [38, 39]. Further prospective studies tracking the longitudinal trajectory of these indices are needed to clarify their temporal relationships with different clinical milestones in CAP.

From a clinical perspective, the SII, PIV, and mGPS offer several practical advantages that support their potential integration into real-world decision-making for CAP. Because these indices are derived from complete blood counts, CRP, and albumin, tests that are routinely performed upon hospital admission, they do not require additional sampling or specialized assays, making them feasible even in resource-limited settings. Their primary clinical value lies in early risk stratification at the point of care: patients identified as high-risk based on elevated SII (> 1801.125), PIV (> 376.065), or mGPS (> 1.385) may warrant closer monitoring, earlier consideration of intensive care unit admission, or more aggressive empirical antibiotic and supportive therapy. Conversely, patients with low scores could be managed with standard protocols, potentially avoiding unnecessary escalation of care. Furthermore, these indices could serve as adjunctive monitoring tools during hospitalization to track the trajectory of the host inflammatory response and response to therapy. To be explicit, these indices are not designed or validated as standalone predictors of CAP severity or mortality. Rather, they should be interpreted as supplementary information that, when combined with clinical variables and validated scoring systems (PSI, CURB‑65), may enhance overall risk assessment. Their fair AUC values (0.628–0.677) confirm that they lack sufficient discriminative power to act alone, but their incremental value is demonstrated by the improved AUC from 0.641 to 0.712 when added to clinical variables. Therefore, we emphasize that these indices should always be used in conjunction with, not in isolation from, established clinical tools and physician judgment. From a clinical decision‑making standpoint, the identification of a high inflammatory state through these indices could directly alter patient management. For example, a patient classified as PSI risk class III (moderate risk) who simultaneously presents with an SII > 1801.125, PIV > 376.065, or mGPS > 1.385 might be considered for closer monitoring on a high‑dependency unit, earlier infectious disease consultation, or more aggressive empirical antibiotic and supportive therapy, even if the PSI alone would not mandate intensive care admission. This is particularly relevant because the per‑unit odds ratios for continuous SII and PIV may appear modest, reflecting the wide biological range of these indices; the clinical impact is best appreciated through validated cut‑off values and subgroup categorisation, as demonstrated in our subgroup analysis. Thus, incorporating these biomarkers into the initial assessment may help reclassify patients who are at higher risk than estimated by PSI alone, potentially averting delayed escalation of care. Prospective interventional studies are needed to test whether management guided by these inflammatory thresholds improves outcomes.

This study has several important limitations. First, it is a single‑center, retrospective analysis, which may introduce selection bias and unmeasured confounding, and the findings may not be generalizable. No external validation cohort was available, and the predictive models have not been tested in independent populations. Second, advanced reclassification metrics such as net reclassification improvement (NRI) and decision curve analysis were not performed; therefore, the clinical net benefit of incorporating SII, PIV, and mGPS into existing risk‑stratification algorithms remains to be quantified. Third, although we assessed multicollinearity and used ROC‑derived cut‑off values, the moderate AUC values indicate that these indices alone are insufficient for clinical decision‑making. Furthermore, the optimal cut‑off values for SII, PIV, and mGPS were derived from ROC analysis within the same study cohort without internal validation (e.g., bootstrapping or cross‑validation), which may lead to overfitting and overly optimistic estimates of sensitivity and specificity. Calibration was assessed only by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test; calibration plots and the Brier score were not computed, limiting a comprehensive evaluation of model accuracy. Fourth, we excluded patients who died within 48 h of admission, which may have introduced selection bias by removing the most severe cases and potentially attenuating observed effect sizes. Additionally, these blood‑based indices reflect systemic inflammation but are not specific to the causative pathogen and may be confounded by comorbidities. Future prospective, multicenter studies with pre‑specified NRI and decision curve analyses are needed to validate the incremental value of these biomarkers and to determine whether their use can alter clinical management and improve outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, SII, PIV, and mGPS are significantly associated with CAP severity and 28‑day mortality. Their individual predictive performance is moderate, and they should not replace validated clinical scores. However, when added to the PSI, these indices provide incremental prognostic value, improving the AUC from 0.672 to 0.712. This suggests that SII, PIV, and mGPS can serve as useful adjuncts to existing severity scores by identifying a subset of patients whose inflammatory burden places them at higher risk than estimated by PSI alone. Prospective, multicenter studies incorporating decision curve analysis and net reclassification improvement are required to definitively establish the clinical utility of integrating these biomarkers into routine CAP management.
