Advancements in Inorganic Chiral Nanomaterials for Anti‐Tumor Immunotherapy
Authors: Siyu Chen, Qian Tang, Xiqian Chen, Yinghao Liang, Jin Qiu, Yongmei Liao, Changqiang Li, Hongru Yu, Fang Xie, Zongjunlin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile the past decade has witnessed unprecedented technological advancements, cancer remains a major health threat, with conventional therapies often constrained by substantial limitations. Immunotherapy, which mobilizes the body's immune system, is hindered by off‐target effects, allergic reactions, and cytokine storms. To enhance the precision, safety, and efficacy of cancer treatments, nanomaterials—especially those with nanoscale chiral properties—are offering a new approach for modulating immune responses. The optical anisotropy factor (g‐factor) of these nanomaterials has demonstrated precise, monotonic control over immune responses in vivo and in vitro, revealing that nanoscale chiral structures can act as key switches for dendritic cell maturation and function. Inorganic nanomaterials (INMs) are gaining attention for tumor immunotherapy due to their stability, biocompatibility, and precise synthesis. Their versatile surface modification, excellent optical/electrical/magnetic properties, and reproducibility further enhance their utility. Inorganic chiral nanomaterials (ICNMs), leveraging atomically precise chiral structures, enable efficient drug loading and radiosensitization to trigger anti‐tumor immunity, overcoming immunosuppressive limitations. Representative ICNMs include chiral gold nanorods, cadmium selenide quantum dots, and cerium oxide nanosheets. Current research focuses on chiral structure control, surface engineering, and multifunctional integration. ICNMs enhance antitumor immunity through targeted immunomodulator delivery, induction of immunogenic cell death, direct immune‐cell regulation, and reshaping of the tumor immune microenvironment. Despite their promise, challenges remain in scalable production, long‐term biosafety, and chiral stability in complex biological settings. Looking forward, ICNMs are poised to enable precise, efficient, and low‐toxicity tumor immunotherapy, driving transformative advances in cancer care.

Introduction

Cancer, characterized by uncontrolled cell division and tumor proliferation that disrupts normal tissue function, remains a significant long‐term threat to global health [1]. Traditional modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, are inherently limited by issues encompassing incomplete metastatic resection, non‐specific toxicity, severe side effects, the development of drug resistance, and suboptimal control of distant metastases [2]. In response, novel strategies, encompassing immunotherapy, gene therapy, and phototherapy, have emerged. Immunotherapy has gained significant momentum given its ability to activate host immune responses to selectively eradicate cancer cells, establish durable immunological memory, and suppress metastatic recurrence. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD‐1)/Cytotoxic T‐Lymphocyte‐Associated Protein 4 (CTLA‐4) blockers) have yielded encouraging clinical results with potential for long‐term disease control [3]. Nevertheless, their efficacy is limited by low delivery efficiency, instability of therapeutic agents, and off‐target effects, highlighting the urgent need for targeted delivery systems to enhance the overall effectiveness of immunotherapeutic interventions.

Advances in nanomedicine have positioned nanomaterials at the forefront of research due to their high degree of modifiability, ability to harness the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, and capacity for immunomodulatory co‐loading [4]. Unlike organic carriers that suffer low drug loading, poor stability, and erratic degradation, inorganic nanomaterials (INMs) offer tunable shapes, LSPR, controlled cytotoxicity, and imaging capacity. Chiral hybrids—inorganic cores decorated with chiral ligands—excel in biological targeting, optical activity, and photoresponsive regulation. Since the first chiral‐ligand induction on gold clusters in 2014, syntheses have accelerated, enhancing tumor accumulation, membrane penetration, and immunity via macrophage modulation or TLR activation, while enabling real‐time image‐guided therapy. Recent chiral copper selenide nanoparticles selectively hit cancer‐associated fibroblasts, trigger ICD (Immunogenic Cell Death), and amplify PD‐L1 antibody efficacy, opening new avenues for tumor treatment [5].

Given the unique advantages of inorganic chiral nanomaterials (ICNMs) in stereoselective targeting, intrinsic immunoregulatory functions, and integrated theranostic capabilities, it is imperative to conduct a systematic analysis that goes beyond mere synthesis and characterization. Unlike existing reviews that primarily focus on material preparation or singular biological applications, this work establishes a comprehensive “chiral structure–immune regulation–therapeutic efficacy” framework. Specifically, we elucidate how nanoscale chirality precisely modulates immune cell functions, reprograms the tumor microenvironment at the molecular level, and reveals its synergistic mechanisms with existing immunotherapies. Furthermore, this review provides a forward‐looking perspective on the clinical translation pathway of ICNMs, critically assessing both opportunities and challenges. Such an integrated analysis is essential to guide the rational design of next‐generation chiral nanomedicines and optimize tumor immunotherapy strategies (Figure 1).

Inorganic Chiral Nanomaterials

Definition and Classification of ICNMs

Chirality, a three‐dimensional structural characteristic where molecules and their mirror images cannot be superimposed, serves as a key distinction between biological and non‐biological materials. In this respect, biological systems are built from chiral components like L‐amino acids and D‐sugars, whose inherent chirality drives physiologically relevant asymmetric biochemical reactions essential for disease treatment and pharmaceutical production [6]. Chiral materials can further impart chirality to INMs through asymmetric assembly or grafting of chiral molecular groups [7], enabling the generation of unique optical signals. Chirality induction in INMs occurs primarily via three mechanisms: intrinsic [8]; ligand—induced, and assembly—based [9]. Chiral NPs and nanocomposites, formed by non‐covalent aggregation of multiple NPs, are key parts of inorganic chiral nanomaterials (ICNMs). When characterizing, focus on their shapes and symmetry. ICNMs, classified by materials into various types, have great potential in biomedicine, optical imaging, and disease treatment.

Characteristics and Mechanisms of Drug Targeted Delivery Employing ICNMs

ICNMs utilize their precisely controllable nanoscale topological chirality to markedly enhance drug delivery through stereoselective interactions, with core advantages spanning chirality matching, endocytosis optimization, stable drug loading, and real‐time tracking [10]. Studies have demonstrated that in immune pathways, dodecyl‐capped cadmium selenide (D‐CdSe) quantum dots interact with the leucine‐rich repeat region of Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) on dendritic cells (DCs), activating the MyD88/NF‐κB signaling axis and boosting antigen cross‐presentation capacity [11].

Endocytic pathways (clathrin‐mediated endocytosis, pinocytosis, macropinocytosis) have distinct mechanisms. Chiral molecules/NPs selectively use specific routes; e.g., L‐Au NPs enhance clathrin recruitment for efficient antigen delivery to Antigen‐Presenting Cells (APCs) [12].

ICNMs' crystalline frameworks resist enzymatic degradation, preserving TLR agonist/cytokine activity and enabling chiral responses. L‐ZnO NPs increase drug accumulation via integrin recognition [13]. Chiral mSiO2 outperforms liposomes in immunomodulator loading. D‐MoS2's chiral H‐bond network with glutathione reduces burst release for precise PD‐L1 inhibitor delivery [14]. Finally, the Circular Dichroism (CD) signals of ICNMs dynamically reflect drug release states, enabling real‐time tracking at target sites. This approach optimizes immunotherapy dosing without the need for exogenous labeling, avoiding interference from fluorescence or isotopes and ensuring authentic immune signal transduction.

Characteristics and Mechanisms of Immune Stimulation

ICNMs not only facilitate the targeted delivery of immunomodulators, enhance drug accumulation within tumor tissues, and mitigate off‐target toxicity in non‐targeted areas, but also enable a more precise action of drugs on tumors. Furthermore, ICNMs can promote the uptake and presentation of antigens, augment immune cell responses, induce ICD in tumor cells, and release danger signal molecules such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP), high‐mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and calreticulin (CRT) [15]. These features amplify innate immunity and provide a spatiotemporally controlled platform to synergistically stimulate adaptive immune responses, significantly enhancing antitumor immune efficacy. Innate Immune Activation

Regulation of Macrophage Polarization

Macrophages play a critical role as immune cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME), with their polarization states classified into M1 and M2 types. These states directly influence tumor‐induced immune suppression and immune escape. M1 macrophages primarily exhibit pro‐inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antitumor activities, resisting pathogens (including intracellular bacteria and protozoa) and initiating antitumor immune responses. Conversely, M2 macrophages mainly exhibit anti‐inflammatory properties, promoting tissue repair, angiogenesis, and immune regulation. They are essential for tissue damage repair, wound healing, parasite immunity (through encapsulation), maintenance of immune tolerance, and resolution of inflammatory responses [16]. ICNMs direct this fate enantio‐selectively. L‐CeO2 nanoparticles activate Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 1 (STAT1), upregulating inducible Nitric Oxide Synthase (iNOS) and IL‐12 expression to promote M1 macrophage polarization and inhibit tumor growth. In 2025, Professor B. J. Thompson found that the right‐handed topology of L‐chiral CeO2 exhibits stronger binding to the leucine‐rich region of TLR4 on M1 macrophages, activating NF‐κB via a MyD88‐dependent pathway to drive M1 polarization [17].

Regulation of Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs)

Neutrophils represent a critical component of the innate immune system, and the NETs they release play a dual role in antitumor immunity: they can eliminate pathogens and tumor cells. However, excessive NET formation may promote tumor metastasis and immune suppression [18]. ICNMs can precisely regulate the dynamics of NET formation through a chirality‐dependent mechanism, thereby influencing the TME. Y. M. Ji's research team reported that D‐AuNRs could significantly enhance NET formation by activating the TLR9‐ROS‐PAD4 pathway, which promoted antigen exposure and inflammatory responses [19]. In contrast, in 2025, Professor N. Aggarwal discovered that L‐ZnO could inhibit excessive NET formation through steric hindrance, leading to a significant decrease in NET levels while alleviating chemotherapy‐induced tumor metastasis.

Regulation of Metabolic Reprogramming

In addition to directly regulating macrophage polarization and neutrophil function, ICNMs can also enhance antitumor immune activity by utilizing the metabolites generated through the metabolic reprogramming of immune cells. These metabolites act as extracellular and intracellular signaling molecules, coupling energy‐metabolic characteristics with phenotypic plasticity within the tumor microenvironment [20]. In 2024, Professor X. Wu found that systemic L‐mSiO2 nanoparticles inhibit IDO1, significantly elevating the tryptophan/kynurenine ratio, reversing CD8+ T‐cell paralysis, and substantially increasing IFN‐γ (Interferon‐γ) secretion [21]. Concurrent studies showed that L‐chiral gold nanoparticles (L‐AuNPs) stabilize HIF‐1α, pushing dendritic cells toward a glycolytic state that accelerates antigen processing, up‐regulates CD80/CD86, and heightens naïve T‐cell priming. Collectively, these findings establish ICNMs as dual‐function immunometabolic switches that correct metabolic dysfunction and reinvigorate antitumor immunity without exogenous intervention, offering a precise strategy for cancer immunotherapy.

Adaptive Immune Activation

Dendritic Cell Maturation and Antigen Presentation

Dendritic cells, which serve as pivotal bridges between innate and adaptive immunity, exhibit enhanced antigen presentation and T cell activation capacities following exposure to ICNMs. ICNMs serve as immunostimulatory “autoadjuvants” with encapsulated antigens, activating DCs without exogenous adjuvants. For instance, L‐mSiO2 and L‐AuNPs stereoselectively bind to the leucine repeat region of TLR4 on the surface of DCs, activating MyD88‐dependent pathways to upregulate CD80/CD86 and MHC‐II assembly. Another study revealed that D‐CeO2 nanosheets selectively engage C‐type lectin receptor CLEC9A, this leads to a significant increase in phosphorylated Syk and induces IL—12 secretion, driving Th1 polarization [22]. ICNMs further regulate endocytic pathways and enhance lysosomal escape, prolonging antigen‐MHC complex half‐life to boost CD8+ T cell activation. For instance, the helical pores of L—mSiO2 extend the antigen—MHC complex half—life and elevate CD8+ T cell proliferation [23].

T Cell Activation and Differentiation

ICNMs‐activated DCs provide strong secondary signals (e.g., enhanced CD80/CD86, 4‐1BBL) to promote naïve Treg activation and clonal expansion. D‐CdSe boosts the secretion of IL‐12 from DCs, driving Th1 differentiation and elevating CD8+ T cell granzyme B [24]. Chiral Bi@C NPs activate the cGAS‐STING‐IFN‐β‐ CXCL10 (Cyclic GMP–AMP Synthase—Stimulator of Interferon Genes—Interferon‐β—C‐X‐C Motif Chemokine Ligand 10) axis, enhancing CD8+ T tumor infiltration. L/D‐ZnO nanorods block Smad2/3 phosphorylation to inhibit TGF‐β signaling, reversing Treg‐mediated immunosuppression. Finally, chiral gold nanomaterials activate tumor‐surface CXCL10/CXCR3, amplifying CTL activity, granzyme B release, and antitumor efficacy [25].

Long‐Term Immune Memory

ICNMs are now understood to enhance long‐term T cell immune memory beyond acute activation. Interestingly, D‐CdSe has been reported to activate mTORC1 signaling, promoting T cell metabolic reprogramming and increasing CD62L+CCR7+ central memory T cells (TCM) [26]. Besides, L‐ZnO sustains demethylated states of memory genes (TCF7/LEF1) by inhibiting DNMT3a, boosting T cell self‐renewal. ICNMs also enable sustained cytokine release (e.g., IL‐7/IL‐15) for prolonged memory T cell survival. For instance, chiral gold nanorod vaccines maintain stable IL‐7/IL‐15 concentrations for more than 180 days, significantly stabilizing memory pools. In B16F10 melanoma models, mice vaccinated with these nanomaterials achieved 100% survival post‐rechallenge, with splenic memory T cells maintained at over 55% [27].

Combined Strategy Based on ICNMs

ICNMs have attracted significant interest in tumor immunotherapy due to their distinctive chiral structures and biocompatibility. Functioning as “multifunctional keys”, they enhance conventional therapies (e.g., radiotherapy/chemotherapy) while leveraging immunomodulatory capabilities to reconfigure the TME, thereby amplifying antitumor immune responses. This review systematically explores ICNMs' synergistic mechanisms in tumor immunotherapy.

Radiation‐Immunotherapy Synergy

Radiation therapy represents a pivotal modality in the treatment of cancer. Nonetheless, the efficacy of this approach is often hampered by tumor cell radiation resistance and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. In light of these challenges, the advent of ICNMs offers promising prospects for enhancing radiation‐immunotherapy combination treatments. Certain ICNMs containing high‐Z elements (e.g., Au, Pt, Bi, W) can enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy by increasing X‐ray absorption and the local radiation dose. Under irradiation, they boost ROS production significantly versus controls, inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress and promoting calreticulin flipping to release DAMPs [28]. Their intrinsic tumor‐targeting capability enables precise radiosensitization while minimizing damage to healthy tissues. Meanwhile, ICNMs possess the ability to activate and modulate the host immune system through multiple mechanisms, including the following aspects (Figure 3).

Reversing the Immunosuppressive Microenvironment

ICNMs enhance the TME by delivering oxygen carriers/catalase to alleviate hypoxia and depleting immunosuppressive cells (Tregs/MDSCs) while downregulating TGF‐β/IL‐10. Intriguingly, chiral MnO2 NPs reduce to Mn2+ within the TME, releasing H2O2 to intensify oxidative stress and induce ICD, thereby activating the STING pathway and upregulating IFN‐β. Chiral Au@SiO2 nanoparticles activate the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway in dendritic cells, significantly boosting IL‐12 secretion [29].

Synergistic Immune Activation

Current evidence suggests that radiotherapy‐induced ICD releases tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs), while ICNMs further enhance CD8+ T cell activation by promoting dendritic cell maturation and MHC‐I expression. This combination therapy also elicits abscopal effects, inhibiting metastatic growth and preventing recurrence. For instance, in Lewis lung carcinoma models, MnO2 NPs combined with radiotherapy reduced tumor volume and increased CD8+ T/NK cell infiltration [30]. In the treatment of 4T1 breast cancer, combining chiral AuNRs with radiotherapy significantly increased the complete regression rate, and no recurrence was observed within 60 days [31]. Moreover, a Phase I trial of chiral Bi@C‐based radio‐immunotherapy demonstrated a notable objective response rate in patients with advanced non‐small cell lung cancer [32].

Chemotherapy‐Immunotherapy Synergy

While chemotherapy remains the primary systemic treatment for malignancies, tumor chemoresistance and therapy‐induced immunosuppression limit its efficacy. Importantly, ICNMs leverage topology‐mediated stereoselective biointeractions to overcome these barriers and enhance chemo‐immunotherapy synergy, offering dual therapeutic advantages (Figure 4). ICNMs have chiral surface structures that act as precision‐guided “missiles”, this enhanced delivery facilitates the direct cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy while promoting the release of tumor antigens and danger signals to activate immune responses. The chiral nanocomposite L/D‐MnO2@Pt is selectively internalized by tumor cells, releasing Mn2+ and Pt via intracellular redox reactions. The Mn2+ further amplifies chemotherapeutic efficacy through Fenton‐like ROS generation [33]. Their helical structure enables pH‐responsive drug release in acidic tumors while activating the cGAS‐STING pathway, significantly increasing IFN‐β secretion and elevating CD80+/CD86+ ratios to amplify antitumor immunity. Immunotherapy‐Enhanced Synergistic Therapy

Immunotherapy is pivotal for tumor treatment, but its efficacy is limited by the immunosuppressive TME and low tumor immunogenicity, resulting in poor monotherapy response rates. It is now understood that ICNMs synergize with immunotherapies via multi‐target activation of immune cells to enhance antitumor efficacy (Figure 5). ICNMs synergistically enhance immune checkpoint blockade by inducing ICD and boosting tumor immunogenicity. Immune checkpoints, which are co‐inhibitory/co‐stimulatory receptors critical for immune balance, include the prominent CTLA‐4 and PD‐1/PD‐L1 axes, whose blockade promotes dendritic cell maturation and cytotoxic T cell activity. The helical pores within chiral mSiO2 show high efficiency in loading PD‐1 antibodies, significantly increasing the proportion of tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells and enhancing IFN‐γ secretion [35]. Meanwhile, D‐MoS2 precisely binds to the IgV domain of PD‐L1, effectively blocking the PD‐1/PD‐L1 interaction [36]. Nanovaccine adjuvants based on ICNMs can target specific immune recognition receptors (e.g., TLR2/4 and the cGAS‐STING signaling pathway), effectively addressing limitations associated with traditional vaccines that primarily induce humoral immunity. For instance, L‐chiral hydroxyaluminum oxide superparticles (L‐AlOOH SPs), developed by Sun Maozhong's research team at Jiangnan University, exhibited a distinct capacity to specifically bind to TLR2 on DCs. This interaction activates the NOD‐, LRR‐ and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and triggers lysosomal rupture, thereby enabling antigens' escape into the cytoplasm, which significantly enhances cross‐presentation efficiency for those antigens. Consequently, this process also promotes CD8+ T cell activation via the MHC‐I pathway. Phototherapy‐Immunotherapy Combination Therapy

Phototherapy, as a non‐invasive or minimally invasive treatment modality, can induce targeted heat in localized areas through light exposure or generate ROS to eliminate cancer cells [39]. However, the immune response it elicits is typically weak and challenging to sustain over extended periods. In this context, ICNMs synergize with phototherapy by enhancing ICD, specifically through the activation of pattern recognition receptor (PRR) pathways and the remodeling of the immunosuppressive TME. This interplay results in a robust and sustained antitumor immune response. The specific mechanisms involved are detailed as follows (Figure 6): Enhancing ICD: ICNMs synchronize nanoscale chirality with ROS to triple oxidative stress, amplifying PDT‐induced ICD and associated DAMPs. In 2022, Professors C. Li and Y. reported that chiral zinc‐oxide nanorods (L/D‐ZnO) combined with PDT elevated intracellular ROS three‐fold, dramatically increased CRT and HMGB1 presentation, and heightened downstream cytokine cues. Clinically, although large‐scale ICNM–PDT trials are still pending, a phase‐I study (NCT04246082) evaluating hypericin‐mediated PDT plus radiotherapy for head‐and‐neck cancer has already verified the feasibility and safety of PDT‐driven immune activation, providing an encouraging translational scaffold for future chiral‐nanomaterial protocols [40].ICNMs combined with phototherapy effectively reverse Treg‐ and M2‐mediated immune suppression, enhancing T cell functionality. In 2024, Professor H. Abdollahzadeh demonstrated that D‐chiral copper‐based nanocatalysts (D‐CuO) mimicking superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities alleviated PDT‐induced tumor hypoxia. This led to a phenotypic shift in macrophages away from the M2 state and a marked downregulation of PD‐L1 expression [41]. Additionally, Professor A. Nowak‐Terpiłowska found that L‐chiral bismuth selenide nanosheets (L‐Bi2Se3) specifically targeted and eliminated Tregs; when combined with PDT, this approach increased the CD8+/Treg ratio sevenfold and significantly prolonged survival in a liver cancer model [42]. However, clinical data specifically involving ICNMs remain limited, necessitating larger‐scale trials and long‐term evidence to establish safety and efficacy benchmarks for chiral nanomedicine in human oncology.

Nowak‐Terpiłowska found that L‐chiral bismuth selenide nanosheets (L‐Bi2Se3) specifically targeted and eliminated Tregs; when combined with PDT, this approach increased the CD8+/Treg ratio sevenfold and significantly prolonged survival in a liver cancer model [42]. However, clinical data specifically involving ICNMs remain limited, necessitating larger‐scale trials and long‐term evidence to establish safety and efficacy benchmarks for chiral nanomedicine in human oncology.

Conclusion

In recent years, ICNMs have made remarkable advances in anti‐tumor immunotherapy due to their unique topological chiral effects and programmable biological functional properties. These materials exhibit extraordinary potential and have achieved significant breakthroughs. This review aims to highlight the latest research trends concerning these materials and provides an in‐depth analysis of their specific applications in anti‐tumor immunotherapy. By precisely regulating chiral parameters such as CD and Δg values and helical conformations, ICNMs can facilitate multi‐level synergistic treatment modalities. This innovation brings new hope for tumor therapy across various dimensions, including targeted delivery, activation of ICD, remodeling of the immune microenvironment, and enhancement of synergistic treatments. However, the clinical translation of ICNMs encounters numerous challenges. First, prolonged circulation drives hepatic and splenic accumulation; cadmium‐ion leakage from CdSe QDs exemplifies potential organ or systemic toxicity [43]. Second, the immune‐stimulation threshold that correlates with specific g‐values remains a black box, hampering dose‐selection protocols [44]. Third, current synthesis methods for chiral nanomaterials lack unified standards, and the relationship between Δg values and therapeutic efficacy is not well understood [45]. Fourth, protein crown adsorption under physiological conditions diminishes the targeting capability of nanomaterials, resulting in a reduced accumulation rate [46]. Besides, in multi‐mechanism synergistic therapy, it is imperative to balance drug loading (> 90%), controlled‐release kinetics (e.g., ROS/pH response), and timing for immune activation to optimize therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, the vast majority of proofs‐of‐concept stem from rodent studies whose hyper‐reactive immune systems poorly predict human responses, complicating safety‐efficacy projections [47]. Despite these obstacles, ICNMs present a novel and promising approach for anti‐tumor immunotherapy. Given that single‐agent immunotherapies often fail to achieve optimal results due to the inherent variability in tumorigenesis, synergistic immunotherapy is anticipated to emerge as an essential strategy for future cancer treatments. However, effectively integrating diverse treatment mechanisms and materials into a cohesive nanoscale system while ensuring rationality and synergy to achieve efficient synergistic immunotherapy presents a substantial obstacle yet to be overcome.
