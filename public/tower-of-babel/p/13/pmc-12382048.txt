Advances in novel cell death mechanisms in breast cancer: intersecting perspectives on ferroptosis, cuproptosis, disulfidptosis, and pyroptosis
Authors: Zhengjun Jing, Wenqin Huang, Jun Mei, Sudhanshu Bhushan, Xinhong Wu, Chengbiao Yan, Hongmei Zheng, Yalong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe advent of molecular classification has ushered breast cancer treatment into the era of precision medicine. Nevertheless, clinical management continues to face significant challenges posed by drug resistance and tumor heterogeneity. Recent advances have identified novel programmed cell death (PCD) mechanisms-including ferroptosis, cuproptosis, disulfidptosis, and pyroptosis-as critical regulators of breast cancer progression, therapeutic responsiveness, and immune microenvironment remodeling. This review systematically elucidates the mechanistic foundations of these PCD pathways and investigates their molecular regulatory networks within breast cancer pathogenesis. Furthermore, we evaluate current targeted intervention strategies and assess their clinical translation potential, ultimately providing a theoretical framework for pioneering therapeutic approaches.Keywords: Breast cancer, Programmed cell death, Ferroptosis, Cuproptosis, Disulfidptosis, Pyroptosis

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy in terms of both incidence and mortality among women worldwide. According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 data released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, there were approximately 2.308 million new cases of breast cancer (accounting for 11.6% of all cancers) and 665,684 deaths, ranking first among female cancers [1]. Statistics from the National Cancer Center of China indicate that in 2022, there were approximately 423,000 new cases and 117,000 deaths from breast cancer in China, with a trend toward younger onset age [2]. Although early-stage breast cancer can achieve a high cure rate through surgery combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy, advanced patients still face severe challenges such as recurrence, metastasis, and drug resistance. For example, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which lacks expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), is insensitive to traditional endocrine therapy and targeted therapy, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 30% [3]. Although HER2-positive breast cancer can improve prognosis through targeted drugs such as trastuzumab, approximately 30%-50% of patients experience treatment failure due to secondary drug resistance [4]. Additionally, immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies) are currently considered partially effective only in metastatic breast cancer with high PD-L1 expression, with an overall response rate of less than 20% [5]. These therapeutic plateau are closely related to the high heterogeneity of breast cancer, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and cell death escape mechanisms, highlighting the urgent need to explore novel therapeutic targets.

Programmed cell death (PCD) is a core mechanism for maintaining tissue homeostasis, and its dysregulation is a key factor in tumor development, metastasis, and resistance. Traditional PCD types, such as apoptosis, mediate cell clearance through the mitochondrial pathway (regulated by Bcl-2/Bax) or the death receptor pathway (e.g., Fas/FasL), and the efficacy of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted drugs (e.g., paclitaxel, PARP inhibitors) relies on the effective activation of PCD [6]. However, tumor cells can evade apoptosis by upregulating anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-2, MCL-1) or mutating pro-apoptotic genes (e.g., TP53), leading to treatment resistance [7]. In recent years, novel PCD mechanisms such as ferroptosis, cuproptosis, disulfidptosis, and pyroptosis have been successively discovered. These mechanisms induce tumor cell death through unique molecular pathways and are closely related to treatment sensitivity in apoptosis-resistant tumors [8–12]. For example, ferroptosis inducers can selectively kill chemotherapy-resistant breast cancer stem cells by targeting glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4); pyroptosis reshapes the TME by releasing pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-18), enhancing the efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors [9–12]. These findings suggest that targeting novel PCD mechanisms holds promise for overcoming the limitations of traditional therapies and providing new directions for precision treatment.

In recent years, with in-depth research on tumor metabolism and microenvironment regulation, various non-apoptotic PCD mechanisms have been revealed, offering new perspectives for breast cancer treatment through their classification and functional characteristics (Table 1). Based on their molecular triggering mechanisms, novel PCD can be categorized into: (1) metabolism-dependent death, such as ferroptosis (driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation) and cuproptosis (copper ion-mediated mitochondrial protein toxicity); (2) redox stress-induced death, such as disulfidptosis (cell cytoskeleton collapse due to abnormal disulfide accumulation); and (3) inflammation-related death, such as pyroptosis (Gasdermin protein-mediated membrane perforation and inflammatory factor release) [12–15]. These mechanisms exhibit significant subtype-specific regulation in breast cancer. Ferroptosis induces selective killing in TNBC by targeting the cystine/glutamate antiporter (System Xc⁻), cuproptosis-dependent mitochondrial respiratory chain inhibition can overcome trastuzumab resistance in HER2-positive breast cancer, and pyroptosis enhances immunotherapy sensitivity in HR-positive breast cancer by activating CD8⁺ T cell immune responses [14, 15, 17]. Additionally, disulfidptosis is closely related to SLC7A11-mediated redox imbalance in the pre-metastatic microenvironment of breast cancer, providing a new target for inhibiting metastasis [12]. These discoveries not only reveal the cell death evasion mechanisms underlying breast cancer heterogeneity but also lay the theoretical foundation for developing subtype-specific combination therapies (e.g., ferroptosis inducers combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors).

This article provides a comprehensive analysis of the research progress and correlations between breast cancer and different PCD mechanisms, discusses the clinical significance and potential of PCD intervention in breast cancer treatment, and explores the potential applications of synergistic interactions among different PCD mechanisms.

Ferroptosis and breast cancer

Molecular mechanisms of ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, non-apoptotic form of PCD, characterized by three core features: iron dys-metabolism, accumulation of lipid peroxidation, and inactivation of GPX4 (mechanism shown in Fig. 1).

Iron dys-metabolism

Iron dys-metabolism

The excessive accumulation of intracellular free iron (Fe²⁺) is a triggering factor for ferroptosis. Iron enters cells through transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1)-mediated endocytosis and is released as Fe²⁺ in the acidic environment of lysosomes. The iron storage protein ferritin releases Fe²⁺ through a degradation pathway regulated by hepcidin, while the iron exporter ferroportin 1 (FPN1) is responsible for iron efflux from cells [9, 18]. In breast cancer, upregulation of TFR1 expression and inhibition of FPN1 expression lead to iron overload, which subsequently generates ROS through the Fenton reaction. In TNBC cells, the overexpression of hepcidin significantly reduces ferritin stability, exacerbating iron-dependent oxidative damage [19].

(2)Accumulation of lipid peroxidation

Accumulation of lipid peroxidation

The core execution mechanism of ferroptosis is the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids (PUFA-PLs). Long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 4 (ACSL4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) catalyze the esterification of PUFAs with phospholipids, generating oxidation-prone PUFA-PLs. Subsequently, lipoxygenases (e.g.,ALOX15) or cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (POR) catalyze the peroxidation of PUFA-PLs in the presence of Fe²⁺, forming lethal lipid peroxides (e.g., 4-HNE, MDA) [9]. Studies have shown that ACSL4 is highly expressed in TNBC, and its silencing significantly inhibits ferroptosis sensitivity [20, 21].

(3)Inactivation of GPX4

Inactivation of GPX4

GPX4 is a key enzyme that inhibits ferroptosis by reducing glutathione (GSH) to convert lipid peroxides (PL-OOH) into non-toxic lipid alcohols (PL-OH) [9]. The activity of GPX4 relies on cystine provided by System Xc⁻ (a cystine/glutamate antiporter composed of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2), which is converted into GSH within cells. Inhibition of System Xc⁻ (e.g., Erastin) or direct targeting of GPX4 (e.g., RSL3) can block the antioxidant defense, leading to uncontrolled lipid peroxidation [21, 22]. Notably, GPX4 is highly expressed in breast cancer stem cells and enhance neoplastic cell proliferation, serving as an important mechanism for their resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [9, 21, 22].

Regulation of ferroptosis in breast cancer

The regulation of ferroptosis in breast cancer exhibits significant subtype specificity, with HER2-positive and TNBC showing heightened sensitivity to ferroptosis and synergistic effects with chemotherapy/immunotherapy becoming a research focus.

Sensitivity of HER2-positive and TNBC breast cancer to ferroptosis

Sensitivity of HER2-positive and TNBC breast cancer to ferroptosis

HER2-positive breast cancer is characterized by HER2 receptor overexpression, leading to sustained activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, which promotes cell proliferation and survival [23, 24]. However, this pathway also exacerbates intracellular iron accumulation by downregulating ferritin heavy chain (FTH1) and upregulating transferrin receptor (TFR1), making cells more susceptible to ferroptosis inducers (Erastin and RSL3) [24, 25]. Studies have shown that the HER2 inhibitor enhances ferroptosis sensitivity by inhibiting the NRF2/GPX4 pathway, and its combination with RSL3 significantly reduces tumor volume in mouse xenografts [24–26]. Additionally, ACSL4 expression levels in HER2-positive breast cancer positively correlate with ferroptosis sensitivity, and silencing ACSL4 completely abolishes Erastin-induced cell death [24]. Studies have demonstrated that oleic acid secreted by adipocytes surrounding TNBC cells significantly inhibits lipid peroxidation, thereby suppressing the ferroptosis process [27]. Contrary to the HER2-positive subtype, in TNBC, ACSL4 utilizes ATP to esterify free fatty acids to CoA and regulates ferroptosis occurrence by influencing the synthesis of long-chain PL-PUFAs [20]. Concurrently, GPX4 expression is significantly upregulated in the TNBC subtype. Inhibiting GPX4 expression can induce ferroptosis, an effect particularly pronounced in the luminal androgen receptor (LAR) subtype of TNBC. Furthermore, studies indicate that combining GPX4 inhibitors with αPD-1 immunotherapy enhances the immunotherapeutic efficacy in TNBC [27].

(2)Synergistic effects of ferroptosis inducers with chemotherapy/immunotherapy

Synergistic effects of ferroptosis inducers with chemotherapy/immunotherapy

Ferroptosis inducers enhance the efficacy of traditional therapies through multiple mechanisms. First, they exhibit synergy with chemotherapy. Erastin combined with paclitaxel achieves dual killing via lipid peroxidation and mitotic catastrophe by inhibiting System Xc⁻ and microtubule stability in TNBC [28]. Second, ferroptosis inducers synergize with immunotherapy in breast cancer. RSL3-induced ferroptosis releases damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs, e.g., HMGB1), promoting dendritic cell maturation and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. Preclinical studies showed that RSL3 combined with PD-1 inhibitors increases the complete response rate in HER2-negative breast cancer mouse models [29]. Furthermore, ferroptosis inducers can reverse drug resistance. In doxorubicin-resistant breast cancer cells, upregulation of GPX4 is a major resistance mechanism, and the GPX4 inhibitor ML162 restores sensitivity to doxorubicin while triggering mitochondrial lipid peroxidation [30].

Challenges and prospects

Although targeting ferroptosis offers new strategies for breast cancer treatment, its clinical translation still faces core challenges such as the lack of biomarkers and tissue-specific regulatory differences.

Limitations of ferroptosis biomarkers

Limitations of ferroptosis biomarkers

Current detection of ferroptosis relies on indirect indicators, such as lipid peroxidation products (MDA, 4-HNE) or iron metabolism-related proteins (e.g., TFR1, FTH1), but these markers lack specificity and are easily confounded by other oxidative stress pathways (e.g., necroptosis) [9, 18]. For example, lipid peroxidation caused by GPX4 inhibition occurs in both breast cancer cells and normal breast epithelial cells, making it difficult to distinguish the therapeutic window from toxicity thresholds. Recent studies suggest that ACSL4 may serve as a predictive marker for ferroptosis sensitivity, but its expression varies dramatically between TNBC and HER2-positive breast cancer (70% positivity in TNBC but only 30% in HER2-positive subtypes), limiting its universal applicability [23, 26]. Additionally, the dynamic regulation of ferroptosis (e.g., iron fluctuations, transient GSH depletion) makes static markers inadequate for accurately reflecting in vivo processes.

(2)Complexity of tissue-specific regulatory differences

Complexity of tissue-specific regulatory differences

Breast cancer subtypes and microenvironments significantly influence ferroptosis responses. TNBC is highly sensitive to ferroptosis due to high ACSL4 and low GPX4 expression, while Luminal subtypes exhibit intrinsic resistance by upregulating antioxidant genes via the NRF2 pathway [28–30]. The immune microenvironment also plays a crucial role that tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) secrete lactate to inhibit ferroptosis in cancer cells, whereas CD8⁺ T cells release IFN-γ to downregulate SLC7A11, enhancing ferroptosis sensitivity [31]. Moreover, breast cancer cells can evade ferroptosis by upregulating the iron efflux protein FPN1 or activating autophagy-dependent ferritin degradation, leading to treatment resistance [32].

(3)Future directions

Future directions

To address these challenges, the following strategies hold promise for advancing the clinical translation of ferroptosis-based therapies. Firstly, multi-omics biomarkers could be developed by integrating lipidomics (detecting PUFA-PL profiles), metabolomics (GSH/cystine ratio), and imaging (MRI quantitative T2 for iron deposition) to establish dynamic monitoring systems [33–35]. Secondly, subtype-specific nanodelivery systems which have being investigated showed promising value, such as HER2-targeted antibody-conjugated liposomes encapsulating RSL3, to selectively kill HER2-positive breast cancer cells [29]. Thirdly, reprogram tumor microenvironment can be reprogrammed by combining ferroptosis inducers with TAM polarization modulators (e.g., CSF-1R inhibitors) to disrupt protective niches. As discussed above, ACSL4 showed significant predictive dysregulation in different subtypes of breast cancer as well as SLC7A11, though HER2 + and TNBC showed different expression levels of them. Multi-omics analysis will further verify the predictive value of these specific genes or proteins, or even provide us novel genes which show stable protective function of ferroptosis.

Cuproptosis and breast cancer

Mechanisms of cuproptosis

Cuproptosis is a copper ion-dependent form of programmed cell death first defined by Tsvetkov’s team in 2022 [14]. Its core mechanism involves copper ion (Cu²⁺)-induced mitochondrial protein toxic stress and ferredoxin 1 (FDX1)-dependent regulation, demonstrating unique potential in breast cancer treatment (mechanism shown in Fig. 2) [11, 33, 34, 36].

Copper ion-mediated mitochondrial protein aggregation

Copper ion-mediated mitochondrial protein aggregation

Excess copper ions enter cells via membrane copper transporters (e.g., CTR1) and directly bind to lipoylated TCA cycle enzymes (such as pyruvate dehydrogenase complex [PDH] and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase [OGDH]) within the mitochondrial matrix. This binding results in the loss of enzyme activity and the formation of insoluble protein aggregates. These aggregates impair mitochondrial respiratory chain function, leading to ATP depletion and a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS), ultimately triggering cell death (Fig. 2). Research indicates that mitochondrial copper levels in breast cancer cells are significantly elevated compared to normal cells, with higher expression of the copper transporter ATP7A in HER2-positive subtypes further exacerbating copper ion accumulation than other subtypes [37].

(2)Central regulatory role of FDX1

Central regulatory role of FDX1

FDX1 is a pivotal regulatory molecule in cuproptosis, with multiple critical functions. Firstly, it catalyzes lipoylation pathway of cells. Acting as an electron carrier, FDX1 reduces lipoic acid to promote the lipoylation of TCA cycle enzymes, enhancing their affinity for copper ions [38]. Secondly, it amplifies cuproptosis signaling. Knockout of FDX1 could completely abolish copper ion-induced protein aggregation and cell death, whereas FDX1 overexpression increased breast cancer cell sensitivity to copper ions [39]. Additionally, FDX1 interacts with copper metabolism. It collaborates with the copper chaperone COX17 to regulate mitochondrial copper homeostasis, and inhibition of COX17 diminishes FDX1 activity, mitigating copper toxicity [40].

Abnormal copper metabolism in breast cancer

Dysregulation of copper metabolic homeostasis is a key driver of breast cancer progression and metastasis. This is primarily characterized by aberrant expression of copper transporters and activation of copper-dependent signaling pathways, offering novel avenues for targeted therapies. Cuproptosis sensitivity in breast cancer is influenced by several factors. Fristly, FDX1 expression levels showed critical functions, with FDX1 mRNA expression in TNBC being lower than in Luminal breast cancer, suggesting subtype-specific resistance mechanisms [37]. Secondly, the concentrations of copper directly regulates the cuproptosis with copper chelators, such as tetrathiomolybdate (TM), which reduces intracellular free copper levels and inhibits cuproptosis, though resistance can be reversed by combining TM with the copper ionophore Elesclomol [41]. Additionally, cuproptosis communicates with metabolic reprogramming intracellular, where breast cancers with enhanced glycolysis suppress TCA cycle activity, reducing lipoylated protein production and enabling escape from cuproptosis [43].

Correlation between high expression of copper transporter ATP7A and metastasis

Correlation between high expression of copper transporter ATP7A and metastasis

ATP7A, a critical transporter regulating intracellular copper efflux, is significantly overexpressed in breast cancer and positively correlates with metastatic potential [37]. Clinical data revealed that ATP7A expression in tumor tissues of metastatic breast cancer patients was much higher than in primary tumors, with high expression strongly associated with reduced disease-free survival [37]. ATP7A may facilitate metastasis through multiple potential pathways: (1) copper-dependent activation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), where copper ions activate lysyl oxidase (LOX) to induce collagen cross-linking and matrix remodeling, promoting EMT [37, 43]; (2) regulation of angiogenesis, where inhibition of ATP7A-mediated copper efflux elevates intracellular copper levels, upregulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) expression to drive tumor angiogenesis [44]; and (3) colonization of metastatic sites, as ATP7A knockdown reduces lung metastasis rates in breast cancer cells in mouse models, underscoring its essential role in metastatic colonization [37].

(2)Translational potential of copper-targeted therapeutic strategies

Translational potential of copper-targeted therapeutic strategies

Therapeutic strategies targeting abnormal copper metabolism include the synergistic application of the copper chelator TM and copper ionophores. TM forms copper-molybdenum complexes to lower free copper levels, inhibiting LOX activity [42]. A clinical trial demonstrated that TM combined with carboplatin extended median progression-free survival (mPFS) to 8.1 months in metastatic TNBC, compared to 5.3 months in the control group [45]. Additionally, TM reverses ATP7A-mediated copper resistance, reducing the half-maximal inhibitory concentration of paclitaxel in resistant cells [46]. Elesclomol, a mitochondrial-targeted copper carrier, induces cuproptosis via an FDX1-dependent mechanism. Preclinical studies show that Elesclomol outperforms trastuzumab in resistant HER2-positive breast cancer models, reducing tumor volume in mice tumor model. When combined with the PARP inhibitor olaparib, Elesclomol synergistically induces DNA damage and cuproptosis, elevating the complete response rate in BRCA1-mutant breast cancer than monotherapy groups [47, 48].

Challenges and prospects

Challenges and optimization directions

Challenges and optimization directions

Similar to ferroptosis, cuproptosis exhibits subtype-specific response variability. Luminal breast cancer type shows reduced sensitivity to Elesclomol due to low FDX1 expression, necessitating the development of subtype-selective carriers [47]. Copper chelators may induce anemia and neurotoxicity, highlighting the need for optimized delivery approaches, such as localized nanodelivery [47, 48]. Meanwhile, biomarker development of cuproptosis in breast cancer model remains incomplete. However, predictive models based on ATP7A/FDX1 expression profiles offer potential for guiding personalized treatment and hold promise for clinical application [49].

(2) Research gaps

Research gaps

Although cuproptosis provides a novel perspective for breast cancer therapy, significant gaps persist in understanding its subtype-specific regulatory mechanisms and clinical translation.

Subtype heterogeneity in cuproptosis sensitivity has not been systematically analyzed

Subtype heterogeneity in cuproptosis sensitivity has not been systematically analyzed

Existing studies highlighted marked differences in cuproptosis responses across breast cancer subtypes, yet the molecular drivers of this heterogeneity remain undefined. Firstly, FDX1 expression levels present contradictions among these molecular subtypes. While FDX1 mRNA levels are lower in TNBC, some clinical samples indicated a negative correlation between FDX1 protein expression and cuproptosis sensitivity, suggesting post-transcriptional regulation (e.g., miRNA-mediated translational suppression) [14, 37, 39]. Secondly, the metabolic microenvironment influences the outcomes of anti-cuproptosis in breast cancer. Despite high ATP7A expression in HER2-positive breast cancer, its glycolytic dominance may attenuate cuproptosis by inhibiting the TCA cycle, resulting in intra-subtype therapeutic variability [50]. Lastly, cross-subtype data are lacking. Current research predominantly focuses on TNBC and HER2-positive subtypes, leaving the cuproptosis regulatory network in Luminal breast cancer largely unexplored.

(2)Dynamic regulatory mechanisms of cuproptosis and microenvironment interactions are unclear

Dynamic regulatory mechanisms of cuproptosis and microenvironment interactions are unclear

The influence of the TME on cuproptosis remains unclarified. The role of immune cells is uncertain as well. CD8⁺ T cells release interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) to downregulate SLC7A11 and promote ferroptosis, but its impact on cuproptosis is still on debated [37, 50]. In the triple-negative breast cancer model, IFN-γ activates the JAK-STAT signaling pathway to induce the expression of transcription factors (including ATF3 and p53), which transcriptionally repress SLC7A11 expression [51]. Mechanistically, IFN-γ-mediated SLC7A11 downregulation reduces cellular GSH levels, impairing the capacity to scavenge copper-catalyzed reactive oxygen species (ROS). This renders cells vulnerable to oxidative stress and potentiates their susceptibility to copper toxicity. Concurrently, diminished SLC7A11 expression may perturb cystine metabolic flux, indirectly affecting TCA cycle intermediates or energy metabolism, thereby sensitizing cells to copper-dependent mitochondrial respiratory vulnerability. Furthermore, IFN-γ potentially modulates other copper metabolism-related genes (e.g., copper transporter CTR1 and ATP7A/B), leading to intracellular copper accumulation or altered distribution. This synergistic regulation enhances the efficacy of cuproptosis inducers [51].

The mechanisms of tumor stromal cells communicated with cuproptosis are also unknown. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) may secrete copper-binding proteins to buffer microenvironmental copper levels, potentially shielding cancer cells from cuproptosis [12, 52, 53]. In the oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) model, CAF-derived exosomal miR-148b-3p downregulates ATP7A expression in tumor cells, thereby inhibiting copper efflux, increasing intracellular copper accumulation, and ultimately promoting cuproptosis. This mechanism suppresses the invasive and metastatic potential of OSCC cells [12]. In contrast, studies in breast cancer models reveal that CAF-cuproptosis crosstalk significantly influences tumor progression, particularly by reprogramming the immune microenvironment (e.g., suppressing CD8⁺ T cells and promoting Tregs) and facilitating metastatic niche formation [53]. However, these studies largely overlook the heterogeneity of CAF subpopulations and their divergent effects. For instance, TGF-β-secreting myofibroblastic CAFs may buffer microenvironmental copper levels by upregulating metallothioneins (MTs), whereas inflammatory CAFs could instead promote cuproptosis [54]. Furthermore, in breast cancer models, SLC31A1 (a key copper importer) is upregulated via a ceRNA network (e.g., the lncRNA MIR210HG/miR-570-3p axis), enhancing copper influx and suppressing T cell activity. Notably, SLC31A1-high patients exhibit a significantly higher incidence of lung metastasis.

Furthermore, spatiotemporal heterogeneity in breast cancer also poses challenges. Copper ion distribution varies between primary tumors and metastatic sites (e.g., brain metastases exhibit higher copper content than bone metastases), yet its effect on treatment response lacks longitudinal investigation [55]. Under physiological conditions and across various disease models, copper concentrations exhibit significant variations among different human tissues, including brain and bone. Moreover, copper transporters and core cuproptosis markers (e.g., FDX1) display distinct expression patterns across tissues. However, high copper content does not necessarily correlate with cuproptosis susceptibility. On one hand, in brain metastases, immunosuppressive cells (e.g., microglia)secreteIL-10, inhibiting CD8⁺T cell infiltration and impairing the immunogenic cell death (ICD) effect associated with cuproptosis. In contrast, in bone metastases, CAFs highly express metallothioneins (MTs), which chelate copper ions and protect tumor cells. On the other hand, in brain metastases, elevated DLAT oligomerization is counteracted by microenvironmental antioxidant factors (e.g., glutathione), attenuating the efficacy of copper ionophores. Meanwhile, in bone metastases, the low-copper environment and osteoblast-derived protective factors (e.g., bone morphogenetic proteins, BMPs) further reduce cuproptosis susceptibility [56, 57]. Nevertheless, the driving mechanisms of cuproptosis in primary tumors and metastatic niches remain unclear. Specifically, how metastasis-specific cells (e.g., astrocytes in the brain, osteoclasts in bone) regulate copper transporters (e.g., CTR1, ATP7A) and execution proteins (e.g., FDX1, DLAT) is poorly understood.

(3)Clinical translation bottlenecks need to be addressed

Clinical translation bottlenecks need to be addressed

As with the aforementioned lack of specific biomarkers for ferroptosis, a robust biomarker framework is absent. Current markers, such as serum ceruloplasmin, fail to differentiate tumor-specific copper accumulation from systemic copper metabolic disorders, complicating patient stratification [58]. Secondly, balancing toxicity and efficacy remains problematic. Elesclomol was discontinued in phase II trials due to cardiotoxicity, necessitating tumor-targeted delivery systems [59]. Moreover, optimization of combination strategies needs future clinical trials, such as the synergy between cuproptosis and PARP inhibitors has been confirmed only in BRCA-mutant models, with no evidence of benefit in wild-type patients [60].

Disulfidptosis and breast cancer

Current research on Disulfidptosis in breast cancer

Disulfidptosis is a novel form of cell death first reported by Liu’s team in 2023. Its core mechanism involves disulfide stress-induced abnormal cross-linking of cytoskeletal proteins, demonstrating unique targeting potential in breast cancer therapy [12].

Molecular definition of disulfidptosis

Molecular definition of disulfidptosis

Disulfidptosis is characterized by the abnormal intracellular accumulation of disulfides (e.g., cystine), which leads to non-physiological disulfide bond cross-linking of cytoskeletal proteins such as actin (ACTB) and cofilin. This disrupts cell morphology and motility, ultimately resulting in membrane rupture and cell death. The process relies on the following conditions: depletion of reduced glutathione (GSH), a critical molecule for maintaining thiol balance. When GSH levels drop below a threshold, the disulfide/thiol ratio becomes imbalanced, triggering a stress response [12]. Additionally, insufficient NADPH production due to glucose metabolism disorders (e.g., downregulation of SLC2A1) restricts the pentose phosphate pathway, reducing NADPH supply and impairing disulfide reduction capacity (mechanism shown in Fig. 3) [51].

(2)Regulation of disulfide stress in breast cancer

Regulation of disulfide stress in breast cancer

Disulfidptosis activation in breast cancer is governed by a redox homeostasis network centered on SLC7A11-mediated cystine uptake and NADPH-dependent disulfide reduction [51]. Firstly, different molecular types of breast cancer showed differential expression profile, that SLC7A11 mRNA expression level was higher in the TNBC and Luminal B, and gain copy number in HER2 positive [61]. SLC7A11 overexpression may also confer resistance in breast cancer. In TNBC, SLC7A11 is significantly overexpressed, leading to enhanced cystine import and GSH synthesis. Clinical cohort studies demonstrate that high SLC7A11 expression correlates with reduced 5-year overall survival and lower response rates to platinum-based chemotherapy [12, 61]. Mechanistically, SLC7A11 sustains intracellular GSH pools, which scavenge disulfide stressors (e.g., cystine overload), thereby suppressing abnormal ACTB cross-linking and disulfidptosis [62]. Secondly, metabolic vulnerabilities in breast cancer could induce therapeutic effect. Glucose deprivation or GLUT1 inhibition (e.g., BAY-876) disrupts NADPH regeneration via the pentose phosphate pathway. In SLC7A11-high TNBC models, GLUT1 suppression reduces NADPH levels, lowering the GSH/GSSG ratio. This redox shift elevates disulfide stress, increasing ACTB cross-linking compared to normal mammary epithelium [52]. Combining the cystine dioxygenase activator CDO1 with GLUT1 inhibition achieves synergistic disulfidptosis, reducing TNBC xenograft volume [63].

(3)Translational medicine advances in targeting disulfidptosis

Translational medicine advances in targeting disulfidptosis

Research has progressed in developing specific small-molecule inhibitors. For example, the SLC7A11-targeting compound erastin-derivative 16a selectively induces disulfidptosis in TNBC cells [63]. Nanodelivery systems provide more precise and less toxic applications. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) loaded with CDO1 mRNA enhance disulfide stress by locally degrading cystine, achieving complete tumor regression in TNBC orthotopic models [64]. Regarding biomarkers, serum levels of ACTB cross-linked products (e.g., ACTB-S-S-ACTB) negatively correlate with TNBC treatment response, suggesting their potential as predictive markers [65].

Potential roles in breast cancer

Based on the above, the regulation of disulfidptosis in breast cancer is tightly linked to SLC7A11-mediated redox homeostasis. Its aberrant expression is a key mechanism by which breast cancer cells evade cell death, offering novel strategies for targeted intervention.

High expression of SLC7A11 and resistance to disulfidptosis

High expression of SLC7A11 and resistance to disulfidptosis

SLC7A11 transports extracellular cystine into cells for GSH synthesis. In breast cancer patients, high SLC7A11 expression is significantly associated with reduced overall survival and a decrease in response rates to platinum-based chemotherapy [66]. In breast cancer, elevated SLC7A11 inhibits disulfidptosis mainly via the following pathways. Firstly, GSH-dependent antioxidant defense plays the most basic and critical role. SLC7A11 overexpression increases GSH levels in TNBC cells, neutralizing disulfide stress (e.g., cystine overload) and reducing ACTB cross-linking [67]. Secondly, SLC7A11 could support for NADPH metabolism. SLC7A11 sustains the cystine-glutamate cycle, boosting NADPH production and enhancing disulfide reduction capacity [73−−76].

(2)Strategies for inducing disulfidptosis by targeting SLC7A11

Strategies for inducing disulfidptosis by targeting SLC7A11

The use of small-molecule inhibitors shows promise based on the importance of SLC7A11 in breast cancer. Due to the intersection between ferroptosis and disulfidptosis pathways (the interplay among different PCD modalities will be elaborated in subsequent sections), studies have employed ferroptosis inducers to concomitantly trigger disulfidptosis, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy against breast cancer. The erastin derivative PE6 selectively inhibits SLC7A11’s cystine transport function, reducing GSH levels in TNBC cells and increasing ACTB cross-linking [68]. Sulfasalazine, an FDA-approved drug, competitively binds SLC7A11 to block cystine uptake. When combined with radiotherapy, it reduces tumor volume in colon adenocarcinoma and other cancer types in mouse models significantly [69].

(3)Challenges and clinical translation potential

Challenges and clinical translation potential

Even the regulation of disulfidptosis showed significant effect in preclinic models, resistance mechanisms still need to be uncover. Some SLC7A11-low breast cancer cells upregulate cystathionine gamma-lyase (CTH) to synthesize cysteine, evading disulfidptosis [70]. Meanwhile, like other PCD types, disulfidptosis also showed distinct varieties and biomarkers in different subtypes of breast cancer need development. A combined scoring model based on SLC7A11 expression and ACTB cross-linked products predicts treatment response, which showed promising clinic value. Most important of all is the control of toxicities, that SLC7A11 inhibitors may cause oxidative damage to normal tissues, necessitating tumor-targeted delivery systems (e.g., HER2 antibody-conjugated nanoparticles).

Future directions

The translational application of disulfidptosis in breast cancer therapy faces challenges such as inadequate targeting and systemic toxicity. Nanomedicine targeting SLC7A11 and disulfide metabolism offers new avenues to address these limitations. Key future research directions include:

Design of smart responsive nanocarriers

Design of smart responsive nanocarriers

Functionalized nanocarriers enable spatiotemporal precision. The nanodrug delivery system demonstrates consistent efficacy across multiple disease models. pH-sensitive liposomes encapsulating SLC7A11 siRNA release specifically in the acidic tumor microenvironment, improving silencing efficiency compared to non-responsive carriers. Redox-triggered degradation and disulfide-containing polymeric nanoparticles (e.g., PEG-SS-PLGA) degrade in high-GSH tumor environments, releasing CDO1 to induce localized disulfide stress [71].

(2)Multi-mechanism synergistic nanotherapy strategies

Multi-mechanism synergistic nanotherapy strategies

As discussed above, therapy strategy of ferroptosis-disulfidptosis combination showed positive feedback in breast cancer model. Nanoparticles co-loaded with SLC7A11 inhibitor (PE6) and ferroptosis inducer (RSL3) synergistically deplete GSH and induce lipid peroxidation, achieving a high cell death rate in TNBC [72]. Emerging evidence suggests that activation of the disulfidptosis pathway can potently stimulate T cells in the tumor microenvironment, highlighting the critical role of immune-metabolic crosstalk. Nanogels loaded with CDO1 mRNA and PD-L1 inhibitors induce disulfidptosis to release tumor antigens while blocking immune checkpoints, increasing CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in mouse models [73]. Given that disulfidptosis fundamentally arises from metabolic dysregulation, reprogramming metabolic pathways also plays a pivotal role. Nanosystems co-loaded with glucose oxidase (GOx) and SLC7A11 inhibitors consume glucose to inhibit NADPH production, boosting disulfidptosis sensitivity [74].

Activation of gasdermin proteins and membrane pore formation

Activation of gasdermin proteins and membrane pore formation

Gasdermin proteins (e.g., GSDMD and GSDME) contain an N-terminal pore-forming domain and a C-terminal auto-inhibitory domain. In the resting state, these domains remain closed through intramolecular interactions [15, 16]. Gasdermin proteins are initiated by protease cleavage in TME. Inflammatory caspases (e.g., caspase-1/4/5/11) or apoptotic caspases (e.g., caspase-3) cleave Gasdermin proteins, releasing the N-terminal domain. For instance, caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD at the Asp276 site, exposing its membrane-perforating activity [15, 16]. Subsequently, inflammatory responses induce conformational changes in Gasdermin proteins, leading to membrane pore assembly and eventual cell lysis. Following oligomerization, the N-terminal domain of GSDMD forms channels with a diameter of 10–20 nm on the cell membrane, resulting in ion imbalance, cell swelling, and content release [15, 16].

(2)Processing and release of IL-1β/IL-18

Processing and release of IL-1β/IL-18

Inflammation-induced Caspase-1 simultaneously cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 to generate mature IL-1β and IL-18 [15]. This triggers an amplified inflammatory cascade with massive cytokine release, where GSDMD conformational changes generate membrane pores, enabling pore-dependent cytokine secretion. Mature cytokines passively diffuse through GSDMD membrane pores into the extracellular space, with subsequent cell membrane rupture further amplifying the inflammatory signal [15, 16].

(3)Regulatory network of pyroptosis in breast cancer

Regulatory network of pyroptosis in breast cancer

Given that pyroptosis fundamentally involves the activation and release of inflammatory factors, it can shape a pro-inflammatory microenvironment, thereby potentiating immunotherapy efficacy in breast cancer. Pyroptosis in breast cancer cells releases IL-1β, recruiting DCs and activating CD8⁺ T cells, thereby enhancing the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy [75]. However, the pyroptosis pathway-characterized by its inflammatory nature-frequently exhibits a dual role in treatment resistance within tumor models. Chemotherapy drugs (e.g., doxorubicin) induce pyroptosis via the caspase-3/GSDME pathway; however, sustained IL-18 secretion may promote cancer stem cell (CSC) self-renewal and thus induce the progression and resistance of tumor [76]. Similar to other PCD types, pyroptosis demonstrates significant tumor subtype-specific regulation. In HER2-positive breast cancer models, trastuzumab suppresses GSDME expression, thereby inhibiting pyroptosis [77]. Concurrently, HER2 signaling activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to block caspase-8 activation, preventing GSDMC cleavage and conferring pyroptosis resistance. The combination of Herceptin with HIF-1α inhibitor PX-478 effectively inhibits GSDMC-mediated pyroptotic metastasis (65% reduction in lung metastases in animal models) [78]. In TNBC, hypermethylation of the GSDME promoter silences its expression, conferring resistance to pyroptosis inducers [79].

Dual roles of pyroptosis in breast cancer

Pyroptosis reshapes the TME by releasing inflammatory cytokines and DAMPs, exhibiting both pro-tumor and anti-tumor effects in breast cancer.

Formation of a pro-tumor inflammatory microenvironment

Formation of a pro-tumor inflammatory microenvironment

Excessive activation of pyroptosis may promote breast cancer progression through the following pathways. First, activation of IL-1β/NF-κB signaling drives metastasis in breast cancer. IL-1β released during pyroptosis activates the NF-κB pathway in tumor cells, upregulating MMP-9 and VEGF expression, which promotes angiogenesis and lung metastasis [77, 78]. Meanwhile, activation of pyroptosis could induce recruitment of immunosuppressive cells. IL-18 induces the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in the TME, inhibiting CD8⁺ T cell activity and facilitating immune evasion [80]. The inflammatory cascade triggered by pyroptosis subsequently induces other forms of cell death, creating a positive feedback loop that amplifies pyroptotic signaling. This microenvironment fosters the maintenance of stem-like properties in cancer cells. Persistent pyroptosis activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway via the HMGB1-TLR4 axis, promoting breast cancer stem cell (BCSC) self-renewal and enhancing tumor regenerative capacity [81].

(2)Immune activation effect enhancing Anti-PD-1 efficacy

Immune activation effect enhancing Anti-PD-1 efficacy

Moderate induction of pyroptosis can synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to exert anti-tumor effects. Moderate pyroptosis enhances antigen release and presentation, thereby potentiating the antitumor efficacy of ICIs. Pyroptotic cells release ATP and HMGB1, activating DCs and increasing tumor antigen cross-presentation, resulting in an increase in CD8⁺ T cell clonal expansion [82–84]. As widely established in the literature, inflammation-associated pyroptosis can remodel the TME, effectively converting immunologically cold tumors into hot tumors. GSDME-mediated pyroptosis significantly increases tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) density and upregulates PD-L1 expression, improving the response rate to anti-PD-1 therapy [85]. Concurrently, pyroptosis has been shown to mediate epigenetic modulation that synergizes with other therapeutic modalities. Histone deacetylase inhibitors (e.g., Entinostat) enhance pyroptosis by derepressing GSDME expression that when combined with PD-1 inhibitors, they achieve a higher complete response rate in TNBC mouse models [82, 86].

(3)Balancing strategies and clinical translation challenges

Balancing strategies and clinical translation challenges

Given the aforementioned dual roles of pyroptosis in tumor modulation, its future therapeutic applications will require spatiotemporally precise regulation. Tumor microenvironment-responsive nanoparticles (e.g., ROS-sensitive carriers) are utilized to locally induce pyroptosis, avoiding systemic inflammatory storms [87]. Concurrently, utilizing biomarkers such as GSDME for clinical guidance is equally critical, given the marked heterogeneity in breast cancer molecular subtypes and their substantially divergent treatment regimens. A combined scoring model based on GSDME expression and TIL density predicts the likelihood of patients benefiting from pyroptosis-immune combination therapy [88]. Ultimately, clinical translation must overcome intrinsic resistance mechanisms. Such as that inhibition of PD-L1/IL-6R crosstalk blocks adaptive immune resistance induced by pyroptosis [70–73].

Combination therapy strategies: synergistic effects of pyroptosis inducers and molecular inhibitors

Pyroptosis inducers significantly enhance the efficacy of ICIs by releasing tumor antigens and activating immune responses, establishing a new paradigm for combination therapy in breast cancer.

PARP inhibitor synergistic mechanisms

PARP inhibitor synergistic mechanisms

The PARP inhibitor niraparib may enhance antitumor immunity through the following mechanisms. PARP inhibitors promote antigen release and presentation by facilitating DCs maturation and antigen presentation efficiency and induce DNA damage and activating GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. Additionally, PARP inhibitors activate the STING pathway, which synergizes with immunotherapy to inhibit tumor proliferation [89].

(2)Clinical trial progress and optimization strategies

Clinical trial progress and optimization strategies

Phase II trial of Niraparib combined with Pembrolizumab in advanced TNBC showed that overall objective response rate (ORR) was 21%. Subgroup analysis showed patients with high GSDME expression showed significantly better outcomes [90]. Precision therapy strategies mainly contains biomarker stratification. A combined scoring system based on GSDME expression and TIL density can predict treatment response [88]. Localized drug delivery systems means development of tumor-targeted liposomes co-loaded with niraparib and IL-15 superagonist to locally enhance pyroptosis and T-cell activation while reducing systemic toxicity [84].

Multi-mechanism crosstalk and synergistic therapy

The heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance of breast cancer necessitate moving beyond the limitations of targeting a single cell death mechanism. By integrating the crosstalk pathways and metabolic interactions among ferroptosis, pyroptosis, cuproptosis, and disulfidptosis, multi-mechanism synergistic therapeutic strategies can be developed (Fig. 5).

Shared oxidative stress pathways between ferroptosis and pyroptosis

Shared oxidative stress pathways between ferroptosis and pyroptosis

Ferroptosis and pyroptosis form a synergistic network through oxidative stress and lipid metabolism regulation. During this synergistic network, ACSL4 plays a dual regulatory role, that it not only drives lipid peroxidation related to ferroptosis but also activates the caspase-1/GSDMD pyroptosis pathway by generating arachidonic acid (AA) [91]. Knockdown of ACSL4 reduces the sensitivity of TNBC cells to ferroptosis inducers (RSL3) and pyroptosis inducers (niraparib) [91–93]. Meanwhile, ROS cascade amplification connect the ferroptosis and pyroptosis. ROS produced by ferroptosis activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting caspase-1 cleavage of GSDMD and forming a positive feedback loop, with ROS levels increase [82, 83]. Lipid nanoparticles co-loaded with RSL3 and niraparib achieve an increased complete tumor regression rate in TNBC models, significantly outperforming monotherapy groups [92].

(2)Association between cuproptosis/disulfidptosis and metabolic reprogramming

Association between cuproptosis/disulfidptosis and metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic reprogramming influences cuproptosis and disulfidptosis by regulating metal ion homeostasis and redox balance. As discussed above, cuproptosis relies on lipoylated TCA cycle enzymes (e.g., PDH, OGDH). Glycolysis-dominant breast cancer inhibits the TCA cycle via the Warburg effect, leading to cuproptosis resistance, with cell survival rates increasing [93]. Breast cancers with high SLC7A11 expression maintain GSH and NADPH pools through enhanced cystine uptake, resisting disulfidptosis and cuproptosis. Inhibiting the glucose transporter GLUT1 blocks NADPH supply could elevate disulfidptosis in breast cancer models [94]. Combination of the copper ionophore Elesclomol with the GLUT1 inhibitor BAY-876 synergistically induces cuproptosis and disulfidptosis, overcoming metabolic adaptation in TNBC and reducing tumor volume [47].

(3)Potential of nanodelivery systems targeting multiple cell death mechanisms

Potential of nanodelivery systems targeting multiple cell death mechanisms

Nanotechnology, leveraging tumor microenvironment responsiveness and multi-mechanism synergy, offers innovative tools for precision therapy. Dual-drug delivery systems for ferroptosis-pyroptosis co-induction showed effective anti-tumor function. Redox-sensitive nanoparticles co-loaded with RSL3 and GSDME-activating peptide trigger dual cell death in high-ROS tumors, increasing CD8⁺ T cell infiltration [95]. Nanotechnology further enables the co-activation of distinct PCD pathways through cuproptosis-disulfidptosis combination drugs. Copper-doped mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with SLC7A11 siRNA induce cuproptosis by releasing Cu²⁺ and disulfidptosis by silencing SLC7A11, synergistically killing TNBC cells [29]. Smart responsive design with pH/enzyme dual-responsive carriers release copper ionophores in the acidic lysosomal environment and activate the pyroptosis pathway under tumor-specific MMP-9 action, achieving spatiotemporal precision [96]. Given that distinct PCDs process universally involve metabolic dysregulation–either as concomitant events or causative factors–nanodelivered metabolism-targeting agents are consequently recognized as a pivotal therapeutic strategy. Folate-modified liposomes target folate receptor-overexpressing breast cancer cells, co-delivering Elesclomol and BAY-876 to selectively exploit metabolic vulnerabilities [97].

(4)Cross-mechanisms of four PCD pathways in breast cancer models

Cross-mechanisms of four PCD pathways in breast cancer models

Based on our aforementioned exploration and discussion of the four PCD pathways, we have identified three shared regulatory mechanisms and activation features among them. First, all four PCD types involve disruption of redox homeostasis, manifested as a cascade of reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts [98]. For instance, ferroptosis induces iron-dependent lipid ROS, Cuproptosis generates copper-catalyzed mitochondrial ROS, and pyroptosis drives inflammasome-activated ROS cascades. The production of these ROS primarily relies on SLC7A11 dysfunction (impaired cystine import) and antioxidant defense collapse of GSH/GPX4, as well as the aberrant NRF2 pathway activation. Second, all four PCD types exhibit distinct metabolic reprogramming features, characterized by dysregulated energy metabolism. Glycolytic inhibition suppresses the TCA cycle, thereby attenuating cuproptosis [99]. Insufficient NADPH production via the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) triggers disulfidptosis and elevated fatty acid synthesis in tumor cells promotes ferroptosis. These metabolic reprogramming processes primarily involve glucose transporters (e.g., GLUT1), key metabolic enzymes (ACSL4, DLAT, PDH) and NADPH-generating enzymes. Third, immune microenvironment remodeling serves as an interactive bridge among distinct PCD pathways [100, 101]. The specific cell death modality dictates the immunogenicity of the TME. Pyroptosis releases IL-1β/IL-18, recruiting abundant dendritic cells (DCs) and CD8 + T cells into the microenvironment. In contrast, ferroptosis secretes HMGB1 to activate antitumor immunity, while cuproptosis induces mitochondrial damage, releasing mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) to stimulate the cGAS-STING pathway and subsequently modulate immune responses [102].

Therefore, based on our in-depth exploration of the fundamental mechanisms and characteristics of distinct programmed cell death (PCD) pathways, we propose that SLC7A11, ACSL4, FDX1, and GSDME represent promising biological markers for different PCD types, breast cancer subtypes, and even other tumor entities. For example, SLC7A11 expression levels are critically associated with ferroptosis, disulfidptosis, and cuproptosis, primarily through its essential roles in regulating GSH synthesis and copper ion transport. This positions SLC7A11 as a promising therapeutic target for synergistically modulating these three cell death pathways. ACSL4 serves as a critical molecular hub that integrates multiple PCD pathways through distinct but interconnected mechanisms. On one hand, it functionally links ferroptosis and pyroptosis by regulating PUFA-PLs metabolism and caspase activation cascades. On the other hand, it bridges disulfidptosis and cuproptosis through inflammatory signal amplification and redox imbalance propagation. This multifaceted coordination collectively reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment by modulating immune cell infiltration.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Research on novel cell death mechanisms in breast cancer offers new perspectives for overcoming treatment resistance. However, clinical translation requires addressing the following key challenges and exploring innovative directions.

Development of biomarker detection systems based on novel cell death mechanisms

Development of biomarker detection systems based on novel cell death mechanisms

Multi-omics integration analysis will facilitate the identification of more specific and stable biological biomarkers. Combining single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and lipidomics to construct a multidimensional biomarker map for ferroptosis (ACSL4/GPX4), cuproptosis (FDX1/ATP7A), disulfidptosis (SLC7A11/ACTB), and pyroptosis (GSDME/IL-1β), enabling precise prediction of treatment response. The integration of AI algorithms with dynamic imaging detection technologies has emerged as a pivotal approach in cancer therapeutics. MRI quantitative T2* imaging (to assess iron deposition) and PET-CT tracing of copper isotopes (⁶⁴Cu) could be used for non-invasive real-time monitoring in breast cancer and other cancers [103]. Liquid biopsy technology has long been recognized as a critical tool for cancer diagnosis, treatment selection, outcome prediction, and therapeutic monitoring-particularly when integrated with advancing genomic profiling methodologies. Detecting lipid peroxidation products (MDA) or ACTB cross-linking levels in CTCs to dynamically assess therapeutic efficacy.

(2)Utilizing organoid models to screen personalized combination therapies

Utilizing organoid models to screen personalized combination therapies

Patient-derived organoid (PDO) is quite helpful in cancer investigation. High-throughput screening of ferroptosis inducers (e.g., RSL3) combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors in breast cancer PDOs, achieving a high clinical matching rate [104]. Co-culture of CAFs and T cells in organoids could evaluate the immune microenvironment remodeling effect of cuproptosis inducers (e.g., Elesclomol), resulting in a high increase in CD8⁺ T cell activation [105]. Using CRISPR screening in organoid libraries to identify disulfidptosis resistance genes (e.g., SLC7A11 amplification, CTH overexpression), guiding targeted interventions.

(3)Focus on the impact of cell death mechanisms on CSCs

Focus on the impact of cell death mechanisms on CSCs

CSCs’ resistance to cell death is regarded as one of the potential mechanism in cancer therapy. As we discussed forehead, breast cancer stem cells evade multiple death pathways by overexpressing GPX4 (ferroptosis resistance), ALDH1A3 (clearing lipid peroxides), and ABC transporters (drug efflux), causing cancer progression and drug resistance [106]. Inhibition of glycolysis (2-DG) or glutamine metabolism (CB-839) in CSCs enhances their sensitivity to cuproptosis and disulfidptosis, reducing survival rates in mouse model [107]. As discussed above and supported by emerging evidence, epigenetic regulation also plays a pivotal role in CSCs. Histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) induce pyroptosis in CSCs by derepressing GSDME expression, reversing their stemness [101].

(4)Future directions for interdisciplinary collaboration

Future directions for interdisciplinary collaboration

Through a retrospective analysis of previous studies and discussions on different PCD mechanisms, we anticipate that future advances in cancer therapeutics and research will critically focus on the following areas. First, artificial intelligence (AI)-driven algorithms and machine learning-based multi-omics data integration platforms will enable the prediction of optimal combination therapies. Second, engineered microbial drug delivery systems-particularly synthetic biology-modified bacteria-will allow targeted delivery of pyroptosis activators (e.g., GSDMD-N), inducing localized immune responses while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Our comprehensive exploration reveals significant heterogeneity across tumor types, particularly in breast cancer models, where diverse PCD pathways exhibit unique characteristics and regulatory mechanisms. Targeting individual tumor subtypes or isolated PCD pathways often yields suboptimal outcomes. Instead, we propose that the crosstalk and coordinated regulation among different PCD types-primarily involving redox homeostasis, energy metabolism, and immune modulation-play a central role in breast cancer. Notably, energy metabolism may serve as a fundamental node for PCD intervention, though the most specific biomarkers (e.g., SLC7A11) still require further validation in large-scale clinical studies.

Finally, whether these PCD interaction mechanisms observed in breast cancer models can be extrapolated to other tumor types remains an open question for future investigation.
