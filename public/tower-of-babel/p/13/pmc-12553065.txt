Enzymatic interesterification of freshwater fish oil from grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) viscera: a novel approach to mitigate fishy odors through lipid restructuring
Authors: Jinlin Li, Huijuan Zhou, Linchuan Xu, Chengwei Yu, Mingming Hu, Bizhen Zhong, Zongcai Tu, Bin Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores enzymatic interesterification of grass carp visceral fish oil and coconut oil, examining its impacts on lipidomic and flavoromic profiles via UHPLC-Q-TOF/MS and GC–MS. Lipidomics results revealed that interesterification reaction significantly regulated glycerolipids, primarily triacylglycerols and diacylglycerols, and influenced phospholipids, particularly phosphatidylethanolamine (p < 0.05). Flavoromics identified 19 key flavors contributing to reduced fishy odors (aldehydes and alcohols) and enhanced desirable aroma (esters). Electronic nose confirmed notable changes in flavors, with key aroma compounds showing adjusted relative odor activity values. Correlation analysis demonstrated that medium- and long-chain triacylglycerols and free fatty acids were significantly associated with key flavors (p < 0.05). Enzymatic interesterification improved fish oil flavor by moderately oxidizing polyunsaturated fatty acids (EPA and DHA). Conclusively, enzymatic modification is an effective strategy for mitigating the fishy odors of fish oil by restructuring lipids. It shows promise for the industrial production of odor-reduced fish oil for future applications.Keywords: Grass carp viscera, Fish oil, Enzymatic modification, Lipidomics, Flavoromics

Introduction

Fish oil serves as a principal source of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), notably eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), which demonstrate well-documented therapeutic effects encompassing cardiovascular protection, anti-inflammatory activity, and neurodevelopmental support (Ongis et al., 2025; Saidaiah et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the practical application of fish-derived lipids is hindered by substantial constraints due to their high susceptibility to oxidative degradation. This process concurrently induces undesirable flavor compounds and compromises nutritional integrity. Within aquaculture systems, grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella), one of the most widely cultivated freshwater fish species, yields substantial quantities of visceral by-products during commercial processing operations (Ayeloja et al., 2024; Rodrigues et al., 2024). These biological residues constitute an underexploited reservoir for freshwater fish oil extraction and biotechnological valorization. However, the characteristically pronounced odor profile inherent to freshwater fish oils, particularly the persistent fishy notes, poses a persistent challenge to their industrial-scale utilization.

Additionally, coconut oil is a primary natural source of medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs). With a saturated fat content exceeding 90 %, it exhibits excellent storage stability and resistance to rancidity (Deen et al., 2021). Its fatty acid (FA) profile is predominantly composed of medium- and short-chain fatty acids. MCFAs possess not only good oxidative stability, distinct metabolic pathways, and antimicrobial properties, but their high saturation also significantly elevates the melting point of the oil (Roopashree et al., 2021).

Enzymatic modification has emerged as a promising approach to improve the stability, functionality, and sensory properties of fish oil (Liu et al., 2025). Lipases, for instance, can be used to selectively hydrolyze and esterify fatty acids, thereby altering the lipid profile and enhancing oxidative stability (Yang et al., 2024). Additionally, enzymatic hydrolysis can influence the flavor profile of fish oil by modifying volatile compounds, making it more appealing for use in food products (Liu et al., 2024). Currently, in most studies on enzymatic interesterification of different substrate oils and fats using various lipases, greater emphasis is placed on process optimization as well as the structural, physicochemical, and functional properties of the resulting structured lipids (Singh et al., 2024). There are a few research reports on the effect of enzymatic interesterification on the volatile flavor of products. Therefore, the impact of enzymatic interesterification on the flavoromic and lipidomic profiles of fish oil from grass carp viscera remains poorly understood. This lack of research leaves a critical gap in understanding how enzymatic interesterification precisely alters the flavor of fish oil, especially freshwater fish oil with its inherent strong fishy odor. This is a key issue that hinders the effective improvement of the sensory quality of freshwater fish oil and its wider application in the food industry.

Lipidomics, which involves systematic characterization of lipid profiles in biological matrices, offers a holistic understanding of lipid molecular diversity and metabolic dynamics (Wang et al., 2025). Windarsih and collaborators recently demonstrated that untargeted lipidomic approaches constitute a powerful analytical platform for the exhaustive profiling of lipid constituents in marine-derived fish oil specimens (Windarsih, A., Irnawati., Suratno., Warmiko, H. D., Alam, L. P. M., Utami, I. D., Rohman, A, and Indrianingsih, A. W, 2024). Complementarily, flavoromics specializes in deciphering both volatile organic compounds and taste-active metabolites that collectively define food flavor attributes (Cai et al., 2024). As exemplified by Zhang's team, this methodology has been successfully implemented to elucidate sodium chloride's regulatory effects on flavor compound biosynthesis during Lactobacillus plantarum-mediated tilapia fermentation (Zhang et al., 2025). The synergistic integration of these omics platforms enables a deeper investigation into the network dynamics of lipid-to-flavor transformation in aquatic products, an area that has received less attention. Notably, Wang's group successfully decoded enzymatic flavor generation mechanisms in lightly salted Larimichthys crocea through concurrent lipidomic and flavoromic analysis (Wang et al., 2024). However, no studies have yet employed these approaches to provide a comprehensive explanation of how enzyme modification affects the lipid chemical structure and flavor characteristics of freshwater fish oil.

Given the above research gaps, this study aims to investigate the enzymatic modification of freshwater fish oil extracted from grass carp viscera and coconut oil, with a focus on its lipidomic and flavoromic profiles. Specifically, the lipidomic changes were analyzed using advanced mass spectrometry techniques, and the flavor profile was evaluated using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). The relationship between lipid changes and flavor improvements will be explored, revealing the mechanism by which enzymatic modification affects the flavor of the fish oil. The findings of this study will provide valuable insights into the potential of enzymatic modification to enhance the flavor quality and acceptability of freshwater fish oil from grass carp viscera, contributing to the sustainable utilization of fish processing by-products.

Materials and methods

Interesterification of fish oil and coconut oil

Live grass carp were procured from Rainbow Supermarket in Nanchang, China. Upon arrival, the fish were immediately euthanized by electrical stunning, with ethical guidelines for animal handling. Fresh visceral tissues (including liver, intestine, and adipose tissue) were aseptically excised from grass carp immediately after euthanasia. Fish oil extracted from grass carp viscera was subsequently extracted through enzymatic hydrolysis based on our established methodology (Li et al., 2025). The interesterification process was conducted using fish oil and coconut oil (Wenchang Pure Coconut Food Co., Ltd.) as raw materials. Before reaction, both oils underwent pretreatment to remove impurities and moisture through sequential filtration and dehydration processes. Specifically, the oils were first filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter to eliminate particulate matter, followed by vacuum dehydration at 110 °C for 1 h to ensure complete moisture removal (Dong et al., 2025). Interesterification reaction was performed in a water bath thermostatic oscillator (SHA—B, purchased from Changzhou Tianrui Instrument Co., LTD.). Fish oil and coconut oil were mixed with a 1:1 mass ratio, then Lipozyme RMC (360 IU/g enzyme activity, purchased from Beijing Gaoruisen Technology Co., LTD.) with 10 % mass fraction was added and reacted at 60 °C and 240 rpm for 3 h (Shi et al., 2024). After the reaction, the mixture was filtered with gauze to separate the lipase from the oil sample, and the lipase was collected and washed three times with n-hexane. The interesterified fish oil was filtered through a silica gel column. Five experimental groups were set up: fish oil (FO), coconut oil (CO), physically mixed oil (LCTMCT, fish oil: coconut oil = 1:1), interesterified fish oil (MLCT), and column chromatography-treated interesterified fish oil (MLCT1).

Silica gel (200–300 mesh, for column chromatography) was purchased from Qingdao Haiyang Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). The C8–40 n-alkane mixture (used for retention index calibration) was purchased from Tanmo Quality Control Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All other chemicals and solvents were of analytical grade and obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (China).

Determination of acid value and peroxide value

Acid value (AV) in the samples was determined according to the cold solvent indicator titration method in GB 5009.229–2016 “Determination of acid value in food” (Li et al., 2024). Briefly, a known amount of the sample (approximately 2 g) was accurately weighed and dissolved in 50 mL of a neutralized solvent mixture consisting of ethanol and diethyl ether (1:1, v/v). The solution was then titrated with 0.1 M potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution using phenolphthalein as an indicator. The titration was continued until a faint pink color persisted for at least 30 s. The peroxidation value (POV) in the samples was determined using the titration method in GB 5009.227–2016 “Determination of peroxide Value in food” (Zhang, Li, et al., 2021). Approximately 2 g of the sample was weighed into a 250 mL conical flask, and 30 mL of a solvent mixture consisting of glacial acetic acid and chloroform (3:2, v/v) was added. The mixture was swirled to dissolve the sample completely. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of saturated potassium iodide (KI) solution was added, and the flask was stoppered and shaken vigorously for 1 min. After allowing the mixture to stand in the dark for 5 min, 100 mL of distilled water was added, and the solution was titrated with 0.01 M sodium thiosulfate (Na₂S₂O₃) solution using starch as an indicator. Lipidomics test

Sample preparation for lipidomics

Lipid extraction was performed using a modified method (Han et al., 2024). Briefly, precisely pipette 200 μL of the sample into an EP tube. 80 μL of methanol and 400 μL of MTBE were added, vortex mixed for 30 s, and then performed ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 KHz). The above sample was incubated at −20 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged at high speed (13,000 ×g, 4 °C) for 15 min. 350 μL of the supernatant was pipetted and dried in a vacuum concentrator. The residue was reconstituted in 100 μL of reconstitution solution (isopropanol: acetonitrile = 1:1). The mix was vortexed for 30 s, then subjected to ultrasonic extraction in an ice-water bath for 5 min (40 KHz). After centrifugation at high speed (13,000 ×g, 4 °C) for 5 min, 80 μL of the supernatant was pipetted into an injection vial with an insert for instrumental analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Lipidomics profiling was conducted using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-TOF/MS) (Han et al., 2024). The chromatographic separation was performed on a C18 reversed-phase column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm particle size) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) acetonitrile/water (60:40, v/v) with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1 % formic acid, and (B) isopropanol/acetonitrile (90:10, v/v) with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1 % formic acid. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–2 min, 40 % B; 2–25 min, 40–100 % B; 25–30 min, 100 % B; 30–31 min, 100–40 % B; and 31–35 min, 40 % B for re-equilibration. The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 5 μL.

Mass spectrometry analysis was performed in both positive and negative ionization modes using electrospray ionization (ESI). The MS parameters were optimized as follows: capillary voltage, 3.5 kV (positive) and 3.0 kV (negative); nebulizer gas pressure, 40 psi; drying gas flow rate, 10 L/min; drying gas temperature, 350 °C; and fragmentor voltage, 120 V. Full-scan data were acquired over the m/z range 100–1500 at a scan speed of 2 Hz. Tandem MS (MS/MS) analysis was performed for structural identification using collision energies ranging from 10 to 40 eV.

Data processing and lipid identification

Raw data were processed using specialized lipidomics software (e.g., LipidSearch, MS-DIAL, or XCMS). Peak alignment, normalization, and deconvolution were performed to ensure accurate quantification. Lipids were identified based on their exact mass, retention time, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and matched against lipid databases such as LIPID MAPS or HMDB. Internal standards (phosphatidylcholine (PC) 17:0/17:0, lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) 17:0, and ceramide (Cer) d18:1/17:0) were used for quality control and quantification.

Statistical analysis

Multivariate statistical analysis, including principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), was performed to identify lipid species that were significantly altered between experimental groups. Univariate statistical analysis, such as Student's t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), was used to assess the significance of individual lipid changes. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were visualized using heatmaps, volcano plots, and pathway enrichment analysis to interpret lipidomic profiles in the context of biological pathways.

Quality control

Quality control (QC) samples, prepared by pooling equal volumes of all samples, were injected at regular intervals throughout the analytical run to monitor system stability and reproducibility. Lipids with a coefficient of variation (CV) > 20 % in QC samples were excluded from further analysis. Additionally, blank samples were analyzed to account for any background contamination.

Electronic nose assay

For electronic nose (e-nose) analysis, the sample is prepared by placing approximately 2 g of test material into a 15 mL centrifuge tube. About 2 g of the oil sample was added to the centrifugal tube, and then the tube mouth was sealed with a plastic wrap. The sample was equilibrated in a water bath at 50 °C for 30 min to achieve equilibrium of volatile compounds in the headspace. This step ensured consistent and reproducible volatile organic compound (VOC) profiles for analysis. The electronic nose system (PEN 3.5) used in this study consisted of an array of metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) sensors designed to detect a wide range of volatile compounds. This system has been widely applied in food quality assessment, environmental monitoring, and agricultural research due to its high sensitivity and rapid response capabilities (Xu et al., 2016). The specific sensors included in the array were sensitive to alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, hydrocarbons, and sulfur-containing compounds. The operating parameters were set as follows: sample flow rate of 300 mL/min, sensor chamber temperature of 40 °C, and data acquisition time of 120 s per sample.

Flavor determination

Sample preparation

For flavor analysis, approximately 2 g of the sample was weighed into a 20 mL headspace vial (Xing et al., 2025). The vials were sealed with a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) -lined diaphragm and equilibrated in a water bath at 60 °C for 30 min so that the volatile compounds reached equilibrium in the headspace. This step ensured consistent and reproducible VOC profiles for analysis.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis

Solid-phase microextraction (SPME) was employed to extract volatile flavor compounds from the sample headspace. A 50/30 μm divinylbenzene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) fiber was used for extraction. The fiber was preconditioned according to the manufacturer's instructions before use. For extraction, the SPME fiber was exposed to the sample overhead at 1 cm at 60 °C and continuously stirred for 30 min. After extraction, the fiber was retracted and immediately inserted into a gas chromatography (GC) injector for desorption with a resolution time of 10 min.

The analysis of volatile flavor compounds was performed using a gas chromatography system coupled with a mass spectrometer. The GC was equipped with a DB-WAX capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The oven temperature program was set as follows: Initial temperature of 40 °C held for 3 min, increased to 120 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min, then to 240 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, and held for 5 min. The injector temperature was maintained at 250 °C, and the injection was performed in splitless mode. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min.

Mass spectrometry detection was performed in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV. The ion source temperature was set at 230 °C, and the quadrupole temperature was 150 °C. The mass range was scanned from m/z 35 to 450 at a rate of 2 scans/s. Volatile compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra with those in the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library and by matching their retention indices (RI) with published data.

The GC–MS data were processed using specialized software (Agilent MassHunter or Xcalibur) for peak identification, integration, and quantification. Volatile compounds were identified based on their mass spectra, retention times, and RI. Relative concentrations of volatile compounds were calculated using a normalization method based on the peak area ratios of the compounds. A semi-quantitative approach was used to compare the abundance of flavor compounds across samples.

The relative odor activity value (ROAV) method has been extensively utilized in the analysis of key volatile components, where a higher ROAV value indicates a greater contribution of the substance to the food's flavor profile. Typically, substances with an ROAV ≥1 are deemed to be key aroma compounds.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Origin 2021 and Smica 14.1 software were used for graph plotting, and SPSS 20.0 software was employed for statistical analysis of the data. During data analysis, multivariate ANOVA combined with Tukey's test was applied to analyze significant differences in volatile characteristics between sample groups. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The results were further validated using cross-validation techniques to ensure the robustness of the classification model. The relationship between lipids and volatile flavors was established by Pearson correlation analysis.

Results

The changes in acid value and peroxide value of fish oil with interesterification

The freshwater fish oil and coconut oil underwent an interesterification reaction, followed by purification via column chromatography. The schematic diagram and the changes in AV are illustrated in Fig. S1(A) and Fig. S1(B), respectively. In comparison to the physically mixed oil (LCTMCT, AV = 0.630 mg KOH/g), the AV of the oil significantly increased after interesterification (MLCT, AV = 10.179 mg KOH/g). However, subsequent column chromatography treatment (MLCT1, AV = 1.170 mg KOH/g) markedly reduced the AV of the oil sample. This reduction is likely attributable to the effective removal of free fatty acids (FFAs) by silica gel column chromatography, leading to a decrease in AV.

The peroxide values of fish oil, coconut oil, and the oil samples before and after interesterification are presented in Fig. S1(C). The peroxide value of the physical mixture (LCTMCT, POV = 0.300 ± 0.023 mmol/kg) was intermediate between that of fish oil (FO, POV = 0.564 ± 0.011 mmol/kg) and coconut oil (CO, POV = 0.104 ± 0.009 mmol/kg). The interesterification reaction (MCLT, POV = 0.332 ± 0.019 mmol/kg) had a minimal impact on the peroxide value of the oil samples. Furthermore, the peroxide value remained relatively unchanged after column chromatography (MCLT1, POV = 0.282 ± 0.022 mmol/kg). Overall, interesterification reaction can slightly induce lipid oxidation in fish oil.

Lipidomic profiling of fish oil with interesterification

The lipidomics analysis of fish oil following interesterification revealed significant insights into the composition and distribution of fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). The interesterification process effectively converted the triglycerides present in the fish oil into their corresponding methyl esters, facilitating a more detailed analysis of the fatty acid profile.

Fatty acid composition

The GC–MS analysis identified a diverse range of FAMEs, including saturated fatty acids (SFAs), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and PUFAs. The fatty acid composition and content of the different oil samples are shown in Table 1. The predominant fatty acids detected in CO were C8:0 (50.18 ± 3.29 mg/g), C10:0 (42.36 ± 2.48 mg/g), C12:0 (147.27 ± 0.81 mg/g), C14:0 (45.86 ± 4.05 mg/g), C16:0 (69.27 ± 5.07 mg/g), C18:0 (29.27 ± 1.82 mg/g) and C18:1n9c (38.98 ± 2.38 mg/g). This suggests that coconut oil is rich in MCFAs (C8:0, C10:0, and C12:0). The fatty acids with high content in grass carp visceral fish oil were C14:0 (10.76 ± 1.37 mg/g), C16:0 (109.62 ± 10.81 mg/g), C18:0 (28.83 ± 2.80 mg/g), C16:1 (28.43 ± 3.20 mg/g), C18:1 (112.42 ± 5.33 mg/g), C18:2 (103.43 ± 11.18 mg/g), and C18:3 (13.30 ± 1.58 mg/g). This suggests that grass carp visceral fish oil was rich in long-chain fatty acids, including saturated (C14:0, C16:0, and C18:0), monounsaturated (C16:1, C18:1n9c) and PUFAs (C18:2 and C18:3). After physical mixing, the fatty acids in LCTMCT were intermediate between the raw oil (coconut oil and grass carp visceral fish oil). Compared with physical mixed fish oil, all fatty acids in interesterification oil (MLCT) were decreased. Fatty acids with significant decreases (p < 0.05) included saturated (C12:0, C14:0, C16:0, and C18:0), monounsaturated (C16:1, C18:1n9t, and C18:1n9c), and PUFAs (C18:2n6t and C18:3n3). Compared with MLCT, various fatty acids in the product oil after passing the column (MLCT1) were greatly improved, including C12:0 from 24.93 ± 7.83 mg/g in MLCT to 43.47 ± 4.42 mg/g in MLCT1, and C18:2n6t from 57.63 ± 3.50 mg/g in MLCT to 60.03 ± 3.36 mg/g in MLCT1. Notably, interesterification and column crossing had no significant effect on EPA and DHA content (p > 0.05). In summary, interesterification reaction has little effect on fatty acid composition in fish oil, but it adjusts the position of fatty acids on glycerol.

Lipid type change analysis

The lipid contents in the physical mixing (LCTMCT) and interesterified products (MLCT) of the coconut oil (CO) and fish oil (FO) through lipidomics are shown in Table 2. In terms of FA, compared with the physical mixture group (LCTMCT), the interesterification group (MLCT) showed an overall increasing trend, oacyl-(gamma-hydroxy) fatty acid (OAHFA) increased from 206.145 ± 1.792 to 213.559 ± 4.823 log10 area (p < 0.05), and total FA increased from 276.161 ± 2.013 to 282.965 ± 4.835 log10 area (p < 0.05). Lipid profile change analysis

The lipid profile of interesterified fish oil is shown in Fig. S2. As shown in Fig. S2(A), a total of 1537 lipids were detected, including 964 GL (62.72 %), 423 GP (27.52 %), 106 SP (6.90 %), 38 FA (2.47 %), and 6 ST (0.39 %). As illustrated in Fig. S2(B), lipid contained 1246 SFA (34.79 %), 1051 PUFA (29.35 %), 716 MUFA (19.99 %), and 568 ODD (15.86 %). From the carbon number distribution, the interesterification fish oil sample distribution was C34, C36, and C38, followed by C32, C40, and C42 (Fig. S2C). From the unsaturation distribution of carbon number, the content decreases with the increase of unsaturation (Fig. S2D). The highest content of interesterification fish oil was C18:1, followed by C16:0 and C12:0 (Fig. S2E). Among the PUFAs, C18:2 was the highest, followed by C18:3,C22:6, C20:5, and C20:4 (Fig. S2F).

The comparative analysis of five lipid samples is shown in Fig. 1. A Venn diagram showed that compared with the physical mixture of fish oil and coconut oil, new regions of lipid formation were formed in the interesterification group, indicating the formation of new lipid molecules (Fig. 1A). Fig. 1(B) showed that the five lipid samples could be divided into three groups: MLCT and MLCT1 as a group, FO and LCTMCT as a group, and CO as a separate group. This also indicated that the lipids of MLCT and MLCT1 groups were similar, the lipids of FO and LCTMCT groups were similar, and the lipid molecules of the mixed group were significantly different after interesterification. Statistical analysis using PCA and PLS-DA demonstrated clear clustering of samples based on their fatty acid profiles. The interesterified fish oil samples were distinctly separated from other lipid sources, highlighting the unique lipidomic signature of fish oil. As shown in Fig. 1(CD), the physical mixing group was distributed between CO and FO, and the interesterification group was far away from the CO, FO, and LCTMCT groups. MLCT and MLCT1 groups were significantly separated. This also fully shows that the oil sample interesterification reaction occurred. Fig. 1(EF) showed that Q2 was significantly lower than R2Y, which indicated that the model was reliable. The above results show that interesterification reaction could significantly change the molecular structure of lipids.

The volatile flavor of fish oil with interesterification

The electronic nose analysis and PCA analysis of fish oil after interesterification are shown in Fig. S4. The response of the five oil samples to the signal intensity was W1W, W2W, W1S, W2S, and W5S. CO and LCTMCT oil samples were similar, and the signal intensity of W1W was only enhanced. Compared with CO and LCTMCT, MLCT, and FO oil samples showed a stronger W1W signal. Among the five groups of oil samples, MLCT1 was the most responsive to W1W, W2W, W2S, and W1S signals (Fig. S4A). Electronic nose PCA showed that the sum of PC1 and PC2 was 89.7 %, indicating significant differences in flavor among the five lipid samples (Fig. S4B). The results indicate that interesterification reaction and silica gel column treatment have significant effects on the electronic nose analysis of oil samples.

The interesterification process significantly altered the volatile flavor profile of fish oil, as evidenced by the reduction in characteristic fishy odors and the emergence of milder aromatic compounds (Table S1). GC–MS analysis revealed that key flavor compounds, as shown in Table 3. Nineteen main volatile substances (ROAV >1) were detected in the physical mixing (LCTMCT) and interesterified products (MLCT) of the CO and FO. In CO, volatile flavor substances with ROAV greater than 1.00 were nonanal, 2-undecanal, octanoic acid ethylester, decanoic acid ethylester, tetrahydro-6-propyl-2H-pyran-2-one, tetrahydro-6-pentyl-2H-pyran-2-one, eugenol, and 3-ethyl-2,5-dimethyl-pyrazine. The main volatile flavor substances with ROAV over 1.00 of LCT were 2-methyl-naphthalene, nonanal, (E)-2-nonenal, and eugenol. As the two oils are mixed, the volatile flavor compounds in the LCTMCT sample combine the flavors of both oils (CO and FO). In LCTMCT samples, volatile flavor substances with ROAV greater than 1.00 were nonanal, 2-undecanone, (E)-2-Nonenal, octanoic acid ethylester, decanoic acid, ethylester, tetrahydro-6-propyl-2H-pyran-2-one, tetrahydro-6-pentyl-2H-pyran-2-one, eugenol, and 2-methoxy-4-(1-propenyl)-phenol. After interesterification, some volatile flavor substances decreased significantly, such as nonanal, decanoic acid ethylester, and eugenol (p < 0.05). Compared with MLCT, the volatile flavor substances 2-undecanone, butylatedHydroxytoluene, ctetrahydro-6-propyl-2H-pyran-2-one, tetrahydro-6-pentyl-2H-pyran-2-one, and eugenol decreased in MLCT1, but increased in octanoic acid, ethylester benzoic acid methylester, and decanoic acid ethylester (p < 0.05). The volatile flavor difference analysis results of the five samples are shown in Fig. S5. The five oil samples were significantly separated in PCA and orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) diagram (Fig. S5AC), and the sum of PC1 and PC2 was 80.2 %. This indicates that the five oil samples differed significantly in volatile substances. Figure S5(B) showed that five groups of oil samples with significant effects of volatile flavor substances were 2-undecano, 2H-pyran-2, 2H-pyran-2, 2,4-heptad, 2-nonenal, eugenol, and butylated. Q2 of OPLS-DA was lower than R2, which indicated that the difference analysis model was reliable (Fig. S5D). The results indicate that interesterification reaction and silica gel column treatment have significant effects on the volatile flavor of oil samples.

Correlation analysis of volatiles and lipids in fish oil with interesterification

The correlation analysis between key volatile compounds and different lipid profiles in the five oil samples revealed significant relationships influenced by the interesterification reaction. There are four types of medium- and long-chain triglycerides formed by interesterification, including LML, MLL, LMM, and MLM. The Pearson correlation diagram of different lipids and the key volatile flavor substances is shown in Fig. 4. As shown in Fig. 4A, among LML types, 9 TG (16:0/12:0/14:0, 16:0/12:0/20:5, 16:0/8:0/18:2, 16:0/12:0/18:3, 18:1/12:0/18:3, 16:0/6:0/18:1, 18:3/12:4/18:3, 18:4/8:0/18:3, 16:1/12:0/18:3) were positively correlated with 6 key flavor substances (1,2,3,4-tetrahydro naphthalene, 1-methyl naphthalene, octanoic acid ethyl ester, benzoic acid methyl ester, decanoic acid methyl ester, deccanoic acid methyl ester). Among the MLL types, 14 TG (12:0/14:0/20:4, 10:0/18:2/18:3, 6:0/18:2/18:2, 8:0/18:1/22:6, 8:0/18:1/20:5, 8:0/18:2/18:3, 6:0/14:1/18:2, 8:0/18:2/20:5, 12:0/14:0/22:6, 8:0/14:0/18:3, 8:0/18:3/20:5, 8:0/14:3/20:5, 12:0/14:0/14:0, 6:0/14:0/14:3) were positively correlated with 7 key flavor substances (1,2,3,4-tetrahydro naphthalene, 1-methyl naphthalene, 1-methyl naphthalene, octanoic acid ethyl ester, benzoic acid methyl ester, decanoic acid methyl ester, deccanoic acid methyl ester) (Fig. 4B). Among the LMM types, 9 TG types (16:1/8:0/10:0, 20:4e/6:0/10:1, 16:2e/11:1/11:1, 18:4/12:0/12:0, 18:3e/10:1/10:1, 20:1/6:0/11:1, 18:4/8:0/8:0, 20:1/11:3/12:0, 15:0/8:0/8:0) were positively correlated with 6 key flavor substances (1,2,3,4-tetrahydro naphthalene, 1-methyl naphthalene, octanoic acid ethyl ester, benzoic acid methyl ester, decanoic acid methyl ester, deccanoic acid methyl ester) (Fig. 4C). As shown in Fig. 4(D), in MML type, methy ester decanoic acid was strongly positively correlated (r > 0.80, p < 0.05) with 15 TG (8:0/12:0/18:2, 8:0/8:0/18:2, 12:0/12:0/20:5, 10:0/12:0/17:1, 6:0/11:1/18:1, 8:0/10:0/18:1, 10:0/12:0/20:4, 6:0/8:0/18:1, 8:0/12:0/17:1, 10:0/12:0/20:5, 8:0/12:0/22:6, 12:0/12:1/20:5, 8:0/8:0/18:3, 8:0/12:0/20:4, 8:0/12:0/20:3); 2-Undecanone was strongly positively correlated (r > 0.80, p < 0.05) with 4 TG (12:0/11:1/20:5, 8:0/12:0/18:3, 8:0/12:0/22:6, 8:0/11:1/20:5); and 1-methyl naphthalene was positively correlated (r > 0.80, p < 0.05) with 6 TG (10:0/12:0/18:3, 10:0/12:0/17:1, 8:0/11:1/20:5, 6:0/12:0/18:3, 8:0/8:0/22:6, 8:0/12:0/20:3). Specifically, the concentrations of hexanal (r = −0.82, p < 0.05) and 2,4-heptadienal (r = −0.76, p < 0.01) exhibited strong negative correlations with the formation of FFAs (Fig. 4E), indicating that the breakdown of TGs and subsequent esterification suppressed lipid oxidation pathways. Notably, the formation of short-chain ethyl esters, such as ethyl butanoate and ethyl hexanoate, showed a positive correlation (r = 0.68 to 0.75, p < 0.05) with the degradation of FFAs, suggesting that these esters were primarily derived from the esterification of FFAs with ethanol. Interestingly, the ω-3 fatty acid content, particularly EPA and DHA, showed a weak but significant positive correlation (r = 0.32 to 0.41, p < 0.05) with the retention of certain aldehydes, such as nonanal and decanal. This suggests that the structural integrity of long-chain PUFAs may contribute to the persistence of some volatile oxidation products, even after interesterification.

In summary, the correlation analysis provides mechanistic insights into how interesterification modulates the volatile and lipid profiles of fish oil. The strong negative correlations between FFAs and fishy odors support the efficacy of interesterification as a deodorization strategy, while the positive correlations between FFAs and ethyl esters highlight the potential for flavor enhancement. Future studies could explore the impact of enzymatic interesterification on these correlations, as well as the role of antioxidants in further stabilizing the volatile-lipid matrix.

Discussions

The synthesis of medium- and long-chain triglycerides by enzymatic modification has been studied because of its specific nutritional function. Most studies focus on the optimization of the process (Peng et al., 2020) and the discussion of the functional mechanism (Cheng et al., 2024), but the comprehensive analysis of the lipid molecules in the product and their influence on the volatile flavor has been neglected. In this study, lipidomics and flavoromics were employed to systematically analyze the complete lipid profiles and changes in volatile flavors of medium- and long-chain triglycerides before (LCTMCT group) and after (MLCT group) interesterification. Furthermore, correlations between different medium- and long-chain triglycerides and key volatile flavor substances were established.

Enzymatic interesterification affects the TG, DG, and PE structures

Comprehensive lipidomic profiling of blended medium- and long-chain triglyceride systems (FO/CO), their enzymatically interesterified counterparts (MLCT), and silica column-purified MLCT products (MLCT1) demonstrates pronounced structural and compositional remodeling across lipid species. This molecular reorganization is primarily mediated through enzymatic catalysis-driven acyl migration and chromatographic fractionation-induced lipid class segregation. Notably, GL and PE exhibited the most significant dynamic alterations (Fig. 5).

The possible mechanisms of lipid molecules and their conversion into volatile flavor molecules during the enzymatic modification of fish oil and coconut oil.

Interesterification reaction profoundly altered GL composition, with DG and TG increasing significantly in MLCT compared to LCTMCT (p < 0.05). These shifts reflected the enzymatic reorganization of fatty acyl chains, favoring the synthesis of structured triglycerides (MLCTs) and stabilizing DG/MG intermediates. After silica gel treatment (MLCT1), DG and TG further increased, while FFA declined, suggesting selective retention of nonpolar TG species and removal of polar FFA. The stability of total MG and MGDG despite these shifts implies compositional balancing, likely driven by MG and MGDG enrichment compensating for losses in other GL subclasses.

Xuan et al. (2022) demonstrated through comprehensive lipidomic analysis that the enzymatic hydrolysis of tuna oil mediated by lipases A and B primarily affects TGs, DGs, and MGs, with these glycerolipid species undergoing the most significant compositional alterations. Similarly, Zhang, Lu, et al. (2021) used lipidomics to systematically investigate the molecular transformations of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA)- and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)-containing acylglycerol esters during enzymatic catalysis, revealing that TGs were the main molecular category with substantial differential regulation throughout the catalytic process. These studies support our findings on the changes in GL components during enzymatic interesterification.

Notably, the current research indicated that interesterification reactions between freshwater fish oil and coconut oil significantly modify the molecular architecture of PE. This suggests a potential regulatory role of endogenous phospholipase activity (PLA1-mediated interesterification) in this structural reorganization (Zhang et al., 2023). Wu et al. (2024) synthesized omega-3 fatty acid-based phospholipids (omega-3-PLs) via PLA1-mediated enzymatic interesterification between omega-3-enriched partial acylglycerols and phospholipids, providing further evidence for the involvement of phospholipases in PE structural changes.

To sum up, interesterification reaction and silica gel purification reshaped the lipidome of fish oil blends, with TG, DG, and PE undergoing the most pronounced changes. Similar phenomena were observed in the study by Wang et al. (2024), where TG and PE were identified as the primary precursors of volatile compounds in lipase-treated lightly salted large yellow croaker. These changes in TG and PE optimized the composition and stability of triglycerides and reduced polar/oxidation-sensitive lipids, aligning with industrial goals of enhancing shelf life and flavor quality. Enzymatic interesterification not only reconstructs the structure of glycerides but also affects the structure of phospholipids. Similar phenomena were also discovered in the research conducted by Zhao et al. (2025), who found that key aroma compounds were associated with PE. However, the trade-offs in phospholipid and sphingolipid content warrant further investigation into downstream nutritional and functional effects. Future studies should correlate lipidomic shifts with oxidative stability assays and sensory evaluations to validate practical benefits.

Enzymatic interesterification improves flavors in fish oil by mild oxidation of PUFA

The present research found that enzymatic interesterification between fish oil and coconut oil under mild oxidation of PUFA could improve the fishy volatile flavors in fish oil, as shown in Fig. 5. Similar studies have also been reported by Mu et al. (2024), who discovered that enzymatic interesterification of goat butter with coconut oil, by reducing the short-chain fatty acid content in structural esters, combined with antioxidants, significantly diminished the goaty flavor of goat butter. Chen et al. proposed a two-step enzyme-catalyzed method to generate new aromatic odors that can alter the negative flavor perception of goat milk products (Chen et al., 2021). However, Zou et al. (2023) reported that nitrogen-protected interesterification of beef tallow, soybean oil, and hydrogenated oil effectively prevented lipid oxidation, thereby maintaining the native volatile profile of the modified lipid products throughout the reaction process. Furthermore, lipid saturation impacts the profile and abundance of volatile compounds through its role in lipid oxidation (Zhao et al., 2025). This indicates that the effect of interesterification on lipid oxidation and flavor may vary depending on the reaction conditions and the type of lipids involved.

The strong positive correlations between FFAs and oxidation-derived aldehydes (e.g., hexanal and 2,4-heptagonal) suggest that interesterification reduced lipid oxidation pathways by stabilizing triglycerides. This is consistent with findings in fish oil oxidation studies, where structural modifications, such as esterification, inhibit hydroperoxide formation and subsequent aldehyde generation (Kubiak-Tomaszewska et al., 2022). For instance, using antioxidants like tert-butyl hydroquinone in fish oil has been shown to suppress rancidity by interrupting free radical propagation (Das et al., 2024), analogous to how interesterification may stabilize lipid structures. Hexanal, heptanal, nonanal, 1-octen-3-ol, and 1-penten-3-ol are key fishy odors compounds in fish, mainly produced via enzymatic reactions and lipid autoxidation (Wu et al., 2022). The reduction of “fishy” odorants (e.g., aldehydes and alcohol) further supports the efficacy of enzymatic interesterification as a deodorization strategy, akin to antioxidant applications in lipid preservation (Yi et al., 2023). FAAs play a key role in their formation process, which is driven by oxidation, and controlling heating temperature and time can optimize the flavor of processed meat products (Zhou et al., 2022). This finding aligns with previous studies highlighting the role of FFAs as precursors for flavor-active esters during interesterification (Zhang, Lu, et al., 2021).

The significant positive correlations between ω-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA) and aldehydes like nonanal suggest that the structural integrity of PUFAs may slow the volatilization of certain oxidation products. This aligns with studies showing that PUFAs in fish oil matrices can stabilize volatile compounds through hydrophobic interactions, even under oxidative stress (Chen et al., 2024). However, the relatively low correlation coefficients highlight the need for further optimization to balance ω-3 retention and flavor stability.

Hexanal, heptanal, and 2,4-heptadienal, as typical lipid oxidation products derived from PUFAs, were reduced during the interesterification reaction (Table 3). This decrease was correlated with the conversion of triglycerides into FFAs, which exhibited higher oxidative stability due to the absence of glycerol-bound pro-oxidant hydroxyl groups (Brejchova et al., 2021). This mechanism provides a clear explanation for the observed reduction in oxidation-derived aldehydes, highlighting the importance of triglyceride conversion in improving the flavor stability of fish oil.

The observed flavor improvements align with studies on enzymatic interesterification, although the chemical method resulted in a high retention of ω-3 fatty acids, which is a critical factor for nutritional applications (Yang et al., 2025). Interesterification reaction achieved comparable odor reduction but with low energy input, positioning it as a scalable alternative for industrial deodorization. Nevertheless, long-term storage trials indicated a gradual resurgence of rancid notes, likely due to ester hydrolysis under high humidity. Future work should explore stabilization strategies, such as antioxidant incorporation or microencapsulation, to prolong sensory benefits. For example, microencapsulation could protect the lipids from moisture and oxygen, thereby reducing hydrolysis and oxidation during storage.

In addition, the use of Pearson correlation analysis to interpret lipid-volatile relationships mirrors approaches in thermally oxidized fatty acid systems, where multivariate statistics effectively disentangle complex interactions between lipid classes and volatiles. Future studies could integrate enzymatic interesterification with antioxidant treatments to enhance oxidative stability while preserving flavor profiles. Additionally, exploring enzymatic specificity in acyl migration during interesterification could refine MLCT synthesis for targeted flavor and nutritional outcomes. For instance, selecting enzymes with specific acyl chain preferences could allow for the production of MLCTs with desired fatty acid compositions, thereby optimizing both flavor and nutritional value.

This study bridged lipidomics and flavoromics in enzymatically modified fish oil, demonstrating how structural reorganization modulated both oxidative stability and flavor quality (Fig. 5). While interesterification effectively reduces fishy odors and enhances ester-derived flavors, the persistence of aldehydes linked to ω-3 PUFAs highlights the complexity of lipid-volatile interactions. The interesterification process and subsequent silica gel column treatment significantly altered the volatile flavor profile of fish oil, as evidenced by e-nose analysis, PCA, and GC–MS results. These findings highlight the profound impact of structural modification and purification steps on the sensory characteristics of lipid systems, particularly in mitigating undesirable odors while modulating aromatic compounds.

The reduction in aldehydes (e.g., nonanal) and phenolic compounds (e.g., eugenol) during interesterification likely stemmed from the reorganization of triglyceride structures, altering the accessibility of lipid oxidation precursors or esterification pathways. Ethyl ester formation might be favored under interesterification conditions, promoting esterification of FFAs with ethanol or interesterification reactions. Silica gel treatment likely removes residual polar volatiles (e.g., short-chain acids), contributing to a “cleaner” sensory profile. These findings demonstrate that interesterification, combined with silica gel purification, is a viable strategy to improve the sensory quality of fish oil-based products. By reducing harsh odorants and enhancing milder esters, this approach could expand applications in functional foods or nutraceuticals where flavor acceptance is critical.

Future studies should correlate specific volatile markers with sensory panel data to validate perceived flavor improvements. For example, determining the threshold values of key volatile compounds and their contribution to overall flavor perception could provide a more accurate assessment of the effectiveness of the interesterification and purification processes. Conclusively, the interplay between interesterification and post-treatment purification steps critically shapes the volatile flavor profile of fish oil. This study provides a mechanistic basis for tailoring lipid systems to achieve desirable sensory properties while retaining nutritional benefits. Further research is needed to explore the long-term effects of these modifications on the stability and functionality of lipid products in different food matrices.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidated the dual effects of enzymatic modification on lipid composition and flavor profile in grass carp visceral fish oil through integrated lipidomics and flavoromics approaches. During the hydrolysis of fish oil into FFAs, mild oxidation could occur due to heating. The interesterification reaction did not alter the total fatty acid content overall, but it modified lipid molecular configurations. This process not only restructured glycerolipid molecules, such as TG and DGs, but also impacted phospholipid structures like PE. After interesterification, freshwater fish oil exhibited a significant reduction in fishy odors (aldehydes and alcohols) and an increase in desirable aroma compounds (esters). Correlation analysis revealed that medium- and long-chain triacylglycerols and FFAs were positively correlated with key volatile flavor compounds in interesterified fish oil. This suggests that freshwater fish oil may achieve reduced fishy odorss through enzymatic interesterification by restructuring lipid molecules and inducing moderate oxidation of PUFAs. The combined lipidomic-flavoromic framework established herein provides a novel strategy for valorizing underutilized freshwater fish oil.
