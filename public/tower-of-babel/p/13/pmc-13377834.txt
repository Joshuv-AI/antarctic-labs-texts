Combining arthroscopic microfracture, autologous bone grafting, and PRP injection for the treatment of Hepple IV-V stage osteochondral lesions of talus
Authors: Hao Han, Qi Lu, Long Yang, Yaozhou Shi, Yuehua Hui, Rongjian Shi, Qinwei Guo, Jie Li, Guangchao Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to evaluate the clinical and radiological outcomes of arthroscopic autologous cancellous bone grafting combined with platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection in patients with osteochondral lesions of talus (OLT).Methods This retrospective study was conducted at Xuzhou Renci Hospital from January 2020 to June 2023, including patients was diagnosed with Hepple stage IV or V OLT by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). All patients underwent arthroscopic debridement and microfracture, autologous cancellous bone grafting, and PRP injection. Preoperative and postoperative assessments were performed using the visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain, the American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle-Hindfoot Scale and Kofoed score for ankle function. Radiological outcomes were evaluated by the magnetic resonance observation of cartilage repair tissue (MOCART). A paired sample t-test was used for statistical analysis to compare preoperative and postoperative data, with a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Results Of the 21 patients with single cystic OLT with a complete set of follow-up data, there were 13 males and 8 females, with a mean age of 49.76 ± 10.24 years, a mean follow-up time of 16.81 ± 2.46 months and a mean illness duration of 12.47 ± 5.51 months. The mean VAS had significant (P < 0.001 decreased from 6 (5, 6) preoperatively to 1 (1, 1.5) at the last follow-up. The mean AOFAS and Kofoed scores significantly (P < 0.001) improved from 63.24 ± 9.81 preoperatively to 89.33 ± 5.47 at the last follow-up and from 28.43 ± 8.77 to 89.14 ± 5.19, respectively. All patients underwent follow-up MRI with a mean MOCART score of 69.10 ± 10.36 at last follow-up. No complications occurred in all patients, and no adverse reactions were observed at the donor knee site.ConclusionArthroscopic microfracture, autologous cancellous bone grafting and PRP injection is a promising technique for treating OLT, with favorable short-term pain relief, functional improvement and a high patient satisfaction rate for Hepple IV–V OLT. It is necessary to conduct large-scale, prospective, randomized controlled trials to further validate the long-term efficacy and safety of this procedure.Keywords Osteochondral lesions of talus (OLT), Arthroscopy, autologous cancellous bone grafting, platelet-rich plasma (PRP).

Introduction

Osteochondral lesions of talus (OLT) is a common cartilage injury that can occur in up to 50% of acute ankle sprains and fractures, especially associated with sports injuries [1, 2]. The typical clinical manifestations include chronic pain, swelling, stiffness, and mechanical symptoms of the ankle joint upon weight-bearing [3]. The secondary arthritis may occur in severe cases, which significantly impairs the patient’s motor function and overall quality of life. Cases particularly classified as Hepple stage IV and subchondral cyst (Hepple stage V) often require surgical intervention. The purpose of surgical treatment is to relieve pain, restore normal joint function, and reconstruct the subchondral bone scaffold [4].

Surgical treatment techniques for OLT are diverse and can be broadly categorized into reconstructive and restorative techniques. Restorative techniques include cancellous bone grafting, autologous chondrocyte implantation, matrix-induced autologous chondrocyte implantation, autologous or allogeneic osteochondral transplantation, or vascularized osteochondral transplantation [5, 6]. These techniques typically involve ankle osteotomy or wedge osteotomy, which disrupts normal bone anatomy and compromises the blood supply around the talus. This is associated with complications such as intraoperative cartilage damage, malunion or nonunion, chronic pain, and secondary osteoarthritis [7]. Reparative techniques include osteochondral stimulation surgery, arthroscopic microfracture, autologous matrix-induced chondrogenesis, arthroscopic debridement, fragment internal fixation, and intramedullary stimulation techniques. Among these, arthroscopic debridement and subchondral bone microfracture is the most widely described surgical procedure [8, 9]. Despite the advantages of minimal invasiveness, rapid postoperative recovery, and fewer complications, the long-term effects of this technique are limited. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) contains a high concentration of platelets, which secretes and releases various growth factors, promoting chondrocyte proliferation, differentiation, and matrix synthesis [10]. PRP has achieved positive clinical results in the treatment of knee osteochondral lesions or osteoarthritis (OA) [11]. The present study aimed to analyze a surgical technique for OLT that combines arthroscopic debridement with microfracture, cancellous bone grafting, and PRP injection procedure.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. A retrospective, consecutive case series design was employed, supplemented by follow-up investigations. All cases between January 2020 and June 2023 were screened for eligibility. A total of 21 patients who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate, completed clinical and radiological follow-up were eventually enrolled in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All patients reported a history of at least one ankle sprain to the affected ankle. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Renci Hospital (ID: XZRCLL-KT-202511001) and complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided informed consent and signed the informed consent forms.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Chronic ankle pain unresponsive to systematic conservative treatment for more than 6 months; (2) Preoperative MRI examination confirmed the diagnosis of osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLT), classified as Hepple IV-V; (3) Treatment with arthroscopic microfracture, autologous cancellous bone grafting combined with PRP injection; (4) Regular follow-up for more than one year after surgery and re-examination of MRI. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Concomitant ankle osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), or gouty arthritis (GA); (2) Concomitant foot bone deformities, soft tissue deformities, or lower limb alignment abnormalities; (3) Infection, tumors, severe immune system disorders, coagulation disorders, or bleeding disorders; (4) Intraoperative conversion to open surgery; (5) A history of prior surgical treatment of the affected ankle.

Surgical technique

The location and depth of talar cartilage lesions were determined through magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 1), and the surgical plan was formulated accordingly, with anterior or posterior arthroscopic approach. All patients received general anesthesia or intra-spinal anesthesia. A tourniquet was applied to the thigh of the ipsilateral lower limb. The patient’s foot was tractioned at a certain angle towards the outer edge of the operating table to fully allow for plantar flexion and dorsiflexion of the ankle joint.

Diagnostic arthroscopy, debridement, and microfracture

All surgeries were performed by a highly experienced senior orthopedic surgeon specialized in sports medicine. Standard disinfection procedures were followed, and a tourniquet were applied for hemostasis. A 4.0 mm, 30° arthroscope (CONMED, Utica, NY) was used for ankle diagnosis and treatment. First, a diagnostic ankle arthroscopy was performed. Talus osteochondral lesions could be better revealed by forcing the ankle into plantar flexion under anterior arthroscopy or by entering dorsiflexion under posterior ankle arthroscopy. Any potential pathological changes were thoroughly examined and assessed such as articular cartilage, ligaments, bone spurs, or synovial tissue. A chisel was used to release the adhesions of the proliferative synovium, and bone spurs and loose bodies were removed. If a component of the deltoid ligament or the lateral collateral ligament was ruptured, a ligament reconstruction surgery was performed simultaneously. The initial treatment for OLT was consisted of thorough curettage and debridement of unstable cartilage and osteochondral tissue. All superficial cartilage, cystic components, and necrotic bone defects were debrided under arthroscopy with a curette until the intact subchondral bone was exposed and formed a 90° angle with the surrounding cartilage wall. The size of the lesion, including its diameter and depth, was further confirmed by arthroscopic measurement with a probe. A microfracture cones were used to establish a 5 mm deep subchondral channel at the bottom and side walls of the cartilage defect area, with a spacing of 3–5 mm between channels (Fig. 2).

Harvesting and transplantation of autologous cancellous bone

The ipsilateral proximal tibia Gerdy’s tubercle was selected as the donor site for cancellous bone collection. After carefully palpation of Gerdy’s tubercle, a 3-cm longitudinal incision was made through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and deep fascia. A 10-mm-long cylindrical bone sample was harvested from the Gerdy’s tubercle using a bone harvest tube of appropriate diameter (8–10 mm). The autologous cancellous bone below was collected with a curette, and the cylindrical bone was used to fill the defect area of the Gerdy tubercle (Fig. 3). A dry arthroscopy technique was used during the implantation of autologous cancellous bone, and a shaver aspirator was employed to aspirate the intra-articular fluid. The prepared cancellous bone was accurately delivered and placed into the lesion through a specially designed delivery guiding device. The graft was injected into the defect area with the assistance of the scraper and probe until a flat cartilage surface was maintained, even at the level of the subchondral bone. After several cycles of ankle extension and flexion, the cancellous bone graft was verified to be securely fixed in the defect site (Fig. 4A-C).

Preparation and injection of PRP

PRP was prepared using the platelet separation system in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 45 mL of venous blood was drawn from each patient and mixed with 5 milliliters of sodium citrate for anticoagulation. The 50-mL total solution was centrifuged at 4 °C, 3000r/min for 10 min, yielding 5 mL of PRP at the end of centrifugation. During the procedure, 1 ml of saline combined with 1000U of thrombin was used to prepare thrombin-activated PRP, which was injected into the lesion site after the PRP solidified into a gel-like consistency. We recommend that patients perform ankle flexion and extension exercises to evenly distribute the PRP solution (Fig. 4D-F).

Postoperative management and rehabilitation protocol

After patients return to the ward, the affected limb was elevated and ice therapy was applied to alleviate swelling and pain. Prophylactic antibiotics, antiedema and analgesic medications were administered, nutritional support and symptomatic treatment to prevent stress ulcers. A protective rehabilitation regimen was implemented for the initial 6 weeks, with continuous passive motion (CPM) exercises for the ankle joint commencing 48 h postoperatively and continuing for 6 weeks. As the patient recovered to the maximum achievable range of motion, the frequency and duration of passive activities were gradually increased. Partial weight-bearing was started after 6 weeks and full weight-bearing was permitted at 8 to 10 weeks postoperatively. Patients typically regain full range of motion within 12 weeks after surgery, the stability of muscles and the ankle joint was enhanced through proprioceptive training.

Observation indicators and efficacy evaluation

Postoperative outpatient follow-up examinations were scheduled at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively. CT or MRI scans were performed to assess talar cartilage recovery, and improvements in subjective symptoms and pain during walking were documented. The patient’s visual analogue scale (VAS), American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle-hindfoot score, and the Kofoed score for the ankle joint were recorded. The Magnetic Resonance Observation of Cartilage Repair Tissue (MOCART) 2.0 score was used to evaluate the recovery of osteochondral lesions (Fig. 5).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data, the continuous data that conforms to the normal distribution was presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). The mean difference between preoperative and postoperative values was calculated, and its 95% confidence interval (CI) was estimated using a paired t-test. Non-normally distributed measurement data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for paired comparison. Results

Demographic data

This study included a total of 21 patients: 13 males and 8 females, with an mean age of 49.76 ± 10.24 years (29–67 years) at surgery. All patients presented with unilateral talus cartilage injury, with 12 cases located on the right ankle and 9 cases on the left. The lesions were classified according to the Hepple classification system: type IV in 12 cases and type V in 9. The preoperative mean body mass index (BMI) was 25.33 ± 2.52 kg/m2 (19.1 ~ 26.3 kg/m2), and the interval time from injury to surgery was 12.47 ± 5.51months (4–26 months) (Table 1).

Clinical efficacy and imaging evaluation before and after surgery

All patients successfully underwent surgery and completed a followed-up period for more than 12 months. As shown in Table 2, significant improvements were observed in VAS, AOFAS, and Kofoed scores from the preoperative at last follow-up (all P < 0.001). The median Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain score decreased significantly from 6 (5, 6) preoperatively to 1 (1, 1.5) at the last follow-up, with a MD of −5.0 (95% BCa bootstrap CI: −5.17 to −4.36, P < 0.001) and a large effect size of 0.89 (expressed as r = Z/√N). The AOFAS score improved significantly from 63.24 ± 9.81 preoperatively to 89.33 ± 5.47 at the last follow-up, with a MD of 26.10 (95% CI: 20.60 to 31.60, P < 0.001) and a large effect size of 2.16 (calculated as Cohen’s d). The Kofoed score also showed substantial improvement, increasing from 28.43 ± 8.76 to 89.14 ± 5.18. The corresponding MD was 60.72 (95% CI: 55.82 to 65.61, P < 0.001), with a very large effect size of 5.65 (calculated as Cohen’s d). All improvements were statistically significant, with large effect sizes, indicating a strong clinical impact of the intervention. Ankle function at the final follow-up was evaluated using AOFAS Ankle-Hindfoot Scale: 12 cases were rated excellent, 9 cases were rated good, with an excellent-good rate of 100% (21/21). Ankle function was also evaluated using the Kofoed score at the last follow-up: 16 cases were rated excellent, 5 cases were rated good, resulting in an excellent-good rate of 100% (21/21). At the last follow-up, the mean MOCART score was 69.10 ± 10.36 (range 53–89). No complications such as wound infection, tissue necrosis, vascular or nerve injury, and donor site pain occurred were observed in all patients after surgery.

Disscussion

The most important finding of present study was that arthroscopic microfracture combined with autologous cancellous bone grafting and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection is effective in the treatment of Hepple stage IV-V osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLT). At a short-term follow-up of at least 1 year, favorable clinical and radiological outcomes were achieved without any donor site morbidity. All 21 patients resumed their daily activities, and this study suggests an alternative therapeutic approach to osteochondral transplantation.

The talus can be anatomically divided into three distinct regions: head, neck, and body, with most of its surface covered by articular cartilage. This cartilage tissue is avascular and lacks neural nourishment, and as a bone without muscle attachment, its nutrition mainly relies on synovial fluid. These factors collectively lead to the difficulty in achieving effective self-repair after injury [12]. OLT is a common cause of ankle pain and dysfunction, and severe cartilage injuries (Hepple IV-V type) with subchondral cyst formation often require surgical intervention [5, 13]. There are numerous surgical methods for OLT and their efficacy remains controversial. Bone marrow stimulation (BMS) is the gold standard for treating OLT [14], primarily indicated for lesions with an area of < 1.5 cm2 and a depth of < 5 mm [15]. However, the fibrocartilage regenerated by microfractures is mainly composed of Type I collagen, which has significant biological differences compared to the Type II collagen that constitutes the normal cartilage of the talus. The elasticity and wear resistance of fibrocartilage are inferior to that of normal cartilage, making it more denaturation to degeneration under shear stress [16, 17]. Researchers have found that the cartilage repair scores observed during secondary arthroscopy examination after BMS were significantly lower than those of cartilage transplantation or retrograde drilling, with 36% of lesions remained incompletely healed [18]. PRP is rich in growth factors, fibrinogen, and various bioactive substances, which can promote the repair of damaged cartilage tissue [19, 20]. In the present study, we evaluated a combined arthroscopic strategy involving microfracture, autologous cancellous bone grafting, and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection for the treatment of Hepple IV-V OLT. This study demonstrates that arthroscopic cancellous bone grafting combined with PRP injection for the treatment of OLT can effectively repair cartilage injury, while simultaneously alleviating pain in the affected limb and improving ankle joint function, with satisfactory short-term efficacy. While the present study demonstrated significant improvements in pain and function with large effect sizes, these results should be interpreted with caution. As a retrospective, single‑arm, non‑controlled case series, this study cannot establish the superiority of the combined procedure relative to conventional microfracture, autologous osteochondral transplantation (AOCT), or other cartilage repair techniques. The large effect sizes observed for VAS, AOFAS, and Kofoed scores reflect substantial within‑patient improvement but do not equate to comparative effectiveness against standard treatments.

It is usually required to perform osteotomy of the medial or lateral malleolus to adequately expose the lesion area due to the intricate anatomical structure of the ankle joint [21], which disrupts the normal anatomical structure and damages the blood supply around the talus. At the same time, there are complications such as intraoperative cartilage iatrogenic injury, delayed healing or non-healing of the osteotomy site, chronic pain or swelling, and the need for secondary surgery to remove internal fixation devices [22]. The advantages of traditional open surgery in providing a surgical field for OLT can now be achieved through arthroscopic procedures. Arthroscopic cancellous bone grafting for OLT has been demonstrated to significantly alleviate pain and improve ankle joint function [23, 24]. In present study, we observed the lesion area and performed bone graft through arthroscopy, whose primary advantages include: (1) the use of mini-incisions reduces surgical trauma and protect the blood supply of local tissues, thereby palliate postoperative pain and inflammatory response, promoting early activity and functional recovery; (2) minimizes complications commonly associated with open osteotomy procedures, shortens hospital stays, and accelerates recovery; (3) eliminates the need for internal fixation and intraoperative fluoroscopy, thereby reducing radiation exposure for both patients and medical staff, and eliminates the requirement for secondary surgeries to remove internal fixation. However, this technique is highly challenging to perform, requiring proficient arthroscopic skills, and the learning curve may be relatively steep. Previous studies have reported that the complication rate of ankle arthroscopy is higher than that of other joints, particularly in terms of neurological complications and postoperative infections [25]. Sugimoto et al reported [26] the mid-term clinical outcomes of posterior ankle arthroscopy in the treatment of posterior ankle impingement syndrome (PAIS), and evaluated the learning curve as well as its impact on the outcomes, concluding that surgical experience with 26 patients is required to master posterior arthroscopy. Zhao et al [27] conducted an investigation and analysis and identified a learning curve cutoff value for 22 cases of all-inside arthroscopic Brostrom-Gold surgery for chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI). The operative time and postoperative hospital stay in the proficient phase were significantly improved compared with those in the learning phase. However, no significant differences were found in clinical function scores between surgical method’s learning and proficiency stages. In our study, all procedures were performed by a senior physician (Guangchao Cao) with proficient arthroscopic surgical experience, and no severe complications was observed.

Cancellous bone possesses numerous trabecular spaces and a soft texture, which can adapt to the shape of the damaged area and provide effective structural support, facilitating the growth of blood vessels and fibrous tissue, and helping to restore the bone integrity and function [7, 28, 29]. Regarding the harvest of cancellous bone, previous literature has reported that the iliac crest, distal tibia, and the ankle bone itself are common donor sites [23, 30]. The iliac crest has demonstrated favorable outcomes in reducing donor site complications and is one of the frequently utilized transplantation sites. In this study, selection of the ipsilateral tibial Gerdy tubercle for obtaining cancellous bone has the following two major advantages: (1) There is no need to enlarge the surgical disinfection area. (2) The Gerdy tubercle is far from the joint surface, thereby minimizing the risk of damage to the articular cartilage surface [31]. In terms of our experience with cancellous bone transplantation: first, the cancellous bone is placed into the defect area and compacted through the bone graft sleeve, while PRP is applied to the surface of the cancellous bone and the effect of gravity can further prevent autologous cancellous bone displacement. A study by Rikken reported on arthroscopic BMS for OLT with at least 8 years of follow-up, and approximately 1/3 of the patients showed progressive cartilage degeneration on imaging, with even the risk of developing end-stage ankle arthritis [16]. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) study on PRP demonstrated that PRP injection after microfracture achieved superior mid-term outcomes compared to hyaluronic acid (HA) [32]. The meta-analysis by Woo et al. included 235 patients with OLT, and the results showed that the effect of the ankle arthroscopy microfracture combined with PRP group was significantly better than that of the simple microfracture or combined with other biological agents [20]. In this study, we achieved precise targeted injections under arthroscopic monitoring, with PRP and thrombin prepared into a gel that firmly adheres to the cartilage defect area, which is beneficial for better repair of OLT. The MOCART score was 69.1 ± 10.4 at 1-year follow-up, which is higher than the treatment effect of simple microfracture reported in previous studies [33, 34], indicating that PRP injection can compensate for the inherent limitations of microfracture surgery and promote the cartilage repair and integration. Although several studies have demonstrated the clinical efficacy of PRP as an adjuvant therapy for OLT, there remains controversies regarding the optimal concentration, preparation method, and timing of PRP in the management of OLT. The studies suggested that the optimal platelet concentration of PRP for OLT is recommended to be 4–6 times the baseline concentration [35–38]. During PRP preparation, substances such as reactive oxygen species and inflammatory factors released by white blood cells can actually impair cartilage tissue, thereby affecting the treatment’s efficacy. Miura et al [39] compared the clinical outcomes and time to recovery between leukocyte-poor PRP (LP-PRP) and leukocyte-rich PRP (LR-PRP) in OLT surgery, concluding that LP-PRP enabled an earlier return to sports and maintained good clinical scores two years postoperatively compared to LR-PRP. Currently, more high-level evidence-based medicine evidence is needed for support.

This study also has some limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size and a short follow-up period. The retrospective design lacks a control group, preventing direct comparison with other surgical procedures. Second, due to medical ethical constraints and the need to avoid iatrogenic injury, cartilage recovery could not be evaluated by second-look arthroscopy postoperatively, nor histological examination was performed. Finally, the most frequently utilized scoring system was the AOFAS score, which primarily incorporates activity levels and patient-reported scores. However, these scores did not provide functional or objective assessments. It is necessary to conduct multi-center, large-sample, long-term follow-up studies to evaluate and verify clinical effects and complications.

Conclusion

Arthroscopic microfracture, autologous cancellous bone grafting and PRP injection is a safe method for OLT. This technique has been proven effective with satisfying pain relief, low complication rate, and significant functional improvement in the treatment of Hepple IV–V OLT.
