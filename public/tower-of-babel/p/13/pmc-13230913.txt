Structural and mechanistic basis for antibody neutralization of the measles fusion protein

ABSTRACT
Measles virus (MeV) is a highly contagious pathogen and a major global health threat. Resurgent infections, driven by insufficient vaccine coverage, waning herd immunity, and the vulnerability of immunocompromised populations, highlight the urgent need for effective countermeasures. While most vaccine-elicited antibodies target the hemagglutinin (H) protein, antibodies against the fusion (F) protein are also potent inhibitors. However, the specific target sites on this class I fusion protein, which undergoes dramatic conformational changes during entry, remain insufficiently characterized. Here, we characterize four mAbs targeting distinct F conformations. Structural analyses map antibody interactions, revealing that three neutralizing mAbs recognize the metastable prefusion conformation, while a non-neutralizing mAb binds only the post-triggered state. Biophysical and functional assays define distinct mechanisms of action: neutralization occurs either by stabilizing the prefusion protein or by preventing the extended intermediate from completing fusion. Uniquely, we detect a novel mechanism where an antibody prematurely triggers F activation but blocks the subsequent refolding required for viral entry. These findings provide the first detailed mapping of neutralizing epitopes on MeV F, establishing a framework for the rational design of F-targeted interventions.

Introduction

Measles is a highly contagious viral disease that has reemerged globally in recent years. Achieving widespread vaccine-induced immunity is critical to prevent outbreaks, particularly as unvaccinated and immunocompromised individuals remain at high risk for severe disease. Although the measles vaccine has an excellent long-term safety and efficacy record, neither global nor American vaccination rates have reached the 95% threshold required for herd immunity1–6. Consequently, the recent resurgence in measles cases reflects a failure to vaccinate rather than vaccine failure. Over the past five years, declining vaccination rates have been fueled by disruptions in routine immunization programs during the COVID-19 pandemic and by the increasing impact of vaccine hesitancy or active anti-vaccination sentiments1–6.

These developments increase the vulnerability of individuals who cannot be vaccinated due to their early age or medical contraindications. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) with total immunoglobulin remains the only recommended option for these individuals; however, its reliability is threatened by declining MeV-specific antibody titers within the donor pool1,7–15. With measles elimination efforts stalled and a growing number of high-risk individuals, there is an urgent need for effective antiviral therapies. However, currently, there is no antiviral treatment approved for measles. Passive immunization using monoclonal or polyclonal antibodies has long been a cornerstone in preventing and treating other viral infections, particularly in individuals who are unable to receive active vaccination. However, only a limited number of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have received clinical approval for the treatment of those viral infectious diseases16–18, and none are available for measles.

Antibodies can exert antiviral effects by targeting viral glycoproteins critical for host cell entry. In MeV, this process involves two envelope proteins: the H protein, which binds host receptors, and the F protein, which mediates membrane fusion. MeV F is a class I trimeric fusion protein undergoing substantial conformational rearrangements from prefusion to postfusion states, a feature shared with many other enveloped viruses19–22. Fusion is a dynamic, multistep process: following H-F interaction and receptor engagement, F transitions through an extended “pre-hairpin” intermediate, during which its heptad repeat regions (HRN and HRC) span the viral and host membranes. These regions then fold into a six-helix bundle (6HB), completing fusion and enabling viral entry.

MAbs targeting MeV H and F offer several advantages: they can neutralize the virus early in infection, avoid the toxicity and off-target effects of small molecules, and provide protection for immunocompromised or vaccine-ineligible individuals1,16. Historical studies suggest that vaccine-induced virus neutralizing antibodies primarily target the H protein, but natural infection elicits robust neutralizing antibodies against both H and F, indicating that F is a promising target for mAbs development23,24. Strategies to block viral entry include targeting: (i) H protein binding to hSLAMF1 (CD150) or Nectin-4 receptors, (ii) F activation via H-F interaction, (iii) conformational transitions exposing the F fusion peptide, and (iv) F refolding and pore expansion. We have generated and characterized a panel of novel chimeric (mouse Fab/human Fc) mAbs targeting MeV F, including the previously characterized antibody 77.125. We present here the structures and functions of four antibodies selected for distinct epitope specificity and mechanisms of action. Three of these mAbs exhibit potent neutralization against the two currently circulating wild-type MeV strains, providing a strong foundation for antiviral development. Our data demonstrate that the MeV F protein contains multiple neutralization-sensitive epitopes that can be targeted by potent mAbs. By combining antibodies with distinct mechanisms, broad and synergistic inhibition can be achieved, raising the barrier to resistance. A cocktail of long-acting humanized anti-F antibodies could serve as a critical countermeasure for exposed health care workers or individuals unable to receive vaccination, for outbreak control, and as a complement to existing PEP strategies.

Results

Neutralization activity and F protein reactivity of chimeric monoclonal antibodies against MeV

We generated and characterized a panel of mouse monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting the MeV F protein. The Fab regions of four of these antibodies were grafted onto a common human Fc backbone. Here, we present the functional and structural properties of these chimeric mAbs, comprising three novel antibodies (Y10F, H8, and C6) and the previously characterized 77.1 (described in ref. 25) for comparison.

We first assessed the ability of these antibodies to recognize a variety of F glycoproteins, including F from two currently circulating wild-type strains (B3 and D8), the vaccine strain (Moraten), and two previously characterized B3 F variants (L454W F), which is characterized by a destabilized prefusion conformation26,27, and E455G F, which contains a stabilized prefusion conformation25,28 (Fig. 1A). Neutralization potency, measured by plaque reduction neutralization tests (PRNT) against four recombinant viruses (B3, D8, B3 F-L454W, and vaccine strain Moraten), revealed that 77.1 and Y10F were the most potent antibodies (Fig. 1B). 77.1 exhibited IC90 values ranging from 0.15-0.45 nM, while Y10F values ranged from 1.3–42 nM. H8 was less potent overall (IC90: 11-63 nM) but showed higher activity against the neuropathogenic F-destabilized B3 F-L454W28 variant (23 nM) compared to Y10F (42 nM). In contrast, C6 exhibited no measurable neutralizing activity (using a specific mechanistic assay described in Supplementary Fig. 1 we determined that the C6 epitope is exposed prior to complete refolding into the postfusion conformation, indicating that C6 is a post-trigger-specific antibody).

We next quantified antibody binding to purified, stabilized, soluble prefusion F ectodomain (FECTO25) using high-throughput surface plasmon resonance (SPR) on a Carterra LSA (Fig. 1C). Unlike constructs locked by disulfide bridges, this F protein relies on natural substitutions to stabilize the prefusion state, which allows it to undergo conformational transitions, but at a more gradual, controlled rate than the wild-type25. Three antibodies (77.1, H8, Y10F) bound this purified, prefusion MeV F. In contrast, the post-trigger-specific control mAb C6 showed no detectable binding to this prefusion-stabilized F, as expected for its post-triggered conformation-specific recognition.

We observed distinct kinetic signatures across the three binders. mAb 77.1 showed the highest affinity (KD = 1.71 ± 0.03 × 10⁻⁹ M). H8 showed an intermediate association and had the slowest dissociation, with a KD of 3.17 ± 2.06 × 10⁻⁹ M. Antibody Y10F displayed the slowest association rate (ka=1.79 × 104 M−1s−1), and dissociated more quickly, yielding a lower overall affinity (KD = 9.99 ± 0.18 × 10⁻⁸ M). To visualize the epitopes of each antibody, we performed negative-stain EM (NS-EM) on Fab-FECTO complexes (Fig. 1D). The structure of mAb 77.1, at the base of prefusion F, was previously described25. For the new antibodies, class averages showed that Y10F engages the prefusion apex of F, whereas H8 can bind both prefusion and postfusion conformations. In contrast, C6 only produced apparent postfusion complexes. Taken together, these results indicate that three mAbs that bind prefusion epitopes are neutralizing (77.1, Y10F, H8), while the mAb that binds only a post-trigger epitope is not neutralizing (C6) (see also Supplementary Fig. 1).

Stability of the F protein in antibody complexes

To assess how antibody binding affects the stability of the prefusion FECTO, we performed nano differential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF; NanoTemper Prometheus Panta) (Fig. 2A). FECTO was incubated with a threefold molar excess of Fab over F trimer for 30 min at room temperature. FECTO alone exhibited a melting temperature (Tm) of 47.2 °C, consistent with prior measurements25. The presence of the post-trigger-specific C6 had a negligible effect on the FECTO Tm ( ~ 0.7 °C). Although prefusion-specific antibodies (Y10F and H8) altered FECTO thermal stability, they did so in distinct ways. Specifically, Y10F increased it by 8.3 °C to 55.5 °C. In contrast, H8 decreased the FECTO Tm by 2.9 °C to 44.3 °C. In parallel, we monitored the cumulant (hydrodynamic) radius as a function of temperature (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 2). The FECTO trimer alone maintained a radius of ~9 nm until reaching an aggregation onset temperature (Ton) of ~39.7‒41.4 °C, after which the radius increased continuously. The sample containing the post-trigger-specific mAb C6 displayed a nearly identical aggregation profile (Ton = 44.6 °C), consistent with its inability to bind the prefusion ectodomain.

In contrast, the neutralizing antibodies distinctively modulated these hydrodynamic properties. The FECTO-Y10F complex appeared more compact initially and maintained this stability up to 52.7 °C, delaying the onset of aggregation relative to the protein alone. The FECTO-H8 complex exhibited a unique profile: despite H8 lowering the melting temperature of FECTO (Tm = 44.3 °C, Fig. 2A), the complex did not undergo immediate aggregation. Instead, it maintained a stable radius until 67.4 °C, a temperature corresponding to the thermal unfolding of the Fab itself. Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that while C6 has no impact on stability, Y10F stabilizes the prefusion trimer, and H8 prevents the formation of large aggregates despite lowering the activation barrier.

Mechanistically, the data suggest opposing modes of action for Y10F and H8: Y10F stabilizes the prefusion conformation and raises the energy barrier for activation, thereby preventing fusion triggering, whereas H8 lowers the activation barrier and promotes premature triggering of the fusion machinery.

To validate these mechanistic insights in the context of native membrane-anchored F, we performed cell-surface staining with the post-trigger-specific mAb C6 after preincubating cells at either 4 °C or 50 °C (the latter to thermally induce F triggering) in the presence of Y10F or H8 (Fig. 2C). Because C6 recognizes an epitope exposed only upon F triggering (Supplementary Fig. 1), its binding serves as a proxy for F activation. At 4 °C, C6 bound F in the presence of H8, but not in the presence of Y10F. At 50 °C, C6 showed no binding to F in the presence of Y10F. These findings corroborate the biophysical data, supporting a model in which Y10F raises the conformational activation threshold of F, thereby maintaining the prefusion state, while H8 lowers this barrier and drives premature activation.

We next asked whether the stabilizing effect of Y10F could block H8-mediated F activation. For the experiment shown in Fig. 2D, cells expressing either wild-type B3 F or the B3 E455G F mutant (which require more energy for activation25,28) were incubated overnight at 37 °C. The following day, Y10F was added for 30 min (at the indicated final concentration), after which H8 was added to achieve final concentrations of 100, 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, 3.13, or 1.56 nM. Cells were then transferred to 37 °C for 60 min. Following incubation, cells were stained with the conformation-specific mAb C6 to quantify exposure of the post-trigger epitope. In the absence of Y10F, H8 induced a dose-dependent increase in F triggering, which was more pronounced for the B3 F wt than for the stabilizing E455G mutant. In contrast, at the highest concentrations of Y10F, H8-induced F triggering was also completely abolished for the B3 F wt.

To define the kinetics of H8-induced triggering in purified protein, we performed time-resolved size-exclusion chromatography and followed the accumulation of signal in the void volume. FECTO, incubated alone at room temperature for 24 h, showed only a small, gradual increase in the 7-10 mL region, indicating slow, spontaneous refolding toward the postfusion state (Fig. 2E). In contrast, FECTO incubated with H8 Fab displayed a much stronger, time-dependent increase in this void-volume peak, accompanied by loss of the intact complex peak (Fig. 2F). Plotting the integrated void-volume signal as a function of incubation time revealed a single-exponential behavior, yielding an estimated half-life of 0.98 ± 0.58 h for H8-induced triggering (Fig. 2G).

To visualize the structural species that arise during this reaction, we next analyzed time-resolved negative-stain electron microscopy datasets of FECTO-H8 mixtures. Across all six time points, we repeatedly identified four main classes: prefusion FECTO without antibody (class 1), prefusion FECTO bound by a single H8 Fab (class 2), a top view of F bound by three Fabs (class 3), and a side view of F in the postfusion conformation with three bound Fabs (class 4, Fig. 2H). A fifth, highly heterogeneous class contained H8 bound to F fragments, free Fab, and other mixed species and was therefore excluded from quantitative analyses. Notably, classes 3 and 4 did not always appear together in the same datasets, suggesting that they may represent distinct conformational endpoints or intermediates rather than 2 distinct views of a single class. Over time, the occupancy of classes 1 and 2 decreased and became negligible at later time points, whereas classes 3 and 4 increasingly dominated the particle population (Fig. 2I-J). This shift in class distribution aligns with the size-exclusion data and supports a reaction pathway in which H8 converts prefusion F into multi-Fab-bound, post-trigger-like conformations and the postfusion state.

Cryo-EM mapping of Y10F and H8 epitopes on the F protein

To define the antibody epitopes on F, we determined cryo-EM structures of the FECTO in complex with Y10F alone, with Y10F and 77.1 bound simultaneously, and with H8 alone.

The FECTO-Y10F dataset exhibited mild preferred orientation, with many particles showing a tilted top view (Supplementary Fig. 3). Despite this, we reconstructed a 2.59 Å map that revealed asymmetric binding of a single Y10F Fab at the apex of FECTO (Fig. 3A). Y10F binds the region in the center of the apex, surrounded by the six glycosylation sites positioned on the three F2 subunits at positions 61 and 67 (Fig. 3A). Both heavy and light chains contribute to a dense interaction network. The heavy chain forms nine hydrogen bonds distributed across three protomers (including one F2 subunit and two F1 subunits), with residues N55 and N31 accounting for six of these nine bonds (Supplementary data 1). The light chain forms five hydrogen bonds with the same two F1 chains. We also simultaneously determined the structure of the FECTO-bound complex with Y10F and 77.1 (Fig. 3C) to test co-occupancy and any binding-induced conformational changes resulting from simultaneous binding of these two antibodies. Although both Fabs were supplied in excess, the complex displayed partial occupancy of each Fab, with individual particles displaying one, two, or three copies of 77.1. The combination of preferred orientation, asymmetric nature of the complex, and incomplete occupancy complicated particle selection. However, through symmetry expansion and focused masking, we obtained a reconstruction in which single copies of 77.1 and Y10F were fully resolved. Superposition of the Y10F-only and Y10F + 77.1 models yielded a Cα RMSD of 0.79 Å, indicating no significant global rearrangement of FECTO. Local differences were restricted to the 77.1 epitope, particularly residues 433-442, which showed an RMSD of ~2 Å, consistent with previous descriptions of 77.1-induced adjustments25. These data demonstrate that Y10F and 77.1 can co-bind F, supporting their use in an antibody cocktail.

In the prefusion state, H8 bound symmetrically to all three protomers at an epitope on the outer face of domain II, opposite the F vertex contacted by 77.1, producing a triangular-like structure. The epitope centered on the single loop in domain II (residues 396-405). The binding was dominated by the heavy chain and was stabilized by ten hydrogen bonds. PISA also identified a single salt bridge attributed to the light chain in proximity to residue G115, the first residue resolved in F1, placing the binding site near the furin cleavage site (Supplementary data 1). H8 binding induced a local shift of the 396-405 loop, with a maximal displacement of 2.5 Å toward the Fab.

H8 recognized the same topological site on postfusion F. Because domain II rotates relative to the rest of the protein during refolding around residue G43325, the Fab reoriented accordingly; whether H8 remains bound throughout this transition is unclear, as the lack of a transmembrane region in FECTO allows for greater degrees of freedom and facilitates the movement of the HRC domain. In the postfusion complex, contacts were again mediated mainly by the heavy chain, now comprising nine hydrogen bonds and two salt bridges, one per antibody chain (Supplementary data 1). The interface areas in pre- and postfusion were comparable (461 vs. 486 Å²). ΔG values for both conformations were estimated to be similar ( − 4.2 kcal/mol for prefusion and −3.1 kcal/mol for postfusion).

In summary, cryo-EM resolved Y10F as an apex-binding antibody that fits between glycan clusters on F2, confirmed the ability of Y10F to co-occupy F with 77.1 without major conformational changes of F, and showed that H8 targets a solvent-exposed loop in domain II that is accessible in both prefusion and postfusion conformations (see Supplementary Fig. 4 for antibody footprint on the F protein).

To further evaluate efficacy, antibodies were evaluated in a dissemination assay in human Epstein-Barr virus-transformed B-lymphoblastic cells (B-LCL)29. Consistent with the virus neutralization results, 77.1 inhibited MeV dissemination better than Y10F and H8, with an IC50 of 12.7 nM. Interestingly, while unable to block infection in neutralization assays, Y10F could inhibit MeV dissemination in B-LCL cultures at an IC50 of 22.3 nM (Fig. 5B).

To confirm functionality in a physiologically relevant epithelial cell context, bronchial ciliated human airway organoids differentiated at air-liquid interface (AO at ALI) were used. Prior to exposure to a mixture of mAbs and MeV, cultures were washed to remove mucus and mechanically disrupted to expose Nectin-4. All three mAbs inhibited MeV infection in this model (Fig. 5C). Notably, 77.1 achieved complete neutralization at all tested concentrations, whereas Y10F and H8 allowed residual infection at 10 nM and minimal infection at 100 nM (Fig. 5D).

We next evaluated the prophylactic efficacy of these mAbs in vivo using a cotton rat model of MeV infection30 (Fig. 5E). Cotton rats were inoculated 14 h before infection with a single dose (1 mg/kg) of the indicated mAbs. Four days after infection, lungs were collected and viral titers quantified (y-axis). A purified polyvalent human IgG (huIgG) preparation, which is used clinically for patients with primary immune deficiency and immune thrombocytopenia purpura, exhibited a neutralization titer of 640 at 60 mg/mL and served as a control. As expected, mAb 77.1 confirmed its strong in vivo efficacy previously demonstrated25. The second-most potent mAb, Y10F, reduced viral titers to levels near the limit of detection. The other two mAbs (C6 and H8) performed comparably to huIgG, each reducing viral titers relative to PBS (diluent)-treated animals but without a significant difference.

We then tested mAb efficacy when administered after infection (Fig. 5F). Cotton rats were inoculated 24 h after infection with a single dose (1 mg/kg) of the indicated mAbs. Four days post-infection, lungs were collected, and viral titers were quantified (y-axis). Under these conditions, mAb 77.1 showed significant efficacy, whereas the other mAbs did not reduce viral titers. We then asked whether the lack of activity in vivo of Y10F and H8 was due to faster clearance. We inoculated cotton rats intraperitoneally with 1 mg/kg of the three mAbs and the Human IgG. Blood was collected at 14, 38, 62, 86, and 110 h post-inoculation. We assessed the neutralizing activity for all and the presence of the three mAbs using a semi-quantitative cell-based ELISA at the time of infection and during the course of the challenge study (Supplementary Fig. 5).

The data show that only sera from animals inoculated with mAb 77.1 exhibited neutralizing activity (Supplementary Fig. 5A, expressed as mIU/mL y-axis), whereas all three mAbs remained at stable circulating levels throughout the experiment (14-110 h post-inoculation). However, at the starting serum dilution used for the neutralization assay (1:40), the other antibodies did not block viral infection. By interpolating the serum binding data onto standard curves of purified mAbs (Supplementary Fig. 5D), we estimated that serum concentrations remained in the low nanomolar range for all three antibodies (Table S2). Specifically, H8 showed the highest retention, with concentrations ranging from approximately 40 to 65 nM, while Y10F and 77.1 remained stable at 17-33 nM (Table S2). These results indicate that the lack of therapeutic efficacy observed for Y10F and H8 in the post-infection setting was not due to rapid clearance or instability of the antibodies in vivo.

To define the stage of the fusion process at which each mAb acts, we used an assay measuring fusion of red blood cells (RBCs) with HEK293T cells expressing modified versions of MeV H and F (Fig. 6B)31. To distinguish F-mediated anchoring from H-mediated attachment, we employed a chimeric receptor-binding protein consisting of the MeV H stalk fused to the head domain of human parainfluenza virus type 3 hemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HPIV3 HN). This H-HN chimera allows releasable attachment to sialic acid receptors (via HN) while still permitting MeV H-mediated triggering of F. As expected, RBCs bound through HN-sialic acid interactions could be released by adding Zanamivir25,31. In untreated conditions, ~38% of RBCs fused with HEK293T cells, ~22% irreversibly bound via F (fusion initiated, but incomplete), and ~40% were reversibly bound via HN only (fusion not initiated). Zanamivir treatment, which blocks HN-mediated attachment, prevented nearly all F-mediated anchoring, confirming that receptor engagement is required for F activation. Treatment with the HRC431 peptide inhibitor yielded mostly irreversibly bound RBCs, indicating that F was activated, but fusion was blocked at a post-triggering step. By contrast, the FIP-HRC425,31 peptide inhibitor prevented nearly all F-mediated anchoring, consistent with inhibition of F activation. MAbs 77.1 and H8 blocked fusion in the same manner as HRC4, with most RBCs irreversibly bound, confirming that these two mAbs permit (77.1) or promote (H8) the F extension and allow fusion peptide insertion into the target cell, but block completion of the F transition. The profile observed in the presence of C6 at the tested concentration (200 nM) was statistically indistinguishable from that of mock-treated samples, consistent with their lack of efficacy. In contrast, mAb Y10F behaves similarly to FIP-HRC431, preventing the initiation of F activation. A dose-response version of this assay, with 77.1 and Y10F, confirmed that these antibodies act through distinct mechanisms: at higher concentrations, 77.1 drives cells into an irreversibly bound state with F trapped in an intermediate conformation, whereas Y10F drives cells into a reversibly bound state with F remaining untriggered (Fig. 6C).

Viral evolution in the presence of the mAbs 77.1 and Y10F

We first evaluated the potential for viral escape from mAb 77.1 by subjecting MeV to increasing concentrations of the antibody over five serial passages. Viral replication ceased after the fifth cycle, likely due to the accumulation of deleterious mutations. Nevertheless, viral populations from cycles four and five were harvested and subjected to deep sequencing to identify potential resistance-associated genetic changes. Remarkably, despite prolonged selective pressure from mAb 77.1, we detected no significant point mutations with allele frequencies ≥10% or sequencing depths ≥20.

We next repeated the viral evolution experiment with both 77.1 and Y10F (since these two antibodies were the most potent in vivo, as shown in Fig. 5E), using a different strategy. Instead of progressively increasing antibody concentrations, viruses were passaged for over 50 days in alternating concentrations of mAbs to permit ongoing replication (see “Methods”). This approach yielded eight distinct viral populations: four selected in the presence of 77.1 and four in the presence of Y10F. Three of the four populations grown with 77.1 showed no mutations in the F protein (Supplementary Fig. 6), while one population contained a single amino acid substitution (M46V) (Fig. 7A). In contrast, all four populations selected with Y10F acquired one or more amino acid substitutions in F (Fig. 7A). Among these, only the F375S variant reached complete fixation (100% allele frequency), whereas the others displayed partial penetrance. Nevertheless, all Y10F-selected populations were resistant to Y10F inhibitory activity but retained sensitivity to 77.1, albeit with reduced potency compared to the parental virus (Supplementary Fig. 7). Similarly, M46V was also resistant to Y10F but retained sensitivity to 77.1, even though it was selected in the presence of 77.1. None of these mutations (Fig. 7B) mapped directly to the binding interface defined in Figs. 3 and 4. Discussion

Measles vaccines are highly effective but cannot be given to infants below the age of 9 months, or to immunocompromised or pregnant individuals, leaving them dependent on herd immunity1,32,33. Population immunity to measles is fragile due to suboptimal vaccine coverage and the extreme transmissibility of MeV (R₀ = 12-18), driving resurgences in the U.S. and worldwide. MeV still causes 10-20 million infections and >200,000 deaths annually, with poor outcomes compounded by the post-measles “immune amnesia” that erodes immunity to other pathogens1–6,34–37. No licensed antivirals exist, and current post-exposure prophylaxis with immune sera shows declining potency7–15. While vaccine-induced neutralizing antibodies primarily target H, natural infection elicits potent antibodies against both the H and F proteins, highlighting F as a promising therapeutic target23,24,38–40. Two recent preprint manuscripts from our group further characterize the human antibody response to MeV41,42. In the first, we show that anti-F protein antibodies are found predominantly in older individuals, suggesting that wild-type infection elicits a stronger humoral response to the F protein than vaccination, which is consistent with prior observations in the literature23,24. The second preprint examines the neutralizing activity of anti-F and anti-H antibodies isolated from human PBMCs. Notably, in that study, the most potent neutralizing antibodies target the F protein, supporting our conclusion that anti-F antibodies can be highly effective in vivo.

In the current study, we investigated the neutralizing activity of chimeric mAbs targeting the MeV F protein. These mAbs were generated in mice, with their variable (Fab) regions grafted onto human constant (Fc) domains. One mAb, 77.1, which was previously described25, binds an epitope present in both the prefusion and extended conformations of the F protein and arrests the F conformational transition at that intermediate step. Here, we evaluated its potential for use in combination therapies and its barrier to viral resistance. A second mAb, Y10F, has previously been shown to possess neutralizing activity43,44, but its sequence, epitope specificity, and mechanism of action were unknown. The other two mAbs, H8 and C6, also with unknown epitope structures, were selected for their distinct binding properties: H8 recognizes both pre- and post-triggered forms of F, whereas C6 binds only a post-triggered epitope.

The three neutralizing mAbs (Y10F, 77.1, and H8), which target three distinct sites, collectively demonstrate that the MeV F protein contains multiple vulnerable epitopes that can be effectively targeted. Y10F binds an apical prefusion epitope, stabilizes the prefusion conformation, and neutralizes infection both in vitro and in vivo. However, for Y10F, ex vivo efficacy is reduced and viral escape can arise through allosteric mutations outside the binding interface. 77.1 recognizes F in the prefusion state but stays bound after triggering and inhibits fusion at a late stage (as we have previously shown25). 77.1 blocks MeV infection with high potency in authentic human tissue ex vivo, remains efficacious in vivo even when administered 24 h post-infection, and displays a high barrier to resistance. H8 engages epitopes accessible in both the pre- and post-triggered conformations and, uniquely, promotes F activation upon binding, revealing a novel mode of antibody-mediated fusion regulation. Interestingly, H8 binds the 396-405 loop, a region critical for the H-F interaction and subsequent fusion triggering. In particular, this region includes residue Leu-394 (L394), which is notable as a known L394S mutation results in reduced fusion function and a 35-50% decrease in H-protein interaction, even though the protein itself is fully processed45. While EM revealed H8-bound postfusion structures, RBC assays indicate that H8 arrests the process at a refolding step. We attribute this apparent discrepancy to the soluble ectodomain’s lack of transmembrane constraints, which allows it to complete refolding transitions that are energetically or sterically restricted in the membrane-embedded, full-length protein. Thus, combining these structural insights with our cell-based triggering, time-resolved SEC, and negative-stain data, we hypothesize that H8 may bind this signal-receiving site, mimic the H protein, and activate F through a similar mechanism. In addition, H8 binding to F might influence the H-F interaction, thereby disrupting the glycoprotein complex on the surface. This binding profile of H8 is similar to that of the 1H1 antibody, which targets the Nipah virus F protein at an analogous epitope on the outer side of domain II46. Like H8, 1H1 is also a neutralizing antibody and recognizes both the prefusion and postfusion states of the F protein. However, no data are currently available to support the 1H1 F triggering mechanism. This confirms that the premature activation induced by H8 represents a unique mechanism among this class of antibodies. In addition, H8 neutralizes a broad spectrum of MeV strains, including hyperfusogenic variants from neuropathogenic isolates28 and blocks fusion mediated by all the F variants obtained from the viral evolution with Y10F and 77.1 (Supplementary Fig. 8). By contrast, C6, which recognizes only a post-triggered epitope in F (Supplementary Fig. 1), failed to block viral entry or fusion, underscoring the importance of antibodies that target pre- and intermediate conformations.

Collectively, these findings establish the MeV F glycoprotein as a rich and underexplored source of neutralizing epitopes. A cocktail of long-acting, humanized anti-F antibodies, such as Y10F and 77.1, could provide durable protection with a high barrier to resistance, complement vaccination, and address a critical therapeutic gap for MeV-exposed seronegative health care workers, and to individuals who are unvaccinated, vaccine-ineligible (either due to early age or medical conditions), or have waning immunity1–6,23,24,38–40.

Inbred cotton rats (Sigmodon hispidus) were purchased from Inotiv (formerly Envigo; West Lafayette, IN) and maintained in a University Laboratory Animal Resources barrier facility at The Ohio State University. Cotton rats were housed in NexGen Rat 900 polysulfone microisolator cages (Allentown Inc., Allentown, NJ) with 12:12 h light cycles, 22 ± 2 °C, and 20–70% relative humidity. Age ranges were 5-7 weeks. Both sexes were used in all experimental groups.

Plasmids

The genes of MeV genotype B3, D8, or A (vaccine) H and F proteins, and the cellular receptor hCD150 were sub-cloned into the mammalian expression vector pCAGGS. The identification codes of the different F proteins are: B3-F (GS63325-1), D8-F (GS77420-3), Moraten-F (GS55588-1), L454W-F (GS55608), and E455G-F (GS74446-1). A codon-optimized F protein sequence derived from the Measles virus strain Ichinose-B95a (Taxonomy ID: 645098) was synthesized by GeneScript and subcloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector. Stabilizing point mutations (E170G and E455G) previously characterized in SSPE and MIBE Measles strains27,28,47 were sequentially introduced using the Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit. The ectodomain construct was generated by removing the transmembrane region and cytoplasmic tail residues (amino acids 496-550) using the Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit. To generate Drosophila S2 stable cell lines, F protein ectodomain construct E170G/E455G was subcloned from the pcDNA vector to the pMT-Puro vector, including a C-terminal enterokinase cleavage site-twin-strep-tag via NEB HiFi DNA assembly.

Antibodies

MeV F-specific murine monoclonal antibodies 77.4 and Y10F were generated as described previously44. Chimeric antibody 77.1 has been functionally and structurally characterized25. The remaining two antibodies, H8 and C6, were derived from murine hybridomas obtained after immunization with stabilized MeV F41, cell-based ELISA was used to detect binding to pre- and postfusion state of the F protein; their Fab regions were subsequently sequenced and cloned into a human Fc backbone, as described for 77.125

Cells

HEK293T (human kidney epithelial), Vero (African green monkey kidney), Vero-hCD150 (SLAMF1)48 (kindly provided by Dr. Y. Yanagi) and Vero-hNectin-449 (kindly provided by Dr. C. Richardson) cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Life Technologies; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Life Technologies; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and antibiotics at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The Vero-hCD150 culture media were supplemented with 1 mg/mL Geneticin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Drosophila S2 cells were cultured in complete Schneider’s Drosophila Medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin) at 25 °C without CO2 or Lonza Insect Xpress Medium. ExpiCHO cells were maintained in ExpiCHO Expression Medium (Gibco) supplemented with 8 mM L-glutamine in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 8% CO2.

Viral rescue

HEK293T cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 on 6-well plates coated with poly-D-lysine. The transfection mixture included purified plasmid constructs coding for viral proteins N (GS58900-1), P (GS58943), and L (GS58944), all sourced from Epoch Life Science according to our design specifications. Additionally, purified plasmids for T7 polymerase (GS58929) and the genome for the MeV B3 mCherry wt virus bearing either the B3 H and F (GS69677-10), or the D8 H and F (GS77420-2), or the H and F from the Moraten vaccine strain (GS77420-1), or the B3 H and F-L454W (GS69677-1) were included. Cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in Opti-MEM medium. The medium was then replaced with warm DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After 1 h, cells were subjected to a heat shock treatment by incubating them at 42 °C in a water bath for 3 h. Subsequently, the cells were returned to a 37 °C incubator for 48 h. After this incubation, the HEK293T cells were detached and transferred to Vero-hCD150 cells in a 6-well plate and incubated at 32 °C for 5 days. RNA from the virus sample was then sequenced, and the virus stock was used for subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments (see25).

In vitro viral entry

Vero-hCD150 cells were plated in 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well). The following day, cells were treated with serial dilutions of the indicated antibodies. Cells were then infected with the indicated viruses for 3 h at 37 °C (200–300 pfu/well). After 3 h, the medium was replaced with medium containing 2% of methylcellulose 2 × complete medium (1:1). After 24 h, fluorescent/infected cells were counted using a Cytation 5 (BioTek) and analyzed with Gen5 3.11 software (min object size 25 µm, max object size 135 µm, threshold 3000). The samples were tested in three separate experiments performed with technical duplicates in five-fold dilutions in 96-well plates. Data obtained from each plate was analyzed in OriginPro 2023, where all biological and technical replicates were fitted with a logistic regression function with shared IC50 and Hill slope values, and A1 and A0 (highest and lowest signals) were used to normalize the data. Based on the normalized data, the percent of inhibition was calculated using the following equation: [1 - X]*100, where X is the fraction of the normalized signal.

Beta-Galactosidase (Beta-Gal) complementation-based fusion assay

The Beta-Gal complementation-based fusion assay was performed as previously described31. In brief, HEK293T cells transiently transfected with the hCD150 receptor and expressing the ω subunit of β-galactosidase were co-cultured with cells co-expressing the MeV glycoproteins F and H together with the α subunit of β-galactosidase. Cell fusion results in α-ω complementation, which was measured in the presence or absence of inhibitory compounds. After 18 h, cells were lysed to terminate the fusion process, and fusion levels were quantified using the Tropix Galacto-Star™ chemiluminescent reporter assay system (Applied Biosystems) and read on a Tecan M1000 microplate reader. Biological replicates were recorded and globally fitted with a logistic function in OriginPro 2023. The obtained parameters were used to normalize the data per sample and plotted with standard error calculated from all data points.

Cell surface staining with conformation-specific anti-F mAbs

HEK293T cells transiently transfected with viral glycoprotein constructs were incubated overnight at 37 °C in complete medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS). The following day, cells were shifted to indicated temperatures and incubation times (as shown in the figures). To detect specific MeV F conformations, cells were incubated on ice for 1 h with chimeric monoclonal antibodies, followed by a 1 h incubation on ice with Alexa Fluor 594 or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-human secondary antibody (1:1000; Life Technologies). Cells were then fixed on ice with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and counterstained with DAPI (1:1000; Thermo Fisher). Imaging was performed using a Cytation 5 system, and relative fluorescence of antibody-bound cells was quantified with BioTek Gen5 software.

Cell surface staining with conformation-specific anti-F C6 mAb: competition between H8 and Y10F mAbs

HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with viral glycoprotein constructs (either wt B3 or B3 E455G F) and incubated overnight at 37 °C in complete medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS: CM). The following day, cells were brought to room temperature, and 50 µl of 2× Y10F (final concentrations: 200, 20, or 2 nM in CM) was added for 30 min. Subsequently, 50 µl of 2× H8 was added to achieve final concentrations of 100, 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, 3.125, or 1.5625 nM in CM, resulting in diluted Y10F concentrations of 100, 10, and 1 nM, respectively. Cells were then transferred to 37 °C for 60 min. Following incubation, cells were placed at 4 °C, the supernatant was removed, and 50 µl of murine C6 mAb 1:500 dilution from an 8 mg/ml stock in CM) was added for 60 min. Cells were washed three times with CM and incubated on ice for 30 min with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:500; Life Technologies). Cells were then fixed on ice with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and counterstained with DAPI (1:1000; Thermo Fisher). Imaging was performed using a Cytation 5 system, and C6-positive cells were quantified using BioTek Gen5 software.

Virus neutralization assay in cell lines

77.1, Y10F, and H8, were serially diluted two-fold, starting at 100 nM, and incubated with 60 TCID50 rMeV-KS-Venus(3)50 (kindly provided by Dr. W.P. Duprex) per well. Following a 1 h incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, 1×104 cells per well were added (Vero-hCD150, Vero-Nectin4, or Vero). Plates were incubated for 7 days and infection was monitored by Venus fluorescence. After 7 days, plates were imaged using an Amersham Typhoon scanner under predefined settings: channel Cy2 (GFP,488), pixel size 10 μm, scan speed normal, and PMT auto. The assay was performed in quadruplicate. In the Vero-hCD150 assays, wells were scored as positive or negative based on the presence or absence of Venus (endpoint titration), whereas in the Vero-hNectin4 assay the Venus positive surface area was calculated with Fiji software. Wells were scored positive if the infection covered >10%. For virus neutralization assay in B-LCLs. mAbs dilutions were prepared as described above and incubated with 106 TCID50 rMeV-KS-Venus(3) per well. Following a 1 h incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, 1×105 B-LCLs per well were added and incubated for 7 days. Plates were imaged using an Amersham Typhoon scanner under predefined settings: channel Cy2 (GFP,488), pixel size 10 μm, scan speed normal, and PMT auto. The assay was performed in quadruplicate, and wells were scored as positive or negative for infection based on the presence or absence of Venus (endpoint titration).

MeV dissemination assay in B-LCLs

A previously established MeV dissemination assay was used to assess the potential of the mAbs to prevent MeV dissemination51. Briefly, B-LCLs were infected with rMeV-KS-Venus(3) for 72 h. Infection rates in live cells were quantified by flow cytometry (BD FACS Lyric), based on Venus expression. Next, 150 MeV-infected B-LCLs were co-cultured with 6 × 104 uninfected B-LCLs per well in the presence of a two-fold dilution series of mAbs starting at 100 nM. MeV dissemination was determined 48 h post co-culture by measuring the percentage of Venus-positive cells by flow cytometry.

Virus neutralization assay in Airway organoids at air-liquid interface (AO at ALI)

AO differentiated at ALI for >6 weeks, exhibiting ciliation and mucus production, were generated as previously described52. Prior to infection, AO at ALI were rinsed with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺-containing DPBS to remove mucus and scratched in a cross-pattern to expose Nectin-4 and facilitate viral entry. Monoclonal antibodies (1000, 100, and 10 nM) were pre-incubated with rMeV-KS-Venus(3) (MOI = 0.1) for 1 h prior to application to the apical surface. After 2 h, the inoculum was removed to restore ALI conditions. Organoids were imaged using the Typhoon scanner at 3 days post-infection, and AO at ALI were semi-quantitatively scored for MeV infection.

Viral evolution assay

Selection of Measles Virus Escape Mutants in the Presence of Monoclonal Antibodies

Cell culture and virus infection

Phase 1: cell culture and virus infection

Vero-hCD150 cells were seeded in two 96-well plates and infected with 100 plaque-forming units (PFUs) per well of recombinant Measles virus (MeV) B3 wild-type expressing mCherry. Infections were carried out in Opti-MEM (Gibco, 31985070) supplemented with 10% penicillin-streptomycin. After 2 h, the infection medium was replaced with serially diluted concentrations of monoclonal antibodies Y10F and 77.1, starting at 10 μg/mL and diluted 1:2 down to 0.04 μg/mL.

Phase 2: passaging under antibody pressure

On day 10 post-infection, wells showing evidence of ongoing viral replication in the presence of highest (i.e., 10 μg/mL) antibody concentration (1-77.1: B1 and G1; 1-Y10F: F1 and E1) were selected. The contents of each well were transferred to T75 flasks containing Vero-hCD150 cells in Opti-MEM supplemented with 10% penicillin-streptomycin and 10 μg/mL of the corresponding antibody.

Due to limited infection after 3 days, additional wells from the antibody selection plates were chosen and expanded: wells 2-77.1 (A3, C3, E3) and 2-Y10F (B2, D2, F2) were transferred to T75 flasks and cultured in Opti-MEM with 10% pen-strep, containing reduced antibody concentrations (1.25 μg/mL for 77.1; 2.5 μg/mL for Y10F).

On day 15, the medium from the initial T75 flasks (i.e., 1-77.1: B1 and G1; 1-Y10F: F1 and E1) was replaced with Opti-MEM + 10% pen-strep without antibody. Virus supernatants were collected and frozen at −80 °C on day 16. Antibody concentrations in the second batch flasks were further reduced on day 17 due to a lack of visible infection (0.625 μg/mL for 77.1; 1.25 μg/mL for Y10F). Viral supernatants from these cultures were collected on days 23 and 28 (2-Y10F: B2, D2, F2; 2-77.1: A3, C3, E3), frozen, and subsequently used for further passages.

Sequential Antibody Reduction and Virus Harvesting

Phase 3: On day 28, all remaining samples were subsequently used to infect T75 flasks of Vero-SLAM cells at 10-fold lower antibody concentrations (0.125 μg/mL for 2-Y10F, 0.0625 μg/mL for 2-77.1, and 0.5 μg/mL for first-round isolates). Viruses were all harvested in the following 10-12 days.

Phase 4: On day 39, these eight virus stocks were then used to infect new T75 flasks of Vero-hCD150 cells. After three h, antibody was reintroduced at two-fold lower concentrations (0.0625 μg/mL for 2-Y10F, 0.0313 μg/mL for 2-77.1, and 0.25 μg/mL for 1-Y10F and 1-77.1).

Phase 5: On day 54, each virus was collected, titered, and sequenced.

In vivo experiments: cotton rats

Inbred cotton rats (Sigmodon hispidus) were purchased from Envigo, Inc., Indianapolis. Both male and female cotton rats aged 5 to 7 weeks were used. For i.n. infection, 105 TCID50 of MeV (strain B3 wt EGFP) was inoculated intranasally to isoflurane-anesthetized cotton rats in a volume of 100 µL. Animals received either the indicated amount of the indicated antibodies, purified human IgG (Carimune, CSL Behring, measles neutralizing titer of 640 at 60 mg/mL), or vehicle 14 h prior to infection or 24 h post-infection. Four days after infection, the animals were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, and their lungs were collected and weighed. Lung tissue was homogenized with a glass Dounce homogenizer. Serial 10-fold dilutions of supernatant fluids were assessed on Vero-hCD150 cells for the presence of infectious virus in 48-well plates using cytopathic effect (CPE) as the endpoint. Plates were scored for CPE microscopically after 7 days, and the tissue culture infectious dose 50 (TCID50) was calculated. The results are presented as TCID50/g of lung tissue.

RBC fusion assay

RBC fusion assays were performed using HEK293T cells transiently expressing the MeV H_Y17H-HPIV3_T193A chimera and MeV F (T461I). Cell monolayers were washed three times with serum-free medium and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min in CO₂-independent medium containing 1% RBCs. Cells were then exposed to varying concentrations of inhibitors and shifted to 37 °C for 60 min.

To quantify RBC binding and fusion, zanamivir was added at a final concentration of 10 mM, and the cells were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min with gentle agitation. The supernatant was collected into V-bottom 96-well plates to measure reversibly bound RBCs. The cells were subsequently incubated at 4 °C for 30 min in ACK lysing buffer, and the supernatant was collected into V-bottom 96-well plates to quantify irreversibly bound RBCs. Finally, the remaining cells were lysed in lysis buffer and transferred to flat-bottom 96-well plates to determine fused RBCs. Absorbance at 410 nm was measured using a Tecan My1000PRO microplate reader to quantify RBCs in each category. Assays were performed in CO2-independent media at pH 7.2 for the bar graphs in Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 9, and at pH 7.6 for the dose response curves in Fig. 6C.

FECTO purification

FECTO was purified as previously described from Drosophila S2 cells25. Protein concentration was estimated using the molar extinction coefficient at 280 nm (48,820 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹).

Antibody expression

Monoclonal antibodies were produced in ExpiCHO cells using the manufacturer’s high-titer transfection protocol with pTRIOZ-mAb plasmids encoding the antibody heavy and light chains. After seven days, cells were removed by centrifugation at 4000 × g, and the clarified medium was adjusted to pH 8.0 with 1 M HEPES (pH 8.0). Antibodies were captured by incubating the medium overnight at 4 °C with protein A agarose. The next day, beads were washed with 20 mL HBS, and bound antibody was eluted with 15 mL 0.2 M glycine (pH 2.0). The eluate was immediately neutralized by adding 10% (v/v) 1 M HEPES (pH 8.0).

For the Fab generation, purified IgG at 1 mg/mL was digested with 2% (w/v) cysteine-activated papain for 4 h at 37 °C in a water bath. The reaction was quenched by adding 0.5 M iodoacetamide to a final concentration of 50 mM. The mixture was diluted tenfold with 10 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) to reduce ionic strength and loaded onto a 1 mL Mono S column pre-equilibrated with 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0). Fab fragments were collected in the flow-through, whereas Fc fragments were eluted in a single step with 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) containing 2 M NaCl. Isolated Fabs were concentrated, buffer-exchanged into HBS, and stored at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined from A280 using the calculated molar extinction coefficients.

Time-resolved size-exclusion chromatography

FECTO was mixed with H8 Fab at a 1:1 molar ratio (monomer: Fab). The mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 15, 90, 180, or 1440 minutes. At each time point, 100 μL of the reaction was injected onto a Cytiva Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column. The column was pre-equilibrated with HBS buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl. Absorbance at 280 nm was recorded throughout the elution. Chromatograms were exported to OriginPro 2025 and baseline-corrected using the spline method. Corrected traces were used to calculate the trapezoidal area between 7 and 10 mL elution volume in Python. This region includes the column void volume and contains FECTO-H8 species associated with triggering. The resulting area values were plotted as a function of incubation time. Data were fitted with a single-exponential function in OriginPro 2025 to estimate kinetic parameters.

Time-resolved negative-stain electron microscopy

Complexes for negative-stain analysis were prepared in the same way as for size-exclusion chromatography. Aliquots were removed from the reaction after 0, 15, 60, 150, 240, and 1140 minutes. Each aliquot was immediately applied to a grid and processed for negative staining. For every time point, approximately 176 movies were collected and processed in cryoSPARC version 4.7.1. Patch motion correction and CTF estimation were performed using default settings. Particles were selected using the blob picker with a diameter range of 120-250 Å. A single round of two-dimensional classification was run with 100 classes and 500 particles per class. The protocol consisted of 30 online iterations, followed by two full iterations. Class occupancy was calculated from the number of particles assigned to each selected class. Class 5 was excluded from the final analysis because it contained heterogeneous particles such as H8 Fab bound to F fragments, Fab alone, and other mixed species. The final class occupancy values were plotted over time using Python, Matplotlib, and Seaborn.

Grid preparation

Negative stain

Negative-stain electron microscopy was performed using 400-mesh copper grids coated with a continuous carbon film (CF400-Cu; Electron Microscopy Sciences). Prior to sample application, the grids were rendered hydrophilic by glow-discharging them in an easyGlow Glow Discharge Cleaning System (Pelco) for 15 s at 15 mA. Protein samples were diluted to an absorbance of 0.01 at 280 nm; 3 µL of this solution was then deposited onto the grid and incubated for 1 minute. The excess sample was removed by blotting, and the grid was washed three times with 3 μL of deionized water, with blotting performed between each wash. Negative staining was performed by applying 3 µL of a 1% uranyl acetate solution to the grid, blotting twice, then adding an additional drop of stain and incubating for one minute before a final blot to remove excess stain. The grids were allowed to air-dry prior to imaging.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

Complex stoichiometries were guided by NS-EM. Except where noted, complexes were incubated with a twofold molar excess of Fab relative to the number of F binding sites. Accordingly, samples were prepared at trimer:Fab ratios of 1:2 for FECTO:Y10F (one F trimer to two Y10F Fabs) and 1:2:6 for FECTO:Y10F:77.1 (twofold excess of 77.1). For H8, Fab was added at a 1:1 ratio per F binding site (1:3 trimer:H8), because this complex was not purified before imaging.

FECTO:Y10F and FECTO:Y10F:77.1 complexes were purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) before grid preparation. The F:H8 complex was applied directly to grids after a 15-minute incubation to minimize postfusion conformational transition that could occur during SEC. Data were collected on graphene oxide (GO) grids as described previously25. All FECTO-Fab complexes were recorded on Quantifoil R2/1 or R2/2 grids covered with GO grids prepared as described previously25. Protein with 0.25-35 absorbance values at 280 nm was placed on grids and blotted for 2-3 s. Grids were loaded to the FEI Titan Krios G3 autoloader and screened for ice quality, graphene oxide layer quality, and particle distribution. Movies were recorded with an exposure rate of 70 e/Å², with 50 frames per movie saved as non-gain normalized LWZ TIFF files. Collection statistics per dataset are reported in Table S1.

Cryo-EM data processing

All datasets were processed in cryoSPARC v4.5 or v4.6 using a standard workflow with dataset-specific adjustments during late refinement. Movies were corrected for beam-induced motion with Patch Motion, and defocus values were estimated with Patch CTF in cryoSPARC Live. Particles were initially picked with the blob picker using an intentionally broad particle diameter (80-120 Å) to over-pick FECTO-Fab complexes. Particles were first extracted and Fourier-cropped to 64 pixels to speed up early processing.

Particle stacks were extensively cleaned by 2D classification following the Deep2D protocol described previously25, and class averages showing well-defined protein features were retained. Two independent ab initio reconstructions were run per dataset, one imposing C1 and one C3 symmetry, each with five classes; for the Y10F dataset, only C1 was used. The best ab initio models were then used for heterogeneous refinement against all classes, iterating until discrete 3D classes emerged. The best-looking class or classes were pooled and subjected to non-uniform refinement (NUR), after which particles were re-extracted with larger Fourier-cropped boxes (160-256 pixels) for further NUR and additional 3D classification to improve compositional and conformational homogeneity.

For final map generation, only particles contributing to the best-resolved class were retained and re-extracted with a Fourier crop chosen so that the 0.143 FSC resolution fell within approximately three-quarters of the box frequency (to avoid box-size-limited resolution). Final refinements included NUR, symmetry expansion where appropriate, focused/local refinement with masks, and targeted 3D classification, followed by a final round of local refinement. The final particle set was subjected to reference-based motion correction and both global and local CTF refinement, after which a final local refinement produced the maps reported.

Model building

Model building proceeded by rigid-body fitting of the previously reported FECTO structure 25 (PDB 8UUP) into each cryo-EM map. Antibody Fab fragments were modeled with AlphaFold2 and docked into the density using UCSF ChimeraX53,54. The combined FECTO-antibody models were then iteratively adjusted manually in Coot55, followed by cycles of real-space refinement in PHENIX56 and additional manual corrections in Coot.

Interface interactions determination

Interface contacts, buried surface area, and estimated binding energy were calculated with PISA (CCP4 suite;57). Interaction counts are reported as unique contacts from all symmetry-related interactions.

Formation of FECTO-Fab Complexes for Determining Hydrodynamic Radius and Differential Scanning Fluorimetry

FECTO (FECTO E170G, E455G, residues 1-495) was mixed to a final concentration of ~1.7 µM (trimer) and incubated with Fab at room temperature for 15-30 min. Fab was added at a threefold molar excess for 77.1, H8, and C6, and at a onefold molar excess for Y10F (ratios calculated relative to the FECTO trimer). The resulting mixtures were used directly for downstream analyses.

nanoDSF and DLS measurements

Thermal unfolding of FECTO with or without Fab, and of Fab alone, was measured on a NanoTemper Prometheus Panta. FECTO-Fab complexes were assembled 30 min before acquisition and incubated at room temperature. For each experiment, three capillaries contained the FECTO-Fab complex, two capillaries contained the corresponding Fab alone, and one capillary contained HBS as a buffer control. Data were collected with a 1 °C/min temperature ramp and 100% excitation laser power. Unfolding profiles are reported as the first derivative of the intrinsic fluorescence at 330 nm. Dynamic light scattering was recorded in parallel, and the cumulant hydrodynamic radius was calculated and plotted as a function of temperature.

Next generation sequencing and variant calling

Viral whole-genome sequencing of MeV samples was performed using a metagenomic next-generation sequencing approach and a previously described protocol58. Libraries were sequenced on NextSeq 2000 with 2x150bp or 1x100bp read format. Raw reads were trimmed and quality filtered with fastp (v0.23.4)58 with the parameters --cut_mean_quality 20 --cut_front --cut_tail --length_required 20 --low_complexity_filter --trim_poly_g --trim_poly_x. For variant calling, filtered reads were processed with the RAVA workflow (default parameters) using the MeV plasmid map as a reference (https://github.com/greninger-lab/RAVA_Pipeline/tree/2025-08-18_DZ_MP)59, (10.1101/2019.12.17.879320). Raw sequencing data are publicly available in NCBI BioProject PRJNA1307578.

Statistics & reproducibility

Data analysis and visualization were performed in Python using NumPy, pandas, SciPy, Matplotlib, seaborn, and statannotations. Unless stated otherwise, group comparisons used two-sided Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon tests. When multiple pairwise comparisons were made within a figure panel, p-values were adjusted with the Holm-Bonferroni method to control the family-wise error rate. Exact p values and test statistics (U) are reported in the figure legends. P-value annotations on plots follow this legend: ns, 5.00 × 10⁻² <p ≤ 1.00 × 10⁰; *, 1.00×10⁻² <p ≤ 5.00 × 10⁻²; **, 1.00 × 10⁻³ <p ≤ 1.00 × 10⁻²; ***, 1.00 × 10⁻⁴ <p ≤ 1.00 × 10⁻³; ****, p ≤ 1.00 × 10⁻⁴.

In animal experiments, individual cotton rats represented distinct biological replicates (per group). This sample size was selected based on historical data from this infection model to achieve adequate statistical power for a non-parametric analysis (Mann-Whitney U test) without exceeding the necessary number of animals.

Phenomenex Jupiter C4 LC column 300 Å (150 × 21.2 mm, particle size 5 μm). MALDI-TOF analysis was performed on a Bruker UltrafleXtreme MALDI-TOF instrument.
