Harm reduction among refugees and displaced populations in Lebanon: a systematic review
Authors: Zeinab Abbas, Aline Hajj, Hala Sacre, Samah Tawil, Marwan Akel, Pascale Salameh, Michele Cherfane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the comprehensiveness of existing policies, strategies, and research regarding harm reduction services for refugees and displaced populations in Lebanon, evaluating service availability, access barriers, and policy gaps to inform targeted and evidence-based interventions and improve health outcomes for this vulnerable group.MethodA systematic review was conducted to examine substance use and harm reduction among refugees in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO. PubMed, EMBASE, and Google Scholar databases were searched using free-text words and MeSH terms, including substance use, drug use, harm reduction, hepatitis C virus (HCV) screening, hepatitis B virus (HBV) screening, refugees, treatment access, and displaced population. Grey literature from the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health, World Health Organization, and other global reports was reviewed separately and included in the PRISMA flowchart, alongside peer-reviewed studies. The risk for bias in included research papers was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tools.ResultsA total of 387 papers (369 peer-reviewed studies and 18 grey literature reports on harm reduction and substance use) were retrieved, and 29 were included in the review after screening and full-text evaluation (17peer reviewed articles and 12 grey literature reports). The results revealed a lack of structured harm reduction policies specifically targeting refugees. The identified barriers include legal restrictions, stigma, and poor integration of harm reduction services into healthcare programs. Of the 12 grey literature documents, only four mentioned harm reduction provision, and none offered a comprehensive strategy to enhance access for this vulnerable group. Many studies were general and lacked focused insights into substance use or harm reduction in refugee settings.ConclusionThe findings suggest that current policies require adaptation, as they inadequately address the specific vulnerabilities and barriers faced by refugee populations. Priority actions include adapting policies to refugee contexts, expanding mobile harm reduction clinics to improve accessibility, and integrating HCV screening into refugee healthcare services. Further targeted research is urgently required to characterize substance use patterns among refugees and develop evidence-informed interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12954-026-01453-7.Keywords: Substance use, Harm reduction, HCV, Treatment access, Refugee, Displaced population

Background

Harm reduction is an approach aimed at preventing overdose and infectious disease transmission while improving the physical, mental, and social well-being of substance users [1]. This strategy has demonstrated effectiveness in addressing the public health crises associated with substance use, infectious diseases, and drug-related harms. In the late 1980s, Western governments began adopting “harm reduction” (HR) policies, focusing on mitigating the negative consequences of drug use rather than aiming to reduce its prevalence [2]. Within a decade, countries that adopted HR as their primary policy successfully controlled the injection-related transmission of blood-borne infections, such as HCV and HBV [2, 3].

In the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, drug policies have taken markedly different paths. In many MENA societies, substance users were labeled as “criminals,” and the hierarchical structure of public health institutions inhibited the adoption of HR policies, which are more effectively implemented by institutions with flexible structures [4]. Flexible structures are frameworks that enable adaptability to changing conditions and can more effectively implement HR initiatives by allowing for rapid adjustments to strategies as needed. Yet, only a few countries in the MENA region adopted HR policies, and many approved them on a limited scale and with limited coverage. Of the 20 countries in the MENA region, three have successfully implemented an effective combination of services, including opioid substitution treatment (OST) and needle syringe exchange program (NSP) [5]. These countries are Iran (2000), Morocco (2010), and Afghanistan (2010). Other countries adopted one of the two HR approaches: six countries have implemented NSP, and two countries have implemented OST [6].

Globally, there are approximately 272 million migrants [7], including nearly 26 million refugees [8]. Migrants and refugees living under harsh conditions in many MENA regions are particularly vulnerable to mental health problems and substance use disorders, often exacerbated by service disruptions related to their status in each country [9]. Lebanon ranks seventh among countries hosting refugees, having experienced a massive influx primarily due to the war in Syria [8]. With a ratio of 156 displaced persons per 1000 inhabitants, Lebanon hosts over 1.5 million displaced Syrians, representing 25% of its population [10]. Despite the concerted efforts of the Ministry of Public Health (MOPH) and international organizations, displaced persons, both Syrians and Palestinians, still face numerous challenges in accessing primary care services [11, 12].

In June 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) and the French National Agency for HIV, Viral Hepatitis, and Emerging Infectious Diseases (ANRS|MIE) signed a memorandum of understanding to support and strengthen their collaboration in research and knowledge sharing to improve global health policy development and implementation in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). This partnership aims to enhance scientific and technical cooperation between the two institutions in the area of HIV, hepatitis, and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in LMCIs [13].

Similarly, the WHO’s global health sector strategies for HIV, viral hepatitis, and STIs for the period 2022–2030 emphasize the importance of people-centered health services. These strategies call for integrating service delivery elements in new ways, ensuring that people receive a continuum of coordinated health services across different levels and sites of care, within and beyond the health sector, according to their needs throughout their lives [13]. However, eliminating HCV and HBV cannot be achieved without focusing on harm reduction services among vulnerable groups, such as refugees, living and interacting with the Lebanese population. Keeping in mind that the right to medical care and treatment is a critical element of the right to health, and, like all other rights, it belongs to everyone, including refugees. This principle aligns with the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3, “Good health and well-being,” which emphasizes the importance of ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being for all at all ages [14].

Conversely, Lebanon reaffirms its refusal to accept any form of asylum request and reserves the right to review the legal status of the Syrian refugees on its territory at any time. This stance can significantly affect many aspects of the refugees’ quality of life and their access to treatment. Consequently, harm reduction efforts targeting this vulnerable group may be compromised, leading to increased transmission of infectious diseases and a higher risk of overdose deaths. Therefore, this systematic review sought to evaluate existing policies, strategies, reports, and published research to determine whether there is an adequate focus on harm reduction services among refugees and displaced populations and identify gaps and challenges impacting refugees’ and displaced populations’ access to harm reduction services, including screening for HCV/HBV (HIV excluded due to low reported yield among substance users in Lebanon and treatment of communicable diseases among substance users. More specifically, this paper aimed to answer whether Lebanon has a policy or strategy focusing on refugees and their access to harm reduction services.

Methods

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [15]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO.

Search strategy

Three databases—PubMed, EMBASE, and Google Scholar—were systematically searched for qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-method studies involving substance use and harm reduction services among the refugee population in Lebanon (last searched May 2024). This search was updated in September 2025, but no additional results have been incorporated. The search strategy used a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, index terms, and free-text keywords, including substance use, drug use, harm reduction, HCV screening, HBV screening, refugees, treatment access for refugees, and displaced populations. These terms were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR) to maximize the retrieval of relevant literature (Supplementary Table 1). HCV and HBV screening were specifically included as search terms because substance use, particularly injection drug use, is associated with increased vulnerability to these blood-borne infections [16, 17], which remain highly prevalent among high-risk populations in Lebanon [18, 19].

Grey literature was systematically searched from 2010 to 2025, to capture Lebanon-specific reports on refugees/displaced populations (aligning with peer-reviewed date limits). The search strategy used a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, index terms, and free-text keywords, including substance use, drug use, harm reduction, HCV screening, HBV screening, refugees, treatment access for refugees, and displaced populations. No language limits were applied, but priority given to English/Arabic sources. Official national, regional, and international websites were also searched to comprehensively assess policy frameworks and strategies related to harm reduction and substance use involving Lebanon or the MENA region. Institutional websites encompassed the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health (MOPH) https://moph.gov.lb/, the Middle East & North Africa Harm Reduction Association (MENAHRA) (https://www.menahra.org/), the World Health Organization (WHO) (https://www.who.int/lebanon), the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) (https://www.unodc.org/), and the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) (https://www.unhcr.org/lb/) (last searched June 2024). Reports focusing solely on Lebanese nationals, excluding those involving refugees and displaced populations, or reports focusing on refugees outside the MENA region, were excluded after initial screening (5out of 23). This search was updated in September 2025, and two reports were added. These grey literature sources were included in the PRISMA flowchart alongside peer-reviewed studies and underwent the same systematic screening process, critical appraisal, and standardized inclusion/exclusion criteria. While grey literature is not peer-reviewed and not always publicly available online (some full documents required direct requests from institutions), it was evaluated using the same methodological approach as empirical research studies.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they (1) addressed substance use, harm reduction services, or treatment access among refugees or displaced populations in Lebanon; (2) were published in English or Arabic; and (3) were published between 2000 and 2025. Peer-reviewed research articles (all study designs), systematic reviews, policies, strategies, guidelines, and official reports were included. Conference abstracts, poster presentations, letters to editors, and commentaries were excluded. Studies were excluded if they did not specifically address refugee or displaced populations, were not conducted in or relevant to Lebanon, or lacked sufficient methodological detail for quality appraisal.

Study selection

Following database searches, all retrieved records were exported to Microsoft Excel, and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (ZA and MC) independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Studies deemed potentially eligible proceeded to full-text review, which was also conducted independently by the same two reviewers. Disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer (PS). When critical data were missing or unclear, corresponding authors were contacted via email with a two-week response timeframe.

Data extraction

Extracted information included study characteristics (first author, publication year, study design, and setting), population characteristics (sample size and demographic details), study objectives, assessment methods and outcomes, main findings, conclusions, and recommendations specifically related to factors affecting treatment access among refugees in Lebanon. Data extraction was performed by one reviewer and verified by a second reviewer to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias were evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools [20, 21], which provide validated instruments for diverse study designs. This included the revised JBI checklist for analytical cross-sectional studies, systematic reviews, and research syntheses, as well as the revised JBI checklist for quasi-experimental studies.​ For peer-reviewed research articles, additional tools, including JBI checklists for qualitative, RCTs, cohort/case-control, and prevalence studies, were used as appropriate [22, 23]. For empirical research papers, the assessed methodological domains included appropriateness of sample selection and recruitment, validity of exposure and outcome measurement methods, and adequacy of confounding control. Grey literature documents were critically appraised for credibility, relevance, and quality of information using the same criteria. Studies were classified as low, moderate, or high risk of bias through domain-based assessment of individual checklist items, culminating in an overall study-level judgment. Studies were excluded if they exhibited significant methodological limitations or a high risk of bias compromising internal validity and reliability. Studies with low or moderate risk of bias, where limitations did not substantially affect the validity of findings in assessed domains, were retained (Fig. 1). Inter-rater reliability was assessed through independent double screening and coding, followed by structured consensus procedures. Overall agreement between reviewers was high, with discrepancies identified for only two articles, which were initially rated as having a moderate risk of bias. Following review and adjudication, it was agreed that the identified methodological limitations were unlikely to materially influence the primary outcomes. These studies were therefore retained in the review, with their risk-of-bias assessments explicitly reported, to preserve the completeness of the evidence base.

Results

A total of 387 papers (369 studies and 18 official documents) were retrieved. After title and abstract screening, 68 records were retained for further evaluation, of which 64 proceeded to full-text assessment, and 29 were included in the final review including 17 peer reviewed articles and 12 grey literature documents (Fig. 1).

Availability of services and research

Peer-reviewed articles, including 12 studies [12, 36, 37, 39, 41–43, 46, 50–53], mainly addressed the availability of services and research. Overall, limited data exist on substance use and harm reduction among displaced populations. To date, only one quantitative study and one qualitative study have assessed substance use and harm reduction services among Palestinian and Syrian refugees in Lebanon [41, 42]. One study reported that engagement in drug-related activities has become normalized and legitimized over time due to the lack of law enforcement within the camps [41], and increased syringe sharing has been reported because of reduced access to injecting equipment [43].

Another study reported the high prevalence of exposure to unsafe injections and procedures among refugees and displaced populations in the MENA region, reflecting a lack of awareness about safe injection practices, unsterilized equipment, and inadequate health infrastructure. Frequent exposure to violence and conflict, often necessitating hospitalization and treatments such as blood transfusions or surgeries, further contributes to this issue [39]. It also noted that HBV prevalence among refugees in LMICs may differ from that in high-income countries due to several factors, such as the “healthy migrant effect,” limited access to health resources, a higher baseline prevalence of HBV in their country of origin, and ongoing exposure to risk factors.

Most studies on mental health provision among refugees in Lebanon focused on general mental health issues, without specific substance use or harm reduction guidelines [51–53].

A qualitative analysis was conducted in Lebanon, Indonesia, and Uganda to develop a consensus-based research agenda for mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) interventions in humanitarian settings for 2021–2030. It aimed to scale and sustain the quality of evidence-based MHPSS interventions across diverse humanitarian sectors [37]. Similarly, secondary data analysis assessed tools for detecting mental disorders and distress among refugees [46]. Both papers provided recommendations to improve access to treatment and scale up interventions among refugees but did not specifically address substance use or harm reduction.

The problem of substance use and the associated risk of blood-borne infections among Lebanese, refugees and displaced populations remains an underexplored area of public health [43, 50]. Poor awareness may increase the risk of blood-borne infections driven by a shift from non-injection to injection drug use [43]. Moreover, undetected HCV cases are highly probable, exacerbated by the lack of harm reduction services in these populations.

Despite some NGO support for Palestinian communities, harm reduction programs are mostly absent inside the camps, except for one treatment center in Bourj El Barajneh camp in Mount Lebanon [41, 42]. HBV, and HCV rates are increasing among refugee drug users, but treatment and screening access remain limited [12, 36]. The Lebanese government provides free HCV treatment only to citizens, leaving refugees and unregistered populations disproportionately without access [12].

Barriers to harm reduction service access

Barriers to service access were addressed by 11 peer-reviewed studies [11, 12, 38, 40, 41, 44, 45, 48, 49, 54, 55] and 5 grey literature reports [27, 28, 31, 33, 56]. Understanding the unique challenges faced by forcibly displaced populations is essential for preventing substance use and treating substance use disorders within humanitarian settings [28], especially given the limited focus on vulnerable groups such as refugees and immigrants [40]. Barriers to treatment and harm reduction among refugees and displaced populations include disrupted social fabrics, causing many Syrian families to become isolated from larger support structures and experience loss of identity [49, 54]. Overcrowding in camp settings can contribute to infectious diseases, especially the spread of sexually transmitted infections, such as syphilis, HIV, and hepatitis [45]. Primary drivers of HCV among key populations include injecting drug use, multiple and concurrent sexual partnerships, gender inequalities, violence, displacement, stigma, and discrimination [38].

Additionally, disease-reporting systems capture more complete data from the Lebanese population than refugees, as refugees often avoid medical care for fear of legal repercussions or deportation, resulting in low linkage to care after testing positive [31, 56]. A 2023 cross-sectional study on the epidemiology, distribution, and risk factors associated with hepatitis C in Lebanon identified barriers to successful hepatitis C elimination, including the absence of a comprehensive screening policy, stigma, neglect of high-risk groups, economic collapse, and inadequate care and surveillance within refugee populations [12]. Fear of legal consequences, data privacy concerns, and lack of appreciation were identified as factors limiting access to substance use treatment and harm reduction services. Similarly, transportation and financial constraints, along with healthcare providers’ bias, including stereotyping and racism, cause health inequalities [11].

The COVID-19 pandemic also exacerbated the situation by increasing injecting drug use and injecting equipment sharing due to shortages of safe injecting materials while disrupting services [28]. Stigma and power imbalances between refugees and providers further discourage treatment seeking [49].

Cost is a notable obstacle, as refugees do not benefit from the same programs available to Lebanese citizens. While Turkey offers free healthcare to all Syrian refugees, Lebanon limits it to UNHCR-registered individuals and those in camps, leaving many without coverage [41, 55]. Lack of low-cost generic HCV treatments further restricts access [41]. Several studies suggest that research efforts on refugees have primarily focused on addressing immediate traumatic reactions, with limited emphasis on recovery, resilience, or the prevention of long-term trauma manifestations. Reports reveal that government expenditure on mental health as a percentage of total health budgets in seven Arab countries is strikingly low, ranging from 2% in Syria and Egypt to 5% in Lebanon, compared to approximately 11% in Germany and the Netherlands [27].

Political instability, damaged infrastructure, and a struggling economy have considerably reduced healthcare availability and restricted access to healthcare services [44]. Mental health professionals are scarce and concentrated in the private sector, limiting access for refugees and low-income Lebanese [33, 45]. Estimates suggest that there are only 1.26 psychiatrists and 3.42 psychologists per 100,000 population, with 97% working in the private sector [33]. Additionally, cultural and religious stigma against injection drug use persists in conservative communities, further hindering care [42, 48].

Policy and strategic gaps

Policy and strategic gaps were mainly identified in grey literature documents, including 11 official reports [24, 25, 27, 29–31, 33, 34, 57–59], in addition to 5 peer reviewed articles [12, 36, 43, 47, 60]. Lebanon recognizes refugees as an “at-risk population” due to their marginalization, significant traumas from conflict and displacement, and adverse socioeconomic situations [29]. The MENA region is likely to face hidden epidemics among some populations, with the potential for these to spread to the general population through sexual transmission [29]. In 2019, the WHO regional framework recommends scaling up comprehensive harm reduction services, including needle exchange, opioid substitution, HIV/hepatitis testing, vaccination, and overdose management, but displaced populations and refugees have limited access to such programs and are often excluded from host population initiatives [57].

Several approaches focusing on mental health and substance use strategies have been implemented and tested among refugee groups in Lebanon [47, 58]; however, harm reduction and the screening and treatment of HCV and HBV remain under-addressed [36]. The WHO suggests task-sharing models and brief interventions in primary care to expand mental health and substance use services, especially tailored to displaced and conflict-affected populations [43, 58].

The program was updated in 2015 under the title “Update of the Mental Health Gap Action Program (mhGAP) guidelines for mental, neurological, and substance use disorders” and again in 2023. However, only the 2015 version serves as a humanitarian intervention guide, emphasizing substance use in refugee populations [27, 33]. The WHO’s Regional Flagship Initiative to accelerate the public health response to substance use in the Eastern Mediterranean Region focuses on reducing morbidity and mortality through evidence-based, context-specific interventions [24, 59, 60]. For refugees and displaced populations in Lebanon and the broader MENA region, this initiative emphasizes expanding equitable access to harm reduction services, integrating substance use disorder treatment into primary health care, and promoting decriminalization and alternative measures to incarceration. The initiative fosters intersectoral coordination and collaboration, engaging civil society and people with lived experience through a newly formed Regional Coalition. Despite progress, challenges remain, including service gaps and social exclusion, especially among marginalized groups like refugees [24, 59, 60]. Similarly, UNODC, in the Drug World Report 2025, underscores the compounded risks faced by refugees, including trauma, social exclusion, legal challenges, and limited access to harm reduction and treatment services [25]. It also notes how criminal networks exploit instability to expand drug markets, further jeopardizing displaced populations. Emphasizing the urgent need for integrated, rights-based public health responses, the report calls for tailored interventions that improve service accessibility, reduce stigma, and strengthen coordination among humanitarian, health, and justice sectors across MENA [25].

In 2013, the UNHCR published guidance for operations in both camp and non-camp settings, applicable to rural and urban areas in LMICs where the agency operates. This guidance included recommendations to ensure that individuals with specific MHPSS needs have access to basic services. It also advocated for making medications for chronic mental, neurological, or substance use (MNS) conditions affordable for all, either free of charge or at subsidized rates [34]. In 2017, another publication focused on strengthening mental health care systems for Syrian refugees in Europe and the Middle East, covering eight countries, including Lebanon. While this paper provided recommendations to scale up interventions among refugees, it did not offer specific guidance on substance use or harm reduction [47].

Furthermore, the WHO has set a target to eliminate viral hepatitis by 2030. The goals include reducing HCV incidence by 80%, diagnosing 90% of affected individuals, and treating 90% of those diagnosed through expanding screening and treatment services [12]. In the absence of an effective vaccine, hepatitis C prevention relies on reducing exposure in high-risk populations and healthcare settings. Accordingly, the WHO recommends primary prevention interventions, such as educating healthcare workers about the safe handling and disposal of sharps, implementing harm reduction strategies for people who inject drugs, and screening blood products for blood-borne infections [30].

At the national level, the National Mental Health and Substance Use Strategies (2015–2020, 2023–2030), including “Mental Health and Substance Use—Prevention, Promotion, and Treatment: Situation Analysis and Strategy for Lebanon 2015–2020,” mention refugees but lack specific guidance or operational plans focused on harm reduction for displaced populations [26, 31].

Similarly, the Inter-Ministerial Substance Use Response Strategy 2016–2021, implemented by several ministries in Lebanon, set the goal to improve equitable access for vulnerable groups, including refugees, but implementation remains limited [31].

A draft of the updated National Mental Health Strategy for Lebanon (2023–2030) focusing on substance use was published in 2023. The third domain, related to promotion and prevention, emphasized the development and implementation of evidence-based strategies for mental health and substance use, the strengthening of priority actions, and advocacy for the prevention and reduction of harmful substance use. However, while refugees were mentioned in the situation analysis, the strategy did not specifically address their needs or those of displaced populations [26]. In 2017, a two-phase approach study identified refugees with priority mental health care needs in Lebanon. It sought to find efficient screening instruments and mechanisms for detecting moderate and severe mental disorders in refugee settings. However, this study excluded a substance use module from the validation process [46].

Screening and treatment programs for HBV and HCV do not fully cover refugees, despite increasing prevalence and associated risks [12, 36]. The Syrian conflict and displacement indicate the need for culturally adapted, evidence-based harm reduction interventions to reduce harmful substance use and infectious disease transmission among refugees. One study aimed to understand the current situation of substance use and the associated risk of blood-borne infections, including HBV and HCV, among Lebanese and displaced populations, particularly Syrians and Iraqis, in the greater Beirut area of Lebanon [43]. It stated that the problem of substance use and risk of blood-borne infections among Lebanese and displaced populations is a neglected area of public health.

Key evidence gaps among Syrian and Palestinian refugees in Lebanon include scarce data on overdose prevalence and trends—reflecting broader national limitations—as well as incomplete hepatitis care cascades (testing, linkage to care, and treatment). Insufficient evaluations of harm reduction service utilization and implementation fidelity further impede effective treatment and prevention efforts. These gaps constrain targeted interventions despite existing services and policies; addressing them via MOPH-led surveillance and prospective cohort studies would markedly enhance public health responses.

Discussion and recommendations

Lebanon’s involvement in regional strategies highlights the significance of addressing substance use-related issues. However, the present findings reveal a notable gap in specific plans or policies targeting refugees and the scarcity of related research papers, despite some documents acknowledging these areas as objectives.

In recent years, Lebanon has implemented several policies and strategies related to substance use response and harm reduction. In 2019, the MOPH designed substance use residential treatment program standards for Lebanon to provide organizations with quality benchmarks and guide them toward excellence. These standards emphasize accessibility as the cornerstone of quality, ensuring that services are available, affordable, and acceptable to all individuals without discrimination based on nationality, culture, religion, political views, age, gender, or disability [61]. Yet, there is a notable lack of data on harm reduction services specifically provided in refugee settings.

Similarly, Lebanon lacks policies and strategies for HCV and HBV screening, both for the general population and vulnerable groups like refugees. This absence is concerning, as diagnosing and reporting communicable diseases are crucial for evaluating and developing disease control programs and supporting overall disease surveillance efforts. Available data suggest rising rates of hepatitis B and hepatitis C among refugee populations, particularly among those engaged in drug and sexual risk behaviors [29]. The WHO reported a fourfold increase in hepatitis incidence in 2016, exacerbated by the large number of Syrian refugees living in informal settlements with inadequate water and sanitation conditions [56].

This review of available policies, strategies, and published studies reveals three main gaps impacting refugees’ access to harm reduction and substance use services, including screening for transmissible infectious diseases such as HCV and HBV. Addressing these gaps is crucial to uncovering the disparities and inequalities that increase the vulnerability of some populations to these diseases. Comprehensive care and support services must be accessible—financially and otherwise—and should account for all people who use drugs and their partners.

Legal barriers and stigmatization

There is a shortage of trained professionals providing harm reduction services within camps. Syrian and Palestinian refugees are often excluded from several professions because they are not classified as Lebanese citizens, which exacerbates their financial problems and limits their access to healthcare. The scarcity of medical professionals specializing in infectious diseases and harm reduction further compounds this issue. Involving trained and experienced healthcare professionals from refugee populations could be mutually beneficial [62]. Recognizing their education and work experience while expanding their professional practice scope would strengthen the host country’s workforce, reduce the financial burden of refugee care, and improve the socioeconomic status of refugees [55, 63].

The absence of tailored policies or special programs aimed at improving refugees’ access to treatment and harm reduction services. Legal status and stigmatization are significant barriers that hinder refugees from obtaining necessary care. While no data exists on programs addressing new psychoactive substances among refugees, reports highlight several legal obstacles and persistent stigmatization of injecting drug users in pharmacies across many MENA countries [64]. Although a 2018 Global Fund grant aimed to integrate HIV and other health services for refugees and displaced populations to fight AIDS, tuberculosis, and malaria in Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, and Yemen [65], it did not include any budget or support for HCV and HBV screening or treatment, despite these diseases being more prevalent among injecting drug users compared to HIV [66].

Cultural and religious influences

Cultural and religious influences in Lebanon shape societal attitudes toward drug use, contributing to heightened stigma and exclusion, especially in conservative communities. This stigma extends to health systems and public policy, where drug users, including refugees with substance use disorders, face discrimination and moral judgments that deter them from seeking services and hinder the effective implementation of policies [42, 48, 49]. This cultural context reinforces legal barriers and results in a lack of tailored harm reduction programs for refugee populations, worsening health disparities and service gaps. Together, exclusionary legal frameworks and conservative cultural attitudes limit refugees’ access to and utilization of harm reduction services, perpetuating risks of substance use harms and infectious disease transmission [41, 67].

Economic factor

Economic constraints pose a critical barrier to accessing harm reduction and substance use treatment services among refugees and displaced populations in Lebanon. The country’s prolonged economic crisis, marked by currency devaluation, inflation, and widespread poverty, has severely undermined the affordability and availability of healthcare. Over 90% of Syrian refugees and more than 80% of Lebanese citizens live below the poverty line, making healthcare costs prohibitive for the most vulnerable groups [68, 69]. Medication shortages caused by supply chain disruptions further restrict access to essential treatments, particularly for chronic conditions and substance use disorders, exacerbating health risks [70, 71]. Refugees face high expenses and limited access to social and health protection schemes, while the fragmented healthcare system, with heavy reliance on private providers, limits equitable service delivery [63, 72]. Moreover, logistical barriers like transportation costs and the closure of health centers due to regional instability increase the difficulty of reaching care [68, 73]. War and periodic conflict further damage healthcare infrastructure, with numerous hospitals and clinics closed or severely impacted, reducing service availability [74, 75]. The economic instability thus compounds existing legal and social barriers, reinforcing disparities in harm reduction service access and outcomes for displaced populations in Lebanon.

Lack of data on substance use and infectious diseases

This review highlights a critical lack of targeted research focusing on substance use among refugees and displaced populations in Lebanon. While some studies mention refugees within broader health assessments, there is insufficient empirical data directly addressing their specific needs and experiences with substance use [41]. This gap, particularly regarding infectious disease transmission, screening, or treatment within the camps [42], limits the ability to develop effective interventions and support systems tailored to this vulnerable population. It also affects the implementation of evidence-based policies, a challenge exacerbated by security and governance issues within camps [76, 77].

The inadequate collection of routine surveillance data results in poorly informed policies, affecting access to treatment and the implementation of appropriate services. Reports indicate that HBV and HCV cases are underreported or underdiagnosed, suggesting insufficient disease surveillance in Lebanon [36]. Under-detection is often due to diseases’ slow onset [78], while underreporting stems from political turmoil, indifference, and inadequate infrastructure [79]. Additionally, “neglected diseases,” prevalent among low-income populations, attract limited investment from the private sector for research and development due to their lack of profitability [80]. Thus, vigilance is critical for controlling common infections such as hepatitis B and hepatitis C, whose incidence can increase in developed countries receiving migrants and refugees from regions with poor health conditions, as seen with the influx of Syrians to Lebanon in 2011 [62].

Recommendations

Refugees, as part of vulnerable groups, do not share the same characteristics or surrounding factors as other populations, thereby requiring specific targeted interventions distinct from other populations. This shall involve a combined approach that acknowledges the distinctive context of refugees in Lebanon, addressing gaps in policy, programming, and research, ultimately improving equitable access to substance use treatment and harm reduction services for refugees and displaced populations.

On a policy level, the Inter-Ministerial Substance Use Response Strategy for Lebanon (2016–2021) provides a foundation but lacks sufficient focus on refugee-specific challenges and should be expanded to include tailored harm reduction policies. Legal frameworks must be developed to guarantee refugee access to substance use treatment and harm reduction services through inclusive health policies fostered by collaborations among governments, NGOs, and international organizations [26, 58, 61]. Moreover, collaboration between governments, NGOs, and international organizations is vital to developing inclusive health policies that guarantee refugees’ access to essential health services.

On a programmatic level, the shortage of healthcare professionals poses a barrier to adequate treatment access in Lebanon, particularly for refugees and displaced populations. Thus, expanding educational and training programs for displaced healthcare professionals could help integrate them into the formal healthcare system while improving service delivery in underserved areas. Expanding mobile clinics and peer-led community programs can overcome access barriers caused by legal restrictions on displaced healthcare professionals. In 2008, the WHO recommended increasing the utilization and coverage of mental healthcare interventions in under-resourced settings by implementing task-shifting or task-sharing to supervised laypersons trained to perform specific tasks [35, 58]. This approach can potentially broaden access to information, improve service provision and safety planning, and facilitate data collection, particularly among hard-to-reach populations such as refugees and displaced populations. One study reported similar suggestions, advocating for scalable programs tailored to Syrian refugees and providing information on the WHO’s newly launched programs for adapting, testing, and scaling up mental health services in neighboring and European countries hosting Syrian refugees [47].

At the level of research, there is a critical need for comprehensive data collection using both quantitative and qualitative methods, including regular Rapid Situation Assessments (RSAs), to understand evolving substance use patterns and social factors among refugees amid Lebanon’s ongoing crises. This approach would provide insights into the evolving landscape of substance use amid ongoing crises such as Lebanon’s economic instability. Additionally, quantitative and qualitative research methodologies are crucial for capturing a holistic view of substance use behaviors and the socioeconomic factors that influence them.

Special attention should be given to the unique circumstances faced by refugees and displaced individuals, including their mental health needs and access to treatment services, given the high levels of anxiety and depression reported among these populations.

As a result, reasonable and applicable proposals for policy briefs, MOPH/UN agency implementation plans, or grant applications in Lebanon’s protracted refugee context include establishing a national refugee-inclusive harm reduction task force under MOPH coordination, integrating UNRWA, UNHCR, and NGOs to systematically address service gaps identified across studies. This task force would coordinate the deployment of UNRWA/MOPH mobile harm reduction units serving Palestinian camps and Syrian informal settlements, offering sterile syringes, naloxone, HCV testing, and referral pathways to treatment, alongside adapting WHO mhGAP digital modules via UNHCR apps for refugee substance use screening targeting youth distress.

Strength and limitations

This review appears to be the first in Lebanon to focus specifically on tailored policies for substance use and harm reduction services targeting refugees and displaced populations. These underserved groups face unique challenges in accessing treatment. This review emphasizes the absence of customized approaches for refugees and displaced individuals regarding substance use treatment. By identifying these gaps, this work can serve as a foundational document for developing specific strategies aimed at improving access to treatment for these vulnerable groups. It also underscores the necessity of addressing their needs within the broader context of public health in Lebanon.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, there is a scarcity of documents specifically addressing Lebanon. Most existing literature pertains to the broader MENA region, which may not accurately reflect Lebanon’s unique legal, socioeconomic, and political contexts. Second, the focus is on mental health over substance use. While some studies do address substance use among migrants, many are centered around general mental health issues. This lack of focused research means that specific treatment strategies for substance use disorders are underexplored. The third limitation lies in the lack of data on overdose mortality. This review points out that there is no available data on overdose-related mortality among migrants or strategies to prevent such incidents. This absence of information limits the ability to formulate effective harm reduction policies tailored to this group.

Conclusion

In conclusion, re-evaluating health policies for vulnerable groups, particularly refugees in Lebanon and the MENA region in general, is crucial for enhancing health outcomes and addressing disparities. A tailored approach within a unified strategy for substance use management among refugees should prioritize enhancing access to treatment and harm reduction services and screening for HCV/HBV (HIV excluded due to low reported yield). This approach includes implementing culturally sensitive and accessible screening and preventive measures. On a broader scale, this study can serve as a foundational reference for establishing effective harm reduction policies in Lebanon targeting refugees and displaced populations. It is essential to identify the most effective, accessible, and applicable harm reduction services tailored to the unique needs of this vulnerable group. Furthermore, there is a pressing need for more focused research targeting refugees to better understand their specific health challenges and the barriers they face in accessing care.
