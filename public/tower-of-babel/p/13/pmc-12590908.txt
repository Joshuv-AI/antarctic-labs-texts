Malaria healthcare service utilization before, during, and 1 year into the COVID-19 pandemic in 20 sub-Saharan African countries using Generalized Estimating Equation
Authors: Angwach Abrham Asnake, Gilbert Eshun, Augustus Osborne, Michael Sarfo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe onset of the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted healthcare delivery including malaria healthcare services in sub-Saharan Africa. There is limited research exploring impact of COVID-19 on malaria healthcare utilization. This study explores the trends in disruption of malaria service utilization across 20 sub-Saharan African countries before, at the initial onset, and 1 year into the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsLongitudinal study was conducted using secondary data from the ‘Premise Malaria COVID-19 Health Services Disruption Survey 2020 and 2021 from 20 malaria endemic African countries’. The study population consisted of individuals aged 16 years and above from the general population across 20 malaria-endemic African countries. The outcome variable for this study was malaria healthcare service utilization, comprising three specific measures: being tested for malaria, visiting a healthcare provider for malaria symptoms and receiving treatment for malaria symptoms at three distinct time periods. Data was extract and analysed using STATA version 18. A Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) approach was used in the statistical analysis.ResultsA total of 4726 participants were included in this study. Before to, during, and 1 year into the COVID-19 pandemic, 71.77%, 72.26%, and 59.12% of participants were tested for malaria, respectively. Also, 80.61%, 79.71%, and 1.25% of participants visited healthcare providers for malaria symptoms before, at the initial onset, and 1 year into the COVID-19 pandemic, respectively. Additionally, 97.97%, 96.30%, and 15.23% of participants received treatment for malaria symptoms before, at the initial onset, and 1 year into the COVID-19 pandemic, respectively. 1 year after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, people were 45% less likely to be tested for malaria, (AOR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.48, 0.63), 99.7% less likely to visit healthcare providers for malaria symptoms (AOR = 0.003, 95% CI 0.002, 0.005), 99.6% less likely to receive treatment for malaria symptoms (AOR = 0.004, 95% CI 0.002, 0.006).ConclusionThis study demonstrates a significant decline in malaria healthcare service utilization 1 year into the COVID-19 pandemic across 20 sub-Saharan African countries. Individuals were markedly less likely to be tested, visit healthcare providers, or receive treatment for malaria symptoms compared to pre-pandemic levels. These findings emphasize the urgent need for resilient health systems and sustained malaria services during global health emergencies to prevent setbacks in malaria control and elimination efforts.Keywords: Malaria, COVID-19, Healthcare utilization, Sub-Sahara Africa, Premise Malaria COVID-19 Health Services Disruption Survey

Background

Malaria is a parasitic infection caused by species of Plasmodium, including of Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium ovale, and Plasmodium malariae, transmitted through the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito [1]. According to the World Malaria report 2024, in 2023, there were approximately 263 million malaria cases globally and a staggering 597,000 malaria-related deaths across 83 countries [2]. The report further estimates a disproportionately high burden of malaria cases in the African region, accounting for about 246 million (94%) cases and 569,000 (95%) deaths, with a heavy toll on children under five years, who account for 80% of all malaria-related deaths in the region [2].

Prior to the advent of COVID-19, nations had adopted various strategies to combat malaria, including the National Malaria Elimination Programme, which aligns with the global vision of eliminating malaria by 2030, as espoused in Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG 3) [3]. However, the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted healthcare delivery globally, with resource-constrained countries being particularly affected [4, 5]. Studies suggest that these disruptions had varying impacts on malaria control efforts across different regions. For instance, Hakizimana et al. [6] reported a decrease in malaria testing of about 4.32 per 1000 patients per month before COVID-19, compared to an increase of about 2.38 per 1000 during the pandemic in three high-endemic districts in Rwanda. In contrast, Emong et al. [7], in their assessment of COVID-19’s impact on HIV, tuberculosis (TB), malaria, and maternal and child health (MCH) services, found no significant difference in the mean number of malaria cases before and during the pandemic. Similarly, Dzianach et al. [8] estimate that disruptions to antimalarial treatment in sub-Saharan Africa during the pandemic led to an excess of 5.9 million malaria cases and 76,000 additional deaths compared to pre-pandemic era.

These disruptions were driven by multiple factors. The pandemic led to reduced access to primary healthcare and malaria case management, shortages of resources for severe malaria admissions, and logistical challenges in distributing key supplies, such as insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and medicines for artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) across regions [9–11]. According to Park et al. [9], COVID-19 lockdowns were a major barrier to healthcare utilization, particularly in resource-constraint settings. Furthermore, stringent control measures, such as social distancing, lockdowns, and economic setbacks, exacerbated the situation by stalling the distribution of malaria interventions. Healthcare workers, forerunner to malaria management, were frequently reassigned to support COVID-19 efforts, further compromising malaria treatment services [9, 12–14]. Akin to the impasse caused by the COVID-19 on health services, the Ebola virus outbreak in Africa also significantly increased the incidence and mortality of malaria in affected communities and countries with malaria deaths outnumbering Ebola related deaths [15].

The World Health Organization has predicted that these disruptions could reverse progress toward malaria elimination and undermine the attainment of SDG 3. For example, the WHO estimates that a 75% decline in malaria treatment coverage during the pandemic could lead to an increase in malaria-related deaths to 769,000—double the deaths recorded in 2018 [14]. Consistent with these projections, Gavi et al. [16] noted an upsurge of 30,000 malaria cases in Zimbabwe in 2020 compared to the same period in 2018 and 2019.

There is significant disruptions caused by COVID-19 to healthcare delivery and malaria treatment [17, 18]. Despite this, there is limited research exploring the utilization of malaria healthcare services before, during, and one-year post-pandemic across sub-Saharan Africa. Understanding these trends is critical to identifying the pandemic’s impact on malaria management and its implications for achieving SDG 3, which is only five years away. Therefore, this study compares and explores trends in malaria service utilization across 20 sub-Saharan African countries before, during, and 1 year into the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Study design and data source

Longitudinal study was conducted using secondary data drawn from two survey datasets: ‘Premise Malaria COVID-19 Health Services Disruption Survey 2020’ and a follow-up ‘Premise Malaria COVID-19 Health Services Disruption Survey 2021’. These surveys were designed to evaluate the change in levels of preventive measures for malaria and malaria service delivery throughout a specified period of the COVID-19 global pandemic. The surveys were designed by Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) in collaboration with Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation (BMGF), and implemented by Premise Data Corporation [19].

The surveys were conducted in 20 African countries were where malaria is endemic: Benin; Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, Liberia, Mali, Mozambique, Niger, Nigeria, Rwanda, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Somalia, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. As the survey aimed to assess disruptions to malaria-related health services among the general population, no additional inclusion criteria applied beyond age (16 years and older) and country of residence in one of the 20 malaria-endemic African countries. Premise Data Corporation distributed the survey via its smartphone-based data collection platform. Premise maintains a global network of users with basic demographic data, including age range, gender, and country of residence. The survey was made available to all users within the Premise network, who were associated with any of the 20 target countries. Users were invited to participate through in-app notifications or prompts, and participation was voluntary. A smartphone-based Premise data collection platform was used for the data collection. Inclusion criteria were members of the general population in the 20 African countries who were aged 16 and above. Weights were not calculated for these surveys [19].

The ‘Premise Malaria COVID-19 Health Services Disruption Survey 2020’ was collected throughout July 2020 which provides information about malaria healthcare utilization for two time periods: from December 2019 to February 2020 (before COVID-19 outbreak in the various countries) and from March 2020 to June 2020 (early onset of COVID-19 outbreak in the various countries). In the follow-up survey, ‘Premise Malaria COVID-19 Health Services Disruption Survey 2021’, data were collected throughout May–June 2021 to give further insight into the changes in malaria healthcare utilization for February 2021–May 2021 (1 year after onset of COVID-19 pandemic in the various countries) [19].

Study variables and its definition

The outcome variable for this study was malaria healthcare service utilization, which included three specific measures: being tested for malaria, visiting a healthcare provider (HCP) for malaria symptoms, and receiving treatment for malaria symptoms. Survey questions related to malaria service utilization were organized into three distinct time periods: before COVID-19 (December 2019–February 2020), during COVID-19 onset (March 2020–June 2020), and 1 year after the pandemic onset (February 2021–May 2021). Explanatory variables included in this study were age, gender, education status, employment status, wealth status, geographical location, and region. Age was divided into four categories: 16–25 years, 26–35, 36–45, and over 45 years. The gender variable was coded as male and female. Education status was categorized into primary and lower, secondary and college, and above college. Work status included employed, students, and the unemployed. Financial situation was a dichotomous variable of whether participants were able to afford basic requirements or not. Geographic location was based on type of residence and included urban, rural, and suburban areas. Regional classification was done by grouping the 20 malaria-endemic countries into four sub-Saharan African regions. East Africa included Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda, Somalia, Tanzania, and Uganda; West Africa included Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Liberia, Mali, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, and Sierra Leone; Central Africa included the Democratic Republic of the Congo; and Southern Africa included Mozambique, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. These variables were selected based on their theoretical relevance and prior research linking sociodemographic and regional factors to healthcare access and malaria outcomes.

Statistical analysis and model building

The data analysis was conducted using STATA version 18. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study population and to present the distribution of malaria healthcare utilization across different categories of explanatory variables. The 2021 Premise Malaria COVID-19 Health Services Disruption Survey did not include questions regarding the reasons for not visiting a health provider or not taking malaria medication in response to symptoms. As the primary objective of this study was to assess trends in malaria-related service utilization over time, the absence of this information in the 2021 survey does not affect the core analysis or findings presented in the main findings. To account for the complex nature of the data, associations between repeated measures of the outcome variables and various factors were evaluated for statistical significance by applying regression models to each outcome variable using a Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) approach. The GEE model incorporated a logit link function with a binomial family and an exchangeable working correlation structure. This method combined repeated measures for each explanatory and outcome variable across three phases ‘Pre-COVID-19 Phase,’ ‘Initial Phase of the COVID-19 Outbreak,’ and ‘1 Year After the COVID-19 Outbreak’ to estimate the population-averaged association between these time periods and the outcome variables. To assess the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on malaria health care utilization, Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) models were employed to account for repeated measurements from the same individuals across survey periods. Both bivariable (crude) and multivariable GEE models were fitted for three outcome variables: being tested for malaria, visiting a healthcare provider for malaria symptoms, and receiving treatment for malaria. In the multivariable models, the following covariates were included to control for potential confounding: age group, gender, educational status, employment status, financial status, residential location, and geographic region. These covariates were adjusted for consistently across all three outcome models. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.05 in the multivariable GEE model were considered significantly associated with the outcome variables.

Ethical considerations

This study did not seek ethical approval for the Premise COVID-19 Health Services Disruption Survey 2020 and 2021 data, which are publicly accessible datasets stored in the IHME and are openly accessible for research purposes. Also, the data have been de-identified with no personally identifying information.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

A total of 4870 individuals were initially screened for inclusion in the study. After excluding incomplete responses and ineligible entries, 4726 participants were included in the final analysis. The majority (61.07%) of the study participants were in the 16–25 years age group, and most participants were male (84.57%). A large proportion (92.71%) had education at the secondary or college level. Among the participants, 45.39% were students, while 43.25% were employed. Over half (57.49%) reported being unable to afford basic needs. The majority of participants were from West Africa (57.64%) and East Africa (37.05%) (Table 1).

Distribution of health care utilization for malaria

The proportion of participants who were tested for malaria was 71.77% before the COVID-19 pandemic, 72.6% during the pandemic, and declined to 59.12% 1 year after the pandemic. Similarly, 80.61%, 79.71%, and 1.25% of participants visited healthcare providers for malaria symptoms before, during, and 1 year after the pandemic, respectively (Fig. 1). A total of 1051 respondents (32.39%) did not visit a healthcare provider because health facilities were closed before the pandemic, and 1019 respondents (35.98%) reported the same issue during the pandemic (Fig. 2). In terms of medication adherence, 40.67% of respondents did not take medication because they did not want to before the outbreak, while 34.55% reported the same during the outbreak (Fig. 3). Treatment coverage remained high before and during the pandemic, with 97.97% and 96.30% of participants receiving treatment for malaria symptoms, respectively; however, this decreased to 15.23% 1 year after the pandemic. Approximately one-third (32.65%) of respondents reported having no bed nets in their households before the pandemic, while more than one-fifth (25.98%) lacked bed nets 1 year after the pandemic.

The effect of COVID-19 pandemic on malaria health care utilization using GEE model

The GEE models showed that, 1 year after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, malaria-related healthcare service utilization declined sharply. Moreover, age, gender, employment status, financial status, and region were significantly associated with malaria healthcare utilization.

1 year after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a significant reduction in malaria-related healthcare services compared to the period before COVID-19. People were 45% less likely to be tested for malaria, with an AOR = 0.55 (95% CI 0.48, 0.63). Individuals were 99.7% less likely to visit healthcare providers for malaria symptoms, with an AOR = 0.003 (95% CI 0.002, 0.005). The likelihood of receiving treatment for malaria symptoms decreased by 99.6%, with an AOR = 0.004 (95% CI 0.002, 0.006). Compared to participants aged 16–25, those in older age groups demonstrated increased likelihoods of malaria testing and treatment: participants aged 26–35 were 1.51 times more likely to be tested for malaria (AOR = 1.51, 95% CI 1.26, 1.82), and 1.49 times more likely to receive treatment for malaria symptoms (AOR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.06, 2.10). Participants aged 36–45 had a 4.45 times higher likelihood of receiving treatment for malaria symptoms (AOR = 4.45, 95% CI 2.33, 8.49). Females had 1.5 times (AOR = 1.50, (95% CI 1.01, 2.23)) greater odds of receiving treatment for malaria. Compared to employed participants students and unemployed participants had 31% (AOR = 0.69 (0.57, 0.85)) and 28% (AOR = 0.72 (0.54, 0.95)) reduced odds of visited HCP for malaria symptoms, respectively. In comparison to participants residing in East African country, participants living in other geographic area had reduced malaria health care service utilization (Table 2).

Discussion

The study highlights a significant decline in malaria healthcare utilization during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, with testing and treatment rates experiencing dramatic reductions. Specifically, malaria testing rates dropped from 71.77% before the pandemic to just 59.12% 1 year later. Similarly, consultations with healthcare providers for malaria symptoms decreased sharply, falling from 80.61% to an alarming 1.25%. This decline underscores the widespread disruption of healthcare services caused by the pandemic, as healthcare systems worldwide were overwhelmed and resources were diverted to address COVID-19. A notable finding is that 32.39% of respondents identified closed health facilities as a barrier to care prior to the pandemic, a figure that rose to 35.98% during the pandemic. The likelihood of receiving treatment for malaria symptoms also saw a staggering reduction of 99.6% 1 year after the pandemic began. Demographic factors played a critical role in healthcare access during this period. Older individuals were more likely to undergo malaria testing and receive treatment, while females demonstrated higher probabilities of obtaining therapy compared to males. Employment status also influenced healthcare utilization, with students and unemployed individuals less likely to seek care compared to their employed counterparts.

The observed decline in malaria testing rates from 71.77% to 59.12% and the near cessation of healthcare visits for malaria symptoms (from 80.61% to 1.25%) reflect the broader challenges faced by healthcare systems during pandemics [5, 21]. Previous studies have shown that pandemics often result in the reallocation of healthcare resources, which diminishes access to critical services like malaria prevention, testing, and treatment [22, 23]. This reallocation can have severe consequences, particularly for vulnerable populations, as reduced access to treatment can lead to increased morbidity and mortality [24]. The implications of these findings are far-reaching. The interruption of malaria healthcare services during the pandemic not only jeopardized individual health outcomes but also posed a significant threat to public health. Delayed or missed treatment for malaria can result in more severe disease progression, increased transmission rates, and higher fatality rates. These challenges underscore the need for resilient healthcare systems capable of maintaining essential services during public health emergencies. Future research should focus on developing strategies to safeguard access to malaria care during crises, including the integration of telemedicine and community-based healthcare delivery systems.

A significant barrier to malaria care identified in this study was the closure of health facilities, a challenge that became more pronounced during the COVID-19 pandemic. The proportion of respondents unable to access care due to facility closures increased from 32.39% before the pandemic to 35.98% during it. This finding reflects systemic weaknesses in healthcare infrastructure, which were further exposed by the pandemic. Similar trends have been observed globally, where healthcare facility closures disproportionately affected marginalized and underserved communities, exacerbating health disparities [25–27]. The inability to access timely care not only delays treatment for acute conditions like malaria but also hinders long-term public health efforts aimed at controlling and eliminating the disease [26]. Addressing these challenges requires innovative solutions to ensure healthcare continuity during emergencies. Potential strategies include expanding telemedicine services, deploying mobile clinics, and strengthening community-based healthcare interventions. Future research should prioritize the development and evaluation of these approaches to enhance healthcare resilience and equity during public health crises.

Additionally, we found a dramatic 99.6% decrease in the likelihood of seeking treatment for malaria symptoms 1 year after the onset of the pandemic. This finding is particularly concerning, as untreated malaria can lead to severe health complications and increased mortality rates [28]. Delays in treatment not only worsen individual health outcomes but also contribute to higher transmission rates within communities, potentially reversing progress made in malaria control efforts [29]. If treatment-seeking behaviour remains suppressed, malaria transmission could escalate, placing additional strain on already burdened healthcare systems. These findings highlight the urgent need for strategies to ensure the availability and accessibility of malaria treatment during health emergencies. Future research should investigate the long-term impacts of reduced treatment-seeking behaviour on malaria epidemiology and explore innovative approaches to delivering treatment, such as home-based care or community health worker programs, during crises.

Furthermore, the study underscores notable demographic disparities in access to malaria healthcare services. Older individuals were more likely to undergo testing and receive treatment, a trend consistent with prior research on age-related differences in health-seeking behavior [30, 31]. This may be attributed to greater health awareness or prioritization of care among older populations [30]. Interestingly, females were found to have higher probabilities of obtaining malaria treatment, which may reflect gender-specific health-seeking behaviours or differences in access to healthcare services. Understanding these demographic patterns is crucial for designing targeted public health interventions. Addressing these disparities requires a deeper understanding of the socio-cultural factors that influence healthcare utilization across different demographic groups. For instance, cultural norms, gender roles, and economic constraints may all play a role in shaping health-seeking behaviour. Future research should explore these factors in greater detail to inform the development of tailored interventions aimed at improving healthcare access for underrepresented and marginalized populations.

Lastly, the study highlights the critical role of socioeconomic factors, particularly employment status, in determining access to malaria healthcare services. Students and unemployed individuals were found to have lower probabilities of seeking care compared to employed participants. This finding aligns with existing evidence that socioeconomic status is a key determinant of healthcare access [32–34]. Individuals with lower socioeconomic status often face additional barriers, such as lack of health insurance, transportation challenges, and financial constraints, which further limit their ability to access timely care [35]. Socioeconomic disparities in healthcare access can exacerbate health inequities, particularly during public health emergencies when resources are limited. Addressing these challenges requires comprehensive policies that prioritize equitable access to healthcare services for all individuals, regardless of their employment or socioeconomic status. Future research should examine the intersection of employment, socioeconomic status, and healthcare utilization during crises to inform the development of policies and programs that promote health equity.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study encompasses three critical periods—before-COVID, during early COVID, and one-year into-COVID: offering a comprehensive understanding of how malaria healthcare service utilization evolved during the pandemic. The GEE method effectively accounts for correlations in repeated measures within individuals, ensuring robust estimates even with clustered data. This approach is particularly suited for longitudinal studies, allowing flexible modeling of within-subject and within-country variability. Sub-Saharan Africa, bearing the highest global malaria burden, makes the findings highly relevant for addressing malaria control in high-burden regions. However, the results may have limited generalizability beyond the countries included in the study, as healthcare systems and pandemic responses vary considerably across regions. Additionally, assessing outcomes only 1 year into the pandemic may not fully capture its long-term effects on malaria service utilization or health outcomes. Furthermore, since participation in online surveys is based on voluntary response, there is a potential for selection bias, which may limit the representativeness of the findings for the general population.

Conclusion

This study examines the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on malaria-related healthcare service utilization. 1 year into the pandemic, the findings reveal a significant decline in access to malaria care, with individuals being 45% less likely to be tested for malaria, 99.7% less likely to consult healthcare providers for malaria symptoms, and 99.6% less likely to receive treatment for these symptoms. These reductions highlight the extensive disruption of healthcare services caused by the pandemic, underscoring the urgent need for resilient healthcare systems that can sustain essential services during public health crises. The results emphasize that the pandemic has not only affected immediate healthcare access but also poses long-term challenges for malaria control efforts. Addressing these challenges is critical to ensuring that essential malaria services remain available, particularly during future health emergencies. Also, the study shows demographic disparities in access to malaria healthcare services. Older individuals were more likely to undergo testing and receive treatment. Females were found to have higher probabilities of obtaining malaria treatment. Students and unemployed individuals were found to have lower probabilities of seeking malaria healthcare treatment compared to employed participants.
