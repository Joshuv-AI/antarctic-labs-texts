Optimizing Sagittal Talar Alignment With Subtalar Arthroereisis Prior to Total Ankle Replacement in PCFD Class E: A Technical Tip
Authors: Julieta Brué, Enrico Pozzessere, Francesco Di Lauro, Lolita Micicoi, Cesar de Cesar Netto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Progressive collapsing foot deformity (PCFD) is a complex 3-dimensional deformity that has been reclassified into 5 distinct pathologic entities (classes A-E), each describing a specific component of the deformity rather than a progressive degree of severity. 1 Class E specifically denotes valgus ankle instability and may coexist with 1 or more of the other classes; when associated with end-stage tibiotalar arthritis, it presents unique challenges for total ankle replacement (TAR). TAR has become a reliable motion-preserving option for end-stage ankle arthritis in appropriately selected patients. 2 However, in patients with concurrent PCFD, the pathologic plantar flexion of the talus inherent to collapsing flatfoot creates a significant biomechanical challenge. If the alignment frame is applied before correction, this deformity can lead to systematic sagittal malorientation of the talar dome cut, potentially resulting in suboptimal component positioning, edge loading, and early failure. 3

Subtalar arthroereisis, traditionally used in flexible flatfoot, limits subtalar hyperpronation through a sinus tarsi implant. Its triplanar corrective effect includes reduction of calcaneal eversion, talar adduction, and talar plantar flexion. 4 We present a technical tip in which subtalar arthroereisis is performed as the very first surgical step to pre-correct pathologic talar plantar flexion and establish a neutral sagittal talar reference before TAR instrumentation. To our knowledge, this specific sequence has not been previously described in the literature.

Indications and Contraindications

Indications include patients with flexible or passively reducible PCFD class E undergoing TAR for end-stage tibiotalar arthritis, particularly when preoperative weight-bearing radiographs and CT demonstrate pathologic talar plantar flexion that risks misleading sagittal referencing for talar preparation. The goal is to restore a more neutral sagittal position of the talus before bone cuts, thereby reducing the risk of malalignment.

Contraindications include subtalar osteoarthritis, rigid or nonreducible subtalar deformity, fixed hindfoot valgus not amenable to sinus tarsi blocking, and cases in which arthroereisis fails to produce a stable, reproducible talar position under intraoperative fluoroscopy.

Technique

Case Description

The illustrated case is a 69-year-old active man with PCFD stage I, class A-E, complete posterior tibial tendon rupture, and end-stage ankle arthritis. He had failed 12 months of conservative management. Preoperative weight-bearing CT showed severe multiplanar deformity with foot and ankle offset (FAO) of 22.48% (normal <4.6%), calcaneal offset of 49.52 mm, and hindfoot alignment of 55.18° valgus (Figure 1).

(D) Weight-bearing CT coronal and sagittal views highlighting tibiotalar valgus tilting and severe plantar flexion of the talus with peritalar subluxation. (E) Preoperative foot and ankle offset (FAO) measurement of 22.48% (normal <4.6%).

The patient was positioned supine on a radiolucent operating table. General anesthesia was supplemented with a preoperative popliteal-sciatic and saphenous nerve block. A well-padded thigh tourniquet was used.

Subtalar Arthroereisis (Step 1: Initial Alignment-Restoring Maneuver)

Subtalar arthroereisis was performed first using the ProStop subtalar arthroereisis implant (Arthrex) (Figure 2). A 1.5-cm incision was made anterior and inferior to the distal fibular tip, parallel to skin tension lines. The sinus tarsi was exposed and cleared of fat while protecting the intermediate dorsal cutaneous nerve and peroneal tendons. Under lateral fluoroscopy, a cannulated guidewire was advanced lateral-to-medial along the subtalar joint axis. Sequential dilation and sizing were performed to select the implant that best corrected talar plantar flexion without overconstraint. The definitive implant was inserted under fluoroscopic guidance. Final anteroposterior and lateral views confirmed central placement within the tarsal canal and satisfactory reduction of pathologic talar plantar flexion. This corrected talar position was then used as the sagittal baseline for all subsequent steps.

(C) Lateral view demonstrating dilator/sizer positioning for optimal implant sizing based on real-time correction of talar plantar flexion. Total Ankle Replacement (Step 2)

TAR was performed via a lateral transfibular approach using the Trabecular Metal Total Ankle system (Zimmer Biomet). After Z-shaped fibular osteotomy and joint exposure, the alignment frame was applied referencing the corrected talar position established by arthroereisis. Tibial and talar preparation, component implantation (size 4 tibial, size 4 talar, +2 polyethylene), and fibular fixation with the FibuLock nail (Arthrex) were completed in standard fashion (Figure 3).

Adjunctive PCFD Reconstruction (Step 3)

Residual deformity was addressed with a medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy (12-mm shift), dorsal opening-wedge Cotton osteotomy with 7.5-mm allograft wedge, percutaneous Achilles lengthening, peroneal longus-to-brevis transfer, Brostrom-type lateral ligament reconstruction, flexor digitorum longus transfer, deltoid reconstruction, spring ligament repair, and modified Kidner procedure (Figure 4).

(D) Lateral view of the Cotton osteotomy with insertion of a 7.5-mm allograft wedge. (E, F) Final anteroposterior and lateral views confirming restoration of overall foot alignment and completion of the reconstruction.

Postoperative Care and Rehabilitation

The patient remained non-weight-bearing in a posterior splint for 2 weeks. Sutures were removed at 2 weeks, and the patient transitioned to a controlled ankle motion (CAM) boot with gentle active range of motion. Progressive weight-bearing was initiated at 6 weeks under physical therapy guidance focusing on peroneal strengthening, propioception, and gait normalization. Transition to a lace-up ankle brace and supportive footwear occurred at 10-12 weeks. Thromboprophylaxis consisted of aspirin and early mobilization.

At 3 months, weight-bearing CT demonstrated excellent correction: FAO improved from 22.48% to 4.98% (77.8% correction), CO from 49.52 mm to 10.84 mm, and hindfoot alignment from 55.18° to 13.45° valgus. The arthroereisis implant remained well-positioned (Figure 5).

Complications

Potential complications of this combined procedure include sinus tarsi pain or implant irritation (reported removal rates of 6%-48% in adult series), 5 implant migration, subtalar over- or under-correction, wound healing issues, infection, and standard TAR-related complications such as subsidence, edge loading, and periprosthetic fracture. In this case, no intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred. At the latest follow-up of 3 months, the arthroereisis implant was retained and asymptomatic, with no clinical or radiographic evidence of implant migration or sinus tarsi pain. Longer follow-up is required to fully assess durability of the construct and the incidence of late complications.

Discussion

Performing subtalar arthroereisis as the initial step before TAR was associated with effective correction of the pathologic talar plantar flexion commonly seen in PCFD with valgus ankle instability (class E).4,6 Three-dimensional morphometric studies have shown that the talus in flatfoot patients exhibits significantly greater plantar flexion (mean +2.6°; P = .02) compared with controls, independent of overall 3D orientation. 7 By correcting this deformity first, the surgeon may obtain a more accurate sagittal reference for TAR instrumentation.

Accurate sagittal positioning of the talar component is critical for TAR longevity. An elevated postoperative gamma angle (>22°) has been associated with anterior talar undercoverage, edge loading, and increased subsidence risk. 8 Recent series have also shown that subtalar arthroereisis as an adjunct in flexible adult PCFD provides meaningful radiographic and patient-reported improvements, although reported removal rates for sinus tarsi pain range from 6% to 48%. 5 In our technique, however, the arthroereisis functions primarily as an intraoperative alignment tool: once the TAR components are seated, articular geometry at the tibiotalar joint is determined by the prosthesis itself. Should later implant removal become necessary, the correction provided by the TAR and concomitant reconstructions is therefore expected to persist, although longer-term studies are needed to confirm this.

This technique is particularly useful when the hindfoot is flexible and subtalar motion can be preserved, as opposed to more rigid hindfoot deformities that may require subtalar arthrodesis.

In line with the approach reported for lateral transfibular TAR with concomitant valgus deformity correction, 9 a single-stage strategy avoids loss of foot correction between stages that would compromise sagittal talar referencing at the time of TAR, requires a single anesthesia and rehabilitation, and uses the lateral transfibular approach to access both the sinus tarsi and the ankle joint. The choice between single- and 2-stage strategies should be individualized.

Although this manuscript presents a single illustrative case to detail the surgical sequence, the senior author has performed this specific maneuver in additional patients undergoing TAR for PCFD with valgus ankle instability (class E) and concomitant tibiotalar arthritis, to address pathologic talar plantar flexion prior to instrumentation. To date, no major complications, implant migration, or hardware removal have been encountered in this broader experience. A cohort study analyzing the clinical and radiographic outcomes of this patient series is currently in preparation and is intended for future submission.

Limitations

This is a single-case technical tip with short-term follow-up of 3 months. The endpoint demonstrated here is intraoperative correction of sagittal talar alignment, not long-term clinical outcomes of TAR. Larger series with ≥1-year follow-up are needed to assess prosthesis survivorship and patient-reported outcomes.

Conclusion

Subtalar arthroereisis performed as the first surgical maneuver before total ankle replacement can effectively optimize sagittal talar alignment in selected patients with flexible PCFD presenting with pathologic talar plantar flexion and concomitant tibiotalar arthritis requiring TAR, including those with associated valgus ankle instability (class E). This simple addition may improve component positioning and warrants further study to confirm long-term durability in this challenging patient population.
