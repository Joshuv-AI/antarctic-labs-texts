Life course, value orientation and self-concept in adolescents with delinquent behavior: a comparative study of delinquent, psychiatric, and healthy youths
Authors: Helena Gampe, Kornelius Winds, Lucas Rainer, Belinda Plattner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJuvenile delinquency is closely linked to complex psychosocial and value-based developmental trajectories. This study compares life-course narratives (childhood and school experiences, daily life structure), value orientation, self-concept, and standardized self-reports of emotions among adolescents with delinquent behavior, adolescents with psychiatric disorders, and healthy peers.MethodsUsing a mixed-methods design, narrative interviews were conducted with all three groups (N = 48). Participants were asked to describe their feelings in relation to these domains in order to assess emotional processing, measured by the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS).ResultsCompared with their healthy peers, narratives from adolescents with delinquent behavior were characterized by greater strain across their developmental course. These descriptions were marked by housing and caregiving instability, insecure bonding experiences with primary caregivers, difficulties in forming coherent and socially oriented value systems, and a tendency toward self-concepts marked by overcompensation. Regarding emotional experience, PANAS results indicate that both the delinquent and psychiatric groups reported higher levels of negative affect than healthy adolescents. Although adolescents with psychiatric disorders also reported adversity, their narratives tended to reflect greater variability in protective factors and more opportunities to benefit from therapeutic and relational support.ConclusionThe findings suggest that delinquent behavior may arise from the cumulative interplay of early adversity, environmental instability, disrupted relational experiences, and challenges in developing individualized values and stable, non-compensatory self-concepts. These results highlight the importance of interventions that address potential moral injury, strengthen reliable relational contexts, and promote socially integrated value orientations. Qualitative approaches provide important insights into these mechanisms and should complement quantitative assessments when working with high-risk youth.

Introduction

Adolescent offenders and adolescents receiving psychiatric care exhibit substantial overlap in key risk and vulnerability factors, including elevated rates of psychiatric disorders, exposure to adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), and disrupted relational environments [1–3]. Despite these shared characteristics, developmental trajectories and intervention outcomes often diverge. While evidence-based psychotherapeutic interventions have demonstrated efficacy in reducing internalizing and externalizing symptoms in clinical adolescent populations [4], outcomes in juvenile justice settings remain more heterogeneous, with comparatively inconsistent effects on recidivism [5, 6]. This raises the question of why similar early risk constellations lead to divergent developmental and therapeutic outcomes.

ACEs constitute a central factor for the development of both psychopathology and delinquent behavior [7, 8]. Traditional cumulative risk models conceptualize adversity in additive terms, often operationalized as the sum of exposure categories [9]. In doing so, individual circumstances are largely disregarded [10]. Dimensional models emphasize mechanisms such as timing, chronicity, and context, highlight that similar exposures may lead to different outcomes, including delinquency, psychiatric disorders, or impaired adaptive functioning [11–14]. In accordance with this, research on offending trajectories has identified meaningful heterogeneity in developmental pathways, particularly with respect to onset and persistence [15, 16].

Importantly, adversity is often not limited to early family environments but extends into later developmental contexts. With increasing age negative experiences can also occur outside the family setting [17]. In particular, school and peer interactions represent a central scope for actions and experiences that may function as protective or risk-enhancing environments depending on relationship quality and exposure to deviant norms [5, 7, 18–20].

ACEs profoundly influence perceptual abilities and emotional processes with consequences for behavior and experience. On the one hand they have been linked to impairments in emotion regulation and altered stress reactivity [21–24]. Furthermore, in the light of adversity emotional processing represents a relevant dimension, as emotions might be perceived as detached from situational context itself [22].

On the other hand, ACEs have been associated with unstable or negatively biased self-concepts [25, 26]. In this context, self-concept refers to adolescents’ cognitive and affective representations of themselves, including perceived competencies, self-evaluations, and identity-related beliefs [27]. Beyond self-related representations, developmental processes also involve the formation of broader systems of meaning that guide perception and behavior.

Ultimately, the value system, with its hierarchical organization, develops into a guiding framework for individual action [28]. Values are shaped by broader social and cultural contexts and are assumed to guide behavior through the evaluation and prioritization of personal goals [29, 30]. Value orientations may provide further insight into how adolescents interpret their experiences and organize their behavior [31, 32].

There is increasing pressure to prevent criminal recidivism specifically in juvenile delinquent populations; at the same time the literature points to complex risk factors, limiting the effectiveness of current interventions [6, 33–35]. Evidence-based approaches such as multisystemic therapy (MST) and functional family therapy (FFT) address these complexities through collaborative goal-setting processes involving adolescents, families, and professionals [5, 36, 38].

This is reflected in qualitative research on such interventions that highlight the role of self-belief, perceived agency, and the experience of being “stuck” in maladaptive trajectories, as well as differences in how young people engage with developmental challenges (e.g. [5, 19]).

A recent systematic review identified recurring themes across qualitative studies, including risk factors for delinquency, interactions with others, and the challenges of transition and desistance [39]. Qualitative studies within the START MST trial have further highlighted the importance of therapeutic relationships and future aspirations [19], and have described how some youth remain on trajectories that are not moving their lives forward, while others begin to navigate the challenges of emerging adulthood more constructively [5]. Together, these findings point to subjective meaning-making as a clinically relevant but understudied dimension in this population.

While thematic analysis, grounded theory, and interpretive phenomenological analysis have been applied in prior qualitative studies of youth offending (e.g. [19, 40]), these frameworks prioritize either theory generation or idiographic experience.

The present study uses qualitative content analysis following Mayring and Kuckartz [41, 42], which is suited to systematic cross-group comparison of categorical patterns. This methodological choice follows from our comparative, multi-group design rather than a single-case idiographic focus. Accordingly, the study investigates how adolescents from juvenile justice, psychiatric, and non-clinical contexts construct their developmental trajectories across key life domains (childhood, school, and current life), exploring these narratives alongside value orientations, self-concept, and associated emotional experiences.

This comparative approach extends prior research by integrating subjective meaning-making across three distinct adolescent groups. To our knowledge, this combination has not previously been examined using this methodological approach. Furthermore, including a standardized measure of positive and negative affect alongside narrative data provides a complementary perspective on how adolescents emotionally relate to their developmental experiences [43].

Methods

Participants

The study was conducted at the University Clinics for Child and Adolescent Psychiatry and Psychotherapeutic Medicine, Christian-Doppler University Hospital, Paracelsus Medical University Salzburg. The hospital provides inpatient, outpatient, and court-mandated treatment services for adolescents.

The delinquent group (Group 1) comprised adolescents receiving treatment through the court-mandated outpatient service. All participants in this group had committed an offence punishable under criminal law. Some participants additionally met criteria for a psychiatric disorder. The psychiatric group (Group 2) comprised adolescents receiving inpatient psychiatric treatment. Participants in this group had a diagnosed psychiatric disorder but no history of offences punishable under criminal law. The comparison group (Group 3) comprised adolescents with no history of psychiatric treatment or diagnosed psychiatric disorder and no history of offences punishable under criminal law.

Group assignment was therefore based on treatment context and legal status rather than diagnostic criteria alone. The non-clinical sample was recruited via word-of-mouth, social media, and sports club networks and consisted of adolescents without current psychiatric treatment or known involvement with the juvenile justice system. The interviewer had no prior therapeutic relationship with the participants.

Participants and their legal guardians provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Salzburg (EC-Nr.: 1042/2025).

All the interviews were conducted individually in a quiet room at the University Clinic for Child and Adolescent Psychiatry in Salzburg. The interviews were carried out by a trained master’s student in psychology.

The exclusion criteria were (1) age younger than 14 years or older than 18 years (2), current severe suicidality (3), presence of psychotic symptoms, and (4) insufficient language proficiency to understand the informed consent and the interview questions. The inclusion criteria were (1) being between the ages of 14 and 18 years and (2) having sufficient knowledge of the German language to follow the interview. Note, that one participant was diagnosed within the diagnosis group F20-29. The person did not have presence of psychotic or delusional symptoms during the inpatient treatment.

Measures

The interview followed a narrative format based on three visual prompts addressing (1) life course (2) value orientation; and (3) self-concept. Emotional processing was assessed by asking participants to describe their feelings in relation to these domains via the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) [44] (German version: [45]).

Guidelines for narrative interviews

The narrative interview guidelines were developed by the first author. They comprised five domains: the life course—covering childhood, school, and daily life structure—followed by value orientation and self-concept. The guidelines are attached to the appendix (Fig. 7a-c). The interview followed a structured chronological sequence (childhood, school, current life, value orientation, self-concept). Participants were encouraged to speak freely within each domain, while the interviewer used prompts and follow-up questions as needed to clarify responses and ensure coverage of all domains. Participants were informed that they could choose not to answer any question.

Emotional processing (PANAS)

Emotional experiences associated with the life domains were assessed using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) [44, 45]. The scale comprises 20 items measuring positive and negative affect on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very slightly or not at all, 5 = extremely). The internal consistency of the PANAS is high (α ≥ 0.84), whereas test–retest reliability is somewhat lower (rtt ≤ 0.66); overall, the instrument demonstrates good psychometric quality [46].

Data processing and analysis

A mixed-methods approach was applied. Interview data were managed using MAXQDA [47], and quantitative analyses were conducted using R [48] and IBM SPSS Statistics [49]. The qualitative analysis formed the basis for subsequent quantitative comparisons. The data analysis followed a six-step process, after the interview transcription.

Transcription and translation of interviews

All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Dialectal expressions were adapted to standard language following established transcription guidelines [50]. After transcription, audio files were deleted, and transcripts were stored in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Identifying information (e.g., districts, school names, sports clubs) was removed or replaced with generic terms.

Interviews were conducted in German, and the initial coding process was also carried out in German. Translations into English were conducted for reporting purposes and checked for accuracy.

Qualitative analysis

Interview material was analyzed using qualitative content analysis following the frameworks of Mayring [42] and Kuckartz & Rädiker [41], applying an inductive approach to category development.

Coding procedure

Coding was conducted using meaning units (text segments), following the principle of “as much as necessary, as little as possible.” Segments were defined as phrases or passages containing a coherent and relevant meaning in relation to the analytical domains.

A segment was coded when it met at least one of the following criteria:

explicit reference to experiences within a life domain (childhood, school, daily life),evaluative statements or interpretations of such experiences,descriptions of emotional reactions associated with these experiences,explicit self-concept),explicit expressions of value orientations.

For the domains of value orientation and self-concept, only explicit mentions were coded. Within life domains, coding additionally included experiential, evaluative, and emotional content.

Multiple coding was applied when segments contained distinct analytically relevant meanings [51]. Repeated references were coded separately when reflecting distinct experiences across biographical stages or life domains, while closely related events within the same episode were coded once to avoid redundancy. Value orientation and self-concept were coded once per participant, as the focus was on their presence rather than frequency.

Coding was conducted by the first author using MAXQDA. Ambiguous segments, category definitions, and coding decisions were regularly discussed with co-authors and the supervisor, and the coding system was iteratively refined through consensus discussions. Formal interrater reliability coefficients (e.g., Cohen’s κ) are widely used in quantitative content analysis but are not required in qualitative content analysis following Kuckartz and Rädiker [41], where analytic rigor is established through transparent category definitions, anchor examples, and iterative consensual validation. We therefore do not report κ, not because such procedures were absent, but because the epistemological framework we applied treats systematic consensus discussions as the appropriate means of ensuring consistency.

Development of the coding system

The coding system was developed inductively through an iterative process. After an initial coding of a subset of the material, the category system was reviewed and revised to ensure conceptual clarity, non-overlap, and an appropriate level of abstraction in relation to the research questions.

Where revisions were made, the material was re-coded to ensure consistency. All coding decisions and modifications were documented in the coding framework.

Categories represent abstracted concepts developed through paraphrasing and generalization of meaning units [41, 42]. For example, statements indicating lack of emotional support, abuse, or absent caregiving were subsumed under “Adverse relationship experiences caregivers.”

Each category was defined by explicit inclusion criteria. For instance, “social integration” was coded when participants described stable peer relationships, a sense of belonging, or regular social participation, but not isolation or conflict. Categories remained grounded in the original material and were supported by anchor examples; codebooks are provided in the appendix (Tables 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8).

Quantifying of qualitative data

For the quantitative comparison of groups (delinquent, psychiatric, and non-clinical), category frequencies were calculated. The unit of analysis for quantification was the coded segment, with each coded segment counted as one occurrence regardless of its length. To account for differences in interview length, frequencies were normalized by calculating occurrences per 1,000 words within each domain and group.

To enhance interpretability and ensure sufficient cell counts, conceptually related categories were aggregated where appropriate. Additionally, pragmatic frequency thresholds were applied (mentions ≥ 5 for life domains; ≥ 3 for value orientation and self-concept).

Group differences in the course of life

Chi-square tests of independence were used to examine frequency differences between groups. To meet the requirements for expected cell counts, conceptually related categories were aggregated (e.g., “emotional neglect” and “humiliation” into “negative emotional experiences”). Where the cell frequencies remained low, Monte Carlo simulations (500 repetitions) were employed. Significant effects were further explored via standardized residuals, with p values adjusted via the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Group differences in emotional processing (PANAS)

To examine the factor structure of the PANAS items in the present sample, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was initially conducted. However, given the relatively small sample size and the limited robustness of factor retention criteria, the results were considered insufficient for deriving a stable factor solution. Therefore, subsequent analyses were based on the two-dimensional structure of the PANAS, comprising positive and negative affect.

For the PANAS analyses, four participants from the psychiatric group and two participants from the delinquent group were excluded due to refusal to complete the questionnaire or insufficient data quality. Analyses were conducted on the remaining sample (N = 42).

For each life domain, mean scores were calculated separately for positive affect (PA) and negative affect (NA) by averaging the corresponding PANAS items according to the original scale structure. These mean scores were used as outcome variables in the subsequent analyses.

To examine differences in affect across life domains and between groups, repeated measures analyses of variance (RM-ANOVA) were conducted with life domain as a within-subject factor and group (delinquent, psychiatric, non-clinical) as a between-subject factor.

Assumptions of sphericity were assessed using Mauchly’s test. When violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test.

For significant main effects, post hoc comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise tests. Effect sizes for the ANOVA main and interaction effects were calculated using generalized eta squared (η²G).

Results

Descriptives

The total sample comprised 48 adolescents (10 females, 20.83%). Each group (delinquent, psychiatric disorder, and comparison) comprised 16 participants, with a mean age of 16.4 years (SD = 1.31), as summarized in Table 3. No additional demographic data were collected. This was intentional, as the study focused on narrative life histories and the narrow age range (14–18 years) limited the analytical value of further variables. Moreover, collecting such data could have increased the risk of re-identification in small, potentially vulnerable subgroups and was therefore avoided in line with GDPR data minimization principles.

Qualitative analysis: life course domains

The relative category frequencies for the life course domains are visualized in Figs. 8, 9 and 10 in the appendix; corresponding codebooks are displayed in Tables 4, 5 and 6 in the appendix.

Childhood

Negatively connotated childhood experiences, particularly adverse relationship experiences with caregivers (5.68) and a generally burdened childhood (4.00), were most pronounced in the delinquent group. These experiences were reflected in statements indicating neglect and emotional deprivation, such as: “I always had to take care of myself.” (P34, Pos. 6) or “They [the parents] left me and my brother behind.” (P11, Pos. 3). Additionally, several participants summarized their early years in global negative terms (e.g., “Childhood was bad.” P9, Pos. 3). Most strikingly, this group uniquely reported involuntary dislocation & homelessness (2.31), illustrated by accounts such as: “… until they threw me out. [Then I] slept somewhere in a basement.” (P30, Pos. 9).

The psychiatric disorder group showed a more heterogeneous profile, combining adverse experiences with relational resources. Negative aspects included experiences of physical abuse (1.96), for example: “My dad became physically aggressive, with me too.” (P18, Pos. 20), as well as feelings of rejection (e.g., “I was an unwanted child. My parents let me know that.” P29, Pos. 15). At the same time, supportive caregiving relationships (3.03) were also reported, as reflected in statements such as: “I always felt at ease around my family.” (P43, Pos. 7).

In contrast, the comparison group predominantly described positive childhood experiences. This was particularly evident in the high frequency of carefree childhood narratives (5.65), often expressed in global positive terms (e.g., “Everything was perfect.” P22, Pos. 7), as well as in reports of supportive caregiving relationships (6.30), such as: “[My parents] always try to be an emotional support for me.” (P15, Pos. 6). These accounts were frequently complemented by descriptions of active embedded leisure activities (e.g., “So often outside, often rode bikes, also as a family…” P27, Pos. 3) (see Fig. 8/Table 4 in the appendix).

School

Negative school-related experiences, particularly being bullied (delinquent group: 1.12/psychiatric disorder group: 1.18; e.g., “… they often laughed at me and also tried to hit me.” P11, Pos. 16; “… I have been bullied because of my appearance.” P17, Pos. 10) and stressful school experiences (delinquent group: 1.12; “School was shit.” P9, Pos. 12; psychiatric disorder group: 1.56; “things at school went downhill.” P6, Pos. 14), were concentrated in the delinquent and psychiatric disorder groups.

In addition, the delinquent group reported perceived unfair blame (1.53; “… because I get punished for things that I actually didn’t do.” P2, Pos. 13) and effortless learning (1.02; “I never studied for a test… and it worked out.” P1, Pos. 39).

The comparison group was characterized by social integration (1.66; “I always had many friends.” P23, Pos. 18) and stress-free school years (1.39; “… I actually like school very much.” P26, Pos. 22). At the same time, this group reported the highest frequency of academic pressure (0.99; “I had to study a lot to get ahead.” P21, Pos. 16) (see Fig. 9/Table 5 in the appendix).

Daily life structure

The delinquent group’s profile was defined by risk-related categories, including delinquency (1.85; “[…] I robbed a person.” P10, Pos. 30), past incarceration (1.04; “I was in pre-trial detention for about two weeks.” P12, Pos. 85–86), and negative peer influence (0.93; “If I am there too often with them, then we’ll do shit again.” P11, Pos. 24). These were accompanied by reports of future goals (1.16; “My goals… would be to start an apprenticeship again with support.” P38, Pos. 71–72) and close friendships (1.40; “I only have one real friend.” P2, Pos. 21).

The psychiatric disorder group’s reports were dominated by psychological strain (1.80; “there is this psychological issue.” P19, Pos. 88), alongside close friendships (1.04; “I still have two real friends.” P6, Pos. 27).

The comparison group reported no negative categories, with a focus on structured daily routine/little leisure time (2.34; “My everyday life consists of school.” P47, Pos. 147) and well-being (1.40; “I’m pleased and have a lot of fun.” P23, Pos. 27) (see Fig. 10/Table 6 in the appendix).

Qualitative content analysis: value orientation and self-concept

The relative category frequencies for the domain value orientation (hierarchy levels 1 and 2) are illustrated in Figs. 11 and 12 in the appendix, with the corresponding codebook reported in Table 7. The third hierarchy level is not visualized due to insufficient frequencies. The domain self-concept is illustrated in Fig. 13 in the appendix, with its corresponding codebook provided in Table 8.

Value orientation

Nuclear family and close friendships emerged as the primary values across all cohorts, with the highest frequencies in the delinquent group (family: 6.04; “Family.” P33, Pos. 42; friends: 5.11; “… those are brothers to me, not friends.” P9, Pos. 29) within the first value hierarchy (Circle 1). Money was predominantly reported in the delinquent group at the primary (3.72; “Money, again money.” P9, Pos. 33) and secondary levels (1.53; “Money.” P33, Pos. 46), whereas in the psychiatric (1.32; “Money.” P8, Pos. 74) and comparison groups (1.73; “it is nice to have a lot of money…” P32, Pos. 152) it appeared at the secondary level (see Figs. 11 and 12; Table 7 in the appendix).

The comparison group was distinguished by societal, achievement-oriented, and transcendent values already present at the first hierarchical level, such as political and social engagement (1.17; “… I am highly engaged in politics.” P21, Pos. 31), knowledge (0.97; “knowledge belongs to circle one.” P7, Pos. 216–17), and religion (0.78; “… religion …” P25, Pos. 48).

The psychiatric disorder group showed similar relational values in Circle 1 (e.g., “My family.” P18, Pos. 116; “Friends are actually important to me.” P19, Pos. 110), but greater differentiation at the secondary level (Circle 2), particularly in creativity (3.19; “Drawing… is also important to me.” P14, Pos. 32), mobile phone/internet use (3.19; “being on the phone.” P44, Pos. 104), and addictive substances (2.39; “… alcohol. So that it’s fun.” P20, Pos. 32) (see Figs. 11 and 12; Table 7 in the appendix).

The third value hierarchy (Circle 3) was sparsely articulated across groups; given the low frequencies, apparent overrepresentations should be interpreted cautiously. We therefore renounced graphical representation of this level (for values and corresponding examples see Table 7 in the appendix).

Self-concept

Positive self-concepts were most common in the delinquent group, primarily reflecting interpersonal orientation (7.24; “I am charming… that’s a good trait.” VP9, Pos. 44; “I can listen well to others… I feel like a psychologist.” P13, Pos. 175) and goal-orientation/ambition (5.79; “I have this motivation to do sports… I look forward to it every time.” P38, Pos. 131–132). Negative self-concepts in this group were dominated by behavior problems (5.02; “I completely freak out.” P9, Pos. 48; “I don’t think before I do something…” P33, Pos. 63).

Negative self-concepts peaked in the psychiatric disorder group, characterized by health problems (mental & physical) (5.44; “I have so many health issues.” P8, Pos. 109; “I have an eating disorder…” P29, Pos. 83) and behavior problems (4.35; “I often shout when something doesn’t suit me.” VP20, Pos. 43).

The comparison group displayed a rather balanced profile of positive and negative self-concept, reflecting interpersonal orientation (4.10; “I am very empathetic.” P4, Pos. 44; “I am very helpful.” P25, Pos. 67), goal-orientation/ambition (2.82; “… my ambition in the things that interest me.” P37, Pos. 261–262), and personality-related weakness alongside motivational deficits (each 2.56; “I get into this negative thinking spiral.” P15, Pos. 109; “I don’t feel like doing things…” P4, Pos. 52) (see Fig. 13; Table 8 in the appendix).

Quantitative analysis of life course domains

Chi-square analyses revealed associations between group membership and categorical experiences across all domains (see Figs. 1, 2 and 3 for standardized residuals). As multiple coded segments may originate from the same participant, the assumption of independence underlying chi-square tests is not fully met. Therefore, results are interpreted as indicative of descriptive patterns rather than strict inferential group differences. Consequently, the chi-square results and standardized residuals are interpreted descriptively and are intended to illustrate distributional patterns.

Childhood

Chi-square analyses indicated differences in the distribution of categories within the domain of childhood, χ²(10) = 171.42, p <.001, with a moderate effect (Cramer’s V = 0.45). Standardized residuals suggested that unstable living situations (5.70) and adverse childhood experiences (4.20) occurred more frequently in the delinquent group. The psychiatric group showed a similar, though less pronounced, pattern, particularly for adverse childhood experiences (3.20), alongside fewer mentions of unstable living situations. In contrast, the comparison group showed a higher frequency of supportive childhood experiences (10.60) (see Fig. 1).

Daily life structure

Chi-square analyses indicated differences in the distribution of categories within the domain of daily life structure (χ²(12) = 159.18, p <.001), with a moderate effect size (Cramer’s V = 0.36). The psychiatric group was primarily characterized by a higher frequency of life dissatisfaction (6.15). In contrast, the comparison group showed a higher frequency of structured leisure activities (5.25).

Discussion

The present study aimed to gain insight into the developmental trajectories of youth with different burden profiles. Using a mixed-methods design, we generated in-depth accounts of life experiences across three groups: justice-involved youth (Group 1), youth with a psychiatric disorder (Group 2), and a comparison group without a history of psychiatric disorder or criminal offending (Group 3).

Both qualitative and quantitative findings indicate meaningful group differences alongside shared adversity. However, the delinquent group was characterized by particularly complex and cumulative developmental patterns.

The following interpretations draw on both the qualitative depth of the narratives and the frequency of category mentions. Category frequencies reflect how often a theme appeared across the interview material and should not be read as rankings of psychological importance within individual accounts. Where qualitative and quantitative patterns converge, findings are presented as mutually reinforcing; where they diverge, this is explicitly noted.

Residential and relational instability as a core differentiating pattern

Narratives from both the delinquent and psychiatric disorder groups reported a variety of ACEs, consistent with previous literature [7, 8]. While both groups share these strains, delinquent juveniles reported higher frequency of ACEs.

This pattern may be considered in relation to developmental taxonomic theory, which distinguishes between adolescence-limited and life-course-persistent antisocial behavior on the basis of differential risk exposure across development [16]. The cumulative and pervasive adversity documented in the delinquent group’s narratives can be tentatively interpreted as aligning with risk profiles often described in relation to life-course-persistent trajectories. Specifically, narratives from the delinquent group were characterized by unstable living situations, repeated transfers between caregiving institutions, and disrupted relational continuity. Participants frequently described these relocations as externally imposed decisions over which they had little or no control [52].

The quantitative comparisons reflect this pattern, indicating a higher frequency of unstable living situations in the delinquent group, elevated levels of ACEs both clinical groups, and predominantly supportive childhood experiences in the comparison group. Although these findings should be interpreted cautiously, they are consistent with the qualitative narrative patterns.

Research in out-of-home care contexts has shown that placement instability is associated with increased externalizing behavior [53]. Although direct links between placement changes and delinquency remain inconsistent, greater placement instability has been related to poorer inhibitory control and elevated oppositional behavior [54].

The participants’ accounts suggest experiences of instability and limited agency, which may complicate the development of stable relationships with caregivers. Consistent with this, attachment insecurity has frequently been observed in youth exposed to caregiving instability [55]. Repeated relational disruptions may therefore hinder the formation of stable attachment bonds and contribute to relational insecurity.

In addition to attachment-related mechanisms, these experiences may also be viewed through the framework of moral injury. This concept emphasizes the psychological impact of perceived betrayal by a protective or authority figure, resulting in the disruption of core moral beliefs about safety, trust, and caregiving [56]. However, as this construct was not directly assessed, this interpretation should be considered exploratory. In this sense, repeated and involuntary relocations, as described by participants, may extend beyond relational discontinuity and relate to broader perceptions of unreliability in caregiving systems.

School context: narcissist regulation and divergent coping patterns

In the school context, delinquent youth reported contradictory narratives regarding academic performance: while they described effortless learning and good academic achievement, they also reported school dropout, absenteeism and suspension, learning difficulties, and perceived unfair treatment. Participants often emphasized that they had the ability to succeed but experienced the school environment - especially imposed sanctions of teachers - as unfair.

Frequent accounts of perceived unfair blame further indicate a tendency to attribute academic difficulties to external factors rather than to personal shortcomings. This discrepancy may be interpreted as a compensatory form of self-regulation aimed at stabilizing vulnerable self-worth [57]. In this context, narcissistic regulation does not imply globally elevated self-esteem but rather a heightened need for positive social evaluation and status in the eyes of others. Empirical research in adolescents indicates that maladaptive narcissistic features are associated with conduct problems and delinquency [58].

Consistent with previous findings, the delinquent group presented low levels of school bonding, which was reflected in suspension and school dropout [59, 60]. School absenteeism, low academic performance, and disengagement from evaluative academic contexts have been widely documented as risk factors for delinquent behavior [61]. Although the present data do not permit conclusions about underlying motivation, withdrawal from academic settings may indicate difficulties in coping with performance-related demands [62].

In contrast, adolescents with psychiatric disorders reported that the psychological burden persisted within the school setting and was often exacerbated by bullying [63, 64]. Participant’s narratives were characterized by enduring victimization and internalized distress rather than retaliatory behavior, suggesting cumulative strain within the academic environment.

Although bullying was also reported in the delinquent group, participants more frequently described aggressive reactions. These responses may be interpreted as attempts to manage perceived social threat or loss of status through externalized behavior, which is consistent with models of narcissistic injury and compensatory aggression [65]. Previous research indicates that bullying—particularly social exclusion—can elicit distinct reaction patterns depending on underlying psychological vulnerabilities [66], which may account for the differing response pattern observed in the present study.

The comparison group described a generally stable school environment, although not free from academic pressure, reflecting high performance expectations—an aspect that has also been highlighted in previous empirical research [67].

Overall, the quantitative group comparisons were consistent with narrative patterns, indicating greater school-related instability in the delinquent group, sustained strain in the psychiatric group, and comparatively unstrained school experiences in the comparison group; again, these statistical findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Daily life: lack of structure and motivational ambivalence

Participants’ narratives of daily lives of were strongly characterized by offending behavior, experiences of detention, and unstructured peer-related leisure time within both close and more casual friendships, including exposure to negative peer influences. Participants frequently described spending time in unstructured social settings, often in connection with risk-related activities. This qualitative pattern was also quantitatively mirrored in a trend towards overrepresentation of delinquency and negative peer influence in this group. These findings are consistent with research linking unstructured socializing and deviant peer affiliation to delinquent behavior [18, 20, 68].

While the comparison group also reported unstructured activities, these activities were embedded within an otherwise structured routine, including regular school attendance and organized extracurricular engagement. In contrast, the delinquent group lacked such structural anchors [69].

At the same time, delinquent youth demonstrated emerging self-reflection, particularly in distancing themselves from past criminal behavior. Although future goals were articulated and future orientation has been empirically identified as a protective factor [70], narratives also conveyed elements of resignation and perceived limited personal agency. Participants’ accounts suggest a tension between articulated future aspirations and perceived opportunities to realize them. Within this context, criminal behavior may present one of several available behavioral options, particularly when alternative pathways are perceived as limited [71].

The observed distancing from past offenses may be interpreted as reflecting early cognitive shifts, which have been described in desistance research as relevant to behavioral change. According to narrative and cognitive transformation models, sustained desistance requires a fundamental redefinition of the self away from an “offender” identity [72]. This pattern resonates with qualitative findings from MST research, in which some youths were described as beginning to address the challenges of emerging adulthood while others remained on trajectories with limited forward movement [5].

The quantitative results partly diverged from the qualitative narratives concerning well-being in the psychiatric disorder group. Although some participants described specific activities associated with “feeling well,” these individual references became less prominent when proportional group distributions were considered and life dissatisfaction accumulated. This finding is consistent with previous literature showing that life satisfaction is often reduced in adolescents with psychiatric disorders [73]. This divergence reflects the different levels of analysis inherent in the mixed-methods design. While the quantitative aggregation captures overall tendencies at the group level, the qualitative data provide context-sensitive and situational insights into individual experiences. Accordingly, the discrepancy does not indicate a contradiction but rather suggests that positive experiences may occur in specific contexts despite an overall elevated burden in daily life.

Self-concept and value orientation: peer-contextualized self-evaluation and stereotypical values

The narrative patterns observed in the school and daily life structure domains were also reflected in the self-concepts of delinquent youth. On the one hand, they described themselves predominantly in positive social terms, emphasizing characteristics such as interpersonal orientation. Although such descriptions might initially appear to reflect stable self-esteem, they may rather represent self-regulatory effort to maintain a coherent and socially acceptable self-image [57].

In line with research on adolescent narcissistic features, positive self-descriptions in youth with conduct problems have been interpreted as strategies to protect vulnerable self-worth, particularly under conditions of social evaluation or failure [58]. In this sense, the pronounced emphasis on socially desirable traits may be less indicative of stable self-esteem and has been described as potentially in nature.

At the same time, initial forms of self-reflection may be indicated by the acknowledgment of behavior problems (e.g. being aggressive, impulsivity) as a negative personal characteristic. The coexistence of positive characteristics and social self-ascriptions and critical evaluation of externalized behavior suggest an ambivalent and potentially transitional self-concept. Prior research has described cognitive distancing from past offending and the emergence of goal orientations as early cognitive shifts that may precede behavioral change [72].

In contrast, the comparison group’s self-descriptions appeared to reflect more differentiated processes of self-concept development. Their narratives neither emphasized exaggeratedly positive nor markedly negative attributes, potentially accounting a comparatively balanced and more stable self-representation [74].

Within the psychiatric disorder group, a tendency toward interpersonal orientation as a personal strength could also be observed. However, in contrast to the delinquent group, this pattern co-occurred with reports of ongoing health problems, functional limitations related to their condition, and low satisfaction with physical appearance. This pattern is consistent with the substantial impact of mental disorders on everyday functioning, particularly during adolescence [75].

Particularly striking were the differences in value orientation across groups. In contrast to theoretical approaches that primarily link specific value types to delinquent behavior [29], the present findings suggest that the structural organization and differentiation of values may be more informative than their nominal category is.

Across all groups, the nuclear family and close friendships emerged as the most salient values within the primary hierarchy, indicating the central role of close relationships. This is particularly noteworthy in the clinical groups, as a substantial proportion of participants reported adverse relational experiences during childhood and, in the delinquent group, additional residential instability.

The finding could be interpreted in light of developmental research: the prominence of relational values despite such experiences aligns with research emphasizing the importance of relatedness and peer relationships during adolescence [76]. At the same time, the narratives suggest that, in the present sample, these values may be shaped in the context of relational disruptions and may reflect an ongoing orientation toward belonging [77].

In the delinquent group, these core values were complemented by material and activity-related categories such as money, sport, and music. This pattern reflects the presence of both relational and material or activity-related value domains. From a social learning perspective, such value constellations have been described as being shaped within peer contexts, where values such as status or financial success may gain relevance, even when they diverge from broader societal norms [18, 78, 79].

In contrast, the psychiatric disorder group displayed a more heterogeneous and less concentrated value structure, including a broader range of domains such as media use, substance-related activities, creativity, and sport. Some of these domains have been discussed in the literature in relation to coping and regulation processes in the context of psychological distress [80, 81]. At the same time, the coexistence of activity-related and more structured domains reflects a broader distribution across value categories rather than a clearly prioritized hierarchy.

The comparison group was characterized by the presence of additional value domains already at the primary level, including political and social engagement as well as relational aspects such as love and companionship. These domains have been linked to prosocial orientations and broader social integration in adolescence [30, 31].

Emotional processing across interview domains

Overall positive affect varied across life domains, with lower levels in the school domain compared to childhood, daily life, and value orientation, a pattern that was consistent across groups. Across the interview narratives, the psychiatric disorder group exhibited significantly lower levels of positive affect compared to both the delinquent and comparison groups, whereas the latter two did not differ. Despite similarly reported ACEs, the delinquent group showed higher levels of positive affect than the psychiatric group. This difference suggests that comparable background experiences may be associated with different patterns of emotional expression.

While this pattern may indicate differences in emotional processing or expression, such interpretations remain tentative, as the present data do not allow conclusions about the relationship between expressed affect and underlying emotional states. Previous research has described inconsistencies between affective expression and experiential background (e.g. [22]); however, the extent to which these findings apply to the present results cannot be determined.

Regarding negative affect, both the delinquent and psychiatric groups reported significantly higher levels than the comparison group, while not differing from each other. Negative affect was highest in the value orientation and self-concept domains and varied across groups depending on the domain, as indicated by a significant interaction effect.

These findings are consistent with research linking elevated negative affect in adolescence to both internalizing and externalizing difficulties [82]. At the same time, the present results indicate that such differences are not only group-specific but also vary across life domains, suggesting a domain-related pattern of affect. However, this interpretation should be made with caution, as affect was assessed repeatedly within a single interview context, and carry-over effects between domains or influences of general mood cannot be ruled out.

Conclusion

The present findings carry important implications for clinical practice. By using a narrative approach that does not explicitly prompt themes such as morality, attachment, or value orientation, this study provides insight into how adolescents themselves structure and prioritize their developmental experiences. The spontaneous emergence of instability and strong peer embeddedness in the delinquent group suggests that these relational contexts are central to how participants organize and narrate their experiences.

Our findings indicate that juvenile offenders present with a complex and potentially fragile self-concept. Although they attribute to themselves social and performance-related competencies, these are not consistently reflected in their everyday functioning. Interventions that are predominantly deficit-oriented may therefore trigger defensiveness and avoidance and should be applied cautiously.

Furthermore, the findings point towards the relevance of the moral injury framework for understanding delinquent developmental trajectories. Repeated experiences of involuntary relocation, disrupted caregiving relationships, and perceived exclusion from decision-making processes may not only undermine attachment security but also disrupt fundamental assumptions about reliability and protection by adult authorities. From this perspective, delinquent behavior may partly reflect adaptations to perceived betrayal or moral disorientation rather than mere norm rejection. Therapeutic approaches that focus exclusively on behavioral correction or norm compliance risk overlooking these underlying experiences of relational and moral rupture. Clinically, the provision of stable, enduring therapeutic relationships appears essential, given the destabilizing impact of frequent changes in care and residential placements. Cross-setting child and adolescent psychiatric care—with consistent key figures across detention, post-release, and longer-term follow-up—may be particularly beneficial.

These conclusions should not be read as a rejection of evidence-based approaches such as MST or FFT, whose effectiveness in reducing offending is well established [19, 36, 37, 40]. Rather, the present findings suggest that these approaches may be further strengthened when they are combined with sensitivity to adolescents’ subjective value systems and relational histories, particularly in contexts where systemic resources such as family involvement are limited. The contribution of the present study lies in identifying mechanisms that could inform the integration of values-based elements into existing structured interventions, not in replacing them.

The value orientation patterns further suggest that interventions may benefit from not exclusively focusing on externally defined normative standards. Instead, therapeutic work may benefit from facilitating reflective elaboration and the integration of personally meaningful values within a broader societal framework. Therapeutic approaches that emphasize individual strengths and create opportunities for experiences congruent with youths’ self-concepts may enhance engagement and treatment responsiveness. Supporting adolescents in articulating and internalizing self-endorsed values—rather than prescribing them—may reduce defensive self-regulation and enhance sustainable behavioral change.

Finally, despite relatively homogeneous value patterns among juvenile offenders, the present findings support individualized, values-based treatment planning rather than standardized intervention models.

The coexistence of articulated goals and perceived limitations suggests that the subjective experience of agency deserves further attention in future research. Particularly in developmental contexts marked by externally imposed transitions and relational instability, the experience of self-efficacy and participation may play a relevant role.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when the findings are interpreted. Although the combined qualitative and quantitative approach allowed for a nuanced understanding of subjective experiences, values, and self-concepts, the analysis relied on retrospective self-reports that may be influenced by recall bias, situational factors, and self-presentation. As a result, the reported experiences should be understood as subjective reconstructions rather than as objective accounts of developmental events, which limits the ability to draw conclusions about actual developmental trajectories or causal processes.

The qualitative analysis also entails interpretive constraints. The identification and interpretation of themes are inherently shaped by analytic decisions, meaning that the findings reflect patterns constructed through systematic analysis rather than direct representations of participants’ experiences. While efforts were made to ensure transparency and consistency, the results should therefore be understood as interpretative rather than purely descriptive, and alternative interpretations of the material remain possible.

In addition, thematic emphasis in narrative interviews does not necessarily correspond to the frequency of coded categories. Frequently coded categories do not automatically indicate greater personal significance, as some experiences were described in depth while others were mentioned only briefly. Consequently, category frequencies should be interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with narrative context, which limits their use for ranking or quantifying psychological relevance across participants.

The large thematic breadth of the data enabled a differentiated description of group-specific patterns but limited the possibility of strong quantitative aggregation. Accordingly, the quantitative findings should be interpreted as supportive rather than confirmatory, and the group comparisons primarily as exploratory indications rather than definitive differences, which restricts generalizability and precludes strong inferential conclusions.

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. The narrative interview approach allowed for an in-depth and content-driven exploration of how adolescents interpret, understand, and make sense of their lives. Rather than responding to predefined categories, participants were able to elaborate on personal experiences, enabling access to implicit value systems, self-concept dynamics, and relational experiences that may remain less visible in standardized assessments. This approach complements existing research by providing insight into how developmental experiences are subjectively organized and interpreted, particularly in populations with complex burden profiles.
