Diverging trends in the incidence and mortality of acute severe trauma in Korea, 2020–2024: an analysis based on the National Emergency Department Information System (NEDIS) data and the 2024 healthcare crisis
Authors: Sunju Kim, Oh Hyun Kim, Myeong Il Cha, Jung Eon Kim, Seung Joon Lee
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Trauma is a leading cause of death among young and middle-aged adults worldwide and, as a prototypical time-sensitive emergency condition, imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden through mortality, long-term disability, and loss of productivity. In Korea, injuries rank among the major causes of death, and severe trauma is recognized as an important public health issue that consumes substantial medical resources across emergency department care, hospitalization, rehabilitation, and long-term care, while also diminishing the quality of life of patients and their families [1,2].

In Korea, the National Emergency Department Information System (NEDIS) was established as a nationwide system that comprehensively collects and manages data on emergency department visits and provides essential evidence for surveillance and policymaking for major time-sensitive emergencies, including acute severe trauma. Alongside NEDIS, several other data sources, such as the National Injury Information Portal operated by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, community-based severe trauma surveys, and the Korea Trauma Data Bank (KTDB), provide information on injury incidence, severe trauma, mechanisms of injury, and patterns of care. Together, these data sources form the foundation for evaluating the trauma system at the national level [3].

Recent studies from Korea and abroad suggest that population aging is emerging as one of the most important structural determinants of the epidemiology and clinical course of severe trauma. Older adults aged 65 years and above are more likely to experience severe trauma even after relatively low-energy mechanisms, such as same-level falls or pedestrian traffic crashes. Because of reduced physiological reserve, multiple comorbidities, and frailty, they have worse outcomes than younger patients despite comparable anatomical injury severity [4]. This pattern has been repeatedly observed in Korean trauma registry and emergency department–based data, supporting the view that geriatric trauma should be managed as a distinct high-risk subgroup.

Meanwhile, multiple injury prevention strategies, including road traffic safety policies, occupational safety regulations, and fall prevention programs, have contributed to reductions in the incidence of some types of injury. Nevertheless, the burden of death and disability associated with severe trauma remains substantial. Injury-related mortality continues to rank among the leading causes of death in younger age groups in Korea, and its concentration in the most economically productive age ranges means that its impact on the healthcare system and on society at large is particularly severe [5].

Against this background, there is growing demand for the Korean emergency and trauma care system to move beyond simply reducing the occurrence of severe trauma and instead focus on minimizing death and severe disability after injury through coordinated prehospital and in-hospital care. The international literature highlights several key components for improving outcomes in severe trauma: appropriate triage and transport in the prehospital phase, rapid transfer to designated regional trauma centers, standardized implementation of damage control resuscitation and massive transfusion protocols, and networks of trauma centers with sufficient patient volume and experience. These elements are increasingly cited as important reference points in discussions about reforming the Korean trauma system [6,7].

Building on these domestic and international discussions of severe trauma epidemiology and trauma system organization, this commentary aims to describe recent trends in acute severe trauma in Korea using the NEDIS data and explore the implications of health system crises for trauma care and trauma performance indicators. Furthermore, by considering structural factors such as population aging, regional disparities, and the configuration of human and material resources, we seek to identify weaknesses in the Korean trauma system and outline future policy and research priorities, thereby providing a basis for constructing a more resilient system of care for patients with acute severe trauma.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Medical Center (No. NMC-2023-08-094). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the use of deidentified data and the retrospective nature of the study.

Data sources

We conducted a retrospective secondary analysis using the NEDIS database, which is overseen by the Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare and managed by the National Emergency Medical Center. NEDIS serves as a nationwide repository that captures clinical and administrative data for emergency department visits across Korea. The study population included patients registered between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. Severe trauma was defined as cases with an International Classification of Diseases–based Injury Severity Score (ICISS) below 0.9, according to the 2020 criteria, indicating a predicted survival probability of less than 90%. The analysis focused on the acute phase, specifically from the time of injury to 24 hours after admission, and excluded visits primarily related to postinjury complications or subsequent follow-up care. To ensure data consistency and quality, the scope of the study was limited to level I and level II emergency departments, defined as regional and local emergency medical centers. During the data extraction and processing phase, all regional trauma centers were integrated and coded as level I institutions to ensure standardized classification.

KEY OBSERVATIONS

Characteristics of acute severe trauma patients, 2020–2024

From 2020 to 2024, a total of 359,929 patients with acute severe trauma (ICISS <0.9) were identified in NEDIS. Children aged 0–17 years accounted for 23,040 patients (6.4%), adults aged 18–64 years for 172,570 (47.9%), and older adults aged ≥65 years for 164,318 (45.7%), indicating that nearly half of all severe trauma patients in Korea are now older adults. The mean age was 58.6±22.2 years, and 232,480 patients (64.6%) were male.

The median time from injury to emergency department arrival was 1.1 hours (interquartile range, 0.7–3.4 hours). Patients were treated at level I (n=156,352, 43.4%) and level II emergency departments (n=203,577, 56.6%). Regarding emergency department disposition, 18.6% were discharged home, 72.2% were admitted (40.4% to general wards and 31.8% to intensive care units), 6.9% were transferred out, and 2.1% died in the emergency department. These findings reaffirm that the acute severe trauma population in Korea consists predominantly of high-risk patients who require admission and often intensive care and that severe trauma has already shifted from a condition predominantly affecting young men to a condition affecting a rapidly aging population with multiple comorbidities.

Five-year trends in the overall cohort

The age- and sex-standardized incidence of overall acute severe trauma was 135.2 per 100,000 persons in 2020, 134.9 in 2021, and 135.4 in 2022, showing little variation, before increasing transiently to 143.0 in 2023 and then decreasing markedly to 121.5 in 2024 (P for trend=0.513) (Fig. 1A). Compared with that in 2023, the incidence rate in 2024 decreased by approximately 15%, indicating that the overall population-level burden of acute severe trauma clearly declined.

However, the age- and sex-standardized mortality rate followed a different pattern: it gradually decreased from 68.9 to 66.0, 65.6, and 61.3 per 100,000 persons between 2020 and 2023, but then rose to 68.4 per 100,000 persons in 2024 (P for trend=0.625) (Fig. 1B). In-hospital mortality, defined as the number of deaths divided by the number of hospital visits, also increased from 6.70% in 2020, 6.52% in 2021, 6.88% in 2022, and 6.60% in 2023 to 7.25% in 2024, with 2024 showing the highest value during the 5-year period (P for trend=0.242) (Fig. 1C). In other words, although the number of acute severe trauma cases in the population decreased, the risk of death among patients with acute severe trauma who reached the hospital paradoxically increased.

Age-stratified analyses made this discrepancy even clearer. Among adults aged 18–64 years, the age- and sex-standardized incidence rate decreased from 103.1 per 100,000 persons in 2023 to 86.6 per 100,000 in 2024, a relative reduction of approximately 16%. During the same period, however, in-hospital mortality increased from 5.25% to 6.13%, representing a 16.8% relative increase, and the age- and sex-standardized mortality rate rose from 40.8 to 49.1 per 100,000 persons.

Children aged 0–17 years accounted for only approximately 6% of all acute severe trauma cases. In this group, the incidence rate decreased slightly from 70.6 to 65.4 per 100,000 persons between 2023 and 2024, and in-hospital mortality remained low and stable (1.97% vs. 1.94%). The standardized mortality rate increased from 6.56 to 7.67 per 100,000 persons, but the absolute level remained low; these findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Among older adults aged ≥65 years, the age- and sex-standardized incidence rate decreased from 397.8 per 100,000 persons in 2023 to 338.7 per 100,000 in 2024, a reduction of approximately 15%. Nevertheless, in-hospital mortality increased from 8.46% to 8.92%, and the age- and sex-standardized mortality rate remained high, increasing slightly from 75.1 to 75.4 per 100,000 persons. These results are consistent with previous reports showing that older trauma patients have a higher risk of death because of reduced physiological reserve and multiple comorbidities.

DISCUSSION

This study used NEDIS data from 2020 to 2024 to reexamine the epidemiology and outcomes of acute severe trauma (ICISS <0.9) in Korea. Among 359,929 patients, adults accounted for 47.9% and older adults for 45.7%, indicating that severe trauma in Korea has already shifted from a condition predominantly affecting young men to one that increasingly affects an aging population with multiple comorbidities (Table 1). Pediatric patients represented only 6.4% of the cohort.Adult patients had the highest proportion of men, whereas older adults were more likely to arrive by public emergency medical services, hospital, or other ambulances, had longer emergency department stays, and were more likely to require admission or interhospital transfer (Fig. 2). These age-group-specific patterns underscore that the Korean trauma system must address the distinct needs of three different populations rather than those of a single “average” trauma patient.

As shown in Fig. 1, the age- and sex-standardized incidence of acute severe trauma remained relatively stable at approximately 135 per 100,000 persons from 2020 to 2022, increased transiently to 143.0 in 2023, and then declined markedly to 121.5 in 2024. This approximately 15% reduction between 2023 and 2024 persisted after adjustment for age and sex, suggesting a clear decline in the population-level volume of acute severe trauma. Age-stratified analyses showed similar trends in both adults and older adults, with incidence among older adults decreasing from 397.8 to 338.7 per 100,000 persons over the same period (Fig. 3). These changes may reflect the effects of road traffic and occupational safety policies, fall prevention efforts, and postpandemic mobility shifts, further compounded by restricted emergency department accessibility and reduced patient visits during the 2024 physician workforce dispute.

In contrast, mortality indicators moved in the opposite direction. Age- and sex-standardized mortality decreased gradually from 2020 to 2023 but rose again in 2024 to a level comparable to that in 2020, and in-hospital mortality was highest in 2024 across the 5-year study period (Fig. 1). When stratified by age group, pediatric mortality remained very low and stable throughout the study period, whereas adults and older adults consistently showed higher mortality, higher intensive care unit admission rates, and higher transfer rates (Figs. 2, 3). The persistently high standardized mortality and in-hospital mortality among older adults, despite the decrease in incidence, identify geriatric trauma as a critical target for outcome improvement within the Korean trauma system.

Several factors may explain this apparent divergence between declining incidence and stagnant or worsening mortality. As the population ages, the proportion of patients with reduced physiological reserve, multiple comorbidities, and frailty increases, contributing to higher mortality even at similar levels of anatomical injury severity [8–10]. In this context, the ICISS <0.9 definition may increasingly identify patients with more complex physiological vulnerability. Changes in diagnostic coding or injury mechanism profiles over time may also have altered the case mix of severe trauma. In addition, prehospital delays, inefficiencies in interfacility transfer, limited trauma team experience, and constraints on operating room and intensive care unit resources are plausible system-level contributors to higher mortality, particularly among older adults and patients with polytrauma.

The healthcare instability in 2024, driven by disputes over physician workforce policy, resident and fellow attrition, and partial reductions in emergency department, operating room, and intensive care unit capacity at some tertiary hospitals, represents one potential contextual factor that may have influenced these mortality patterns [11]. It is conceivable that, during this period, presentations of minor trauma decreased while delays in definitive care for severe trauma increased because of resource limitations. However, the present analysis relied on annual secondary NEDIS data and could not distinguish periods with and without labor disputes or directly link hospital-level staffing and bed availability to patient outcomes. Consequently, causal relationships between the 2024 healthcare crisis and changes in trauma mortality cannot be established. More granular time-series analyses and stratified studies by hospital type and region are needed to clarify these effects.

A key strength of this work is the comprehensive use of NEDIS, which captures virtually all emergency medical institutions in Korea, to present age- and sex-standardized incidence and mortality rates, in-hospital mortality rates, and age-group-specific clinical and resource-use patterns (Table 1, Fig. 2) [3]. These data provide an important foundation for interpreting trauma system performance in the context of demographic change, regional disparities, and the configuration of human and material resources. Nevertheless, important limitations remain. The definition of severe trauma was based solely on ICISS, and detailed clinical information, such as physiological parameters, operative procedures, and granular injury mechanisms, was limited [12]. Risk-adjusted, hospital-level performance metrics could not be derived, and structural characteristics of the trauma system, including trauma center designation, surgical and intensive care unit capacity, and integration with prehospital services, were not incorporated into the analysis.

Despite these limitations, the findings indicate that the Korean trauma system must move beyond quantitative goals of reducing case numbers and instead focus on qualitative goals of improving survival and functional outcomes, particularly among older adults. Routine use of NEDIS-based age- and sex-standardized incidence and mortality rates and in-hospital mortality as trauma quality indicators, combined with linkage to more detailed clinical registries such as the KTDB to develop risk-adjusted outcome measures, would enable more precise monitoring and targeted quality improvement [13]. On this foundation, geriatric-focused trauma strategies, efforts to reduce regional disparities, and system safeguards to protect essential trauma services during future shocks could help the Korean trauma system evolve into a more resilient model that supports survival and recovery for patients with acute severe trauma.
