Incidence and Predictors of Successful Lead Extraction With Short Active Mechanical Extraction Sheaths Only
Authors: Caroline Wiederkehr, Noah Kuster, Michelle Bebié, Jan Steffel, Andreas Haeberlin, Alexander Breitenstein, Daniel Hofer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTransvenous lead extraction (TLE) is increasingly performed for malfunction or infection of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED). Active mechanical rotational sheaths facilitate extraction and are available in short and long sizes, but it is unclear how many patients short active mechanical extraction sheaths (SAMES) are sufficient for TLE, and in which patients they may facilitate TLE. This study aimed to evaluate the incidence and predictors of successful lead extraction using only SAMES.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent TLE at the University Hospital Zurich from 2013 until 2023 regarding the use of short or long active mechanical extraction sheaths, as well as clinical and radiological success and complications.ResultsA total of 568 TLEs were performed from January 2013 until December 2023. In 55 cases (9.7%), TLE was successful using only SAMES. Independent predictors for successful lead extraction using only SAMES in multivariate analysis were shorter lead dwell time (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, p < 0.001) and use of anticoagulation (OR: 0.44, 95% CI: 0.19–1.00, p = 0.05). Complications occurred at a lower rate when only SAMES were used (All complications 12.7% vs. 24.2%, minor complications 9.1% vs. 18.7%, perioperative death 0% vs. 1.7%).ConclusionIn 10% of patients undergoing TLE, SAMES are sufficient for successful lead extraction. SAMES may be a sensible choice as the primary extraction tool in patients with chronic anticoagulation and shorter lead dwell time, as these factors predict a higher likelihood of success in TLE with SAMES only.

Introduction

With the increasing number of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED), such as pacemakers, defibrillators, and devices to restore cardiac resynchronization therapy, there is also an increasing number of transvenous lead extractions (TLEs) due to lead malfunctions and infections [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. In cases where simple traction on the leads is not enough for successful TLE, specialized extraction tools such as active mechanical rotational or laser sheaths are used. These tools are safe and effective when used in high‐volume centers [7]. The two commercially available bidirectional active rotational mechanical extraction sheaths (Evolution® RL, Cook Medical, and Tightrail™, Philips) are available in short and long length versions, both having been proven to be safe and effective in TLE. The short active mechanical sheaths (Evolution® Shortie RL, Cook and Tightrail™ Sub‐C, Philips) are primarily designed to overcome fibrosis and calcifications at the vessel entry site of the device leads [8, 9, 10, 11]. However, it is unclear how many patients short active mechanical extraction sheaths (SAMES) are sufficient for TLE and in which patients SAMES are likely to facilitate TLE. This study aimed to evaluate the incidence and predictors of successful TLE using only SAMES.

Methods

Study Design

We retrospectively analyzed all patients undergoing TLE at the University Hospital Zurich from the beginning of 2013 until December 2023. Every TLE procedure was considered as a separate entity to allow for multiple TLEs within the same patient to be included in the study, as long as the second TLE procedure was not performed during the same hospital stay. As such, multiple leads were sometimes extracted during one TLE procedure. When only short and no long active mechanical sheaths were used during the entire procedure, we classified this event as “short sheaths only.” Lead explantation procedures (see 4.3.3 Definitions) were excluded from the analysis, as were patients undergoing lead extraction during open chest surgical procedures. Due to the retrospective design, the selection of extraction tools and their sizes was left to the operator's discretion, with only active mechanical rotational lead extraction tools being utilized during the study period. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (BASEC‐NR: 2018‐01540).

Extraction Procedure

The TLE was performed in a stepwise approach: when simple traction failed, lead locking stylets were inserted and TLE performed with mechanical rotating sheaths starting with a short sheath (TightrailTM Sub‐C, Philips, or Evolution® Shortie RL, Cook Medical) for vascular entry followed by switching to a long sheath (TightrailTM, Philips, or Evolution® RL, Cook Medical) if necessary for the remaining TLE. During the study period, we routinely used short extraction sheaths during every beginning of a lead extraction before switching to the longer extraction sheath once vessel entry had been achieved (visualized by passing of the short extraction sheath along the targeted lead beyond the clavicle). The choice of short extraction sheath manufacturer as well as size (9 F/11 F/13 F) was left to the discretion of the operator (see Figure 1 for examples of short and long active mechanical sheaths).

Data Collection and Analysis

Each TLE was individually evaluated to assess success, failure, and complications. Data collection was carried out by reviewing operation reports, discharge summaries, and chest X‐rays. Complications were assessed within 30 days following TLE. All patient data was anonymized. For statistical analysis, R version 4.1.2 for Windows (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) was used. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). A Mann–Whitney U‐test was used to compare procedural and outcome data. Proportions were compared using Fisher's exact test. To identify predictors for successful extraction using short sheaths only, uni and multivariate logistic regression models were fitted. Variables for the multivariate analysis were selected a priori based on previously described factors, which may impede TLE. Moreover, procedure‐related variables were assessed. The full multivariate model included all variables from the univariate models with a p < 0.1. An α‐level of ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Definitions

Definitions are shown in Table 1 and are identical to those previously used and published by our group [12]. They were designed to align as closely as possible with the 2009 and 2017 Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) expert consensus and the 2018 European Heart Rhythm Society (EHRA) expert consensus statement on lead extraction, as well as with those used in large multicenter registries to allow for meaningful comparison [7, 13, 14, 15]. However, due to incomplete documentation in patient records, certain modifications were made in comparison to these expert consensus statements and multicenter registries. First, we did not differentiate between postprocedural and intraprocedural complications due to a significant lack of data availability in patient charts. Second, as the attribution of complications can be challenging—especially when patients undergo additional procedures during their hospital stay—we opted to report all complications identified in the medical charts during the hospital stay for TLE. As in the EHRA and HRS expert consensus statements, clinical success of TLE could be achieved without radiological success, since clinical success and failure of TLE are primarily determined by the indication for TLE. In cases of infection, only complete CIED removal was considered a clinical success in our study, although a residual lead of < 4 cm may be acceptable according to the consensus. For non‐infection‐related indications, clinical success was still considered even with a remnant of > 4 cm, provided the remaining lead was unlikely to increase the risk of complications (e.g., vascular obstruction) and there were no major procedure‐related complications [7]. Death following TLE was tracked for up to 30 days after the procedure in this study. The short sheaths only group consisted of patients where TLE was completed with the usage of only SAMES, whereas the short and long sheaths group consisted of patients where TLE was completed with short and long active mechanical rotational extraction sheaths.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 556 Patients underwent transvenous extraction of 1081 leads during 568 interventions from January 2013 to December 2023 (Table 2). In 12 patients, TLE was performed twice during the study period. Mean age was 67 years (56–76 years), and 193 (34%) were pacemaker‐dependant. Main indication for TLE was lead dysfunction in 304 cases (53.5%), followed by systemic infection (97 cases, 17.1%) and device upgrade (72 cases, 12.7%). The mean number of extracted leads per intervention was 2. Mean lead dwell time was 7.8 years (4.4–12.4 years) (Table 2). A total of 422 cases were performed with short and long extraction sheaths, 91 cases were performed without extraction sheaths, and in 55 cases (9.7%), only short sheaths were used for TLE. Mean age in the short sheath only cohort was 69 years (60–78 years), and the main indication for TLE was lead dysfunction (31 cases, 56.4%). The mean number of extracted leads by SAMES was 2. Patients in the short sheaths only group were older (69 years vs. 65 years, p = 0.046), had a higher incidence of Diabetes (30.9% vs. 19.0%, p = 0.049), had significantly lower lead dwelling time (4.4 vs. 9.3 years, p < 0.001) and significantly lower amount of device interventions after implantation (40% vs. 60.2%, p = 0.002) (Table 2).

Outcome

In the overall cohort, 525 out of 568 TLE (92.4%) resulted in complete radiological success, and 22 TLE (3.9%) resulted in partial radiological success, resulting in a combined radiological success of 96.3%. Complete radiological success (98.2% vs. 90.3%, p = 0.07) and clinical success (96.4% vs. 93.1%, p = 0.56) was numerically higher in the SAMES group but without statistical significance (Table 3). In the SAMES group, only 1/55 TLE (1.8%) resulted in radiological failure due to the inability to remove the whole lead with a significant remnant left in the patient. Clinical success was achieved in 53/55 Patients (96.4%) in the SAMES group; clinical failure occurred in two Patients (3.6%), with two deaths within 30 days (one due to occlusion of the abdominal aorta, another with COVID). General anesthesia was significantly less often performed when only SAMES were used (74.5% vs. 91.9%, p < 0.001). Complications occurred in 120 of 568 TLEs (21.1%), of which 93 (16.4%) were minor, 27 (4.8%) were major, 13 (2.3%) resulted in death within 30 days and 8 (1.4%) resulted in perioperative death. Major (3.6% vs. 5.5%, p = 0.76) and minor complications (9.1% vs. 18.7%, p = 0.09) occurred in a numerically lower rate in the SAMES group but without statistical significance. There were no perioperative deaths in the SAMES group compared to 7 (1.7%) in the short and long sheaths group. Complications in cases where only SAMES were used consisted of two major complications (3.6%, one postoperative death because of occlusion of the abdominal aorta, one postoperative death because of sepsis with Staph aureus and COVID). Minor complications in the SAMES group consisted of two cases (3.6%) of electrode dislodgement with need for revision, two cases (3.6%) of hematoma without need of intervention, and one case (1.5%) of upper extremity thrombosis.

Predictors of Successful Extraction Using Only Short Sheaths

Discussion

In this single‐center retrospective analysis, we demonstrate that 10% (55/563) of TLE could be successfully completed with the use of SAMES only with high rates of clinical (96.4%) and radiological (98.2%) success. Lower lead dwelling time (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, p < 0.001) and chronic anticoagulation (OR: 0.44, 95% CI: 0.19–1.0, p = 0.05) may predict successful TLE with SAMES only.

TLE is typically performed for malfunction or infection of lead‐based CIED [16, 17]. Because of increasing numbers of CIED implantation in the last decades, the number of performed TLE has similarly increased (i.e., in our center from 15 cases in 2013 to > 100 cases in 2024) [12]. We previously reported on potentially lower rates of successful TLE and higher complication rates in newly developing high‐volume centers, because of lower operator and anaesthesiologic experience, a trend towards non‐mandatory indications for TLE, as well as an increasingly comorbid and complex patient population being evaluated for TLE [12, 18]. In the beginnings of TLE, simple traction was the only available technique, with the development of passive mechanical sheaths and the principle of countertraction shortly after [19, 20]. These passive mechanical sheaths provided rigidity to apply force and break the tissue surrounding the lead, but the lack of flexibility limited the utility. The development of active sheaths (mechanical, laser, radiofrequency) further facilitated the extraction procedure and allowed for successful TLE in leads with increased lead welling time [21, 22, 23]. Both commercially available active mechanical rotational extraction sheaths (Cook Evolution® RL, Philips Tightrail™) have been shown to be safe and effective for TLE with clinical success rates > 95% [8, 9, 10, 11, 24, 25, 26, 27]. While there are short and long versions of these mechanical extraction sheaths available, it has been unclear in which patients SAMES may facilitate or even suffice for TLE.

Patient Selection for TLE With SAMES

SAMES were primarily designed to overcome the difficulties of venous access in cases of extensive scarred or calcified tissue around the target cardiac lead at the vessel entry point. As such, SAMES may not always be necessary for successful TLE. However, in some patients, SAMES may suffice to complete TLE. We previously demonstrated high success rates and low complication rates with the routine use of SAMES at the beginning of a TLE procedure in an early analysis of 45 patients [28]. In the current study, analyzing all 568 performed TLE at our institution, we now demonstrate that up to 10% of TLE can be completed with the use of only SAMES, with a combined radiological success rate of 98.2% and a clinical success rate of 96.4%. Furthermore, we demonstrate that shorter lead dwelling time (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, p < 0.001) and the use of anticoagulation (OR: 0.44, 95% CI: 0.19–1.0, p = 0.05) are independent predictors of successful TLE with SAMES, allowing a more individualized approach to patient selection for TLE with SAMES. When planning a TLE procedure in a patient with a relatively low lead dwell time and on chronic anticoagulation, it might be sensible to start TLE with a short active mechanical sheath, because of the high likelihood of successful extraction with SAMES only.

Complications in TLE With SAMES

The long mechanical extraction sheaths feature a more flexible shaft and were designed to facilitate extraction in more distal locations in the veins or in the heart. Major complications in TLE are often associated with venous or myocardial adhesions, which must be overcome during extraction and can result in vascular or myocardial tear, with pericardial tamponade being the most common major complication [29]. When working with short sheaths, special consideration has to be given to the risk of cardiac avulsion, being one of the more common major complications in TLE (0.19%–0.96%) [30].

While we observed similar total major complication rates to previous publications, we also observed a lower complication rate in TLE with SAMES only versus TLE with both short and long extraction sheaths (major 3.6% vs. 5.5% p = 0.76, minor complications 9.1% vs. 18.7% p = 0.09), which may not be surprising given that complications rates increase with longer dwelling times typically necessitating long extraction sheaths [12, 31, 32]. In the 55 cases where SAMES only were used, 7 patients (12.7%) suffered from complications (2 major, 5 minor); 2 cases of major complications that were not directly related to TLE (1 postoperative death due to sepsis and 1 postoperative death due to postoperative occlusion of the abdominal aorta). Minor complications consisted of 2 cases of dislodgement of reimplanted electrodes, 2 cases of hematoma, which did not need intervention, and 1 case of subclavian vein thrombosis. With a lower number of complications in the SAMES only group and without major complication that were directly related to TLE in the SAMES only group, we conclude that TLE with SAMES only appears to be a safe approach.

Conclusion

In 10% of patients undergoing TLE, SAMES are sufficient for successful TLE. Anticoagulation and shorter lead dwell time are predictors for successful TLE with SAMES. TLE with SAMES only appears to be safe and may be associated with a lower incidence of complications, which primary may be explained by lower lead dwelling times in the population with successful TLE with SAMES only.

Limitations of This Study

The main limitations of this study are its single‐center and retrospective design, small sample size, and limited observation period. Moreover, the definitions of success, failure, and complications vary across previous studies, making comparisons challenging. Complications were only assessed within 30 days following TLE, limiting conclusions about long‐term complications.
