Inhibition of CDK1 Promotes Immunogenic Cell Death in Neuroblastoma
Authors: Yiquan Li, Tianyun Lan, Rongpu Liang, Liting Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroblastoma is characterized by multiple immune evasion strategies, making it critical to explore the use of immunotherapy. CDK1 is known for its role in regulating cell cycle progression and is aberrantly expressed in various tumors, yet its role in NB and immunogenic cell death remains unclear.MethodsWe investigated the association between the expression of CDK1 and the outcome of neuroblastoma according to the TCGA database and confirmed the results by tissue arrays. Furthermore, we evaluated the correlation between CDK1 expression and immune cell infiltration as well as cytokine effectiveness. Finally, in vitro and in vivo experiments were used to confirm the effects of a CDK1 inhibitor.ResultsCDK1 was overexpressed in advanced neuroblastoma and was associated with poor prognosis. High CDK1 expression correlated with reduced immune cell infiltration and decreased levels of effector cytokines including IL-12, IFN-γ, and IRF1. Furthermore, the inhibition of CDK1 slowed the growth of neuroblastoma cells and promoted immunogenic cell death in neuroblastoma. CDK1 inhibition via RO-3306 suppressed tumor growth and induced hallmark features of ICD, including CRT exposure, HSP70 expression, and HMGB1 release.ConclusionCDK1 inhibition not only slows neuroblastoma progression but also triggers DAMP release consistent with ICD and immune activation. CDK1 may be a potential prognostic marker and effective treatment target for improving immunotherapy efficacy in neuroblastoma patients.Keywords: neuroblastoma, CDK1, immunogenic cell death

Introduction

Neuroblastoma (NB) is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children and originates from neural crest cells.1 The high heterogeneity of NB gives it unique and diverse biological and clinical characteristics, and tumors can undergo spontaneous regression or extensive spread and metastasis.2 Low- and intermediate-risk NB patients can be cured by surgical resection combined with or without chemotherapy, whereas high-risk NB patients typically have a 5-year survival rate of only approximately 50% even after receiving multimodal high-intensity combination therapy (induction chemotherapy, surgery, autologous stem cell transplantation, radiotherapy, disialoganglioside 2 (GD2) monoclonal antibody therapy, and retinoic acid maintenance therapy).3 Therefore, the treatment of high-risk NB requires the development of new and more effective treatment methods to further improve survival. Given the significant therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy in hematological tumors and other solid tumors, such as lung cancer4 and melanoma,5 immunotherapy offers promising directions for the treatment of high-risk NB. However, NB utilizes multiple immune escape mechanisms, such as the downregulation of MHC class I molecules by high-risk NB, which constricts T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity and inhibits NK cell function in NB patients.6 The combination of multiple mechanisms enables NB to evade immune monitoring and killing.7 Therefore, the application of immunotherapy in NB requires further exploration.

The mutation load of high-risk NB cells is relatively low; therefore, the MHC class I molecular presentation pathway presents fewer tumor-related antigens, resulting in weaker antigenicity of tumor cells.8,9 This weak antigenicity also prevents antigen-presenting cells (APCs)10 in the surrounding lymph nodes of tumors from effectively activating immune cells that specifically kill tumors, including CD8+ T cells, Th1 cells, and macrophages.11 Therefore, immunotherapy for high-risk NB has not yielded satisfactory outcomes.

The immunogenicity of tumors refers to the ability of tumor antigenic substances to induce host immune responses. Antigens can stimulate specific immune cells; promote their activation, proliferation, and differentiation; and ultimately produce immune effector antibodies in immune cells. The low immunogenicity of NB is an important reason for the lack of immune cells in its microenvironment,8 and it is also the main factor leading to poor immunotherapy efficacy.12 Previous studies have confirmed that cytotoxic drugs not only promote tumor cell death but also facilitate the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)13 and induce immunogenic cell death (ICD).14,15 DAMPs include calreticulin (CRT) on the cell surface, high mobility group protein 1 (HMGB1) secreted by tumor cells, ATP molecules released from cells, and heat shock proteins (HSP70, HSP90).16 These DAMPs are liberated during ICD and can bind to pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on the surface of DC cells, initiating a series of cellular reactions that ultimately activate innate and adaptive immune responses.17,18 The immunogenic death of tumor cells promotes the maturation of DC cells and recruits and activates the immune response of CD8+ T cells, leading to tumor killing effects19 and making it a hot research topic in tumor immunotherapy.

Cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1), the first identified member of the CDK family, regulates the transition between the G2 phase and mitosis.20 CDK1 is overexpressed in various tumors and participates in the process of tumor cell proliferation.21 Furthermore, the inhibition of CDK1 by inhibitors has shown potential for preventing tumor progression through the maintenance of cell proliferation.22 Recent research has shown that CDK inhibitors can induce the ICD of tumor cells and then activate immune cells, which enhances the antitumor efficacy of CDK inhibitors.23 However, the function of CDK1 and the antitumor efficacy of CDK1 inhibitors (CDK1is) in neuroblastoma are still unclear and require further research. RO-3306 is a specific and reversible inhibitor of CDK1. Its kinase selectivity has been validated through cellular assays, where treated cells exhibit a large, flattened appearance, suggesting that RO-3306 efficiently inhibits CDK1 without affecting CDK2 or CDK4.24 RO-3306 also delays the onset of S phase and entirely prevented the phosphorylation of Ser811 on the retinoblastoma protein (Rb) during the early G1 phase. Moreover, RO-3306 induces selective cell death by significantly increasing the proportion of apoptotic cells in tumorigenic cell lines relative to nontumorigenic cell lines.25,26

Therefore, in this study, we investigated the association between the expression of CDK1 and the outcome of NB according to the TCGA database and confirmed these effects through a tissue array. Furthermore, we determined that the expression of CDK1 was negatively correlated with immune cell infiltration and cytokine activity by the R2 platform. Finally, in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that the inhibition of CDK1 slowed cell growth and triggers DAMP release which promotes ICD in NB (Supplementary Figure 1).

Methods and Materials

Cell Culture

The mouse Neuro-2a and human SKNBE and SKNSH cell lines were obtained from iCell Bioscience (Shanghai, China). Neuro-2a cells were cultured in MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), while SKNBE and SKNSH cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS. All the cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Immunohistochemistry Staining

Neuroblastoma and normal adrenal gland tissue microarrays (catalogue number: N264001) were purchased from the Zhongke Guanghua, Changsha Yaxiang Biotechnology Co., LTD (China). The slices were deparaffinized and hydrated with different concentrations of ethanol and then incubated with Tris-EDTA antigen retrieval solution. After being blocked with 5% BSA, the samples were incubated at 4 °C overnight with the following primary antibodies: anti-Ki67 (1: 200, Abcam, ab15580); anti-CDK1 (1:500, Abcam, ab265590). The tissue sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L, 1:500, Abcam, ab6721), stained with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. The stained tissue was photographed by microscopy (Nikon, ECLIPSE Ni-U) or a digital slide scanner (3DHISTECN, pannoramic MIDI).

Apoptosis

A total of 5×105 SKNSH and SKNBE cells were cultured in 6-well plates and incubated with DMSO, 10 μM, 15 μM, 20 μM, or 25 μM of the CDK1 inhibitor Ro-3306 (MedChemExpress, HY-12529) for 24 h. Then, the cells were collected, resuspended in binding buffer and stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGA1102) for 15 min at RT in the dark. The apoptotic status was detected by a flow cytometer (BD Bioscience, FACSCanto) and measured by FlowJo.

Cell Cycle

SKNSH cells and SKNBE cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with DMSO or 10 μM, 15 μM, 20 μM, or 25 μM CDK1i for 24 h. A total of 5×105 cells were collected, washed with PBS and then fixed in 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight. The treated cells were stained with propidium iodide/RNase A from the Cell Cycle Detection Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGA511) at RT for 30 min in the dark. Finally, cell cycle analysis was performed by flow cytometry.

Migration Assays

SKNSH and SKNBE cells (5×105 cells) were treated with DMSO, 10 μm, 15 μm, 20 μm, or 25 μm CDK1i for 24 h. The cells were subsequently digested and counted. For each group, 5×104 cells were collected and resuspended in 200 μL of cell culture medium without FBS. The cells were embedded in the upper transwell chamber (Corning, 3422; pore size, 8 μm). A total of 600 μL of cell culture medium supplemented with 20% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the upper compartment of each insert was removed, and the membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A) and stained with crystal violet (Solarbio, C8470). The migration states of both cell lines were determined using light microscopy.

Invasion Assays

SKNSH and SKNBE cells (5×105 cells) were treated with different concentrations of CDK1i for 24 h. Then, 5×104 cells from each group were collected and cultured in serum-free medium on the top of transwell chambers precoated with Matrigel (Corning, 354277). A total of 600 μL of culture medium containing 20% FBS was added to the bottom of the transwell chambers. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the top compartment of each insert was removed, and the membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The invasion states of both cell lines were evaluated using a light microscopy.

Colony Formation Assay

2000 SKNSH and SKNBE cells treated with DMSO, 10 μm, 15 μm, 20 μm, or 25 μm CDK1i in advance were incubated in 6-well plates for 10 days at 37 °C. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and visualized by crystal violet staining for 30 min. Finally, the number of colonies was photographed and counted.

Immunofluorescence Staining

A total of 1×105 cells SKNSH and SKNBE cells were seeded in glass bottom cell culture dishes (15 mm) and treated with DMSO, 10 μm, 15 μm, 20 μm, or 25 μm CDK1i for 24 h. After being washed with PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and then permeated with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. For detecting proteins on the membrane, permeabilization was not needed. Next, the cells were blocked with 5% BSA at RT and incubated overnight at 4 °C with following primary antibodies: anti-Calreticulin (CRT, 1:100, Abcam, ab2907), anti-HSP70 (1:50, Abcam, ab5439), and anti-HMGB1 (1:250, Abcam, ab79823). The cells were subsequently incubated with FITC- or CY3-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200, Servicebio, GB22403; GB21403). After the nuclei were stained with DAPI, the cells were observed through a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Ti2) or a laser confocal microscope (Leica, STELLARIS STED).

Flow Cytometry

SKNSH and SKNBE cells were pretreated with DMSO or Ro-3306 at concentrations of 10 μm, 15 μm, 20 μm, or 25 μm for 24 h. Tumors were excised and mechanically dissociated into a cell suspension. For surface staining, the cells were collected and resuspended in the appropriate staining buffer. For intracellular staining, the cells were fixed and permeabilized using a Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BD Pharmingen, 562574) and subsequently stained with specific antibodies. The cells were incubated with primary antibodies against CRT (Abcam, ab92516) and HSP70 (Abcam, ab2787), followed by staining with APC-conjugated (Abcam, ab130805) and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated (Servicebio, GB25301) secondary antibodies for cytometric analysis. To determine the proportion of T cells, the cells were incubated with anti-CD45-Perp/Cy5.5, anti-CD3-BV605, anti-CD4-FITC, or anti-CD8-APC/Cy7 (BioLegend) antibodies. To assess the percentage of mature dendritic cells, the cells were incubated with anti-CD11c-PE/Cy7, anti-CD80-FITC, or anti-CD86-APC (BioLegend) antibodies. To analyze the phenotype of the macrophages, the cells were incubated with anti-CD11b-FITC, anti-CD206-BV421, or anti-F4/80-APC (BioLegend) antibodies.

Public Database

R2 is an interactive web portal that allow in-depth analysis of TCGA gene expression data. R2: Genomics Analysis and Visualization Platform is publicly available at http://r2.amc.nl. Transcriptome RNA-seq data from the TARGET-NBL cohort were obtained from the TCGA database using “TCGAbiolinks” package in R version 4.4.0. This study was reviewed and approved by the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Approval Number: II2026-038-01)

Immune Analysis

The ESTIMATE algorithm was used to assess the correlation between the CDK1 expression level and the tumor immune microenvironment, including the immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores, in the TARGET-NBL cohort. Additionally, the ssGSEA algorithm was utilized to compare the infiltration degrees of immune cells between the high- and low-CDK1 expression groups. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the associations between the expression levels of CDK1 and various cytokines.

Subcutaneous Tumor Xenograft Model

A subcutaneous tumor xenograft model was established by injecting 1×106 Neuro-2a neuroblastoma cells into the right flank of male BALB/c mice aged 6--8 weeks (Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center, Guangzhou, China). After one week of acclimation, a total of 18 mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group): control (DMSO), low-dose Ro-3306, and high-dose Ro-3306. Tumor growth was monitored with calipers every two days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = 0.52 × length × width2. When the tumors reached a volume of 500mm3, mice were treated with tail vein injections of either DMSO or Ro-3306 at 0.5 mg/mL or 1.0 mg/mL. Each mouse received 200 μL per injection, corresponding to doses of approximately 5 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg body weight, respectively. And mice received repeated doses: one injection every four days, for a total of five injections. After treatment, mice were monitored daily for general health, body weight, and tumor size. Tumor volume was measured with calipers every two days after inoculation, calculated using the formula V = 0.52 × length × width2. At the experimental endpoint, all mice were euthanized for tissue collection. Humane endpoints were defined as tumor volume >1500 mm3, body weight loss >20%, or evidence of distress (hunched posture, inactivity, reduced food/water intake). At the endpoint, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation at a displacement rate of 20–30% of chamber volume per minute, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. Death was confirmed by the cessation of heartbeat and respiration. Tumors were collected for subsequent immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry analyses. All animal procedures adhered to protocols approved by Ruiye Model Animal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China; approval number: RYEth-20230424230). The animal studies were approved by both the Animal Care and Use Committee of Ruiye Model Animal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (approval number: RYEth-20230424230) and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. SYSU-IACUC-2026-B0022). All procedures were performed in accordance with the National Standard of the People’s Republic of China GB/T 35892–2018 “Guideline for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals”.

Data Analysis

The results are expressed as the means ± SDs. The statistical significance of differences between groups was analyzed by the Log rank test or Student’s t test. Correlations between two parameters were analyzed by Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis in GraphPad Prism 7. All the data were analyzed using a two-sided test, and P < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant criterion. *P <0.05, **P <0.01, ***P <0.001 and **** P <0.0001, ns, not significant.

Results

Overexpression of CDK1 is Associated with Poor Outcomes in Neuroblastoma Patients

First, we estimated the mRNA level of CDK1 according to the data from the TCGA cohort by the R2 platform. The results revealed that CDK1 is highly expressed in pan-cancer tumor tissues (Supplementary Figure 2) and the expression level of CDK1 increased with progression stage in NB (Figure 1A). Next, we detected the expression of CDK1 in a neuroblastoma tissue array and found that the CDK1 expression level in NB patients with stage III–IV disease was greater than that in patients with stage I-II disease (Figure 1B and Supplementary Figure 3). Moreover, CDK1 expression was much higher in NB with MYCN amplification than in NB without MYCN amplification (Figure 1C). We also assessed the prognostic value of CDK1 in neuroblastoma patients on the basis of the TCGA database. Patients with high CDK1 expression had worse event-free survival and overall survival than those with low CDK1 expression did (Figure 1D). These results indicate that CDK1 is associated with poor prognosis in neuroblastoma patients.

The Expression of CDK1 is Negatively Correlated with Immunogenicity and Effector Immune Cell Infiltration in Neuroblastoma

To investigate the influence of CDK1 on immune cells, we analyzed the correlation between CDK1 expression and several genes related to immunogenicity using the TCGA database. First, we investigated the relationship between CDK1 and immunogenicity. Neuroblastomas with high CDK1 levels had lower immune scores, indicating a small degree of immune cell infiltration (Figure 2A). Moreover, high CDK1 expression was associated with a low immune stromal score (Figure 2B). The estimated score revealed that neuroblastomas with CDK1 overexpression may have low immunogenicity and a suppressive immune microenvironment (Figure 2C). CDK1 expression was positively associated with tumor purity (Figure 2D). Furthermore, we investigated the correlation between CDK1 expression and immune cell infiltration. The expression of CDK1 exhibited a weak negative association with DC infiltration in NB (Figure 2E, Supplementary Figure 4A and B), which indicated that CDK1 may restrict antigen presentation. The numbers of CD8+ T cells and NK cells infiltrating the TME were negatively correlated with CDK1 expression (Figure 2F and G, Supplementary Figure 4C–F). Moreover, neuroblastomas that highly expressed CDK1 contained more activated CD4+ T cells than memory CD4+ T cells did (Figure 2H, Supplementary Figure 4G–H). These results suggest that CDK1 is negatively correlated with immunogenicity and effector immune cell infiltration in neuroblastoma.

The Expression of CDK1 is Negatively Correlated with That of Effector Cytokines in Neuroblastoma

Cytokines play important roles in the tumor environment and can regulate immune cell maturation, differentiation, and proliferation. Therefore, we analyzed the correlation between CDK1 expression and several cytokines in NB. CDK1 was negatively associated with the expression of IL-12, IRF1, and TNF (Figure 3A–C), which are critical effector cytokines in antitumor immunity. However, its correlation with TNF and IL-2 was not statistically significant (Figure 3D and E). Additionally, CDK1 was weakly positively correlated with the expression of IL-4 (Figure 3F), a cytokine typically associated with immunosuppressive Th2 responses. These results indicate that CDK1 might inhibit effective cytokines and promote the production of immunosuppressive cytokines in NB to create an immunosuppressive TME.

Inhibition of CDK1 Slows the Growth of Neuroblastoma Cells

To investigate the role of CDK1 in the progression of neuroblastoma, Ro-3306, a CDK1 inhibitor, was applied to the culture media of MYCN-amplified SKNBE and MYCN-non-amplified SKNSH cells. Western Blot and qPCR results confirmed that CDK1 expression (both mRNA and protein) is higher in the SKNBE cell line compared to SKNSH (Supplementary Figure 5). Furthermore, upon treating both cell lines with the CDK1 inhibitor, we observed that SKNBE cells exhibited greater sensitivity to the treatment than SKNSH cells (Supplementary Figure 6). The results showed that the CDK1 inhibitor induced the apoptosis of SKNBE and SKNSH cells in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 4A and B). CDK1 is a vital protein in the cell cycle. Thus, the suppression of CDK1 arrested the cell cycle in the G2/M phase in SKNBE and SKNSH cells, especially when the concentration of Ro-3306 was 25 μM (Figure 4C and D). Moreover, SKNBE and SKNSH cells were subjected to migration and invasion assays after being cultured with Ro-3306 for 24 h. Ro-3306 significantly restricted the migration of SKNBE and SKNSH cells. In the 25 μM group, almost no cells migrated to the lower chamber (Figure 4E and F). Similarly, the invasion of SKNBE and SKNSH cells was clearly reduced after the inhibition of CDK1 (Figure 4G and H). Thus, inhibition of CDK1 may induce dysfunction in neuroblastoma and restrain its progression.

Inhibition of CDK1 Promotes Immunogenic Cell Death in Neuroblastoma

Our observations that inhibition of CDK1 induced neuroblastoma cell death and the expression of CDK1 was associated with immune cell infiltration indicated that immunogenic cell death may be induced by a CDK1 inhibitor. After incubation with different concentrations of Ro-3306, SKNBE and SKNSH cells were detected by flow cytometry. The levels of calreticulin (Figure 5A and B) and HSP70 (Figure 5C and D) on the cell membrane increased markedly after the inhibition of CDK1. The results were further verified through an immunofluorescence assay, which demonstrated that Ro-3306 intervention induced CRT exposure on the cell membrane (Figure 6A and B). Similarly, the expression of HSP70 in the SKNBE and SKNSH cell membranes was significantly increased, as detected by LSCM (Figure 6C and D). Moreover, HMGB1 secretion significantly increased after Ro-3306 treatment, which resulted in a reduction in HMGB1 in the nucleus (Figure 6E and F). These results indicate that the inhibition of CDK1 promotes immunogenic cell death in neuroblastoma.

Inhibition of CDK1 Slows the Growth of Neuroblastoma Cells and Enhances the Antitumor Immune Response in vivo

To more rigorously evaluate the impact of CDK1 on neuroblastoma growth, we established a xenograft model in BALB/c mice using Neuro-2a cells. The mice were intravenously administered DMSO or Ro-3306 at concentrations of 0.5 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL every four days for a total of five treatments. Compared with the control condition (DMSO), the administration of the CDK1 inhibitor Ro-3306 significantly reduced both tumor volume and weight (Figure 7A–C). Notably, tumor growth was markedly inhibited in the Ro-3306-treated groups. IHC staining demonstrated that the Ki67 level was reduced in the Ro-3306-treated group, indicating that Ro-3306 effectively inhibited tumor cell proliferation (Figure 7D). We also evaluated the phenotype and function of immune cells using flow cytometry. The proportion of CD11c+CD80+CD86+ dendritic cells (DCs) was significantly elevated following Ro-3306 treatment, suggesting an increased conversion rate from immature DCs to mature DCs (Figure 7E and F). Additionally, we observed reduced infiltration of F4/80+CD206+ cells in the Ro-3306-treated group, suggesting a decrease in the M2 macrophage population following Ro-3306 administration (Figure 7G and H). Moreover, the proportion of CD3+CD8+ T cells increased, whereas the percentage of CD3+CD4+ T cells decreased. This shift is likely due to mature DCs presenting tumor antigens, thereby enhancing T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity (Figure 7I–K). Notably, calreticulin expression was markedly upregulated, and nuclear HMGB1 levels were significantly reduced after CDK1 inhibition (Figure 7L and M), which is consistent with our in vitro findings.

Discussion

NB is a malignant solid tumor originating from the sympathetic nervous system in children. Unlike adult malignant tumors, pediatric NB exhibits fewer mutations,6 which requires distinct treatment strategies. However, immune checkpoint therapy remains severely limited in NB treatment.27 The immune microenvironment of children in the high-risk group of NB is significantly inhibited, characterized as an immunological “cold tumor” with multiple immune escape strategies.28 The mutation load of high-risk NB cells is relatively low, so the MHC class I molecular presentation pathway can present fewer tumor-related antigens, resulting in weaker antigenicity of tumor cells. Therefore, finding ways to activate the immune response against neuroblastoma has become the key to improving immunotherapy.

A key molecular feature of high-risk NB is MYCN amplification, which strongly correlates with aggressive disease and poor prognosis. Importantly, previous studies have demonstrated that MYCN amplification is associated with elevated expression of multiple cell cycle regulators, including CDK1. CDK1 inhibition–induced down-regulation of MYCN, which thereby decreased the transcriptional activation of MYCN on the survivin promoter.29 Consistent with these findings, our own analysis of publicly available neuroblastoma datasets (eg, R2 platform) revealed significantly higher CDK1 expression in MYCN-amplified tumors compared to non-amplified tumors (Figure 1). This transcriptional relationship may explain, at least in part, the elevated CDK1 expression observed in high-risk NB and its association with poor patient outcome. Moreover, MYCN-driven proliferative signaling and CDK1 activity may cooperatively sustain tumor growth and therapy resistance. These observations suggest that CDK1 not only serves as a downstream effector of MYCN oncogenic activity but may also represent a targetable vulnerability in MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma. Future studies exploring whether CDK1 inhibition exerts preferential efficacy in MYCN-amplified models would provide further mechanistic and translational insight.

Previous studies have confirmed that CDK1 inhibitors not only promote cell death but also induce immunogenic release.30 However, their efficacy in NB has not yet been confirmed. This study confirmed that CDK1 is highly expressed in NB and is associated with poor prognosis. And we found that inhibition of CDK1 promotes tumor cell apoptosis and suppresses tumor cell proliferation and migration and these findings are consistent with previous reports.31,32 However, research on the regulation of the neuroblastoma immune microenvironment by CDK1 is currently lacking. Therefore, this study investigated the correlation between CDK1 and immune cells in neuroblastoma and found that the expression of CDK1 is related to immune cell function and indicated its potential role in regulating the immune microenvironment. And the mechanism by which CDK1 regulates the immune microenvironment requires further investigation.

This study focused mainly on the role of ICD in neuroblastoma (NB) and the potential of CDK1 inhibition as a therapeutic strategy. ICD, a regulated form of cell death, promotes the release of DAMPs, which facilitate DC maturation and activate immune responses that are crucial for effective cancer immunotherapy.33 Beyond its established role in cell cycle progression, our data reveal a previously underappreciated immunomodulatory function of CDK1 in neuroblastoma. Using Ro-3306, a selective CDK1 inhibitor, we observed that CDK1 inhibition induced hallmark molecular features associated with immunogenic cell death, including cell surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT), upregulation of HSP70, and secretion of HMGB1. These DAMPs are known to promote dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation.34–39 Importantly, these in vitro findings were accompanied by significant remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment in vivo: CDK1 inhibition increased infiltration of mature dendritic cells and CD8+ T cells while reducing the proportion of immunosuppressive M2 macrophages. This shift from a “cold” to a more “hot” immune landscape suggests that CDK1 inhibition may help overcome immune evasion in neuroblastoma.

This study confirms that CDK1 can serve as a viable target for modulating the immune microenvironment in NB and enhancing immunotherapy. Inhibition of CDK1 using Ro-3306 not only altered the immune landscape by increasing the proportion of mature dendritic cells and CD8+ T cells but also reduced the presence of M2 macrophages. Dendritic cells play essential roles in the activation and priming of CD8+ T cells, and their maturation enhances T-cell-mediated tumor immunity.40 Another key observation from this study is the reduction in M2-like macrophages following CDK1 inhibition. Tumor-associated macrophages in neuroblastoma are predominantly M2-polarized and contribute to immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and therapy resistance.41 The shift away from an M2-dominant phenotype suggests that CDK1 inhibition may reprogram the tumor microenvironment not only by promoting immunogenic death-associated signaling but also by alleviating myeloid-mediated suppression. Whether this effect is direct (eg, via CDK1 activity in macrophages) or indirect (eg, secondary to T-cell-derived cytokines) remains unknown and represents an important direction for future mechanistic studies using lineage-specific genetic models.

We recognize a critical limitation of the present study: while the release of DAMPs and subsequent immune remodeling are consistent with ICD, they are not sufficient to conclusively establish its occurrence. According to established consensus in the field, definitive proof of ICD requires demonstration that vaccination with dying tumor cells confers protective immunity against subsequent tumor challenge in immunocompetent hosts. Furthermore, distinguishing immune-dependent from immune-independent antitumor effects requires CD8+ T-cell depletion or efficacy studies in immunodeficient mouse models. Although RO-3306 has been widely recognized as a selective CDK1 inhibitor, potential off-target effects cannot be fully excluded. To rigorously address this concern and ensure CDK1-selective inhibition, we used a range of concentrations (including low concentrations) consistent with published selective usage. This approach minimizes potential confounding effects from off-target kinases while effectively targeting CDK1. Previous studies have demonstrated that RO-3306 inhibits CDK1 at effective concentrations, and its kinase selectivity has been validated by phenotypic and biochemical assays.42–44 Future studies using genetic silencing (eg, siRNA, CRISPR/Cas9, or rescue experiments) will be necessary to further confirm the CDK1-specific effects in neuroblastoma.

In summary, our findings confirm that CDK1 is a promising target for modulating the immune microenvironment in neuroblastoma and enhancing immunotherapy. By inducing triggers DAMP release consistent with ICD, the inhibition of CDK1 not only slows tumor progression but also stimulates the immune response, thereby positioning CDK1 as a potential prognostic marker and therapeutic target for neuroblastoma.
