Leptospira interrogans encodes a canonical BamA and three novel noNterm Omp85 outer membrane protein paralog﻿s
Authors: Everton B. Bettin, André A. Grassmann, Odir A. Dellagostin, Johann Peter Gogarten, Melissa J. Caimano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The Omp85 family of outer membrane proteins are ubiquitously distributed among diderm bacteria and play essential roles in outer membrane (OM) biogenesis. The majority of Omp85 orthologs are bipartite and consist of a conserved OM-embedded 16-stranded beta-barrel and variable periplasmic functional domains. Here, we demonstrate that Leptospira interrogans encodes four distinct Omp85 proteins. The presumptive leptospiral BamA, LIC11623, contains a noncanonical POTRA4 periplasmic domain that is conserved across Leptospiraceae. The remaining three leptospiral Omp85 proteins, LIC12252, LIC12254 and LIC12258, contain conserved beta-barrels but lack periplasmic domains. Two of the three ‘noNterm’ Omp85-like proteins were upregulated by leptospires in urine from infected mice compared to in vitro and/or following cultivation within rat peritoneal cavities. Mice infected with a L. interrogans lic11254 transposon mutant shed tenfold fewer leptospires in their urine compared to mice infected with the wild-type parent. Analyses of pathogenic and saprophytic Leptospira spp. identified five groups of noNterm Omp85 paralogs, including one pathogen- and two saprophyte-specific groups. Expanding our analysis beyond Leptospira spp., we identified additional noNterm Omp85 orthologs in bacteria isolated from diverse environments, suggesting a potential role for these previously unrecognized noNterm Omp85 proteins in physiological adaptation to harsh conditions.

Introduction

Leptospirosis is an emerging zoonotic disease with worldwide distribution caused by pathogenic spirochetes of the genus Leptospira1,2. Each year, an estimated one million cases of human leptospirosis occur in the world, leading to ~ 59,000 deaths3. The disease is most prevalent in underdeveloped regions, and often associated with factors like inadequate infrastructure, urban overpopulation, seasonal flooding, and frequent contact with reservoir hosts1,3–5. In developed countries, leptospirosis is associated with occupational exposure to infected animals (e.g., veterinarians, farm and meat workers) or recreational activities (e.g., adventure racing and freshwater sports)1,5. Pathogenic Leptospira spp. persist in nature within zoonotic cycles involving reservoir mammalian hosts1,6. L. interrogans, the species commonly linked with human disease, is transmitted primarily via contact with urine from infected rodents, particularly rats and mice1,6–8. Like saprophytic Leptospira spp., pathogenic species can survive for weeks in water or wet soil4. Upon transmission, pathogenic leptospires transition from a free-living to a parasitic lifestyle, rapidly disseminating from the site of inoculation to distal tissues9,10. In reservoir hosts, leptospires are subsequently cleared from all tissues except the kidneys, where they colonize the proximal renal tubules6,7,11. Once colonized, reservoir hosts continuously shed large numbers of leptospires in their urine for weeks to months12–14. Conversely, incidental or symptomatic hosts, including humans, if unable to control the infection, develop a wide range of clinical manifestations, ranging from mild flu-like symptoms to renal failure, jaundice, hemorrhage, and respiratory distress15,16.

Despite efforts to understand the genetic programs that enable Leptospira to cycle between free-living and pathogenic lifestyles, only a handful of leptospiral virulence determinants have been identified17,18. Genome-wide transcriptomic and proteomic studies19–25, including analyses of leptospires cultivated within rat peritoneal dialysis membrane chambers (DMCs)14,20, suggest that pathogenic Leptospira spp. have evolved distinct sensory mechanisms and/or regulatory pathways to sustain this biphasic lifestyle. However, identification of gene products required during the free-living phase is challenging due to the paucity of molecular tools and model systems for studying leptospires in water and/or soil microenvironments26–28. Moreover, like other pathogenic spirochetes29–32 and many non-model organisms33–35, Leptospira spp. encode numerous hypothetical proteins of unknown or poorly characterized function17,30,36. This knowledge gap is particularly notable for β-barrel outer membrane proteins (βB-OMPs), which are predicted to play essential roles in nutrient acquisition, protein translocation and secretion, signal transduction, and host cell interactions15,17,20,37–42. While a handful of leptospiral βB-OMPs have been investigated as potential vaccinogens43–48, with few exceptions23,49, they have been poorly investigated regarding their function and contribution(s) to virulence, transmission, and/or survival outside the host.

Transport, folding and assembly of nascent βB-OMPs into the outer membrane (OM), an energetically challenging process, is orchestrated by the β-barrel assembly machinery (BAM) complex50–60. The core component of the BAM complex is BamA54,57,61, which consists of a C-terminal OM-embedded 16-stranded bacterial surface antigen D15 β-barrel (IPR000184) and five N-terminal periplasmic polypeptide transport (POTRA) domains50,54,56,62. Omp85 proteins containing BamA-like β-barrels belong to a larger superfamily that also includes the evolutionary related TpsB family (IPR005565)53,62,63. FhaC, a prototype for the TpsB family, secretes a partner protein (FhaB) through the OM to the extracellular milieu63,64. The Omp85/TpsB superfamily were further categorized by Heinz and Lithgow53 into 12 subfamilies based on their N-terminal periplasmic regions50,53. FhaC65,66 and Hmw1B67 subfamilies of TpsBs and the Omp85s TamA68, Sam5069, BamA/BamA-like53,70, patatin-like53,71 and Lipo53,70 subfamilies all contain one or more periplasmic POTRA-like domains50,53,56,70. Additional Omp85 subfamilies are characterized by the presence of divergent WD40 and peptidase domains or metalloprotease domains53. The last Omp85 subfamily is distinguished by the absence of an N-terminal periplasmic domain50,53,56. The function(s) of ‘noNterm’ Omp85 subfamily members are poorly understood. Using bioinformatics, Grassmann et al.47 identified several βB-OMPs in L. interrogans, including a putative BamA ortholog (LIC11623) and a putative Omp85-like protein (LIC11254) lacking a recognizable periplasmic domain. Herein, using structural modeling and sequence alignments, we determined that LIC11623/BamA includes a noncanonical, extended POTRA4 domain that is conserved across Leptospiraceae. We also identified two previously unrecognized leptospiral Omp85 orthologs (LIC12252 and LIC12258), both of which lack periplasmic domains. Two of the three leptospiral ‘noNterm’ Omp85 orthologs were upregulated by leptospires in urine collected from mice infected with L. interrogans compared to leptospires cultivated in vitro and/or in rat peritoneal cavities. Mice infected with a L. interrogans lic11254 transposon mutant shed tenfold fewer leptospires in their urine compared to mice infected with the wild-type parent. Analyses of pathogenic and saprophytic Leptospira spp. identified five groups of noNterm Omp85 paralogs, two of which are encoded almost exclusively by pathogenic Leptospira spp. Expanding our analysis beyond Leptospira, we identified additional noNterm Omp85 proteins in bacteria isolated from diverse environments, suggesting a potential role for members of this previously unrecognized noNterm Omp85 subfamily in physiological adaptation to harsh conditions.

Results

Leptospirainterrogans encodes a presumptive BamA ortholog and three noNterm Omp85 paralogs

Using bioinformatics and genome mining, we identified four loci (lic11623, lic12252, lic12254 and lic12258) in L. interrogans encoding hypothetical proteins containing a conserved bacterial surface antigen (D15) domain (IPR000184), a characteristic feature of Omp85 family members (Fig. 1A)53. Of the four, only LIC11623 contains POTRA domains (IPR010827), strongly suggesting that it serves as the BamA component of the BAM complex in L. interrogans. The other three lack recognizable periplasmic domains, placing them in the noNterm Omp85 subfamily53. Notably, lic12252, lic12254 and lic12258 co-localize on chromosome I (Fig. 1B) and their β-barrels share higher sequence similarity compared to the β-barrel for LIC11623/BamA (Supplementary Figure S1). Next, we compared high confidence (average pLDDT > 86) AlphaFold2 models72 for all four leptospiral Omp85 proteins (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figure S2). As expected, all four are predicted to form 16-stranded β-barrels. In line with its predicted function as a BamA, LIC11623 contains five periplasmic POTRA domains (Fig. 2A). Models for the noNterm Omp85 paralogs display only short (22 – 43 aa) periplasmic regions (Fig. 2B). The β-barrels for all four leptospiral Omp85 proteins contain features conserved across the Omp85 family. Most notably, β-strands β1 and β16 for all four barrels are offset (Fig. 3A); repositioning of these β-strands is thought to form a lateral gate that enables insertion of nascent βB-OMPs into the OM56,58. All four leptospiral Omp85 β-barrels also contain a highly conserved (L/M)RG(W/Y) motif within their extracellular loop 6 (ECL6) (Fig. 3B). In the solved structures for other Omp85 family members62,65,68, ECL6 interacts tightly with an aspartate residue within a highly conserved FxDxG motif located in β13, forming a ‘lid-lock’ structure that prevents misrouting of substrates through the barrel pore65. In BamAs from other diderms50,55,65, ECLs 4, 6 and 7 form a dome-like structure on the surface of the β-barrel, occluding the external opening (Fig. 3C). While the model for the leptospiral BamA ortholog displays a similar dome structure, ECL7 is extended compared to its orthologs in E. coli and in the spirochetes Treponema pallidum and Borrelia burgdorferi (Fig. 3C and D). In the leptospiral noNterm Omp85s, the dome is even more pronounced than that of BamA, largely due to extended ECLs 3 and 4 (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Figure S3). LIC11623, the presumptive leptospiral BamA, contains a non-canonical extended POTRA4 domain

The periplasmic POTRA ‘arm’ of BamA plays a vital role in the proper folding and insertion of nascent β-barrels into the OM via the BAM complex54. BamAs typically contain five tandem POTRA domains, including the previously characterized BamAs from T. pallidum73,74 and B. burgdorferi75. As shown in Fig. 1, LIC11623, the presumptive leptospiral BamA, contains only four recognizable POTRA domains, all of which show strong structural similarity to their E. coli counterparts (Fig. 4A and B). The model for LIC11623, however, displays a noncanonical ‘extended’ POTRA-like domain (designated POTRA4) positioned between the canonical POTRA3 and POTRA5 domains (Figs. 1 and 4C). Sequence alignment of the extended POTRA4 domain with POTRA4 domains from E. coli and 13 representative spirochete species revealed three insertions, two large and one small, within the leptospiral BamA POTRA4 (Fig. 4C and D). The two larger insertions introduce a disordered loop between β-strand β1 and α-helix α1 and a large α-helix-rich structure between β2 and β3. The smaller insertion is predicted to add a turn to the linker region connecting α1 and α2. Interestingly, the presumptive BamAs from Turneriella parva and Leptonema illini, free-living members of the family Leptospiraceae76,77, also contain unique insertions within their respective POTRA4 domains. Identification of candidate BAM accessory factors in L. interrogans

The composition of BAM complexes differs widely across bacteria56,59,78,79. In E. coli, in addition to BamA, the complex includes four accessory periplasmic lipoproteins: BamB, BamC, BamD and BamE78,80–82. While only BamA and BamD are essential for viability, all five components are required for proper OM biogenesis80,83–85. Prototypical BamBs display an eight-bladed β-propeller fold (IPR017687) (Supplementary Figure S5) that shares sequence and/or structural similarity with quinoprotein alcohol dehydrogenase-like (IPR011047), pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) β-propeller repeat (IPR018391 and IPR002372), and WD40/YVTN repeat-like (IPR015943) domains. Although none of the putative lipoproteins encoded by L. interrogans contain recognizable BamB or PQQ repeat domains, the lipoprotein LIC13070 contains quinoprotein alcohol dehydrogenase and WD40/YVTN repeat domains. AlphaFold2 predicts a seven-bladed β-propeller conformation for LIC13070 rather than an eight-bladed β-propeller typical of BamBs86 (Supplementary Figure S5). Interestingly, the BamB ortholog in B. burgdorferi also is predicted to form a seven-bladed β-propeller structure77 (Supplementary Figure S5). In an attempt to obtain a three-dimensional model for a leptospiral BamA-LIC13070 complex, we took advantage of the deep learning protein structure prediction tool AlphaFold-Multimer72,87. However, the resulting model showed no interaction between LIC13070 and the BamA POTRA arm, as would be expected for BamB86 (Supplementary Figure S6). In E. coli, BamD is a 245 residues lipoprotein containing BamD (IPR017689) and BamD-like (IPR039565) domains, both of which belong to the tetratricopeptide helical domain superfamily (TPR, IPR011990). L. interrogans lacks a protein containing a recognizable BamD or BamD-like domain. Of the 63 proteins in L. interrogans containing TPR domains, only three (LIC12587, LIC10596 and LIC10493) are small putative lipoproteins without other unrelated functional domains and for which AlphaFold2 predicts a fold similar to that of BamD (Supplementary Figure S5). As with LIC13070, none were predicted to form a complex with the BamA POTRA arm88 (Supplementary Figure S6). No putative orthologs for BamC (IPR010653, IPR014524 and IPR042268) or BamE (IPR026592, IPR037873 and IPR007450) were identified in L. interrogans.

Trafficking of nascent unfolded βB-OMPs polypeptides from the Sec transporter to the BAM complex is mediated by the periplasmic chaperones SurA and/or Skp60,89. While no Skp ortholog was found in L. interrogans, two possible SurA orthologs (LIC12922 and LIC13000) were identified. The crystal structure for LIC12922 revealed strong structural similarities to the SurA in E. coli90. In E. coli, SurA is thought to engage the BAM complex via interactions with BamA POTRA1 and 2, BamB and BamE91. Using AlphaFold-Multimer with Amber relaxations, we generated models for the leptospiral BamA in complex with either LIC12922 (Fig. 5) or LIC13000 (Supplementary Figure S6). As observed for BamA-SurA in E. coli, LIC12922 is predicted to interact with BamA POTRAs 1 and 2 (Fig. 5). The predicted complex also shows interactions between BamA POTRA5 and the N-terminal region of the core domain in LIC12922, which are not seen in the E. coli complex91. The β-augmentation interaction between β2 of BamA POTRA1 and the N-terminal core of SurA observed in E. coli also is evident in the predicted BamA-LIC12922 complex (Fig. 5). In contrast, the predicted complex for BamA-LIC13000 does not resemble a functional BamA-SurA complex (Supplementary Figure S6). Conservation and genome organization of leptospiral noNterm Omp85 paralogs correlate with pathogenicity clades

Using InterPro, we searched for Omp85 orthologs in 25 Leptospira spp. across the pathogenic (P1 and P2) and saprophytic (S1 and S2) subclades92. All species examined contained a readily identifiable BamA ortholog and 2–4 noNterm Omp85 proteins (Supplementary Table S1). The majority (19 of 25) of species, including all highly pathogenic P1 species, encoded three noNterm Omp85 paralogs. Phylogenetic analyses of noNterm Omp85 orthologs from all 25 species identified five distinct paralogous groups (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figures S8–S10). Group 1 paralogs, represented by LIC12252, were the only noNterm Omp85 proteins conserved in both pathogenic and saprophytic Leptospira spp. With a few exceptions, groups 2 and 4, represented by LIC12254 and LIC12258, respectively, were found almost exclusively within pathogenic Leptospira spp. However, only two (L. licerasiae and L. wolffii) of the five P2 species encoded a group 4 paralog. L. ilyithenensis and L. ognonensis, S2 subclade species, were the only saprophytic Leptospira spp. to encode a group 4 paralog. To assess the possibility of phylogenetic reconstruction artifacts due to alignment of divergent sequences, we explored different approaches to sequence alignment and phylogenetic reconstruction (i.e., Maximum Likelihood and Bayesian). The phylogeny depicted in Fig. 6 was calculated from a MAFFT global alignment using an unalign-level of 0.8; this approach aligns regions of uncertain homology to gaps in the other sequences, thus minimizing artifacts due to the alignment of non-homologous sites. All combinations of alignment and phylogenetic reconstruction resulted in phylogenies with the same relations between the groups and subclades (Supplementary Figure S8), the only exception being the maximum likelihood reconstruction based on the muscle alignment following GBLOCK filtering (Supplementary Figure S8E). In this case, L. licerasiae and L. wolffii group 4 orthologs were basal to the other members of groups 4 and 5; however, the bootstrap support value for this grouping was only 53%. This low support value reflects that GBLOCKS filtering, in contrast to the unalign-option in MAFFT, removes questionable alignment columns completely, thereby lowering the within groups resolution. Overall, these data suggest that the group 1 paralogs serve a conserved function within all Leptospira spp., while the other paralogs likely serve niche-specific functions related to pathogenic (group 2 and 4) or saprophytic (group 3 and 5) lifestyles.

Mice infected with a transposon mutant containing an insertion in LIC12254 show reduced pathogen burdens in urine

Our finding that two of the three noNterm Omp85 paralogs encoded by L. interrogans were upregulated in urine from infected mice (Fig. 8A) raised the possibility that these novel βB-OMPs are required for survival during transmission. To explore this possibility, we took advantage of an existing L. interrogans serovar Manilae strain L495 transposon mutant (Man1435), which contains an insertion at amino acid position 365 in lic12254 (limlp11185 in serovar Manilae)26 (Supplementary Figure S11). At the outset, we compared growth of the transposon mutant (lic12254-tn) with its isogenic wild-type parent (WT) in vitro. As shown in Fig. 8B, both strains grew identically in EMJH medium at 30 °C. We next compared pathogen burdens in kidneys and urine during murine infection. Beginning 14–15 days post-infection, urine collected weekly from C3H/HeJ mice infected with either WT or mutant strains was examined for motile leptospires by darkfield microscopy. At this early time point, both strains were recovered at comparable levels in urine, confirming the ability of the mutant to establish infection, disseminate to the kidneys, and colonize the renal tubules. While burdens for the WT parent remained high (> 107 leptospires per ml) throughout the entire experiment, burdens for the lic12254-tn mutant in urine decreased ~ tenfold between days 14 and 42 (Fig. 8C). Similar results were obtained in two independent experiments. By qPCR, kidney tissues harvested from mice infected with either WT or mutant strains 42-days post-infection contained nearly identical burdens (Fig. 8D). Reduced survival of the lic12254-tn mutant in urine suggests a potential role for noNterm Omp85 paralogs for survival under harsh environmental conditions.

Orthologs for the leptospiral noNterm Omp85s are present in free-living bacteria from diverse ecological niches

To investigate a potential function(s) for the leptospiral noNterm Omp85 paralogs, we used a multiple sequence alignment of LIC12252, LIC12254 and LIC12258 as input for homology searches across InterPro reference proteomes using HMMER (ver. 2.40)93,94. The results revealed 64 significant hits (< E-20) across 56 bacterial species (Supplementary Table S2), including T. parva and L. illini (family Leptospiraceae) which encode a single noNterm Omp85 paralog each; 17 hits were exceptionally high confidence (< E-80). With the exception of T. parva, all other high confidence hits were from free-living bacteria within the phylum Bacteroidetes (Supplementary Table S2). Twelve of the 17 hits belong to the order Cytophagales, which includes non-spore forming, rod-shaped Gram-negative bacteria that move through a gliding or flexing motion95. Members of this poorly characterized order are found in diverse ecosystems, including soil96–98, saline water99–102 and sea ice103 (Supplementary Table S2). Despite their phylogenetic distance, the leptospiral noNterm Omp85s shared 64–68% amino acid similarity with those identified in Cytophagales (Supplementary Table S2). The gene neighborhoods for the noNterm Omp85s varied within the phylum Bacteroidetes and differed from those observed in Leptospira spp. (Supplementary Table S1). The discovery of a conserved region that is present within only a subset of noNterm Omp85 proteins and absent in all other Omp85/TpsB subfamilies led to the designation of a novel domain of unknown function, DUF5982 (IPR046024), which comprises the first two transmembrane β-strands and a small periplasmic region (Supplementary Figure S12). The sequence logo generated by alignment of all IPR046024 hits illustrates the high conservation of this domain (Supplementary Figure S12).

Discussion

Omp85 proteins are ubiquitous among Gram-negative bacteria, where they function as major drivers of OM homeostasis and adaption to various external milieus51,53,54. Heinz and Lithgow53 previously categorized the members of the Omp85/TpsB superfamily into 12 subfamilies based on composition of their periplasmic domains. The authors also postulated that the diversity and abundance of Omp85 proteins within a bacterial species correlates with the range of environments that it inhabits53. Given the complex life cycle of pathogenic Leptospira spp., it is reasonable to presume that these unique spirochetal bacteria have evolved mechanisms to modulate OM biogenesis and protein secretion in response to endogenous or exogenous signals. Herein, we describe the presumptive leptospiral BamA orthologs along with a previously unrecognized subfamily of noNterm Omp85 proteins encoded by Leptospira spp. as well as other evolutionarily distant bacteria associated with harsh environmental conditions.

The transport and folding of βB-OMPs in bacteria involve a series of handover events starting in the cytoplasm and ending at the OM. After synthesis, unfolded βB-OMPs are translocated across the cytoplasmic membrane by the Sec translocon and guided through the periplasm by Skp and/or SurA chaperones to the BAM complex56,62,89. BamA, along with the accessory lipoproteins BamBCDE, functions as the final foldase responsible for insertion of nascent β-barrels into the OM54,56–58,61. Depletion of BamA results in dysfunctional OM biogenesis and loss of cell viability75,80,83 while phenotypes associated with loss of the four accessory lipoproteins vary83,104,105. For example, in E. coli, depletion of BamD phenocopies that of BamA, whereas BamB-deficient cells survive but exhibit extensive defects in OM biogenesis80,82,83. The composition of the BAM complex varies among diderm bacteria78,106. Using co-immunoprecipitation of BamA complexes, the Akins group identified and characterized BamB (BB0028) and BamD (BB0324) orthologs in B. burgdorferi79,107, both of which lack BamB and BamD conserved domains. The candidate BamB and BamD lipoproteins in L. interrogans described herein also share low sequence similarity and varying structural similarity with the corresponding E. coli prototypes (Supplementary Figure S5). Despite extensive database mining, orthologs for BamC and BamE have yet to be identified in any pathogenic spirochete73,79. Nonetheless, we cannot dismiss the possibility that the BAM complex in spirochetes, including Leptospira, involves evolutionarily distinct accessory proteins. On the other hand, in E. coli85,108 and Thermus thermophilus109, BamA can insert and fold βB-OMPs into liposomes in vitro without the assistance of accessory lipoproteins. Thus, it is possible that BamA functions as a stand-alone foldase in Leptospira. It also is tempting to speculate that the extended POTRA4 in the leptospiral BamA promotes folding of βB-OMPs in the absence of accessory lipoproteins. POTRA4 is also in proximity to SurA in the structural complex predicted by AlphaFold-Multimer (Fig. 5) and could be involved in capturing unfolded βB-OMPs from SurA. Consistent with studies in other bacteria91,110–112, our model for leptospiral BamA-SurA complex predicts a direct interaction between the P2 and core domains of SurA with BamA POTRA1 and 2. Experimental approaches to determine the extent to which the leptospiral BamA requires collaboration with non-canonical BAM components are necessary to address these outstanding questions.

All members of the Omp85/TpsB superfamily described to date are implicated in βB-OMP assembly or the translocation of protein across the OM50,53. While BamA is presumed to be the primary βB-OMP foldase in Leptospira spp., one or more of the noNterm Omp85 proteins described herein could function as assembly units for a subset of βB-OMPs and/or as auxiliary foldases under stressful conditions during infection and/or transmission. Along these lines, Sam50, a mitochondrial Omp85, retains βB-OMP assembly activity even after the removal of its single POTRA domain113. Alternatively, leptospiral noNterm Omp85 could translocate one or more proteins across the OM. Some members of the Omp85/TpsB superfamily are part of Type V secretion systems (T5SS) functioning as either two-partner secretion systems (TPS) or autotransporters (AT)114,115. These T5SS consist of an OM-embedded Omp85/TpsB β-barrel and a passenger component. While ATs are formed of a single polypeptide chain, the β-barrel and passenger components of TPSs are encoded separately, usually in an operon66,114,115. TPS passenger components vary but include peptides with enzymatic activity and adhesins115. Interestingly, genes encoding proteins with adhesion-related functions are located within the neighborhood of leptospiral noNterm Omp85 loci (Fig. 7). Most notably, a gene encoded immediately downstream of all group 4 orthologs in P1 species encodes a large lipoprotein (LIC12259 in L. interrogans) composed of seven N-terminal bacterial immunoglobulin-like (Big) domains (IPR032812) and C-terminal alpha integrin domain (IPR028994). Several bacterial adhesins contain Big and/or integrin domains116–120, including LigA and LigB in Leptospira121–124. Orthologs of another lipoprotein, LIC12253, which contains a DUF1566 (IPR011460) domain, are encoded immediately downstream of a noNterm Omp85 in all Leptospira spp. investigated (group 2 in P1 and P2 subclades and group 3 in S1 and S2 subclades). Domingos et al.125 suggested that LIC12253 is surface-exposed and binds laminin, supporting the idea that this lipoprotein may functions as an adhesin. It is noteworthy that the lipoprotein translocase responsible for flipping lipoproteins from the inner to outer leaflets of the OM is unknown in Leptospira. βB-OMPs have been shown to assume this activity in other bacteria (e.g., SLAM in Neisseria meningitidis126 and LptD in B. burgdorferi127). An argument against the leptospiral noNterm Omp85s being involved in protein transport across OM is the presence of the extracellular dome structure covering the opening of the barrel (Fig. 3C), a structure absent in TPSs and ATs66,67. Whether the leptospiral noNterm Omp85s function as βB-OMP foldases or transporters remains to be experimentally determined.

Transmission of pathogenic leptospires from urine of an infected reservoir to a naïve host is critical for the epidemiological cycle of leptospirosis2. Prolonged exposure to urine of infected animals presents leptospires with a number of physiological stressors, including nutrient limitation, acidic pH, high osmolality, and increased levels of sodium and urea128,129. After being excreted in urine, leptospires then must persist in water and/or soil for days to months before encountering a new host130,131. Pathogenic leptospires shed from infected animals survive in urine for up to 24 h132, but laboratory cultivated organisms added to urine fail to survive133, indicating that an adaptation process is required by Leptospira for survival in urine. In this study, we found that a L. interrogans mutant containing an insertion within LIC12254, a group 2 noNterm Omp85 paralog, is shed in urine at decreasingly lower levels than the WT over time. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a gene product that contributes to survival of L. interrogans in urine. Remarkably, lic12254-tn leptospires reach the kidneys at WT levels, strongly suggesting that the decreased burdens observed in urine are due to cell death rather than reduced renal colonization. Consistent with this phenotype, lic12254 was expressed at significantly higher levels in urine compared to leptospires cultivated either in vitro or in DMCs. Expression of a second noNterm Omp85 paralog, lic12258, also was upregulated in urine. Interestingly, orthologs for LIC12254 and LIC12258 are found almost exclusively in pathogenic Leptospira spp. (Fig. 6). Given the sequence similarity between these two paralogs, it is likely that they serve redundant functions, enabling the lic12254-tn mutant to survive at reduced levels in urine. These data suggest that increased expression of one or more noNterm Omp85 proteins is required for adaptation of L. interrogans to survive in the urine during transmission following shedding.

Omp85/TpsB proteins previously were classified by Lithgow and Heinz53 into ten subfamilies based on the composition of their N-terminal periplasmic domain(s); Omp85 proteins lacking periplasmic regions were grouped, by default, into the “noNterm” subfamily. The authors noted that the barrel domains of some noNterm Omp85s shared sequence similarity with Omp85 metalloprotease proteins, suggesting a common origin. However, the DUF motif (DUF5982) described herein is present in only a subset of noNterm Omp85 proteins and absent in all other Omp85/TpsB subfamilies, including Omp85 metalloproteases. All DUF5982-containing noNterm Omp85 proteins identified as part of these studies are encoded by diverse bacteria isolated from water and/or soil environments97–101,134. Collectively, our findings suggest that the noNterm Omp85 subfamily is more diverse than previously thought and likely requires further classification. Future dissection of noNterm Omp85s in Leptospira spp. should provide insight into how this previously unrecognized subfamily of proteins contributes to bacterial growth and survival in unique ecological niches.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experimentation was conducted in accordance with “The Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Eighth Edition)135” and ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines using protocols reviewed and approved by the UConn Health Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Animal Welfare Assurance [AWA] number A347-01).

Structural analysis

Conserved domain searches were performed using InterPro136. Signal peptides and signal peptidase cleavage sites were identified using SignalP 5.0137. Three-dimensional structure models for Omp85 proteins were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank or the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database72. Protein complexes were predicted using AlphaFold-Multimer72 with Amber relaxations available on ColabFold v1.5.387. Structures were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.5.3 Schrödinger, LLC, and UCSF Chimera 1.16138. pLDDT values were extracted from models and analyzed using GraphPad Prism v10.1.1.

Generation of mammalian host-adapted leptospires

Mammalian host-adapted L. interrogans serovar Manilae strain L495 were obtained by cultivation of leptospires within rat peritoneal dialysis membrane chambers (DMCs) as previously described9,20. Briefly, 8–10 ml of Ellinghausen–McCullough–Johnson–Harris (EMJH) medium supplemented with 10% bovine serum albumin (GeminiBio, West Sacramento, CA) containing 104 leptospires per ml was used to fill sterile dialysis tubes (MWCO = 8000 Da) under strict aseptic technique. The resulting DMCs were then implanted into the peritoneal cavity of female Sprague–Dawley rats (150–174 g). After 10 days, DMCs were recovered, and the content evaluated by darkfield microscopy before downstream use.

Quantitative qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from L. interrogans serovar Manilae (3 biological replicates per condition) cultivated in vitro at 30 °C, in DMCs, or recovered in urine harvested from infected C3H/HeJ mice as previously described20. cDNAs were assayed in quadruplicate in 25 µl reactions performed with SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR or Probe (lipL32139) Super Mixes (Bio-Rad). Oligonucleotide primers used for qRT-PCR targeting noNterm Omp85 sequences are the following: LIC12252F-GGCTTTATTCAGGAGTTTCTTCTGGCCCT, LIC12252R-ACCGACTTTACAAATTTTCCGTCGTTCAC, LIC12254F-AACCAATTACTCTGGTTTTCCAAATAATGACGC, LIC12254R-GTACTTTTTACAAATTGACCGTCAAAGGTTTTG, LIC12258F-CTCGTACCGGTTCGGAAATTACTCCAAC and LIC12258R-ATGCCAAACTCCATCCGAGTGACCA. Transcript copy numbers were calculated using internal standard curves (107–101 copies) generated using gel-purified amplicon (lic12252, lic12254 and lic12258) or cloned amplicon (lipL32). lipL32-normalized transcript values for noNterm Omp85 paralogs were compared using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Tukey’s post hoc test and 95% confidence interval (GraphPad Prism v10.1.1., San Diego, California USA).

Virulence studies

Low-passage wild-type L. interrogans serovar Manilae strain L495 and a transposon mutant containing an insertion in limlp11185 (lic12254-tn)26, kindly provided by Dr. Mathieu Picardeau (Pasteur Institute), were cultivated in EMJH medium under static conditions at 30 °C. Transposon insertion was confirmed by sequencing. The lic12254-tn strain was maintained under antibiotic selection with kanamycin (40 µg/ml). L. interrogans cultures were passaged in vitro no more than twice before being used in these experiments. Culture viability (i.e., motility) and density were assessed by darkfield microscopy using a Petroff-Hausser counting chamber (Hausser Scientific Co., Horsham, PA). Five 10- to 12-week-old female C3H/HeJ mice (Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbour, ME) were inoculated intraperitoneally with 106 (WT or mutant) leptospires in 100 μl of EMJH. Beginning 14–15 days post-infection, urine was collected weekly from infected mice as previously described14. Animals were administered furosemide (2–10 mg/kg) intraperitoneally, placed in a metabolic chamber, and urine collected for up to 1 h. Burdens in urine were assessed by darkfield microcopy as described above. Virulence of wild-type and mutant strains was assessed in two independent experiments. Quantitation of leptospiral burdens in mice kidneys was performed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using a TaqMan-based assay for lipL32 as previously described14,139. Copy numbers for lipL32 were determined using an internal standard curve for the lipL32 cloned into pCR2.1 TOPO (Invitrogen). Average values for each strain were compared using an unpaired t test with two-tailed p values and 95% confidence interval (GraphPad Prism v10.1.1. software).

Identification of Omp85 orthologs in Leptospira spp. and other bacteria

Omp85 orthologs in 25 Leptospira spp. genomes (Supplementary Table S1) were identified by searching for proteins containing a bacterial surface antigen (D15) domain (IPR000184) using EMBL-EBI InterPro database140. Amino acid sequences of noNterm Omp85 paralogs in L. interrogans serovar Copenhageni strain L1-130 were aligned using Clustal Omega and used as an input for an HMM search on HMMER web server 2.4093,94. Searches were performed against the reference proteome database excluding Leptospira (taxid:171) and using E-value ≤ e-20 as a cut off.

Multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic comparisons

Identity and similarity among Leptospira spp. orthologs were determined using Sequence Manipulation Suite after sequence alignment by Clustal Omega141. To minimize artifacts, multiple sequence alignments used for phylogenic trees were calculated using complementary approaches. A muscle alignment142 was calculated using the default options as implemented in SEAVIEW143, then filtered in GBLOCKS144 using the most permissive setting as implemented in SEAVIEW, removing divergent alignment columns from further analyses. In addition, alignments were calculated using MAFFT145. All MAFFT alignments were calculated with the maximum iterations set to 1000. A first alignment was calculated using the local pair option, a second alignment was calculated using the global pair option, and a third was calculated using the global pair option with an unalign-level set to 0.8. The latter aligns regions with uncertain homology to gaps in the other sequences, thereby minimizing alignment artifacts due to aligning non-homologous sites, while retaining faster evolving regions to resolve the phylogenies between closer related sequences. All alignments were evaluated in iqtree2146 using the model test option under the Bayesian Information Criterion to find the most appropriate substitution model (Supplementary Table S3). Reliability of branches was assessed using iqtree’s ultrafast bootstrap option (1000 samples). For the GBLOCKS filtered muscle alignments, normal bootstrap resampling (1000 samples) also was performed. In addition, the global MAFFT alignment was evaluated using MrBayes3.2147 with the following settings: prset statefreqpr = fixed(empirical); lset rates = invgamma; mcmcp samplefreq = 1500; printfreq = 1500; mcmcp savebrlens = yes; mcmcp ngen = 3,000,000.
