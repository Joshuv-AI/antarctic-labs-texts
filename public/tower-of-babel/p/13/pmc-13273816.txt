Association between automated brain volumetry and visual classification of hippocampal atrophy in MRI
Authors: Fabrício N Garrafiel, Ricardo B Soder, Ricardo P Paganin, Maria Rosa A da Silva, Andrei Bieger, Vitor V Vidaletti, Fernando J Lazzareti, Eduardo R Zimmer, Jaderson C da Costa, Cristiano S Aguzzoli, Lucas P Schilling

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVisual assessment of medial temporal atrophy (MTA) is widely used in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) imaging evaluation. Introduction

Temporal lobe atrophy is a common finding in neurodegenerative disorders, especially in amnestic presentations of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, distinguishing pathological atrophy from normal age-related changes remains challenging. Variability in the morphology of medial temporal structures is expected during aging and may contribute to interobserver differences in radiological interpretation, leading to potential inconsistencies across evaluation methods (Ringman et al., 2010; Koikkalainen et al., 2016). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a key diagnostic tool in suspected AD, both to exclude alternative causes of cognitive impairment and to characterize disease-related atrophy patterns, particularly hippocampal atrophy as a marker of neurodegeneration in typical amnestic presentations (Ahdidan et al., 2015; Persson et al., 2022).

MRI has been widely explored for its ability to provide anatomical and quantitative information relevant to neurodegenerative disease assessment. Among these approaches, the Mesial Temporal Atrophy (MTA) scale proposed by Scheltens et al. is one of the most widely used visual rating methods for evaluating hippocampal atrophy in clinical practice (Scheltens et al., 1992). Although clinically useful due to its simplicity and applicability in routine MRI interpretation, visual MTA assessment is influenced by demographic factors such as age and sex, in addition to the inherent subjectivity of qualitative interpretation, which may affect reproducibility and interobserver agreement (Molinder et al., 2021; Rau and Urbach, 2021). Quantitative MRI-based approaches, particularly automated hippocampal volumetry, provide objective measurements that can be statistically adjusted for these factors (Ahdidan et al., 2015; Brewer et al., 2009).

Automated hippocampal volumetry has therefore emerged as a complementary tool for evaluating neurodegeneration by providing reproducible and quantitative estimates of hippocampal atrophy (Brewer et al., 2009; Wittens et al., 2021). Previous studies have demonstrated significant associations between hippocampal volumetry and visual MTA scores, including automated approaches for visual rating support systems, although discrepancies between qualitative and quantitative assessments may still occur, particularly in subtle or intermediate atrophy patterns (Molinder et al., 2021; Velickaite et al., 2020; Gebest et al., 2025; Mårtensson et al., 2019). In addition, visual MTA scores may progressively increase with aging even in the presence of relatively preserved hippocampal volumes, suggesting that visual and volumetric approaches may capture partially distinct aspects of medial temporal lobe changes (Velickaite et al., 2020).

Despite growing interest in automated volumetric methods, the anatomical and clinical characteristics of discrepant cases between visual assessment and quantitative volumetry remain insufficiently understood. Better characterization of these disagreement patterns may help clarify the complementary roles of visual interpretation and quantitative biomarkers in hippocampal atrophy assessment, particularly in clinically heterogeneous populations.

This study aimed to compare hippocampal volumetry obtained through automated segmentation with MTA scores assigned by experienced neuroradiologists. By analyzing the similarities and discrepancies between quantitative and qualitative assessments, we sought to better understand the strengths and limitations of each method and their potential implications for imaging-based evaluation of hippocampal atrophy. We hypothesized that visual MTA ratings would be significantly associated with automated hippocampal volumetric measurements, while a subset of individuals would demonstrate discordance between methods, reflecting complementary aspects of hippocampal degeneration.

Methods

In this retrospective cross-sectional study, we utilized 677 T1-weighted volumetric brain MRI scans from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI, http://adni.loni.usc.edu/). ADNI procedures were approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centers, and all participants provided written informed consent. Each subject contributed a single baseline MRI examination to the analysis. The study sample included cognitively unimpaired (CU), mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD dementia individuals at baseline. MCI and dementia due to AD individuals met clinical criteria set forth by McKahnn et al. (2011) (McKhann et al., 2011).

We selected the ADNI 3 cohort of the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative because it is based on a fully standardized multi-site 3T MRI acquisition protocol and, for volumetric quantification, employs FreeSurfer version 6.0. According to the ADNI MRI Core documentation, FreeSurfer v6.0 was implemented in ADNI-3, in which structural MRI scans were acquired using a standardized 3T high-resolution sagittal 3D T1-weighted MP-RAGE protocol (TR = 2300 ms, TI = 900 ms, minimum full-echo TE, 1×1×1 mm isotropic resolution, and 2× accelerated acquisition). Moreover, compared with earlier ADNI grant phases, ADNI 3 offers more complete availability of processed volumetric output (including hippocampal and intracranial volumes) for the full sample made publicly accessible via the LONI Data Archive (Alzheimer’s Disease Neuro Imaging III, 2018).

To minimize selection bias, all eligible ADNI-3 participants with available baseline T1-weighted MRI, hippocampal volumetric measurements, and visual MTA ratings were included. Only one MRI examination per participant was analyzed, and all scans were acquired using a standardized ADNI imaging protocol.

Hippocampal volumes were adjusted for estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) using a residual normalization approach based on linear regression, as previously described by Jack et al. (1998). Adjusted volumes were calculated from the residuals of the regression between hippocampal volume and eTIV, allowing volumetric measures to be statistically independent of intracranial size.

Abbreviations: ICV, Intracranial Volume; VOI, Volume of Interest. The residual normalization approach adjusts regional brain volumes for intracranial volume (ICV) using linear regression. In this method, the expected volume based on ICV is estimated, and the adjusted volume is obtained from the regression residual plus the mean volume, yielding values that are statistically independent of ICV. When comparing groups, the regression slope derived from cognitively unimpaired controls is applied to all groups, under the assumption that this slope reflects the normal relationship between regional brain volume and ICV, which may be altered in pathological conditions.

Two experienced neuroradiologists (R1 and R2) independently assessed hippocampal atrophy severity using the MTA scale (Scheltens et al., 1992). The raters were blinded to demographic and diagnostic information to minimize bias. Mismatch cases were defined as individuals whose hippocampal volumes were outside the ±2 standard deviation range of the mean hippocampal volume corresponding to each MTA category. These cases were considered discordant between quantitative volumetry and visual assessment and were subsequently analyzed according to demographic and clinical characteristics.

Pre-processed volumetric data were obtained from the ADNI database, including automated hippocampal segmentations generated by the ADNI using FreeSurfer version 6.0. Right and left hippocampal volumes were analyzed separately for all correlation and regression analyses.

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1, https://www.r-project.org/). Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess the association between quantitative volumetric measures and visual MTA scores, as MTA ratings are ordinal and volumetric measures may not follow a normal distribution. Linear regression models were used to evaluate the association between hippocampal volumetry and MTA scores, adjusting for age, sex, and estimated intracranial volume to account for potential confounders.

Mismatch and concordant groups were additionally compared regarding demographic and clinical variables. Comparisons included age, diagnosis group, normalized hippocampal volume, asymmetry indices, and MTA severity categories. Inter-rater agreement between neuroradiologists was assessed using weighted Cohen’s kappa statistic. No formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied because analyses were hypothesis-driven and exploratory.

Mean hippocampal volumes progressively decreased across diagnostic groups. Cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals presented mean right and left hippocampal volumes of 3880 mm3 ± 425 and 3756 mm3 ± 395, respectively, whereas participants with AD showed mean volumes of 2979 mm3 ± 546 on the right and 2822 mm3 ± 523 on the left. Individuals with MCI presented intermediate volumetric values (right: 3631 mm3 ± 534; left: 3493 mm3 ± 508).

Inter-rater agreement between neuroradiologists demonstrated substantial concordance. Unweighted Cohen’s kappa values were 0.611 for the right hemisphere and 0.652 for the left hemisphere (both p < 0.001). When weighted agreement was considered (quadratic weights), kappa values increased to 0.831 for the right MTA and 0.853 for the left MTA (both p < 0.001). The substantially higher weighted kappa values suggest that most disagreements occurred between adjacent MTA categories rather than representing gross classification errors. Spearman’s correlation coefficients between raters were 0.85 for the right hemisphere and 0.86 for the left hemisphere. Fig. 1 illustrates inter-rater agreement and disagreement distribution.

A total of 129 mismatch observations were identified across hemispheres and raters, corresponding to 62 unique individuals presenting at least one discordant visual-volumetric classification. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated a moderate-to-strong association between visual medial temporal atrophy (MTA) ratings and automated hippocampal volumetric measurements. Higher MTA scores were consistently associated with lower hippocampal volumes across both hemispheres and raters, supporting the clinical relevance of visual MTA assessment as an indirect marker of hippocampal atrophy, while also highlighting its limitations as a qualitative surrogate measure. In addition, we identified a subset of cases showing discordance between qualitative and quantitative assessments, highlighting the complexity of hippocampal atrophy evaluation and the potential complementary roles of visual interpretation and automated volumetry.

Our findings reinforce previous evidence demonstrating significant associations between visual MTA scores and hippocampal volumetry in neurodegenerative disorders. Spearman’s correlation analyses demonstrated moderate-to-strong inverse associations between hippocampal volumes and visual MTA scores across hemispheres and raters (ρ ≈ −0.67, p < 0.001). However, a substantial proportion of variance remains unexplained, suggesting that visual MTA ratings and quantitative volumetry may capture overlapping but not identical aspects of medial temporal lobe degeneration. This finding is particularly relevant in intermediate or subtle atrophy patterns, in which qualitative and quantitative approaches may diverge.

Inter-rater agreement for MTA classification was substantial, particularly when weighted agreement was considered, supporting the reproducibility of visual MTA assessment among experienced neuroradiologists. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that the MTA scale remains a practical and reproducible tool in routine neuroradiological evaluation, especially in settings where automated volumetric methods are not routinely available. However, despite good reproducibility, visual interpretation remains inherently subjective and may be influenced by factors such as anatomical asymmetry, image quality, reader experience, and demographic characteristics (Rau and Urbach, 2021; Mårtensson et al., 2019). Furthermore, the moderate inter-rater disagreement observed in a subset of cases highlights the limitations of MTA as a qualitative surrogate marker of hippocampal atrophy and reinforces that visual and quantitative approaches should be regarded as complementary rather than interchangeable methods.

Automated hippocampal volumetry provides objective and reproducible quantitative measurements, although technical limitations may also influence volumetric estimates. Segmentation variability, scanner-related differences, image artifacts, and post-processing pipelines may contribute to discrepancies between volumetric and visual assessments, particularly in subtle or intermediate atrophy patterns. Mismatch analyses demonstrated that discordance should not necessarily be interpreted as failure of either method, but rather as reflecting methodological differences and potentially distinct biological aspects of hippocampal degeneration.

Mismatch analyses demonstrated that discordant cases were not significantly associated with age, diagnosis group, normalized hippocampal volume, or asymmetry indices. In contrast, mismatch frequency varied significantly according to MTA severity category, occurring more frequently in lower and intermediate MTA scores. These findings support the interpretation that discrepant cases may represent biologically transitional or diagnostically challenging patterns rather than simple methodological inconsistencies. Whether these discordant cases represent early neurodegenerative changes, normal anatomical variability, or methodological limitations cannot be determined from the present cross-sectional data and warrants further longitudinal investigation.

This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding longitudinal progression of hippocampal atrophy or the temporal evolution of mismatch cases. Consequently, it remains unclear whether discordant visual-volumetric classifications represent stable individual variability or reflect differences in the temporal sensitivity of visual and quantitative measures during disease progression. Future follow-up studies will be necessary to determine whether one method identifies neurodegenerative changes earlier than the other.

Second, although the ADNI cohort provides highly standardized MRI acquisition and processing protocols, the use of publicly available data may limit generalizability to more heterogeneous real-world clinical settings. Finally, additional biomarkers, including amyloid and tau status, longitudinal cognitive trajectories, and white matter microstructural measures, were not available for integrated analysis. Such information could help determine whether mismatch cases represent early neurodegenerative changes, normal anatomical variability, or methodological limitations, and may improve the biological and clinical interpretation of discordant visual-volumetric classifications. In addition, although age, sex, and intracranial volume were included as covariates in the regression analyses, other factors that may influence volumetric estimates, such as image quality, scanner-related variability, and segmentation performance, were not explicitly modeled. However, the use of the ADNI-3 standardized acquisition protocol and centralized FreeSurfer processing pipeline likely mitigated, although not completely eliminated, the impact of these sources of variability.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that automated hippocampal volumetry and visual MTA scoring provide substantially convergent information regarding hippocampal atrophy across the Alzheimer’s disease spectrum. Hippocampal volumes progressively decreased from cognitively unimpaired individuals to participants with MCI and AD, while visual MTA ratings showed moderate-to-strong correlations with quantitative volumetric measures and substantial inter-rater agreement.

Nevertheless, a subset of participants demonstrated relevant discordance between visual and quantitative assessments, suggesting that these methods may capture partially distinct aspects of medial temporal lobe degeneration. Visual MTA assessment remains a practical and widely accessible tool in routine clinical evaluation, whereas automated volumetry provides objective quantitative measurements that may complement qualitative interpretation, particularly in subtle or intermediate atrophy patterns.

Taken together, these findings support the complementary use of visual and volumetric approaches for hippocampal atrophy assessment. Future studies should further investigate the biological and anatomical characteristics of discordant cases and evaluate integrated visual-quantitative strategies in clinically heterogeneous populations.
