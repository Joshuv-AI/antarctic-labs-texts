Effects of Remimazolam Dose and Bolus Injection Rate on Hiccups During Painless Colonoscopy: A Double-Blind Randomized Clinical Trial [Response to Letter]
Authors: Yanping Lu, Xuewei Wang, Henjing Zou, Changhui Shao, Guangyi Lai, Shan Ou
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We thank Wang and Wang for their careful reading of our article1 and for three constructive comments that give us a welcome opportunity to clarify our findings.

On the first point, we agree that a 2×2 factorial trial of this size has limited power to detect an interaction between remimazolam dose and bolus injection duration. We had stated in our Methods that the trial was primarily powered for between-group differences in hiccup incidence, while analyses of interaction and secondary endpoints were considered exploratory. Our Limitations section made the same caution concrete: because the observed hiccup incidence in the 0.4 mg kg−1 group (25.6%) greatly exceeded the pilot estimate (<5%), the study was likely underpowered for smaller between-group differences. We therefore accept that the non-significant interaction term (P = 0.598) is best read as a failure to detect an interaction rather than as evidence that dose and injection rate act independently. Resolving this question will require studies powered specifically for interaction effects,2 and we are grateful to the authors for drawing the distinction so clearly.

On the second point, the authors rightly note that the higher-dose regimen was accompanied by fewer body movements, fewer supplemental boluses, and lower MOAA/S scores, all consistent with deeper and more stable sedation. We would add one statistical clarification. In a dose-defined factorial design, dose and sedation depth are not independent: the administered dose is itself a determinant of sedation depth. Sedation depth therefore sits on the causal pathway from dose to hiccup reduction and functions as a mediator of the dose effect rather than as a confounder to be removed. This reading aligns with the mechanism we proposed in our Discussion, namely that deeper, more stable GABAergic sedation may lower the excitability of brainstem reflex circuits, including vagal–phrenic components, that generate hiccups. We readily acknowledge that, with sedation depth assessed only clinically (MOAA/S) and without the bispectral index or effect-site concentration modelling, we cannot quantitatively separate the direct pharmacologic effect of dose from the contribution of sedation depth. Objective pharmacodynamic assessment, including BIS monitoring and effect-site concentration modelling, could better characterize sedation intensity and drug-exposure profiles in future studies.3,4

On the third point, we share the authors’ interest in the divergence between hiccup incidence and hiccup duration. As discussed in our article, we proposed a pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic threshold-window account: a rapid bolus over 10s produces a steeper early rise in effect-site concentration that may trigger hiccups more readily but also moves patients past the putative excitability window into deeper sedation, whereas a slower bolus over 20s blunts the early peak and lowers the chance of triggering an episode, yet in susceptible individuals may extend the time spent within an intermediate excitability range and so prolong any episode that does occur. We framed this account as hypothesis-generating, and we agree that the clinical significance of hiccup duration beyond incidence remains uncertain. We therefore endorse the recommendation to incorporate clinically meaningful endpoints in future studies, such as interruption of endoscopic manipulation, the need for rescue medication, and procedural difficulty scores, which together would give a fuller picture of the practical impact of remimazolam-associated hiccups.5

We thank the authors again for comments that will help shape the mechanistic and multicentre studies needed to refine remimazolam sedation strategies.
