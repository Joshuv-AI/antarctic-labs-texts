ITPR3 ‐associated neuropathy: Report of a further family with adult onset intermediate Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease
Authors: Javier Cabello‐Murgui, Jesús Jiménez‐Jiménez, Juan J Vílchez, Inmaculada Azorín, Pilar Martí‐Martínez, Elvira Millet, Vincenzo Lupo, Teresa Sevilla, Rafael Sivera
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and Purpose ITPR3 encodes type 3 inositol‐tri‐phosphate receptor (IP3R3), a protein expressed in Schwann cells, predominantly in the paranodal region, and involved in the regulation of Ca2+ release from the endoplasmic reticulum. Dominant variants in ITPR3 have recently been recognized as a rare cause of intermediate Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT).MethodsWe collected the clinical data of a family with autosomal dominant neuropathy whose proband was diagnosed with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) for many years. The genetic diagnosis was achieved by whole exome sequencing.ResultsThe proband developed symmetrical sensory–motor neuropathy with demyelinating features at 32 years old. He was diagnosed with CIDP and received numerous immunomodulatory treatments. However, his condition progressed, leading to severe proximal leg and hand atrophy that confined him to a wheelchair at 60 years. The patient's two sons began to exhibit symptoms suggestive of neuropathy shortly after age 30 years, and the condition was reoriented as inherited. Exome sequencing identified a heterozygous c.4271C > T variant in the ITPR3 gene segregating with the disease. Nerve conduction studies showed a combination of demyelinating and axonal features that vary by nerve, disease duration, and patient. A uniform thickening of the nerves was identified on nerve echography, as was distal symmetric fatty infiltration in lower limb muscle imaging.ConclusionsThe c.4271C > T ITPR3 variant causes a late onset CMT that can be considered an intermediate CMT. Considering the electrophysiological findings and the distribution of IP3R3, we hypothesize that this variant could start as nodal dysfunction that progresses to widespread nerve degeneration.Keywords: intermediate CMT, ITPR3, late onset CMT, node of Ranvier, nodo‐paranodopathy

INTRODUCTION

The term "intermediate Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease" (CMT‐I) is applied to patients with a hereditary sensory–motor neuropathy owning clinical, electrophysiological, and pathological characteristics that cannot be clearly classified as demyelinating (CMT‐1) or axonal (CMT‐2) [1]. Although no standard definition for CMT‐I has been fully accepted to date, it has been broadly considered when motor conduction velocities (MCVs) in the upper limb nerves ranged between 25 and 45 m/s, usually associated with increased distal latencies (DLs) and a preservation of the compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes [1, 2]. Although variants in more than 100 genes have been found causative of CMT, few of them manifest as CMT‐I [2, 3, 4], being pathogenic variants in GJB1 (CMTX), the most frequent genetic defect associated with this phenotype. CMT associated with DRP2 variants is another well‐documented X‐linked intermediate disorder, and dominant variants of ITPR3, which encodes type 3 inositol‐tri‐phosphate receptor (IP3R3), have recently been shown to cause CMT‐I [5, 6, 7]. IP3R3 is mostly located in the paranodal region of Schwann cells and seems to be functionally related to Cx32. Furthermore, dysfunctions in other proteins that act in the IP3R pathway (e.g., TRPV4, FIG4, SBF2) have also been proved to be associated with neuropathy [3].

We report a family with a late onset neuropathy whose proband became symptomatic in the 4th decade of life, being initially misdiagnosed and treated for chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP). However, several decades later, the patient's sons started to have symptoms of polyneuropathy that led to reconsideration of the diagnosis, and consequently a pathogenic variant in ITPR3 was detected in all affected family members.

METHODS

The family reported here is comprised by the proband (II‐2) his two affected sons (III‐1 and III‐2), and his healthy brother (II‐1; Figure 1). Written informed consent was obtained from the participants in this study, and the protocols were approved by the local research ethics committee.

Motor and sensory nerve conduction studies (NCS) were performed under standard conditions at a controlled temperature of ≥32°C. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the feet and lower limb muscles was performed on two patients (III‐1 and III‐2) following the procedure described previously, and muscle fatty substitution was graded from 0 to 4 according to the modified Mercuri score [8, 9].

Peripheral nerve ultrasound examination was completed in Patients III‐1 and III‐2 using a Canon Medical Systems Aplio i700 echograph (Otawara, Japan, 2020) equipped with a high‐frequency broadband probe up to 18 MHz and using a protocol described previously [6]. In each nerve, the cross‐sectional area (CSA) was determined in axial planes by tracing nerves just inside their hyperechoic rims. We obtained bilateral measurements of the C5–C7 nerve roots; median, ulnar, and radial nerves in the arm and forearm; and sural, peroneal, and posterior tibial nerves in the lower limbs. Cutoff values for an abnormal CSA were derived from normative data available in our laboratory that defined the upper limit [10]. Data from ultrasonography was analyzed both online and offline using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

CMT‐1A duplication was analyzed in the proband by multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification. Whole‐exome sequencing (WES) was performed on Individual III‐1, and candidate variants were studied by Sanger sequencing in the other family members, following a protocol described previously [11]. The ITPR3 variant detected was classified following the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines [12], and segregation analysis was performed on all available family members (II‐1, II‐2, III‐1, and III‐2). The Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) was consulted to determine the relative frequency of the variant identified in the general population.

RESULTS

Both the family's pedigree (Figure 1) and the summary of the clinical data (Table 1) are presented here. The proband (II‐2, male) was first referred to our unit at 32 years of age having experienced his first symptom, an alteration of gait, some months earlier. He later developed clumsiness in both hands and on examination, ankle extension weakness was identified (Medical Research Council [MRC] grade 3 bilaterally), with mild cavus feet and atrophy of the anteroexternal muscles in both legs. Thenar atrophy and bilateral weakness in the intrinsic muscle of the hands (MRC 4) were also detected, as well as areflexia in the lower limbs, tactile and vibratory hypoesthesia in the hands and feet, and abnormal gait due to sensory ataxia.

In the proband, electrophysiological studies revealed prolonged distal latencies, decreased conduction velocities, and prolonged F‐wave latency in median nerve (Table 2). The patient underwent extensive evaluation with normal laboratory results, which included the analysis of the complete blood counts, glucose and lipid profiles, proteinogram, immunofixation, porphyrins, vitamin B12, and folic acid. CMT‐1A duplication was ruled out, and the clinical and electrophysiological studies performed on his parents and brother (I‐1, I‐2, and II‐1) were normal. A diagnosis of CIDP was made supported by the NCS (prolonged DLs, MCV > 70% in two nerves, prolonged F‐wave latency, and motor conduction blocks) and the normal clinical and electrophysiological evaluation of the other family members (Figure 1).

None of the immunomodulatory treatments administered over the following years (intravenous immunoglobulin, plasma exchange, corticosteroids, azathioprine, cyclosporine, and cyclophosphamide) offered substantial benefits, and the subject's weakness progressed, causing severe muscle atrophy of proximal legs and hand muscles resulting in a loss of independent ambulation at age 60 years. At that time, we evaluated the possibility of performing a more extensive genetic study, but the patient refused, as his offspring presented no symptoms at that age.

More than 30 years after the proband's initial evaluation, his eldest son (III‐1) requested a consultation because he had begun to experience symptoms resembling those of his father and he had been diagnosed with a neuropathy based on electrophysiological studies. The first evaluation was carried out at the age of 38 years, when he complained of unsteady gait and clumsiness in both hands. He also indicated he had suffered pain and hypoesthesia in his feet and hands since he was 30 years old. On clinical examination, mild distal weakness was observed in the first toe extensors and abductor pollicis brevis. He also had apallesthesia up to the ankles and paresthesia in both toes. The electrophysiological studies showed an increased DL in the median and ulnar nerves, with slowed MCVs in the median nerve (Table 2).

A genetic cause for the neuropathy was suspected, and his brother (III‐2) was also evaluated at the age of 35 years. He had minimal symptoms that had first appeared 2 years before consisting of occasional paresthesia and hypoesthesia in the feet, and a mild loss of dexterity in the hands. Clinical examination showed no motor or sensory deficits, and osteotendinous reflexes were preserved. Mildly cavus feet and hammer toes were observed. Moreover, the electrophysiological study revealed reduced sensory nerve action potentials and CMAPs in the ulnar and peroneal nerves, respectively, with normal MCVs in the upper limbs. However, there was a mild prolongation of DL in the median nerve and of the F waves in the nerves of the upper and lower limbs (Table 2).

WES in Individual III‐1 identified the c.4271C > T variant in the ITPR3 gene in heterozygosity, which was found in all three affected individuals but not in the asymptomatic Subject II‐1 (Figure 1). A segregation study could not be performed on Individuals I‐1 and I‐2 because they were deceased. The c.4271C > T variant modifies an amino acid at codon 1424 of the ITPR3 protein from Thr (hydrophilic/polar) to Met (hydrophobic/nonpolar). Thr1424 is conserved across multiple species (Phylop score is 7.79) This change was predicted to be deleterious in 10 different computational predictions, is not found in gnomAD, and is classified as probably pathogenic according to ClinVar and ACMG criteria.

Muscle MRI revealed a symmetrical fatty infiltration of the intrinsic, flexor, and extensor foot muscles in both patients (Figure 2). Involvement of both the gastrocnemius, and to a lesser extent the peroneal musculature, was evident in Subject III‐1, following a length‐dependent pattern. No hyperintensities were detected in any muscle by short T1 inversion recovery sequences. Nerve ultrasound reflected a global increase in the CSA of all the nerves tested in Patients III‐1 and III‐2, in both the proximal and distal segments (Figure 3; see also the CSA values compared to age‐matched controls in Table S1). Nerve thickening was quite homogeneous and more prominent in the more severely affected patient (III‐1), although no clear segmental nerve thickening was detected. The morphology of the fascicular pattern was mostly normal, but enlarged fascicles were evident in a few nerves.

DISCUSSION

We describe a family in which symptoms first appeared in the proband during the 4th decade of life, mimicking CIDP. The appearance of symptoms in the offspring decades later made us reconsider the diagnosis, and the c.4271C > T (p.Thr1424Met) variant of ITPR3 was detected. This is the sixth family described with a neuropathy associated with ITPR3, and the phenotypic characteristics of this condition are still to be clearly defined [7].

In the proband, the initial diagnosis of inflammatory neuropathy was suspected because of the clinical course, the absence of a family history, and supportive NCS (prolonged DLs and F waves, and reduced MCVs). His first nerve conduction studies met the current criteria for defined CIDP [13, 14, 15]. However, no treatment regime provided any substantial benefit, and the disease progressed over decades until the patient lost the capacity for independent ambulation in his 60s. The diagnosis of CIDP rests upon a combination of clinical, electrodiagnostic, and laboratory features, and on the exclusion of other disorders that may mimic CIDP [13]. In this regard, it is important to consider that certain inherited neuropathies may manifest subacutely and present conduction block on NCS or even an elevated CSF protein [16, 17].

The initial diagnostic challenge was enhanced in our case by the late onset and absence of family history, which were suggestive of a sporadic neuropathy. In this clinical scenario, it is essential to take into account that although most CMT subtypes manifest in the first decades of life, there is a subgroup of late onset inherited neuropathies, and that de novo variants may appear in any individual [18, 19, 20]. In our family, as both parents of the proband were evaluated clinically and by electrophysiology, we hypothesize that the variant detected in him is probably de novo, but we were not able to confirm this by testing his deceased parents.

The IP3R3 protein is involved in metabotropic Ca2+ signaling in the myelin sheath of peripheral nerves, and it is expressed in Schwann cells, particularly close to Cx32 in the paranodal region [21]. Although IP3R3 and Cx32 do not colocalize, their expression in the same membrane areas raises the possibility that Schwann cell Ca2+ signaling may control gap junction activity. Alternatively, gap junctional channels may act as conduits for the radial spread of Ca2+ signals initiated during action potential propagation [21, 22]. To date, only five ITPR3 variants have been described as a cause of hereditary neuropathy; they are summarized in Table 3 [7, 22, 23, 24]. The same ITPR3 variant reported here (p.Thr1424Met) was the first variant detected in a family before this gene was confirmed as responsible for CMT [24]. Clinical data of the proband is quite concordant with our findings, with symptom onset in the 4th decade and intermediate conduction velocities. The p.Val615Met change produces a similar phenotype to that described here, with onset after the 3rd decade of life and intermediate conduction velocities [7]. By contrast, the p.Arg2524Cys variant was described in a single Ashkenazi Jewish individual with an earlier onset and a more severe neuropathy [7]. Functional studies in cell lines of these three variants (p.Val615Met, p. Thr1424Met, and p.Arg2524Cys) exhibited decreased inositol 1,4,5‐trisphosphate receptor Ca2+ release channel activity causing elevated basal [Ca2+], decreased endoplasmic reticulum store Ca2+ content, and subsequent inappropriate activation in stable cell lines expressing human IP3R3. The p.Thr1424Met and p.Val615Met mutants resulted in “leaky” altered but functional IP3R3 channels, in contrast to the channel function in the p.Arg2524Cys mutant, in which the response to stimulation was completely absent. This is concordant with the reported disease severity, as the p.Thr1424Met and p.Val615Met variants were associated with a milder phenotype compared to Arg2524Cys [21]. There are no clinical or functional data regarding other published variants.

The node of Ranvier (NoR) is a molecularly complex structure in which axoglial interactions are organized into functional domains (node, paranode, and juxtaparanode) that are necessary to maintain saltatory conduction in myelinated nerve fibers. Dysfunctions in this structure are pathogenic in diverse neuropathies, now referred to as nodopathies [25, 26, 27]. Electrophysiology studies indicate that nodopathies may share axonal and demyelinating features, making these challenging to diagnose. In this regard, NCS of the three affected individuals are very interesting, as they combine demyelinating features in certain nerves, whereas in others the findings are suggestive of axonal degeneration. We observed widespread characteristic demyelinating findings in the most severely affected proband (increased DL, slow MCV with conduction blocks and prolongation of F waves in all the nerves tested), whereas Patient III‐1 had increased DL in both the median and ulnar nerves, but the MCV was only slowed in the median nerve with a partial conduction block. Finally, in Patient III‐2 we only identified prolongation of DL in the median nerve and prolongation of the F wave in the ulnar and tibial nerves regarding findings suggestive of demyelination. Given the normal MCVs of the median and ulnar nerves of Patient III‐2, if prolonged F waves had not been detected, his neuropathy might easily have been classified as axonal. In all three patients, there is always a parameter denoting nerve conduction alteration, although they are only widespread in patients with greater involvement. Interestingly, this variability in MCVs was also reported in the family with the p.Val615Met variant, the younger patients presenting a more preserved median MCV than the proband (45, 39, and 33 m/s respectively) [7]. In both variants, conduction velocities vary by nerve, disease duration, and patient, which makes us think of a dynamic pathophysiological mechanism. Considering that IP3R3 is expressed in Schwann cells, particularly in the paranodal region [28], we can speculate that the process could originate in NoR, causing a failure of saltatory conduction from one node to the next. The question is whether the action potential‐dependent Schwann cell Ca2+ signaling is essential for the integrity and maintenance of myelin. Further work looking at the downstream effects of these IP3R3 mutations on cell structure and propagation of Ca2+ signaling in Schwann cells may be necessary to identify the specific pathophysiological mechanism.

Regarding the nerve echography, we detected a uniform and symmetric enlargement of the CSA in all tested nerves, both proximally and distally, which was more prominent in the most symptomatic sibling. These findings would support the evolution toward hypertrophic neuropathy. Additional patients will be required to more conclusively define the evolution and pathophysiological process in ITPR3 variants. This echography pattern is similar to that described in CMT‐1A and other demyelinating types of CMT, but not to those in CMT‐2, where the CSA remains normal or only mildly reduced [29, 30, 31, 32]. Muscle MRI is also an important diagnostic tool and potential biomarker in neuropathies, with specific features correlated with weakness, severity, and subtypes of inherited neuropathies [33, 34, 35, 36]. The studies performed in our patients revealed symmetric fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy in the foot muscles of Patients III‐1 and III‐2, and also in the distal muscles of the legs of Patient III‐1, suggesting a genetic neuropathy rather than CIDP.

In summary, our results provide further evidence that a Thr1424Met variant can cause late onset CMT. The variability of NCS in the different members of this family is wide; following the current classification, where it would best fit is in the intermediate phenotype. Understanding the ITPR3 phenotype in depth and evaluating the IP3R3 function in neuronal or animal models will be necessary to evaluate Ca2+ flux as a therapeutic target in CMT.
