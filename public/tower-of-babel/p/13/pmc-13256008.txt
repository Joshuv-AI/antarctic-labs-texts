Alcohol consumption and risk of atrial fibrillation: a pairwise and network meta-analysis
Authors: Zain Ul Abideen Asad, Ali Haider Jafry, Usman Akbar, Christopher Beard, Jehanzeb Ahmed Khan, Satyam Krishan, Muhammad Hurera, Syeda Maheen Raza, Shari Clifton, Jessica Reese, Safi U Khan, Rakesh Gopinathannair, Estelle Torbey, Sunny S Po, Usman Baber, Stavros Stavrakis, Prashanthan Sanders
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsDespite well-established evidence linking alcohol consumption to an increased risk of atrial fibrillation (AF), a clear threshold for safe intake remains undefined. Most clinical guidelines recommend abstinence or moderation, but lack specificity regarding quantitative limits and evidence-based thresholds for primary prevention of AF.Methods and resultsWe performed this systematic review, pairwise meta-analysis, and network meta-analysis to assess the association and dose–response relationship between varying quantities of alcohol consumption and incident AF. The predefined alcohol intake categories included: no intake, very low (<12 g/day), low (12.1–24 g/day), moderate (24.1–48 g/day), high (48.1–60 g/day), and very high (>60 g/day). The pairwise meta-analyses compared each intake category to no intake, and a frequentist network meta-analysis was conducted to integrate direct and indirect comparisons. Pooled risk ratio (RR) for AF incidence were estimated using random-effects models, and a subgroup analysis by sex was performed. Twenty-six studies with nearly 15 million participants were included. As compared to those with no alcohol intake, very high alcohol intake was associated with a 75% increased risk of incident AF (RR 1.75; 95% CI: 1.25–2.44, P = 0.04), intake below 48 g/day was associated with modestly lower AF risk, and the lowest risk was observed at <12 g/day (RR 0.72; 95% CI: 0.63–0.83). The weighted mean follow-up was approximately 6.4 years. Network meta-analysis confirmed these findings and demonstrated a significantly increased AF risk with very high compared to low (RR 1.91), moderate (RR 2.03), and high (RR 2.00) levels. Sex-stratified analyses showed similar results.ConclusionAlcohol consumption is associated with a nonlinear, threshold-dependent relationship with incident AF. While low to moderate intake may not increase AF risk, intake beyond 60 g/day significantly increases the AF risk. These findings may suggest that a more liberal approach to alcohol consumption, particularly at low to moderate levels, could be reasonable in the general population, though further prospective studies are needed to confirm causality and refine individual risk stratification.

In the largest meta-analysis to date, integrating pairwise meta-analysis, meta-regression, and network meta-analysis, we found a J-shaped association between alcohol intake and incident AF.

Introduction

Despite major advances in pharmacological and catheter ablation-based treatments the long-term outcomes for patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) remain modest.1,2 The durable efficacy of a single catheter ablation procedure is limited, with pooled 12-month single procedure success rates of 66.6% for paroxysmal and 51.9% for persistent AF.3 The advances in catheter ablation have overshadowed the role of upstream risk factor modification that remains underutilized despite strong evidence linking modifiable risk factors to the development and recurrence of AF. With the increasing prevalence of AF and limited success of available treatments, a paradigm shift is needed to address the impact of risk factor modification for both primary and secondary prevention of AF.4,5

Modifiable risk factors such as alcohol consumption, obesity, obstructive sleep apnoea, hypertension, and diabetes play a critical role in the pathogenesis of AF.6 The association between alcohol intake and AF was first described as ‘holiday heart syndrome’; yet, subsequent studies have yielded conflicting results.7 Some meta-analyses have suggested linear dose-dependent increase in AF risk,8–10 and others have shown a J-shaped curve, suggesting a lower AF risk with low levels of alcohol intake.11,12 While alcohol consumption is a well-recognized modifiable risk factor for AF, the precise quantity associated with increased risk remains unclear. Current guidelines broadly recommend abstinence or moderation but do not define evidence-based thresholds. This lack of specificity limits personalized counselling and impedes public health efforts aimed at AF prevention.

The understanding of the dose–response relationship between alcohol consumption and the incidence of AF is essential as it can define the individualized risk factor modification and also inform public health recommendations. Therefore, we performed this systematic review and meta-analysis to study the association between different quantities of alcohol consumption and incident AF in males and females. We performed pairwise meta-analyses to examine direct comparisons, and a network meta-analysis to integrate both direct and indirect comparisons.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and PRISMA network meta-analysis extension for network meta-analyses.13–15 The review protocol was developed a priori, however, it was not registered in PROSPERO.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following inclusion criteria: (i) observational studies, case–control studies or randomized controlled trials of patients without a history of AF; (ii) reporting incidence of AF or atrial flutter stratified according to different quantities of alcohol intake; (iii) a comparison arm comprising individuals without alcohol intake or minimal alcohol intake; and (iv) published in a peer-reviewed journal. We excluded (i) studies not reporting the incidence of AF according to different quantities of alcohol intake, (ii) studies without a comparison arm, and (iii) studies that reported only hazard ratio (HR) or risk ratio (RR), but event rates could not be calculated from the provided data. We imposed no restriction on sample size, language, or follow-up duration.

Search strategy and data sources

A comprehensive systematic search strategy was developed under the guidance of an experienced medical librarian (S.C.). We searched Ovid® on Medline and Embase databases and included MeSH terms and keywords relevant to the research question with no date limits (full search strategy in Supplementary material online, Tables S1 and S2). The most recent literature searches were executed on 8 March 2024.

Study selection

Two reviewers (C.B. and J.A.K.) screened the abstracts and titles independently, and conflicts were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (A.H.J.). The retrieved references were exported to Covidence® for search management.16 Full articles were reviewed if the necessary information could not be elicited from the titles or abstracts.16 Data were extracted using a standardized form and included the study characteristics, demographics, comparators, outcomes, and follow-up duration. We also reviewed full texts for beverage type (beer, wine, spirits) when available; however, reporting was inconsistent and rarely linked to AF outcomes by beverage type, so beverage-specific data were not extracted systematically, and no subgroup analyses were performed.

Risk of bias assessment

The Newcastle-Ottawa scale was used to assess for the risk of bias (see Supplementary material online, Table S3).

Outcome measures

Our primary outcome of interest was the incidence of AF and the RR according to different quantities of alcohol intake in active drinkers. The secondary outcomes included the incidence of AF for former drinkers and people with no history of alcohol consumption (never-drinkers). Similar to several prior studies, we defined a standard drink as containing 12 g of alcohol. We stratified alcohol intake into six categories: no intake (0 g/day), very low intake (0.1–12 g/day), low (12.1–24 g/day), moderate intake (24.1–48 g/day), high intake (48.1–60 g/day), and very high intake (> 60 g/day). Alcohol intake was not reported uniformly across the included studies. Several studies provided intake as categorized ranges (e.g. drinks/week or g/day), whereas others reported mean intake or category midpoints. When only a range was provided, we assigned participants to pre-specified categories using the reported mean intake (or midpoint when mean was not available) after standardizing to grams/day (12 g per standard drink), consistent with prior meta-analyses. Subgroup analysis was performed for males and females.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

We performed the pairwise meta-analyses for direct comparisons between different quantities of alcohol consumption vs. control (no alcohol intake), using a random effects model. Treatment effects were reported as RRs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method for dichotomous data. Statistical significance was considered for a two-tailed P-value of <0.05. The I2 index and its 95% CI were used to measure the proportion of total variability in the effect estimates attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than chance. The between-study variance (τ2) was estimated using the DerSimonian–Laird method. The estimated RRs, whether pooled estimates or for individual studies, represented a comparison between increasing alcohol intake groups and a cohort comprising no, or at times, minimal alcohol intake. Data analysis was performed using Review Manager Version 5.4 (Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014).

Dose–response and network meta-analysis

To examine the shape of the association between alcohol consumption and AF, we constructed a dose–response curve by plotting the pooled RR and their 95% CI for each alcohol intake category (0, 6, 18, 36, 54, 75 g) against the corresponding mean daily alcohol consumption. Error bars indicated 95% CIs around each RR estimate, allowing visual assessment of a potential relationship.

We conducted a frequentist network meta-analysis using the netmeta package (version 1.3-0) in R (version 4.3.0) to integrate both direct and indirect evidence across all intake levels. The network meta-analysis used no alcohol intake (0 g/day) as the common reference node, enabling indirect comparison across all exposure levels by leveraging shared comparators. Network plots were generated using the igraph package (version 1.2.10), with edge thickness scaled by inverse variance to visually represent the relative precision of each direct comparison. We evaluated the transitivity assumption by reviewing the distribution of key study-level covariates across comparisons and found no major imbalances. Global inconsistency could not be assessed as the network did not contain any direct head-to-head comparisons between non-zero alcohol intake levels. Consequently, all comparisons between non-zero intake categories in the network meta-analysis are based on indirect evidence via the common reference (0 g/day) and should be interpreted as such.

An overview of study framework and major findings is provided (Figure 1). The literature search yielded 3195 studies; 26 studies met the inclusion criteria after screening (see Supplementary material online, Figure).10,17–41 No studies were excluded due to inability to extract alcohol-intake data according to our pre-specified categorization approach. After standardizing all exposures to grams/day (12 g per standard drink) and mapping reported values (including means) to our pre-specified intake categories, all eligible studies could be incorporated into the quantitative synthesis. No randomized controlled trials were identified, and observational studies were included (case control = 1, retrospective = 1, and prospective = 24). Very low (<12 g/day) vs. no alcohol intake

Ten studies (387 494 patients) reported AF incidence in patients with very low (<12 g/day) alcohol intake compared to no alcohol intake. A total of 6828 events occurred in 248 773 patients (2.7%) in the very low intake group, and 5025 events occurred in 138 721 patients (3.6%) with no alcohol intake. Compared with no alcohol intake, very low alcohol intake was associated with lower risk of incident AF (RR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.63–0.83; I2 = 90%; P = <0.00001) (Figure 2). Subgroup analysis based on sex revealed a statistically significant lower incident AF with very low alcohol intake in both men (RR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.69–0.88; I2 = 72%; P = 0.0001) and women (RR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.55–0.81; I2 = 91%; P = <0.0001) when compared to no alcohol intake.

Low (12.1–24 g/day) vs. no alcohol intake

Fourteen studies (10 469 293 patients) reported AF incidence in patients with low (12.1–24 g/day) alcohol intake compared to no alcohol intake. A total of 64 701 events occurred in 3 791 868 patients (1.7%) in the low intake group, and 170 728 events occurred in 6 677 425 patients (2.6%) with no alcohol intake. Compared with no alcohol intake, low alcohol intake was associated with lower risk of incident AF (RR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.80–0.94; I2 = 96%; P = 0.0004) (Figure 3). This association was observed in both men (RR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.72–0.95; I2 = 86%; P = 0.006) and women (RR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.57–0.99; I2 = 95%; P = 0.04) on subgroup analyses.

Low alcohol intake and incident AF. Forest plots comparing low alcohol intake (12.1–24 g/day) vs. no alcohol intake for incident AF in the overall cohort and by sex.

To investigate the sources of heterogeneity (I2 = 96%), we performed a multivariable meta-regression analysis that included mean age, sex, and hypertension prevalence. In the subset of studies reporting all three covariates (k = 7), the model accounted for 24.09% of the heterogeneity (R2), but the overall test of moderators was not statistically significant (P = 0.18). None of the individual characteristics, such as age (P = 0.61), sex (P = 0.25), or hypertension prevalence (P = 0.13), were significant predictors of the effect size, suggesting that the observed heterogeneity is not primarily driven by these baseline demographics (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2).

Moderate (24.1–48 g/day) vs. no alcohol intake

Seventeen studies (9 687 946 patients) reported AF incidence in patients with moderate (24.1–48 g/day) alcohol intake compared to no alcohol intake. A total of 39 811 events occurred in 1 753 900 patients (2.3%) in the moderate intake group, and 203 498 events occurred in 7 934 046 patients (2.6%) with no alcohol intake. Compared with no alcohol intake, moderate alcohol intake was associated with a slightly lower risk of incident AF, although the estimate was borderline (RR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.75–1.00; I2 = 99%; P = 0.04) (Figure 4). Subgroup analysis based on sex revealed no significant difference in the two arms in men (RR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.73–1.11; I2 = 92%; P = 0.32) and women (RR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.71–1.42; I2 = 93%; P = 0.97).

Moderate alcohol intake and incident AF. Forest plots comparing moderate alcohol intake (24.1–48 g/day) vs. no alcohol intake for incident AF in the overall cohort and by sex.

High (48.1–60 g/day) vs. no alcohol intake

Eleven studies (8 815 338 patients) reported AF incidence in patients with high (48.1–60 g/day) alcohol intake compared to no alcohol intake. A total of 25 390 events occurred in 1 012 351 patients (2.5%) in the high intake group, and 199 308 events occurred in 7 802 987 patients (2.6%) with no alcohol intake. Compared with no alcohol intake, high alcohol intake was not significantly associated with incident AF (RR: 1.08; 95% CI: 0.93–1.26; I2 = 99%; P = 0.32) (Figure 5A). Subgroup analysis based on sex revealed no significant difference in terms of AF incidence for men (RR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.84–1.20; I2 = 64%; P = 0.94) and women (RR: 1.57; 95% CI: 0.72–3.43; I2 = 95%; P = 0.26).

Very high (>60 g/day) vs. no alcohol intake

Four studies (11 825 patients) reported AF incidence in patients with very high (>60 g/day) alcohol intake compared to no alcohol intake. A total of 89 events occurred in 1667 patients (5.3%) in the very high intake group, and 931 events occurred in 10 158 patients (9.1%) with no alcohol intake. Compared with no alcohol intake, very high alcohol intake was associated with increased risk of incident AF (RR: 1.75; 95% CI: 1.25–2.44; I2 = 63%; P = 0.001) (Figure 5B). Subgroup analysis for men and women could not be performed due to insufficient data reporting in the included studies.

Former drinkers vs. current drinkers

Three studies (35 534 patients) reported AF incidence in patients who were former drinkers compared to patients with current alcohol use. A total of 262 events occurred in 10 931 patients (2.4%) who were former drinkers, and 727 events occurred in 24 603 patients (2.9%) with ongoing alcohol intake. The pooled estimates across the three studies suggested former drinkers had no significant difference in AF incidence (RR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.82–1.12; P = 0.75; I2 = 0%; P = 0.58) compared with current drinkers (Figure 6A). Similar results were observed in the subgroup analysis for both men (RR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.82–1.13; P = 0.70; I2 = 0%; P = 0.65) and women (RR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.33–1.83; P = 0.33; I2 = 10%; P = 0.56).

Former drinkers and incident AF. (A) Forest plots comparing former drinkers vs. current drinkers for incident AF. (B) Forest plots comparing former drinkers vs. never drinkers for incident AF.

Former drinkers vs. never drinkers

Three studies (124 501 patients) reported AF incidence in patients who were former drinkers compared to patients with no history of alcohol use. A total of 275 events occurred in 11 307 patients (2.4%) in former drinkers, and 2862 events occurred in 113 194 patients (2.5%) with no history of alcohol intake. The pooled estimates across the three studies suggested former drinkers had no significant difference in AF incidence (RR: 1.19; 95% CI: 0.77–1.83; P = 0.02; I2 = 76%; P = 0.43) compared to never drinkers (Figure 6B).

Dose–response analysis

A categorical dose–response meta-analysis was conducted by pooling RRs for incident AF across six alcohol intake levels, using 0 g/day (no alcohol intake) as the reference. Risk estimates remained below 1.0 at low and moderate intake, crossed the null at high intake (48.1–60 g/day), and rose substantially at very high intake (>60 g/day: RR1.75; 95% CI: 1.25–2.44) (Figure 7A).

Dose–response and network meta-analysis. (A) Dose–response relationship between alcohol intake and incident AF. (B) Network meta-analysis integrating direct and indirect comparisons across alcohol intake categories.

Network meta-analysis

The network meta-analysis included six alcohol intake categories: no intake (0 g/day), very low (<12 g/day), low (12.1–24 g/day), moderate (24.1–48 g/day), high (48.1–60 g/day), and very high (>60 g/day). The network geometry was fully connected and incorporated both direct (solid lines) and indirect (dashed lines) comparisons (Figure 7B). In head-to-head comparisons, very high intake was associated with significantly higher AF risk compared with low (RR 1.91; 95% CI: 1.17–3.09), moderate (RR 2.03; 95% CI: 1.34–3.23), and high intake (RR 2.00; 95% CI: 1.22–3.27), while comparisons between low vs. moderate (RR 0.97; 95% CI: 0.75–1.20) were not statistically significant, whereas moderate vs. high showed a small but statistically significant difference (RR 0.89; 95% CI: 0.64–0.99). Examination of local inconsistency also revealed no meaningful discrepancies between direct and indirect estimates for individual contrasts.

Discussion

This updated meta-analysis is one of the largest to date to assess the association between different quantities of alcohol consumption and incident AF. All included studies were observational, and no randomized trials were identified, therefore findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Our pairwise meta-analysis showed that there was a 75% increased RR of developing AF with very high quantities of alcohol consumption (>60 g/day) as compared to no alcohol intake. Alcohol consumption of <48 g/day was associated with a modestly lower risk of incident AF. There were no significant differences in these associations between men and women. The observed dose–response relationship was non-linear with a steep increase in risk beyond 60 g/day alcohol intake. The findings from our network meta-analysis showed that very high alcohol intake was associated with a two-fold greater AF risk compared with low, moderate, and even high intake levels. In contrast, differences between other adjacent categories were small or non-significant.

These findings underscore the importance of individualized counselling on alcohol consumption, particularly in patients with elevated AF risk or pre-existing cardiovascular disease. It is important to acknowledge that based on this data, while low to moderate intake of alcohol may not increase, and may slightly reduce the AF risk as compared to abstinence, there appears to be a threshold effect beyond which the risk rises steeply.

Low levels of alcohol intake have been reported to have cardioprotective effects, with prior studies demonstrating a low cardiovascular mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events regardless of pre-existing cardiovascular disease.42,43 This is in large part due to a J-shaped relationship between alcohol intake and several cardiovascular risk factors, as low levels of alcohol intake have been associated with improved lipid profile, protective for atherogenesis, and a reduced risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, and chronic kidney disease.44–48 These non-linear relationships are mirrored in the association between alcohol intake and AF incidence in our study.

High levels of alcohol intake, on the other hand, particularly binge drinking, have been shown to predispose to AF. This is mediated by direct myocardial injury, shortened action potential and refractory periods, perturbations in autonomic function (initial sympathetic activation, followed by rebound parasympathetic overdrive), and impaired left atrial mechanics, all of which contribute to adverse atrial remodelling and set the stage for AF development.49,50 Although very low/low intake was associated with lower incident AF in pooled observational data, this should be interpreted cautiously given the likelihood of residual confounding. Modest, non-zero alcohol use may cluster with healthier lifestyles and social contexts (e.g. Mediterranean-style diet patterns, higher activity, and wine with meals), which could partly explain the lower AF incidence. In addition, the ‘no alcohol’ group may include former drinkers or individuals abstaining due to illness (sick-quitter effect or abstainer bias, although reasons for cessation were reported inconsistently), further exaggerating differences. Together, these factors may produce an apparent inverse association at low intake levels without implying a causal benefit.

Our findings should also be interpreted in the broader context of alcohol’s effects on upstream cardiovascular risk factors, particularly hypertension, a key driver of incident AF. A recent dose–response meta-analysis of observational cohorts suggested a causal association between alcohol intake and higher hypertension risk, especially above ∼12 g/day.51 This contrasts with the inverse association observed in our AF analysis at <48 g/day and suggests that lower-intake signals may reflect residual confounding rather than a truly protective effect. Even if incident AF does not consistently rise at low intake in observational datasets, alcohol-related increases in blood pressure at relatively low thresholds could still contribute to longer-term AF risk through hypertension-mediated atrial remodelling. This perspective supports an integrated, comorbidity-focused approach to AF prevention and management, consistent with frameworks such as the atrial fibrillation better care (ABC) pathway and the AF Network (AFNET) and European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA) consensus.52,53

Current guidelines recommend abstinence or moderation in alcohol intake, suggesting <1 standard alcoholic drink per day to minimize the risk of AF. This is based on prior studies suggesting a linear dose–response relationship between alcohol and incident AF. For patients with a known diagnosis of AF pursuing rhythm control, the current guidelines have a Class I recommendation to minimize alcohol intake to ≤3 standard drinks per week or eliminate alcohol intake to reduce the risk of recurrent AF.54

Our findings are similar to those of prior smaller meta-analyses conducted by Giannopoulos et al. and Jiang et al.11,12 Giannopoulos et al. analysed 16 studies with over 13 million patients, studying alcohol consumption at none/low (<14 g/week), moderate (<168 g/week), and heavy (>168 g/week) categories. The authors concluded that up to 14 standard drinks per week seemed to actually decrease the risk of incident AF, with regression analysis showing a J-shaped association. Their meta-analysis did not report sex based subgroup analyses.10 Jiang et al. included 13 studies with nearly 10 million participants, studying the effects of alcohol at different numbers of drinks per day. They observed a linear relationship in men, while reporting a potential non-linear J-shaped relationship in women, in contrast to our findings, which support a non-linear (J-shaped) pattern in both men and women, with no consistent evidence of sex-based differences in the overall direction of association.9 Compared with these prior meta-analyses, our study is the largest to date, including 26 studies and a sample size of nearly 15 million people, and it evaluates the association in both men and women. Additionally, we have examined the dose–response relationships and conducted a network meta-analysis, allowing both direct and indirect comparisons. By standardizing alcohol exposure into pre-specified grams/day categories (up to five non-zero strata), we were able to provide a more granular assessment of dose–response and non-linearity and to better identify a potential threshold at very high intake (>60 g/day). We hope that these categories are carried forward in future studies rather than arbitrary definitions that vary between studies to allow uniformity in comparisons. In sex-stratified analyses, the lower AF incidence observed at very low and low intake levels was consistent in both men and women, with limited sex-disaggregated evidence at higher intake levels. Overall, our results demonstrate a non-linear relationship with a threshold effect at very high alcohol intake. Importantly, our analysis focused on incident AF in individuals without a prior AF history and thus, implications for AF recurrence after catheter ablation or other rhythm control strategies remain uncertain.

Some important directions for future research are also suggested by our analysis. First, it is important to understand the biological mechanisms of the observed association between alcohol intake and incident AF, including the role of autonomic tone, atrial remodelling, and inflammation. Second, future prospective studies and clinical trials should characterize the drinking patterns, such as binge drinking vs. regular alcohol consumption, and their effect on incident AF. Third, it is important to determine whether these observed associations vary by sex, age, comorbidities, and its impact on incident AF and recurrent AF after catheter ablation.

Our study has several limitations. The most important limitation is that this is a meta-analysis of observational studies since no randomized trials were identified during the systematic search. Because the network lacked direct comparisons between non-zero alcohol categories, the relative effects between drinking levels (e.g. low vs. moderate) are based entirely on indirect comparisons anchored to the non-drinking reference, and inconsistency could not be formally evaluated. As a result, inference relies on the assumption that study populations and key effect modifiers are comparable across intake strata, which is challenging to verify in observational data. Accordingly, these network estimates should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and complementary to the pairwise analyses rather than definitive evidence of causal differences between drinking strata. Alcohol intake was self-reported in all included studies, and therefore, susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias. Moreover, it is difficult to ascertain how the drinking patterns changed for individual participants during the course of follow-up. We were also unable to account for social determinants of health (e.g. socioeconomic status, access to care), which may influence both alcohol exposure patterns and AF detection.55 Although we standardized alcohol intake into pre-specified categories, studies reporting only mean intake may have included substantial within-category variability, introducing exposure misclassification. Another limitation could be the confounding factors for the development of AF, since AF has several complex risk factors that were not accounted for in this study. We were also unable to categorize the effects of alcohol intake according to the type of alcoholic beverage consumed. Finally, our study evaluated incident AF using a conventional binary definition based on the presence or absence of AF, and could not assess whether alcohol influences AF burden, which is an emerging clinically meaningful metric.56 More large-scale prospective studies with close follow-up, diligent monitoring of alcohol intake, and better accounting for confounders are needed to determine the relationship between alcohol intake and AF incidence and studies using continuous or device-based monitoring are needed to determine whether alcohol affects AF burden and progression.

This large-scale meta-analysis of over 15 million individuals across 26 studies provides a comprehensive and updated assessment of the relationship between alcohol consumption and incident AF. The findings reveal a non-linear, threshold-based association, with the lowest risk at low to moderate alcohol intake and a significant increase in AF risk beyond 60 g/day. These findings highlight the importance of individualized alcohol counselling as part of AF prevention strategies and reinforce the need for future research.
