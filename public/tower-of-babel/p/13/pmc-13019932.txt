High-throughput phenotypic screening of Medicines for Malaria Venture's Hit Generation Library 1 identifies new nematocidal chemotypes
Authors: Aya C Taki, Joseph J Byrne, Bill CH Chang, Benoît Laleu, Timothy NC Wells, Abdul Jabbar, Robin B Gasser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParasitic helminths impose substantial health and economic burdens on humans and livestock, and widespread resistance to existing anthelmintic classes underscores the urgent need for new chemotypes with distinct mechanisms of action as part of integrated parasite control strategies. Here, we performed a large-scale phenotypic screen of the Medicines for Malaria Venture Hit Generation Library 1 (HGL1), testing 139,916 compounds (98.7% of the 141,786-compound library) against exsheathed third-stage larvae of Haemonchus contortus, with selective cross-species evaluation in Caenorhabditis elegans. Using infrared-based motility and developmental assays in 384-well format, the platform delivered excellent performance across > 360 plates (mean Z′ = 0.799 ± 0.012; signal-to-background = 65.6 ± 9.8). We identified 272 primary hits (0.194%) and confirmed 110 active compounds with reproducible inhibition of larval motility and development. Of these, 39 exhibited IC50 < 10 μM and 33 caused complete developmental arrest at ≤ 12.5 μM, accompanied by characteristic phenotypes such as eviscerated, curved and coiled forms. Four of the 39 compounds were non-toxic to HepG2 cells (CC50 ≥ 20 μM; MC50 ≥ 20 μM), and a subset displayed favourable physicochemical properties (logD < 3; polar surface area < 100 Å2; metabolic stability > 60%), with ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion) profiling available for a prioritised subset. Integration of potency, selectivity and/or ADME data enabled the prioritisation of candidates, including 16 that met stringent criteria for medicinal-chemistry progression. These findings demonstrate that a chemically curated library originally developed for antimalarial discovery might yield potent, selective nematocidal scaffolds and support a scalable framework for repurposing discovery libraries across divergent parasite groups.Keywords: Haemonchus contortus, Caenorhabditis elegans, Early anthelmintic discovery, Phenotypic screening, High-throughput screening, MMV Hit Generation Library 1, Nematocidal compounds, Drug resistance

Introduction

Parasitic helminths, including trematodes (flukes), cestodes (tapeworms) and nematodes (roundworms), infect billions of humans and animals worldwide, causing chronic disease, reduced productivity and major economic losses (Despommier et al., 2019; Bowman, 2020). Collectively, these parasites represent some of the most neglected but globally consequential infectious agents, tightly linked to poverty, food insecurity and limited access to medical or veterinary care (Kaminsky and Mäser, 2025; WHO, 2025a, 2025b). In livestock, helminth infections often reduce weight gain, milk and wool yield, and fertility (Bowman, 2020; Charlier et al., 2020), while in humans they contribute to malnutrition, anaemia and/or impaired physical and cognitive development (WHO, 2025a). Their pervasive impact in a range of hosts exemplifies the One Health principle, in which diseases at the human–animal–environment interface affect public health, agriculture and economic resilience in tandem (UN, 2025). Parasitic nematodes account for a major burden on global food and fibre production (Charlier et al., 2020; Bowman, 2020). Gastrointestinal nematodes of the order Strongylida (clade V) are particularly widespread and damaging in ruminants and monogastric livestock. Notable genera include Haemonchus, Ostertagia, Teladorsagia, Trichostrongylus, Cooperia and Nematodirus, which collectively cause billions of dollars in annual losses (Roeber et al., 2013; Charlier et al., 2020). In humans, soil-transmitted nematodes such as Ancylostoma duodenale, Necator americanus and Ascaris lumbricoides infect more than one billion people, often co-endemic with animal parasites in resource-limited settings (Jourdan et al., 2018; Despommier et al., 2019; Mawrie et al., 2025; WHO, 2025a, 2025b).

The control of these parasites relies largely on a limited number of anthelmintic classes. Since the 1960s, benzimidazoles, imidazothiazoles, macrocyclic lactones, amino-acetonitrile derivatives and spiroindoles have formed the basis of parasite treatment and management. Yet resistance in animals to each has emerged within years of introduction (Kotze and Prichard, 2016; Charlier et al., 2020). Multidrug resistance is now widespread, compromising animal production and threatening sustainable control. In human medicine, treatment of soil-transmitted nematodes still largely relies on benzimidazoles, for which declining efficacy is increasingly reported (Walker et al., 2021; Coffeng et al., 2024; WHO, 2025a). This growing resistance situation emphasises the need for continued efforts to screen for novel chemotypes and new modes of action. Phenotypic high-throughput screening (HTS) has re-emerged as a route to anthelmintic discovery (Herath et al., 2021). Unlike target-based approaches, phenotypic screens capture whole-organism responses, enabling the identification of compounds that perturb complex biological processes in worms. The development of automated motility and developmental assays has allowed large-scale testing of chemical libraries against parasitic and free-living nematodes under controlled conditions. Assays employing larvae of Haemonchus contortus and the free-living nematode Caenorhabditis elegans are used for quantifying motility and developmental inhibition (Taki et al., 2021a, 2021b). In these assays, the infrared-based WMicroTracker ONE platform measures motility by detecting beam interruptions caused by larval movement, achieving Z′-factors of 0.7–0.8 and throughput of ∼10,000 compounds per week (Taki et al., 2021a). This system combines biological relevance with potential industrial-scale efficiency, enabling systematic exploration of chemical diversity for nematocidal activity.

Parallel advances in small molecule library design have led to the generation of curated, drug-like compound collections optimised for physicochemical balance, solubility and chemical tractability. A notable example is the Medicines for Malaria Venture (MMV) Hit Generation Library 1 (HGL1), developed to replenish the antimalarial pipeline with chemically diverse, developable scaffolds (Dechering et al., 2022). HGL1 comprises 141,786 compounds selected from 8.7 million commercial candidates through multi-parameter optimisation, excluding reactive and pan-assay interference motifs (Baell, 2016) while maximising chemical diversity. Screening of HGL1 against the malaria parasite, Plasmodium falciparum using a NanoLuc-based phenotypic cascade identified 75 screening actives (0.05% hit rate) and 33 confirmed actives with potent, reproducible activity, demonstrating the robustness of the library and workflow (Dechering et al., 2022). The associated chemical-space analysis revealed broad coverage relative to known drugs, indicating its potential as a source of unexplored scaffolds.

Given the relative evolutionary conservation of some core metabolic, ion-transport and signalling pathways, the HGL1 library represents a resource for the discovery of drugs across some parasite groups. Applying this chemically curated collection to the phenotypic screening of nematodes provides a unique opportunity to identify novel anthelmintic candidates while leveraging an open-access, reproducible discovery framework. This approach aligns with current global priorities to expand the accessible chemical space for antiparasitic discovery and to democratise innovation for neglected parasitic diseases of both animals and humans (UN, 2025; WHO, 2025a, 2025b). Here, we evaluate the MMV HGL1 for activity against H. contortus and C. elegans using validated, high-throughput phenotypic assays. The study was designed to determine whether a chemical library originally created for the discovery of anti-malaria drugs could yield nematocidal compounds with potential for future optimisation. By integrating advanced library design with phenotypic screening technologies, this work establishes a framework for early-phase drug discovery across divergent parasite groups within an open, collaborative paradigm.

Materials and methods

Nematode procurement and maintenance

The H. contortus Haecon-5 strain was maintained in helminth-free Merino sheep at the University of Melbourne under approved ethics (permit 23983). Six-month-old males were orally infected with 7000 third-stage larvae (L3s) and monitored until patency (∼28 days). Faeces were cultured at 27 °C and >90% humidity for seven days to obtain L3s (Preston et al., 2015). Larvae were harvested by the Baermann technique, washed and stored in physiological saline at 11 °C for up to six months. Immediately before use, L3s were exsheathed in 0.15% sodium hypochlorite for 20 min at 38 °C (Taki et al., 2021a), washed five times (800×g, 5 min, 22–24 °C) and suspended in supplemented Luria–Bertani broth (LB∗; 100 IU mL−1 penicillin, 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin, 0.25 μg mL−1 amphotericin B). Adult female H. contortus were collected from abomasa of infected sheep at necropsy, washed repeatedly in phenol-red-free RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with the same antimicrobial cocktail (RPMI∗) and used immediately for adult-stage assays. The Caenorhabditis elegans Bristol N2 strain was maintained on nematode-growth medium plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50 at 20 °C (Brenner, 1974). Synchronous fourth-stage larvae (L4s) were generated by bleaching gravid adults, hatching eggs overnight in M9 buffer to obtain arrested first-stage larvae (L1s) and regrowing them to L4 on nematode-growth medium plates (Taki et al., 2021b). The H. contortus Haecon-5 strain and the Caenorhabditis elegans Bristol N2 strain represent well-characterised laboratory lines widely used for anthelmintic screening studies; both strains are considered drug-susceptible.

Compound library, high-throughput screening and dose–response assays

The Medicines for Malaria Venture Hit Generation Library 1 (HGL1; information available at www.mmv.org) contains 141,786 structurally diverse, drug-like compounds selected from 8.7 million commercial candidates using multi-parameter optimisation for novelty, solubility and balanced physicochemical properties while excluding reactive or pan-assay-interference motifs (Dechering et al., 2022). Compounds were supplied at 10 mM in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted to 40 μM in LB∗ immediately before use, giving a final screening concentration of 20 μM (≤0.4% DMSO). Screening was performed using a WMicroTracker ONE instrument (Phylumtech, Argentina), which quantifies larval motility via infrared beam interruptions (Simonetta and Golombek, 2007; Taki et al., 2021a). For primary screening, each well of a 384-well plate (Corning 3860) contained 80 xL3s of H. contortus in 20 μL LB∗; 20 μL of compound or control was added (final volume 40 μL). Negative controls consisted of LB∗ with 0.4% DMSO (n = 16 per plate), and positive controls were monepantel (20 μM), moxidectin (20 μM) and M-666 (20 μM; Le et al., 2019). Plates were incubated at 38 °C, 10% CO2 and >90% humidity for 90 h, and motility was recorded for 15 min (Mode 1; Threshold Average). Counts were normalised to plate controls, and compounds producing ≥70% motility reduction were designated primary hits. Plate quality was assessed using the Z′-factor (Zhang et al., 1999); plates with Z′ ≥ 0.7 and S/B > 10 were accepted. Plates were then incubated to 168 h, fixed in 2.5% Lugol's iodine and examined microscopically (Leica M80). Compounds inhibiting xL3-to-L4 development and/or inducing non-wild-type phenotypes (eviscerated, curved, coiled, straight or skinny) were recorded as active. To confirm activity of primary hits, a secondary screen was performed of each compound at 20 μM on H. contortus xL3s in triplicate, measuring motility reduction at 90 h and 168 h and assessing for xL3-L4 development inhibition and non-wildtype phenotypes at 168 h. Confirmed actives were subsequently evaluated in a nine-point dose–response format (50 μM–0.2 μM) in 96-well plates containing 300 xL3s of H. contortus or 100 L4s of C. elegans per well in 100 μL LB∗. Reference standards were monepantel and moxidectin. Plates were incubated under identical conditions, and motility or development was assessed at 168 h (H. contortus) or 40 h (C. elegans). IC50 values were calculated by four-parameter logistic regression (GraphPad Prism v9.5.0). Following confirmatory (secondary) screening, compounds that consistently reduced H. contortus xL3 motility by ≥ 70% were tested in triplicate for activity against adult female H. contortus in 24-well plates, each well containing 50 μM compound in 500 μL RPMI for 24 h (Taki et al., 2020, 2022).

Cytotoxicity, mitotoxicity, ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion) and data analysis

HepG2 human hepatoma cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5.5 × 104 cells per well in 80 μL DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and the same antimicrobial additives used for nematode cultures) and incubated for 16 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were exposed for 48 h to seven two-fold serial dilutions of each compound (50–1.6 μM). For mitotoxicity assessment, cells were serum-starved for 4 h prior to exposure and assayed in galactose-supplemented Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium in place of glucose (Kamalian et al., 2015). Cell viability was quantified by crystal-violet staining (Śliwka et al., 2016). Doxorubicin (10 μM) and M-666 (10 μM) served as positive controls, whereas monepantel and moxidectin were included as non-toxic reference compounds. CC50 and MC50 values were calculated using four-parameter logistic regression.

Physicochemical properties were compiled for all confirmed active compounds and included calculated lipophilicity (logD) and topological polar surface area (TPSA). ADME profiling for a prioritised subset of compounds was provided by Medicines for Malaria Venture (MMV) and comprised standard in vitro assessments of absorption-, distribution-, metabolism- and excretion-related properties. Physicochemical and ADME parameters are reported in Table S1.

All experimental datasets generated in this study, including raw motility counts, concentration–response curves and cytotoxicity outputs, are provided in the Supplementary Information and deposited in an open-access repository (Zenodo; doi pending). Statistical significance was assessed for experimental assay data using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc test or unpaired t-tests (p < 0.05), as appropriate. Selectivity indices (SI) were calculated as SI = CC50/IC50 for compounds for which both potency and cytotoxicity data were available; when CC50 values were reported as threshold values (e.g. ≥ 20 μM), SI values were expressed as minimum estimates.

Results

A total of 139,916 compounds from the MMV HGL1 library were screened against H. contortus xL3s at 20 μM in 384-well format. Assay performance remained stable throughout the campaign, with a mean Z′ of 0.799 ± 0.012 and a signal-to-background ratio of 65.6 ± 9.8, confirming the robustness and reproducibility of the screening platform (Fig. 1; Table 1 and Table S1). The primary screen identified 272 compounds (0.194 %) that reduced larval motility by ≥ 70 % at 90 h. These hits, representing a broad range of chemical scaffolds, were progressed to secondary screening for confirmation.

Secondary screening identified 110 reproducibly active compounds, 109 (99 %) of which induced distinct, non-wild-type larval phenotypes, most commonly the eviscerated form (n = 78) and curved morphology (n = 11), while 75 compounds caused complete developmental arrest, 12 without overt deformation (Table 1; Table S1). These consistent and well-resolved phenotypes reflect disruptions to key aspects of larval biology, including cuticular integrity, tissue organisation, moulting and osmotic regulation, and provide preliminary evidence that the active chemotypes may interfere with diverse molecular pathways. Dose–response profiling of the 110 confirmed actives revealed quantifiable IC50 values ranging from 0.36 to 48.68 μM (mean 12.67 ± 1.2 μM). Thirty-nine compounds exhibited IC50 < 10 μM, and 33 completely inhibited xL3-to-L4 development at concentrations ≤12.5 μM (Fig. 2a, b, d), highlighting a substantial subset with high potency and pronounced developmental effects.

To assess the broader biological relevance of these chemotypes, all 110 confirmed actives were evaluated against L4s of C. elegans. Only three compounds reduced C. elegans motility by ≥ 70 % at 40 h (Table S1), indicating limited cross-species activity and underscoring marked susceptibility differences between the parasitic and free-living nematodes. These findings emphasise that while C. elegans provides useful comparative data, it is not a direct predictor of H. contortus responses. In parallel, activity against the adult female stage of H. contortus, the clinically relevant, blood-feeding stage responsible for pathogenicity in sheep, was assessed using the top 16 confirmed actives (those achieving ≥70 % xL3 motility inhibition at 90 h). Of these, six compounds (MMV1685284, MMV1754683, MMV1733283, MMV1651879, MMV1771652 and MMV117488) achieved 88–100 % motility inhibition at 50 μM, five showed moderate activity (50–67 %), and the remaining five produced limited inhibition (25–33 %) (Fig. 2c). One of the top 16 actives (MMV1685284) caused lethality in C. elegans, further reinforcing species-specific differences in nematode drug susceptibility.

Physicochemical profiling of the 110 confirmed active compounds showed that 67 (∼60 %) possessed favourable drug-like properties, including calculated lipophilicity within the moderate range (logD <3) and topological polar surface area <100 Å2 (Table S1). The 16 top-ranked compounds (≥70 % xL3 motility inhibition at 90 h) were advanced to cytotoxicity and mitotoxicity evaluation, and 14 of those commercially available were also subjected to ADME profiling provided by MMV. Four compounds (MMV1712399, MMV1698561, MMV1650613 and MMV1644003) were non-toxic to HepG2 cells (CC50 ≥ 20 μM; MC50 ≥ 20 μM), whereas two compounds (MMV1685284 and MMV1733283) were highly cytotoxic (CC50 < 0.1 μM). Selectivity indices (SI = CC50/IC50) were calculated for compounds for which both potency and cytotoxicity data were available and are reported in Table S1. Collectively, these integrated datasets define the activity, selectivity and safety profiles of the confirmed actives and provide a solid foundation for subsequent optimisation and mechanistic investigation.

Discussion

This large-scale phenotypic screen of the MMV HGL1 collection identified 110 confirmed active compounds from 139,916 tested, demonstrating that diverse, potent and selective nematocidal chemotypes can be obtained from a curated drug-like library. Assay performance remained consistently high (mean Z′ = 0.79; S/B = 65.6), and 39 compounds inhibited H. contortus larvae with IC50 < 10 μM while 33 produced complete developmental arrest at ≤ 12.5 μM. These results support the design principles underlying HGL1, emphasising structural diversity, balanced physicochemical properties and exclusion of pan-assay-interference motifs, and indicate the robustness and scalability of the H. contortus screening platform. The screening workflow implemented here aligns with the multi-layered selection strategy described by Dechering et al. (2022) for antimalarial discovery, in which high assay quality (Z′), structured confirmation, quantitative dose–response assessment and early cytotoxicity and ADME filtering are used to minimise false positives and prioritise tractable chemotypes.

Previous phenotypic screening studies against H. contortus have evaluated smaller chemical libraries, including the Pathogen Box and Pandemic Response Box collections (Preston et al., 2016; Taki et al., 2021a, 2021b, 2022; Shanley et al., 2022). Compared with these earlier studies, the present work evaluates a substantially larger and chemically curated library while maintaining comparable screening thresholds and assay performance criteria. The integration of potency, cytotoxicity and physicochemical descriptors in the present study extends earlier approaches by incorporating early ‘developability’ considerations into compound prioritisation.

The phenotypic diversity induced by active compounds provides insight into perturbations of key biological processes. The five dominant larval morphotypes – eviscerated, coiled, curved, straight and skinny – are well established for H. contortus assays (e.g., Nguyen et al., 2019; Ramage et al., 2021; Taki et al., 2021a; Shanley et al., 2022) and reflect interference with biological processes involving cuticular integrity, tissue organisation, moulting and osmotic regulation (cf. Jiao et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020). Eviscerated and curved morphologies are consistent with disruption of the xL3–L4 transition, whereas developmental arrest without overt morphological deformation may reflect inhibition of essential metabolic or biosynthetic pathways. These phenotypes provide informative entry points for mechanistic studies, including transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling of chemically perturbed development.

Cross-species evaluation showed that three of the 16 prioritised compounds were also active against C. elegans, although one compound (MMV1685284) induced lethality in C. elegans. Such cross-activity confirms assay translatability but should not be interpreted as evidence of conserved target engagement. Comparative analyses show pronounced divergence in susceptibility and structure–activity relationships between H. contortus and C. elegans, with compounds potent in one species often showing limited activity in the other (Shanley et al., 2024a, 2024b). Proteomic analyses likewise reveal species-specific patterns of compound-stabilised proteins, indicating distinct pharmacodynamic profiles (Shanley et al., 2024a). Accordingly, C. elegans appears to serve primarily as a genetically tractable system for functional interrogation rather than a predictor of parasitic nematode activity. These observations, together with the stringent screening criteria applied here, indicate the precision of this study relative to earlier helminth screens that used smaller libraries and lacked structured confirmatory stages (e.g., Preston et al., 2015; Preston et al., 2016).

Integration of physicochemical, ADME and cytotoxicity data further refined the active chemical space. Of the 110 confirmed actives, 67 (∼60%) exhibited favourable drug-like properties, including calculated lipophilicity within the moderate range (logD <3) and topological polar surface area <100 Å2, often associated with membrane permeability and reduced metabolic clearance. Sixteen and fourteen top-ranked compounds underwent cytotoxicity and ADME profiling, respectively; four were non-toxic in HepG2 cells, whereas two displayed high cytotoxicity, emphasising the importance of early selectivity assessment. Stratification of candidates into priority groups, integrating potency, developmental inhibition, physicochemical suitability and/or preliminary safety, identified compounds most compatible with medicinal-chemistry progression and a set of structurally diverse scaffolds for future exploratory target identification (cf. Table S1). This multi-parameter framework links optimisation opportunities with the broader objective of diversifying anthelmintic chemotypes. The 16 compounds identified in this study represent early discovery candidates and will require medicinal-chemistry optimisation to improve potency, selectivity and pharmacological properties before further development as anthelmintic leads can be considered. Evaluation of optimised compounds in relevant infection models, such as experimentally infected sheep or rodent systems, will represent an important step in validating the translational relevance of the in vitro screening platform.

The present investigation illustrates the value of redeploying curated diversity libraries across parasite systems. HGL1 spans broad chemical space while retaining favourable drug-like properties (Dechering et al., 2022), and several scaffolds identified here are also represented in the Pathogen Box and Pandemic Response Box (Preston et al., 2016; Jiao et al., 2020; Shanley et al., 2022). Cross-referencing these resources may facilitate recognition of chemotype families with wider antiparasitic potential, although mechanistic convergence across taxonomic groups should not be assumed. From a translational perspective, the findings reinforce the continued importance of phenotypic screening, particularly in parasites for which molecular target validation is not yet routinely undertaken (cf. Shanley et al., 2025), and demonstrate that academically operated platforms can deliver sustained, industrial-grade performance. In conclusion, this study establishes a scalable and repeatable framework for high-throughput anthelmintic discovery and demonstrates that curated chemical diversity originally assembled for antimalarial research can be productively leveraged to identify new chemotypes active against nematodes. More broadly, the work emphasises the potential of open-access compound libraries to accelerate anthelmintic discovery and support collaborative strategies aimed at addressing the growing challenge of drug resistance.
