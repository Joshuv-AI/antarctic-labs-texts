Cervical conization and high risk - HPV status changes in relation to menopause: a retrospective longitudinal study based on generalized additive mixed models
Authors: Jie Zhou, Lin jie Su, Juan Chen, Hong bo Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection outcomes and cervical lesion recurrence after conization is well documented. However, the temporal dynamics of human papillomavirus (HPV) status post-conization, especially in postmenopausal women, remain unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study consecutively enrolled 598 patients who underwent conization, all of whom had baseline HR-HPV positivity and histologically confirmed cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) 2–3. All patients had at least one follow - up HPV test after surgery. Temporal changes in HPV status following conization and their association with menopause were analyzed using Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMM).ResultsAmong the 598 patients, GAMM analysis revealed a significant decrease in HPV positivity risk over time. Adjusting for confounders, the overall risk of HPV positivity decreased by 59% every 4 months within the first 24 months post-conization (OR: 0.41, 95% CI: 0.35–0.47). After 24 months, the risk continued to decline by 7% every 4 months (OR: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.81–1.06). Menopause status significantly influenced these temporal changes; postmenopausal patients had a 45% increased risk of HPV positivity every 4 months after conization compared to premenopausal patients (OR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.13–1.85), particularly within the first 24 months (p = 0.0032).ConclusionsIn patients undergoing conization, longitudinal HPV infection status changes were independently associated with menopause. The risk of HPV positivity decreased significantly in the first 24 months post-surgery, with a more pronounced decline in premenopausal patients, informing HPV testing strategies post-conization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11657-0.Keywords: HPV infection, Menopause status, Time-varying pattern (TVP)

Novelty and impact

The longitudinal changes in HPV infection status after conization exhibit a time-varying curve, with a significant decrease in HPV positivity risk within 24 months post-surgery, and higher HPV positivity risk in menopausal patients compared to premenopausal patients.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a common malignant gynecological tumor in women, and persistent high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection is the primary cause [1, 2]Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) is a precancerous lesion of cervical cancer. Conization has been widely recognized as an important treatment for cervical precancerous lesions and human papillomavirus (HPV) clearance [3]. However, approximately 5–25% of women still have persistent HPV infection after surgery, which is associated with an increased risk of residual lesions and disease recurrence [4–6]. Moreover, these patients are at risk of developing vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia [7] and long-term risk of cervical invasive lesions [8]. HPV testing after conization can serve as a surveillance tool to identify women at higher risk of recurrence. However, more than one-third of patients with recurrent detection have early post-conization HPV negativity, and most return to HPV positivity during routine screening, indicating the need for repeated HPV assessment [9]. Previous studies have shown that significant independent predictors of HPV clearance after conization involve age, menopause status, and lesion grade [10]with lower clearance rates observed in patients over 55 years of age [11]. More importantly, compared to younger patients, older women receiving treatment for high-grade CIN face a significantly elevated risk of developing cervical or vaginal cancer [12]. This underscores the unique clinical challenges in managing cervical lesions among perimenopausal women, attributable to age-related anatomical changes, which markedly reduce the sensitivity of colposcopic detection [13, 14]. Furthermore, this population exhibits distinct HPV clearance patterns: HPV infections in older women may reflect historical exposures rather than recent sexual activity [15]. Although HPV 16 prevalence is lower in older women [16]its progression to invasive carcinoma is more aggressive [17]. These biological peculiarities highlight the urgent need for optimized surveillance strategies tailored to this high-risk group.

Previous studies have demonstrated that conization is effective for both short-term and long-term clearance of HPV infections [18, 19]. However, in clinical practice, HPV retesting often occurs at relatively fixed time points. The timing and frequency of HPV retesting vary due to the relatively high cost of HPV testing and the requirement for specialized sampling by gynecologists [20]. Moreover, most prior studies have focused on HPV status within a specific time frame [21]without considering the dynamic changes in HPV infection status over time. Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMM) can readily accommodate unbalanced and unequally spaced observations [22]making them an ideal tool for analyzing longitudinal data. Given the lack of information on the dynamic changes in HPV status following conization, this study aims to investigate the temporal changes in HPV status after conization and their association with menopause using GAMM.

Methods

Study design and cohort characteristics

This study is a retrospective cohort study. Data were collected from patients who underwent cervical conization at the Yue bei People’s Hospital in Shao guan City, Guangdong Province, from July 1, 2018, to June 30, 2021. Indications for cervical conization in this study included: (1) histological confirmation of high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) in cervical biopsy specimens; (2) thinPrep cytologic test (TCT) results indicating HSIL with negative or unsatisfactory colposcopic findings. (3) significant discrepancy between cervical cytology results and histopathological diagnosis from colposcopy-guided biopsy. Inclusion criteria for follow-up: (1) patients eligible for cervical conization who had received their first conization; (2) with complete and reliable clinical and follow-up records; (3) with preoperative HR-HPV infection and a final pathological diagnosis of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or 3 (CIN2-3). The final pathological diagnosis was established based on the highest grade of histological findings from both biopsy and conization specimens. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients lacking menopause status results; (2) preoperative HPV negative or low-risk HPV infection; (3) patients with immune system diseases or undergoing immunosuppressive therapy; (4) patients who were pregnant during the follow-up period; (5) patients who did not return for postoperative HPV retesting and were lost to follow-up; (6) Cases with final pathological diagnoses of Invasive Cervical Carcinoma (ICC) and low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) or lower. (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of study population

The collected data included age, menopause status, HPV Genotype, pathological biopsy, surgical methods, hospitalization mode, insurance type, and margin status. HPV detection was performed using a 23-type HPV genotyping kit certified by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA). The kit employs multiplex PCR amplification (using the BIOER GeneTouch PCR instrument) combined with reverse dot blot hybridization technology to detect 17 high-risk types (including HPV 16/18, etc.) and 6 low-risk types of HPV. TCT detection was performed using the ThinPrep 2000 automated slide preparation system to process samples and stained with the Papanicolaou method. The results were independently interpreted by two senior pathologists according to the 2014 Bethesda System for Reporting Cervical Cytology. The surgical approaches included Loop Electrosurgical Excision Procedure (LEEP) and Cold Knife Conization (CKC), performed by attending gynecologists or higher-qualified physicians in the standard operating rooms. Pathology results were assessed and confirmed by two pathologists with senior titles. Patients with pathological diagnoses of cervical cancer had been excluded. A positive margin was defined as the presence of CIN 2 or higher within 1 mm of the margin. All data were extracted from the electronic medical records system of Yuebei People’s Hospital by two professionals and cross-checked to ensure accuracy and reliability. As the study involved no personally identifiable information, the Medical Ethics Committee of Yuebei People’s Hospital (approval no. KY-2023-135) waived the requirement for written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the same committee and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Follow-up

The study recorded the timing and results of all post-conization HPV retesting, TCT and biopsy under colposcopy. Recurrence or persistence was defined as the histological confirmation of CIN of any grade in specimens obtained from total hysterectomy after conization, follow-up cervical biopsies or endocervical curettage performed [23]. To clearly reflect the long-term trends in HPV follow-up, the study used 4-month intervals as time nodes, which is common and reasonable in clinical research and helps better understand the long-term impact of HPV.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population. Categorical variables are presented as absolute counts (percentages), and continuous variables as means ± standard deviation (SD). Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMM) were employed to investigate changes in HPV status over time between groups (Fig. 2), and to adjust for covariates while examining the relationship between post-conization HPV status and menopause (Table 2). GAMM integrates the generalized additive model (GAM) with the generalized linear mixed model (GLMM), making it suitable for analyzing nonlinear relationships, temporal/spatial dependencies, and handling random effects arising from repeated measures or hierarchical data structures. Model I was unadjusted, whereas Model II was adjusted for covariates such as age, single/multiple infection, etc. This is the primary distinction between the two models.

Results

Patient characteristics

Study population

Among 598 patients identified in our database, 475 premenopausal and 123 postmenopausal women ultimately underwent conization. The average age of premenopausal patients was 39.26 years old (SD 7.91), significantly lower than that of postmenopausal patients (SD 5.68, p < 0.001). The distribution of HPV single and multiple infections was similar, with 69.68% of pre-menopausal patients and 68.29% of post-menopausal patients having HPV single infection (p = 0.765). The infection rate of HPV 16/18 was relatively similar, at 50.11% and 52.85% (p = 0.588). Significant differences were observed in surgical methods, with 81.89% of premenopausal patients and 95.93% of postmenopausal patients underwent LEEP surgery (p < 0.001). And the type of hospitalization was slightly different, 16.21% of premenopausal and 13.01% of postmenopausal patients were day surgery patients (p = 0.382). There also was a significant difference in insurance status, with 45.05% of premenopausal patients and 69.92% of postmenopausal patients having resident medical insurance (p < 0.001). The status of surgical margins of the surgical specimen was also similar, with 84.42% of premenopausal patients and 78.05% of postmenopausal patients having negative margins (p = 0.093). However, a significant difference was observed in the proportion of hysterectomy, with 19.16% of premenopausal patients and 60.98% of postmenopausal patients undergoing hysterectomy (p < 0.001). The incidence of recurrence and persistence of post-operative lesions was higher in postmenopausal patients than in premenopausal patients (39.02% vs. 19.58%, p < 0.001). (see Table 1).

Differences in HPV infection dynamics following conization between premenopausal and postmenopausal patients

GAMM were used for data analysis, and Figs. 2 and 3 illustrate the temporal trends of HPV status. Figure 2 shows the overall trend of HPV positivity following conization, with a rapid decline in the early stages (within 6 time points, per 4 months), which then gradually slows and stabilizes over time, but there is a slight increase from 24 months to 36 months. After adjusting for variables such as age, single/multiple infection, and HPV 16/18 infection, the early rapid decline remaine significant, with a 59% reduction in HPV positivity risk every 4 months (odds ratio [OR]: 0.41, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.35–0.47).

Figure 3 displays the HPV status trajectories following conization for both premenopausal and postmenopausal groups. Although both groups exhibited a similar overall trend of rapid decline followed by stabilization, significant differences were observed at certain time points. In the early stages (within 6 time points, per 4 months), the decline in HPV positivity was more pronounced in premenopausal patients. Specifically, after adjusting for covariates, the risk of HPV positivity decreased by 62% every 4 months in premenopausal patients (OR: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.33–0.45), compared to a 53% reduction in postmenopausal patients (OR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.32–0.67), with a significant interaction between the groups (p = 0.0032). After 6 time points (per 4 months), both groups continued to show a slow decline, with premenopausal patients having a lower HPV infection risk than postmenopausal patients. The risk of HPV positivity decreased by 11% every 4 months in premenopausal patients (OR: 0.89, 95% CI: 0.75–1.04), and by 5% in postmenopausal patients (OR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.74–1.21) (see Table 2)

Discussion

The present study employed a GAMM to investigate the longitudinal relationship between post-conization HPV status and menopause. Our findings revealed that the infection status of HPV exhibited a nonlinear change over time when analyzed using GAMM. Consequently, we introduced the index of “time-varying curve,” and the temporal impact was significantly influenced by menopausal status (p = 0.0032). Compared with the non-menopausal group, the risk of HPV positivity in the menopausal group increased by 45% every four months after conization (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.13 to 1.85), particularly within 24 months postoperatively. Our study results provide the first evidence that menopausal status can influence the trajectory of HPV clearance after conization, thus emphasizing the necessity for developing age - stratified management strategies. The use of Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) represents a methodological advance over traditional linear models, permitting precise characterization of the nonlinear temporal patterns in HPV status.

Previous studies have explored the outcomes of HPV status following conization. A study reported that the HPV detection rate was 67.2% within the first 6 months after surgery [24]. For patients with negative surgical margins, the HPV test results at 6 months after the surgery can be negative, and they can resume regular screening [25]. If the HPV positive rate within 3 to 4 months after the surgery is 20.4%, this result can identify women who have a risk of CIN2 + recurrence within the next 2 years [26]. Although these studies have demonstrated the important role of cervical conization surgery in treating CIN 2–3 and clearing HPV, to date, there is no consensus on the number of tests and follow-up visits required during the post-treatment monitoring [27]. Our study, employing GAMMs, uncovered the risk profile of HPV clearance over time following surgery. It not only quantified the dynamic changes but also identified two critical time windows for clinical decision-making (24 and 36 months), thereby filling this gap. This approach surpasses previous static assessments of HPV status at isolated time points.

Our study considered the temporal changes in HPV status following conization. We observed that within 24 months post-surgery, the risk of HPV positivity rapidly decreased, which then slowed down after 24 months. The initial significant decline is largely attributed to the effective removal of virus-containing lesions through surgery, as infected cells are physically excised from the Organism. For instance, one study reported a significant decrease in the positivity rate of HPV-related biomarkers within 6 months after conization, particularly for high-risk HPV [28]. Additionally, another study found that at 4 to 6 months and 8 to 12 months post-surgery, the persistence rate of HPV was 9.5% [29]. On the other hand, following surgical resection of the affected tissues, residual viral antigens can trigger a robust immune response. Immune cells, including T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, are mobilized to identify and eliminate residual viral particles and infected cells [30]. The local inflammatory response at the surgical site also contributes to the recruitment and activation of immune cells. Cytokines and chemokines released by inflammatory cells attract additional immune cells to the site of infection, thereby enhancing immune surveillance and clearance capabilities [31–33]. During this phase, the immune system is highly active, effectively suppressing viral replication and dissemination. This ultimately leads to clearance of the human papillomavirus (HPV) and results in a negative HPV status. Our findings are similar to those of a study that reported a relatively high rate of HPV negativity at 24 months post-surgery [34]. This suggests that 24 months may be a critical time point, indicating that conization can significantly reduce HPV positivity risk within this period. This observation could provide an important “window of observation” in clinical practice. For patients who test positive for HPV shortly after surgery, there may be a reasonable expectation of turning negative by 24 months, thus avoiding excessive testing for most patients. This approach would effectively reduce the economic and psychological burden on patients. These insights position our study as a paradigm shift in post-conization surveillance, transitioning from fixed-interval testing to risk-adapted monitoring based on menopausal status and time-dependent HPV dynamics. However, if HPV is detected at 24 months post-surgery, patients may be at higher risk of persistent infection. For such individuals, screening intervals should be shorter and testing more rigorous. Interestingly, a slight increase in HPV positivity was observed at 24–36 months, suggesting the possibility of viral recurrence or reinfection. This finding may reveal the complex interplay between the dynamics of the immune response and viral latency. Over time, the immune system may gradually enter a state of “exhaustion”, Although the immune system generates memory cells against HPV, the quantity and functionality of these memory cells may diminish over time. Moreover, persistent immune activation and response can deplete the energy and resources of immune cells, leading to a gradual decline in their function [35]. This phenomenon of immune exhaustion is commonly observed in chronic infections and long-term immune stimulation and may also occur following conization [36]. Additionally, HPV has the ability to establish latent infections, with its genome integrating into the host cell genome and remaining dormant within host cells [37]. When immune surveillance is weakened, the latent viral genome may be reactivated, initiating replication and expression of viral proteins, thereby leading to HPV repositivity [38]. This state of immune “exhaustion” provides a favorable environment for the reactivation of latent viruses. Therefore, 36 months post-conization may represent an important time point for screening, necessitating enhanced monitoring.

As shown in Fig. 3, in the early stages, the decline in HPV positivity risk was more pronounced in the premenopausal group, with significant differences observed between the groups. This finding underscores the potential role of menopausal status in HPV infection risk. One of the main challenges in achieving HPV negativity after conization in postmenopausal women is the alteration of the vaginal and cervical microenvironment [39]. Another study investigated the association between androstenedione and reproductive hormones during the menopausal transition, revealing that hormonal changes may influence HPV persistence [40]. Declining estrogen levels may compromise the integrity of the genital epithelial barrier and reprogram immune-cell function, thereby increasing susceptibility to human papillomavirus infection [41]. Furthermore, despite the fact that nearly half of the postmenopausal population has undergone hysterectomy, the HPV clearance rate among postmenopausal women remains significantly lower than that of younger women during follow-up. This discrepancy may be attributed to the significant reduction in Lactobacillus species and the concomitant increase in anaerobic bacteria within the vaginal microbiota following menopause. Such alterations have the potential to undermine local immune defenses [42, 43]. Moreover, postmenopausal immune alterations are not confined to the local immune milieu but also encompass systemic immune responses. Postmenopausal estrogen decline may potentiate the suppressive activity of immunoregulatory cells—namely regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells—thereby facilitating persistent human papillomavirus infection and accelerating cervical carcinogenesis [44]. Studies have revealed that postmenopausal women exhibit marked changes in systemic inflammatory markers, such as disturbances in global inflammatory indices—including the aggregate index of systemic inflammation, systemic immune-inflammation index, and systemic inflammation response index—which may impair overall immune function and human papillomavirus clearance [45, 46]. This finding underscores the importance of continued regular HPV screening and monitoring for postmenopausal women who have undergone hysterectomy. These studies highlight the complexity of HPV infection dynamics in older women and emphasize the necessity of managing persistent HPV in this population.

Considering that persistent HPV infection after cervical conization may be associated with multiple factors, including persistent or recurrent cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN 2–3) and the patient’s margin status, among others [47]. As shown in Fig. 4, within the first 20 months after conization, the risk of HPV positivity significantly decreased in both the persistent/recurrent group and the non-persistent/non-recurrent group. However, the risk remained higher in the persistent/recurrent group, which is consistent with previous studies. For instance, after large loop excision of the transformation zone (LLETZ), HR-HPV positivity has been associated with a higher rate of reoperation and recurrence of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia [48].

Postmenopausal women have been reported to have a higher risk of positive surgical margins following conization, particularly within the endocervical canal [49]. However, our study, employing Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) analysis, indicates that in the postmenopausal cohort, the positivity of the endocervical and ectocervical margins may not be significantly correlated with HPV outcomes post - conization (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 5). This observation may be attributed to menopause-associated cervical microenvironmental reprogramming, in which oestrogen deficiency triggers mucosal atrophy and local immunosuppression, thereby diminishing the relative contribution of surgical-margin status to persistent HPV infection [39, 50, 51]. Menopausal status may be a pivotal factor influencing the persistence of lesions following conization, rather than being solely attributable to the positivity of the endocervical and ectocervical margins [52]. Further studies are needed to clarify these associations.

This study has the following strengths, we employed GAMM to fit the HPV status trajectories for the overall cohort following conization, stratified by menopausal status. We then used GAMM to model the temporal changes in HPV status. This approach captured the differences in HPV status trajectories between the premenopausal and postmenopausal groups, while accounting for both the overall group trends and individual variability [53]. Unlike the conventional linear models that typically fit group HPV status over time, GAMM allows for a more nuanced analysis by incorporating individual deviations from baseline HPV status and assessing how changes in HPV status over time are associated with premenopausal or postmenopausal status. Additionally, within the mixed-effects modeling framework, individual deviations before and after specific time points are offset, and potential confounding factors are also controlled for in the GAMM. The time-varying risk curves generated provide an intuitive portrayal of the HPV clearance trajectories in patients across different menopausal statuses, thus enabling a shift from population - based to risk - stratified follow - up management.

However, our study has several limitations. First, the data were obtained from a single center. Second, although multiple potential confounding variables were adjusted, residual confounding may not have been fully resolved, including vaccines, smoking and contraceptive use.

In summary, the longitudinal changes in HPV status over time exhibit a curvilinear relationship, with a significant early decline that is independently associated with menopause. This will aid in the management of HPV testing strategies for patients following conization.

This retrospective study was conducted using anonymized data. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Yuebei People’s Hospital (Approval No. KY-2023-135). Yuebei People’s Hospital is an affiliated hospital of Shantou University. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Medical Ethics Committee of Yuebei People’s Hospital due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data.
