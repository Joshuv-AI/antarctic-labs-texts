Comparison of artificial intelligence–based chatbots and expert periodontists in responding to patient questions: a multi-dimensional analysis
Authors: Canan Akdeniz, Arzum Güler Doğru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge Language Model (LLM) –based chatbots are increasingly used in patient information processes. The aim of this study was to compare the performance of ChatGPT (GPT-5.1), Gemini (2.5 Flash), and Claude (Sonnet 4.5) with expert periodontologists in responding to periodontal questions. Responses were evaluated in terms of scientific accuracy, completeness, conciseness & focus, empathy, and clarity, and differences among groups were investigated.MethodsThe question pool was developed de novo based on clinical experience. The literature and online search trends were used to support content coverage. The questions were evaluated using Lawshe’s content validity method, and 20 open-ended questions were included. Expert responses were prepared by three experienced periodontologists based on the literature and clinical guidelines. The questions were then submitted using a standardized prompt to ChatGPT (GPT-5.1), Gemini (2.5 Flash), and Claude (Sonnet 4.5), and the first responses were recorded. All responses were anonymized and evaluated by nine independent periodontologists using a 5-point Likert scale in terms of scientific accuracy, completeness, conciseness & focus, empathy, and clarity. Statistical analyses were performed using the Friedman test with Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons, and inter-rater agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient.ResultsNo significant difference was found among the groups in terms of scientific accuracy (p = 0.425). A significant difference was observed for completeness (p < 0.0001), with LLM-based chatbot responses achieving higher scores than expert responses. For conciseness & focus, a significant difference was found (p < 0.0001); Gemini demonstrated lower scores compared to the other groups, while no significant differences were observed among ChatGPT, Claude, and expert responses. A significant difference was observed for empathy (p < 0.0001), with all LLM-based chatbot responses scoring higher than expert responses. For clarity, a significant difference was found (p < 0.0001), with a difference observed only between ChatGPT and Gemini. Inter-rater agreement was within a good range across all domains.ConclusionsThis study showed that LLM-based chatbots can generate responses with a level of scientific accuracy comparable to expert periodontologists. However, despite providing more comprehensive and empathetic responses, some models demonstrated limitations in terms of conciseness & focus and clarity. These findings indicate that model differences should be considered in patient education and information-seeking processes. Expert responses were able to present a similar level of scientific accuracy using fewer and more focused expressions. This may make it more difficult for patients to maintain focus and to perceive information in a structured manner when using AI-generated responses. Overall, AI systems may serve a supportive role in patient information processes; however, physician supervision remains necessary in clinical use. The appropriate integration of LLM-based chatbots into patient information processes may enhance patient communication and access to information, provided that they are used as supportive tools rather than independent decision-makers in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-09315-1.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, ChatGPT, Large language models, Periodontology, Patient education, Empathy, Clarity

Introduction

AI is a branch of computer science that focuses on developing systems capable of performing tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as learning, problem-solving, pattern recognition, language understanding, reasoning, and decision-making. These systems are generally designed to simulate intelligent human behavior and emulate human-like cognitive processes [1].

Natural language processing (NLP) is a subfield of AI that enables computers to understand, analyze, and generate human language [2]. By integrating linguistics, machine learning, and other AI techniques, NLP transforms unstructured text or speech into data that machines can process, allowing computers to interpret language and produce human-like responses [3, 4]. This technology is used in various applications, including chatbots, automatic translation, text analysis, and the evaluation of clinical data in healthcare [5].

Within this field, large language models (LLMs) represent a class of NLP models trained on large-scale text corpora to understand and generate human language [6]. Therefore, the relationship between NLP and LLMs is hierarchical: NLP is the broader field, whereas LLMs are a class of models used within NLP for language understanding and generation [7].

Chatbots are software systems designed to interact with users through natural language via text or voice to provide information, perform tasks, or facilitate access to services [8]. According to their response-generation approach, chatbots can be classified as rule-based, retrieval-based, generative, and hybrid systems [8, 9]. LLM-based chatbots use LLMs to generate flexible, natural-language responses [10, 11]. Systems such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are described in the literature as LLM-based chatbots [10–12].

LLM-based chatbots have been reported to provide several clinical and patient-oriented advantages in dentistry. Studies indicate that the use of these systems can improve oral health education and oral hygiene behaviors among patients [13]. In orthodontic patients in particular, the use of chatbots has been associated with better maintenance of gingival health [14]. Applications developed for children and caregivers have also been shown to improve toothbrushing habits and increase oral health awareness [15, 16]. In addition, these systems may support positive health behavior changes by providing patients with a non-judgmental environment for obtaining information [13, 17].

Although LLM-based chatbots offer several advantages in dentistry, they also have important limitations. Studies have shown that these systems may sometimes generate incorrect, vague, or overly general information, and the responses provided may not always be consistent with evidence-based clinical guidelines. In addition, most models do not clearly indicate the sources of the information they use, and the reliability of their responses may vary depending on how questions are formulated [18, 19]. Furthermore, issues such as overreliance on incorrect information, data privacy concerns, and the lack of clear regulatory frameworks represent important challenges in the use of chatbots in healthcare [20–23]. These limitations highlight the need for further evaluation of the reliability of LLM-based chatbots in healthcare settings.

Several studies have investigated the accuracy and quality of responses generated by LLM-based chatbots to patient questions in dentistry. These studies indicate that LLM-based chatbots can generally provide correct answers to many questions; however, the accuracy and completeness of these responses do not always reach the level of expert responses [19, 24–26]. Some research has also reported that these systems may be perceived by patients as more empathetic and easier to understand [27–29]. Nevertheless, most existing studies focus on specific aspects, and research comprehensively evaluating responses generated by LLM-based chatbots to patient questions in dentistry in terms of scientific accuracy, completeness, conciseness & focus, empathy, and clarity, together with expert responses, remains limited.

The aim of this study was to compare the responses provided by ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and expert periodontologists to questions that may be asked by patients in clinical practice. The responses were evaluated in terms of scientific accuracy, completeness, conciseness & focus, empathy, and clarity. The null hypothesis of this study was that there would be no significant difference among the responses generated by ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and expert periodontologists with respect to these parameters.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was reviewed and approved by the Dicle University Faculty of Dentistry Local Ethics Committee (Protocol No: 2025-85; Date: 26 November 2025). The study was conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. No patients were involved in this study, and no clinical interventions were performed. Only responses generated by LLM-based chatbots and expert opinions were evaluated.

Sample size determination

In this study, an a priori power analysis was performed to determine the required sample size for comparisons among four groups. Assuming a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power (1–β) of 0.80, the minimum number of response items required was calculated as 44 based on an effect size of f = 0.45, consistent with a previous study [30]. A total of 80 response items were evaluated in the present study, exceeding the minimum required sample size.

Question development

The aim of this study was to compare the responses provided by LLM-based chatbots and expert periodontologists to questions that are frequently asked by patients regarding periodontology. The responses were evaluated by nine volunteer periodontologists in terms of scientific accuracy, completeness, conciseness & focus, empathy, and clarity.

The development of the question pool followed a systematic and multi-step process to ensure both clinical relevance and scientific validity. Initially, online search trends and frequently queried topics were reviewed using alsoasked.com to identify common patient questions based on Google searches. These findings were integrated with the routine clinical experience of three experienced volunteer periodontologists, who collaboratively developed a preliminary pool of 28 questions.

The preliminary question pool was intentionally designed to represent real clinical encounters commonly observed in routine periodontal practice rather than hypothetical scenarios. The questions covered different domains of periodontology, including disease signs and symptoms, risk factors, prevention, treatment procedures, maintenance care, esthetic issues, and periodontal–systemic relationships. To avoid imbalance across domains, care was taken to ensure that the question pool did not focus disproportionately on a single topic area. All questions were formulated as open-ended patient questions, focused on a single concept, written in language consistent with how patients typically express their concerns, and standardized in wording and format to ensure consistency.

Subsequently, a targeted literature search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar to verify the scientific relevance, accuracy, and comprehensiveness of the preliminary question pool. The literature was used to validate the scientific content of the questions rather than to generate them.

To evaluate the content validity of the question pool, Lawshe’s content validity method was applied [31, 32]. Accordingly, the questions were independently assessed by 10 volunteer periodontologists using a three-point scale: “essential,” “useful but not essential,” and “not necessary.” The Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated for each question.

For the CVR calculation, the number of experts rating an item as “essential” (nₑ) and the total number of experts (N) were used in the formula . For ten raters, the critical CVR value was set at 0.62 at a significance level of 0.05, and questions with a CVR ≥ 0.62 were included in the study [31, 32]. The CVR values of the retained questions are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Following this evaluation process, a total of 20 open-ended questions requiring text-based responses and representing scenarios commonly encountered in clinical practice were selected for the final analysis. All questions used in the study are presented in Table 1. Each question was presented in a standardized format to ensure consistency in the responses.

Study questions

Collection of chatbot and expert periodontologist responses

All questions were prepared in a standardized format to ensure consistency in the responses and were individually directed to each chatbot. The questions were submitted using a predefined, uniform prompt. The prompt used in this study was as follows: “Assume the role of a periodontologist and provide a precise answer to the following patient question.”

All questions were asked in English, and the responses were also obtained in English. This approach was adopted to evaluate the models in their primary language of operation.

The questions were submitted by a single researcher using newly created accounts on the following platforms, with standardized and identical formatting: ChatGPT (OpenAI, https://chatgpt.com/ ; accessed on 5 December 2025; GPT-5.1), Gemini (Google, https://gemini.google.com/ ; accessed on 5 December 2025; 2.5 Flash), and Claude (Anthropic, https://claude.ai/ ; accessed on 6 December 2025; Sonnet 4.5). All models were evaluated using the versions available at the time of the study.

All chatbots were accessed via their official web interfaces, and the publicly available versions of each model were used to reflect real-world user access.

Responses were collected within a 24-hour period between 5 and 6 December 2025 to minimize variability due to potential model updates.

During data collection, separate Google sessions were used for each chatbot. To minimize the potential influence of prior interactions or model memory, all chatbots were accessed through newly created Google accounts with no previous usage history. Each question was submitted in a clean browser session with cleared cache and cookies to minimize contextual bias. Only the first response generated by each chatbot was recorded and included in the evaluation.

This approach was designed to simulate real user–chatbot interaction and to capture the initial response as it would typically appear to a patient or clinician seeking immediate information.

Expert responses were prepared collaboratively by three experienced periodontologists. The responses were developed through a process of discussion and consensus among the experts. During this process, the answers to each question were structured based on current scientific literature, international clinical guidelines, and clinical experience. The accuracy and comprehensiveness of the prepared responses were verified through comparison with the relevant literature.

To ensure the objectivity of the evaluation process, the researchers who prepared the expert responses and the researcher who collected the AI–generated responses were not involved in the evaluation phase. Expert responses were prepared prior to obtaining the chatbot responses to prevent any influence from AI outputs.

All questions used in the study and the corresponding responses are presented in detail in the Supplementary Material.

Evaluation of LLM-based chatbots and expert periodontologist responses

Responses obtained from three different AI models and expert periodontologists were anonymized. They were subsequently presented to nine academic periodontists, all of whom voluntarily participated in the study. To ensure the objectivity of the evaluation process, the source of each response was concealed. Prior to evaluation, a separate blinding key identifying the origin of each response was created and securely stored.

The evaluation was conducted by nine experienced periodontologists in accordance with methodologies used in similar studies [33]. All evaluators were specialists in periodontology and had between 5 and 15 years of clinical and academic experience following completion of their specialty training. In addition, all evaluators were affiliated with academic institutions and had previous experience in periodontal research and education. To further ensure the reliability of the evaluation process, inter-rater agreement among evaluators was assessed. As in previous studies, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used [34, 35].

ICC values were interpreted as follows: <0.50 poor, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, and >0.90 excellent reliability [36].

Scoring system and evaluation criteria

In the evaluation process, responses were analyzed across five main dimensions: Scientific Accuracy, Completeness, Conciseness & Focus, Empathy, and Clarity. Each criterion was assessed independently, and the score assigned to one criterion did not influence the evaluation of the others. All responses were rated using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = excellent) [30, 34, 35, 37]. The overall study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Study workflow

Scientific accuracy

The extent to which the response is consistent with current scientific evidence and clinical guidelines and does not contain incorrect information. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: 1 (Very poor): The response contains clear scientific errors. 2 (Poor): The response is partially correct but includes important scientific errors or inconsistencies. 3 (Moderate): The response is generally correct but contains some incomplete or unclear aspects. 4 (Good): The response is scientifically accurate and largely consistent with current evidence. 5 (Excellent): The response is fully accurate, up-to-date, and based on scientific evidence.

Completeness

The extent to which the response adequately addresses all essential and clinically relevant aspects of the question. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: 1 (Very poor): The response fails to address most aspects of the question. 2 (Poor): The response addresses only a limited portion of the question. 3 (Moderate): The response covers the main points but lacks some important aspects. 4 (Good): The response addresses most aspects of the question adequately. 5 (Excellent): The response comprehensively addresses all essential aspects of the question.

Conciseness & focus

The extent to which the response remains directly focused on the question without including unnecessary or irrelevant details. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: 1 (Very poor): The response is largely unfocused or contains substantial irrelevant information. 2 (Poor): The response is partially focused but includes notable unnecessary details. 3 (Moderate): The response is generally focused but contains some irrelevant details. 4 (Good): The response is mostly focused with minimal unnecessary information. 5 (Excellent): The response is fully focused, clear, and free of unnecessary details.

Empathy

The extent to which the response uses a patient-centered, understanding, and supportive tone. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: 1 (Very poor): The response is impersonal, mechanical, or ignores the patient perspective. 2 (Poor): The response demonstrates limited empathy and insufficient consideration of patient concerns. 3 (Moderate): The response is somewhat empathetic but lacks consistency in patient-centeredness. 4 (Good): The response uses a supportive, understanding, and patient-centered tone. 5 (Excellent): The response is highly empathetic, reassuring, and strongly patient-centered.

Clarity

The extent to which the response is clear, simple, and easily understandable for a patient. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: 1 (Very poor): The response is overly complex, uses excessive technical terminology, and is difficult to understand. 2 (Poor): The response is partially understandable but generally uses complex or technical language. 3 (Moderate): The response is generally understandable but includes some unclear or overly technical expressions. 4 (Good): The response is clear and simple, and largely understandable for a patient. 5 (Excellent): The response is very clear, simple, and easily understandable for a patient.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and median with interquartile range (median [IQR]).

For the comparison of more than two related groups, non-parametric tests were preferred, and the Friedman test was used to evaluate differences between groups. For each question, the median rating across the nine evaluators was calculated separately for each response source. Thus, each question served as a repeated measurement block, and the four response sources (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Expert) were compared across the same set of 20 questions using the Friedman test. When statistical significance was observed, pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc analyses. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Inter-rater agreement among evaluators was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

AI is currently used in a wide range of applications, including virtual health assistance, patient education, reduction of healthcare worker burnout, mental health services, digital scribing, support for treatment planning, diagnostic processes, interpretation of radiological images, and cancer detection [13, 38, 39]. In some specific tasks, AI has been reported to achieve—and in certain cases even surpass—human performance. These developments have raised the question of whether AI might replace physicians.

However, the existing literature indicates a consensus that AI is unlikely to replace physicians; rather, it is expected to play a supportive role [38, 40]. One of the main reasons for this is that current AI systems lack the general understanding, empathy, and nuanced communication skills that form the foundation of the therapeutic doctor–patient relationship [41, 42]. Furthermore, many core clinical tasks—such as physical examination, complex clinical assessment, managing uncertainty, and ethical decision-making—cannot yet be reliably performed by AI [41, 42].

Qualitative studies involving physicians and AI experts suggest that AI will primarily automate routine and well-defined tasks, while physicians will increasingly focus on higher-value roles such as communication, coordination, and complex decision-making [42, 43]. Similarly, surveys conducted among physicians indicate that most do not expect their profession to be fully replaced by AI; instead, they anticipate a transformation of their roles and view AI as a tool that needs to be learned and integrated into clinical practice [44, 45].

AI systems are increasingly being developed to perceive, simulate, and generate appropriate responses to human emotions [46, 47]. Recent studies indicate that these systems can establish a convincingly empathetic interaction with users; however, they lack human-like internal emotional experience and moral subjectivity [48–50]. Current critiques emphasize that such responses are not derived from an inherent capacity for empathy but are instead based on learned or programmed patterns that mimic human emotions [48, 49, 51]. Therefore, although machines cannot provide the full “reciprocal emotionality” present in deep human relationships, they can offer a sense of companionship and “being with someone” to users [51, 52].

The academic literature further highlights that AI systems still face significant theoretical and technical limitations in understanding emotions and establishing sustained, long-term empathetic interactions [47, 49, 53]. Nevertheless, empathetic messages generated by AI have, in some cases, been evaluated as more compassionate than those produced by human experts [54, 55]. Although individuals continue to prefer human sources for emotional support, the integration of “artificial emotional intelligence” into communication has been shown to enhance user satisfaction [50, 56]. However, the prevailing view in the literature is that AI cannot fully replicate human bonding, and therefore, it is unlikely to completely replace doctor–patient interactions in the foreseeable future [42, 57].

In this context, the findings of the present study reveal a noteworthy pattern. Although the existing literature emphasizes that AI systems do not possess genuine empathy, our results showed that LLM-based chatbots achieved higher scores than expert responses in terms of empathy. This finding may be explained by the fact that the responses were evaluated in written form and did not reflect a real clinical interaction setting. AI systems can effectively generate empathetic expressions through learned linguistic patterns [48], which may lead to higher perceived empathy in written evaluations. This may be attributed to the ability of these systems to produce structured, user-friendly, and communicatively effective language [58]. These findings are consistent with the current literature. A recent systematic review reported that in 13 out of 15 studies, LLM-based chatbots achieved higher empathy scores compared to healthcare professionals. In contrast, only two studies in dermatology found human responses to be more empathetic, both of which utilized ChatGPT-3.5 and Med-PaLM 2. Notably, in all studies using GPT-4, LLM-based chatbot responses were consistently rated as more empathetic. However, most of the studies included in this review were conducted in 2023–2024 [59], and more recent models such as GPT-4.5 [60] and GPT-5—released in February 2025 and suggested to have improved emotional intelligence—have not yet been evaluated in the literature. In this respect, the use of a more recent model (ChatGPT 5.1) in our study may enhance the contribution of our findings to the existing body of literature.

In terms of conciseness & focus, although the expert group demonstrated higher mean scores than the other groups, this difference was not statistically significant. In our study, the Gemini model demonstrated significantly lower performance specifically in terms of conciseness & focus. This finding may be related to the more detailed nature of Gemini-generated responses, which may have contributed to a more dispersed presentation of information and reduced emphasis on the core message. Additionally, the Gemini model performed worse than ChatGPT in terms of clarity. This difference suggests that variations in how information was presented between the two models may have influenced clarity ratings. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between Gemini and the other models in terms of scientific accuracy, completeness, and empathy. A recent study reported that the Gemini model demonstrated lower performance than other LLMs in terms of scientific accuracy, completeness, and comprehensibility. However, in that study, responses were generated in May 2025, and the exact model version was not specified [61]. In contrast, in our study, the questions were posed in December 2025. Considering the rapid evolution of LLMs, differences between model versions over time may account for the observed discrepancies in performance.

In the study conducted by Maruska et al., which compared dentist responses with chatbot-generated responses in an online forum, chatbot responses received significantly higher scores than dentist responses in terms of both quality and empathy [37]. Additionally, chatbot responses were preferred in most cases. Similarly, in the study by Ayers et al., which evaluated 195 patient questions, chatbot responses were preferred in 78.6% of cases and also received significantly higher scores for both quality and empathy [29]. These findings are consistent with our results, particularly in demonstrating that LLM-based chatbot responses tend to receive higher empathy scores. One possible explanation is that the longer and more detailed responses generated by LLM-based chatbots may enhance the perception of empathy among patients. Consistent with this interpretation, both our findings and the findings of Ayers et al. and Maruska et al. demonstrated that LLM-based chatbot responses were longer and more detailed than those provided by dentists [29, 37]. Although previous studies also reported higher overall quality scores for chatbot responses, direct comparison with our findings is challenging. This is because, in both studies, quality was treated as a multidimensional construct encompassing several components, including completeness, accessibility, accuracy, precision, objectivity, consistency, relevancy, timeliness, and intelligibility, and these components were evaluated collectively under a single composite score rather than being analyzed individually. Therefore, it is not possible to determine which specific components contributed to the higher quality scores.

LLM-based chatbots achieved higher scores in terms of completeness compared to expert responses. This can be attributed to their ability to draw upon extensive medical knowledge, together with the contributions of model architecture, prompting strategies, and retrieval mechanisms, enabling them to generate more comprehensive responses [62, 63]. However, although more comprehensive responses may provide more complete information, excessively detailed responses may also increase patients' cognitive burden and make it more difficult to identify the key message [64, 65]. Recent evidence further suggests that response length alone does not necessarily determine patient comprehension. Rather, readability, logical organization, and the way information is presented appear to have a greater influence on patient understanding than response length itself [66, 67]. Therefore, maximizing completeness alone may not be sufficient to improve patient education. Instead, achieving an appropriate balance between completeness, readability, and conciseness is likely to facilitate more effective patient communication.

In terms of scientific accuracy, no significant differences were observed among the groups, and all groups demonstrated similarly high levels of accuracy. This finding indicates that LLM-based chatbots can generate responses with a level of scientific accuracy comparable to clinicians in the context of patient information. Notably, in a study comparing ChatGPT and Claude, both models demonstrated similar performance in terms of accuracy and completeness, with ChatGPT showing only a slight advantage in overall quality [68]. Similarly, previous studies have reported that responses generated by LLM-based chatbots were evaluated comparably across different dental specialties in terms of reliability and overall quality, with no statistically significant differences observed between groups [69]. These findings further support previous evidence suggesting that LLM-based chatbots may provide an acceptable level of accuracy and reliability, particularly for general information delivery [70].

Beyond this, although LLM-based chatbots can provide accurate and generally reliable information, it should be considered that they may have limitations in terms of the depth and contextual specificity required for clinical decision-making processes [69]. This suggests that, despite their strength in general information delivery, LLM-based chatbots may be insufficient in providing individualized and actionable clinical guidance. This interpretation is further supported by studies evaluating the role of LLM-based chatbots in clinical performance assessment [70]. For example, GPT-4-generated periodontology examinations have been shown to provide broader content coverage and yield higher student scores; however, they also demonstrated significantly lower discriminative ability, resulting in a more homogeneous distribution of student performance [71]. Further supporting this interpretation, a study comparing ChatGPT-4o with expert evaluations in endodontic education found a low level of agreement and no significant correlation between AI and expert assessments. The same study further demonstrated that, although LLM-based chatbots may perform adequately in information provision, they have limitations in areas requiring clinical context, visual interpretation, and experience-based decision-making. Additionally, AI-generated responses were reported to remain superficial in some cases and to inadequately reflect the nuances that are critical in clinical decision-making. Therefore, LLM-based chatbots should be considered not as independent tools in clinical decision-making, but rather as supportive instruments [34, 68].

The findings of this study indicate that LLM-based chatbot systems may serve as supportive tools in patient information processes regarding periodontal diseases. In recent years, the use of written patient information tools in clinical practice has been increasing [72]. In addition, there is growing evidence indicating rising levels of workload and burnout among healthcare professionals [73]. In this context, the use of LLM-based chatbots for written patient information under physician supervision may have the potential to support time management and contribute to clinical workflow. Furthermore, their ability to provide patient-oriented information, when combined with physician-verified scientific accuracy, may enhance patient–physician communication. However, differences observed across models suggest that model selection remains an important consideration for clinical implementation. Therefore, LLM-based chatbots should be considered supportive tools for patient information rather than independent sources of clinical advice and should be used under physician supervision.

Although LLM-based chatbots may support patient information, their integration into clinical practice also raises important ethical considerations. Inaccurate, incomplete, or non-verifiable responses generated by LLM-based chatbots may contribute to misinformation if interpreted without professional review [62]. Furthermore, patients may perceive LLM-based chatbots as reliable sources of medical information. As a result, excessive reliance on these systems may influence healthcare decisions. It may also reduce patients' willingness to seek professional advice when needed [74–76]. These considerations highlight the importance of maintaining appropriate human oversight when integrating LLM-based chatbots into patient care [62].

This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation was based solely on written responses. Therefore, important aspects of real clinical interactions—such as non-verbal communication, dynamic patient–clinician exchange, and contextual depth—could not be assessed. Second, all questions and responses were conducted in English, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other languages and patient communication contexts. The study was also limited to the specific versions of three AI models available at the time of data collection; considering the rapid evolution of LLMs, these findings may vary over time. Although precautions were taken to minimize the influence of prior interactions and contextual memory, all responses generated by a given chatbot originated from the same underlying model. Therefore, model-specific characteristics may have contributed to similarities among responses within each chatbot. Additionally, expert responses were prepared collaboratively by three experienced periodontologists. While this approach strengthened scientific consistency, the expert group represented a single consensus response source rather than independent clinician responses. Therefore, it may not fully reflect the variability of individual clinician responses encountered in real clinical practice. Finally, although the question pool was systematically developed and its content validity was assessed, the analysis was restricted to 20 commonly encountered questions in periodontology. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to all areas of dentistry or to more complex, individualized clinical scenarios.

This study has several notable strengths. First, the simultaneous evaluation of AI–generated and expert responses across multiple dimensions—namely scientific accuracy, completeness, conciseness & focus, empathy, and clarity—positions this study among the relatively limited number of comprehensive, multi-dimensional analyses in the literature. In addition, the anonymization of responses and the blinding of evaluators contributed to minimizing potential bias during the assessment process. The development of the question pool based on clinical practice, together with the systematic assessment of content validity, enhances the clinical relevance of the study. Furthermore, the use of up-to-date versions of AI models represents an important strength, as it allows the findings to reflect the current state of the technology.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that LLM-based chatbots have strong potential for delivering clinical information in response to patient questions related to periodontal diseases. They demonstrated a level of scientific accuracy comparable to expert periodontists while receiving higher ratings for completeness and perceived empathy, suggesting that these systems may offer a significant advantage in patient information processes. However, the way information is presented is as critical as the amount of content. In particular, the longer and more dispersed narrative style observed in the Gemini model may lead to a loss of focus in the core message. In this context, improving AI systems to generate shorter and more focused responses emerges as a key priority. Following such advancements, future research should investigate whether perceived empathy can be maintained when responses are delivered in a more concise format.

Nevertheless, the higher perceived empathy scores observed in LLM-based chatbots may be related to the methodological design of this study, which evaluated only text-based interactions. This limitation may restrict the direct generalizability of the findings to real clinical settings. Therefore, future studies should incorporate voice- and image-based interactions to better reflect real clinical scenarios. In addition, randomized controlled trials evaluating the impact of AI–physician interactions on patient outcomes are needed to more comprehensively determine the clinical value of these systems.

Overall, LLM-based chatbots should be considered supportive tools in patient education and information processes and should be used under physician supervision in clinical practice.
