Reinforcing Anterosuperior Medium‐to‐Large Rotator Cuff Repairs With the Long Head of the Biceps Tendon and Dermal Allograft Patch Using a Knotless Double‐Row Technique
Authors: Gabriel Antonio A Escueta, Joe Chih‐Hao Chiu, Al Kung‐Tseng Hung, Jijulal C U, Abdel Kareem Adnan Khdairat, Vijay Krishnan K
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe a surgical technique repairing medium‐to‐large anterosuperior rotator cuff (RC) tear that combines long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) augmentation with a dermal allograft patch using a knotless double‐row repair. LHBT augmentation is done using a triple‐loaded anchor placed 5 mm posterior to the bicipital groove at the bone‐cartilage junction. One suture strand creates a lasso‐loop, rip‐stop configuration to secure and posteriorly reroute the LHBT, improving anterior cable coverage. The remaining medial anchor sutures function as mattress sutures through the supraspinatus. Knots are tied, but the suture limbs are left uncut to secure the graft later to the cuff. A 3 × 3 cm, 2 × 2 mm thick dermal allograft is prepared. The uncut medial row sutures and a nonabsorbable tape suture (for lateral fixation) are passed through the graft. Using a custom cannula and traction sutures placed on the corners, the graft is shuttled and positioned on the bursal side of the repaired tendon. Once positioned, the medial sutures are secured. All remaining sutures (medial limbs, nonabsorbable tape suture, and traction sutures) are loaded into a lateral row anchor and impacted into the greater tuberosity. The final construct sandwiches the repaired tendon beneath the allograft, increasing construct thickness and ensuring complete footprint coverage.

Double‐row fixation techniques have been developed to improve tendon coverage at the rotator cuff (RC) footprint and reduce the detrimental effects of synovial fluid leakage on tendon healing in RC repairs. 1 However, the footprint coverage of the RC is not always complete after surgery, 2 depending on the preoperative patient physiology such as tear chronicity, tear pattern, tissue quality, tendon retractability, remnant tendon length, and muscle fatty infiltration. 3 , 4

For RC tears that are reparable but where “complete” coverage of the footprint cannot be achieved, various grafts—such as human dermal allografts, 5 xenograft dermal matrices (bovine, 6 synthetic materials, 7 or composite xenograft/synthetic materials 8 )—can be used to augment the repaired tendon. Scientific evidence with 1 year follow‐up currently indicates that this approach can reduce the retear rate following RC repairs. 6 , 9

Also, long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) is now commonly used to augment RC repair, as it is available locally, free of additional costs, less technically demanding, and decreases superior migration and subacromial contact pressure because of the spacer effect provided by the LHBT. 10

In this article, we describe a surgical technique that combines LHBT and dermal allograft patch augmentation using a knotless double‐row technique for anterosuperior medium‐to‐large RC repairs. We utilize commonly used implants for cuff repairs, without the need for additional fixation devices such as more anchors, bioabsorbable, or PEEK staples. The goal is to achieve a simple and cost‐effective method for securing the dermal allograft patch on top of the repaired tendon, thereby providing more footprint coverage.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Surgical Indications

The indications are patients with (1) medium to large rotator cuff tears (RCTs) according to the Rodeo classification 11 and less than Patte stage 3 tendon retraction, 12 (2) acromiohumeral distance > 7 mm on the anteroposterior radiograph with humerus in neutral rotation, 13 and (3) supraspinatus muscle fatty infiltration < Goutallier grade 3 (Figure 1). 14 The contraindications are (1) subscapularis tears > Lafosse type 3, 15 (2) fractures, (3) infection‐related pathologies, (4) partial or complete tear of LHBT, and (5) severe glenohumeral joint osteoarthritis and deformity of the humeral head on preoperative radiographs (Table 1).

Patient Preparation and Arthroscopic Portals

All patients are placed under general anesthesia with an interscalene nerve block and are placed in a beach‐chair position with an arm traction device. Five portals (posterior, anterior, lateral, anterolateral, and anteroinferior portals) are created to facilitate the repair (Figure 2).

Patient positioning, Right shoulder. The patient is placed in the beach chair position under general anesthesia combined with an interscalene nerve block. Five arthroscopic portals are typically utilized for subscapularis repair. (A, anterior portal; AI, anteroinferior portal; AL, anterolateral portal; L, lateral portal; P, posterior portal.)

Surgical Technique

Diagnostic arthroscopy using a 30° arthroscope is performed via the posterior portal, and the intra‐articular anatomy is thoroughly evaluated for pathologies. Should there be a subscapularis tear, it is assessed according to the Lafosse classification 15 and is repaired. Additionally, the LHBT is pulled into the intra‐articular space to assess its integrity and inspected along the bicipital groove to detect any hidden lesions. Subsequently, the arthroscope is moved to the subacromial space to evaluate the size and morphology of the supraspinatus tear. After thorough multidirectional release of the supraspinatus and subscapularis, the reducibility and retractability of the tendon to the footprint is assessed.

Long Head of Biceps Tendon Augmentation for RC Repair

A triple‐loaded suture anchor (Y‐Knot RC, ConMed Linvatec, Largo, FL, USA) is implanted at the bone‐cartilage junction, 5 mm posterior to the root of the anterior rotator cable, serving as the medial row (Figure 3A). A lasso‐loop is created using 1 strand (black/white) from the Y‐Knot RC anchor (Figure 3B). The other limb of this suture (black/white) is passed through the lasso‐loop (Figure 3C) to create a rip‐stop configuration. The lasso is then pulled to reroute the LHBT posteriorly and is secured with 7 half‐hitches (Figure 3D). The posteriorly rerouted LHBT provides greater soft tissue coverage at the anterior cable. Adequate transverse humeral ligament release is performed to decrease strangulation of the posteriorly rerouted LHBT. Tenotomy is not performed to keep the integrity of the whole construct.

RC Repair

The remaining two pairs of sutures from the medial row anchor are placed intra‐articularly. Using a BirdBeak suture passer (Stryker, San Jose, CA), the sutures are passed through distinct sections of the supraspinatus musculotendinous junction (posterior, middle, anterior) (Figure 4A and 4B) to construct 2 mattress sutures (Figure 4C). The knots are tied to secure the medial row fixation (Figure 4D), at which point partial coverage of the supraspinatus footprint is achieved. The suture limbs are left uncut and are preserved for the subsequent fixation of the dermal allograft.

Right shoulder, viewing from the lateral portal. Supraspinatus repair. (A,B) Two pairs of sutures from the medial row anchor are placed intra‐articularly. A BirdBeak suture passer is used to pass the sutures through distinct sections of the supraspinatus musculotendinous junction (posterior (arrowhead), middle, and anterior). (C) Two mattress sutures are created (arrow). (D) The knots are tied to secure the medial row fixation.

Dermal Allograft Preparation and Supraspinatus Fixation

First, a 5 cc syringe with the nozzle removed is used to create a custom, elongated cannula that facilitates the passage of all sutures. Extracorporeally, a 3 × 3 cm, 2 mm thick dermal allograft (L&C BIO) is prepared (Figure 5A). PDS sutures are placed at the 4 corners to serve as traction sutures. The four suture limbs from the medial row anchor, which previously secured the supraspinatus tendon, are passed through the medial edge of the dermal allograft. These sutures serve to secure the graft to the bursal side of the supraspinatus once it is implanted. Additionally, a FiberTape suture taken from a SwiveLock anchor (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is passed through the anterior and posterior aspects of the dermal allograft to construct a reverse mattress suture, which will subsequently be used for lateral row fixation. The dermal allograft preparation is shown in Figure 5B.

All sutures are passed through the custom cannula and retrieved through a single arthroscopic portal. The two medial traction sutures are retrieved through the anterior and posterior portals, respectively, to facilitate the shuttling of the dermal allograft into the intra‐articular space.

Two knot pushers are prepared. The same set (color) of uncut medial row sutures that passed through the supraspinatus can be shuttled through the same knot pusher (Figure 5C). When the two medial traction sutures are retrieved through the anterior and posterior portals, these two knot pushers can help introduce the dermal allograft into the shoulder joint, preventing the graft from flipping (2‐knot‐pusher technique). Once the dermal allograft is wrapped and shuttled into the shoulder joint (Figure 5D), it will be positioned upon the superior aspect (bursal side) of the supraspinatus. The two original medial mattress sutures are tied and secured (Figure 5E). After knot tying, one limb of the suture is cut, while the other limb is preserved for lateral row fixation.

Once both medial mattress sutures are secured, all remaining sutures (the two preserved medial row suture limbs, both ends of the FiberTape, and the two lateral traction sutures) are loaded into a 4.75 mm lateral row SwiveLock anchor. The anchor is then impacted into the lateral row fixation site on the greater tuberosity to achieve lateral fixation (Figure 5F).

Finally, the medial portion of the dermal allograft covers the superior surface of the repaired supraspinatus tendon, effectively increasing the thickness of the repaired tendon. The lateral row provides complete coverage of the supraspinatus footprint, effectively extending the length of the repaired tendon (Figure 5G). The final construct is illustrated in Figure 5H. DISCUSSION

The described technique combines posterior rerouting of the LHBT with a dermal allograft patch fixed using a knotless double‐row construct for anterosuperior medium‐to‐large RCTs. This strategy addresses both mechanical and biological factors that are known to contribute to high retear rates after RC repair, particularly in larger tears. 1 , 4 , 6 Patch augmentation and biologic scaffolds have emerged as attractive adjuncts, with randomized and cohort studies showing lower retear rates compared with conventional repair alone. 8 , 11 , 12 , 13

Our technique integrates two emerging augmentation concepts in cuff repairs—LHBT rerouting and dermal allograft overlay—into a single construct using commonly used implants for cuff repairs. The posteriorly rerouted LHBT functions as an in situ autograft that reinforces the anterior cable and partially restores superior capsular continuity, limiting superior migration and reducing subacromial contact pressures. 14

In conclusion, the current technique uses a relatively small (3 × 3 cm) dermal allograft, which is sufficient to cover the supraspinatus footprint. By “sandwiching” the repaired tendon between the bone and the allograft, the construct increases overall thickness and effectively extends the functional tendon length back to the footprint, which is critical in cases with shorter residual tendon length. The knotless lateral row anchor consolidates all remaining sutures—medial row limbs, FiberTape, and traction sutures—into a single lateral fixation point, simplifying the construct while ensuring broad footprint compression. The construct aims to maximize footprint coverage, increase tendon thickness and effective length, and restore superior stability of the glenohumeral joint while relying on widely available implants and reproducible steps.
