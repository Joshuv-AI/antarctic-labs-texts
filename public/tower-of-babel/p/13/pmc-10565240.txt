Barriers to adherence to home-based pulmonary rehabilitation among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in Iran: a descriptive qualitative study
Authors: Ramin Sami, Kobra Salehi, Marzieh Hashemi, Shila Haghighat, Vajihe Atashi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesChronic obstructive pulmonary disease is a chronic disease with numerous complications. Therefore, its effective management depends on home-based pulmonary rehabilitation. The effectiveness of home-based pulmonary rehabilitation programmes in turn depends on patient adherence to them. The aim of this study was to explore the barriers to adherence to home-based pulmonary rehabilitation among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.DesignQualitative descriptive design.SettingThe lung care wards of Al-Zahra and Khorshid teaching hospitals and the comprehensive lung care clinic of Khorshid hospital, Isfahan, Iran.ParticipantsParticipants patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (n=20), their family caregivers (n=15) and healthcare providers (n=14) recruited via purposive sampling.ResultsThe five main categories of the barriers to adherence to home-based pulmonary rehabilitation among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were patient-related barriers, caregivers’ caregiving burden, limited support for patients, healthcare providers’ limited professional competence and ineffective home-based pulmonary rehabilitation planning.ConclusionDifferent and complex factors related to patients, families and healthcare providers can affect adherence to home-based pulmonary rehabilitation among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Healthcare authorities can use the findings of this study to develop strategies to support patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and their family caregivers and thereby reduce the physical and mental complications of Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a highly prevalent chronic disease,1 a major health problem and a leading cause of disability among individuals above 40 years.2 It is a progressive disease with irreversible airflow limitation manifested by dyspnoea, cough and sputum production.3 Currently, there are 300 million patients with COPD in the world4 but there is no precise statistic on COPD prevalence in Iran, though a study reported a prevalence rate of 4.9%.5

COPD is associated with many different consequences. Its associated progressive pulmonary dysfunction, aggravation of pulmonary problems over time and gradual decline of afflicted patients’ health status alter patients’ social life, physical functioning and daily activities.6 Statistics shows that around 70% of patients with COPD have problems in performing their daily activities.7 Afflicted patients gradually lose their social roles and experience different psychological problems such as fear, anxiety, depression, isolation and dependence on others which reduce their quality of life.8 A study reported that 61% of patients with COPD had poor quality of life.9 Moreover, with an annual death rate of more than three million,4 COPD is currently the fourth-leading cause of death and will turn into the third-leading cause of death by 2030.10

Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) is an inseparable component of COPD management. It is an interdisciplinary intervention that consists of nutritional therapy, psychological support, physical exercise and patient education. Studies showed that conventional outpatient PR improves physical activity capacity and quality of life and reduces dyspnoea, acute disease exacerbations, need for hospitalisation and depression among patients with COPD.11 The standard protocol of conventional PR lasts 6–12 weeks, during which patients need to refer to rehabilitation settings 2–3 sessions per week and receive education and services for 1–4 hours per session. Although this is a rational protocol, patients usually have poor adherence to it due to factors such as the need for frequent attendance at rehabilitation settings, heavy costs of transportation and patients’ limited access to rehabilitation settings.12 Studies in the USA reported that the accessibility rate of PR services is 2.6%–3.7%.13 Therefore, home-based PR (HBPR) is recommended to improve patients’ access and adherence to PR services and reduce the costs of COPD management.14

Given the chronicity of COPD, its effective management largely depends on home care and HBPR.15 HBPR is an advanced strategy to reduce disease complications and improve independence and quality of life. It is particularly effective when patients have severe physical dysfunction, poor psychosocial status and limited access to healthcare services.16 A study showed that HBPR can reduce disease management costs, disease exacerbations, and need for hospital care, and improve quality of life.17 Another study reported that HBPR reduced disease exacerbations and improved patient satisfaction, physical activity and quality of life.18 Moreover, a systematic review revealed that HBPR improves quality of life and physical activity and reduces fatigue and respiratory symptoms.14

The success of HBPR programmes depends on patient adherence to them.19 Adherence refers to following the recommendations of healthcare providers respecting medication intake, dietary modifications, lifestyle modifications, physical exercises and acceptance of treatment-related restrictions.20 Studies report that non-adherence to healthcare programmes and recommendations is one of the major problems in HBPR.14 21 For example, a study showed that 29%–50.2% of patients with COPD did not perform recommended therapeutic activities at home.22

Different factors can affect adherence to HBPR. For example, a study showed that poor understanding about disease conditions, disease exacerbations, anxiety, depression and cigarette smoking were the major barriers to adherence to HBPR.23 Another study reported poor socioeconomic status and poor social support as barriers to HBPR.24

An essential step to improve the effectiveness and the success of HBPR is to explore and manage its barriers in the immediate context.2 25 26 Nonetheless, most studies in this area compared the effects of inpatient and HBPR programmes.20 27 Moreover, some studies assessed adherence to HBPR programmes through quantitative designs23 28 and hence, did not provide in-depth data on the barriers to HBPR. Therefore, qualitative studies are needed to produce deeper context-based insight about these barriers. This study was conducted to bridge these gaps. The aim of the study was to explore the barriers to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD.

Methods

Design

This study was conducted in 2022 using the descriptive qualitative design. This design is usually used for the in-depth description and exploration of phenomena or problems. It can provide responses to specific research questions about the concerns, attitudes, emotions, viewpoints and experiences of healthcare providers and policymakers as well as the facilitators and barriers to health-related phenomena in a given sociocultural context. It is specifically beneficial when there is limited information about the phenomenon of interest.29

Participants

Participants were 49 patients with COPD (n=20), their family caregivers (n=15) and healthcare providers (n=14). Inclusion criteria for patients were definite diagnosis of COPD at least 1 year before the study, affliction by stage 2–4 COPD, participation in less than 50% of HBPR sessions, ability to establish verbal communication, no hearing impairment, no affliction by cognitive or mental disorders such as severe depression and agreement for participation in the study. COPD diagnosis was established based on spirometry findings and the criteria of the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD)30 (table 1). According to the GOLD, the four stages of COPD are as follows: stage 1: forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1): >80% and FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC): <70%; stage 2: FEV1: 50%–79% and FEV1/FVC: <70%; stage 3: FEV1: 30%–49% and FEV1/FVC: <70%; and stage 4: FEV1: <30% and FEV1/FVC: <70%. Inclusion criteria for other participants were agreement for participation in the study and history of care provision to patients with COPD for at least 2 years. Sampling was performed purposefully and with maximum variation in terms of age, gender, educational level, work experience and organisational position.

Setting

Participants were selected from the lung care wards of Al-Zahra and Khorshid teaching hospitals and the comprehensive lung care clinic of Khorshid hospital, Isfahan, Iran. The clinic was a PR clinic established in 2018 for care provision to patients with chronic pulmonary problems. It was the only PR centre in Isfahan province in Iran. The staff of this clinic were lung subspecialists, physical medicine and rehabilitation specialists, nurses, nutrition specialists, physiotherapists, social workers, psychologists and psychiatrists. Patients were referred to this centre by their treating physicians to receive healthcare services and education. Rehabilitation services in this centre were provided face to face. Patients who could not refer to the centre received rehabilitation services at home through an HBPR programme. Initially, a lung subspecialist assessed patients for any contraindication to HBPR. Patients who had no contraindication to HBPR and consented to receive HBPR underwent primary assessment for HBPR that included the assessment of quality of life, anxiety, depression, body mass index and exercise capacity through the 6 min walking test. Then, patients and their family caregivers participated in three 1-hour educational sessions to receive education about physical exercise and self-care. A physical medicine specialist provided patients with a physical exercise programme developed based on the results of the 6 min walking test. Moreover, they were provided with a physical exercise workbook and a self-care educational booklet to document their physical exercises and self-care activities. Patients and their family caregivers also received education about the supervision of physical exercises and self-care activities. A nurse also made telephone contacts with patients to encourage their engagement in physical exercises and self-care activities. At the end of the 6-week HBPR course, patients referred to the study setting for the final patient assessment.

Data collection

Data were collected in March–December 2022 through semistructured interviews held in Persian. The time and the place of the interviews were determined based on participants’ preferences and the length of the interviews varied from 30 to 90 min based on participants’ conditions. An interview guide was used to guide the interviews. The validity of the guide was confirmed by three nursing faculties of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Isfahan, Iran, who were experienced in qualitative studies. Moreover, the guide was piloted in two pilot interviews held by the fifth author and appraised by the second and the fifth authors. During interviews, participants were encouraged to share their experiences about the barriers to their adherence to HBPR. Examples of interview questions for patients and family caregivers were, ‘Can you explain about your experiences of HBPR?’, ‘Which factors positively/negatively affect your physical activity at home?’, ‘What are your problems in HBPR?’, ‘Did HBPR affect your other activities?’ and ‘Can you explain why you abandoned HBPR?’ Examples of interview questions for participating healthcare providers were ‘What are your experiences of HBPR’, ‘How is HBPR performed for patients with COPD?’ and ‘What are the barriers to patient adherence to HBPR?’. Further interview questions were determined based on participants’ responses to the main interview questions. Moreover, probing questions such as ‘What do you mean by this?’, ‘Can you explain more about this?’, ‘Why’ and ‘How’ were used during interviews to encourage participants to provide more in-depth explanations about their experiences (see online supplemental material). All interviews were audiorecorded and transcribed word by word by the fifth author at most 48 hours after holding them. Sampling and data collection were kept on up to data saturation, that is, when no new data were obtained from the interviews. The number of the new codes significantly decreased after the 35th interview and reached zero in the 45th interview. Nonetheless, five more interviews were conducted which produced no more new codes and thereby, saturation was achieved.

Data analysis

Data were analysed concurrently with data collection using inductive content analysis as proposed by Graneheim and Lundman.31 Inductive content analysis is used when there is limited information about the phenomenon of interest. In this method, there are no predetermined categories. Rather, categories are developed and labelled based on the raw data. Accordingly, each interview was considered as a unit of analysis and the fifth author listened to its audio file several times and frequently perused its transcript to grasp its main ideas and immerse in the data. Then, she determined and coded meaning units, that is, the words, sentences and paragraphs that included important points about the study aim. She worded the codes using either participants’ own words or her own wording. Meanwhile, she grouped the codes into subcategories according to their similarities, compared subcategories with each other, and grouped them into larger categories according to their interrelationships.

Trustworthiness

The criteria of credibility, dependability, transferability and confirmability were used to ensure trustworthiness.32 Confirmability was ensured through external peer checking by three qualitative researchers who confirmed the accuracy of data analysis. Sampling with maximum variation respecting participants’ age, gender, educational level and work experience as well as provision of clear explanations about the study context helped improve transferability. Credibility was also ensured through member checking by several participants who assessed some coded transcripts of their interviews and confirmed the congruence of our findings with their own experiences. Dependability was ensured by precisely documenting all steps of the study and generating an audit trail.

Patient or public involvement

Patients/members of the public contributed to the data collected and were involved in member checking to ensure the rigour of the study.

Results

Twenty patients with COPD, 15 family caregivers and 14 healthcare providers (49 in total) participated in this study. Most participating patients were male (75%), and the mean of their age was 63.70 years. On the other hand, most participating family caregivers were female (73.33%), and the means of their age and caregiving experience were 48.53 and 6.46 years, respectively. The mean of the work experience of participating healthcare providers was 6.64 years.

The barriers to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD fell into five main categories, namely patient-related barriers, caregivers’ caregiving burden, limited support for patients, healthcare providers’ limited professional competenceand ineffective HBPR planning (table 2).

Patient-related barriers

Patients with COPD had problems which reduced their ability to adhere to HBPR. These problems were their inappropriate attitude and poor motivation, unrealistic expectations, psychological tensions, dissatisfaction with life and conflict between HBPR and occupational conditions.

Inappropriate attitude and poor motivation

Most participating patients noted that they had not understood the importance and the necessity of HBPR and were doubtful about its effectiveness and hence, had limited motivation for participation in HBPR programmes.

I don’t believe in the effectiveness of these home-based exercises and education. I felt my conditions worsened and hence, I discontinued the exercises (P. 6).

Unrealistic expectations

Some patients expected to have no need for medications or regular medical visits while receiving HBPR. However, the non-fulfilment of such unrealistic expectations caused them negative feelings such as disappointment and failure and reduced their adherence to HBPR.

HBPR should eliminate my need for regular medical visits. I expected to get complete recovery and experience no more symptoms (P. 1).

Psychological tensions

Most patients reported that they had no hope for future, desired to be alone and cry, and had no morale to do their affairs.

I am usually in no mood for doing my affairs. I mostly like to sit in a corner without any interference by others. I have no hope. I will not regain my previous conditions even if I perform these exercises (P. 10).

Dissatisfaction with life

Most participating patients reported dissatisfaction with life and felt unhappy with their conditions and with their inability to live like others. They noted dissatisfaction with life, fatigue and sense of inefficiency due to their conditions, frequent hospitalisations and dependence on others in doing HBPR-related recommendations.

I have experienced suffering since the onset of my life, and now I am ill and disabled, can’t do anything, am always at home, and feel like a burden to others. I feel absurdity (P. 17).

Conflict between HBPR and occupational conditions

Some participating patients noted that their heavy workload, inappropriate occupational conditions and time limitation caused conflicts between HBPR-related recommendations and their occupational responsibilities and conditions.

I work in a steel mill. My work shifts are so that I work 48 hours in front of furnace and am off for 48 hours. I can’t exercise at home with such work conditions and great fatigue (P. 17).

Caregivers’ caregiving burden

The family caregivers of patients with COPD experienced heavy caregiving burden and physical, mental and social stress due to caregiving to their patients which were associated with different problems such as burn-out, fatigue and psychological distress. Study participants noted that family members’ poor collaboration with the main family caregivers made the caregivers feel lonely and undermined their physical and mental health. On the other hand, most participating family caregivers reported heavy caregiving burden and reduced caregiving ability due to their limited caregiving-related knowledge. Given the dependence of patients on their family caregivers for doing their activities, family caregivers’ encounter with any problem reduced their ability to actively engage in patient rehabilitation and thereby, negatively affected patient adherence to HBPR. The four subcategories of this category were family members’ limited collaboration with the main family caregivers, subjective burden of caregiving, objective burden of caregiving and need for information.

Family members’ limited collaboration with the main family caregivers

Family members’ limited collaboration with the main family caregivers and their limited engagement in caregiving caused the main family caregivers to have limited time to perform their own personal affairs and personal and social roles. Therefore, they gradually reduced their support for patients in the process of HBPR.

The whole burden is on my shoulders but I can’t do all tasks alone. Other family members should also collaborate. This is not fair. They never mind when I ask them to help. I have become really tired (P. 23).

Subjective burden of caregiving

Most participating family caregivers reported a set of negative feelings such as loneliness, anger, disability, burnout and constant concern due to their perceived inability to give care, non-fulfilment of their personal needs and their heavy caregiving burden.

I feel great strain. I think I am no more able to spend time [on caregiving]. I am exhausted (P. 26).

Objective burden of caregiving

Affliction of a family member by COPD and the lengthy process of HBPR negatively affected family caregivers’ interpersonal relationships, physical and mental health, and social, occupational, and recreational activities.

Rehabilitation [of my patient] at home takes me so great deal of time that I can’t do my own personal affairs. For example, I have not had any medical visit for my own hypertension. I can’t go anywhere, to a party or a recreational activity. My whole day is spent on checking whether my patient is accurately performing the exercises (P. 29).

Need for information

Most participating family caregivers reported that they received no information from healthcare providers and hence, needed information about COPD, HBPR and their role in HBPR. Some participating family caregivers emphasised that the effectiveness of HBPR greatly depended on family caregivers’ adequate knowledge about it.

No information was provided to us about HBPR. They have to hold formal educational classes and provide education to me that want to give care to my patient at home (P. 28).

Limited support for patients

Most participants reported inadequate support for patients and highlighted the essentiality of providing patients with strong support during HBPR. The three subcategories of this category were inadequate family support, inadequate professional support by healthcare providers and inadequate peer support.

Inadequate family support

Most participants believed that COPD is a family problem rather than a personal problem. Therefore, they highlighted the necessity of family members’ engagement in the process of treatment, particularly HBPR. However, they reported that family members did not engage in HBPR due to their indifference, fatigue and poor mood for caregiving and noted that such non-engagement led to patients’ poor adherence to HBPR.

Family members have significant role in pulmonary rehabilitation, particularly HBPR. However, most family members don’t closely collaborate with us, don’t participate in classes, and have no mood for allocating time to caregiving to their patients. They say they have limited time (P. 47).

Inadequate professional support by healthcare providers

Participants stated that healthcare providers’ indifference towards patients’ concerns, non-follow-up of patients’ conditions and no feedback about patients’ conditions in HBPR acted as barriers to patient adherence to HBPR.

Healthcare providers gave me a program and asked me to perform it at home but they don’t follow me up and don’t listen to me at all. I also didn’t receive any feedback from them in this period. I became disappointed and abandoned the program (P. 3).

Inadequate peer support

Participants believed that one of the main sources of support for patients with COPD is their peers, that is, those whose conditions are similar to theirs. Patients can use their peers’ experiences and information to manage their challenges and improve their health. However, most participants noted that patients had no effective interaction with their peers due to the fact that HBPR services were provided at home and hence, they had poor morale and adherence to HBPR.

Unfortunately, we have no association for patients to collect them together once in a while. Certainly, visiting patients who have experienced improvements in their conditions due to HBPR, talking with them, and using the experiences have significant positive effects (P. 45).

Healthcare providers’ limited professional competence

The limited professional competence of healthcare providers was one of the major barriers to HBPR. Professional competence refers to the characteristics and abilities that have significant influence on the accurate and effective provision of HBPR services. Participants highlighted that healthcare providers’ professional competence is essential to maintain the continuity of HBPR and noted that their limited professional competence negatively affects patient adherence to HBPR. This category had four subcategories, namely poor professional attitude and motivation, limited professional accountability, limited specialised knowledge and skills, and ineffective patient education.

Poor professional attitude and motivation

Participants highlighted that some healthcare providers did not have positive attitude and belief about HBPR and its importance and thereby, could not positively encourage their patients to adhere to it. Moreover, they reported that some healthcare providers had poor motivation for accurately performing HBPR measures due to limited managerial support for them, limited managerial feedback to their practice, limited managerial attention to their problems. They also noted that healthcare providers’ poor motivation disappointed patients and reduced their adherence to HBPR.

There is no difference between an employee who accurately performs tasks and one who doesn’t accurately perform tasks. Do you believe that I received no positive feedback despite my wholehearted effort? It is no longer important to me how the tasks are performed and do not assign high priority to check whether patients accurately perform their exercises (P. 43).

Limited professional accountability

Participants highlighted that given the significant role of HBPR in improving patients’ health, healthcare providers need to have great accountability towards patients and closely monitor their adherence to HBPR. However, they noted that healthcare providers did not comprehensively assess patients’ and their family caregivers’ conditions, were not easily accessible to them, and referred patients for their questions to their colleagues and hence, negatively affected patient adherence to HBPR.

It is very important that we completely assess the conditions of patients who are going to receive PR at home, answer their questions, and be easily accessible to them in order to accurately manage their emerging problems. Inattention to these issues may cause patients to give up rehabilitation(P. 38).

Limited specialised knowledge and skills

Participants considered healthcare providers’ adequate specialised knowledge and skills as essential prerequisites to successful HBPR and noted that their limited knowledge and skills may reduce patients’ trust in them and undermine their adherence to HBPR.

Many of the rehabilitation team members have limited knowledge of HBPR. For example, when I ask them how to manage dyspnea or save energy at home, they know nothing. Nonetheless, they expect patients to closely adhere to the rehabilitation program (P. 38).

Ineffective patient education

Most participants reported ineffective patient education by healthcare providers due to factors such as the use of ineffective educational strategies, inappropriate amount of information in each educational session, no use of appropriate teaching aids and provision of education without considering patients’ needs. They highlighted that these problems negatively affected patient adherence to HBPR.

Patient education is the most important component of HBPR. However, our recommendations and prescriptions can’t be called education. Patients really don’t learn anything. Colleagues don’t want to spend adequate time, provide complete explanations, and display a video to help patients better understand the educational materials (P. 44).

Ineffective HBPR planning

Participants highlighted the importance of effective planning for HBPR, quality education for HBPR staff and careful managerial supervision of their practice. The four subcategories of this category were lack of updated and culturally appropriate practical guidelines, inefficient in-service staff training, inadequate supervision and poor collaboration among healthcare providers.

Lack of updated and culturally appropriate practical guidelines

According to the participants, updated and culturally appropriate guidelines are necessary for HBPR which clearly explain strategies for patient assessment and HBPR staff’s job specifications.

Foreign guidelines are not useful for us. A team of experts should be assembled to develop a culturally appropriate guideline based on the country conditions, equipment, etc. and update the guideline every several years (P. 46).

Inefficient in-service staff training

Participants noted that despite the positive effects of in-service HBPR-related staff training programmes such as knowledge improvement and HBPR quality improvement, staff received limited quality training in this area and had no access to quality in-service training programmes due to the shortage of experienced instructors.

Education about HBPR is very poor. There are no educational classes for us in which experts provide us with comprehensive explanations. We are performing patient care measures that we don’t know whether they are accurate or inaccurate (P. 40).

Inadequate supervision

Participating healthcare providers considered managerial supervision as an influential factor on the accurate and appropriate performance of HBPR-related tasks and noted that managerial supervision improved their practice. However, they highlighted that managers had limited time for HBPR supervision, had inadequate supervision of patient care and care documentation, and did not value HBPR supervision.

The manager is very busy because he has other responsibilities beside supervision and has no time for supervision. I haven’t seen so far that he checks patients’ medical records for accurate care documentation or patient follow-up assessment. The members of healthcare team should feel that managers value this program (P. 39).

Poor collaboration among healthcare providers

Most participants reported that factors such as poor collaboration and communication among healthcare providers, their unclear job specifications, their interprofessional conflicts and their uncertainty were associated with patient bafflement and dissatisfaction, undermined their adherence to HBPR and reduced their opportunities to prevent COPD complications.

Based on the results of the six-minute walking test, the patient judges that his condition has improved, while the lung specialist believes that the condition of the patient has worsened. I don’t really know what the specialist means or what his expectation of patient’s condition is. These problems also baffle us and patients and lead to patients’ withdrawal from HBPR (P. 42).

Discussion

This study explored the barriers to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD. The five main categories of these barriers were patient-related barriers, caregivers’ caregiving burden, limited support for patients, healthcare providers’ limited professional competence and ineffective HBPR planning.

One of the main categories of the barriers to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD was patient-related barriers. In agreement with this finding, a study showed that chronic physical problems that undermined patients’ functional abilities reduced their motivation and hope and thereby, altered their adherence to treatment regimen.33 Another study reported that patients with COPD limited their physical activity due to their fear over disease and dyspnoea aggravation.34 On the other hand, a study found that patients who understood the importance and necessity of treatment were more likely to continue it, experienced lower levels of anxiety and depression, and had higher quality of life.35 Our findings also revealed psychological tensions as a patient-related barrier to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD. Similarly, a study revealed that disappointment, anxiety and depression increased the risk of disease complications, reduced quality of life and caused patients to discontinue outpatient PR.36 Another study found significant direct relationship between non-adherence to home-based treatment exercises and the levels of anxiety and depression.37 We also found that unrealistic expectations acted as a patient-related barrier to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD. In line with this finding, a study showed that incongruence between medical recommendations and patients’ beliefs and expectations negatively affected patients’ intention to adhere to the recommendations.38 Another study reported that patients’ limited knowledge of rehabilitation was associated with their misconceptions about rehabilitation and discontinuation of rehabilitation measures.39 The members of PR rehabilitation teams can improve patients’ adherence to HBPR, foster their collaboration and reduce the likelihood of PR failure through informing them about HBPR priorities, establishing realistic and achievable goals, reminding them of care measures and follow-up visits and encouraging their family caregivers’ engagement in HBPR.

Caregivers’ caregiving burden was the second main category of the barriers to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD. Findings showed that family caregivers experienced caregiving-related problems, such as inability to balance their different roles and activities, mainly due to the limited collaboration of other family members. Family caregivers can monitor disease symptoms, supervise accurate intake of medications, assess patients for disease aggravation and make appointments for medical visits.40 However, the unpredictability of the COPD course and the instability of patients’ needs make caregiving to these patients difficult for their family caregivers and significantly increase their caregiving burden.41 Meanwhile, there are limited sources of support for these family caregivers in Iran and limited data about their needs.42 Therefore, caregiving to these patients without the collaboration of other family members is almost impossible for the main family caregiver.43 In developed countries, different community-based services are available to reduce the caregiving burden of family caregivers, while the whole caregiving burden in Iran is on family members and main family caregivers may be unable to even have several hours of free time to do their personal affairs. Our findings also showed that family caregivers experienced heavy subjective and objective caregiving burden. A study reported that family caregivers, particularly patients’ spouses, carried heavy caregiving burden due to the multiplicity of their responsibilities and roles and hence, experienced conflicts in performing their occupational roles.44 Shortage of community-based nursing services, inadequacy of professional support for family caregivers and healthcare providers’ exclusive focus on patient care in Iran impose heavy caregiving burden on family caregivers and cause them different problems. Therefore, they clearly need strong professional and social support.45 Careful assessment of family caregivers’ needs and appropriate interventions to fulfil their needs and reduce their caregiving burden are also essential. Moreover, our findings revealed that family caregivers had limited knowledge of HBPR and hence, needed caregiving-related information. Family caregivers in Iran accept the heavy responsibility of caregiving almost without any previous knowledge and skills, and hence, they experience great confusion and different problems during caregiving. Moreover, there is no effective interaction between family caregivers and healthcare providers and the need of family caregivers for information is not adequately fulfilled.46 Improvement of family caregivers’ caregiving-related knowledge can empower them to make more sensible decisions,47 while inattention to the improvement of their knowledge can reduce their well-being and increase their caregiving burden.48 Lack of quality education for patients and family caregivers is a major barrier to the maintenance of care continuity in developing countries.49 Lack of family therapy is also due to problems in healthcare systems as well as families’ level of knowledge and contextual background. Therefore, development and implementation of family therapy and public education programmes are recommended.

Limited support for patients was the third main category of the barriers to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD. Findings indicated that inadequate family support acted as an HBPR barrier. A study showed that due to the chronicity of COPD and the multiplicity of patients’ problems, family support gradually reduced and family members gradually became indifferent to patients and treatment.50 Another study showed conflicts between patients and family caregivers due to caregiving-related demands and reported that these conflicts caused fatigue and dissatisfaction for caregivers and hence, reduced their support for their patients.51 Social changes in our country such as reduced size of families and houses, increased divorce rate, and increased women employment rate have increased the problems of patients with chronic illnesses and reduced family support for them.44 In order to improve patient adherence to HBPR, family members need to actively engage in the process of PR, patient education should be provided in the presence of family members, and educational interventions should aim at changing the attitudes of both patients and family members.52 Our findings also revealed that inadequate support by healthcare providers negatively affected adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD. Similarly, a study showed lack of an efficient patient follow-up system, limited patient support by physicians, and lack of healthcare providers’ appropriate communication with patients and their family members as barriers to patient adherence to treatments.39 Another study reported that lack of professional support for patients aggravated patient anxiety and doubtfulness, reduced their coping ability, and hence, reduced their treatment acceptance.53 On the other hand, strong professional patient support by healthcare providers enhances patient satisfaction and adherence to treatments.54

The fourth main category of the barriers to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD was healthcare providers’ limited professional competence. Limited professional competence, ineffective fulfilment of patients’ needs, provision of incomplete and contradictory information to patients and failure to actively involve patients in HBPR can lead to uncertainty, confusion and dissatisfaction among patients and reduce their ability to prevent complications.55 On the other hand, healthcare providers’ great professional competence enhances patients’ trust and satisfaction with healthcare services and improves their motivation to continue their treatments.56 A study showed that healthcare providers’ professional competence had significant influence on patients’ attitudes and behaviours and introduced it as a prerequisite to treatment acceptance and treatment adherence among patients.57 Participating healthcare providers highlighted that they had limited motivation for the provision of quality HBPR services due to factors such as lack of tangible and intangible incentives, lack of constructive feedback to their practice and lack of attention to their problems. A study showed that motivation affects personal power and behaviour, facilitates the attainment of goals, and increases interest in doing tasks and activities and reported support, rewards, knowledge improvement, good social position and sense of worth as the main sources of motivation for healthcare providers.58 Our findings also indicated that some healthcare providers had limited knowledge and skills and hence, could not provide effective education to patients. Ineffective education refers to a routine education that is not based on patients’ educational needs and is provided by indifferent and inattentive staff.59 A study reported that allocation of inadequate time to patient counselling, non-responsiveness to their questions and provision of education that was irrelevant to patients’ and family members’ needs were among the determining factors of treatment non-acceptance.60 Low-quality patient education and care are among the most prevalent barriers to the continuity of care in developing counties.61 We also found limited professional accountability as a main barrier to patient adherence to HBPR. Accountability is the basis of healthcare providers’ professional practice and its shortage has destructive effects on care quality62 and can lead to patient injury.63 Routine-based practice of healthcare providers due to their limited professional accountability is a major problem in Iran and can considerably reduce care quality.64

Ineffective HBPR planning was the fifth main category of the barriers to HBPR. Findings showed poor collaboration as a main barrier in this area. Similarly, a study found limited collaboration and interaction and intense competition among healthcare providers and reported that these problems can negatively affect the outcomes of rehabilitation services.39 Another study reported that rehabilitation staff had limited technical and communicational competence.65 Although teamwork is a key prerequisite to rehabilitation service provision, quality teamwork-related education and strategies are needed to improve teamwork among healthcare providers. Our findings also revealed inadequate supervision of rehabilitation staff’s practice as a barrier to HBPR. Supervision and feedback giving are among the influential factors on healthcare providers’ motivation for behavioural modification.66 We also found that managers had multiple roles and responsibilities and hence, had limited time for the supervision of healthcare staff’s practice. Similarly, a study showed that managers’ heavy workload due to their role multiplicity reduced their time for effective supervision.67

Limitations

Two participants agreed just on telephone interview and did not consent to audio record their interviews. Therefore, we had to make notes of their interviews and hence, missed some parts of their speech. Another limitations of this study was that the study was conducted in the sociocultural context of Iran, and therefore, sociocultural differences should be considered while interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

This study concludes that the major barriers to adherence to HBPR among patients with COPD are patient-related barriers, caregivers’ caregiving burden, limited support for patients, healthcare providers’ limited professional competence and ineffective HBPR planning. The findings of this study can improve the knowledge of governmental and healthcare authorities and the general public about the barriers to HBPR and help develop more effective strategies to improve patient adherence to HBPR. Examples of these strategies are the revision of care protocols, development of culturally appropriate guidelines, provision of stronger professional support to patients, family therapy, development of purposeful educational programmes for patients, closer managerial supervision of HBPR programmes and implementation of periodical in-service training programmes for HBPR staff.
