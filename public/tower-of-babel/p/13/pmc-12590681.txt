Genetic diversity assessment and morpho-agronomic characterization of mungbean (Vigna radiata (L.) Walp.) germplasm using multivariate analysis
Authors: Mohammad Irfan, M. Ashraf Bhat, Uzma Rashid, Khairah Mubarak Alwutayd, Nora M. Al Aboud, Areej S. Jalal, Z. A. Dar, Sabina Nasseer, Mohd Altaf Wani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The present study assessed the phenotypic and genetic diversity of 115 mungbean (Vigna radiata L.) genotypes using a combination of qualitative, quantitative, and multivariate analyses. Frequency distribution of qualitative traits revealed substantial morphological variation, with most genotypes exhibiting good early plant vigor, semi-erect plant type, indeterminate growth habit, and green reniform leaves. Traits such as curved pods, pod pubescence, above-canopy pod position, and lustrous seed surfaces were prevalent, while seed coat color was predominantly green or olive, and seed shape was nearly equally distributed between oblong and oval. Quantitative traits showed wide variability, with early flowering and maturity observed in several genotypes. Best Linear Unbiased Predictions (BLUPs) across 12 environments revealed significant variability in days to flowering, maturity, pod length, seed number, and seed weight, with moderate to high heritability for most traits, particularly seed diameter and hundred-seed weight. Mineral nutrient content also varied broadly, though heritability was generally low for micronutrients like iron, zinc, and manganese. Correlation analysis highlighted strong positive associations among key agronomic traits such as pod length, pods per plant, seed size, and plant height, whereas seed diameter showed negative correlations with multiple yield components, indicating potential trade-offs. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) identified seed diameter, seed size, hundred-seed weight, and days to flowering and maturity as the major contributors to phenotypic variation. The PCA biplots and scatter plots revealed distinct genotype groupings and identified outliers such as genotypes 57, 83, and 110 with unique trait profiles, offering opportunities for trait-specific selection. A circular phylogenetic tree further confirmed substantial intra-specific diversity and identified genetically distinct genotypes (e.g., 14, 27, 78), reinforcing their potential utility in breeding programs. These findings provide a comprehensive understanding of mungbean diversity and support the selection of promising genotypes for future genetic improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07561-z.

Introduction

Climate change and nutritional security are pressing challenges confronting agriculture today and in the future [1]. Rising global temperatures, erratic rainfall patterns, and increasing soil degradation threaten food production systems, necessitating the development of climate-resilient and nutritionally rich crops [2]. In this context, mungbean (Vigna radiata), an economically significant legume, has gained increasing attention due to its high tolerance to drought and its potential to enhance both food and nutritional security [3]. Mungbean is a nutrient-dense crop, rich in protein, dietary fiber, and essential minerals such as potassium, making it a valuable alternative to animal protein sources [4]. It serves dual purposes in human and animal nutrition, being consumed as both a vegetable and a grain [5]. Furthermore, Mungbean contributes significantly to sustainable agriculture by improving soil fertility through biological nitrogen fixation, which in turn reduces reliance on synthetic fertilizers [6]. Its adaptability to diverse agroecological conditions and short growth cycle further contribute to its significance as a climate-resilient crop, particularly in regions of Asia and Africa where small-scale farmers rely on it for both subsistence and income generation [7]. A comprehensive understanding of genetic resources is crucial in any breeding program, as it aids in the selection of suitable parental lines and broadens the genetic base of the crop [8]. However, the increasing demand for food and the introduction of new cultivars have led to a reduction in genetic resources, causing genetic erosion of valuable germplasm [9]. The primary centers of origin of mungbean hold a wealth of diverse germplasm resources that must be conserved and systematically evaluated for their agronomic potential [10]. Mungbean (Vigna radiata (L.) Walp.) is a short-duration, self-pollinated, diploid legume species (2n = 2x = 22) widely cultivated for its high protein content, fast growth, and adaptability to diverse agroclimatic conditions [11]. As an important pulse crop, mungbean contributes significantly to the nutritional security and agricultural sustainability of millions of smallholder farmers, particularly in South and Southeast Asia [4]. The seeds are a rich source of easily digestible protein (20–25%), complex carbohydrates, dietary fiber, minerals such as iron and zinc, and various bioactive compounds, making them an essential component of vegetarian diets [12]. Moreover, mungbean is valued for its role in sustainable cropping systems due to its nitrogen-fixing ability, low input requirements, and suitability for intercropping and crop rotation [13].

Globally, mungbean is grown on approximately 7.3 million hectares, with an estimated annual production of 6.3 million tonnes (FAOSTAT, 2023). The crop is predominantly cultivated in Asia, with India, Myanmar, China, Thailand, and Pakistan being the major producers [14]. India ranks first both in terms of area (around 5.2 million hectares) and production (approximately 2.5 million tonnes), contributing nearly one-third of the world’s mungbean output [15]. Major mungbean-growing states in India include Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, and Madhya Pradesh. Despite its nutritional and agronomic advantages, mungbean yields remain relatively low and stagnant due to several factors, including narrow genetic diversity, susceptibility to biotic and abiotic stresses, and limited adoption of improved varieties [16]. Genetic enhancement of mungbean requires access to a broad and well-characterized germplasm base. Morphological and agronomic characterization of germplasm collections provides a primary yet essential step in identifying trait variability, understanding genetic relationships, and selecting superior genotypes for breeding programs. However, conventional characterization based on a few traits may not capture the full extent of diversity present in the gene pool [17]. This study is crucial as it explores the genetic diversity and morpho-agronomic characteristics of mungbean (Vigna radiata) germplasm, which is vital for enhancing the crop’s productivity and resilience. By using multivariate analysis, the research aims to identify key genetic variations and agronomic traits that influence yield, disease resistance, and environmental adaptability. Understanding this diversity is essential for selecting superior mungbean varieties that can thrive in diverse climatic conditions, ensuring food security, and supporting sustainable agricultural practices. This comprehensive assessment also contributes to the conservation of genetic resources and the development of improved cultivars for future agricultural challenges. In this context, multivariate statistical techniques, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA), cluster analysis, and correlation analysis, serve as powerful tools to dissect complex trait relationships, reduce data dimensionality, and group genotypes based on multiple trait expressions. Several studies have reported genetic variability in mungbean for yield and associated traits, but most of them are limited in scope, involving few genotypes or environments. Comprehensive characterization using large, diverse panels of germplasm under multi-environment conditions remains relatively scarce, especially when linked with both qualitative and quantitative traits. Moreover, the increasing interest in climate-resilient and nutrient-dense crops emphasizes the need to identify genotypes with favorable combinations of agronomic performance and adaptability traits.

The necessity to conduct this research stems from the critical need to enhance the productivity, adaptability, and nutritional value of mungbean. Despite its agronomic importance, mungbean improvement has been constrained by limited genetic base utilization and insufficient characterization of available germplasm. Climate change, evolving pest and disease pressures, and the increasing demand for nutrient-dense, short-duration crops further underscore the urgency to identify and develop resilient genotypes. Assessing genetic diversity and morpho-agronomic traits using multivariate analysis allows for a comprehensive understanding of trait interactions, variability, and genotype performance under field conditions. This information is vital for selecting superior lines for breeding programs focused on yield improvement, stress tolerance, and sustainability. Additionally, such studies contribute to the conservation and efficient utilization of genetic resources, ensuring long-term crop improvement and food security.

In this context, the present study was undertaken to assess the genetic diversity and morpho-agronomic variation in a diverse panel of 115 mungbean genotypes using multivariate analyses. The specific objectives of the study were: (1) To evaluate the variability in qualitative and quantitative traits among mungbean germplasm under field conditions. (2) To estimate trait heritability and inter-trait associations for yield-related and morphological traits. (3) To apply multivariate techniques (PCA, BLUP, and clustering) for identifying patterns of diversity and discriminating superior genotypes. (4) To identify promising genotypes for direct use in breeding programs aimed at yield improvement, stress adaptation, and nutritional enhancement. This study aims to provide a scientific basis for mungbean genetic improvement by revealing the structure and extent of trait diversity in the existing germplasm pool, thus contributing to the development of more resilient and productive mungbean cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and field trials

In this study, 115 mungbean genotypes were evaluated using an augmented block design to assess their qualitative and quantitative traits. For qualitative trait assessment, genotypes were characterized based on eighteen descriptors along with micro & macro-nutrient evaluation at the Faculty of Agriculture (FoA), SKUAST-K, during 2022–2024. For quantitative trait evaluation, the 115 genotypes including five standard checks (CO-6, HUM-6, MGG-351, PMB-27, and SML-115) were tested across four major mungbean growing regions (FoA Wadura Baramulla, KVK Kupwara, FoH Shalimar Srinagar, MRCFC Khudwani Kulgam) in the temperate zones of the Western Himalayas. Details of all genotypes, including checks, are provided in Fig. 1, Table S1. Uniform standard agronomic practices were applied across all environments to ensure consistent crop growth. Each genotype was planted in a single 2-meter-long row, with rows spaced 60 cm apart and plants within each row spaced 10 cm apart.

Quantitative trait data across twelve environments were analyzed using Best Linear Unbiased Predictions (BLUPs), with genotypes treated as random effects. BLUPs for all quantitative traits were calculated using PBTools version 1.4 [19]. The resulting BLUP values were then used for variability analysis, correlation analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), and cluster analysis, all conducted using R software (version 4.5.0).

Results

The ANOVA revealed highly significant effects (p < 0.001) of environment, genotype, and genotype × environment (G×E) interaction on the traits studied, indicating substantial variation due to each of these sources (Table 2).

The environment showed a strong influence, suggesting that growing conditions across the 12 environments significantly affected trait expression. Genotype effects were also highly significant, confirming considerable genetic variability among the 16 genotypes, which is promising for selection and breeding. The significant G×E interaction indicates that genotypes responded differently across environments, emphasizing the need for multi-environment evaluation to identify stable and adaptable genotypes. While replication effects were significant, reflecting minor experimental variation, the non-significant residual variance suggests that most of the variability was adequately captured by the main sources. These findings justify the use of BLUPs and multivariate analyses for accurate and environment-inclusive selection of superior mungbean genotypes.

Frequency distribution of quality traits in mungbean

The frequency distribution of mungbean genotypes based on qualitative traits is presented in Fig. 3. Out of the total, 115 genotypes, majority of genotypes (106) showed a semi-erect plant type and an indeterminate growth habit. Most genotypes had light green immature pods were observed in 90 genotypes, while mature pod color was mostly brown in 63 genotypes. Pod shape was typically curved at the tip, and thin pod wall thickness was predominant among the genotypes. Predominant colour of leaves was green and leaf shape was found reniform (79 genotypes), followed by Lanceolate (36 genotypes). Pod pubescence was present in 113 genotypes, and the two genotypes were found with no pubescence. Seed shape was almost equally distributed between oblong (63 genotypes) and oval (52 genotypes), whereas green seed coat color was most common, found in 96 genotypes, followed by olive in 19 genotypes. Additionally, a lustrous seed surface was observed in the majority of genotypes (93).

Estimates of variance

The analysis of genetic variability among the studied traits revealed considerable environmental influence on most characters, as indicated by much higher phenotypic coefficients of variation (PCV) compared to genotypic coefficients of variation (GCV). Micronutrient traits such as Fe, Zn, Ca, P, K, Na, and Cu showed low genotypic variances with large differences between GCV and PCV, suggesting that their expression is highly affected by environmental factors, making direct selection for improvement less effective. In contrast, traits like Mn, hundred seed weight (HSW), and grains per pod (GPP) exhibited relatively higher GCV and genetic advance, indicating the presence of substantial genetic variability and better prospects for improvement through selection. Agronomic traits such as days to maturity (DM), branches per plant (BPP), days to 50% flowering (DFF), pod length (PL), pods per plant (PPP), and seed diameter (SD) displayed low GCV and genetic advance values, suggesting limited genetic control and stability across environments. Discussion

Understanding the genetic variability among crop genotypes is a cornerstone of any effective crop improvement program. In this study, the frequency distribution of 115 mungbean genotypes across qualitative and quantitative traits, along with multivariate and heritability analyses, revealed significant phenotypic and genetic variation, which is essential for developing high-yielding, nutritionally enriched, and stress-resilient cultivars.

Morphological diversity and trait distribution

The wide range observed in qualitative traits such as growth habit, plant type, leaf shape, seed coat color, and pod characteristics indicates substantial variability within the studied mungbean genotypes. The predominance of semi-erect plant type and indeterminate growth habit (106 genotypes) aligns with findings by Yoseph et al. [20], who reported similar trait patterns as advantageous for extended flowering and fruiting periods, contributing to yield stability in marginal environments. The reniform leaf shape being dominant (79 genotypes) may be linked to optimal photosynthetic efficiency under field conditions, a trait previously associated with higher biomass accumulation Kumar et al. [21]. The predominance of curved pod shapes and thin pod walls is indicative of the genetic makeup of traditional varieties, with modern breeding often selecting for straight and thick pods to facilitate mechanical harvesting and reduce shattering losses [22]. Above-canopy pod positioning observed in nearly all genotypes can enhance harvesting efficiency and reduce pod loss, a trait increasingly emphasized in mungbean Ideotype breeding [23].

Quantitative trait variation and heritability patterns

The frequency distribution of days to flowering and maturity highlighted considerable differences in phenological development, offering a valuable spectrum for breeding for early or late maturity depending on regional agroecological needs [24]. The presence of genotypes flowering as early as 62 days and maturing by 75 days (e.g., G10, G11, G36, G38) is consistent with short-duration cultivars reported in tropical mungbean programs [25], which are beneficial for intercropping systems and escaping terminal drought. Seed yield components such as 100-seed weight, seeds per pod, and pod number per plant displayed broad variability. Notably, genotypes like G75 and G74 exhibited the highest 100-seed weight (>7 g), a trait positively correlated with market value and milling recovery [26]. Similarly, high pod and grain counts in genotypes like G31 and G95 align with earlier work by Sandhu et al. [27], where these traits were key contributors to yield per plant. The high Shannon-Weaver Diversity Index (0.73 to 0.97) across traits further confirms a rich phenotypic base. Traits such as number of pods per plant, flowering time, and branch count recorded the highest diversity, indicating their strong discriminative power and utility in varietal classification and selection. Such high diversity values echo findings from global mungbean core collections [28], reinforcing their use in association mapping and gene discovery.

Qualitative trait variation and heritability patterns

The analysis of micronutrient traits in mungbean revealed moderate to high genetic variability and heritability for several elements, indicating their potential for genetic improvement. Traits such as calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) exhibited relatively higher heritability estimates (ranging from 64.2% to 74.7%), suggesting that these traits are largely governed by genetic factors and can be effectively improved through selection. Sodium (Na), copper (Cu), and manganese (Mn) also showed moderate heritability (57.4%–70.1%), though environmental influence was more pronounced. In contrast, iron (Fe) and zinc (Zn) had lower heritability values (60% and 50%, respectively), with residual variances comparable to or exceeding genetic variances, indicating stronger environmental effects and less reliability in selection across environments. These results are in concordance with the study of previous authors [29] who demonstrate that integrated breeding approaches that combine classical selection with modern “omics” technologies are increasingly being employed to exploit the existing genetic variability for micronutrient improvement. These results suggest that while traits like Ca, Mg, P, and K are promising targets for nutritional enhancement through breeding, traits such as Fe and Zn may require more precise selection strategies and environmental control to achieve meaningful genetic gains, which is especially important for biofortification and developing nutrient-rich mungbean varieties.

Heritability insights and environment interaction

The multi-environment BLUP-based heritability estimates provided insights into the stability and genetic control of traits across different environments. Traits such as seed diameter, 100-seed weight, and plant height displayed high heritability across most environments, indicating strong genetic control and reduced environmental influence. This is consistent with studies by Yadav et al. [30], who highlighted the heritability of yield-related traits as crucial for direct selection in early generations.

Conversely, traits like pod length and number of seeds per pod showed moderate heritability in many environments, suggesting genotype × environment interaction. Mineral traits such as Fe, Zn, and Mg showed wide phenotypic variation but exhibited low to moderate heritability, indicating a stronger environmental component or polygenic inheritance, as suggested by Kumar et al. [31]. These findings emphasize the need for multi-location and multi-season evaluations when selecting for nutritional quality in mungbean.

Trait associations and trade-offs

Correlation analysis highlighted strong positive relationships among key yield traits such as pod length, seed size, pod number, and plant height. These relationships support the possibility of simultaneous selection for multiple yield-enhancing traits, corroborating previous reports by Kgasudi et al. [32], who emphasized the utility of indirect selection in mungbean breeding. Interestingly, seed diameter showed strong negative correlations with several yield traits, indicating a potential physiological or resource allocation trade-off. This suggests that while increasing seed size (diameter) may benefit grain quality, it could adversely affect seed number or pod traits unless carefully managed through balanced breeding. Similar trade-offs were observed by Kuzbakova et al. [22] in other legume species, where emphasis on one yield component often affected another. The weak and inconsistent associations between phenological traits (flowering and maturity) and mineral content suggest that mineral uptake and accumulation are likely governed by distinct physiological mechanisms, possibly involving root traits and soil interactions, as noted by Freschet et al. [33].

Estimates of variance

The evaluation of genetic variability among the studied mungbean genotypes revealed considerable differences between genotypic and phenotypic parameters, indicating varying degrees of environmental influence on trait expression. In most cases, the phenotypic coefficient of variation (PCV) was markedly higher than the genotypic coefficient of variation (GCV), demonstrating that environmental factors substantially affected the observed variability. This pattern was particularly evident for the micronutrient traits such as Fe, Zn, Ca, P, K, Na, and Cu, where PCV values were much higher than their corresponding GCV values. Such a disparity implies that the expression of these traits in mungbean is largely influenced by environmental conditions, and hence, direct phenotypic selection may not lead to significant genetic improvement. For instance, Fe and Zn exhibited moderate GCV (28.75 and 14.32, respectively) but very high PCV (81.32 and 40.38), suggesting that their phenotypic variation is mainly environmentally driven. In contrast, traits such as Mn content, hundred seed weight (HSW), and grains per pod (GPP) displayed relatively higher GCV and moderate genetic advance, suggesting the predominance of additive gene action and better prospects for improvement through selection. The high GCV (80.64) and genetic advance (0.146) observed for Mn content indicate substantial inherent genetic variability that can be effectively utilized in breeding programs aimed at biofortification. Likewise, HSW and GPP exhibited moderate GCV (2.39 and 3.48, respectively) and corresponding genetic advances (0.1357 and 0.3218), signifying that these yield-contributing traits could respond favorably to simple selection methods, as their inheritance appears to be largely additive. On the other hand, agronomic traits such as days to maturity (DM), days to 50% flowering (DFF), branches per plant (BPP), pod length (PL), pods per plant (PPP), and seed diameter (SD) showed low GCV and genetic advance values. The wide differences between PCV and GCV for these traits suggest a strong environmental influence and low heritability, thereby limiting the effectiveness of selection based on phenotypic performance. Similar findings were reported by earlier researchers [34] who emphasized the strong environmental dependence of micronutrient accumulation in mungbean seeds, low genotypic variance in traits like Ca and Mg further supports the dominance of environmental factors and the relative stability of these traits across genotypes.

Multivariate analyses and genotype discrimination

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) provided clear clustering of traits and genotypes, revealing that the first four principal components accounted for a significant portion of trait variability. Seed diameter, 100-seed weight, and seed size were major contributors to PC1 and PC2, highlighting their pivotal role in phenotypic differentiation. These results mirror studies by Azam et al. [26], who demonstrated that PCA can successfully uncover complex trait interrelationships in legume germplasm collections. The biplots effectively differentiated outlying genotypes such as 110, 112, and 113, which exhibited unique trait combinations, potentially harboring rare alleles or unique gene combinations. Such genotypes are invaluable for broadening the genetic base of breeding pools and for use in pre-breeding programs as demonstrated by Singh et al. [35].

Cluster analysis and breeding implications

Hierarchical clustering grouped genotypes into approximately 4–6 major clusters, suggesting considerable phenotypic variability. The grouping pattern supports the presence of distinct genetic lineages or trait-based divergence, echoing previous diversity studies in mungbean as reviewed by Sharma et al. [36]. Clusters containing elite genotypes with favorable yield, early maturity, and superior nutritional traits offer valuable starting points for trait introgression and heterotic group formation [26]. The cluster mean analysis of both micronutrient and morpho-agronomic traits revealed significant genetic variability among the mungbean genotypes, offering valuable insights for targeted improvement. Cluster IV emerged as the most promising group, combining high concentrations of essential minerals particularly iron (Fe), copper (Cu), calcium (Ca), and manganese (Mn) with superior agronomic traits such as pod length, seeds per pod, seed size, hundred seed weight, and seed diameter. This makes Cluster IV highly suitable for dual-purpose breeding focused on both yield enhancement and nutritional biofortification. Cluster III also demonstrated strong potential, showing the highest levels of zinc (Zn), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and sodium (Na), along with favorable yield-related traits like branches per plant, pod length, and seed size. These genotypes could be strategically used to improve multiple nutrient traits alongside productivity. Cluster II displayed moderate performance across both nutrient and agronomic traits, suggesting its genotypes may serve as stable, balanced performers in breeding programs. In contrast, Clusters I and V exhibited generally lower means for most traits, although specific attributes like higher seed weight in Cluster I and elevated magnesium and calcium content in Cluster V may still offer utility in trait-specific breeding. The results highlight Clusters III and IV as priority groups for selection and hybridization, particularly for developing high-yielding, nutrient-rich mungbean cultivars tailored to address both productivity and micronutrient deficiencies. The presence of unique clusters with outlier genotypes also opens possibilities for hybrid development and mapping populations aimed at identifying quantitative trait loci (QTLs) or genomic regions linked to agronomic and nutritional traits [37].

Conclusion

The frequency distribution analysis of both qualitative and quantitative traits in 115 mungbean genotypes revealed substantial phenotypic variability, which is a valuable resource for mungbean improvement programs. Qualitative traits such as growth habit, pod pubescence, seed shape, and color demonstrated clear variation, with certain traits showing near-universal expression indicating their potential as stable morphological markers. Notably, traits like seed colour and diameter hold promise for increasing market appeal. Quantitative traits also displayed broad diversity, with early flowering and maturity observed in specific genotypes (e.g., G10, G11, G36, G38) making them candidates for short-duration cropping systems. High-yield potential was evidenced in genotypes like G31, G34, and G68, which showed superior pod number, while G75 and G74 recorded the highest seed weights. The Shannon–Weaver Diversity Index confirmed high trait diversity, particularly for number of pods per plant, flowering time, and number of branches highlighting these as key selection indices. Traits like seed weight, seed diameter, and flowering time also exhibited high heritability across environments, indicating strong genetic control and stability under varying conditions. Correlation and PCA analyses further emphasized the interconnectedness of seed-related traits and their influence on yield, while negative associations with days to maturity suggest that early maturing lines may possess favorable seed attributes. Hierarchical clustering delineated five distinct phenotypic groups, offering a framework for genetic structure understanding and selection strategies.
