A Novel 3D Semi‐Automated Full Quantification Technique for Detection of Intraneural Phospho‐α‐Synuclein in Skin Biopsies
Authors: Sophia Lonczewski, Verian Bader, Louisa Ortmann, Louisa Basner, Markus Stücker, Ralf Gold, Raphael Scherbaum, Eun Hae Kwon, Konstanze F Winklhofer, Lars Tönges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundParkinson's disease (PD) and multiple system atrophy (MSA) are synucleinopathies marked by α‐synuclein aggregation, while progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) is a tauopathy. Clinical overlap between these diseases complicates diagnosis. Detection of intraneural S129 phospho‐α‐synuclein (pαSyn) via immunofluorescence staining (IF) in skin biopsies shows diagnostic promise. However, prior studies rarely addressed differentiation between synucleinopathies and tauopathies and lacked assessment of varying pαSyn burden—particularly relevant for this aim.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study, we analyzed skin biopsies from 29 PD, 5 MSA, and 4 PSP patients. Samples obtained at C7 and Th12 were double‐immunostained with pαSyn and PGP9.5, a pan‐axonal neurite marker.ResultsOur novel method digitizes biopsy sections semi‐automatically and performs computer‐assisted 3D signal reconstruction. The resulting full volumetric quantification of intraneural pαSyn load enables burden‐dependent test results based on ROC‐derived cut‐offs. Applied as a differential diagnostic test, it showed excellent discrimination of synucleinopathies from tauopathies, achieving AUCs of 0.912 (C7) and 0.934 (Th12), with 88.2% sensitivity and 100% specificity. Intraneural pαSyn load was significantly higher in PD and MSA compared to PSP (C7 p = 0.004; Th12 p = 0.002), with no difference between PD and MSA.ConclusionsThis novel technique refines IF by increasing objectivity and allowing gradual pαSyn‐burden assessment, offering potential as a confirmatory differential biomarker. Validation in larger, neuropathologically confirmed cohorts of these preliminary small‐group results is warranted to fully evaluate the diagnostic and prognostic potential.Keywords: multiple system atrophy, Parkinson's disease, progressive supranuclear palsy, skin biomarkers, synuclein

Introduction

Synucleinopathies, such as Parkinson's disease (PD) and multiple system atrophy (MSA), are neurodegenerative diseases characterized by the accumulation of α‐synuclein (αSyn). In PD, this protein forms Lewy bodies in neurons, while αSyn‐containing glial cytoplasmic inclusions (GCIs) are the pathological hallmark of MSA. Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) represents a tauopathy with aggregates composed of 4‐repeat tau isoforms in neurons, oligodendroglia, and astrocytes [1]. Differential diagnosis mainly relies on clinical findings, which is challenging in the early disease course, since symptoms show a wide spectrum with considerable clinical overlap [2, 3, 4]. As definitive diagnosis requires neuropathological confirmation, αSyn pathology in peripheral tissues and fluids, such as skin and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), shows promise for the development of diagnostic tools to improve therapeutic decisions and prognosis.

Seeding amplification assays (SAA) like Real‐Time Quaking‐Induced Conversion (RT‐QuIC) enable indirect quantification of pathological αSyn via its seeding effect [5], while immunofluorescence staining (IF) provides a more direct approach by visualizing αSyn phosphorylated at residue S129 (pαSyn) in situ within skin nerve fibers [6, 7]. Previous IF studies, including the large‐scale multicenter Synuclein‐One trial, confirmed high pαSyn detection rates in synucleinopathies. However, IF has so far rarely been applied for differentiation from tauopathies and remains limited to binary assessment, classifying samples as positive once a single pαSyn deposition is subjectively identified by the investigator (Figure S1A) [6]. A refined, fully quantitative microscopic analysis allowing for receiver operating characteristic (ROC)‐based test thresholding is needed to reflect varying pαSyn burden. This is particularly relevant for differential diagnostic purposes, as low‐level pαSyn signals in the skin—occasionally reported in tauopathies [8, 9]—could be correctly classified as negative when falling below the diagnostic cut‐off.

This study aimed to advance the IF approach by developing a semi‐automated method enabling three‐dimensional (3D) reconstruction and full volumetric quantification of intraneural pαSyn in entire biopsy sections. In a first application, ROC‐based analyses were used to define cut‐off values for a confirmatory differential diagnostic test distinguishing synucleinopathies (PD, MSA) from tauopathies (PSP) and to assess its diagnostic performance.

Methods

Between October 2018 and June 2021, 30 patients with PD fulfilling both the UK Brain Bank Criteria [10] and the Movement Disorders Society (MDS) Criteria [2], 6 patients diagnosed with at least possible MSA according to the second consensus criteria [3] and 4 patients fulfilling the MDS criteria for PSP [4] were recruited for the collection of skin biopsies at the Department of Neurology in St. Josef Hospital of Ruhr University Bochum.

The main exclusion criteria included drug or alcohol abuse, history of stereotactic surgery, electroconvulsive therapy, severe dementia, and severe psychiatric conditions.

One MSA patient was initially misdiagnosed with PD and was reclassified in the further course. Genetic testing had been performed in a subset of participants within an observational study or due to suspected monogenic forms based on the criteria of the guideline of the German Society for Neurology [11]. One MSA and one PD patient tested positive for LRRK2 and GBA1 mutations during the study and were excluded. Overall, 29 PD patients, 5 MSA patients, and 4 PSP patients were included in the analysis.

Study procedures followed the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of the Ruhr University Bochum (registration number 18‐6360, date of approval: 12.09.2018) and registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (ID: DRKS00020752). All subjects gave their written informed consent before enrollment. For clinical assessments, see Supporting Information.

Under local anesthesia, skin punch biopsies with a 3‐mm diameter were taken from the symptom‐predominant side of the neck at C7 and from the back at Th12 [12]. This region was selected based on consistent evidence suggesting a proximal‐to‐distal pαSyn deposition gradient in PD, whereas findings for atypical parkinsonian syndromes or for PD with orthostatic hypotension remain rare and more heterogeneous [8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Biopsies were washed with 1× phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, washed with 1× PBS, placed in 30% sucrose solution at 5°C, embedded in Neg‐50 (Epredia 6502, #11912365) after 1–14 days, and cryopreserved at −80°C [16, 17]. Using a freezing sliding microtome (HM550, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), each sample was cut into 20‐μm sections and mounted on a microscope slide [16, 17, 18]. The samples were stored at −80°C [18].

After a 30‐min block in 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1× PBS, antibodies against PGP9.5 (Zytomed Systems, #516‐3310, 1:400), a pan‐axonal marker, and against serine‐129‐phosphorylated α‐synuclein (pαSyn) (Biolegend, #825701, 1:8000) were incubated overnight in a humid chamber in 1% BSA and 0.3% Triton in 1× PBS at 5°C [17, 18]. After washing with 1× PBS, appropriate highly cross‐adsorbed fluorescent secondary antibodies (Alexa 555, Invitrogen, #A32727, 1:1000; Alexa 488, Invitrogen, #A32731, 1:1000) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in a humid chamber. After washing with 1× PBS, the sections were quenched with TrueBlack in 70% EtOH (Biotium, #23007, 1:20) for 40 s. Sections were washed and mounted with DAPI Fluoromount‐G (SouthernBiotech, #0100‐20).

All samples were blinded prior to image acquisition. For each biopsy site, the section with the largest surface area was selected for analysis and was acquired semi‐automatically in several tiles using a laser scanning microscope equipped with an automated stage (LSM880, ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany). The imaging scale was based on previous IF studies, focusing on prominent pαSyn accumulations. Digitization of the large sample volume was enabled by a 20× objective (Plan‐Apochromat NA 0.8, ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) and five axially acquired z‐planes with a 5.713‐μm interval and a 5.4‐μm optical section, resulting in > 94% thickness coverage. The PGP9.5 signal was excited at 488 nm and emission was recorded between 493 and 556 nm. The pαSyn signal was excited at 561 nm and emission was recorded between 566 and 620 nm. ZEN black 2.1 SP3 (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) was used to stitch the tiles.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM, Ehningen, Germany). Data were tested for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparison of two groups, the unpaired t‐test for parametric data and the Mann–Whitney test for non‐parametric and ordinal data were applied. For multiple group comparisons of parametric data, the one‐way ANOVA, and for non‐parametric and ordinal data, the Kruskal–Wallis test and the Dunn–Bonferroni test were used. The Fisher–Freeman–Halton test was applied to compare the variance of nominal data. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was supplemented by cut‐point analysis according to the Youden index and the closest‐to‐(0,1) criterion [19]. The Spearman's rank correlation test was used for correlation analysis. A two‐tailed p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For nonparametric analyses involving fewer than 30 subjects, exact significance was reported. If calculation of the Kruskal–Wallis test was numerically restricted, exact significance was assessed using the critical value tables of Meyer and Seaman [20].

3D Microscopic Technique for Full Quantification of Intraneural pαSyn Load

An overview of how our novel confirmatory differential test was developed based on full pαSyn quantification is shown in Figure S1B,C. Our method consists of two main steps, described in detail below: (i) 3D reconstructions of nerve fibers and intraneural pαSyn based on brightness thresholds, resulting in quantifiable intraneural pαSyn load (panel B), and (ii) determination of a suitable threshold for a positive synucleinopathy test result through ROC analysis (panel C).

The volumetric signal reconstruction in the first step was performed blinded using the surface function of Imaris 9 (Bitplane, Zurich, Switzerland). It included any nerve tissue in the sample—dermal nerve bundles, single nerves, and the innervation of adnexal structures like sweat glands, blood vessels, and erector pili muscles. Due to strong autofluorescence, epidermal cells and the sebum of sebaceous glands were manually excised in the software, while their innervation remained included in the analysis.

To quantify pαSyn load in skin nerve fibers, neurites and intraneural pαSyn were digitally 3D reconstructed across the entire biopsy section (Figure S1B). An animation of the rendering workflow is presented in Video S1. The PGP9.5‐positive neurite signal was reconstructed in 3D according to a section‐specific brightness threshold set in contrast to background staining. Within the PGP9.5 volume, the pαSyn signal was reconstructed in 3D accordingly, based on previous studies [12, 21] (Figures 2 and S2). This computer‐assisted step ensured consistent detection and quantification of all valid signals. Both resulting volumes were quantified in voxels using the software. Precipitates, identified by the second antibody control, were visualized by an equally ultra‐intense signal in both the PGP9.5 and pαSyn channels and by a homogenous signal with sharp borders in all planes and were excluded from the quantification (Figure S3). As a correlate of the intraneural pαSyn load, the ratio of total pαSyn volume to total PGP9.5 volume was calculated for each biopsy section. This allowed normalization of the pαSyn volume to the innervation density to account for peripheral neurodegeneration.

Quantitative analysis of phospho‐α‐synuclein (pαSyn) in a dermal nerve bundle. Using the Imaris 9 software, PGP9.5‐positive neurite signal and intraneural pαSyn signal were reconstructed in 3D according to a defined brightness threshold set in contrast to background staining. Both volumes were quantified, and the ratio of pαSyn volume to PGP9.5 volume was calculated as a correlate of the intraneural pαSyn load (Figure 4). Rendered neurite volumes are displayed transparently for optimal pαSyn visualization.

pαSyn Load Evaluation and Diagnostic Performance of the 3D Semi‐Automated Full Quantification Technique

Based on the significant difference in pαSyn load between synucleinopathies and tauopathies, a confirmatory differential diagnostic test was developed (Figure S1C). To determine the threshold for a positive test result, intraneural pαSyn load was subjected to ROC analysis (Figure 5). Optimization toward higher specificity—essential for confirmatory testing—was supported by the Youden index and the closest‐to‐(0,1) criterion. Both revealed identical cut‐points of ≥ 1.041E‐3 for C7 and ≥ 1.090E‐3 for Th12 in defining a positive test result for synucleinopathy (Figure 4). Overall, the Area Under the ROC curve (AUC) showed excellent discrimination between synucleinopathy and tauopathy, with values of 0.912 for C7 and 0.934 for Th12.

ROC curve of intraneural phospho‐α‐synuclein (pαSyn) load. The gray dotted line represents the line of chance with AUC = 0.5. With an AUC = 0.912 at C7 and an AUC = 0.934 at Th12, both sites show excellent discrimination between synucleinopathy and tauopathy. Threshold cut‐points for a positive test result were set as shown in Figure 4 by using the Youden index and the closest‐to‐(0,1) criterion.

Using these diagnostic thresholds, the differential test showed a sensitivity of 88.2% for detecting synucleinopathies and 100% specificity, as the pαSyn load of all tauopathy cases fell below the cut‐point (Table 2). Overall, a diagnostic accuracy of 89.5% was yielded. For both biopsy sites, 25/29 PD patients (86.2%), 5/5 MSA patients (100%), and none of the 4 PSP patients (0%) tested positive.

Correlation With Clinical Data

Across all cohorts, no significant correlation was found between pαSyn load in dermal neurites and clinical parameters (data not shown).

Discussion

In this study, we introduce a semi‐automated IF method enabling highly sensitive 3D reconstruction and fully quantitative analysis of total intraneural pαSyn in skin biopsy sections. Providing a gradual readout rather than binary classification, our method allows burden‐dependent test results based on ROC‐derived cut‐offs. Applied as a confirmatory differential diagnostic test, it showed excellent discrimination between synucleinopathies (PD, MSA) and tauopathies (PSP), achieving 88.2% sensitivity and 100% specificity.

Differential IF applications distinguishing synucleinopathies from tauopathies remain rare and are complicated by the occasional detection of cutaneous pαSyn in tauopathies [8, 9], likely reflecting central protein co‐pathologies [22]. As conventional binary IF approaches classify a sample as positive once a single pαSyn‐positive structure is identified, this easily leads either to false positives, reducing specificity, or, when applying higher thresholds (visually or in amount), to a loss of sensitivity (Figure S1A) [8, 9, 21]. Our fully quantitative approach addresses this limitation by enabling a gradual, pαSyn burden‐dependent readout based on ROC‐derived cut‐offs. Low pαSyn loads, such as in tauopathies, can thereby be classified test‐negative when below the diagnostic threshold (Figure S1C). This yielded maximal specificity (100%), critical for differential diagnostics, while still maintaining high sensitivity (88.2%)—overall representing good test metrics. For comparison, one study reported only 92.3% specificity due to pαSyn detection in two of 26 tauopathy patients, with 100% sensitivity [8]. Lacking neuropathological diagnosis confirmation, the authors, besides synuclein co‐pathology, discussed clinical misdiagnosis as possible explanations. Another group found pαSyn in ~11% of tauopathy cases and raised the positivity threshold to at least two positive biopsy sites, reaching 100% specificity but only 56% sensitivity [9], similar to the same specificity and 67% sensitivity in a third study [21]. Some IF studies have already introduced semi‐quantitative elements by relating the number of pαSyn‐positive deposits to the number of innervated structures [16, 23] or nerve fiber density [24], but still defined positivity once a single deposit was observed. Only two studies defined a minimal deposits count [13, 25]. In contrast, our exact volumetric measurements enable a more gradual threshold definition and refined pαSyn‐burden assessment.

Studies comparing IF and RT‐QuIC for distinguishing synucleinopathies from non‐synucleinopathies showed similar accuracy, with slightly better performance for IF [7, 26]. With AUCs of 0.912 for C7 and 0.934 for Th12, our data demonstrate discriminatory performance between synucleinopathies and tauopathies comparable to SAA. For example, Wang et al. reported similar AUCs using postmortem skin biopsies: 0.975 for RT‐QuIC and 0.913 for Protein Misfolding Cyclic Amplification (PMCA) [27]. Overall, we emphasize our test metrics, including their comparisons with conventional IF approaches and SAA, as preliminary due to the limited PSP group size.

Based on our promising test performance, we recommend that future studies include a subset of healthy controls to strengthen analytical validity—both by supporting validation of signal rendering and by establishing a technical baseline, which may enable further conclusions to be drawn from pαSyn load. For clinical validity, however, the study design must reflect the intended application setting. Our study addressed the main clinical challenge—the differential diagnosis of patients with phenotypical overlap—by developing an in vivo confirmatory test that could potentially replace current postmortem diagnosis confirmation. Unlike for screening, this context deals exclusively with symptomatic patients. Consequently, healthy controls must be excluded from the quantitative analysis, as otherwise they risk overestimating group differences, distorting cut‐points, and inflating specificity due to the greater difference in underlying pathological profiles.

This must be considered when comparing our results to previous IF studies reporting widespread sensitivities of 40.7%–94% with 100% specificity in distinguishing synucleinopathies from healthy controls or mixed non‐synucleinopathy groups [7, 13, 16]. Notably, several studies have detected pαSyn in a small proportion of healthy controls [6, 9, 25, 28], which might reflect incidental Lewy body or tau pathology in the brain of elderly individuals without neurological diseases [29]. For instance, in the large‐scale Synuclein‐One trial, 3.3% of healthy controls tested positive for peripheral pαSyn using binary IF assessment [6]. Therefore, the burden‐dependent readout of our method may also offer technical advantages for screening, which has so far been mainly relevant in a research context when healthy controls and prodromal‐stage patients are included in trials for disease‐modifying therapies [30].

To our knowledge, only two fully quantitative fluorescence microscopy methods for intraneural pαSyn detection have been reported. Melli et al. calculated the colocalized percentage of pαSyn‐positive areas relative to PGP‐positive areas in a region of interest (ROI) within a single sweat gland. However, the test result still relied on additional conventional binary assessment [9]. Similarly, Isonaka et al. generated a colocalization index based on pre‐defined pixel correlation strength [28]. In contrast to those planar analyses of single ROIs, our method digitally captures both area and depth across the entire skin section. This is particularly advantageous since larger sample volumes, for example through section thickness, reveal more pαSyn‐positive sites [27, 31].

Another major limitation of previous IF studies is their reliance on subjective binary assessment by blinded investigators to determine whether or not pαSyn signal is present (Figure S1A). While two studies demonstrated high inter‐laboratory reproducibility, discrepancies between raters in classifying true versus unspecific staining still occurred in individual cases [17, 32]. In comparison, our technique improves objectivity by applying binding intensity cut‐offs as standardized criteria for signal detection, implemented via computer‐assisted analysis (Figure S1B). Semi‐automated digitization of entire biopsy sections is also machine‐assisted and, given the large samples, reduces observer‐dependent error.

A further key challenge is the biomarker‐assisted differentiation within the disease spectrum of synucleinopathies such as PD and MSA. Consistent with the Synuclein‐One Study, our data show a trend toward higher mean pαSyn levels in MSA compared to PD [6]. However, as our results and other IF studies demonstrate, this comparison is not significant when based solely on total intraneural pαSyn levels [14, 18, 33]. Only two studies endorsed the analysis of multiple biopsy sites and the calculation of a pαSyn distribution coefficient to effectively distinguish MSA from PD [9, 13]. The RT‐QuIC approach has also been described as insufficient for reliably differentiating PD and MSA, as it only measures pathological αSyn indirectly by kinetically assessing its seeding effect [5, 34]. In contrast, more direct in situ pαSyn detection using IF has shown promise by incorporating pαSyn distribution patterns, such as nerve fiber quality or localization within different skin adnexa [15, 18]. A recent study identified pathologic signatures in cutaneous Schwann cells typical of MSA that were absent in PD and DLB, mirroring MSA brain pathology [35]. Moreover, in MSA, a predominantly somatic fiber involvement has been reported, whereas PD shows widespread autonomic fiber involvement [14, 15, 18, 33]. In this study, we included all innervated structures in total in the quantification. No adnexa‐stratified analyses were performed, as single biopsy sections do not provide sufficient representation of all adnexal structures, and evaluating several sections with multiple adnexa analyses per section would currently exceed technical feasibility. However, with advancing imaging and computational resources, our method could be expanded toward differentiated analysis. Although it has been suggested that applying PMCA using disease‐specific pαSyn strains in CSF may distinguish MSA from PD [36], the issue of cross‐reactivity among αSyn SAAs across different neurodegenerative disease proteins needs to be clarified [5]. Additionally, RT‐QuIC faces considerable technical variability, as indirect quantification is highly dependent on reaction conditions and different options for measurement [5].

In line with other fluorescence microscopy‐based studies, we found no significant correlation between pαSyn levels and clinical parameters [7, 35, 37]. A few studies reported correlations with disease severity and duration, particularly when including multiple biopsy sites, adnexa‐specific analyses, or combined Parkinsonian subgroups [6, 13, 25]. RT‐QuIC studies yielded varying results on cutaneous αSyn burden as a correlate of disease severity and duration, with some showing no correlation [7, 38] and others indicating a positive association [39]. One longitudinal study applied the proximity ligation assay (PLA) for αSyn detection [40]. After 2 years, PD patients showed progression of intra‐epidermal nerve degeneration, but no change in pathological αSyn levels. It remains unclear whether pαSyn presence represents a bystander or a pathogenic correlate of neurotoxicity, and which pathways of pαSyn spreading dominate in PD [41].

Peripheral pαSyn has been detected even in early or prodromal disease stages, which is particularly beneficial for diagnostic applications in the body‐first subtype of PD [42]. Compared to CSF sampling, skin biopsies are less invasive and hold great promise as diagnostic biomarkers, addressing the clinical need for supportive in vivo diagnostic tools.

Limitations

The analysis was restricted to a single biopsy section, which may not fully capture the variability of skin adnexa across sections. Future studies should analyze multiple sections to reduce histologic variability and enable analyses stratified by adnexal structures or nerve fiber quality. This could also address the present limitation that no distinction can be made between PD and MSA. Currently, our method poses a practical challenge, as each step from image acquisition to 3D reconstruction and quantification is laborious and time‐consuming, partly due to computational power. These constraints are expected to diminish with advancing technology. The small PSP comparison group, particularly given the general uncertainty of clinical diagnoses, limits the generalizability of our findings. Our approach requires further analytical validation of the method through comparison with healthy controls and validation in larger cohorts, including refinement of test cut points and confirmation of diagnostic accuracy metrics through neuropathological confirmation of diagnosis. Finally, our results suggest differential diagnostic utility, but due to the study's design, they do not confirm suitability for screening applications.

Conclusions

This study introduces a refined IF‐based method for highly sensitive detection of intraneural pαSyn. Its full volumetric quantification enables gradual assessment of pαSyn load through burden‐adjusted test results, addressing key limitations of conventional binary signal assessment. The semi‐automated, computer‐assisted workflow enhances objectivity. Preliminary data in a small tauopathy group demonstrated excellent discrimination of synucleinopathies, highlighting its potential as a confirmatory differential diagnostic biomarker. Further analysis of pαSyn distribution patterns may reveal differences within synucleinopathies. Larger longitudinal studies are needed to fully evaluate the diagnostic and prognostic potential of this novel technique.
