Higher signal/noise quotient value of subscapularis tendon on the throwing side in elite female water polo athletes: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hongxing Xun, Hong Shao, Xuehui Feng, Wenfeng Liu, Meng Liu, Binghong Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study innovatively employed magnetic resonance imaging in conjunction with isokinetic strength testing to examine the differences between no throwing shoulder and throwing shoulder injuries in elite female water polo players.MethodsThis study examined the radiographic findings as well as the internal and external rotation strengths of 16 elite female water polo players. A 3.0 T magnetic resonance imaging scanner was utilized to qualitatively and quantitatively assess the tendon structure of the shoulder. Concurrently, isokinetic strength testing of the shoulder Joint at 90° abduction was conducted in isokinetic concentric mode at speeds of 60°/s × 5 and 180°/s × 10. The peak torque/body weight (PT/BW) from multiple sets of movements was recorded.ResultsAll the athletes completed the tests, and magnetic resonance imaging did not reveal any full-thickness tears of the rotator cuff in either the throwing shoulder or the non-throwing shoulder. However, the supraspinatus tendon, subscapularis tendon, and long head of the biceps brachii presented varying degrees of signal abnormality. Further analysis revealed that the signal/noise quotient of the subscapularis tendon in the throwing shoulder was significantly greater than that in the no-throwing shoulder (p < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were detected between the two shoulders for the other tendons. Additionally, the PT/BW of the throwing shoulder for external rotation was less than that of the no-throwing shoulder at an angular velocity of 180°/s (p < 0.05).ConclusionsElite female water polo athletes experience the same injuries as no-throwing shoulders do in their throwing shoulders, but the subscapularis tendon on the throwing side has a higher signal/noise quotient value.Keywords: Shoulder injuries, Elite female water polo athletes, Infraspinatus tendon, Signal/noise quotient, Isokinetic strength

Introduction

Water polo, a traditional sport popular in European countries, has now become an Olympic sport with high demands on the shoulder joint [1–3]. It is a water-based contact sport that combines swimming, overhead throwing, and confrontation [4]. The sport requires both swimming at a steady, low-angle speed and throwing at speeds exceeding 19.7 m/s. Combining throwing and swimming activities may increase the risk of shoulder injury [5].

According to recent studies, the prevalence of shoulder pain in water polo players ranges between 24 and 51%, and the incidence of shoulder injuries in female water polo players is 2.38 times greater than that in male players [6–8]. Current studies have reported that shoulder injuries are mainly rotator cuff injuries, labrum injuries and subacromial impingement, which may be related to passing, throwing and underwater confrontation in the open-chain mode [9, 10]. In addition, we found that for athletes who only engage in overhead throwing (such as baseball, volleyball, and tennis), the injury rate of the throwing shoulder is significantly higher than that of the non-throwing shoulder [11, 12]. Hodgins et al. [13] found that the throwing shoulder often has a high injury rate in asymptomatic adolescent baseball players. However, for swimmers, Holt et al. [14] found that their subscapularis tendon and supraspinatus tendon are more susceptible to injury, but there is no significant difference between the two shoulders. Water polo players are both swimmers and throwers, and there is still limited research on whether there is a difference between throwing shoulder and non-throwing shoulder injuries. Additionally, due to the relatively short history of female water polo elite athletes and the small group size, there are still few targeted studies. Currently, knowledge about the characteristics of shoulder joint injuries remains limited, especially in elite female water polo players.

With the increasing popularity of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), injury characteristics are easier to specify [15]. An MRI study conducted by Klein et al. showed abnormalities in the subscapularis and infraspinatus tendons in semi-professional male water polo players that were not a common type of injury seen in single swimmers or throwers [1]. As a noninvasive assessment method, the signal intensity (SI) of the region of interest (ROI) is collected for quantitative analysis to observe tissue damage [16, 17]. After removing the influence of background signals, the tendon signal/noise quotient (SNQ) can reflect the tendon texture [18, 19]. The higher the SNQ value is, the worse the tendon texture. It is often used clinically to determine graft maturity and revascularization. Studies have shown that inflammation, edema and revascularization of tendons on MRI may increase the SI [18, 20]. Furthermore, the ratio of internal rotation (IR) to external rotation (ER) muscle strength in water polo players is an important predictor of shoulder injuries. Maintaining shoulder IR and ER muscle strength within a reasonable range is beneficial for reducing sports injuries [21, 22]. Aginsky et al. [23] found that female water polo players had increased shoulder IR muscle strength and decreased ER eccentric capacity. Such a difference in strength may lead to muscle imbalance and postural abnormalities, which in turn result in shoulder injuries. Therefore, incorporating isokinetic muscle strength assessment will further enrich our understanding of the characteristics of shoulder joint injuries in female water polo players.

A better understanding of the potential injury risks of water polo could help improve screening methods and injury prevention strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to combine MRI with routine examinations to identify the characteristics of shoulder joint injuries in elite female water polo athletes. We hypothesized that the injury severity was equal in both shoulders and that there was no significant difference in SNQ and peak torque/body weight between them. These findings may help differentiate the pathology of shoulder injuries in female elite water polo players and provide assistance for injury prevention.

Methods

This cross-sectional study assessed differences between no-throwing shoulders (NTS) and throwing shoulders (TS) in elite female water polo athletes. The study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of the Hunan Institute of Sports Science (approval number: TKSEC202501).

Participants

Athletes were included in the study if they (1) had undergone systematic training with a provincial water polo team for more than 5 years; (2) held a first-level athlete certificate or higher; or (3) no acute shoulder injury requiring medical intervention in the past 6 weeks [1]. Sixteen elite athletes (2 international masters, 9 masters, and 5 first-level athletes) from the Provincial Women’s Water Polo Team were recruited for this study. The exclusion criteria were playing the position of a goalkeeper and having previous shoulder surgery. Athletes with nonsurgical shoulder trauma (e.g., full-thickness rotator cuff tear) were also excluded. All the participants were informed about the content, methods, and potential risks associated with the study before they signed informed consent forms.

Magnetic resonance imaging

All the athletes received an MRI without arthrography of both shoulders. A 3.0 T MRI scanner (GE, USA) was used for the MRI scans in this study. Athletes were placed in a supine position, with their palms rotated posteriorly and sandbags placed on them. The supraspinatus, infraspinatus, subscapularis, and long head of the bicipital brachii tendons were selected for scanning. An experienced senior radiologist, blinded to the study, performed an MRI evaluation.

The signal intensities of the supraspinatus and long head of the bicipital brachii tendon were measured via the coronal proton density fat-saturated sequence, whereas those of the other regions were measured via the transverse proton density fat-saturated sequence. The specific four scanning sequence parameters are as follows: (1) OAx fs PD Shim transverse proton density fat-saturated sequence, FOV: 22 × 20 cm; matrix: 256 × 320; pixel: 0.8 × 0.6; slice thickness: 3 mm, interslice gap: 2 mm; TR: 2381; TE: 46.3, (2) OSag fs PD Shim sagittal proton density fat-saturated sequence, FOV: 18 × 18 cm; matrix: 288 × 224; pixel: 0.8 × 0.6; slice thickness: 4 mm, interslice gap: 0.5 mm; TR: 1859; TE: 35, (3) OCor fs PD Shim coronal proton density fat-saturated sequence, FOV: 17 × 17 cm; matrix: 288 × 224; pixel: 0.8 × 0.6; slice thickness: 3 mm, interslice gap: 1 mm; TR: 2390; TE: 42, (4) OCor T1 FSE coronal T1 sequence, FOV: 17 × 17 cm; matrix: 320 × 224; pixel: 0.8 × 0.6; slice thickness: 3 mm; interslice gap: 1 mm;

For SNQ measurements, the same radiologist was assigned to evaluate the images, with one responsible for recording. We located four regions of interest (ROIs) at the proximal, middle, distal, and 2 cm anterior to the shoulder joint and calculated the SI. The proximal ROI was close to the footprint; the middle and distal ROI were continuous with the proximal area. The SNQ was quantitatively calculated on the basis of the SI of the tendon: the mean SI of the tendon/SI of the background [20, 24]. Each measurement was conducted three times by a clinician who was blinded to the participants’ information, using the integrated software Horos v3.3.6. The point selection positions are shown in Fig. 1. A qualitative assessment of the tendon structure was performed on the basis of the research of Klein et al. [1], Tendon lesions were divided into normal, tendinopathic (signal abnormalities), partial tear, and full-thickness tear of the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, subscapularis, and long heads of the biceps brachii tendons, respectively. The senior radiologist made qualitative judgments on the above tendons.

The supraspinatus (A), infraspinatus (B), and subscapularis (C) tendons were selected at the proximal, middle, and distal portions, represented as S1, S2, and S3, respectively. A background signal 2 cm anterior to the shoulder joint was identified as S4. The SNQ of the tendon ends was calculated via the formula SNQ = [(S1 + S2 + S3)/3]/S4. For the long head of the biceps brachii tendon, three slices were selected and represented by D, E, and F, with measurements recorded as S1, S2, and S3, respectively, whereas the background signal 2 cm anterior to the shoulder joint was designated S4. The same SNQ formula was used for the calculations (n = 16)

Isokinetic strength assessment

The CSMI HUMAC NORM 2009 (USA) was used to assess the strength of the internal (IR) and external rotation (ER) muscles of the shoulder joint. Strength in the IR/ER position was performed in the supine position with the shoulder abducted to 90° and the elbow flexed to 90°. Before isokinetic testing, athletes should warm up thoroughly. The warm-up program includes seated rowing, scapula stability training and shoulder no-load rotation training, each movement lasts 3 min. During the test, the chest strap and pelvic strap are used to fix the trunk and pelvis to prevent compensation. The isokinetic concentric mode was used during the test, with speeds of 60°/s and 180°/s, and repetitions of 5 and 10, respectively. The maximum peak torque (PT) of multiple sets of movements was recorded. Experiments that are not completed fully, have incomplete range of motion, or have non-standard movements are considered invalid trials and will not be recorded in the data.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via SPSS 21.0 software. Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations, and categorical data are expressed as frequency counts (percentages). The chi-square test and Wilcoxon rank sum test were used to compare categorical variables. MRI and isokinetic data were compared by Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank sum test for nonparametric data. The reliability of SNQ and isokinetic strength was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

MRI evaluation

All the participants had intact tendon‒bone junctions, with no complete tendon tear. Through SNQ analysis of the TS and the NTS, it was found that the SNQ value of the subscapularis tendon in the TS was significantly greater than that in the NTS (p < 0.05), Cohen’s d value is 0.64, whereas there were no significant differences in the other tendons (Table 2). And the ICC value is 0.91. On the basis of the qualitative assessment of tendon structure, we detected more signal abnormalities in the supraspinatus tendon, subscapularis tendon, and long head of the biceps brachii, both in the TS and NTS. As shown in Fig. 2, injuries to the NTS were as follows: supraspinatus tendon (87.5% tendinopathies, 12.5% partially torn), infraspinatus tendon (81.25% normal, 18.75% tendinopathies), subscapularis tendon (62.5% normal, 37.5% tendinopathies), and long head of the biceps brachii tendon (31.25% normal, 68.75% tendinopathies). The TS injuries were as follows: supraspinatus tendon (81.25% tendinopathies, 18.75% partially torn), infraspinatus tendon (68.75% normal, 31.25% tendinopathies), subscapularis tendon (43.75% normal, 56.25% tendinopathies), and long head of the bicep brachii tendon (43.75% normal, 56.25% tendinopathies). However, there were no significant differences in the severity of injury to each tendon between the NTS and TS.

Discussion

This study innovatively utilized MRI, combined with isokinetic strength testing, to evaluate the differences in injury between the TS and NTS of female water polo players. Higher SNQ values ​​were detected in the subscapularis tendon of the TS than in the NTS, contrary to our hypothesis. Additionally, the PT/BW of the TS external rotation was less than that of the NTS at an angular velocity of 180°/s.

As an objective evaluation index, MRI serves as a useful tool to assess tendon structural integrity [25]. This study revealed that athletes with TS subscapularis tendons had greater SNQ, suggesting that an increased tissue water content leads to greater damage [19]. The qualitative study by Klein et al. [1] found more MRI abnormalities in the subscapularis and infraspinatus tendons in the throwing shoulder, which is similar to our research. This may be related to the amount and speed of throwing. The throwing speed and internal rotation strength of female water polo players are positively correlated. The subscapularis provides the main force support for internal rotation. High-intensity throwing may cause damage to the subscapularis tendon and result in shoulder pain. Wheeler et al. [26] also support this view, finding that increased throwing movements and reduced rest time in the throwing shoulder raise the risk of shoulder pain. This may also lead to abnormalities in the subscapularis tendon because it is heavily involved in throwing. Compared with NTS, MRI abnormalities of the subscapularis tendon in TS have been reported in healthy athletes. Most opinions [1, 27] suggest that this result should be interpreted carefully—it does not necessarily indicate a pathological condition but highlights the importance of prevention. For this reason, we recommend female water polo athletes monitor their training load, number of throws, and report shoulder pain.

In the qualitative assessments, we found all the athletes exhibited some degree of bilateral supraspinatus tendon injury, which was characterized mainly as tendinitis or tearing. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Klein et al. [1], who found no significant difference in the degree of tendon damage between the NTS and TS. Sein et al. [28], studied swimmers and reported a high incidence of bilateral supraspinatus tendinopathy. Water polo players demonstrate a special shoulder joint movement pattern. During high-intensity intermittent swimming, prolonged and overhead movements put the shoulder joint in a state of impact, fatigue, and imbalance. Like the TS, the NTS also needs to perform confrontation and paddling functions [22, 29, 30]. As a vital dynamic stabilizer of the glenohumeral joint, the supraspinatus tendon experiences increased load and is thus prone to injury [31, 32].

Previous studies using imaging assessments have focused mainly on male water polo players. Minelli et al. [30], performed an MRI analysis on 92 male water polo players with shoulder Joint symptoms and reported that 84.8% of the athletes had capsulolabral complex lesions, with isolated anterior labrum lesions having the highest incidence at 30.4%, whereas 7.6% had isolated supraspinatus tears. Galluccio et al. [10], studied 42 Italian professional male water polo players and used dynamic ultrasound to examine the shoulder tendons. The results revealed that acromion impingement and supraspinatus tendon tear of the TS were the most common injuries, both occurring at a rate of 21.43%. The most prevalent shoulder injuries among male water polo players are rotator cuff injury, acromion impingement, and shoulder dislocation, while there are still few reports on the characteristics of female shoulder injuries. Unlike the above studies, our research focused on the supraspinatus tendon, infraspinatus tendon, subscapularis tendon, and long head of the biceps brachii. According to the qualitative evaluation of the MR images, all the tendons exhibited varying degrees of damage. This may be because water polo players pass and throw in an unsupported and open-chain action mode, which leads to reduced humeral rotation, increased scapular rotation, and a high incidence of shoulder impingement [33]. Although there are numerous studies on shoulder injuries among water polo players, inconsistencies in research methods, participants, and examination techniques mean that there is currently no consensus in the academic community regarding the characteristics of these injuries.

This study revealed that at an angular velocity of 60°/s, there was no significant difference in the PT/BW of the two shoulders in internal and external rotation, suggesting that muscle strength for shoulder joint rotation is relatively balanced at low angular velocities. The characteristics of water polo differ from those of land sports. While athletes are in the water, they not only rely on their lower limbs to tread water but also engage their upper limbs of the NTS to paddle, enabling changes in direction, turning, and maintaining body balance. Linde et al. [34], reported that no significant difference exists in the concentric movement of the shoulder Joint among water polo players. At an angular velocity of 180°/s, the PT/BW of the TS in female water polo players was significantly lower than that of the NTS. This may be attributed to a partial loss of the external rotation muscle group during rapid throwing movements, which could lead to a decrease in strength. Water polo throwing is a whipping action transmitted by the whole-body power chain. Studies have reported that the average throwing speed of the world’s top male water polo outside players is 25.3 ± 1.4 m/s, with a maximum speed reaching 29.8 m/s; the throwing speed of excellent female water polo outside players is 15.95 ± 1.15 m/s, whereas for centers, it is 16.15 ± 1.13 m/s. This speed places a considerable load on the eccentric force of the shoulder joint over a specific period [35, 36]. The TS bears a large load during the eccentric phase, increasing the risk of injury. Strengthening the eccentric strength of the athlete’s shoulder external rotator muscles and enhancing the elasticity of the posterior capsule of the shoulder joint can help reduce the risk of tendon injury [37, 38]. In addition, we found that the external rotation muscle strength of water polo players was weakened during the isokinetic test with high speed and a large number of sets (180°/s × 10). This may be due to the poor fatigue resistance of their rotator muscles, which was confirmed by Savannah et al. by comparing them with athletes who had not performed shoulder overhead exercises [39].

There are several limitations to the current study. First, the sample size was small; a larger number of participants could facilitate subgroup analyses to compare the differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic shoulder injuries. Second, this study is a cross-sectional study, and it cannot be used to establish cause‒effect relationships between variables. Third, we employed only MRI and isokinetic muscle strength data for evaluation, whereas many lesions may require arthroscopy for a clear diagnosis. Fourth, the isokinetic test in the supine position cannot fully simulate the throwing motion, which may lead to an inaccurate assessment; future research can further optimize this approach. Additionally, no mechanism of injury analysis was conducted, indicating that further research is necessary.

Conclusions

Elite female water polo athletes experience the same injuries in their NTS as in their TS, but the subscapularis tendon on the throwing side has a higher SNQ value.
