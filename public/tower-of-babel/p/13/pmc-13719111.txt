ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Despite recent advances, the pathophysiology of functional neurological disorder (FND) remains incompletely understood. Structural neuroimaging studies have identified gray matter alterations in somatomotor-, salience-, limbic-, and default mode network–associated areas, although findings have been inconsistent. Mega-analyses, which combine individual-level data across studies, can help clarify structural alterations.METHODS:We conducted a mega-analysis of brain structural morphometrics derived from T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging scans from 15 international research groups. After across-site harmonization with ComBat, we compared 493 patients with functional motor disorder and functional seizures with 564 healthy control participants. Euler numbers were included to account for head motion.RESULTS:The FND cohort showed reduced cortical thickness in the bilateral superior frontal gyri (left d = 0.22, right d = 0.21) and sulci (d = 0.22 and 0.23), bilateral superior precentral sulcus (d = 0.22 and 0.26), right precentral gyrus (d = 0.25), right paracentral gyrus and sulcus (d = 0.23), right cuneus (d = 0.23), and right inferior opercular gyrus (d = 0.21); we also found reduced left postcentral gyrus surface area (d = 0.25) and right hippocampal volume (d = 0.22). No regions were different in relative surface area. There were no associations between morphometrics and illness duration or lifetime history of depression or anxiety. Differences between motor and seizure variants were not identified.CONCLUSIONS:This large mega-analysis suggests subtle morphometric differences, particularly in prefrontal and motor regions. This may represent predisposing vulnerabilities, compensatory mechanisms, or FND-specific alterations. Improved neuropsychiatric characterization of FND research cohorts will help further contextualize the biological relevance of structural alterations.

Functional neurological disorder (FND), previously known as conversion disorder, is a common, costly, and potentially disabling neuropsychiatric disorder (1–3). It is characterized by neurological symptoms involving disturbances in voluntary control of movement or alterations in awareness (4,5). The 2 most prevalent subtypes of FND include functional motor disorder (FND-motor) and functional seizures (FND-seiz) (4). Individuals often experience more than one symptom type simultaneously or over time (6).

FND is one of the most common reasons for individuals presenting to outpatient neurology and neuropsychiatry clinics (5,7), and it causes equitable disability to multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, and epilepsy (8,9). Despite available treatments, many patients experience long-term disability (10). There is a pressing need to better understand the neurobiology of FND to aid the development of novel mechanistically informed interventions (11).

Form follows function: the brain’s structure and function are intricately linked, and changes in activity and functional connectivity may be associated with morphological alterations (12,13). Morphometric alterations have been reported in areas such as the frontal and limbic cortices, but the findings from single-center studies with small to moderate sample sizes have been heterogeneous (maximum n = 48) (14). Systematic reviews of structural neuroimaging have highlighted alterations in the prefrontal cortex (including paralimbic structures), motor regions, and limbic areas (14–16). Some studies have identified differences in morphometrics related to symptom severity, illness duration, and predisposing vulnerabilities (17–20).

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies in FND-motor have shown reduced volumes in subcortical structures such as the bilateral basal ganglia and right thalamus (21–23) and larger volumes in subcortical regions such as the left amygdala, striatum, and cerebellum and bilateral thalamus (24). Cortical studies in FND-motor have shown smaller volumes in the left sensorimotor cortices (24) and right post-central gyrus (23) and greater cortical thickness in the bilateral premotor cortex (17).

Structural neuroimaging studies of FND-seiz have shown smaller volumes in the middle frontal gyrus, anterior cingulate gyrus, and bilateral cerebellum (18) and smaller thickness in the frontal gyri, superior temporal, and limbic regions (18,25–28). There is also evidence of smaller subcortical volumes in the left amygdala and hippocampus (29) and reduced surface area in frontal and insular regions in FND-seiz (30).

Overall, the literature is heterogeneous, and some studies have shown limited or no differences (19,31–35). One possible reason is the high prevalence of mental health disorders in patients with FNDs (36), which only a subset of studies have accounted for (28). Other reasons may be the relatively small sample sizes and the potentially subtle differences expected to exist between patients with FND and control participants. Given this, big data approaches are necessary.

Large-scale analyses of shared data—for example via mega-analyses—help overcome heterogeneity observed in individual studies (37–39). Mega-analytic approaches to neuroimaging datasets have distinct advantages, including a consistent methodology, improved ability to address confounding variables, a uniformly applied analysis model, and the ability to include unpublished structural data (37). A further advantage is the improved power.

Cortical thickness and surface area are considered orthogonal components that are affected by distinct underlying processes; thus, they can be considered separate components. The primary correlate of cortical volume is cortical surface area, but volume loss is best captured by cortical thickness (40). Thus, separate analysis of these 2 components offers a better understanding of underlying cortical changes than the analysis of volume measures alone (28).

A comprehensive analysis of all available FND structural neuroimaging data is a critical next step in developing a more complete understanding of the underlying structural neurobiology (11). In this mega-analysis, we aimed to obtain data from research groups that had published any study involving a structural MRI sequence in patients with FND-motor and FND-seiz (including mixed cohorts) and to aggregate raw individual participant data into a single analysis.

We hypothesized that previously identified cortical and subcortical regions, such as the frontal, motor, (para)limbic, and basal ganglia, would be significantly different in patients with FND versus healthy control (HC) participants. We aimed to probe these general hypotheses using a whole-brain approach, extracting morphometric values from datasets, harmonizing the data to reduce intersite variation, and comparing regions via multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA). Secondary analyses were also performed to contextualize the potential impact of FND-motor versus FND-seiz, lifetime history of depression or anxiety, and biological sex differences.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

The study obtained King’s College London ethical approval from the Research Ethics Office, Psychiatry, Nursing and Midwifery Research Ethics Panel (LRS-20/21–22422) on April 15, 2021. Included studies were identified via an initial systematic literature search (see the Supplemental Methods).

Data Requests

We contacted research groups for T1-weighted MRI scans as well as demographic and clinical information. In total, 26 authors were contacted, and 20 responded. Of these 20, 4 could not access data, 1 could not arrange a data sharing agreement, and 1 declined to participate. Further studies that met the inclusion criteria (n = 3) were identified via word of mouth (one additional site). We asked groups to send as much cohort characterization information as was available.

Participants

Patients were included in the main FND group if they had any FND-seiz or FND-motor, as defined by the information given by the contributing groups (see the Supplemental Methods for more information).

MRI Data Preprocessing and Harmonization

The analysis pipeline for preprocessing and data harmonization was based on that used in Vignando et al. (37). MRI data were preprocessed with FreeSurfer version 6.0.0 (41). Individual cortical thickness, cortical surface area, and subcortical volume measures were extracted based on the Destrieux atlas (number of regions entered in the principal component analysis [PCA]: n = 148 for thickness, n = 148 for surface area, n = 20 for subcortical volumes) (42). For a complete description of the analysis pipeline, see the Supplemental Methods.

ComBat Harmonization

To minimize variance due to different scanners and maintain variance related to biological features, we used the ComBat harmonization method (an empirical Bayesian algorithm) on the parcellation results (43,44). This method has been previously used successfully in studies of cortical thickness, cortical surface area, and subcortical volumes (37,43) (see Supplemental Note S1 for more details).

Motion Correction

Subtle in-scanner motion may produce artifactual alterations in cortical thickness (45); this may be particularly noteworthy in FND-motor (11). Euler numbers have been shown to be a reliable numerical estimate of image quality and motion (46). Euler numbers were calculated using the function in Free-Surfer, and a mean value was produced ([left + right]/2). We were unable to obtain a Euler number for 1 participant, who was not included in relevant analyses.

Case-Control Matching

Prior to between-group analyses, case-control matching was performed via fuzzy matching in SPSS 24 (IBM Corp., 2016) with tolerance factors for age and sex set to 1 year and 0, respectively. Case-control matching was repeated separately for each of the subanalyses (FND vs. HC, FND-motor vs. HC, FND-seiz vs. HC, FND-motor vs. FND-seiz, FND sex-matched sample, lifetime depression+ vs. depression-, and lifetime anxiety+ vs. anxiety-).

Feature Selection

To reduce dimensionality, we conducted a feature-selection PCA. This ensured computational tractability and interpretability for the MANCOVA via an unbiased, data-driven structure approach. We did not undertake feature selection for subcortical volumes due to a smaller number of regions (n = 20). The PCA feature selection was performed independently for each of the subanalyses (FND vs. HC, FND-motor vs. HC, FND-seiz vs. HC, FND-motor vs. FND-seiz, FND sex-matched sample, lifetime depression+ vs. depression-, and lifetime anxiety+ vs. anxiety-). For a complete description, see the Supplemental Methods.

Group Difference Analyses

We used MANCOVAs to compare the FND group with the HC group, while controlling for age, sex, estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) (subcortical), and Euler number (cortical thickness and surface area). Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate correction (see Supplemental Tables S2–S7 for all regions entered into the significant MANCOVAs). The primary analyses were patients with FND versus HC participants; there were several secondary analyses as outlined below.

The models were calculated using SPSS and R version 4.0.0 (R Core Team, 2017). Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were derived from ηp2 values. Figures were created by overlaying regional labels on a brain render with color coding based on effect size.

Clinical Characteristics

We aimed to include as confounding variables any clinical information that was available for >60% of participants. Depression and anxiety caseness were established if the groups had indicated the presence of either current or lifetime depression or anxiety or via information derived from clinical scales (see the Supplemental Methods).

Secondary Analyses

The analyses outlined above were repeated for each of the separate subanalyses (FND vs. HC, FND-motor vs. HC, FND-seiz vs. HC, FND-motor vs. FND-seiz, FND sex-matched sample, lifetime depression+ vs. depression-, and lifetime anxiety+ vs. anxiety-).

Illness Duration

We ran a partial correlation analysis between illness duration in the significant regions in the FND group, while controlling for age and sex (and eTIV in the case of subcortical volumes).

Leave-One-Site-Out Analysis

The MANCOVA for the overall FND cohort was repeated, with manual removal of 1 of the 15 study cohorts at a time to check by visual inspection whether a single site was driving the results. Because there was no indication of a single site effect, we did not calculate bias or variance estimators.

Exploratory PCA

To interpret the results from the MANCOVAs further, we used an exploratory post hoc PCA to identify latent dimensions and determine how well variables fit into these dimensions. We aimed to explore how the regions that differ interact and contribute to variance in FND (37). For a complete description, see the Supplemental Methods.

RESULTS

Fifteen groups contributed, offering published or unpublished data as NIfTI files or preprocessed datasets. We began processing with 553 FND and 594 HC MRI scans; 23 FND and 30 HC scans were discarded due to insufficient quality. Further clinical scans were removed due to missing clinical data (n = 24) or not meeting diagnostic criteria (n = 13). The final sample comprised 493 patients (372 female [75.5%]; mean [SD] age = 38.2 [12.7] years) and 564 HC participants (402 female [71.3%]; mean [SD] age = 38.1[12.7] years); (see Table 1). In total, there were 314 patients in the FND-motor group and 193 patients in the FND-seiz group (n = 14 in both groups).

Gross Metrics

Mean eTIV was significantly different between groups (1,442,791 mm3 in patients with FND vs. 1,492,294 mm3 in HCs; t1055 = 5.325, p < .001). This difference remained significant (p < .001) when age and sex were included as covariates.

There was a significant difference between total surface area of patients with FND and HC participants (164,703 mm2 in patients with FND vs. 169,943 mm2 in HC participants; t1055 = 5.431, p < .001). This difference remained significant (p < .001) when age and sex were included as covariates.

There was a significant difference between mean Euler number in patients with FND and HC participants (-62.5 [55.1] vs. -54.4 [52.0], t923 = 2.31, p = .21). There was no significant difference between mean Euler values in patients with FND-motor and HC participants (-54.8 [53.4] vs. -54.4 [52.0], t761 = 0.109, p = .913). There was a difference between mean Euler values in patients with FND-seiz and HC participants (-74.8 [54.5] vs. -54.4 [52.0], t636 = 4.37, p <.001).

Illness Duration.

Illness duration data were available for 384 patients (77.9% sample), and mean illness duration in the overall group was 5.2 years (SD = 5.9; minimum = 0, maximum = 35 years; median = 3 years). There were no significant correlations between illness duration and any of the regions on any of the morphometrics (p > .10).

Depression and Anxiety.

Lifetime depression data were available for 306 patients (62.1% of the sample). Lifetime anxiety data were also available for 306 patients (62.1% of the sample). Insufficient clinical data were available for all other clinical variables and for HC participants.

Depression.

After propensity score matching, 118 (88.06%) of the patients with a lifetime history of depression were matched to participants with FND without a lifetime history of depression (see Table S8).

There was no overall effect of depression on cortical thickness (p = .099), surface area (relative) (p = .436), or subcortical volumes (p = .255).

Anxiety.

After propensity score matching, 105 (80.15%) of the patients with anxiety were matched to participants with FND without a lifetime history of anxiety (see Table S9).

Three was no overall effect of anxiety on cortical thickness (p = .560), cortical surface area (relative) (p = .128), or subcortical volumes (p = .136).

DISCUSSION

In this mega-analysis of structural MRI in FND, we identified subtle but consistent differences in brain morphology compared with HC participants, particularly lower cortical thickness. These findings are consistent with current conceptualizations of FND as predominantly a disorder of functional network alterations rather than consistent macroscopic structural changes (14,47). Nevertheless, effect sizes were of a small magnitude. At around 0.05 mm, the observed differences were also small; average cortical thickness is estimated to be around 2.5 mm, so regional differences represented up to approximately 2% of average thickness (48). Such changes are within the range of test-retest variability for most scanners.

The presence of subtle morphological changes across a large multisite dataset strengthens the evidence for structural correlates of FND and provides important guidance for future work. The study suggests that the amalgamation of multiple datasets in disorders such as FND is achievable, encouraging the future use of large data approaches that may help to surmount some of the limitations of smaller-scale studies and provide granular linkage between pathophysiology and illness presentations. Furthermore, the inclusion of Euler numbers suggests that differences in cortical thickness and relative surface area between FND and HC participants are not fully accounted for by head motion.

There were lower cortical thicknesses in regions responsible for motor control, including the precentral and paracentral areas, as well as planning and initiation of movement, such as the superior frontal gyri. Most of the regions that showed significant differences at the FND cohort group level also showed them at the FND-motor group level, suggesting that the lower values may be particularly associated with changes in motor abilities. These findings are consistent with a dataset outside of this analysis in which reduced cortical thickness was found in right paracentral and precentral regions (18).

In this study, there were lower thicknesses in regions responsible for motor planning and initiation. These alterations may reflect preexisting predisposing vulnerabilities, such as impaired prefrontal regulation of motor or emotional processing, consistent with theoretical models of corticolimbic dysfunction (49). Alternatively, they could be consequences of chronic symptoms, stress, or aberrant neuroplastic changes. The lack of correlation with illness duration leans toward a mechanistically important role (or trait marker), but longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these possibilities, especially in view of functional MRI studies that have shown duration-activation relationships (50,51).

Many regions previously identified as significant in smaller, single-site studies were not replicated in this large-scale analysis. This may reflect the increased statistical rigor and heterogeneity introduced through data harmonization, but it also underscores the importance of mega-analytic approaches in identifying findings with the highest generalizability across patient populations.

We did not observe alterations in regions commonly implicated in emotional processing in FND, such as the amygdala. One possible explanation is that variability in amygdala volume across individuals might have resulted in net null effects at the group level. It would be informative to assess structural changes in more granular detail, for example via interrogation of amygdalar nuclei or hippocampal subfields, to investigate whether FND is associated with increased heterogeneity in limbic regions (52). We also did not find any changes in the temporoparietal junction, a region implicated in self-agency and action-ownership, which has been suggested to be involved in the pathogenesis of FND (53).

Subgroup Interpretations

There was an absence of significant structural differences between the FND-motor and FND-seiz subgroups, although the subtype comparisons with HC participants showed different patterns of change. Several factors may explain this result. First, the shared pathophysiology may manifest in subtle, overlapping alterations (e.g., in frontal or limbic regions) that are not sufficiently distinct to yield significant subgroup differences in morphometric measures. Second, our sample, while large, may lack sufficient power to detect small subtype-specific effects, particularly given the heterogeneity of FND presentations.

The current data did not allow us to fully explore other potential sources of variance, including the influence of comorbid depression, anxiety, or sex-based effects. These are factors that have shown variable associations with brain structure in previous FND studies (35,54,55). These limitations underscore the need for even larger, stratified datasets and complementary imaging modalities to parse out subgroup-specific differences.

Patients in the FND-seiz group showed increased right orbitofrontal thickness. While long-term treatment with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (56), lithium (57), and some antipsychotics (58) has been associated with prefrontal cortical thickening, we lacked sufficient medication history to assess this, and therefore such effects remain speculative in our sample.

The study did not reveal any differences in cortical thickness between patients with and without a lifetime depression history. Such changes might have been expected according to previous research (39). The fact that no differences were found in this large sample suggests that the methodology of assigning lifetime depression status might not have adequately separated the groups. We suggest that future imaging studies on FND attempt to record current and past depression status with increased granularity.

Network-Level Interpretation

A potentially unifying theme among the implicated regions is their integration within distributed cortical networks involved in motor control, executive function, and interoceptive awareness. Many of the affected regions, such as the superior frontal gyrus, precentral gyrus, and inferior frontal regions, are components characterized by multifaceted intracortical connectivity and serve integrative roles across sensorimotor and cognitive domains (59–61). The structural convergence on such regions may point to shared microarchitectural vulnerabilities in FND, although this hypothesis warrants further study using histologically informed imaging metrics.

PCA identified 2 distinct dimensions of cortical thickness variation in the FND cohort, offering insight into potential network-level abnormalities. The primary component included the bilateral superior frontal gyri, superior precentral sulci, and inferior opercular gyrus, which are regions implicated in motor planning, cognitive control, and salience processing, suggesting an involvement of frontoparietal and salience networks (62,63).

Together, these components highlight a possible disruption in higher-order cognitive control, supporting the notion that multinetwork dysfunction underlies functional symptoms (11,62,64). The co-alteration suggests that aberrations in structure may influence or reflect maladaptive network dynamics; for example, the observed patterns could contribute to impaired top-down modulation of motor output or interoceptive prediction errors, which are core components of current theoretical accounts of FND via predictive coding frameworks (64).

Another notable absence was the lack of robust PC-based evidence for a single unifying structural network underlying FND. While some clustering of affected regions was observed, the diversity of implicated areas suggests that FND does not have a singular morphological signature. Instead, the disorder may involve multiple routes to network-level dysfunction (65), supporting a model of neurobiological degeneracy (many-to-one mapping), wherein diverse structural changes can give rise to similar phenotypic outcomes (66).

Future Directions

Additional studies on structural data could include emerging methods such as source-based morphometry and mode-based morphometry (67,68). Mega-analytic approaches to other imaging modalities such as diffusion-weighted imaging or functional MRI may shed further light. Further granular analysis of structural imaging, particularly methods that incorporate deep phenotypic characterization, neurotransmitter analyses [e.g., neurotransmitter correlation analysis (69)], or genetic correlations (70,71), could help elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms. High-field-strength research is also needed to investigate potential mechanistically relevant roles for brainstem structures (72). Future studies could also explore how gray and white matter changes interact to support symptom generation and recovery (54).

The effect size of other (non-FND) factors on the cortex may be greater than that of FND and therefore obscure observable morphometric differences, even in a large study such as this. Nevertheless, FND-related microanatomical changes relevant to mechanism may still be present. This underscores the need for a better understanding of microanatomical changes in FND.

Limitations

Despite the successful application of harmonization procedures to the data, this is unlikely to surmount all variability associated with site effects (73). We were also unable to characterize childhood maltreatment and adverse life experiences in our dataset despite them likely contributing (74). We were also unable to consider medication effects. Some of the included groups had small datasets. Although we used conservative post hoc corrections, this study utilized multiple comparisons through several different analyses.

Conclusions

In this mega-analysis, we found subtly lower cortical thickness in motor and cognitive control regions in patients with FND compared with HC participants. These results are consistent with models of FND as a multinetwork disorder. Nevertheless, there are identifiable, although small, structural correlates that warrant further investigation.

This study provides a proof of concept for future big data approaches and offers several hypotheses for future work on the structural underpinnings of FND and granular analysis of genetic or neurotransmitter expression. Analysis of functional imaging data would be highly valuable in future investigations because alterations in functional networks may emerge even when structural changes are confined to a single region.
