Lower Allele Frequency of TERT-rs2242652 in Sub-Saharan African Populations Compared With American Populations and Hepatocellular Carcinoma Risk

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. The higher incidence and earlier onset of HCC in sub-Saharan Africa suggest a potential role for a genetic predisposition. This study evaluated the association of TERT-rs2242652-(A), a variant linked to lower HCC risk, with HCC in populations from Ghana, Nigeria, and Cameroon, compared with a population from the United States.METHODSThe study included 537 patients with HCC: United States (n = 348), Ghana (n = 79), Nigeria (n = 43), and Cameroon (n = 67). The control group had 2,872 cancer-free individuals: United States (n = 2,399), Ghana (n = 323), Nigeria (n = 85), and Cameroon (n = 65). Whole-exome sequencing was conducted using germline DNA, and data for TERT-rs2242652 were analyzed. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs were calculated using unconditional logistic regression. Chi-square tests assessed protective allele frequencies.RESULTSIn the US cohort, TERT-rs2242652 was significantly associated with lower HCC risk (OR, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.58 to 0.96]; P = .02), with an allele frequency of 18.8% (15.4% in HCC cases v 19.3% in controls). In the combined sub-Saharan African population, no significant association was observed, but there was a trend toward decreased HCC risk (OR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.53 to 1.22]; P = .29), with an allele frequency of 12.2% (10.6% in HCC cases v 12.9% in controls). Separate analyses of Ghanaian, Nigerian, and Cameroonian populations showed similar nonsignificant trends. The protective allele frequency in the combined African populations was significantly lower than in the US cohort (P < .0001).CONCLUSIONIn sub-Saharan African populations, there was a lower frequency of the HCC protective allele TERT-rs2242652 compared with European Americans. These findings underscore the importance of multiethnic genetic studies in understanding population differences in HCC risk and developing prevention strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer and ranks third among cancer-related deaths globally, accounting for 75%-85% of all primary liver malignancies.1-4 HCC typically develops in individuals with chronic liver disease and cirrhosis.5 There are significant geographic differences in HCC incidence, with sub-Saharan African countries exhibiting higher rates compared with other regions.2

Although environmental factors account for the majority of HCC cases, hereditary risk factors contribute significantly to interindividual differences in HCC risk.6-8 Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and several candidate gene studies conducted in North American, Han Chinese, and European populations have identified a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in the telomerase reverse transcriptase gene (TERT, rs2242652 G>A) as a protective factor against HCC.9-11 However, no study has yet examined whether the variant is also associated with reduced risk of HCC in African populations or whether the protective allele is found with a similar allele frequency in Africans as in other populations.

Our study addresses this knowledge gap by examining the association between the TERT-rs2242652-(A) variant and HCC risk in sub-Saharan African populations compared with a US population. Using a case-control design, we analyzed the polymorphism in individuals from Ghana, Nigeria, and Cameroon and compared the findings with the population from the United States.

METHODS

Participant Enrollment

We performed a multicenter international case-control study. The study included a total of 537 patients diagnosed with HCC according to the American Association for the Study of Liver Disease guidelines5,12 and 2,872 control patients recruited from tertiary centers from four countries. Specifically, 348 HCC cases and 2,399 age-, sex-, and self-reported ancestry-matched controls without HCC from the US cohort were identified from the Tapestry Study,13 Mayo Clinic Biobank,14 and the Biobank for GI Health Research, comprising patients recruited from the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, Jacksonville, Florida, and Phoenix, Arizona between 2009 and 2024. In Ghana, 79 HCC cases and 323 controls without HCC were recruited from three tertiary hospitals (Cape Coast University Teaching Hospital, Korle Bu Teaching Hospital, and Komfo-Anokye Teaching Hospital) between 2017 and 2018. In Nigeria, 43 cases and 85 controls were recruited from two tertiary centers (Jos University Teaching Hospital and the University of Lagos College of Medicine) between 2018 and 2021. The Nigerian data were collected for a previous study, and all control patients did not have HCC but were HIV-positive based on the design of that study.15 Finally, 67 cases and 65 controls from Cameroon were recruited between 2017 and 2021 at the Mbingo Baptist Hospital.

Germline Genetic Sequencing

We analyzed the existing sequencing data from Tapestry and Mayo Clinic Biobanks as described previously.13,14,16,17 Whole-exome sequencing (Exome+, Helix, Inc, San Mateo, CA) was conducted on germline DNA from both cases and controls.16 Quality control checks were conducted following protocols described previously.16,18 Variant calling for TERT-rs2242652 was performed by aligning sequencing data to the GCRh38 version of the human reference genome. Ancestry estimation was carried out using SMARTPCA from EIGENSOFT v7.2.1 and the 1000 Genomes reference panel. Individuals were classified into five major ancestry categories: Africans, Europeans, Admixed Americans, South Asians, and East Asians. The first two principal components (PCs) were extracted from this data set (Appendix Fig A1) and used as covariates in our analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were examined using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, whereas continuous variables were examined using medians and ranges. Unconditional univariable logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratio (ORs) and 95% CIs to assess the association between TERT-rs2242652 and HCC risk by country of origin. The protective allele frequencies (PAF) were assessed with chi-square tests. Multiplicative interaction terms were used to determine whether there was any difference between the protective A allele of TERT-rs2242652 with HCC risk across sites. All tests were two-sided, and P values of <.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 and GraphPad software version 10.1.2.

Ethics

All participating sites obtained approval from their local Institutional Review Boards (IRBs), and all patients provided informed consent. Additional ethical approval was obtained from the Mayo Clinic IRB for this study (IRB#: 22-004700).

Ancestry Analysis Shows Separation Between Groups

Appendix Figure A1 shows a principal component analysis (PCA) of genetic variation among individuals from Ghana, Nigeria, and the United States, along with reference populations from the 1000 Genomes Project. The Ghanaian and Nigerian populations clustered closely with the African populations from the 1000 Genomes Project. In contrast, most US participants clustered with European populations, with smaller subsets aligning with admixed American and East Asian ancestry. The genetic ancestry closely reflected the participant's self-reported race/ethnicity (Table 1).

Lower Prevalence of TERT-rs2242652 in Sub-Saharan African Populations

Figure 1 shows the prevalence of the protective allele (A) in the US and sub-Saharan African populations. There was a lower PAF for the TERT-rs2242652 (A) variant in the whole African population including cases and controls (PAF = 12.2%) compared with the whole US population (PAF = 18.9%). This difference was statistically significant (P < .0001). Additionally, when examining only control groups, the A allele was significantly more common among US controls (19.4%) than among African controls (12.9%, P value for between-group difference = .0008). Among individuals with HCC, the protective allele was observed at a lower frequency in sub-Saharan Africans (10.6%) compared with Americans (15.5%), but the difference was not statistically significant (P > .05).

Lower Risk of HCC With TERT-rs2242652

In the US cohort, TERT-rs2242652 G > A was associated with a significantly lower risk of HCC (OR, 0.75 for AG/AA v GG; 95% CI, 0.58 to 0.96; P = .02), with an overall PAF of 18.9% (15.5% in HCC cases v 19.4% in controls; Fig 2, Appendix Fig A2).

ORs and 95% CIs TERT rs2242652 (G>A) for each population. OR, odds ratio.

In the combined sub-Saharan African population, a similar trend toward a protective effect of the A allele was observed; however, this result did not achieve statistical significance (OR, 0.80 for AG/AA v GG; 95% CI, 0.53 to 1.22; P = .29), with an overall PAF of 12.2% (10.6% in HCC cases v 12.9% in controls).

Analysis by African country revealed similar trends consistent with the protective effect of the A allele; however, none reached statistical significance. The ORs for HCC were 0.71 in the Ghanaian (95% CI, 0.39 to 1.35; P = .29), 0.75 in the Cameroonian (95% CI, 0.33 to 1.61; P = .48), and 0.92 in the Nigerian (95% CI, 0.39 to 2.10; P = .86) populations (Fig 2, Appendix Fig A2).

DISCUSSION

HCC disproportionally affects populations in Africa, leading to earlier onset and higher mortality rates compared with populations in other parts of the world.12,19,20 Environmental and viral factors like Aflatoxin exposure and HBV and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection are well-established contributors, but less is known about the role of germline variants on HCC risk in African populations. Our multicenter case-control study found that the TERT-rs2242652 A allele tends to be protective against HCC risk, with similar effect magnitude in sub-Saharan African (OR = 0.80) and US (OR = 0.75) populations. However, the overall allele frequency of this protective allele was significantly lower in the sub-Saharan African populations.

African populations harbor the greatest genetic diversity globally but represent only 1.1% of participants in GWAS.21 This underrepresentation in research limits our understanding of disease mechanisms and genetic risk in African populations. Findings from this study contribute to advancing understanding of the influence of germline variation on HCC risk across genetically diverse groups and helps to address underrepresentation of African populations in liver cancer research.

The TERT gene encodes the catalytic subunit of telomerase, an enzyme essential for maintaining telomere length and genomic stability. The A allele of rs2242652 has previously been associated with increased telomere length and a protective effect against HCC in European and Han Chinese populations. The A allele was significantly associated with reduced risk of HCC in our US cohort (OR = 0.75, P = .021). Similarly, among African participants, the A allele showed a consistent trend toward reduced HCC risk in the combined African analysis (OR = 0.80, P = .29) and across individual countries (Ghana OR = 0.71, Cameroon OR = 0.75, Nigeria OR = 0.92), although none of these findings reached statistical significance. Smaller sample sizes in the African cohorts (n = 662 combined) compared with the US cohorts (n = 2,747 combined) may have led to insufficient statistical power.

In sub-Saharan Africa, the patients diagnosed with HCC were substantially younger (median age 44-51 years) than in the United States (median age 66 years). Also, the prevalence of hepatitis B infection was markedly higher in the African cohorts. In contrast, US patients more commonly had metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol-related liver disease, and hepatitis C infection. Exploratory analyses restricted to participants with HBV or HCV infection revealed a consistent trend: the prevalence of the TERT-rs2242652 A allele was lower among HCC cases than among controls (not shown). However, the sample sizes in these subgroups were insufficient to make definitive conclusions regarding a potential interaction between this SNP and viral hepatitis in modulating HCC risk. All of the main environmental risk factors for HCC (hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and MASLD) have been reported to be associated with telomere shortening.22-26 However, it is not established whether these etiologies contribute to telomere shortening at different rates or whether there is any interaction between the SNP in TERT and HCC etiology.

The US cohort in this study was of primarily European descent, with a limited representation of African American patients. The available data in the Genome Aggregation Database27 indicate that the PAF is 13.2% among more than 20,000 Africans/African Americans, which is slightly higher than in the sub-Saharan populations in our study. Genomic analyses show that African Americans typically have a mean African ancestry of 70%-80% and a European ancestry of 15%-20%, with most African ancestry tracing to West African (Niger-Kordofanian) origins.28-30 This admixture likely accounts for the intermediate prevalence of the TERT allele among African Americans. Future research is warranted to determine whether this allele is associated with reduced HCC risk in African American populations.

This study has several limitations. First, although the TERT-rs2242652 SNP provides some protection against HCC, it likely explains only a small portion of overall HCC risk.9-11 Environmental factors remain the primary contributors to HCC development. Other genetic factors such as a SNP in PNPLA3 may also play a significant role.16 Future research involving larger populations is needed to evaluate polygenic risk and to stratify HCC risk by etiology, which may help clarify the reasons for early-onset HCC in sub-Saharan populations. Second, all Nigerian controls and cases were drawn from a previous study in which all control participants were HIV-positive. Although HIV infection may influence HCC risk, existing evidence on this association is conflicting.19 Another potential confounder was the difference in diabetes prevalence between populations, which is a risk factor for HCC development. Finally, this study lacked clinical information about environmental and risk factors such as aflatoxin B1 exposure, use of direct-acting antivirals for hepatitis B and C, tumor staging, histopathology, and participant muscle mass index. This limits the ability to adjust for known environmental and clinical modifiers of HCC risk.

In conclusion, to our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate TERT-rs2242652 G>A in sub-Saharan African populations, revealing lower protective allele frequency compared with a US population. The reduced frequency of this protective allele might contribute to the younger HCC onset and higher mortality in sub-Saharan Africa. These findings highlight the importance of conducting diverse, multiethnic genetic studies to address disparities in the clinical application of genetic discoveries for HCC prevention, risk stratification, and treatment.
