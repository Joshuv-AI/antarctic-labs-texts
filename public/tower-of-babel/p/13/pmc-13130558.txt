Background

Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, represents a major global health threat, particularly in the pediatric population [1, 2]. Pediatric sepsis is a leading cause of mortality and long-term morbidity worldwide, with case-fatality rates remaining persistently high, ranging from 10% to 20% in many settings [3, 4]. For survivors, the burden of new or exacerbated functional impairments, including neurocognitive and renal sequelae, highlights the critical need for optimal and timely management [5, 6]. Therefore, the early and accurate prediction of adverse clinical outcomes, such as 30-day mortality and the development of Multiple Organ Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS), is paramount to support clinical decision-making during the initial hours of management and to guide life-saving intervention [7].

Traditional risk stratification relies predominantly on clinical signs, physiological parameters, and illness severity scores like the Pediatric Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (pSOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) [8, 9]. While foundational, these scoring systems often rely on the manifestation of established organ dysfunction, limiting their utility in capturing the rapidly evolving, high-risk state of sepsis in its earliest stages [10, 11]. The pathophysiology of pediatric sepsis is highly complex and heterogeneous, characterized by a dynamic, non-linear interplay between uncontrolled inflammation, immune dysregulation, metabolic derangement (such as tissue hypoperfusion), and coagulopathy [12–14]. Relying on a limited number of clinical variables often fails to adequately capture this intricate, multi-systemic insult, resulting in suboptimal sensitivity and specificity for early prognosis.

To overcome these limitations, a comprehensive approach integrating diverse biomarkers that reflect various pathological axes is necessary, and several indicators have emerged as promising candidates. Beyond conventional markers like Procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP), key inflammatory cytokines such as Interleukin-6 (IL-6) reflect the systemic inflammatory storm [15, 16]. Furthermore, simple, cost-effective derived indices—including the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) and the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII)—provide readily available insights into the host’s innate immune response and inflammatory burden [17, 18]. In terms of metabolic dysfunction, Lactate serves as a crucial indicator of tissue hypoxia and anaerobic metabolism, with persistent hyperlactatemia being a well-established prognostic marker for mortality in septic shock [19–21]. While inflammatory markers reflect the immune system’s activation, lactate levels provide a window into the systemic metabolic consequences of this activation, specifically microcirculatory failure and mitochondrial dysfunction [22]. Therefore, the simultaneous assessment of immune status (via SII) and metabolic stress (via Lactate) may offer a more holistic view of the patient’s physiological trajectory than either marker alone. Given the high-dimensional nature and complex, often non-linear relationships among these physiological, inflammatory, and metabolic variables, Machine Learning (ML) techniques offer a superior advantage over traditional statistical methods. ML algorithms, such as LASSO regression for robust feature selection and Random Forest (RF) for model construction, can effectively discern subtle patterns and interactions within the data [23]. Furthermore, utilizing explainability tools like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) allows for transparent validation, ensuring that the final predictive model is not only accurate but also clinically interpretable [24].

This study aims to develop and validate a comprehensive, high-precision prognostic model for pediatric sepsis by integrating a broad spectrum of early clinical, physiological, and laboratory indicators, with a specific focus on the interplay between composite immune indices (SII) and critical metabolic markers (Lactate). By employing advanced Machine Learning techniques—including LASSO feature selection and multi-algorithm comparison—we intend to identify the most influential early biomarkers and establish a robust predictive system that accurately forecasts adverse outcomes (such as 30-day mortality). This novel system is designed to significantly improve early risk stratification, thereby facilitating timely, targeted, and personalized treatment strategies to enhance clinical outcomes for children with sepsis.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was a single-center, retrospective cohort study conducted at Tianjin Children’s Hospital. We reviewed the clinical data of pediatric patients diagnosed with sepsis who were admitted between January 2020 and June 2025. A total of 500 pediatric patients were enrolled, with 300 patients diagnosed with sepsis and 200 controls. Based on their 28-day survival status, patients were stratified into two groups: the “Good Prognosis” group (patients who survived) and the “Poor Prognosis” group (patients who died within 28 days). The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Ref. W-2025–021).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Diagnosis of sepsis as per the established guidelines, (2) Age ≤ 14 years, (3) Receiving regular treatment at the hospital, (4) Complete clinical data available.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) Congenital diseases, (2) Malignant tumors, (3) Immunodeficiency disorders, and (4) Patients transferred to other hospitals.

Data collection

A comprehensive set of clinical and laboratory data was collected from all pediatric patients diagnosed with sepsis. The demographic information collected included age, gender, and preexisting conditions such as chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes, liver and kidney disease, and immunodeficiency disorders, which could influence the onset and prognosis of sepsis. Data on the length of hospital stay and the severity of illness at admission, including whether the patient had experienced shock or organ dysfunction, were also recorded.

Physiological parameters, including body temperature (T), heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), blood pressure (BP), urine output, and oxygen saturation (SpO2), were measured regularly to monitor the patient’s clinical condition. These measurements were vital in assessing the severity of sepsis and organ function. Additionally, severity scoring systems such as APACHE II and SOFA scores were calculated to evaluate the degree of illness and the presence of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).

Blood samples were drawn within 24 hours of diagnosis to assess inflammatory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and lactate levels. White blood cell (WBC) parameters, including WBC count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, and monocyte count, were also recorded. Derived indices, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), were calculated as follows:

NLR = Neutrophil count/Lymphocyte count;

PLR = Platelet count/Lymphocyte count;

SII = (Platelet count × Neutrophil count)/Lymphocyte count.

Further clinical characteristics documented included the patient’s history of surgery (especially post-surgical sepsis), the cause of hospitalization (infection, trauma), and the presence of MODS.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was the 28-day survival status of the pediatric patients. Secondary outcomes included the development of organ dysfunction, including respiratory failure, renal failure, liver failure, and heart failure, as well as the occurrence of septic shock. These outcomes were categorized and compared between patients who survived and those who died within 28 days.

Machine learning model construction and evaluation

To develop a robust prognostic model for predicting 28-day mortality in pediatric sepsis, a multi-step machine learning workflow was implemented using R software (Version 4.4.1) and the caret package.2.6.1 Data Preprocessing and Feature SelectionPrior to model construction, data preprocessing was performed. Missing values were imputed using the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm (k = 5) to preserve data structure. Continuous variables were standardized (Z-score normalization) to ensure equal weight distribution across features, particularly for distance-based algorithms like SVM and KNN. Rather than assuming simple linear additivity, our machine learning workflow utilized non-linear algorithms to explicitly capture the synergistic interactions between the SII and Venous Lactate. The model integration reflects the algorithmic ability to evaluate the joint contribution of immune dysregulation and metabolic stress, with these interactive effects further validated through SHAP interpretability analysis. To address multicollinearity and dimensionality, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was employed for feature selection. A 10-fold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal lambda parameter that minimized the binomial deviance. Features with non-zero coefficients at the optimal lambda were identified as the most critical predictors and were selected for subsequent model building.

Based on the features selected by LASSO, ten distinct machine learning algorithms were constructed: Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Neural Network (NeuralNet), Decision Tree (DT), Partial Least Squares (PLS), and Generalized Boosted Regression Models (glmBoost).To prevent overfitting and ensure the generalization ability of the models, a 10-fold cross-validation strategy was implemented within the training cohort.4 Hyperparameters for each algorithm were optimized using a grid search approach. For instance, the number of trees (ntree) and variables tried at each split (mtry) were tuned for RF, while the learning rate (eta) and maximum depth (max_depth) were optimized for XGBoost.

The performance of the established models was evaluated in both the training and independent validation cohorts. The discriminative ability was assessed using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), along with accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Calibration curves were plotted to evaluate the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. To overcome the “black-box” nature of complex machine learning models, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were utilized to interpret the best-performing model.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R software (V4.4.1). Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For non-normally distributed data, variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), and comparisons between groups were made using the Mann-Whitney U test. For normally distributed variables, means and standard deviations (SD) were reported, and t-tests were used for comparisons. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with differences between groups tested using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test, where appropriate.

To further enhance the predictive power of the model, machine learning techniques, including LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression, were employed for feature selection. The variables identified as significant by LASSO were used to develop a predictive model based on machine learning algorithms. To assess model interpretability, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were calculated, providing insight into the contribution of individual features to the final predictions.

The predictive model’s performance was assessed using several evaluation metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Calibration curves were plotted to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was performed to quantify the net benefit of the model in clinical decision-making across different risk thresholds.

To examine the relationship between specific biomarkers and sepsis prognosis, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed for individual biomarkers and their combinations. The optimal cut-off values for each marker were determined using the Youden Index to maximize both sensitivity and specificity. Multivariable logistic regression was used to explore the associations between selected biomarkers and clinical outcomes, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Baseline characteristics between the control group and sepsis group

Markers of illness severity, including APACHE II and SOFA scores, were markedly higher in the sepsis group (13.9 ± 4.7 vs. 5.3 ± 1.8, p < 0.001; 6.2 ± 2.9 vs. 1.2 ± 0.5, p < 0.001), indicating significant physiologic derangement. Acute complications, such as septic shock (27.7%) and MODS (31.3%), were seen exclusively in the sepsis cohort.

Laboratory findings revealed elevated inflammatory markers, with CRP and PCT levels significantly higher in septic children (p < 0.001). Hematologic indices such as NLR, PLR, and SII were also increased, reflecting a hyperinflammatory state with neutrophil predominance and lymphocyte depletion. Metabolic abnormalities were evident, with lactate levels nearly three times higher (p < 0.001), indicating tissue hypoperfusion. Coagulation dysfunction was demonstrated by a tenfold increase in D-dimer levels (p < 0.001), and renal injury was indicated by elevated creatinine levels.

Overall, these findings demonstrate profound differences between septic and non-septic pediatric patients across inflammatory, metabolic, immune, and organ function domains, highlighting the systemic nature of sepsis and supporting the need for early biomarker-driven risk stratification.

As shown in Fig. 1, the selected features included Age < 3 years, Chronic comorbidities, CRP, PCT, lactate, NLR, PLR, SII, and D-dimer.

Comparison of clinical characteristics between good and poor prognosis groups in sepsis

Children in the Poor Prognosis group were significantly younger, with nearly two-thirds under 3 years of age (63.9% vs. 38.4%, p < 0.001), highlighting the increased vulnerability of younger patients. The prevalence of chronic comorbidities, including congenital heart disease, chronic pulmonary conditions, and renal or hepatic impairments, was nearly doubled in the Poor Prognosis group (37.1% vs. 20.2%, p = 0.002), suggesting that baseline health status plays a critical role in determining sepsis outcomes.

Markers of disease severity were substantially higher in non-survivors. The APACHE II and SOFA scores were markedly elevated in the Poor Prognosis group (18.6 vs. 10.8, p < 0.001; 8.4 vs. 4.1, p < 0.001), reflecting more severe organ dysfunction and systemic injury at presentation. Additionally, the incidence of septic shock (55.7% vs. 14.3%, p < 0.001) and MODS (62.9% vs. 16.3%, p < 0.001) was significantly higher among non-survivors, underscoring the rapid clinical deterioration seen in these patients.

Inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers were markedly different between the two groups. CRP levels were more than double in the Poor Prognosis group (115 vs. 52 mg/L, p < 0.001), and PCT increased nearly fourfold (12.6 vs. 3.1 ng/mL, p < 0.001). Lactate concentrations, indicating impaired tissue perfusion, were more than twice as high in the Poor Prognosis group (4.6 vs. 2.1 mmol/L, p < 0.001), suggesting more severe metabolic derangement.

Immune dysregulation was evident in the Poor Prognosis group, with significantly higher NLR, PLR, and SII values (14.5 vs. 6.8, 268 vs. 142, and 2620 vs. 980, respectively; all p < 0.001). These changes reflect overwhelming innate immune activation coupled with adaptive immune suppression, a hallmark of fatal sepsis trajectories.

Coagulation abnormalities were also more pronounced in non-survivors, with D-dimer levels more than threefold higher (3.9 vs. 1.2 mg/L, p < 0.001), indicating severe coagulopathy. Renal dysfunction was more prevalent among non-survivors, with significantly higher serum creatinine levels (67 vs. 42 µmol/L, p < 0.001), consistent with acute kidney injury in the setting of septic shock.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed and validated a machine learning-based prognostic model for pediatric sepsis by integrating early clinical characteristics, inflammatory indices, and metabolic biomarkers. Our analysis of 500 pediatric patients demonstrated that the pathophysiology of sepsis is characterized by a complex interplay of immune dysregulation, coagulopathy, and metabolic derangement. We identified a core set of predictors-including PCT, SII, NLR, PLR, and Lactate-that robustly distinguish septic patients from controls and predict 28-day mortality. Notably, our results highlight the superior performance of composite immune indices (specifically SII) and the critical warning value of Lactate, which were further validated in an independent cohort. By employing LASSO feature selection and SHAP interpretation, we bridged the gap between complex algorithmic predictions and clinical interpretability, providing a potential tool for early risk stratification in the “Golden Hour” of sepsis management.

Traditional inflammatory markers such as CRP and PCT are widely used for sepsis diagnosis; however, their kinetic delay and lack of specificity can limit their prognostic utility in the hyperacute phase [25, 26]. Our study demonstrates that derived hematological indices, particularly SII and NLR, provide enhanced prognostic accuracy. Consistent with recent literature, we observed that non-survivors exhibited significantly elevated SII and NLR levels compared to survivors [27, 28]. Mechanistically, these indices reflect the duality of the host immune response to sepsis: a “storm” of innate immunity (neutrophilia) coupled with adaptive immune suppression (lymphopenia) [29, 30]. Neutrophils release cytotoxic enzymes and reactive oxygen species that, while combating infection, can cause collateral tissue damage and organ failure. Concurrently, sepsis-induced lymphocyte apoptosis leads to immune paralysis, rendering the host vulnerable to secondary infections and mortality [31]. The SII, which integrates platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts, provides a more holistic view of this systemic thrombo-inflammatory status than single lineages alone [17]. Compared to the recently validated models by Alpern et al. and the Phoenix criteria-based machine learning approach by Chanci et al., our model offers a distinct clinical advantage by prioritizing the ‘immune-metabolic’ axis [32, 33]. While the PECARN study demonstrates exceptional performance for early screening using broad clinical phenotypes, it requires integration with complex electronic health record (EHR) systems. In contrast, our focus on the SII-Lactate synergy provides a more accessible tool for rapid risk stratification. This approach captures subtle, non-linear immune dysregulations that clinical signs—the bedrock of the Phoenix criteria—may miss during the hyperacute phase, thereby offering a ‘biological-metabolic’ perspective that complements existing clinical-sign-heavy models.

Pediatric sepsis presents with high heterogeneity, where linear scoring systems (like pSOFA) may fail to capture non-linear interactions between physiological parameters. Our study utilized ten different ML algorithms, with PLS, glmBoost, and NeuralNet achieving AUCs exceeding 0.87. The added value of the ML approach in this study extends beyond mere predictive accuracy. While traditional statistical comparisons confirm the association of single markers with mortality, they often fail to account for the complex, non-linear interactions inherent in septic pathophysiology. Our ML-based framework identified that the synergy between immune dysregulation (SII) and metabolic stress (Venous Lactate) is a more potent predictor than either marker in isolation. Specifically, SHAP interpretation revealed that the impact of lactate on mortality risk is not a simple linear function but is significantly modulated by the patient’s inflammatory state (SII) and age. A key strength of this study is the mechanistic integration of the SII and Venous Lactate. In our ML approach, ‘integration’ refers to the model’s capacity to recognize the non-linear interplay between the host’s thrombo-inflammatory response and systemic tissue hypoperfusion. Our findings, supported by SHAP analysis, demonstrate that the combined ‘immune-metabolic’ profile provides a more nuanced risk assessment than assessing these markers as independent factors, aligning with the multi-systemic nature of pediatric sepsis.

By transitioning from population-level associations to individualized risk decomposition, this ML approach provides a more nuanced clinical understanding, allowing for precise risk stratification that conventional logistic models may overlook. This performance surpasses traditional logistic regression models often cited in previous studies. However, the “black-box” nature of ML often hinders clinical adoption. By implementing SHAP analysis, we provided transparent insight into how the model arrives at predictions. For instance, the SHAP waterfall plots revealed that while high markers of organ damage push the risk prediction up, younger age (<3 years) and high Lactate are equally critical drivers. This interpretability allows clinicians to validate the model’s logic against their medical intuition, fostering trust in AI-assisted decision-making.

While our study primarily focuses on 28-day mortality, the practical utility of the SII-Lactate model lies in its integration into the initial phase of care. Because these biomarkers can be obtained from the first blood draw, they provide an objective risk score during the early stage of sepsis management. This early risk identification—rather than sepsis recognition alone—allows clinicians to prioritize aggressive resuscitation and closer monitoring for patients with a ‘poor prognosis’ profile, thereby bridging the gap between diagnosis and personalized intervention.

Furthermore, our study validates the indispensable roles of D-dimer and Lactate as established prognostic pillars in pediatric sepsis. The substantial elevation of D-dimer observed in the poor prognosis group signifies the unbridled activation of the coagulation cascade and secondary fibrinolysis, a trajectory often culminating in Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), which is a major contributor to mortality [34, 35]. Concurrently, hyperlactatemia serves as a hallmark of tissue hypoperfusion and mitochondrial dysfunction, reflecting the metabolic cost of the systemic inflammatory response [36]. The retention of these standard markers in our machine learning model ensures that it remains grounded in the canonical pathophysiology of septic shock-encompassing both coagulopathy and metabolic failure-while being significantly refined by the immunological insights provided by SII.

Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, selection bias is inevitable, and the specific patient population may not fully represent the demographic or pathogen diversity found in other geographic regions. Second, although we validated the model in an independent cohort, the overall sample size remains relatively modest, necessitating large-scale, multi-center validation to ensure the model’s generalizability. Third, a notable limitation is the heterogeneity of lactate sampling sources. In this retrospective design, lactate levels were obtained from both arterial and venous sources, reflecting real-world clinical urgency during the ‘Golden Hour.’ While this reflects pragmatic practice where arterial access may not be immediately established, the potential numerical discrepancy between venous and arterial values could introduce measurement bias. Fourth, our analysis relied on baseline biomarker levels (Lactate and SII) measured at admission; we did not incorporate dynamic changes, such as lactate clearance rates or the trajectory of SII during the hospital stay. Dynamic monitoring could potentially provide superior prognostic information regarding the patient’s response to initial resuscitation. Finally, due to the retrospective design, we established associations but cannot infer causality. Future prospective studies with standardized sampling protocols are required to determine whether therapeutic strategies explicitly targeting these biomarker thresholds can directly improve survival outcomes in pediatric sepsis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes a robust and clinically interpretable machine learning model for pediatric sepsis prognosis. We identified that the synergistic integration of composite immune indices (SII) and metabolic biomarkers (Lactate) significantly enhances risk stratification beyond traditional clinical scores. These biomarkers collectively capture the critical pathophysiological nexus of systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and metabolic failure. Given their cost-effectiveness and rapid availability, our findings advocate for the routine assessment of SII and Lactate to facilitate the early risk stratification of children with sepsis, supporting clinical decision-making during the critical initial hours of treatment to improve survival rates.
