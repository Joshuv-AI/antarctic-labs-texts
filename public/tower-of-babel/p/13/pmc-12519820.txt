Decellularized extracellular matrix bio-patch with strong and biodegradable wet adhesive interface for sutureless soft tissue defect repair
Authors: Xiaoya Chen, Binbin Guan, Wei Jing, Yiqian Huang, Pengfei Wei, Lei Sui, Bo Zhao, Zuoying Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Decellularized extracellular matrix-based bio-patches offer superior biocompatibility and bioactivity, making them an ideal implantation material for soft tissue defect repair. However, the fixation of these patches typically relies on sutures or staplers, causing secondary tissue damage, stress concentration, and excessive inflammatory cell infiltration. Here, we develop a novel self-adhesive small intestinal submucosa patch (A-SIS) fabricated by in situ polymerizing a biodegradable hydrogel adhesive layer on the surface of the SIS patch. The A-SIS demonstrated increased tensile strength, biodegradability, and strong wet adhesion to various soft tissues (including skin, heart, stomach, and intestine), with shear strength reaching 100 kPa and interfacial toughness of 700 J/m2 for porcine skin. In vivo studies showed that A-SIS patches significantly reduced surgical manipulation time and promoted tissue integration with fewer inflammatory cell infiltrations compared to sutured SIS patches. Moreover, A-SIS patches effectively accelerated the healing of diabetic ulcers and full-thickness wounds in preclinical porcine models. This work not only reports a novel design and fabrication of decellularized extracellular matrix-based bio-patch with strong self-adhesive interface, but also offers a promising sutureless solution for clinical soft tissue defect repair.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03738-8.

Introduction

Soft tissue injuries resulting from external trauma or endogenous factors are highly prevalent and required meticulous wound management and effective tissue regeneration [1–3]. For example, abdominal wall defects caused by trauma or the resection of primary or secondary tumors are prone to hernia and life-threatening intestinal obstructions, posing a significant challenge to surgeons and imposing an economic burden of over ten billion dollars on patients and healthcare systems [4]. As the most common soft tissue injuries, skin wounds, particularly those associated with chronic diseases like diabetes, also challenging to heal spontaneously [5].

Tension-free repair techniques for soft tissue injuries have long been advocated, facilitating the commercialization and clinical adoption of various surgical patches [6, 7]. Currently, synthetic patch materials such as polypropylene and polyester, with their excellent mechanical strength, dominate the field of tension-free surgical repair [8]. However, these artificial materials often induce severe foreign body reactions, leading to fibrous capsule encapsulation that hinders tissue regeneration and limits surgical outcomes [9]. In contrast, decellularized extracellular matrix based biological grafts, as an ideal alternative to synthetic materials, offer superior biocompatibility and significantly reduced postoperative adverse reactions, providing the possibility for durable and stable tissue repair [10–13]. Particularly, small intestinal submucosa (SIS)-based biomaterials, such as Surgisis® (COOK Inc.) patches, have undergone decades of clinical validation [14]. These patches are rich in extracellular collagen matrices that can rapidly promote tissue ingrowth and neovascularization, achieving a tight and lasting integration with surrounding tissues [15]. As the SIS gradually degrade, new regenerative tissue is formed, effectively maintaining the integrity and functional recovery of the abdominal anatomy, offering patients a safer and more effective treatment option [16].

In most cases, the fixation of SIS patches still relies on sutures or staplers, a process that is not only time-consuming but also causes stress concentration and secondary tissue damage [17]. Additionally, surgical patches fixed with sutures struggle to conformally integrate with tissues, providing excessive space for inflammatory cell infiltration during the early stages of implantation, which can promote the formation of fibrous capsules at the interface between the implant and tissue, triggering postoperative complications such as infection and chronic pain [18]. Bioadhesive offers the potential for sutureless wound closure [19–21]. However, current commercial bio-glues, such as TISSEEL, and COSEAL, adhesion failure always occurs due to their low substrate toughness and adhesive strength, especially in wet conditions [22, 23]. Therefore, achieving a strong adhesion between surgical patches and tissues is an urgent need to prevent secondary tissue damage, reduce inflammatory responses, decrease complications, and improve surgical success rates.

In recent years, significant advances have been made in advanced hydrogels for tissue regeneration [24–26]. For tissue adhesion and wound closure, ​​introducing adhesive groups​​ represented by catechol that enhance physical interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding, electrostatic) between hydrogels and tissues is the most common strategy [27, 28]. In our previous work, composites of SIS patch and GelMA hydrogel crosslinked with plant polyphenol tannic acid could adher to tissue surfaces, promoting healing of bacteria-infected wounds [29]. However, its adhesion strength remained low, at only ~ 60 kPa. Topologic adhesive based on polymer chain diffusion and in-situ chemical crosslinking can achieve high-strength tissue adhesion, but the diffusion rate of polymer chains is slow, typically requiring ​​over 10 min of activation [30]​​. Furthermore, all the above strategies ​​need to construct a hydrated hydrogel layer on the patch surface​​, where difficulties in packaging and long-term storage of hydrogel-patch composites.

This study aims to develop a degradable SIS patch with a strong self-adhesive interface to enable sutureless implantation and promote the repair of soft tissue defects. A-SIS were fabricated by in situ polymerizing a hydrogel adhesive layer on the surface of SIS patches, with the adhesive layer comprising chitosan and poly(acrylic acid) grafted with N-hydroxysuccinimide ester (PAA-NHS ester) crosslinked by N, N′-bis(acryloyl)cystamine (BACA). Notably, because BACA rather than MBAA is used as the crosslinker, A-SIS is fully degradable. The wet adhesion performance of the patches was evaluated through lap-shear and 180-degree peel tests using various soft porcine tissues (including skin, heart, stomach, and intestine). The effectiveness of the adhesive SIS (A-SIS) patches in promoting abdominal wall regeneration and chronic wound healing was assessed in rat and porcine models (Fig. 1).

Results and discussions

The self-adhesive SIS patches was fabricated by in situ polymerizing a tough hydrogel adhesive on the surface of SIS patches. (Fig. 2A) The original SIS patch, composed of decellularized extracellular matrix fibers, has a thickness of 60–100 micrometers and a rough surface. (Fig. 2B) After modification with an adhesive layer of ~ 40 micrometers, the surface becomes smooth. (Fig. 2C) The introduction of the adhesive layer significantly increases the tensile strength of the SIS patch from 17.0 MPa to 51.9 MPa, but reduces its elongation at break. (Fig. 2D&E)

Both the adhesive layer and matrix SIS patch are biodegradable. (Fig. 2F) In the presence of collagenase and cysteine, the SIS patch can degrade over 70% within 72 hours. By contrast, the A-SIS patch degrades faster initially due to the presence of the dried adhesive layer, reaching 89.5% within 72 hours. (Fig. 2G) Unlike the traditional non-degradable N, N’-methylenebis(acrylamide) (MBAA) crosslinker [31, 32], BACA is degradable under physiological conditions as the disulfide bonds undergo the thiol-disulfide exchange reaction [33]. Hence, the adhesive layer of A-SIS first degrades into water-soluble PAA, PAA-NHS, and chitosan. Chitosan can be degraded by enzymes which hydrolyse glucosamine-glucosamine, glucosamine-N-acetyl-glucosamine, and N-acetyl-glucosamine-N-acetyl-glucosamine linkages [34]. Although PAA and PAA-NHS do not undergo further degradation, they exhibit no associated toxicity [20]. Both SIS and A-SIS patches show good cytocompatibility, with cell viability greater than 95% compared to the control group. (Fig. 2H)

The adhesion of A-SIS patches to wet tissues is primarily based on a dry crosslinking strategy that absorbs interfacial water. After absorbing interfacial water, the adhesive layer of A-SIS can form a conformal match with tissues, simultaneously, the carboxylic acid groups of PAA form intermolecular bonds (hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions) with tissue surfaces. Within minutes, the NHS ester groups grafted on the PAA couple covalently with primary amine groups on tissues [35]. (Fig. 3A) To evaluate the wet adhesion performance of the A-SIS, we tested the shear strength through lap-shear tests (ASTM F2255) (Fig. 3B) and interfacial toughness through 180-degree peel tests (ASTM F2256) (Fig. 3C). The highest shear strength and interfacial toughness of A-SIS was reached after 123.1 kPa after pressing for 1 min, and 762.4 J/m2 after pressing for 5 min, respectively. Intermolecular interactions dominate the initial adhesion of A-SIS, and even use PAA without grafted NHS esters, the shear strength can reach 82.5 kPa after gentle pressing for 5 s, with an interfacial toughness of 563.8 J/m2. Covalent crosslinking is more critical for the long-term stable adhesion of A-SIS. After pressing for one hour, A-SIS still has a shear strength of 100.9 kPa and an interfacial toughness of 703.2 J/m2, while A-SIS without NHS esters can achieve a shear strength of 67.2 kPa and an interfacial toughness of 480.8 J/m2. After storage at room temperature for 12 and 24 h, A-SIS has a shear strength of 90 kPa and an interfacial toughness of 650 J/m2, while A-SIS without NHS esters has a shear strength of only 30 kPa and an interfacial toughness reduce to 150 J/m2.

We compared the adhesion capabilities of SIS patches fixed with adhesive layers (i.e., A-SIS) and commercial adhesives (Coseal and Tisseel) on various soft tissue surfaces. A-SIS demonstrated unprecedented patch-fixing capabilities, with 123.1 kPa and 725.2 J/m2 for skin, (Fig. 3D) 99.8 kPa and 619.7 J/m2 for heart, (Fig. 3E) 112.7 kPa and 675.6 J/m2 for stomach, (Fig. 3F) and 76.6 kPa and 530.2 J/m2 for intestine (Fig. 3G). Although Tisseel has a slightly stronger ability to fix SIS patches on soft tissue surfaces than Coseal, its adhesion strength is only ~ 9 kPa, and the interface toughness is only ~ 80 J/m2. Adhesion between these commercial adhesives and tissue wet surfaces typically occur when adhesive molecules pass through the hydration layer and bond with the reactive groups or polymer networks of the substrate [18]. However, this process is time-consuming owing to the low diffusivity of large molecules. In contrast, the adhesive processes of A-SIS combine both covalent and noncovalent interactions: the noncovalent interactions are responsible for rapid adhesion, which can occur simultaneously with water removal; while covalent interactions account for stable adhesion [35]. Hence, the developed A-SIS achieve such high shear strength and interfacial toughness in a short time, while other commercial adhesives are much weaker.

To simulate clinical abdominal wall defects, a sagittal incision (1 cm injury site) was made on the abdominal wall of SD rats [36]. Commercial SIS patches and A-SIS were shaped to conventional dimensions (2 cm × 1 cm) and then implanted into the defect site to assess their effectiveness in promoting abdominal wall regeneration. (Fig. 4A) The SIS patches were fixed with sutures, while A-SIS patches were directly adhered to the defect site. (Fig. 4B) This direct adhesion significantly reduced surgical manipulation time, with A-SIS patches fixing to the defect surface in 10 s, compared to the approximately 3 min required for suturing of SIS patches. (Fig. 4C) Macroscopically, degradation of SIS patches and A-SIS was observed at 7- and 14-days post-operation, with the A-SIS group showing a higher degree of degradation compared to the SIS patches, and the abdominal wall defect was almost completely healed 14 days post-operation (FigureS1). Harvested tissues were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin for sectioning and histological staining. As shown in the Fig. 4D, both SIS patches and A-SIS showed significant wound healing at 7 days post-operation compared to the untreated control group. Particularly, in the A-SIS group, fewer inflammatory cell infiltrations were observed, while the SIS patches, despite their good biocompatibility and retained endogenous bioactive factors (such as VEGF, TGF-β) [37] showed more inflammatory cell infiltrations and thicker fibrous tissue. This may be due to the inability of sutured SIS patches to form conformal interfacial integration with tissue surfaces, allowing inflammatory cells to infiltrate more easily into the SIS-tissue interface. At 14 days, the defects in A-SIS groups were essentially completely repaired. Although the inflammation in the SIS group had significantly subsided, but the fibrous tissue near the implant in the SIS group was still thicker compared to the A-SIS group. (Figure S2&S3)

To investigate immune cell infiltration at the implant-tissue interface, we performed immunofluorescent staining for macrophages (F4/80) and T cells (CD3) at 7- and 14-days post-implantation, while using CD206 and iNOS to represent anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory macrophages, respectively. (Fig. 5A) Quantitative analysis of the number of positively stained cells of each type at the implant-tissue interface was performed from immunofluorescence images. (Fig. 5B & C) The results showed that at 7 days post-implantation, A-SIS had significantly fewer T cells and macrophages than the blank and SIS groups. However, the proportion of M1 phenotype macrophages in the A-SIS group was also higher than in the SIS group, which indicates that although the adhesive interface may cause a slight pro-inflammatory response, the overall immune response is lower due to significantly fewer macrophage infiltrations. At 14 days postoperatively, inflammation in the SIS group subsided, yet the number of T cells and macrophages was still higher than in the A-SIS group. Notably, the proportion of M2 macrophage phenotypes in the SIS group was significantly enhanced, which may lead to fibrosis [38]. By contrast, the M1-M2 macrophage phenotype ratio in the A-SIS group remained stable, which is more conducive to tissue regeneration than excessive M2 polarization [39].

To further investigate the inflammation microenvironment, pro-inflammatory genes (including IL-1β and IL-6) and anti-inflammatory genes (including IL-4 and IL-10), were detected through RT-qPCR. As shown in Fig. 5D&E, at 7 days post-implantation, the SIS patch demonstrated characteristic anti-inflammatory capacity, evidenced by significantly downregulated expression of IL-1β and IL-6 versus the Blank group, along with significantly upregulated expression of IL-4 and IL-10. Though the A-SIS group showed relative anti-inflammatory gene expression to the Blank control, its pro-inflammatory gene expression was the lowest among all three groups. In accord with immunofluorescence results, expression of pro-/anti-inflammatory genes both increased in the A-SIS group by day 14, whereas the SIS group exhibited dominant expression of anti-inflammatory genes. This further indicates that while the adhesive interface induces a certain level of inflammation compared to SIS alone, the conformal integration of A-SIS with tissue surfaces significantly reduces overall inflammatory cell infiltration, therefore leading to minimum pro-inflammatory gene expression.

Diabetic ulcers are a major type of chronic wound [40]. Diabetes causes persistent inflammation, neuropathy, tissue hypoxia, and insufficient angiogenesis, making diabetic wounds difficult to heal [41, 42]. Here, we applied A-SIS to a diabetic rat full-thickness wound model to assess its ability to promote chronic wound healing in vivo. (Fig. 6A) As shown in the Fig. 6B & S4, wounds untreated (i.e., PBS group) and treated with commercial Tegaderm dressings did not heal spontaneously within 14 days, while the wound healing rate of the SIS patch and A-SIS groups significantly improved, with the A-SIS group showing the highest wound closure rate. The final wound closure rates for these four groups at 14 days were 80.1% (Blank group), 84.4% (Tegaderm group), 91.2% (SIS patch group), and 95.7% (A-SIS group). (Fig. 6C)

H&E staining was used to observe the details of tissue regeneration in the wound defects. (Fig. 6D&E) Until the 14th day, the Blank group, Tegaderm group, and SIS group failed to achieve complete epidermal regeneration, and obvious scar tissue was found at the top. A relatively complete epidermal layer observed only in the A-SIS group at 14 days, with the highest tissue maturity and regeneration of sweat glands and hair follicles. Based on H&E staining images, we quantified the length of immature tissue in the dermis and the thickness of the epidermis. (Fig. 6F) The A-SIS group had the shortest length of immature tissue in the dermis and the thinnest epidermal thickness. Masson staining and Sirius Red staining further demonstrated that A-SIS-treated diabetic wounds had the most mature collagen fiber deposition similar to undamaged tissue. (Fig. 6G & S5)

Encouraged by the effective promotion of soft tissue healing by A-SIS in rodent models, we further constructed a porcine full-thickness wound model to explore the application of A-SIS in a wound model similar to human wounds [43, 44], using commercial gelatin sponges as a control. (Fig. 7A) Compared to the blank control group, wounds treated with gelatin sponge and A-SIS showed an accelerated wound healing process. (Fig. 7B & S6) At day 21, the final wound closure rates for these three groups were 71.4% (Blank group), 82.7% (GS group), and 91.6% (A-SIS group), respectively. (Fig. 7C) H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining, further confirmed the quality and maturity of the newly formed skin tissue. (Fig. 7D-F) Compared to the other two groups, wounds treated with A-SIS had significantly shorter intervals of immature tissue (Fig. 7E) and were observed to have densely packed, parallel-aligned collagen fibers rather than scar tissue. In summary, A-SIS promotes wound healing in a preclinical pig full-thickness wound model, demonstrating potential for clinical translation. In addition, since hydrogel adhesives have already been widely used as drug carriers, these technologies can be seamlessly integrated into the current self-adhesive patch platform. For example, by loading exosomes, growth factors, anticancer drugs, or antibiotics, [45, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50] the platform can be adapted to treat large defects and a wider range of disease conditions, including tissue defects after tumor resection and bacterial-infected wound.

Conclusion

Our study presents a biodegradable SIS patch with an integrated strong adhesive interface, designed for sutureless implantation and effective repair of soft tissue defects. The A-SIS demonstrated increased tensile strength, maintained cytocompatibility and biodegradability. More critically, the A-SIS patches have shown strong wet adhesion capabilities, as confirmed by lap-shear and peel tests, enable secure attachment to tissues without the need for sutures, reducing surgical time and reducing the level of infiltration of inflammatory cells into the interface between A-SIS and tissue, leading to improved healing outcomes compared to conventional sutured SIS patches as confirmed in rat abdominal wall defect model. Furthermore, the application of A-SIS patches in diabetic ulcer in rat and porcine full-thickness wound models has showcased their potential to accelerate skin wound healing. In summary, the A-SIS provides a typical example of self-adhesive bio-patches, and offers a promising sutureless solution for soft tissue defect repair.
