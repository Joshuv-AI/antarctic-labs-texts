Cuproptosis-related gene TMPO affects the healing of diabetic foot ulcers through TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway
Authors: Xiaoxiang Wang, Wentao Chen, Wenlian Zheng, Fujun Wang, Lusheng Xu, Sitong Zhou, Ronghua Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCuproptosis, a form of programmed cell death distinct from apoptosis, is associated with oxidative stress and inflammation. However, its role in diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) remains unclear. In this study, we utilized the GEO database and employed differential expression analysis and machine learning approaches to identify the cuproptosis-related gene Thymopoietin (TMPO). In vitro cellular experiments demonstrated that TMPO expression was upregulated under high glucose conditions, and its knockdown promoted cell proliferation and migration while activating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. In vivo animal experiments revealed that TMPO knockdown reduced inflammation, improved tissue regeneration, enhanced microvascular and collagen formation, and facilitated wound healing. Our study established a connection between DFU and cuproptosis, identifying that TMPO regulates diabetic wound healing through the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. These findings provide new insights and potential strategies for DFU treatment.Subject areas: Cell biology

Introduction

The global prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) reached 9.3% in 2019, and it is projected to rise to 10.2% by 2030.1 Approximately 10–15% of patients with DM develop foot ulcers, which are a serious chronic complication.2 Individuals with moderate to advanced diabetes are at risk of foot infections, ulcers, and even deep tissue damage.3 Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) significantly impact patients' quality of life and impose a substantial financial burden due to the difficulty in treatment, prolonged healing periods, and high costs.4 Even after healing, DFUs are prone to recurrence, with more than 40% of patients developing new ulcers within one year, 60% within three years, and 65% within five years.5 Worldwide, a lower limb is amputated every 30 s due to DFU.6 Early and effective management of DFUs can improve the quality of life and reduce the risk of complications, including amputation and mortality.3 Therefore, timely intervention and the identification of predictive biomarkers for DFU are critical. While the exact pathophysiology of DFU remains unclear, it is commonly linked to vascular disease, foot infections, and peripheral neuropathy.7 In patients with DM, Foot infections trigger an inflammatory response that leads to the release of inflammatory factors across multiple tissues, disrupting the stability of the foot’s internal environment and exacerbating the infection.8 However, it is important to note that infection is not the sole contributor to the inflammatory response in DFU. Other factors, such as poor blood glucose control, vascular dysfunction, and impaired wound healing, can also contribute to chronic inflammation, which further impedes the healing process.9 This heightened inflammation may also contribute to the disease’s progression.10,11

Over the past two years, cuproptosis has received lots of attention due to its damage to humans. The dynamic balance of copper is tightly regulated by protein transporters and chaperone proteins to allow for its correct distribution and to avoid uncontrolled redox reactions, in which excess redox can overwhelm the cell and lead to death.12 Cuproptosis was defined by Tsvetkov et al. to this particular form of copper-induced regulation of cell death.13 Currently available data on cuproptosis mainly focus on tumor prediction,14,15,16,17,18,19 and cuproptosis is regarded as a promising therapeutic method for oncological diseases.20,21 Interestingly, specific mechanisms and effects of cuproptosis on disease have not been explored as apoptosis, ferroptosis, and necroptosis in depth.22 It has been reported that blood copper concentration is positively correlated with the prevalence of DM in Chinese hypertensive adults with high HDL-C concentrations.23 Redox imbalance caused by excess copper triggers inflammatory reactions that worsen DM cardiomyopathy.24 In inflammatory bowel disease, regulatory factors associated with cuproptosis exhibit extensive differential expression.25 Several important regulatory genes play important roles in aberrant proliferation, as well as inflammatory processes in various immune cells.26 The above studies indicate that cuproptosis is closely related to the inflammatory response. However, whether cuproptosis plays a role in DFU remains to be clarified.

In this work, data analysis and data mining techniques were employed to screen for differentially expressed cuproptosis genes in DFU by comparison with normal tissues. The possibility of related genes as biomarkers of DFU was verified by in vivo animal experiments and in vitro cell experiments. This work promotes the understanding of the cuproptosis mechanism in DFU, provides support for understanding the underlying molecular mechanism of DFU, and offers novel ideas for predicting and treating DFU.

Results

Differences in cuproptosis-related genes between the control and diabetic foot ulcers groups

The overall flow chart for the study design is depicted in Figure S1. To investigate the potential role of CRGs in DFU, we initially combined and normalized the gene expression profiles from the GSE68183 and GSE80178 datasets (Figure S2A). Upon performing this normalization, we observed that the overall gene expression patterns of the control and DFU groups were highly similar, indicating the need for a more focused analysis of differential expression to reveal significant differences between these groups (Figure S2B).

The 10 CRGs, previously identified as being involved in cuproptosis (FDX1, LIAS, LIPT1, DLD, DLAT, PDHA1, POHB, MTF1, GLS, and CDKN2A), were assessed for differential expression between control and DFU samples. A detailed differential analysis revealed that two genes, DLAT and DLD, were significantly down-regulated in the DFU group compared to the control group (Figures 1A–1C). This suggests that their decreased expression could be linked to impaired wound healing in patients with DFU, although the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation. Notably, the remaining CRGs, such as FDX1, LIAS, and others, did not show significant differential expression, which may suggest their regulation could be either independent of DFU pathology or modulated by other factors not captured in this dataset.

In addition to differential expression analysis, the interaction network of CRGs was examined using the STRING database, which helped visualize the relationships among these genes (Figure 1D). This analysis revealed complex inter-gene interactions, especially between DLAT and DLD, which exhibited a strong positive correlation (r = 0.88, p < 0.05) in DFU samples, suggesting a potential cooperative role in the pathogenesis of DFU. Conversely, MTF1 and LIPT1 showed a significant negative correlation (r = −0.7, p < 0.05), indicating a potential regulatory antagonism between these genes that may influence cellular responses under DFU conditions (Figure 1E). Further, the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was conducted to calculate the CRG scores for each sample, providing a more refined assessment of CRG activity in both groups. The results revealed that the CRG scores of DFU samples were significantly lower compared to control samples (Figure 1F), suggesting a reduction in cuproptosis-related processes in DFU tissues. This reduction could contribute to the chronic inflammation and impaired healing commonly observed in diabetic foot ulcers.

Thus, our findings indicate that the cuproptosis gene may contribute to the development of DFU. These results provide a foundational understanding of how cuproptosis-related genes might contribute to the impaired healing process in DFU and suggest potential biomarkers for further exploration of therapeutic strategies.

Potential biological functions of cuproptosis-related genes

Cuproptosis, as an emerging form of programmed cell death, is associated with a variety of biological processes, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular stress responses. These processes are particularly relevant in the context of DFU, where chronic inflammation and impaired wound healing are prominent features. To explore the potential biological functions of CRGs in DFU, we further classified the patients with DFU into two groups based on their CRG scores: high CRG scores and how CRG score (Figure 2A). This classification allowed us to perform differential analysis between these groups to identify CRGs with significantly altered expression, which may provide insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying DFU pathology. 940 differentially expressed CRGs were obtained, including 765 differentially expressed up-regulated genes and 175 differentially expressed down-regulated genes. To investigate the potential biological functions of cuproptosis-related genes in DFU, we performed functional enrichment analysis. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the up-regulated DE-CRGs were predominantly associated with several key biological functions, including ncRNA processing, chromosomal region, and catalytic activity acting on RNA (Figure 2B). These findings suggest that CRGs may play significant roles in gene expression regulation, particularly through post-transcriptional modifications and chromatin remodeling. On the other hand, the down-regulated DE-CRGs were closely linked to processes such as keratinocyte differentiation and response to decreased oxygen levels (Figure 2D). Keratinocyte differentiation is essential for skin regeneration and wound closure, while oxygen level sensing is critical in hypoxic wound environments typical of DFU, where inadequate vascularization is a common challenge. The down-regulation of these processes could impede proper tissue regeneration and healing in patients with DFU. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis further identified that the up-regulated DE-CRGs were primarily enriched in pathways such as the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, and RNA degradation (Figure 2C). The TGF-β/Smad pathway is well-known for its role in tissue repair, fibrosis, and immune regulation, which makes it highly relevant to wound healing in DFU. Specifically, TGF-β signaling promotes fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix secretion during wound healing, and its dysregulation can lead to impaired healing and chronic ulcers. In contrast, the down-regulated DE-CRGs were found to be involved in pathways related to keratinocyte differentiation and wound healing, further reinforcing the notion that CRGs play a vital role in the cellular responses that are essential for tissue regeneration and repair in DFU. We further visualized the expression patterns of genes enriched in the pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis pathway (Figure 2E) and the RNA degradation pathway (Figure 2F). The enrichment analysis results revealed the potential molecular mechanisms underlying DFU wound healing, encompassing critical processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, energy metabolism, oxygen response, and tissue repair (Table S1). Notably, the upregulated and downregulated DEGs likely contribute to impaired wound healing, persistent infection, and chronic ulcer formation through distinct molecular pathways. These enriched pathways offer valuable insights into potential therapeutic targets for the treatment of diabetic foot ulcers.

The biological pathways influenced by gene expression in high- and low-CRG score groups were analyzed. The results show that metabolism of RNA, cell cycle (mitotic), translation, processing of capped intron-containing pre-mRNA, and other biologically relevant pathways were upregulated in patients with high CRG scores (Figures 3A–3D). Nevertheless, chemokine receptors binding chemokines, the PID API pathway, the WP vitamin D receptor pathway, the WP nuclear receptors meta-pathway, and other biological pathways were down-regulated in patients with high CRG scores (Figures 3E–3H). Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was carried out to determine the differences in the pathways between the high-and low-CRG score groups. The results showed that it mainly affected metal sequestration by antimicrobial proteins, Wiemann telomere shortening, and chronic liver damage DM, Horton SREBF targets, Liu cMYB targets DM, the BioCarta FXR pathway, Weber methylated LCP in fibroblast up, and other biologically relevant pathways (Figure 3I). These insights into gene expression and pathway differences underscore the complex molecular landscape of DFU, offering potential avenues for targeted therapeutic interventions.

Co-expression of genes in expression profiles

In order to study the co-expression of genes in the expression profiles of patients with DFU, using the R package Weighted Gene Correlation Network Analysis (WGCNA), a co-expression module was constructed using the genes that are expressed in the entirety of this dataset of patients with DFU. The samples were clustered using the Pearson correlation coefficient (Figure 4A). Next, the scale-free network was constructed (Figure 4B), where R2 was more than 0.85 with a high network connectivity. Finally, through the dynamic tree cut algorithm, a total of 18 gene co-expression modules were obtained (Figure 4C). Next, the correlation between different modules and CRG score was analyzed (Figure 4D). Notably, the light cyan module had a significant positive correlation and the strongest correlation with CRG score (r = 0.9, p = 2e-04). The midnight blue module was negatively correlated with CRG score. The light cyan module with the strongest positive correlation with DFU was selected for subsequent analysis, and the correlation between genes within the light cyan module and CRGs was further calculated (Figure 4E).

Protein-protein interaction network and single-cell transcriptomic integration analysis

Understanding the interactions between proteins is crucial for uncovering the molecular mechanisms underlying complex diseases such as DFU. The PPI network provides valuable insights into how proteins collaborate to regulate cellular functions such as wound healing, inflammation, and tissue repair. We intersected the differentially expressed CRGs with genes from the co-expression modules identified in our WGCNA, focusing on those most relevant to DFU. This approach resulted in a list of 55 DFU-associated differentially expressed CRGs. These genes were then mapped to the STRING database to identify protein interactions, which revealed a network comprising 42 proteins and 230 protein-protein interaction pairs (Figure 5A). The PPI network analysis highlighted several key proteins with high connectivity, suggesting their central roles in DFU pathology. Notably, NUP107, SMC2, MCM4, NUP133, and TMPO emerged as the top five hub genes within the network, based on their degree of connectivity and functional significance (Figure 5B). To contextualize the PPI-defined hub genes at the single-cell level, we further analyzed the GEO scRNA-seq dataset GSE231643.27 After rigorous quality control of the DFU single-cell data, dimensionality reduction and visualization were performed using t-SNE, which resolved 12 discrete clusters across the healing and non-healing groups (Figure 5C). We then used violin plots to examine the distribution of the five hub genes across cell populations. The results indicate that these genes are predominantly expressed in fibroblasts and epidermal/keratinocyte cells (Figures 5D–5H), cell types that are essential for diabetic wound healing. NUP107, a crucial component of the nuclear pore complex, regulates the transport of molecules between the nucleus and cytoplasm, playing an essential role in cell cycle progression and proliferation. In DFU, the reduced expression of NUP107 can hinder keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation, vital for tissue repair, thereby contributing to chronic non-healing ulcers. SMC2, a member of the structural maintenance of chromosomes family, is involved in chromosomal cohesion and DNA repair, particularly in response to oxidative stress and inflammation, both prevalent in DFU. The impaired expression of SMC2 can disrupt DNA repair mechanisms, delaying tissue regeneration and preventing wound closure. MCM4, as a component of the minichromosome maintenance complex, is crucial for DNA replication and cell cycle progression during the proliferative phase of wound healing. A decrease in MCM4 expression disrupts this process, leading to reduced keratinocyte proliferation and delayed wound closure, which is characteristic of non-healing DFU. NUP133, similar to NUP107, is involved in nuclear transport and regulates cellular responses to stress, including those induced by high glucose levels in DFU. The dysregulation of NUP133 impairs the inflammatory response and the resolution of chronic inflammation, contributing to persistent non-healing wounds. Lastly, TMPO, a nuclear membrane protein, interacts with various signaling pathways involved in inflammation and oxidative stress. High expression of TMPO in DFU may maintain a pro-inflammatory environment, impairing wound healing and contributing to chronic ulceration. Together, these genes highlight critical cellular mechanisms that influence the progression from healing to non-healing in DFU.

Diabetic foot ulcers-related ceRNA regulatory network

The ceRNA network represents a complex regulatory mechanism in which various RNA species, such as mRNAs, lncRNAs, and miRNAs, interact through shared microRNA response elements. This regulatory interplay plays a critical role in modulating gene expression and cellular functions, making it particularly relevant in diseases such as DFU, where gene regulation is disrupted. To explore the ceRNA network in DFU, we performed a comprehensive analysis to construct a regulatory network by integrating miRNA-mRNA and miRNA-lncRNA interactions.

Initially, we identified potential miRNA-mRNA interactions using the miRTarBase database (v8.0), which provides high-confidence miRNA-target gene associations. Next, the lncRNAs that the miRNAs might bind to were predicted with the help of the Starbase and LncBaseV2 databases. In order to make the results more reliable, the intersection of the prediction results of the two databases was taken. Finally, by integrating mRNA-miRNA interaction pairs and miRNA-lncRNA interaction pairs, a DFU-associated ceRNA regulatory network consisting of 19 miRNAs, 4 mRNAs, and 14 lncRNAs was obtained. It was visualized with Cytoscape software (Figure S3). The network has a total of 192 interaction pairs.

Correlation of hub genes

The alteration of hub genes in control and patients with DFU was analyzed. According to the correlation analysis, the correlation between the aforementioned hub genes was significantly altered in DFU (Figures S4A and S4B), with significant positive correlation in the expression levels between each hub genes, including in DFU, TMPO with NUP107 (r = 0.9, p < 2.2e-16) (Figure S4C) and SMC2 and NUP107 (r = 0.83, p = 0.0017) (Figure S4E) in DFU However, there was no significant correlation between the expression levels of the two in the control group (Figures S4D and S4F). This suggests that the expression pattern of the key genes has been altered in DFU and that they are important for DFU.

Molecular typing

To explore cuproptosis-associated subtypes of patients with DFU, patients were subtyped by unsupervised consistent clustering using expression profiling data of key DFU genes (Figure 6A and 6B). 3 distinct subtypes were identified, where cluster1 contained 1 DFU patient, cluster2 included 3 patients with DFU, and cluster1 contained 8 patients with DFU (Figure 6C). Due to the small number of samples in cluster1, cluster1 was not included in the subsequent analysis. By PCA analysis, it can be seen that clusters 2 and 3 can be clearly distinguished (Figure 6D). The expression levels of key genes in DFU expression levels were all significantly elevated in subtype 3 (Figure 6E and 6F).

Immune infiltration and immune-related factors

To explore the immune landscape of DFU cuproptosis subtypes, the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types were assessed across samples. The relative proportions of these immune cell subtypes are illustrated in a stacked bar graph (Figure 7A). Using the Wilcoxon test, a comparison of immune cell infiltration between DFU subtypes 2 and 3 revealed significantly lower levels of Macrophages M0 in subtype 3, suggesting their potential role in distinguishing DFU subtypes (Figure 7B). Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between activated NK cells and plasma cells (r = 0.66, p < 0.05) (Figure 7C). Subtype 3 exhibited significantly higher stromal and immune scores compared to subtype 2 (Figure 7D). Furthermore, key genes showed significant negative correlations with activated dendritic cells, neutrophils, and regulatory T cells (all p < 0.05), while displaying significant positive correlations with effector memory CD4+ T cells (all p < 0.05) (Figure 7E).

As shown in Figure S5A, the Wilcoxon test was used to compare immune cell infiltration levels between the control and DFU groups. Significant differences were observed in six immune cell types: memory B cells, CD8+ T cells, Macrophages M0, and activated dendritic cells were significantly elevated in patients with DFU, while Macrophages M1 and activated mast cells were notably reduced (Figure S5B). In recent years, multiple studies have demonstrated that the immune microenvironment of DFU exhibits a high degree of dynamic heterogeneity across different stages of pathogenesis, tissue locations, and infection statuses. During ulcer progression, the polarization state and proportion of macrophages may undergo dynamic adjustments in response to changes in the local microenvironment. In some chronic non-healing ulcers or under specific microenvironmental conditions, the proportion of M1 macrophages may even decrease—a finding that contrasts with reports from some previous literature indicating an increase in M1 macrophages in patients with DFU. This discrepancy suggests that the immune microenvironment in DFU is characterized by complex and dynamic alterations.28 Correlation analysis revealed a strong negative association between monocytes and resting mast cells (r = −0.76, p < 0.05) (Figure S5C). Using the ESTIMATE algorithm, stromal scores were found to be significantly lower in patients with DFU, while immune and ESTIMATE scores showed no significant changes (Figure S5D). Furthermore, ssGSEA analysis highlighted the association between key genes and immune cells in DFU. Key genes exhibited significant negative correlations with activated CD4+ T cells, activated dendritic cells, CD56dim natural killer cells, effector memory CD8+ T cells, gamma delta T cells, macrophages, memory B cells, regulatory T cells, and T helper type 17 cells (all p < 0.05). Conversely, they showed significant positive correlations with activated B cells, effector memory CD4+ T cells, and T helper type 2 cells (all p < 0.05) (Figure S5E). These results underscore the importance of immune cell infiltration and immune-related mechanisms in DFU pathogenesis, suggesting that targeting immune responses could enhance therapeutic outcomes.

Phenotypic experimental verification

Fibroblasts and HaCat cells play profound roles in the process of wound healing and tissue repair. Fibroblasts, as a crucial cell type during wound healing, are involved in the synthesis and repair of collagen, while HaCat cells, being epidermal cell lines, can reflect the process of epidermal repair.29,30 Therefore, we have selected these two cell types to investigate the role of key genes in wound healing, aiming to cover different aspects of the healing process. qRT-PCR indicated that the expressions of NUP107, SMC2, MCM4, and NUP133 genes in a normal glucose cell environment were higher than those in a high-glucose environment. The expression of TMPO in a high glucose cell environment was higher than that in a normal glucose environment (Figures 8A–8J). Further WB (Figures 8K–8L) and IF (Figures 8M–8N) also confirmed that the expression of TMPO in the high-glucose environment was higher than that in the normal control group, indicating that TMPO is a key target gene for the prediction and treatment of DFU. Additionally, IHC validation (Figure 8O) further confirmed that TMPO expression in diabetic foot ulcer tissues was significantly higher than in normal skin tissues, reinforcing its role in DFU pathogenesis.

Validation of the function of the cuproptosis-related gene thymopoietin

To explore whether Elesclomol combined with CuCl2 induces cell death under high-glucose conditions, HaCaT and fibroblast cells were exposed to varying concentrations of Elesclomol + CuCl2. Treatment resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability, with HaCaT and fibroblast cells showing over 50% viability loss at 30 nM and 20 nM Elesclomol, respectively (Figure 9A). Time-course experiments (0, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 h) further confirmed that cell viability declined progressively with prolonged treatment (Figure 9B). These findings indicate that Elesclomol + CuCl2 reduces cell viability in a dose- and time-dependent manner under high-glucose conditions. Additionally, cells were co-treated with TTM (a copper chelator inhibiting cuproptosis), Nec-1 (a necroptosis inhibitor), Ferrostatin-1 (a ferroptosis inhibitor), CQ (an autophagy inhibitor), and ZVF (a pan-caspase inhibitor) alongside Elesclomol + CuCl2 to evaluate their impact on cell viability. As depicted in Figures 9C and 9D, only TTM significantly restored cell viability, suggesting its specific role in counteracting Elesclomol + CuCl2-induced cell death. Furthermore, we assessed changes in ACO2 expression in DFU tissues and normal skin tissues using IHC. The results demonstrated that ACO2 protein expression was significantly downregulated in DFU tissues compared to normal skin tissues (Figure 9E). As ACO2 is a core component of the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle and a known target of cuproptosis, this marked reduction strongly suggests that cuproptosis occurs in the pathological microenvironment of human diabetic wounds. To further verify that cuproptosis indeed occurs under our experimental conditions, we assessed the Fe-S cluster protein ACO2 by western blot from HaCaT and fibroblast cells. Compared with the vehicle group, Elesclomol + Cu2+ markedly reduced ACO2 protein abundance in both cell types, whereas co-treatment with the copper chelator TTM partially restored ACO2 levels (Figure 9F). These findings provide direct evidence of a cuproptosis hallmark (loss of Fe-S cluster proteins) in DFU-relevant cells and corroborate the specificity of copper-dependent cell death inferred from the TTM rescue in viability assays.

To explore the role of TMPO in high-glucose conditions, stable TMPO knockdown cell lines were generated in HaCaT and fibroblast cells using shRNA plasmids. The efficiency of TMPO silencing was verified through RT-qPCR and western blot analysis (Figures 9G–9J). Functional assays, including CCK-8 and wound healing, demonstrated that TMPO knockdown significantly enhanced the proliferation and migration capabilities of both cell types (Figures 9K–9P). Previous KEGG pathway analysis had linked cuproptosis-related genes to the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. Western blot results further confirmed that TMPO downregulation activates the TGF-β/Smad pathway, influencing diabetic wound healing (Figure 9Q and 9R).

Inhibition of thymopoietin can accelerate diabetic wound healing in vivo

To evaluate the impact of TMPO on diabetic wound (DW) healing, we conducted in vivo experiments using mice treated with AAV-sh-TMPO to knock down TMPO expression. Wound healing was monitored over a 14-day period (Figure 10A). Compared to the DW group, TMPO silencing significantly accelerated wound healing (Figure 10B and 10C). To validate the efficiency of TMPO silencing in the db/db mouse model, we conducted qRT-PCR analyses on wound tissue samples harvested on day 14. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in TMPO mRNA levels in the AAV-TMPO group compared to the AAV-NC group (Figure 10D). Histological analysis revealed that the downregulation of TMPO increased microvascular density and collagen deposition in wound tissue, improved collagen fiber organization, increased the ratio of type I/III collagen, and enhanced epidermal thickness (Figure 10E). ELISA results indicated that TMPO silencing reduced levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) while elevating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Figure 10F–10I). These findings suggest that the high expression of TMPO and its associated pathways impede diabetic wound healing.

Discussion

In this study, we identified five key genes through bioinformatics analysis and subsequently demonstrated that TMPO is most closely associated with the development and progression of DFU via basic experiments. Upon knocking down TMPO, we observed that high-glucose conditions promote cell proliferation and migration, which may be achieved through the activation of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. Furthermore, our in vivo experiments revealed that TMPO knockdown improves epidermal thickness, promotes microvascular formation, enhances collagen fiber content, and reduces inflammatory response, ultimately accelerating DFU healing.

DFU is a serious condition that results from multiple contributing factors, primarily peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, foot infections, and immunosuppression.31 If left untreated, it may lead to amputation.32 Copper plays a critical role in various biological functions, including mitochondrial respiration, antioxidant defense, and the synthesis of biochemical compounds, and is closely linked to the severity and progression of DM.33 Elevated serum copper levels have been observed in patients with DM and in Streptozotocin-induced diabetic rat models.34 Molecular mechanisms by which oxidative stress affects diabetic ulcer healing continue to be discovered, and multiple of these mechanisms are caused by the mitochondrial overproduction of reactive oxygen species.35 Chronic hyperglycemia in patients with diabetes leads to increased mitochondrial ROS production, thus activating lipid peroxidation and causing cellular dysfunction through the destruction of macromolecules and death.36,37 The presence of copper ions may result in a redox imbalance where the antioxidant defenses of glutathione and superoxide dismutase are insufficient to fend against the harmful effects of ROS,38 thereby inducing an inflammatory response. In addition, a crucial characteristic of DFU is chronic inflammation, which can delay wound healing and cause tissue damage.39 Moreover, unstable copper levels have been associated with oxidative damage in the brains of patients with Alzheimer’s disease,40 particularly in vulnerable regions, where copper dysregulation correlates with neuronal loss and neuroinflammation.41 Therefore, it can be concluded that copper probably contributes to the onset of peripheral neuropathy in DFU.

While copper is biologically essential, it can also exhibit cytotoxicity. Excessive copper accumulation disrupts iron-sulfur clusters and generates harmful reactive oxygen species through the copper-driven Fenton reaction.42 To prevent detrimental intracellular copper buildup, cells employ dynamic homeostatic mechanisms that maintain copper concentrations at minimal levels. Tsvetkov et al. revealed that cuproptosis operates through a unique mechanism distinct from other forms of cell death, closely tied to mitochondrial metabolism.43 Copper plays vital roles in cardiovascular, neurological, and immune functions, but elevated intracellular levels are linked to various diseases, including anemia, cancer, liver and kidney disorders, heart disease, and brain damage. Its dual role in physiology and toxicity has also been exploited in cancer therapy.44 The specific mechanisms by which cuproptosis contributes to tissue damage and inflammation in DFU remain unclear, warranting further investigation. Early identification of cuproptosis-related biomarkers is crucial for understanding DFU pathophysiology and developing predictive and therapeutic strategies.

In this article, CRGs were first explored. After merging the expression profiling datasets GSE80178 and GSE68183 and eliminating batch effects, the data were normalized and standardized. ANOVA results suggest that the development of DFU may be significantly influenced by the CRGs. Furthermore, functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed, and the expression patterns of selected enriched genes were visualized. The results indicate that the development of DFU is closely associated with cellular proliferation, differentiation, energy metabolism, and tissue repair. These findings suggest that changes in DEGs may influence wound healing and chronic ulcer formation through distinct pathways, offering potential therapeutic targets for treatment. To explore the co-expression of genes in the expression profiles of patients with DFU, co-expression modules were established by utilizing the R package WGCNA. Clustering the samples and the dynamic shear tree algorithm, 18 gene co-expression modules were obtained. The analysis revealed that the strongest association between the CRG score and the light cyan module was a substantial positive correlation. In contrast, the CRG score was negatively correlated with the midnight blue module. To obtain the 5 DFU-related differentially expressed CRGs (NUP107, SMC2, MCM4, NUP133, TMPO), the differentially expressed CRGs with the genes in the module most relevant to CRG scores in WGCNA were intersected. These genes have been shown to be associated with cell differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammation or oxidative stress. In this study, qRT-PCR results revealed that the expressions of NUP107, SMC2, MCM4, and NUP133 genes were down-regulated, but the expression of TMPO was up-regulated in a high-glucose environment. In addition, WB, IF, and IHC also confirmed that the expression of TMPO was higher than that of normal controls in the high glucose environment. This indicates that TMPO can be used as important research targets for the future exploration of diabetic wound mechanisms.

TMPO is a protein associated with the inner nuclear membrane and a thymus hormone. TMPO is closely related to the inflammatory response, and the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of epigallocatechin are regulated by it.45 TMPO has also been reported to reduce neuroinflammation after ischemic stroke46 and to inhibit the activity of NLRP3 inflammasome to alleviate nephritis.47 Wound healing in diabetes is often difficult due to poor blood sugar control, microvascular disease, infection, and chronic inflammation. TMPO plays an essential part in regulating immune cell function, including affecting the polarization state of macrophages. Macrophages are vital for wound healing, and abnormal expression of TMPO can lead to excessive or persistent inflammatory response, thus delaying wound healing in diabetes. Moreover, patients with diabetes are often accompanied by increased oxidative stress, which is also an important factor affecting wound healing. TMPO may play a role in diabetic wound healing by regulating signaling pathways associated with cellular stress response and affecting the ability of cells to cope with oxidative stress.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the cuproptosis-related gene TMPO is notably involved in the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. Silencing TMPO significantly promoted cell proliferation and migration in a high-glucose model. Our findings suggest that high glucose conditions may induce cell-cycle arrest (e.g., in G1 or G2/M phases), which is alleviated upon TMPO knockdown, leading to an accelerated transition into the S phase and enhanced proliferation. High-glucose environments often increase inflammation and oxidative stress, which impair cell migration. By downregulating TMPO, we observed improved cell migration, likely due to the modulation of inflammation-related genes and reduced stress responses. Moreover, TMPO downregulation appears to facilitate diabetic wound healing by activating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. TGF-β signaling promotes fibroblast activation during the early stages of wound healing, stimulating extracellular matrix secretion. This pathway regulates the cell cycle and cytoskeletal dynamics, enhancing fibroblast migration and proliferation.48 In diabetic wound repair, TMPO knockdown may sustain TGF-β/Smad pathway activity, improving fibroblast migration and contributing to wound closure and tissue regeneration.49 Chronic inflammation in high-glucose conditions hinders diabetic wound healing, and TGF-β signaling can modulate immune cell polarization to reduce inflammation. Following TMPO knockdown, the TGF-β/Smad pathway activation may help resolve local inflammation and foster a more favorable healing environment.50 In vivo experiments showed that silencing TMPO via adeno-associated virus injection significantly accelerated wound healing compared to controls, with increased microvessel formation and collagen fiber alignment. Pro-inflammatory cytokine levels decreased, while the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was upregulated, consistent with our in vitro findings.

In summary, we identified five potential CRGs in DFU through bioinformatic analysis and ultimately determined TMPO as the key gene in this study via basic experimental validation. Knockdown of TMPO further activated the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, thereby promoting diabetic wound healing. These findings enhance our understanding of DFU and provide a new theoretical basis for potential future clinical applications in patients with DFU.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. While we have identified the cuproptosis-related gene TMPO as a potential therapeutic target for DFU through in vitro and in vivo experiments and explored its related signaling pathways, further functional research is required to fully understand the mechanisms of TMPO and its interactions with upstream and downstream molecules in DFU. This will be the focus of our future investigations.

Method details

Data acquisition and pre-processing

The gene expression datasets GSE8017851 and GSE68183 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database through the GEOquery package in R. GSE80178 comprised 12 foot skin samples, including 3 control and 9 DFU samples. Similarly, GSE68183 contained 6 foot skin samples, with 3 control and 3 DFU samples. Both datasets were utilized in this research. To ensure data consistency, the two datasets were combined, and batch effects were eliminated using the “removeBatchEffect” function from the limma package. Subsequently, the merged data underwent normalization and standardization processes.

Cuproptosis gene acquisition and differential analysis

The analysis incorporated 10 previously identified cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs): FDX1, LIAS, LIPT1, DLD, DLAT, PDHA1, POHB, MTF1, GLS, and CDKN2A.52,53 Based on the control and DFU group classifications within the dataset, differential expression analysis was conducted using the R package limma to identify differentially expressed CRGs. Genes exhibiting a logFC >0.58 and p < 0.05 were classified as up-regulated, while those with logFC < −0.58 and p < 0.05 were deemed down-regulated. To assess the enrichment levels of these gene sets, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the “GSVA” package in R, which calculates enrichment scores for each gene set across individual samples. Based on the median CRGs scores, DFU samples were divided into high and low CRGs score groups. Differential enrichment analysis was then applied to these groups using limma, with up-regulated CRGs defined as logFC >0.58 and adj p < 0.05, and down-regulated CRGs as logFC < −0.58 and adj p < 0.05.

Gene enrichment analysis

R package “clusterProfiler” was adopted for Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation analysis, pathway and disease enrichment analysis.54 Besides, the R package “pathview” was employed to visualize the results of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways with significant differences. GSEA was employed to explore differences in pathway and biological process activities across samples from the expression datasets.55 The “c2.cp.all.v7.0.symbols” gene set, obtained from the MSigDB database,56 was used for GSEA, with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25 indicating significant enrichment. Using the R package GSVA, pathway scores were computed for each sample based on their gene expression matrices through the ssGSEA method. Enrichment analysis was subsequently performed using the limma package57 to identify differentially active functions or pathways.

Identification of co-expression genes

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was utilized to identify co-expressed gene modules and investigate their associations with phenotypes, as well as to pinpoint core genes within the network.58 The optimal soft threshold, determined to be 13, was selected using the “pickSoftThreshold” function. A scale-free network was constructed based on this threshold, followed by the creation of a topological overlap matrix and hierarchical clustering. Modules containing at least 50 genes were dynamically identified, and their feature genes were calculated. Inter-module relationships were assessed using module eigengenes and hierarchical clustering, resulting in the identification of 18 distinct modules. Spearman correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the relationships between modules and their associations with clinical traits. Finally, genes from modules most closely linked to CRGs scores in DFU patients were intersected with differentially expressed CRGs to identify DFU-associated cuproptosis-related differential genes.

Building interaction networks

The STRING database integrates experimental data, text-mining results from PubMed abstracts, synthesized data from other databases, and predictions derived from bioinformatics methods. In this study, the STRING database was employed to build a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for DFU-related differentially regulated genes (DRGs) using a confidence coefficient of 0.15. The network was visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.8.2). Additionally, the cytohubba plugin in Cytoscape was utilized to analyze the PPI network, identifying the top 5 hub genes based on their connectivity and significance.59,60

Competitive endogenous RNAs network construction

The ceRNA mechanism constitutes a sophisticated gene regulatory paradigm wherein diverse RNA species (mRNAs, pseudogene transcripts, lncRNAs, miRNAs) engage in cross-regulatory interactions through shared miRNA response elements. This molecular interplay provides novel dimensional insights into transcriptome dynamics, enabling multidimensional interpretation of complex biological processes.

This investigation implemented a three-phase computational framework:

Phase I: miRNA-mRNA interaction mapping

miRTarBase (v8.0) was interrogated for high-confidence miRNA-target gene associations, establishing foundational regulatory pairs through sequence complementarity analysis.

Phase II: lncRNA-miRNA interaction profiling.

Starbase (v3.0) and LncBaseV2 prediction algorithms were concurrently employed to decode lncRNA-mediated miRNA sponging events, generating secondary interaction datasets.

Phase III: Integrative network modeling.

A tripartite ceRNA network topology was computationally reconstructed through systematic integration of miRNA-mRNA and miRNA-lncRNA interaction matrices, ultimately delineating key pathogenic pathways in diabetic foot ulcer progression.

Cluster analysis

Consensus Clustering is a technique designed to identify the optimal number of clusters and their composition within a dataset, such as microarray gene expression data. In this study, the R package “ConsensusClusterPlus” was applied to perform consistent clustering based on DFU key genes, enabling the classification of DFU patients into distinct subtypes. The clustering process involved setting the cluster range between 2 and 7, with 80% of the total samples resampled 1000 times. The algorithm used hierarchical clustering (clusterAlg = “hc”) with a Euclidean distance metric to ensure robust and reproducible results.

Immune infiltration correlation analysis

CIBERSORT employs an inverse convolution algorithm to analyze expression profiling data, enabling the estimation of immune cell proportions. In this study, the CIBERSORT online tool (https://CIBERSORT.stanford.edu/) was adopted to assess immune cell infiltration in both control and DFU groups. The distribution of immune cells was visualized with the help of boxplots generated with the R package “ggplot2.” Additionally, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was applied to quantify specific immune cell infiltration and immune response activity by calculating the enrichment scores of gene sets for each sample. Immune cell-related gene sets, sourced from literature, were analyzed using the “GSVA” package in R. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationship between key genes and immune cell infiltration, with results visualized through heatmaps created using “ggplot2.” Furthermore, ESTIMATE (Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumors using Expression data) was employed to calculate stromal, immune, and combined scores for each sample based on stromal and immune gene sets, providing insights into the tumor microenvironment.61,62

Cell culture

The human keratinocyte (HaCaT) and fibroblast cell lines were procured from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China) under sterile conditions. Cells were maintained under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and penicillin/streptomycin cocktail (Servicebio). Cellular propagation occurred in a 37°C humidified environment with 5% CO2 tension, where medium replacement was implemented at 48-h intervals. Subculture procedures were initiated when cellular confluence attained 70–80% density threshold. Male cell lines were used in this study. Cells were not specifically tested for pathogens but were maintained in standard, sterile cell culture conditions.

Animal models

Six C57BL/6 mice and eighteen db/db mice (aged 6–8 weeks, male and female) were acclimated for 1 week (mice were obtained from Zhuhai Baishitong Biotechnology Co., Ltd., animal identification number: 44822700058115). The db/db mice were randomly divided into PBS control group, AAV-NC group, and sh-TMPO group. After anesthesia, the dorsal hair of the mice was shaved, and a full-thickness wound with a diameter of 1 cm was created using a skin biopsy punch. Wound healing was photographed and evaluated on days 1, 7, and 14 after treatment. The wound healing rate was statistically analyzed using ImageJ software. On day 14, skin tissue samples were taken for histological examination, followed by euthanasia. Mice were housed in a controlled environment at a temperature of 22°C–24°C with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Animals had access to water and standard chow ad libitum. Male mice were primarily used in the study. Mice were tested and confirmed pathogen-free by the supplier. This experiment was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval number: [2024]067).

shRNA and lentivirus production and transfection

TMPO-specific shRNA constructs (VectorBuilder, China) were annealed into pLKO.1-puro backbone to generate gene-silencing plasmids. Plasmid sequences are cataloged in Table S1. Viral transduction followed manufacturer’s protocol: 1) Cell seeding in 6-well plates at optimized density, 2) 12-h incubation with viral particles, 3) PBS rinsing and DMEM replenishment, 4) 24-h puromycin selection (1 μg/mL) initiating stable cell line development.

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay

Cellular growth curves were established using CCK-8 assay (Biosharp). HaCaT/fibroblast suspensions were seeded in 96-well plates at 5×103/3×103 cells per well respectively. At defined intervals (0-72h), 10 μL CCK-8 solution was introduced per well followed by 2-h incubation (37 °C). Optical density quantification at 450 nm was performed using Multiskan FC system (ThermoFisher), with viability calculated as: (Experimental OD450/Control OD450) × 100%.

Wound-healing assay

Transfected and control cells were plated in a 6-well plate. Upon reaching approximately 90% confluence, a scratch wound assay was conducted using a 10 μL pipette tip to evaluate cell migration. Two hours before creating the scratch, the cells were incubated in serum-free medium containing 10 mg/mL mitomycin C to prevent cell proliferation. Images of the scratch were taken at 0, 24, and 48 h using phase-contrast microscopy, and the wound gap distances were quantified using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time PCR(qRT-PCR)

Nucleic acid extraction was conducted with TRIzol reagent following manufacturer specifications. Complementary DNA synthesis ensued using RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with quantitative amplification subsequently performed on an SYBR Green RT-PCR system (Takara Bio, Japan). Experimental controls included β-Actin as endogenous reference for data normalization. Transcript quantification employed the comparative threshold cycle method (2−ΔΔCt) for relative expression analysis. Custom-designed primer sequences are cataloged in Table S1.

Western blotting

Cellular protein extraction from HaCaT cells was performed with RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime Biotechnology). Quantitative analysis of lysates employed a BCA detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with 20 μg aliquots electrophoresed on 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes. Post-transfer processing included: 1) membrane blocking with 5% BSA, 2) primary antibody incubation (4°C, 16 h), and 3) room temperature secondary antibody treatment (60 min). Chemiluminescent signals were captured using an AI800 digital imager (General Electric), followed by densitometric quantification via ImageJ analysis platform. Antibody panels comprised: TMPO, TGF-β1, Smad2/3, p-Smad2/3 (all 1:1000; Affinity Biosciences), P21 (1:1000 Affinity; 1:2000 Abcam), ACO2 (1:1000; Signalway Antibody) and β-Actin (1:10,000; Abcam).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Samples were either preserved at −80 °C or promptly assessed using human ELISA kits to quantify IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-10 levels. The assays were performed following the manufacturer’s guidelines, and absorbance readings were taken at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). Results were averaged from a minimum of three independent experiments.

Immunofluorescence assay

Cellular specimens (HaCaT and fibroblast lineages) underwent immobilization in 4% paraformaldehyde (4°C, 30 min) with subsequent triple PBS rinses. Membrane permeabilization was achieved through 0.1% Triton X-100/TBST exposure (RT, 30 min), followed by nonspecific epitope blocking with 5% BSA under identical temperature conditions. Primary antibody incubation proceeded at 4°C overnight (16–18 h), succeeded by species-matched HRP-conjugated secondary antibody probing (goat anti-rabbit IgG, Affinity Biosciences, 1:500 dilution; RT, 60 min). Nuclear counterstaining utilized DAPI chromogen (Servicebio, 10 min), with final fluorescence signal acquisition performed on a Zeiss LSM confocal platform (Oberkochen, Germany) under standardized imaging parameters.

Immunohistochemistry

The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 4 μm thickness were prepared using a microtome for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. Primary antibodies used included TMPO polyclonal antibody (1:200, Affinity, USA) and CD31 polyclonal antibody (1:400, SAB Signal way Antibody, USA).

Masson staining

Masson’s trichrome staining was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions (Servicebio, China) to assess collagen deposition. Staining involved Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for nuclei, Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin for cytoplasm, and aniline blue for collagen. After staining, sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Images were acquired using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan), and collagen deposition was quantified with ImageJ software.

H&E staining

For hematoxylin-eosin staining protocol implementation, initial steps involved xylene-based deparaffinization followed by gradual ethanol dehydration. After rehydration through descending alcohol gradients, sections were counterstained through sequential immersion in Mayer’s hematoxylin solution (5–10 min) and 0.5% acid alcohol differentiation (30 s). Subsequent neutralization was achieved using 0.5% ammonium hydroxide solution prior to eosin counterstaining (2–3 min), with inter-step distilled water rinses maintained throughout the procedure.

Sirius red staining

Sirius Red Staining was employed for collagen fiber visualization. Tissue sections underwent 60-min immersion in saturated picric acid-Sirius red solution at room temperature. Post-staining protocol included three critical phases: 1) initial destaining in acid-alcohol solution (3 min), 2) progressive dehydration through ethanol series (80%–95%), and 3) xylene clearing for optical transparency enhancement. Mounted specimens in neutral resin were subjected to polarized light microscopy analysis using standardized imaging parameters.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0) and R (v4.3.1). Unless stated otherwise, data are presented as mean ± SEM from ≥3 independent experiments. The exact value of n and what n represents are specified in each figure legend. R programming is used for data processing and statistical analysis. Independent Student’s t test was used for normal distribution data, Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) was used for nonnormal distribution data, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for overall analysis of multiple samples.
