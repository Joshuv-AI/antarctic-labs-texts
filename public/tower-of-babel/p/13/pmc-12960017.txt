Evaluation of the Effect of Food and Formulation on the Pharmacokinetics of the SHP2 Inhibitor Migoprotafib Within a Phase I Study in Cancer Patients
Authors: Julia Suchomel, Christine M Bowman, Po‐Chang Chiang, Mallika Dhawan, Jennifer Eng‐Wong, Paloma Hauser, Ryan Johnson, Ariel R Muliadi, Swathi Pisupati, Vidya Ramakrishnan, Leonie Wagner, Sandhya V Mandlekar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMigoprotafib is a potent and selective inhibitor of Src homology‐2 domain‐containing phosphatase 2 (SHP2) under investigation in Phase I (PhI) trials both as monotherapy and in combination with multiple therapies for patients with metastatic solid tumors. The PhI study reported herein aimed to assess both a new tablet formulation and the impact of a high‐fat meal on the pharmacokinetics (PK) of migoprotafib at the recommended Phase II dose (RP2D) of 60 mg in patients. In two distinct cohorts, patients were administered migoprotafib as a tablet or capsule formulation to assess the impact of formulation, or as a tablet under fasted or fed conditions to assess the impact of food. In an evaluation of the effect of formulation in 20 subjects, the geometric mean ratios (GMRs) [90% CI] of the tablet: capsule formulation were 101 [89.8–114], and 103 [86.2–122], for AUC0‐∞ and C max, respectively. Consumption of a high‐fat meal prior to migoprotafib administration resulted in comparable AUC0‐∞ and reduced C max compared to fasted administration in 17 subjects, with GMRs [90% CI] of 92.2 [74.5–114], and 42.4 [30.6–58.9], for AUC0‐∞ and C max, respectively. These findings informed subsequent dosing recommendations for migoprotafib in ongoing and future clinical studies.Keywords: absorption, cancers, exposure response, formulation, pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Migoprotafib, a potent and selective allosteric small molecule inhibitor of Src homology‐2 domain‐containing phosphatase 2 (SHP2), has been investigated for its potential in treating advanced and metastatic solid tumors in multiple PhI trials, both as a monotherapy [1] and in combination therapies. SHP2 is involved in tumorigenesis via its role as a key mediator of rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (RAS)/mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway signaling. As a regulator of the MAPK pathway, SHP2 inhibition may counteract feedback activation mechanisms that lead to therapeutic resistance; therefore, migoprotafib has been evaluated in a PhI study (NCT04449874) in combination with the KRAS G12C inhibitor divarasib [2]. In addition to its tumor‐intrinsic regulation of growth factor signaling, SHP2 has been implicated in the regulation of immune responses such as immune cell signaling, cytotoxic T‐cell activation, and macrophage polarization, suggesting its potential to be used in combination with immunotherapy to enhance activity. As such, migoprotafib was evaluated in a PhI study (NCT05487235) in combination with atezolizumab (anti‐PDL1). With multiple tumor contexts in which the inhibition of SHP2 activity could be beneficial, migoprotafib is considered an appealing combination partner with both targeted and immune‐directed therapies to enhance their activity across a variety of tumor types.

Herein, we report the findings from two cohorts within the atezolizumab combination study, designed to evaluate the impact of formulation and food on the pharmacokinetics of a single 60 mg dose of migoprotafib in a subset of study participants. Prior to conducting this assessment, migoprotafib had been administered to patients exclusively as a capsule under fasted conditions. Consequently, a series of in vitro studies were conducted to anticipate the clinical outcomes of the assessments prior to administration of the new tablet formulation to patients under simulated fed and fasted conditions.

Dissolution tests were performed for both tablet and capsule formulations at pH 1.0, 4.5, and 6.8, as well as in fasted‐ and fed‐state simulated intestinal fluids. The studies showed no differences in dissolution profiles that were considered clinically significant. Additionally, the bioaccessibility, or fraction of both formulations that is released in the gastrointestinal tract and becomes available for absorption, was assessed using the TNO Gastrointestinal (Tiny‐TIM) [3] Model (TNO Triskelion, Netherlands), which simulates the human gastrointestinal transit. The bioaccessibility profiles of the tablet and capsule were found to be comparable when administered in the fasted state, suggesting minimal impact of formulation when administered in the clinic. In the fed‐state, the tablet formulation showed a less rapid increase in the bioaccessible fraction when compared to the capsule; however, this had no impact on the observed total bioaccessible amount measured within 5 h (the total run time of fasted conditions that mimic the upper human gastrointestinal transit). This difference suggested a potential reduction in maximum concentrations (C max) for fed compared to fasted subjects administered the tablet formulation; however, overall exposure was expected to be similar. Bioaccessibility profiles of the capsule were expected to be similar under both fasted‐ and fed‐state conditions. As the overall exposure of migoprotafib was expected to be comparable regardless of formulation and not predicted to increase if administered under fed rather than fasted conditions, the RP2D of 60 mg was selected for the assessment.

The findings from this clinical study enabled two key advancements for the migoprotafib development program. First, they validated a transition to a tablet formulation developed as a market formulation for future pivotal clinical studies; second, they provided important guidance on dosing recommendations for migoprotafib regarding food intake.

Methods

The study protocol and informed consent form were reviewed and approved by independent ethics committees or institutional review boards before initiation of any study‐related procedures, and the study comprised 31 activated sites in 7 countries, including Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, Korea, New Zealand, and the United States. The study was conducted in full conformance with good clinical practice as described in the International Council for Harmonization Guideline E6, Good Clinical Practice, and was consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki. All subjects provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

Overall Study Design Plan

A PhI, open‐label, multicenter study to evaluate the safety, PK, and efficacy of migoprotafib in combination with atezolizumab was conducted in patients with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors. The effect of formulation and food was assessed in a subset of two distinct cohorts of patients who participated in a two‐period, fixed‐sequence, 14‐day PK lead‐in period consisting of two single doses of migoprotafib with a 7‐day washout between each dose. Considering the minimal likelihood of a drug–drug interaction between migoprotafib and atezolizumab, all participants received the first dose of migoprotafib in combination with atezolizumab (840 mg, IV, q2w) on Day 1. Consistent with FDA guidance to reduce the impact of inter‐patient variability on study outcomes, a two‐treatment, fixed sequence study design was utilized for both assessments, with each patient functioning as their own control. Following completion of the PK lead‐in period, all patients transitioned to a regimen of migoprotafib QD (40 or 60 mg) in combination with atezolizumab (1200 mg q3w) on Day 1 of a 21‐day cycle.

To assess the effect of formulation on the PK of migoprotafib, patients were administered a single dose of migoprotafib (60 mg tablet), fasted, on Day 1 followed by administration of a single dose of migoprotafib (60 mg capsule), fasted, on Day 8, with a 7‐day washout period following each dose. Under fasted conditions, each dose was administered following a fast of at least 8 hours (h) from all food and drink (except water) prior to dose administration. Patients did not consume food for at least 4 h after the dose. An overview of the study design is shown in Figure 1A.

Study Schematic: A 2‐period, fixed‐sequence crossover cohort to assess the impact of (A) formulation and (B) food on the PK of migoprotafib. The 168‐h postdose PK sample was collected predose on Day 8 of the PK run‐in period for the control sequence, and predose on Day 1 of Cycle 1 for the test sequence.

To evaluate the effect of food on the PK of migoprotafib, patients were administered a single dose of migoprotafib (60 mg tablet) under fed conditions on Day 1, and under fasted conditions on Day 8, with a 7‐day washout period following each dose. For fed conditions, following a fast of at least 8 h, patients consumed a high‐fat meal 30 min before the drug administration, completing the meal within 30 min. Composition of the high‐fat breakfast meal was based on FDA recommendations for assessment of the impact of food on drugs [4]. Under fasted conditions, each dose was administered following a fast of at least 8 h from all food and drink (except water) prior to dose administration. Patients did not consume food for at least 4 h after the dose. An overview of the study design is shown in Figure 1B.

For both formulation effect and food effect assessments, each dose was administered with 240 mL of room‐temperature water. Plasma samples for PK analysis of migoprotafib were collected pre‐dose and 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 24, 48, 96, and 168 h following each dose.

Nonclinical studies indicate that CYP3A4 is the predominant enzyme involved in migoprotafib metabolism, and that migoprotafib may be a sensitive CYP3A4 substrate. Furthermore, the impact of acid‐reducing agents on migoprotafib exposure is unknown. As such, the concurrent use of CYP3A4 inhibitors, CYP3A inducers and acid‐reducing agents, including proton pump inhibitors, H2 blockers, and antacids, was prohibited during the PK lead‐in period for both the formulation and food‐effect assessments.

Bioanalyical Methods

The concentration of migoprotafib in human K2EDTA plasma samples was quantified using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry assay. The LLOQ was 2.0 ng/mL and the ULOQ was 2000 ng/mL, with migoprotafib‐d5 as the internal standard. In method validation (three passing precision‐and‐accuracy runs), inter‐run accuracy (bias) and precision (RSD) were determined at the LLOQ and at four other QC levels (low, low‐mid, medium, and high). Across all QC levels, inter‐run accuracy ranged from −11.5% to 3.9%, and inter‐run precision ranged from 2.8% to 12.1%. These results met the acceptance criteria of ±15% bias (±20% at the LLOQ) and ≤ 15% RSD (≤ 20% at the LLOQ). Plasma aliquots (25 μL) were processed by supported‐liquid extraction using water as the load solvent and ethyl acetate as the extraction solvent. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Fortis Diphenyl column (50 × 2.1 mm, 5 μm) with a gradient of 20 mM ammonium formate (MP A) and acetonitrile (MP B). The flow rate was 1.00 mL/min with MP B at 33% initially. From 0.20 to 1.10 min, MP B was increased to 70%. At 1.10 min, the flow rate was raised to 1.40 mL/min, and MP B was ramped to 97% by 1.25 min, then held until 2.30 min. From 2.30 to 2.60 min, MP B was decreased to 50% and the flow rate returned to 1.00 mL/min. At 3.00 min, MP B was reset to 33%. The total run time per injection was 3.2 min, and the typical retention time for both migoprotafib and the internal standard was 0.85 min. Detection was performed on a Sciex API 4500 mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in positive mode, monitoring the transitions m/z 455.3 → 294.1 for migoprotafib and m/z 460.3 → 299.1 for the internal standard. All samples were stored at −70°C and analyzed within the validated long‐term frozen stability period.

PK and Statistical Methods

Standard noncompartmental analysis methods were used to determine PK parameters for migoprotafib. PK and statistical analyses were conducted using Phoenix WinNonlin software (Certara, Version 8.5.2.4), and plasma concentrations and PK parameters were summarized using descriptive statistics by treatment. Statistical analyses were performed using the mixed model approach to determine the geometric least squares means (GLSMs), geometric mean ratios (GMRs), and corresponding 90% confidence intervals (CIs) of migoprotafib AUC0‐∞, AUC0‐t, and C max, with actual treatment as a fixed effect and subject as a random effect for test and reference periods to evaluate the effect of formulation and food on migoprotafib. Sample size of a minimum of 15 patients per assessment was based on precedent set by other PK studies [5] and was not based on power calculations; enrollment continued until a minimum of 15 patients completed both the control and test periods for each assessment.

Safety Analysis

Safety assessments occurred from screening through study completion. Safety and tolerability were assessed by evaluating the incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs), changes from baseline in clinical laboratory tests, vital signs, and 12‐lead electrocardiograms (ECGs). The AE severity grading scale for the NCI CTCAE (version 5.0) was used for assessing AE severity.

Summary of Adverse Events Observed in the PK Lead‐In Formulation and Food Effect Assessment

A summary of treatment related adverse events that were determined by the investigator to be study‐drug related during the 14‐day PK lead‐in period is provided in Table S2. Of the 26 subjects enrolled in the formulation effect assessment, 14 (53.8%) reported at least one AE related to study treatment, including 3 treatment related serious AEs in two subjects (neutrophil count decreased, platelet count decreased and nausea). Among the 19 subjects enrolled in the food effect assessment, 11 (57.9%) experienced AEs related to study treatment with no serious AEs reported. Overall, the safety profiles observed in both the formulation and food effect assessments were consistent with the known individual safety profiles of migoprotafib and atezolizumab, and no new safety signals were identified.

Discussion

PK studies conducted in healthy subjects offer significant logistical and scientific advantages. They allow for long washout periods without the ethical concern of withholding beneficial care and provide a fixed, highly controlled environment that minimizes variability. Despite these benefits, conducting PK assessments in cancer patients [5], as opposed to healthy subjects, is often necessary and offers distinct advantages, including the opportunity to potentially capture real‐world variability in drug absorption and tolerability attributable to disease‐ and treatment‐related factors. While the maximum recommended dose level for advanced cancer patients is determined by therapeutic benefit relative to acceptable risk, a subtherapeutic dose may be necessary in healthy subjects for safety reasons. At the time of study conduct, subtherapeutic dose levels would have been necessary for a healthy subject study, as variability observed in the clinical first in human (FIH) study for the capsule formulation was high, available safety data [1] were insufficient to support administration of clinically relevant doses, and the novel tablet formulation had not yet been evaluated in humans. In the previously conducted FIH study [1], among the 25 patients administered migoprotafib as a single agent at the RP2D of 60 mg QD, the most common grade ≥ 3 treatment‐related adverse events were diarrhea, fatigue, and thrombocytopenia. Fifteen patients (60%) at this dose level required AE‐related dose interruptions. Therefore, considering the AEs observed at this dose level and the need to evaluate a clinically relevant dose, it was deemed appropriate to conduct this study in patients rather than healthy volunteers. A study design where patients received both the test and control treatments was selected to mitigate the confounding effect of moderate to high inter‐subject PK variability observed for migoprotafib in the FIH study while also enabling, for the first time, an evaluation of intra‐subject variability. As terminal elimination half‐life had not been previously assessed in the FIH study due to insufficient washout times (a maximum of 48 h between doses), the PK lead‐in design included a 7‐day washout period that was sufficient to assess a geometric mean half‐life ranging from 27 to 36 h for both assessments.

However, this approach also presents significant challenges and increased complexity compared to studies conducted in healthy subjects, as an extended PK lead‐in period prior to initiating the intended daily dosing regimen could postpone the receipt of beneficial care for the patients. To mitigate this risk, this study evaluated the effect of formulation and food on migoprotafib in combination with an approved IV dose of atezolizumab, 840 mg q2w. To ensure documentation of patient eligibility, the timing of the fast and the consumption of the meal were recorded, and a threshold for completion of the high‐fat meal was also noted. Despite these precautions, 8 subjects (approximately 18% of the 45 subjects enrolled in the combined food and formulation assessments) were excluded due to protocol deviations related to fasting status or incorrect administration of the dose or formulation. To optimize compliance in patient‐based studies, it is advisable to maintain clear documentation of compliance and ensure significant, ongoing communication with clinical sites to continuously assess patient eligibility and achieve target enrollment.

Prior to evaluating the impact of formulation and food in this study, dosing recommendations for migoprotafib stipulated fasted administration of the capsule formulation, and the tablet formulation had not been previously administered to humans. Systemic exposures observed with migoprotafib were consistent across both formulations, demonstrating comparable bioavailability. These findings supported a transition from the capsule formulation used in the FIH study to the tablet formulation for all ongoing and future clinical investigations of migoprotafib.

Findings from the food effect assessment demonstrated that administering migoprotafib with a high‐fat meal led to a modest decrease of approximately 8%–9% for AUC0‐∞ and AUC0‐t compared to the fasted state, an effect unlikely to be clinically meaningful based on the known migoprotafib exposure‐response relationship [6]. However, a substantial 58% reduction in C max was also observed under fed conditions. As the clinical development of migoprotafib has focused primarily on combination regimens exploring diverse mechanisms of action, the clinical significance of a reduction in C max was evaluated for each combination individually. When efficacy and safety are driven by total drug exposure, the observed food effect is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. However, in scenarios where therapeutic outcomes may potentially depend on peak concentrations, further investigation, such as evaluation of a standard versus high‐fat meal or fasting duration, would be warranted, as a high‐fat meal represents the worst‐case scenario and may not be as relevant as a standard meal in real‐world settings. As study outcomes were consistent with the Tiny‐TIM predictions described earlier, PBPK modeling could be considered for evaluation of the effect of a standard rather than high‐fat meal.

Accordingly, in combination with divarasib, a KRAS G12C inhibitor, an exposure–efficacy analysis [6] demonstrated no correlation between C max and efficacy, indicating that the reduced C max observed with food is not clinically relevant in this setting. Consequently, dosing recommendations for migoprotafib when administered with divarasib have since been revised from requiring fasting to permitting administration regardless of food intake.

In conclusion, this study comprehensively characterized the effect of formulation and food on the PK of migoprotafib. The results demonstrate that the capsule and tablet formulations exhibit comparable bioavailability. Furthermore, although administration with food leads to a significant reduction in C max, overall systemic exposure remains unchanged. Results from this study supported the transition from capsules to tablets in ongoing and future clinical studies of migoprotafib, and dosing recommendations.
