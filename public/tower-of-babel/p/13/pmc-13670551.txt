Non-pharmacological preoperative interventions for the prevention of emergence delirium in children under general anesthesia: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Xiyao Zhang, Shan Dai, Qian Li, Huaping Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmergence delirium (ED) is among the most distressing complications of general anesthesia. Although numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions in managing pediatric ED, the relative efficacies of these interventions remain unclear. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the efficacies of different non-pharmacological preoperative interventions to prevent ED in children undergoing general anesthesia.MethodsThe Embase, Cochrane Library, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases were searched from their inception to December 31, 2025, to identify relevant randomized controlled trials of non-pharmacological preoperative interventions to prevent ED in children undergoing general anesthesia. Two reviewers independently evaluated the literature, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias. STATA 18.0 software was used for network meta-analysis. Network and contribution plots were generated, inconsistency tests were performed, and interventions were ranked by efficacy using the surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) curve.ResultsEleven randomized controlled trials that involved 1,294 children were included. Network meta-analysis showed that compared to usual care, multimodal preoperative anxiety intervention (odds ratio [OR] = 0.08, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.02 to 0.45), preoperative 30-min eye patch training (OR = 0.10, 95%CI: 0.05 to 0.22), and preoperative operating room visitation (OR = 0.10, 95%CI: 0.03 to 0.38) significantly reduced the incidence of ED. SUCRA ranking indicated that the multimodal preoperative anxiety intervention (SUCRA = 85.5%) was likely to perform better in preventing ED, while virtual reality ranked lowest. The inconsistency tests showed no significant inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence (P = 0.09). Considerable heterogeneity was present (I2 = 68.7%), which could not be explained by prespecified moderators.ConclusionsAmong the included interventions, the multimodal preoperative anxiety intervention showed the most favorable effect in reducing ED, although this finding is based on a single trial and should be considered exploratory. Preoperative operating room visits and eye-patch training have also demonstrated promising applicability under specific conditions. Based on the current evidence, virtual reality showed no significant benefit in this analysis, and no firm recommendation can be made. Further high-quality studies are warranted to validate and refine personalized intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04052-y.

Introduction

Emergence delirium (ED) is a distressing complication of pediatric anesthesia, and is defined as a disturbance in awareness and attention to the surrounding environment that occurs within 45 min of recovery from anesthesia in children [1]. It is characterized by disorientation, perceptual alterations, hypersensitivity to stimuli, and psychomotor agitation, including excessive sensitivity to stimulation and hyperactive motor behavior during the immediate postanesthetic period [2–4]. Compared with intravenous anesthesia, inhalational anesthesia is associated with a higher risk of ED [5], particularly when sevoflurane is used, with a reported incidence ranging from 10% to 80% [6]. During episodes of agitation, children may exhibit adverse behaviors such as kicking, screaming, and body thrashing, which may result in wound dehiscence, airway obstruction, falls from the bed, accidental extubation, and other undesirable outcomes [7, 8]. Although ED in children is typically transient, evidence suggests that its effects can persist beyond the immediate recovery period, leading to postoperative behavioral changes such as prolonged hospitalization, sleep disturbances, and separation anxiety [9–11]. Furthermore, ED may cause significant anxiety and psychological stress in parents and family members [12].

Currently, pharmacological interventions are the mainstay of clinical strategies to reduce the incidence of pediatric ED. Agents such as dexmedetomidine, midazolam, ketamine, and fentanyl are reportedly effective in mitigating pediatric ED [6, 13–17]. However, the use of pharmacological interventions is accompanied by dose-dependent adverse effects, including respiratory depression, delayed emergence, bradycardia, and hemodynamic fluctuations [18–21]. Consequently, identifying safe, effective, inexpensive, and easily implemented non-pharmacological interventions has emerged as a crucial direction for research.

The exact pathophysiological mechanisms underlying ED remain incompletely understood; however, a prevailing body of research has identified preoperative anxiety as a key predictor of its occurrence [22–25]. Accordingly, non-pharmacological preoperative interventions aim to mitigate anxiety through psychological, cognitive, and behavioral preparation. Common strategies include preoperative video distraction [26], parental presence [27], visual preconditioning [28], virtual reality (VR) [29], and preoperative tours of the operating room [30]. These interventions divert the children’s attention away from the unfamiliar and threatening medical environment, thereby enhancing their sense of familiarity. Although non-pharmacological preoperative interventions offer potential cost-effectiveness and suitability for routine clinical care [31, 32], it remains unclear which specific interventions are most effective in preventing pediatric ED.

Network meta-analysis, as an advanced evidence synthesis method, allows the simultaneous integration of direct and indirect comparative evidence, thus establishing connections between studies that lack direct comparisons and allowing the ranking of all interventions within a single network based on their efficacies [33]. Using this technique, this study systematically evaluated the effectiveness of various non-pharmacological preoperative interventions, compared to usual care or other interventions, in reducing the incidence of ED after general anesthesia in individuals aged < 18 years.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines. The study strictly adhered to the items in the PRISMA checklist. The network meta-analysis (NMA) was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on December 14, 2025 (registration number: CRD420251272506).

Literature search and search strategy

Five electronic databases were searched: Embase, Cochrane Library, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search period spanned from the inception of each database until December 31, 2025. A search strategy combining Medical Subject Headings terms and free-text words was employed. Boolean logic was used with search terms that included “delirium”, “agitation”, “children”, and “pediatric”. We also manually searched the reference lists of the existing literature to identify other potentially eligible studies. The detailed search strategy is summarized in Supplementary Material 1.

Inclusion criteria and study selection

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) participants less than 18 years of age; (2) only non-pharmacological interventions; (3) control groups receiving usual care; (4) outcome measures including the incidence of ED; (5) study type restricted to randomized controlled trials; and (6) articles published in the English language. Studies were excluded if they (1) involved pharmacological approaches; (2) included children diagnosed with mental disorders; and (3) were observational studies, systematic reviews, posters, or abstracts.

Selection of samples and data extraction

Two reviewers (ZXY and DS) independently performed the literature search, selection, and data extraction. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (LQ) until a consensus was reached prior to inclusion. For data extraction, the identified articles were imported into EndNote 21.0 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) by two reviewers (ZXY and DS) for duplicate detection. After removing duplicate records, studies were selected based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Two reviewers (ZXY and LQ) extracted the data and entered them into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). The extracted data included the following: first author, publication year, country where the study was conducted, sample size, patient age, type of surgery, and ED assessment tool used.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (ZXY and DS) independently assessed the risk of bias for the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool version 2.0 (RoB2) [34]. This tool evaluates bias in five specific domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. For each domain, studies are judged to have a “low risk of bias,” raise “some concerns,” or have a “high risk of bias.” In cases where disagreements arose between the two reviewers’ assessments, a third reviewer (LQ) was consulted to verify the results. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the three reviewers until a consensus was reached.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using STATA 18 software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). The primary outcome was the incidence of ED, expressed as an odds ratios (OR). A random-effects model was used for all network meta-analyses. Initially, to visually illustrate the comparative relationships among different non-pharmacological interventions, a network evidence plot was generated using STATA 18.0 software. In the network plot, each node represents a specific intervention, with the node size corresponding to the total sample size for that intervention. The lines connecting the nodes indicate the presence of studies with direct comparisons, and the thickness of these lines reflects the number of studies that contributing to each direct comparison. To evaluate the contribution of each direct comparison to the overall estimates of the network meta-analysis, a contribution plot was generated. This plot clearly displays the weight contributed by each direct comparison in the grouping of each effect size.

An inconsistency test was conducted to assess the agreement between direct and indirect evidence. Inconsistency was evaluated locally by calculating the inconsistency factor, its 95% confidence interval (CI), and the corresponding p-value. If the inconsistency factor is approximately 0, its 95% CI includes 0, and P > 0.05, this indicates no significant inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons. If inconsistency is detected, the node-splitting method is used to assess the local inconsistency for each study.

An effectiveness ranking of each intervention was performed. The surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) curve was calculated to obtain the probability that each intervention would be ranked at a specific position. SUCRA values range from 0% to 100%, and higher values generally suggest a higher probability that the intervention achieves better efficacy. Additionally, cumulative ranking probability plots were generated to visualize the ranking results.

Finally, to assess potential publication bias, a comparison-adjusted funnel plot was planned for any comparison involving more than ten studies. Symmetry in the funnel plot, with scattered points generally evenly distributed around the zero line, suggests a low risk of publication bias. Additionally, confidence in the evidence was evaluated using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework [35]. The CINeMA framework assesses six key domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Each domain is rated as having “no concerns”, “some concerns”, or “major concerns”. For each treatment comparison, an overall judgment was made across all domains, with confidence ratings of high, moderate, low, or very low.

Results

Search results

A total of 6,258 articles were identified using the five databases: PubMed (n = 293), Cochrane Library (n = 1,468), Embase (n = 1,472), Scopus (n = 1,235), and Web of Science (n = 1,790). After removing duplicates, 3,221 articles remained. After the review of titles and abstracts, 49 articles were selected for full-text review. After excluding 38 articles based on full-text evaluation, 11 randomized controlled trials investigating non-pharmacological preoperative interventions were ultimately included. Characteristics of the included studies

This study included 1,294 children (693 boys). Regarding geographical distribution, all 11 studies were conducted in Eastern countries, including China, Thailand, and South Korea. The Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium Scale (PAEDS) was used to assess the incidence of ED in 90.9% of studies. The types of surgeries included tonsillectomy, hernia repair, neck surgery, ophthalmic surgery, and otorhinolaryngological surgery, among others. The multimodal preoperative anxiety intervention incorporated a range of strategies, including anxiety reduction, age-appropriate distraction (e.g., various cartoon videos and video games), enhancement of postoperative adaptation (e.g., respiratory training and eye-covering pretreatment training), and preoperative provision of parental support and companionship. Risk of bias-assessment

The methodological quality of most included studies was acceptable, and the overall risk of bias was within a manageable range. Among the 11 included studies, seven were rated as having “some concerns”, primarily due to the inability to blind participants and personnel to the intervention. Consequently, the risk-of-bias assessment across the five domains for all 11 studies resulted in five studies being judged to have “some concerns”, whereas six studies were judged to have a “low risk of bias”, The risk-of-bias assessment is summarized in Supplementary Materials 2.

Network plot

The network plot of non-pharmacological preoperative interventions is presented in Fig. 2, which illustrates the evidence network for strategies to reduce preoperative anxiety in children. The network comprises 11 nodes, each representing a distinct preventive strategy, with multiple direct comparisons between the nodes. Usual care and parental presence are the most frequently connected nodes, indicating their common roles as controls or comparators within the evidence network. Other frequently compared interventions include video distraction and its various combinations with parental presence (e.g., tablet-based video distraction and visual preconditioning video distraction). In addition, interventions such as VR, preoperative operating room visits, breathing training, and eye mask covering (applied for 30- and 60-min durations) are also integrated into the network, demonstrating their inclusion in comparative effectiveness studies. The network plot shows good overall connectivity, which supports a comprehensive comparison of the effects of various interventions for managing preoperative anxiety in children.

Effectiveness of non-pharmacological preoperative interventions

The comparative effectiveness of non-pharmacological preoperative interventions is presented in Table 2. The forest plot presented in Fig. 3 displays the study effect sizes for pairwise comparisons among various non-pharmacological interventions. Compared to usual care, multimodal preoperative anxiety interventions (OR = 0.08, 95%CI: 0.02 to 0.45), 30-min preoperative eye-covering for children undergoing ophthalmic surgery (OR = 0.10, 95%CI: 0.05 to 0.22), and preoperative operating room tours (OR = 0.10, 95%CI: 0.03 to 0.38) significantly reduced the incidence of ED in children undergoing general anesthesia. VR (OR = 1.14, 95%CI: 0.46 to 2.83) demonstrated the least favorable effect on reducing the incidence of ED in this population. The wide confidence intervals for certain comparisons indicate substantial uncertainty, and all estimates should be interpreted cautiously. According to the cumulative ranking probabilities and ranking order, the multimodal preoperative anxiety intervention (SUCRA = 85.5%) is optimal for preventing ED in pediatric patients undergoing general anesthesia, as shown in Fig. 4. Inconsistency test results

To evaluate the consistency between direct and indirect evidence within the network meta-analysis, a local inconsistency test was performed. The results did not reach statistical significance (χ² = 4.79, df = 2, P = 0.0911), indicating that no significant local inconsistency was detected within this constructed network of non-pharmacological interventions for the prevention of ED in children.

Publication bias

This study used Egger’s test combined with a funnel plot to assess publication bias in the network meta-analysis results, which included 11 studies and 25 effect sizes, thereby verifying the reliability of the findings. Based on the distribution characteristics of the funnel plot -constructed with the effect sizes on the horizontal axis and the standard errors of the effect sizes on the vertical axis - the scatter points of the effect sizes of the individual studies were distributed on both sides. Although the overall scatter points did not exhibit an absolutely symmetrical inverted funnel shape, there was no pronounced clustering on one side or trend of extreme deviation, and no significant absence of small-effect studies was observed, suggesting the absence of obvious small-study effects. Combined with Egger’s test results (P = 0.157 [> 0.05]), this indicates absence of significant publication bias in this network meta-analysis, and the risk of publication bias between the included studies is low. However, with only 11 studies, the analysis is underpowered. The absence of significant asymmetry should be interpreted with caution, and publication bias cannot be ruled out. The funnel plot is summarized in Supplementary Materials 3.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess whether the choice of assessment tool influenced the primary findings, we conducted a prespecified sensitivity analysis by excluding the only study that used the Nursing Delirium Screening Scale (NU-DESC) and re-running the network meta-analysis using only studies that employed the Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED) scale. The results showed that after excluding this study, the overall heterogeneity in the network decreased from 68.7% to 63.7%; the P-value for the global inconsistency test remained 0.09, indicating no significant inconsistency. The effect estimate for the multimodal preoperative anxiety intervention (MPAI) compared to usual care (OR = 0.08, 95%CI: 0.02 to 0.45) and its top ranking (SUCRA remained first) did not change substantially. In summary, the core conclusion of this study—that MPAI is the most effective non-pharmacological intervention for reducing the incidence of emergence delirium in children—is robust. Detailed results are summarized in Supplementary Materials 4.

Certainty of evidence

On synthesizing the CINeMA assessments across the five core domains for all pairwise comparisons in the network meta-analysis (including both direct and indirect evidence), the overall quality ranged from moderate to low. Specifically, among the 66 comparisons, none (0%) was judged as high quality, 50 (75.8%) as moderate quality, and 16 (24.2%) as low quality. Most comparisons were of moderate quality, indicating that the comparative efficacy results are generally reliable but warrant cautious interpretation of low-quality evidence (e.g., comparisons involving VR or certain multimodal interventions). Further large-sample, well-designed studies are needed to improve the evidence base, particularly for comparisons currently rated as low or moderate. The CINeMA assessment results for all comparisons are summarized in Supplementary Materials 5.

Meta-regression analysis

Exploratory univariate meta-regression analyses were conducted to examine whether publication year, mean age, surgery type, intervention category, or ED assessment scale could explain the observed between-study heterogeneity (𝐼2 = 68.7%). Given the limited number of included studies (n = 11), these analyses should be interpreted with caution because of insufficient statistical power. No covariate reached statistical significance (all P > 0.05). Specifically, publication year was not significantly associated with treatment effect (coefficient = -0.166, P = 0.081), and neither was mean age (coefficient = 0.542, P = 0.213). Surgery types categorized as ENT, mixed, or adenoid surgery showed no significant association with ED incidence (joint test, P = 0.092). Intervention type (video-based, eye-covering, operating room visit, and multimodal) did not significantly influence the pooled effect (joint test, P = 0.854). The type of delirium assessment scale (PAEDS vs. Nursing Delirium Screening Scale [NU-DESC]) also did not account for the heterogeneity (P = 0.188). A multivariable meta-regression adjusting for age and surgery type simultaneously yielded consistent findings (joint test, P = 0.119). The non-significant findings may reflect either a true absence of effect or inadequate power to detect existing associations.

Discussion

This network meta-analysis systematically evaluated the preventive effects of 11 non-pharmacological preoperative interventions on ED in children undergoing general anesthesia, including 11 randomized controlled trials involving 1,294 children.Compared to usual care, multimodal preoperative anxiety interventions, 30-min preoperative eye-covering, and preoperative operating room tours significantly reduced the incidence of ED in children. Based on the SUCRA classification, the multimodal intervention appeared to be the most promising strategy for preventing ED, whereas VR ranked the lowest among the included options, suggesting it may be less effective than usual care in this context.Given that several interventions (e.g., multimodal, VR, breathing training, PVOR) are based on single small trials, the ranking results require replication in independent samples before definitive conclusions can be drawn.

ED occurrence is highly correlated with preoperative anxiety. Therefore, non-pharmacological interventions that effectively alleviate preoperative anxiety are theoretically more likely to reduce the incidence of ED. Multimodal interventions integrate various strategies, such as psychological counseling, visual distraction, and behavioral guidance, which may more comprehensively address multiple dimensions of childhood anxiety, enhance their adaptability to unfamiliar environments and psychological sense of security, and consequently result in better emotional stability during the emergence period. Eye-covering may simulate the postoperative visual state, allowing children to adapt in advance and reducing agitation caused by perceptual abnormalities during emergence. Preoperative visits to the operating room reduce unfamiliarity through environmental familiarization, alleviating preoperative anxiety and decreasing the appearance of ED.

In contrast, VR demonstrated no significant advantages in this study. Although VR technology offers high immersion and engagement and is theoretically effective for distracting children, its effectiveness may be limited by factors such as unfamiliarity with the equipment, inadaptability of content to children’s cognitive levels, or inappropriate timing of use. Some children may experience anxiety or fear during the VR experience, which could increase the preoperative psychological burden and weaken the interventional effects. Furthermore, the operational complexity of VR devices and individual differences may pose challenges to their widespread application in pediatric pre-anesthesia interventions.

Compared with pharmacological interventions, non-pharmacological techniques may not achieve the same magnitude of effect as drugs such as dexmedetomidine or midazolam [6, 13–17].Their main advantage over pharmacological interventions lies in the absence of dose-dependent adverse effects, including respiratory depression, delayed emergence, and bradycardia [18–21].This renders them particularly suitable for children with mild-to-moderate anxiety or as adjuncts to pharmacological interventions. Studies have confirmed the short-term anxiety-relieving effects of single interventions, such as preoperative video distraction [26] and parental presence [27].

Several factors may explain the considerable heterogeneity (I² = 68.7%) observed in this network meta-analysis. These include variability in intervention implementation, timing of ED assessment, anesthetic protocols, unmeasured baseline anxiety, and within-region practice variations. Due to the limited number of included studies (n = 11), our meta-regression lacked sufficient power to systematically identify these sources (all P > 0.05). Future research with larger sample sizes and standardized reporting is warranted.

From a clinical practice perspective, multimodal interventions, which offer comprehensiveness, broad applicability, and flexible implementation, can be recommended as routine preoperative strategies for pediatric anesthesia. Although preoperative operating room visits have demonstrated significant effectiveness, their application is constrained by medical resource allocation and infection control requirements. Although eye-covering has pronounced effects in ophthalmic surgery, its applicability is limited to specific patient populations. Our finding that virtual reality (VR) ranked lowest diverges from a recent larger network meta-analyses [36], which found that VR significantly reduced preoperative anxiety (SMD = -1.14, 95% CI: -1.54 to -0.74) and postoperative pain, meanwhile, a randomized trial [37] reported that a VR-based educational program reduced emergence delirium (PAED score: 1 vs. 3 points; P = 0.001). The discrepancy likely reflects that our VR estimate was based on a single small trial (n = 80), whereas larger analyses synthesized multiple studies with greater power. Moreover, VR effectiveness may depend on implementation quality—including content design, exposure duration, equipment familiarity, and age-appropriateness. Thus, the negative signal in our analysis should not be interpreted as definitive evidence against VR efficacy but rather highlights the need for adequately powered trials with standardized protocols.

Conclusions

Multimodal preoperative anxiety interventions are currently the most effective non-pharmacological strategy to prevent ED in children undergoing general anesthesia. Although considered a promising strategy, it requires further validation in larger, multicenter studies before routine implementation. Preoperative operating room visits and eye-covering also demonstrate promising applicability under specific conditions. Based on current limited evidence (one small trial showing no significant benefit), virtual reality cannot be recommended as a routine preventive measure, but its potential may depend on implementation quality and requires further investigation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, all included trials were conducted in East Asian countries (China, South Korea, and Thailand). Cultural factors—such as a greater acceptance of parental presence during anesthesia induction in collectivist societies—may limit the generalizability of our findings to Western or other non-East Asian populations. Second, the number of included studies was small, and interventions such as breathing training and virtual reality were supported by only one study each, resulting in low evidence strength. The limited number of studies also precluded subgroup analyses by surgical type, age group, or anesthetic technique, preventing adjustment for potential confounders. Future large-sample, multicenter, double-blind, randomized controlled trials using standardized assessment tools and diagnostic criteria for emergence delirium are needed. Aspects of virtual reality (e.g., content design, timing, device adaptability) require further optimization. Finally, the non-significant results for potential moderators should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no association; larger studies are warranted to examine whether age, surgical type, or anesthetic protocol meaningfully modify the preventive effects of non-pharmacological interventions on ED.
