Glyphosate Adsorption on Metal–Organic Framework M3(BTC)2: Mechanistic Insights and Descriptors for the Adsorption Energy from Density Functional Calculations
Authors: Araya Putthabal, Siriya Sukhonthachat, Michael Probst, Anawat Thivasasith, Jumras Limtrakul, Thana Maihom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Adsorption-based removal has emerged as an effective strategy for mitigating the harmful impact of glyphosate. Here, the adsorption of glyphosate over the coordinatively unsaturated metal–organic framework (MOF) M3(BTC)2 (M = V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn) was investigated by means of density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Among the studied MOFs, Zn3(BTC)2 and Ni3(BTC)2 show the highest adsorption energy. The Lewis acidity of the MOFs, as determined by ammonia adsorption, is found to significantly influence glyphosate adsorption. Decomposing the adsorption free energy reveals that the adsorption is dominated by the bond energy. A moderately strong linear correlation between the adsorption energies of glyphosate and ammonia suggests that ammonia adsorption can serve as a descriptor for predicting the glyphosate adsorption energy. To enhance the predictive accuracy, we employed the sure independence screening and sparsifying operator (SISSO) method, which explores a large pool of mathematical combinations of DFT descriptors to identify the most relevant ones. The resulting models enable accurate and transferable prediction of glyphosate adsorption energy.

Introduction

Glyphosate (N-phosphonomethyl-glycine) is one of the most widely used nonselective herbicides, valued for its effectiveness in controlling weeds across a broad range of crops. − However, its excessive agricultural use has raised concerns over the contamination of soil, aquatic environments, and agricultural products, posing significant risks to both environmental and human health. , Therefore, the removal of residual glyphosate to reduce its negative impact is crucial for both environmental protection and food safety. Several techniques have been explored to this aim, including membrane separation, biodegradation, oxidation, and adsorption. − Among these, adsorption is particularly promising due to its ease of operation, high efficiency, and overall safety. −

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), a class of porous materials, have emerged as effective adsorbents due to their tunable surface composition and pore structure, which can be tailored by altering the metal centers or organic linkers. These qualities make MOFs an attractive option for adsorbing the glyphosate. − Previously, MOFs with coordinatively unsaturated sites (CUSs), such as Cu3(BTC)2 (BTC = benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylate), first reported by Chui et al., have demonstrated high potential for both glyphosate adsorption and sensing applications. − For example, Galaço and co-workers demonstrated the activity of Cu3(BTC)2, synthesized through continuous flow, for both detecting and adsorbing glyphosate. They reported that Cu3(BTC)2 effectively adsorbs glyphosate from water within about 30 min of contact. However, despite these advancements, the specific interactions between glyphosate and MOFs remain insufficiently explored and have not yet been thoroughly detailed, to the best of our knowledge.

In this work, we employ density functional theory (DFT) calculations with the M06-L functional to investigate the adsorption of glyphosate on MOFs containing CUSs. First, the adsorption complex structures are analyzed to uncover the mechanisms governing the interactions of glyphosate with Cu3(BTC)2 MOFs. Binding energies and the effects of different transition metals in the isostructural series M3(BTC)2 (M = V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn) are then studied to understand the influence of Lewis acidity in the M3(BTC)2 MOFs and to predict the most efficient metal centers. Furthermore, we develop descriptors for predicting glyphosate adsorption energy using the sure independence screening and sparsifying operator (SISSO) method. The results provide insight into the structure–property relationships of Lewis acidic M3(BTC)2 MOFs and their potential for the adsorption and separation of organophosphate pesticides.

Details of the Electronic Structure Calculations

DFT has been extensively used to study the adsorption behavior of various molecules on M3(BTC)2 MOFs, with particular attention to the influence of different metal centers on their adsorption properties. − Among the commonly used functionals, M06-L, recommended for systems involving transition metals and inorganic compounds, has demonstrated reliable performance in describing not only adsorption phenomena but also reaction mechanisms in MOFs including CUSs. − In this work, the geometry optimization of all species were performed with the M06-L density functional as implemented in Gaussian 16 Revision B.01. We employed the def2-SVP basis set for the C, O, H, and P atoms, while for the transition metal atoms, the Stuttgart effective core potentials and associated basis sets (SDD) were used. During the geometry optimization, the interacting molecules and the entire M3(BTC)2 structure were allowed to relax, except for the terminating hydroxyl groups, to maintain structural consistency across different metal substitutions and to isolate the intrinsic electronic effects of the metal centers. All coordinates of the optimized structures are available in the Supporting Information. To model the solvation by water, the polarizable continuum model (PCM) was used. Vibrational frequency calculations were performed on the optimized geometries to identify the nature of all of the stationary states and were used to obtain the thermodynamic properties. The Gibbs free energy of a structure is obtained from the electronic energy as given in eq 1 G=E0+Etot+kBT−TStot

where G is the Gibbs free energy of a calculated structure, E 0 is the total electronic energy of the structure, E tot is the internal thermal energy, k B is the Boltzmann constant, T is the temperature, and S tot is the internal thermal entropy. The adsorption free energy (ΔG ads) of glyphosate on zeolites is defined as 2 ΔGads=G(MOF‐glyphosate)−G(MOF)−G(glyphosate) where G(MOF-glyphosate) is the total energy of glyphosate adsorbed on M3(BTC)2 MOF, while G(MOF) and G(glyphosate) refer to the total energies of MOFs and glyphosate, respectively. We tested the importance of correcting the basis set superposition error (BSSE) using the counterpoise method. The BSSE-corrected adsorption free energies exhibit the same overall trend as the uncorrected values (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information). For consistency and clarity, the results presented in the manuscript are therefore based on calculations without BSSE correction. The atomic charges and orbital energies were calculated with the natural bond orbital (NBO) method. The amount of charge transfer upon adsorption, Δρ, was calculated according to the equation: 3 Δρ=ρ(ads‐glyphosate)−ρ(glyphosate) where ρ­(ads-glyphosate) is the charge of the adsorption complex and ρ­(glyphosate) is the charge of a glyphosate molecule. The interactions between Lewis-type NBOs (electron donors) and non-Lewis NBOs (electron acceptors) are analyzed using second-order perturbation theory of the Fock matrix. The stabilization energy E (2) for each donor (i) and acceptor (j) orbital is estimated according to eq as 4 E(2)=ΔEij=−qiFij2εi−εj where qi represents the occupancy of the donor orbital, Fij is the off-diagonal Fock matrix element between orbitals i and j, and ε i and ε j are the diagonal elements corresponding to the orbital energies. The equilibrium constants (K eq) derived from DFT thermodynamic analysis can be calculated via eq as 5 ln⁡Keq=−ΔHRT+ΔSR where ΔH is the enthalpy change, R is the ideal gas constant, T is the assumed temperature in the range of 250–600 K, and ΔS is the change in the internal thermal entropy.

Results and Discussion

Glyphosate Adsorption Complexes

We begin by examining glyphosate adsorption on Cu3(BTC)2, a well-known paddlewheel MOF commonly used for sensing and adsorbing glyphosate. , The Cu–Cu bond distance in Cu3(BTC)2 is 2.49 Å, while the average Cu–O bond length is 1.96 Å. These values are consistent with extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) data, which report values of 2.58 Å for Cu–Cu and 1.96 Å for Cu–O. Glyphosate can bind to the Cu3(BTC)2 active site through four adsorption modes utilizing its different oxygen atoms. The optimized structures of the resulting complexes are illustrated in Figure , and the corresponding adsorption energies are shown in Figure . In all adsorption modes, the corresponding oxygen atom in glyphosate interacts with the Cu site through its lone pair. The interaction slightly changes the lengths of the Cu–Cu bond in Cu3(BTC)2. The intermolecular O···Cu distances are 2.13, 2.23, 2.20, and 2.36 Å, for the O1, O2, O3, and O4 adsorption modes, respectively. We also observe hydrogen bonding between the hydrogen atom of the hydroxyl group in glyphosate and the oxygen atom of Cu3(BTC)2 for the O1 and the O2 adsorption modes with H­(glyphosate)–O­(MOF) bond distances of 1.82 and 1.79 Å, respectively. These interactions induce electron density transfer from glyphosate to the MOF, yielding glyphosate becoming positively charged by +0.120e, +0.073e, +0.091e, and +0.096e in the respective modes. According to the NBO analysis, all interactions are dominated by the bond formed between the sp-orbital of the O atom in glyphosate and the corresponding orbital of Cu, which has mostly p-character (Figure ). The adsorption free energies of glyphosate (ΔG ads) on Cu3(BTC)2 range from −4.2 to −17.5 kcal/mol. The O1 adsorption mode has the highest adsorption energy (Figure ). The results show that glyphosate preferentially adsorbs on the Cu3(BTC)2 MOF through both the coordination between the phosphoryl oxygen and the Cu site and the hydrogen bonding between its hydroxyl group and the oxygen atoms of the MOF. This observation is in good agreement with the adsorption behavior of organophosphate compounds on Lewis acid sites in other porous materials, such as metal-substituted zeolites and Zr-based metal–organic frameworks. ,

It is known that Cu3(BTC)2 possesses two open metal sites. To evaluate the effect of multiple adsorbates, we calculated the adsorption of two glyphosate molecules on both open metal sites. The optimized structure is shown in Figure S2. The adsorption free energy per molecule in the two-molecule system is −16.4 kcal/mol, which is similar to the single-molecule adsorption energy (−17.5 kcal/mol). This result indicates that the presence of a second adsorbate does not significantly affect the adsorption strength or alter the overall trend. This observation is consistent with previous reports on the adsorption of various hydrocarbon molecules on Cu3(BTC)2. , Therefore, in this study, we focused on single-molecule adsorption.

To ensure that the size of the chosen MOF cluster is adequate, we performed additional fully periodic calculations using the rPBE-D3 method, which is widely recognized as suitable for periodic simulations of MOF systems. − The calculation details are described in the Supporting Information. The optimized adsorption structures are shown in Figure S3. The results show that the electronic adsorption energies of glyphosate in the O1–O4 configurations, without thermal corrections, follow the same trend for both the cluster and periodic models, with the O1 mode consistently exhibiting the strongest adsorption (Figure S4). These findings support the adequacy of the cluster model used in this study for accurately describing glyphosate adsorption on MOF surfaces.

Screening of M3(BTC)2 MOFs

In addition to the Cu-based species discussed above, we study the adsorption of glyphosate on the M3(BTC)2 MOFs containing metal atoms V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Zn. Here, we focus on the O1 adsorption mode, which turned out by some margin to be preferred in the Cu case. The adsorption energies for all MOFs are presented in Table , while the optimized structures of glyphosate adsorption are provided in Figure S5 of the Supporting Information. It can be observed that the trend in the structural changes of the adsorption complexes is similar to that observed for Cu3(BTC)2. The electron density analyzed from NBO is shifted from glyphosate to the M3(BTC)2 MOFs, leading to a more positive charge of the glyphosate molecule (Δρgly) as shown in Table . The calculated adsorption energies are in the range of −14.1 kcal/mol (Cr3(BTC)2) to −21.8 kcal/mol (Zn3(BTC)2). The adsorption energies are within the range of those observed for Lewis acidic zeolites (−10.5 to −25.0 kcal/mol). However, they are higher than those reported for NU-1000 MOFs, as well as for graphene and transition metal-doped graphene (e.g., Cu, Pd, Pt). , As presented in Table , Zn3(BTC)2 and Ni3(BTC)2 MOFs show the strongest adsorption with nearly identical energies of −21.8 and −21.7 kcal/mol, respectively. To explain the adsorption strength, we analyze the second-order stabilization energy E (2) using eq . Higher values of E (2) indicate stronger donor–acceptor interactions, leading to greater stabilization upon adsorption. The E (2) values for the interaction between the oxygen atom of glyphosate and the metal sites in Zn3(BTC)2 and Ni3(BTC)2 are 22.6 and 21.5 kcal/mol, respectively, as shown in Table . They are higher than those observed for the other MOFs, which indicates a stronger donor–acceptor interaction, leading to the adsorption energy being superior to other MOF systems. The equilibrium constant (K eq) for glyphosate adsorption on MOFs can be determined using the DFT thermodynamic data according to eq . The correlation between K eq and the temperature is shown in Figure a. Since Zn3(BTC)2 and Ni3(BTC)2 exhibit higher adsorption equilibrium constants than those of the other MOFs over the studied temperature range, these two MOFs should be the most promising glyphosate adsorbents.

The Lewis acid strength of adsorbing materials has been shown to significantly influence the adsorption of glyphosate. To evaluate this property, the adsorption energy of ammonia (NH3) on the MOFs is calculated. Ammonia is selected as a probe molecule due to its role as a Lewis base, capable of donating lone pair electrons to Lewis acid sites. Its interaction energy is widely accepted as a reliable measure of the Lewis acid strength of materials. Figure S6 shows the optimized structures of ammonia adsorbed on M3(BTC)2 MOFs, along with their corresponding binding energies. The adsorption energies are found in a range of −8.6 to −17.5 kcal/mol. These values are within the range previously reported for tetravalent metal-substituted zeolites and metal-exchanged zeolites, such as Cu and Mo. , As shown in Figure b, we also observe a moderately strong linear correlation between the relative adsorption free energies of ammonia (ΔG NH3 ) and glyphosate (ΔG ads), with an R 2 (coefficient of determination) of 0.843. This highlights the important role of the acid strength of M3(BTC)2 MOFs in glyphosate adsorption, indicating that optimization of this property can improve the adsorption energy.

We also investigated the impact of structural variability in M3(BTC)2 on the adsorption energies. We decompose the glyphosate adsorption free energy (ΔG ads) into two components as shown in Figure a. The first one is the free energy required to distort the M3(BTC)2 framework and glyphosate, referred to as the strain energy (ΔG strain), while the second one is the free energy of adsorption of the already distorted with glyphosate onto the distorted MOF, referred to as the bond energy (ΔG bond). , Figure b shows glyphosate adsorption free energy along with its parts, the strain energy, and the bond energy. The bond energy is consistently larger than the strain energy, with bonding to the metal site requiring the largest distortion free energy. The trend of the bond energy is similar to that of the adsorption free energy. The strain energies for Zn3(BTC)2, Cu3(BTC)2, Ni3(BTC)2, Fe3(BTC)2, and Mn3(BTC)2 are almost the same, ranging from 3.9 to 4.6 kcal/mol. Thus, the higher bond energies of Zn3(BTC)2 (−26.4 kcal/mol) and Ni3(BTC)2 (−25.6 kcal/mol) compared to those of the other MOFs result in more favorable adsorption free energies. These findings suggest that the bond energy dominates the adsorption strength.

Predictive Descriptors

Descriptors for predicting the adsorption energies of glyphosate on MOFs are identified with the aim of estimating the adsorption energies in a simple way. As reported above, we observe a moderately strong linear correlation between the adsorption energies of glyphosate (ΔG ads) and ammonia (ΔG NH3 ) (see Figure b). The best linear relationships to estimate ΔG ads from ΔG NH3 have a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 1.2 kcal/mol: ΔGads=0.761ΔGNH3−9.127kcal/mol 6

This linear regression model only requires the calculation of ammonia adsorption energies; however, its predictive power is modest. A high absolute error of 5.3 kcal/mol is also observed in predicting the adsorption energy of glyphosate. We can improve it by generating more complex descriptors using the SISSO method. This approach was previously employed for effective descriptors based on primary descriptors derived from DFT calculations. It allowed the prediction of transition states and activation energies for reactions on porous materials, particularly zeolites and MOFs. − In this work, the primary descriptors include geometrical and electronic parameters of M3(BTC)2, namely the metal–metal bond distance (B M–M; M = V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn), average metal–oxygen bond distance (B M–O), total dipole moment (D i), charge of the metal at the site interacting with glyphosate (M c), spin density of the metal (M s), energy of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO; E H), energy of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO; E L), energy of the next-to-highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO – 1; E H–1), and energy of the second lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO + 1; E L+1). The ammonia adsorption energy (ΔG NH3 ) as a descriptor for the acidity is also included. The SISSO method is capable of screening an extensive set of candidate descriptors, generated by combining the primary descriptors through various mathematical expressions. We apply mathematical operators (+, −, ×, ÷, exp, exp–, –1, 2, 3, 6, √, log, | |, sin, cos) to generate an extensive set of nonlinear expressions, forming the candidate space for analysis. In this work, we restrict the dimensionality (number of descriptors, D) and the maximal number of mathematical operations in each term to 3.

As noted, all SIS-selected features across different descriptor dimensions, along with their corresponding R 2 values, are provided in Table S1 of the Supporting Information. The comparison between ΔG ads values obtained from DFT and those predicted by SISSO, along with their corresponding RMSE and R 2 values for the trained models, is shown in Figure . Due to the nonlinear nature of the predictive model, the effect of each descriptor can only be evaluated by isolating its variation. ΔG NH3 consistently appears across all predictive equations, confirming its significance. This highlights the importance of the acid strength of MOFs in glyphosate adsorption and suggests that optimizing this property could enhance the adsorption performance. While other descriptors, such as the geometrical parameter B M–M and the electronic properties M c, E H, and E L, are not directly correlated with ΔG ads, they are connected to structural strain and the electronic environment of the active site. These properties are relevant to adsorption behavior and are therefore selected by the SISSO algorithm as independent variables in the predictive model.

SISSO Models for Predicting Glyphosate Adsorption Energy

The prediction accuracy of the trained model increases noticeably with greater model complexity. We assess the robustness of the SISSO models using the leave-one-group-out cross-validation (LOOCV) method. The model performance is evaluated based on the R 2 and the RMSE between the predicted and actual values (see Table S2). The low RMSE values (0.03–0.44 kcal/mol) and high R 2 values (0.9706–0.9999) indicate that all SISSO models are reasonably robust and not prone to overfitting. We also test all SISSO models by using them to predict ΔG ads for Ti3(BTC)2, a MOF not included in the original data set (optimized structures shown in Figure S3). The predicted ΔG ads values are −20.6, −20.0, and −20.1 kcal/mol for 1D, 2D, and 3D, respectively. In other words, the absolute SISSO errors are 2.1, 2.7, and 2.6 kcal/mol. Comparing them to the DFT value of −22.7 kcal/mol, this amounts to an error of roughly 10% for an extrapolation outside of the original data.

Conclusions

We used DFT calculations with the M06-L density functional to study the adsorption of glyphosate over the coordinatively unsaturated metal–organic framework M3(BTC)2, M = V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn. Glyphosate binds to Cu3(BTC)2 in its most stable adsorption configuration via an interaction between the phosphoryl group (PO) and a Cu site on the MOF. The adsorption free energies (ΔG ads) on M3(BTC)2 range from −14.1 kcal/mol (Cr3(BTC)2) to −21.8 kcal/mol (Zn3(BTC)2). Glyphosate exhibits the strongest adsorption on Zn3(BTC)2 and Ni3(BTC)2. The orbital stabilization energy is the highest in these two systems. The Lewis acid strength of M3(BTC)2 MOFs, as estimated from ammonia adsorption, also plays an important role in glyphosate adsorption. Decomposing the adsorption free energy demonstrates that the bond energy is the principal factor governing adsorption strength. A fairly strong linear correlation between glyphosate and ammonia adsorption energies suggests that the ammonia adsorption energy alone can serve as a descriptor for estimating organophosphorus adsorption with moderate accuracy in a linear relationship. To enhance predictive accuracy, we apply the SISSO statistical learning technique to identify the optimal combination of physical and electronic properties of MOFs for predicting the glyphosate adsorption energy. The resulting expressions significantly improve the quality of the prediction of the adsorption energies.
