Evaluating the impact of an emotional well-being training program on medical professionals: A quasi-experimental study
Authors: S. Shankar, M. Mohamed Ilyas, J. Shyamprakash, K. Narayanasamy, M. Sargunam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Burnout and psychological distress are prevalent among medical professionals, particularly in resource-limited settings like India, where systemic stressors exacerbate workplace strain.Aim:To assess the effectiveness of a culturally adapted emotional wellness training program in reducing burnout and enhancing well-being among Indian doctors.Materials and Methods:A quasi-experimental one-group pretest-posttest study was conducted with 30 doctors at a Government Medical College in South India. A 10-week intervention included mindfulness, cognitive-behavioral strategies, and peer support. Outcomes were measured using the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-9), and WHO-5 Well-Being Index, analyzed with paired t-tests and correlation analyses.Results:Post-intervention, psychological distress decreased (Global Severity Index: 0.40 to 0.10, P < 0.001), work engagement improved (vigor: d = 0.45; dedication: d = 0.82), and well-being increased (WHO-5: 57.53 to 73.67, P < 0.001). Negative affect moderated outcomes, with no role-based differences (P > 0.05).Conclusion:The program effectively reduced distress and enhanced engagement, showing feasibility in low-resource contexts, though sustainability requires systemic support.

Mental health, encompassing not only the absence of psychological illness but also a state of positive well-being, is increasingly recognized as essential for professional functioning across occupations.[1] Among healthcare workers, this aspect is particularly critical due to the unique occupational stressors they encounter, including prolonged working hours, high-stakes decision-making, and emotional demands stemming from patient care.[2] Globally, medical professionals, such as physicians and nurses, exhibit heightened vulnerability to psychological distress, with burnout prevalence often exceeding 50% in demanding specialties like emergency medicine and surgery.[3] Burnout, classified as an occupational phenomenon in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), manifests through emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment, posing risks to both individual health and healthcare system efficacy.[4] Chronic exposure to such stressors has been linked to impaired cognitive functions—such as attention and problem-solving—essential for clinical practice, alongside physical comorbidities like cardiovascular disease and immune dysfunction.[5]

The consequences of burnout extend beyond the individual, affecting patient safety, care quality, and institutional stability through increased medical errors, absenteeism, and staff turnover.[6] While these challenges are universal, their intensity and context vary across regions. In high-income countries, interventions like mindfulness-based stress reduction and organizational reforms have shown promise in mitigating burnout.[7] However, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like India, systemic factors amplify the burden. India’s doctor-patient ratio of 1:1,445—well below the World Health Organization’s recommended 1:1,000—reflects chronic understaffing, compounded by inadequate mental health infrastructure and cultural stigma surrounding psychological support.[8] These conditions elevate the risk of psychological attrition, with studies indicating that Indian healthcare workers face unique stressors, such as resource scarcity and societal expectations, not fully addressed by Western models.[9]

Despite the pressing need, few interventions have been tailored to the sociocultural and systemic realities of LMICs. Most evidence-based programs originate from resource-rich settings, limiting their applicability where structural constraints dominate.[10] For instance, mindfulness programs alone may not suffice in contexts where workload and administrative burdens overshadow personal coping capacity.[11] In India, the scarcity of mental health resources—coupled with a projected shortage of healthcare workers—underscores the urgency of developing accessible, culturally resonant strategies to bolster resilience.[12] Preliminary studies suggest that integrating local practices, such as yoga, with evidence-based techniques could enhance acceptability and effectiveness.[13] Yet, rigorous evaluations of such adapted programs remain sparse, leaving a critical gap in understanding their impact on Indian medical professionals.[14]

This study addresses this gap by evaluating a culturally adapted emotional wellness training program designed to reduce burnout and enhance well-being among doctors in India. Incorporating mindfulness, cognitive-behavioral strategies, and peer support, the intervention aims to foster emotional resilience in a resource-constrained setting. By targeting both individual coping mechanisms and workplace dynamics, it seeks to offer a scalable model for improving healthcare worker retention and patient outcomes amidst pervasive systemic stressors.[15]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

A quasi-experimental one-group pretest-posttest design assessed well-being, work engagement, and psychological distress before and after a 10-week intervention. The lack of a control group stemmed from logistical constraints at a single institution.

Participants

Thirty doctors from a Government Medical College in South India, holding MBBS, MD, MS, DM, or MCH qualifications and engaged in clinical practice, participated. Exclusion criteria included medical students, trainees, and individuals with a self-reported history of severe mental illness currently under treatment.

Procedure

The Institutional Ethics Committee of a Government Medical College in South India approved the study (IEC No. 710/ME- G/2024-113, dated 01/03/2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Of 38 initial enrollees, eight withdrew due to scheduling conflicts (n = 5) or stigma concerns (n = 3). The 10-week program included weekly 90-min sessions on mindfulness, cognitive-behavioral strategies, and peer support, conducted in a hospital setting with flexible scheduling and pseudonym-based confidentiality.

Intervention

The Emotional Wellness Training Program included stress management techniques such as breathing exercises, cognitive restructuring strategies, and peer-support discussions. These were delivered through lectures, group-based activities, and a participant manual outlining the 10-week training schedule. The program was piloted on a small group prior to the study, and all sessions were delivered by trained mental health professionals [refer to Table 1].

Instruments

WHO-5 well-being index

A brief measure of current mental well-being, consisting of five positively worded items rated on a 6-point Likert scale (0 = at no time to 5 = all the time). Total raw score (0–25) is multiplied by 4 to give a percentage score from 0 to 100; higher scores indicate better well-being. A score below 50 suggests poor well-being and may indicate depression. Suitable for screening and outcome evaluation in clinical and research settings. Cronbach’s α =0.82.[16]

Utrecht work engagement scale (UWES-9)

A validated tool to assess positive work-related state of mind, comprising three dimensions—vigor, dedication, and absorption—measured across nine items using a 7-point scale (0 = never to 6 = always). Total and subscale scores are computed by averaging item responses. Higher scores denote greater work engagement. Widely used among healthcare professionals and across occupational groups. Cronbach’s α =0.85–0.92.[17]

Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI)

A 53-item self-report inventory measuring psychological distress and symptom patterns across nine dimensions, including depression, anxiety, and somatization. Rated on a 5-point scale (0 = not at all to 4 = extremely). Yields three global indices: Global Severity Index (GSI), Positive Symptom Total (PST), and Positive Symptom Distress Index (PSDI). Offers a reliable overview of current psychopathology for clinical screening. Cronbach’s α =0.89.[18]

Personality inventory for DSM-5 brief form (PID-5-BF)

A 25-item self-report tool assessing five maladaptive personality trait domains—Negative Affect, Detachment, Antagonism, Disinhibition, and Psychoticism—based on DSM-5-dimensional personality model. Responses range from 0 (very false or often false) to 3 (very true or often true). Used in both research and clinical settings to evaluate personality pathology. Cronbach’s α =0.72–0.86.[19]

Statistical analysis

The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to assess differences between clinical and nonclinical groups. For pretest-posttest comparisons, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was utilized. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to examine relationships between variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using appropriate statistical software.

RESULTS

The study included 30 participants with a mean age of 42.67 years (SD = 6.10); 60% were male, and 86.7% were married. Clinical department doctors comprised 60% of the sample [Table 2]. Among PID-5-BF traits, Negative Affect (M = 2.93, SD = 3.97) and Detachment (M = 1.90, SD = 3.34) had the highest scores [Table 3]. Post-intervention analysis, using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, revealed a significant reduction in psychological distress and improvements in work-related well-being. Brief Symptoms scores (Global Severity Index, Positive Symptom Total, Positive Symptom Distress Index) significantly decreased (P < 0.001). Work engagement (Vigor, Dedication) and WHO work satisfaction significantly increased (P < 0.05) [Table 4]. Spearman’s correlation analysis showed that Negative Affect had a significant positive correlation with pretest distress scores (Global Severity Index: rs = 0.417, P = 0.022; Positive Symptom Distress Index: rs = 0.454, P = 0.012) and a negative correlation with Vigor (rs = −0.391, P = 0.033). Detachment showed significant positive correlations with pretest Global Severity Index (rs = 0.431, P = 0.017) and Positive Symptom Distress Index (rs = 0.383, P = 0.037). Antagonism correlated positively with pretest Positive Symptom Total (rs = 0.378, P = 0.039) and post-test Vigor (rs = 0.430, P = 0.018) and Dedication (rs = 0.457, P = 0.011). Disinhibition had a significant negative correlation with post-test WHO work satisfaction (rs = −0.373, P = 0.042). No significant correlations were found for Psychoticism or age with most variables [Table 5]. The Mann-Whitney U test revealed no significant differences between clinical and non-clinical groups for PID-5-BF traits, pretest, or post-test scores (all P > 0.05) [Table 6].

DISCUSSION

This quasi-experimental study demonstrated that a 10-week emotional well-being training program significantly reduced psychological distress and enhanced work engagement and well-being among Indian medical professionals. The large effect sizes in distress reduction (d = 1.07–1.48) surpass those reported in mindfulness-only interventions (d = 0.35–0.60),[20] likely due to the programs multimodal design integrating mindfulness, cognitive-behavioral techniques, and peer support. These components addressed both personal coping and contextual stressors, such as understaffing, prevalent in Indias healthcare system.[21] The significant improvements in vigor (d = 0.45) and dedication (d = 0.82) align with the Job Demands-Resources model, which posits that building personal resources buffers burnout and boosts engagement.[22] However, the lack of change in absorption (P = 0.414) suggests that systemic factors such as high patient loads or administrative pressures may hinder deep task immersion, a finding consistent with studies in under-resourced settings.[23] The moderating role of negative affect, linked to higher symptom severity (rs = 0.417–0.454), indicates that individuals with heightened emotional reactivity may benefit from tailored strategies, such as extended cognitive restructuring.[24] Conversely, the positive correlation between antagonism and post-intervention vigor and dedication (rs = 0.430–0.457) suggests that assertive traits, when channeled constructively, could enhance resilience in high-pressure roles.[25] The absence of differences between clinical and nonclinical staff (P > 0.05) contrasts with prior research highlighting greater burnout in patient-facing roles.[26] This homogeneity may reflect pervasive systemic stressors in Indias healthcare context, where resource scarcity impacts all roles similarly.[27] Culturally resonant elements, such as yoga and peer-led discussions, likely boosted engagement and acceptability, supporting evidence that local adaptations improve intervention uptake in LMICs.[28] Compared to studies from high-income settings, where organizational reforms often complement individual training,[29] this programs success in a resource-limited context highlights its feasibility. However, the unchanged absorption scores underscore a key limitation: individual-focused interventions may not fully address structural barriers like workload or staffing shortages.[30] Studies combining personal training with systemic changes such as task-shifting or workload caps report more sustained effects (d > 1.0),[30] suggesting a hybrid approach could amplify benefits.

In India, integrating mandatory well-being training into the National Medical Commission’s continuing medical education framework could target rural and urban doctors alike. Annual mental health screenings using validated tools (e.g., WHO-5, BSI) and incentives for hospitals achieving high well-being metrics could institutionalize support. In LMICs, governments should fund scalable programs led by non-specialists and develop national guidelines to prioritize workplace mental health.[23]

Limitations

The small sample (n = 30) and single-site recruitment limit generalizability, while the quasi-experimental design without a control group weakens causal inference. Self-reported measures risk social desirability bias, and the absence of objective indicators (e.g., absenteeism rates, cortisol levels) restricts data robustness. The 10-week duration and immediate posttest assessment preclude insights into long-term efficacy, and the focus on individual resilience overlooks systemic contributors like understaffing or policy gaps.[24] Cultural dynamics specific to the study site may also not reflect broader Indian healthcare settings.

CONCLUSION

This study underscores the potential of a culturally tailored, multimodal emotional well-being program to improve psychological health and work engagement among Indian doctors. While promising in its effects, the findings advocate for integrating individual interventions with systemic reforms to sustainably enhance mental well-being in resource-constrained medical environments.
