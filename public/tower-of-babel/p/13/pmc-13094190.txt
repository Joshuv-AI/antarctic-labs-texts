Association between craniosynostosis and neurodevelopmental disorders: a nationwide claims database
Authors: Takanori Yanai, Satomi Yoshida, Koji Kawakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in children and have been increasing in recent years, the identification of risk factors and early intervention are important. Cranial deformities may be risk factors for these conditions, but the association with craniosynostosis, especially the non-syndromic form commonly seen in clinical practice, is unclear. Therefore, we examined this association using a nationwide database of 22 million people records.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the JMDC claims database, identifying mother-child pairs born between January 2005 and March 2024 with at least three years of follow-up. Exposure was defined as a diagnosis of non-syndromic craniosynostosis (referred to as craniosynostosis), excluded syndromic such as Apert and Crouzon syndromes. Outcomes were incident ASD and ADHD classified by ICD-10 codes. Cox proportional hazard models adjusted for maternal psychiatric history and neonatal complications calculated hazard ratios (HRs), and a sibling subcohort analysis adjusted for genetic and environmental factors.ResultsAmong 338,117 mother-child pairs, 1,145 children had craniosynostosis. Over a median follow-up of 6.0 years, 16,410 children were diagnosed with ASD and 7,325 ADHD. In adjusted models, craniosynostosis was associated with an increased risk of ASD (HR 2.18, 95% Confidence Interval (CI) 1.80–2.64) and ADHD (HR 2.40, 95%CI 1.77–3.24). Kaplan-Meier curves diverged from early childhood onward, and these findings persisted in sibling analyses.ConclusionCraniosynostosis is significantly associated with the incidence of ASD and ADHD. Physicians and healthcare professionals should recognize cranial anomalies early and conduct careful follow-ups regarding children’s growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06726-5.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), Craniosynostosis, Cohort study, Neurodevelopmental disorders, Risk factors

Background

The prevalence of neurodevelopmental disorders in children has increased in recent years [1]. For example, US data indicate that approximately 1 in 54 individuals are diagnosed with ASD [2]. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, developmental disorders include autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). ASD and ADHD frequently co-occur, with reported overlap rates ranging from 30-80% [2–4]. The evidence supports early intervention for neurodevelopmental disorders, leading to improvements in social adaptation and benefits for their families [5].

In this context, several studies have investigated potential risk factors for developmental disorders. For ASD, the risk factors include genetic predisposition, parental age, maternal smoking, prenatal exposure to antiepileptic and antibacterial drugs, which have also been implicated [6–8]. In our previous study, childhood viral meningitis or encephalitis may further elevate risk [9]. During the analysis process, a significant association between cranial abnormalities and ASD was incidentally suggested. Therefore, we decided to conduct a new analysis focusing on cranial abnormalities.

Craniosynostosis is a disorder characterized by the premature fusion of cranial sutures, and occurs in approximately 1 per 1,800–2,200 live births [10]. Craniosynostosis also affects brain development due to increased intracranial pressure, which may impair cerebral blood flow [11]. Several studies from Australia and the United States have reported links between craniosynostosis and ASD [12, 13]. However, the former Australian studies include syndromic, and the latter US studies have few cases and no comparison with negative controls. Therefore, large-scale studies demonstrating a link between cranial bones and neurodevelopmental delay are limited worldwide. Because craniosynostosis is relatively rare, conducting sufficiently powered epidemiological research remains challenging. Therefore, this study leveraged a large-scale medical database to resolve and explore the association between craniosynostosis and developmental disorders.

Methods

Data source and study design

This retrospective cohort study used data from January 2005 to March 2024 in the Japan JMDC claims database [14] maintained by JMDC, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan), and aggregates de-identified claims data from health insurance unions and their dependents across Japan. The database has been operational since 2005 and includes data on approximately 20 million individuals (approximately 15% of the Japanese population), primarily workers and their families [15]. The JMDC database records basic patient information (e.g., age and sex) and clinical diagnoses based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD-10) [16]. It tracks patient claims longitudinally, including across multiple institutions, and links family members through a shared identification number [17].

Participants, outcomes, and exposures

This study included children aged < 12 years and their mothers aged 16–50 years, each followed for at least 3 years after childbirth between January 2005 and March 2021; follow-up was censored at age 12 or March 2024, whichever occurred first. The primary outcomes were ASD and ADHD, defined as the first claims of an ICD-10 diagnosis (F84 for ASD, including childhood autism, atypical autism, and pervasive developmental disorder) and F90 for ADHD recorded before age 12. An algorithm-based validation study reported a specificity of 99.6% for ASD diagnosis [18]. The exposure was the first claim of craniosynostosis before 2 years of age, identified by the ICD-10 code Q75. This included subtypes such as trigonocephaly, scaphocephaly, and brachycephaly, each analyzed separately. Individuals with facial deformities (Q87), syndromic craniosynostosis (e.g., Apert, Pfeiffer, and Crouzon syndromes, ICD-10 code Q75.1), or chromosomal abnormalities (including Down syndrome, ICD-10 codes Q90–Q99) were excluded. Although no validation studies exist for craniosynostosis and ADHD, these conditions are not routinely coded in pediatric practice and are considered to have a certain degree of specificity.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on literature linking maternal and child-related factors with ASD. Maternal covariates included mental disorders diagnosed within 3 years before childbirth and infections during pregnancy.

Maternal mental disorders were defined using the following ICD-10 codes: F20–F29 for schizophrenia and related disorders; F30–F39 for mood disorders (e.g., depression and bipolar disorder); F40–F48 for neurotic, stress-related, and somatoform disorders (including anxiety, phobias, and posttraumatic stress disorder); F50–F59 for disorders associated with physical factors (e.g., anorexia and sleep disorders); F60–F69 for adult personality and behavioral disorders; and F70–F79 for intellectual disabilities [19].

Child-related covariates included neonatal complications, such as early gestational age, low birth weight, and epilepsy [20–24]. These were identified using the following ICD-10 codes: P05 for fetal growth and malnutrition, P07 for short gestation and low birth weight; P08 for long gestation and high birth weight; P21–P29 for specific perinatal conditions (primarily respiratory and cardiovascular); and G40 for epilepsy. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

In the primary analysis, the Cox proportional hazards model was used to evaluate the association between craniosynostosis and the development of ASD and ADHD. Follow-up began at birth and continued until the onset of either condition; participants without a diagnosis were censored at age 12. Multivariable models estimated hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (minimally adjusted) was adjusted for sex and birth year. Model 2 (maternal factor model) was adjusted for maternal age, history of mental illness, and infections during pregnancy. Model 3 (child-specific model) further included neonatal and congenital factors, such as low birth weight, neonatal asphyxia, and congenital malformations.

Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to illustrate the cumulative ASD risk over time across exposure groups. To better account for familial factors (e.g., genetic predisposition), sensitivity analyses were conducted using two additional cohorts, including one with children who had siblings [25]. Cox proportional hazard analyses were conducted on these subcohorts using the same covariate adjustment models.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata SE (version 17.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) software. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Overall, the study involved 338,117 mother-child pairs, of whom 1,145 (0.3%) children were diagnosed with craniosynostosis, and 16,410 (4.8%) and 7,325 (2.1%) children were diagnosed with ASD and ADHD, respectively. The median follow-up period was 6.0 years (interquartile range [IQR], 4.5–8.5 years). The median age at diagnosis was 12 months (IQR: 8–15 months) for craniosynostosis and 45 months (IQR: 35–63 months) for both ASD and ADHD. Of the 156,847 children with siblings, 7,766 (4.9%) met criteria for ASD and 3,909 (2.5%) for ADHD; these subgroup counts are shown in Supplementary Table 2 (Fig. 1).

The primary findings are summarized in Table 2. Among children diagnosed with ASD, 109 children had exposure factors. In Model 3, which was adjusted for both maternal and child covariates, the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) for ASD was 2.18 (95% CI: 1.80–2.64; p < 0.001). For ADHD, 46 children had exposure factors, and the aHR was 2.40 (95% CI: 1.77–3.24). Figure 2 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves for the cumulative incidence of ASD and ADHD according to the exposure status. In the unadjusted univariate regression analysis, these curves showed a marked and significant deviation for both outcomes. Sensitivity analyses showed high HRs for both ASD and ADHD in the sibling subcohort, further confirming a significant increase in prevalence among the exposed group (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

Our study investigated the association between craniosynostosis and the risk of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) using a large-scale medical database. The principal findings demonstrated that children with craniosynostosis had significantly higher rates of both conditions compared to those without, even after extensive adjustment for confounding factors. The adjusted hazard ratios for ASD and ADHD were 2.18 and 2.40, respectively. These findings were validated by sibling-based sensitivity analyses, further supporting craniosynostosis as a risk factor for both conditions.

There is limited literature directly comparable to our findings. Among the few available studies, a 2022 cohort analysis based on the Australian administrative data set (n = 550,000) reported a 5.27-fold increased risk of ASD in association with craniofacial anomalies, particularly in cases involving additional anomalies [12]. However, that Austrian study included syndromic craniosynostosis such as Treacher-Collins syndrome and Apert syndrome, which may have overestimated the risk. Furthermore, a recent study conducted by the Rochester Epidemiology Project found that 4% of 10,000 infants were diagnosed with nonsyndromic craniosynostosis, and 7.5% of these infants exhibited developmental delays [13]. However, due to the limited sample size and absence of a control group, definitive risk evaluation was not achievable. In our study, we explicitly excluded syndromic craniosynostosis, created conditions similar to those encountered in actual clinical practice, and calculated the hazard ratio by comparing with negative controls. This provides clinical insight that even in cases without apparent symptoms, careful observation of neurological development is warranted.

Additionally, the prevalence of respiratory distress syndrome was high in the craniosynostosis group. Typically, respiratory distress syndrome develops 1–2 days after birth, differing from the age at diagnosis of craniosynostosis. Although a causal relationship remains unclear due to this temporal gap, a hypothesis exists that constraints within the uterus and head compression during the fetal period may be a common factor contributing to both respiratory disease and cranial deformation [26]. This association may suggest a potential common etiology.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the second globally and the first in Asia to utilize a comprehensive dataset comprising 350,000 mother–child pairs (700,000 individuals), through extensive confounder adjustments and sensitivity analyses. Moreover, the JMDC database primarily comprises data from private company employees; approximately 80% of the parental generation are office workers, suggesting high generalizability [27, 28].

However, this study has certain limitations. First, diagnoses were based on ICD-10 code, which may limit diagnostic accuracy. Notably, no validation studies exist for craniosynostosis. The exposure variable in this study, along with its subclassification, remains a topic of ongoing debate [29]. Although we considered craniosynostosis subtypes, the limited number of cases precluded meaningful statistical analyses. Furthermore, exposure and outcome rates were higher than those reported in previous epidemiological studies. This discrepancy is attributable to the characteristics of Japan’s universal health insurance system, in which physicians often assign a diagnosis code to justify reimbursement for necessary examinations, such as cranial computed tomography, to rule out the disease. An autism diagnosis is required to obtain coverage under the “outpatient psychological therapy fee” category, which may lead to misclassification or overestimation. Consequently, the exposure group likely included children who did not have the condition or had only mild deformities. However, this potential misclassification would likely bias the results toward the null, leading to an underestimation of the true risk. Despite this dilution effect, we observed a significantly increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, suggesting that the association might be stronger than estimated.

Second, selection bias is a concern. In general, parents of children who are identified with abnormalities in early childhood tend to seek professional medical care earlier. Therefore, the exposed group may be more likely to visit doctors who specialize in neurodevelopmental delays [30]. Third, as with all observational studies, this analysis identifies associations and does not infer causation. It is worth noting that this study primarily focuses on appearance, and the results should be interpreted with caution. In particular, it does not encourage the use of helmets or other remedies.

Despite these limitations, this is the first large-scale study to demonstrate the potential for neurodevelopmental risk in cases of non-syndromic craniosynostosis. While there is no immediate need to expand the indications for surgery, careful follow-up is still recommended, and this study is expected to contribute to the development of clinical guidelines [31]. For instance, developmental outcomes may be enhanced through routine measurement of cranial diagonals during infant health checkups, with conversion to cranial vault asymmetry index scores, or through the application of 3D measurement tools to guide treatment indications [32]. It is important to underscore that these findings are strictly epidemiological and should not be interpreted as advocating or endorsing any specific therapeutic intervention.

Conclusion

In our cohort of 338,117 mother–child pairs, the adjusted hazard ratio for ASD among children with non-syndromic craniosynostosis was 2.1 for ASD and 2.4 for ADHD incidence, which implies that healthcare professionals are advised to continue neurodevelopmental observation for children with cranial deformities.
