Synchronous ipsilateral adrenal cortical adenoma and ganglioneuroma: A case report
Authors: Carolyne Lallawmzuali, Adil Aziz Khan, Sana Ahuja, Sufian Zaheer
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSynchronous tumors comprising of cortical as well as medullary differentiation are rare in the adrenal gland. Collision tumors comprise of two independent neoplasms arising from the cortex and the medulla without any histological admixture. Adrenal cortico-medullary mixed tumors are rare tumors comprising of adrenal cortical and chromaffin cells intermixed with each other. Ganglioneuroma is a rare benign tumor originating from the paravertebral sympathetic ganglia and is rarely seen in the adrenal medulla. The presence of a synchronous ganglioneuroma with an adrenal cortical adenoma in the ipsilateral adrenal gland is exceedingly rare. We report a collision tumor comprising of an adrenal cortical adenoma and a ganglioneuroma detected as an incidental finding in a pediatric patient who presented with hypertension and palpitations.

INTRODUCTION

Adrenal cortico-medullary mixed tumors (CMMTs) are rare tumors, comprising of adrenal cortical and chromaffin cells intermixed with each other.[1] On the other hand, a collision tumor is one where two independent neoplasms, without any substantial histologic admixture at the interface, coexist adjacent to each other.[2] Till date, very few CMMTs and collision tumors have been described in the adrenal gland.

Adrenal cortical adenoma is the most common adrenal neoplasm. It is a benign tumor, composed of cells derived from the adrenal cortex. It is usually nonfunctional, however, can sometimes present with hormonal symptoms such as virilization or Cushing syndrome.[3] Ganglioneuroma is a rare benign tumor originating from the paravertebral sympathetic ganglia and is rarely seen in the adrenal medulla. They are hormonally inactive and silent and therefore are usually detected incidentally.[3]

We present a rare case of a collision tumor comprising of an adrenal cortical adenoma and a ganglioneuroma, detected as an incidental finding, in a patient who presented with hypertension and palpitations.

CASE REPORT

A 14-year-old girl presented to the outpatient department with complaints of uncontrolled hypertension and palpitations. Laboratory investigations revealed hypokalemia and blood gas analysis revealed metabolic alkalosis. Other biochemical investigations showed high levels of plasma aldosterone along with reduced plasma renin levels, thus confirming the diagnosis of primary aldosteronism. Computed tomography showed a well-defined marginated round to oval lesion in the left adrenal gland [Figure 1a].

(b) Gross image exhibiting an encapsulated globular yellow mass (marked with blue arrow - adrenal cortical adenoma). Attached to it is an ovoid brownish yellow lesion (marked with orange arrow - ganglioneuroma). (c-f) Photomicrographs of adrenal cortical adenoma exhibiting an encapsulated tumor composed of tumor cells with minimal pleomorphism, distinct cell borders, abundant foamy cytoplasm, and round, central nucleus. Immunohistochemistry shows positive expression for Melan-A (e) and inhibin (f) in the tumor cells (×100 and ×200). (g-i) Photomicrographs of ganglioneuroma exhibiting admixture of mature ganglion cells and Schwann cells with positive immunostaining for S-100 (i) (×100 and ×400)

We received an encapsulated globular mass measuring 2 cm × 1.8 cm × 1.2 cm with homogenous yellow cut surface. Another ovoid mass measuring 1.7 cm × 0.8 cm × 0.3 cm was found attached to the globular lesion, the cut surface of which was brownish yellow [Figure 1b].

Histopathological examination from the globular mass revealed a capsulated tumor with cells arranged in cords, nests, and islands. The individual tumor cells had distinct cell borders, abundant foamy cytoplasm, and round, central nucleus. There was only minimal nuclear pleomorphism with no capsular or vascular invasion. Very scant peripherally pushed normal adrenal parenchyma was observed at the periphery. Necrosis and mitosis were absent and the modified Weiss score was 0. On immunohistochemistry, these cells were positive for Melan-A and inhibin and were negative for CD10. The Ki67 proliferation index was very low [Figure 1c-f].

The ovoid lesion, on microscopy, revealed a tumor with a different histology. The sections showed an admixture of mature ganglion cells and Schwann cells. The ganglion cells were large with central to eccentric nucleus, prominent nucleoli, and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. The elongated and spindled Schwann cells were arranged in fascicles in a background of collagenized stroma. There was no atypia, mitosis, or necrosis. Both the tumors were present adjacent to each other [Figure 1b], however, there was no histological admixture of the cells even on the microscopic examination. On immunohistochemistry, the Schwann cells were positive for S-100, while the ganglion cells expressed S-100 and synaptophysin [Figure 1g-i].

After resection of the tumor, the patient’s symptoms resolved completely and she was asymptomatic at the last follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Adrenal cortical adenomas are the most common tumors arising from the adrenal cortex. They are usually nonfunctional, however can occasionally present with hormonal symptoms such as virilization, hyperaldosteronism, or Cushing syndrome.[3] Ganglioneuromas are rare benign tumors composed of Schwann cells, nerve fibers, and ganglion cells and are commonly localized in the posterior mediastinum and retroperitoneum. They are rarely seen in the adrenal glands.[3]

Mixed adrenal neoplasms, composed of more than one cell type, are called composite tumors. When the neoplasms show mixed histological appearance, they are called mixed corticomedullary tumors (MCMT). A composite tumor may have more than one medullary element. Composite pheochromocytoma is a composite tumor with both pheochromocytoma and ganglioneuroma components.[4] Collision tumors, on the other hand, are neoplastic lesions comprised of two or more distinct populations which maintain distinct borders.[5] Adrenal collision tumors is the coexistence of two adjacent, however histologically separate neoplasms, involving the adrenal gland without any histologic admixture at the interface.[6] The term is used to describe the appearance of a single radiographic mass consisting of two histologically distinct adrenal neoplasms.[7]

The presence of a synchronous ganglioneuroma with an adrenal cortical adenoma in the ipsilateral adrenal gland is exceedingly rare and very few cases have been reported in the literature till date. Kang, in 2022, reported a MCMT comprising of the above two tumors.[4] However, synchronous separate tumors of adrenal cortical and medullary differentiation have been previously reported by Rodriguez-Justo et al.[8] Similar to the present report, they observed two separate tumors: ganglioneuroma and adrenal cortical adenoma detected simultaneously in the adrenal gland. Zhou et al. reported an adrenal collision tumor in a 45-year-old female patient comprising of histologically distinct adrenocortical adenoma and myelolipoma.[7] The present case also showed a single well-defined mass on the imaging; however, the histological examination revealed two histologically distinct tumors with no admixture. Liu and Kumar described a rare adrenal collision tumor comprising of adrenal cortical adenoma, myelolipoma, and metastatic breast cancer.[9]

Adrenal collision tumors are almost exclusively found in adult females. However, contrary to the existing data, the present case is the first one to be reported in a 14-year-old child. Majority of the patients presented with symptoms related to hormone hypersecretion and hypertension and diabetes were the most common symptoms. Immunohistochemical stains may be required to aid in the diagnosis. The adrenal cortical cells stain positive for inhibin and Melan-A, whereas, the Schwann cells are positive for S100 protein, with ganglion cells showing positive expression for synaptophysin.[4]

CONCLUSION

Adrenal collision tumors are typically benign and are cured by surgical resection. They may be detected on imaging investigations, however, the final diagnosis is made on the histopathological examination, which can also help in ruling out malignancy.
