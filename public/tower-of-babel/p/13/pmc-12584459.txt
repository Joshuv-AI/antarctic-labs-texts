Evaluating chikungunya outbreak size and emergency vaccination strategies in Miami: an exploratory modeling study
Authors: A M de Roo, M Boer, G T Vondeling, T Louwsma, A C Sloof, M J Postma, C N Mores, R C Christofferson, J van der Schans, L A de Jong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe risk of vector-borne diseases such as chikungunya – caused by the chikungunya virus (CHIKV) and transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes – is increasing as globalization and climate change continue to expand the habitat of its primary vectors. In 2024 alone, nearly 500,000 chikungunya cases were reported worldwide. Miami-Dade County, Florida, is particularly vulnerable due to the endemic presence of Aedes aegypti and its role as a major entry point for international travel and trade. This study explores the possible impact of a CHIKV outbreak in urban Miami and the effectiveness of an emergency response vaccination program as a preventive measure.MethodsWe created a dynamic vector-host model with a SEIR-SEI structure including local temperature and demographic data. To capture uncertainty, we defined multiple scenarios with varying vector–host ratios (VHRs) and vaccination coverage levels. Emergency vaccination was modeled as an outbreak response strategy to explore its potential impact on outbreak size.ResultsOur scenario-based analysis showed that CHIKV transmission in Miami can happen year-round, with peak outbreak potential from May to August. Outbreak likelihood strongly depended on the VHR, ranging from an annual average of 5% (VHR of 0.75) to 21% (VHR of 2). The VHR also had a major impact on outbreak size, with a median of 224 cases (IQR: 50–740) at a VHR of 1, and 21,053 cases (IQR: 8,900–36,990) at a VHR of 1.5. Outbreak response vaccination reduced outbreak size by a median of 19% (IQR: 2.5%–38%) at a VHR of 1 and 58% (IQR: 48%–66%) at a VHR of 1.5, assuming 10% population coverage.ConclusionWhile empirical validation is not yet possible due to the absence of large-scale outbreaks in Miami, our exploratory modeling provides forward-looking insights into how outbreaks might unfold and how emergency vaccination could mitigate their impact. Background

The chikungunya virus (CHIKV), an alphavirus transmitted primarily by Aedes (Ae.) aegypti (Linnaeus, 1762) and Ae. albopictus (Skuse, 1894) mosquitoes, is a growing global health challenge [1, 2]. Since its discovery in Africa and Asia in the 1950s, CHIKV has spread to new regions following the expansion of its vectors, primarily driven by climate change, urbanization, and human mobility [3–5]. Currently, autochthonous CHIKV transmission has been documented in 114 countries across Africa, Asia, Oceania, the Americas, and Europe [3]. In 2024, almost 500,000 CHIKV cases were reported worldwide, while the Americas recorded 3.7 million suspected and confirmed CHIKV cases since the virus’s introduction in 2013-14, underscoring the potential for widespread transmission in new regions [6, 7]. CHIKV infection can cause a febrile illness with debilitating joint pain, which can persist for months or even years in some cases, substantially affecting quality of life [2, 8–10]. Although severe complications and deaths are rare, the lack of specific treatments beyond supportive care highlights the virus’s substantial health burden [11–14].

In the United States (US), Florida is particularly vulnerable to CHIKV outbreaks due to its climate, which facilitates year-round activity of Ae. aegypti – one of the primary vectors of not only CHIKV but also dengue and Zika viruses [15, 16]. Miami-Dade County specifically faces a substantial risk of local outbreaks due to its status as a major international destination in the US, with frequent travel to and from CHIKV endemic regions. Following the introduction of CHIKV in 2014, Miami experienced an outbreak with 82 reported cases, followed by approximately 30 cases in 2015; since then, only a few sporadic cases have been reported [17–19]. This previous outbreak demonstrates the potential for CHIKV to establish autochthonous transmission in Miami, where the combination of a favorable climate, available competent vectors, high population density, and economic reliance on tourism amplifies the potential impact of an outbreak. The susceptibility of Miami to other Aedes-borne diseases has been demonstrated as well, such as in 2016 when the city became the epicenter of a Zika virus outbreak in the US, with 29 cases reported [19, 20]. A study using this Zika outbreak to estimate the potential impact of a CHIKV outbreak on tourism estimated that a CHIKV outbreak in the Wynwood neighborhood alone could lead to economic losses of $30 million and nearly 250 jobs, highlighting the potential ripple effects of a public health crisis [21].

Despite considerable efforts to control vector populations, the risk of CHIKV transmission in Miami remains [16]. Florida employs several surveillance and control measures to target Aedes mosquitoes, including adult mosquito trapping to monitor local vector presence, as well as larvicide and insecticide spraying to control mosquito populations. While these methods can cover a broad area in a short time, they face both public perception and effectiveness challenges. Chemical insecticides, for example, raise questions about potential health and environmental risks, especially in densely populated urban areas [22]. Additionally, insecticide resistance in Ae. aegypti is an ongoing challenge for vector control efforts [23]. The continued occurrence of locally acquired arboviral diseases, such as dengue, suggests that combination intervention strategies are necessary for maximum mitigation of CHIKV transmission risk [24, 25]. As an additional measure, the FDA approved IXCHIQ, a live-attenuated, single dose vaccine, in the US in November 2023 [26]. IXCHIQ is indicated for the prevention of disease caused by CHIKV in individuals 18 years of age and older who are at increased risk of exposure to CHIKV [27]. This vaccine offers an additional layer of defense, providing opportunities for targeted immunization strategies to complement vector control.

Although Miami has not yet experienced a large-scale chikungunya outbreak, the risk of introduction remains substantial due to ongoing transmission in countries such as Brazil and the continued importation of cases into Florida [19, 28]. In this study, we present an exploratory, scenario-based modeling analysis to estimate the possible size of a chikungunya outbreak in Miami and to evaluate the effectiveness of an emergency vaccination program with IXCHIQ. This forward-looking approach provides evidence to support public health preparedness in Miami and other regions vulnerable to chikungunya introduction by helping decision-makers anticipate outbreak scale, understand the role of mosquito abundance, evaluate vaccination impact, and allocate resources efficiently – critical for justifying resource allocation and ensuring timely and targeted intervention planning.

Methods

Model description

We constructed a stochastic vector-host model in Microsoft Excel to simulate the dynamics of a potential CHIKV outbreak in Miami, Florida. The model assumed a CHIKV naïve population, so all individuals have a risk of contracting CHIKV and there is no natural immunity at model start. The human host population was divided into Susceptible (Sh), Exposed (Eh), Infectious (Ih), and Recovered (Rh) compartments, and the mosquito vector population in Susceptible (Sm), Exposed (Em), and Infectious (Im) compartments (Fig. 1). In Miami, the ratio of Ae. aegypti to Ae. albopictus is approximately 12:1 and potentially as high as 166:1 in more urban areas [9]. To simplify the model and account for the limited role of Ae. albopictus in disease transmission in urban areas, only Ae. aegypti was considered for this model.

Schematic representation of the SEIR-SEI model, showing the human and mosquito compartments and the vaccination scenario. Sh = Susceptible humans, Eh = Exposed humans, Ih = Infectious humans, Rh = Recovered humans, Sm = Susceptible mosquitoes, Em = Exposed mosquitoes, Im = Infectious mosquitoes

The model assumed an initial human population of 455,924, reflecting the population size of urban Miami as of July 1 st, 2023 [29]. The simulation started with the introduction of one exposed individual. The basic structure of the stochastic SEIR-SEI model was based on ordinary differential equations (ODEs), which describe how humans and mosquitoes transition between compartments in the model. To reflect the dynamics of CHIKV outbreaks, the model was run for one year, as previous CHIKV outbreaks typically do not exceed this duration [3]. A timestep of 0.2 days was chosen to ensure calculation accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. Stochasticity was introduced to the model by applying Poisson sampling to the transition rates between the compartments calculated from the ODEs at each timestep. For each continuous transition rate, a discrete number was drawn with a Poisson distribution, with a mean equal to the expected transition rate derived from the ODEs. Monte Carlo simulations were conducted by running the model 250 times for each starting month across all 12 months, resulting in 3000 simulations per scenario. This number of iterations was selected based on a convergence analysis, which assessed the stability of the main outcomes as the number of iterations increased, see Appendix 1. This approach accounted for the inherent randomness in biological processes, such as mosquito biting, CHIKV transmission, and human recovery, by simulating variability in the number of transitions that occur during each timestep. The main outcomes of interest were the outbreak likelihood throughout the year, the potential size of a CHIKV outbreak, and the influence of vaccination on CHIKV cases given an outbreak. The modelled cases represent all infections, regardless of whether they are reported or unreported, and irrespective of whether individuals are symptomatic or asymptomatic. An outbreak was defined as the occurrence of 20 CHIKV cases – chosen as a conservative and pragmatic threshold for triggering potential public health intervention – and its likelihood was calculated as the percentage of simulations that resulted in 20 or more cases. The median and interquartile range (IQR) were reported for outbreak sizes and related outcomes due to the non-normal distribution of data.

The mosquito compartment consisted of three health states: susceptible (Sm), exposed (Em), and infectious (Im). A ‘recovered’ health state was not incorporated as mosquitoes were assumed to be unable to recover and therefore remain infectious until they die. The initial mosquito population proportions were assumed to range from 75% to 90% depending on the starting month (see Appendix 2). As available trap data to validate these were limited and likely underestimate true mosquito abundance [30], these values were not empirically derived but served as seasonally informed estimates. The model allowed the mosquito population to adjust over time through temperature-dependent birth and death rates. Only the female mosquito population was modelled, as males do not contribute to transmission. The female population was derived using a fixed female: male ratio and this population determined the mosquito birth rate and dynamics across the susceptible, exposed, and infectious compartments (see Appendix 3). A female mosquito population threshold was used to prevent unrealistically high mosquito numbers. A mosquito transitioned from susceptible to exposed through contact with a CHIKV-infected human. Therefore, the probability of CHIKV infection in the susceptible state depended on the biting rate of mosquitoes (a), the mosquito susceptibility to infection (c), the number of susceptible mosquitoes (Sm), the number of infectious humans (Ih), and the total human population size (Nh). Furthermore, the transition from exposed to infectious corresponded to the inverse of the extrinsic incubation period (𝜔m). The mosquito birth rate (𝛿), mosquito mortality rate (𝜇), and extrinsic incubation period (𝜔m) were temperature-dependent inputs and were calculated using monthly average temperatures in Miami from 2013 to 2023.

Vector-host ratios

To account for the heterogeneous micro-spatial distribution and seasonal variation of Ae. aegypti mosquitoes in Miami, all model outcomes were assessed across varying vector-host ratios (VHRs). The VHR represents the ratio of the population size of Ae. aegypti mosquitoes to the population size of humans. We included four VHRs (0.75, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0), selected based on prior studies of chikungunya and dengue, although higher ratios have also been reported [15, 31]. We considered the VHR of 2.0 to represent a worst-case, upper-bound scenario. By accounting for VHR heterogeneity, we explored the impact of uncertainty in VHRs on model outcomes.

Vaccination

The model incorporated an emergency response vaccination program with IXCHIQ, initiated after the detection of an outbreak (defined as ≥ 20 chikungunya cases). The vaccine was modeled as a strategic measure to reduce the impact of a CHIKV outbreak by flattening the epidemic curve. An additional compartment was introduced into the model to represent vaccinated individuals (Fig. 1). Vaccination with IXCHIQ was assumed to provide 98.9% protection against CHIKV infection based on immunogenicity data from a phase 3 clinical trial [32]. The assumed protection was reached 14 days after administration of a single dose [33]. As two-year clinical data showed a durable immune response and our model focuses on outbreak response within a one-year time horizon, potential waning of immunity does not impact our findings and was therefore not included in our exploratory model [34]. A vaccination coverage of 10% was used in the base case, with this coverage assumed to be reached gradually over 30 days after initiation. To account for uncertainty regarding vaccination coverage, we assessed the cases prevented as function of the vaccination coverage. Scenarios with coverage rates of 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 30% over the total population were included. Because 18.4% of the Miami population is under 18 years and only people aged 18 years and older are eligible for vaccination, these coverage rates translated to 6.13%, 12.25%, 18.38%, 24.51%, and 36.76%, respectively, for the eligible population [29]. Both individuals in the susceptible and exposed compartments were eligible for vaccination, as their serostatus is unknown at the time of vaccination. We used the same random seeds for the vaccination and no vaccination scenarios to ensure identical baseline conditions across simulations.

Additional scenarios

To further explore the sensitivity of key assumptions, we examined two additional scenarios. First, we evaluated the effect of importing multiple cases simultaneously, compared to the base case of a single imported case. Second, we assessed the impact of delayed outbreak detection, modeling detection after 100 cases rather than 20 as in the base case. Further details on these scenarios are provided in Appendix 4.

Model validation

Because Miami has not experienced a large-scale CHIKV outbreak, empirical calibration of the model was challenging. To address this, we used a scenario-based, explorative, approach, examining multiple VHR and coverage rate scenarios, and conducted a convergence analysis to achieve stable estimates, as described above. External plausibility was evaluated by comparing model outputs with reported outbreaks in the Americas and prior chikungunya modeling studies. While empirical validation remains a priority for future work, this approach provides a proactive framework to examine potential outbreak dynamics and intervention strategies, supporting preparedness planning without waiting for an actual outbreak.

Results

Potential for CHIKV transmission in Miami throughout the year

Our results indicate that CHIKV transmission in Miami could occur at any point in the year, with optimal emergence of local transmission potential from May to August. The average outbreak likelihood is 9% at a VHR of 1, ranging between 4% in December and 14% in July. A VHR of 0.75 results in an average outbreak likelihood of 5% (range 1%–9%). A VHR of 1.5 and 2 resulted in an average outbreak likelihood of 15% (range 9%–19%) and 21% (range 14%–26%), respectively (Fig. 2).

Chikungunya virus outbreak likelihood per starting month. Shown is the likelihood of an outbreak of at least 20 cases for each vector-host ratio (VHR). In general, an outbreak can occur at any point in the year, with the highest transmission potential between May and August. With a VHR of 1, the likelihood of an outbreak ranges from 4% to 14%. A VHR of 2 leads to an outbreak likelihood that ranges from 14% to 26%

As with the likelihood of a CHIKV outbreak, variation in VHR also leads to substantial variability in case numbers, as shown within model runs where outbreaks occurred. At VHR 1, the median size of an outbreak was 224 (IQR: 50–740) in the first year of an outbreak, corresponding to 0.05% of the population, with up to 4,467 cases in the largest modelled simulation. At VHR 1.5, the median outbreak size was 21,053 (IQR: 8,900–36,990), corresponding to 4.6% of the population, while a VHR of 2 could result in 228,837 cases (IQR: 130,312–296,106) (Table 1).

Vaccination mitigates CHIKV transmission

An outbreak response vaccination program decreases the number of chikungunya cases in all VHR scenarios, although the impact is more profound in larger outbreaks. Given an outbreak and a vaccination coverage of 10%, vaccination leads to a median of 19% (IQR: 2.5% – 38%) reduction of chikungunya cases in Miami when a VHR of 1 is assumed. Vaccination reduced chikungunya cases with 58% (IQR: 48% – 66%) and 43% (IQR: 34% – 52%) when the VHRs were 1.5 and 2, respectively (Table 1). The lower reduction at a VHR of 2 reflects that, under higher transmission intensity, the outbreak growth exceeds what can be mitigated with a vaccination coverage of 10%. In some model runs, particularly those with fewer than 50 cases, vaccination had no measurable impact because of the small outbreak size and the start of vaccination only after 20 detected cases.

Vaccination consistently reduced outbreak size across all VHRs, with greater impact at higher coverage levels (Fig. 3). For example, at 10% coverage, the median outbreak size was reduced by 2%, 28%, 64%, and 47% at VHRs of 0.75, 1, 1.5, and 2, respectively. At 30% coverage, reductions ranged from 10% (VHR 0.75) to 95% (VHR 1.5). Vaccination was less impactful at a VHR of 2 than at 1.5 across all coverage rates. These findings demonstrate the need for a higher coverage rate when higher VHRs are encountered, as a larger outbreak requires more vaccines to prevent the same relative number of cases.

Scenario analysis

Importing 10 cases simultaneously led to an increase in outbreak size compared to the base case of a single imported case. At a VHR of 1, the outbreak size increased nearly tenfold, from 220 to 2,025 chikungunya cases. At a VHR of 1.5, cases increased more than threefold, from 21,053 to 69,009. Delayed outbreak detection – from 20 to 100 cases – also led to larger outbreaks, with increases ranging from 57 (VHR 1) to 7,062 additional cases (VHR 2) (Table 2). In the VHR 0.75 scenario, vaccination would not be initiated at all if an outbreak were only detected after 100 cases.

Discussion

Our study provides a conceptual framework to explore how chikungunya outbreaks in Miami might unfold and how emergency vaccination could mitigate their impact, serving as an early evaluation to inform preparedness planning, guide public health strategies, and stimulate future research. The results demonstrate that Miami could experience a CHIKV outbreak year-round, and that outbreak size is dependent on not only the month of introduction, but also the VHR. We found that the outbreak likelihood is particularly sensitive to the VHR, with the probability increasing from 9% at a VHR of 1 to 21% at a VHR of 2. Our results suggest transmission is possible throughout the year, with peak potential between May and August. The key concern with year-round susceptibility is that CHIKV outbreaks may not subside due to seasonal forcing alone, making human intervention essential to control spread. Our results align with prior studies indicating a continuous local transmission risk in Miami [15, 16, 35]. However, a 2012 study by Ruiz-Moreno et al. estimated a much higher likelihood of transmission at 52.5% at a VHR of 1, assuming mosquitoes only feed on human blood [15]. This difference likely stems from methodological choices: while Ruiz-Moreno et al. analyzed the risk of at least one successful transmission, we specifically looked at the risk of an outbreak, defined as at least 20 chikungunya cases. This difference highlights that one successful, autochthonous CHIKV transmission event in Miami is probable but does not always cause a sustained outbreak. Additionally, our model incorporates temperature effects directly on mosquito birth rate, mortality rate, and extrinsic incubation period, whereas Ruiz-Moreno et al. included additional mosquito compartments with temperature-dependent effects on mosquito life stages. These structural model differences likely influence mosquito population dynamics, thereby affecting outbreak risk outputs.

Beyond outbreak likelihood, our model highlights substantial variation in outbreak size depending on the VHR, with chikungunya cases ranging from 48 (VHR 0.75) to 228,837 (VHR 2). In our model, the VHR is a scenario-based input used to explore how different levels of mosquito abundance affect chikungunya transmission dynamics and intervention outcomes. While a VHR of 2 represents the upper-bound estimate, it is not implausible: this value has been described as the upper limit for dengue transmission in Miami [35], and other studies have considered VHR scenarios up to 3 [15] or even 10 [31]. We did not estimate VHRs from empirical data, as mosquito counts are difficult to obtain accurately [30]. Instead, the VHR serves as a conceptual proxy for mosquito burden relative to the human population. By varying the VHR across a plausible range, we capture a spectrum of outbreak scenarios that support planning under uncertainty and spatial variability in vector density. Importantly, our VHR estimates must be considered in the context of existing vector control measures, as changes in vector control measures can influence VHRs. Seasonal fluctuations further complicate estimates, as previous research showed that the VHR can exceed 1 in July and August, potentially leading to larger outbreaks when CHIKV transmission begins during these months [36].

Due to the absence of prior large-scale CHIKV outbreaks in Miami or similarly sized U.S. cities, direct validation of our model is challenging. To support model credibility, we conducted a convergence analysis to determine the number of runs needed for stable estimates of key outcomes. Additionally, a comparable outbreak in Paramaribo, Suriname, where 1,336 cases were reported over nine months in 2015–2016 in a population of approximately 241,000, suggests that several thousand cases in Miami (with roughly double the population) are plausible under VHR 1–1.5.5 scenarios [37]. This aligns with other outbreak patterns in the Americas, where reported case numbers have ranged from 50 in a neighbourhood in Salvador, Brazil (2017), to nearly 196,000 in Ceará, Brazil (2016–2017) [3, 38, 39]. Typically, outbreaks lasting less than a year, as modelled in our study, resulted in a few hundred cases, except for the 2014 outbreak in Puerto Rico, with over 28,000 reported cases (of which around 4,500 were confirmed) [3, 40]. While reporting quality varies, these examples support the credibility of our projections. Our results also align with prior modelling work: Ruiz-Moreno et al. estimated that up to 25% of Miami’s population could be infected with CHIKV [15], which translates to over 114,000 people based on 2024 population estimates. While that study reported prevalence and we report cumulative cases, both underscore the potential for large outbreaks if transmission is sustained. Based on our findings, we estimate that Miami’s VHR likely ranges between 1 and 1.5 in peak transmission months, highlighting the need for seasonally adjusted vector control and outbreak response strategies.

An initial chikungunya case in Miami could be introduced by a traveller returning from an endemic or outbreak-prone region, particularly during periods of increased international travel such as holidays. Simultaneous introduction of multiple infected individuals could accelerate transmission even more and lead to larger outbreaks, as supported by our scenario analysis showing higher peak incidence and overall case counts. Miami’s strong ties with the Caribbean and Latin America, regions with recurrent CHIKV transmission, make such introductions more likely during regional outbreaks. Local transmission potential is predicted to be highest between May and August, when Ae. aegypti populations peak and environmental conditions are most favourable. Given that Miami’s human population is mostly unexposed and therefore susceptible to CHIKV, as well as the present and projected expansion of Ae. aegypti populations due to extrinsic factors such as urbanization, aging neighbourhood infrastructure, and climate change [5], the risk of substantial outbreaks remains, and could increase in the future. Miami’s 2016 Zika outbreak with over 200 locally transmitted cases [41], though involving a different virus, underscores the city’s vulnerability to arboviral transmission and highlights the importance of targeted preparedness during periods of elevated risk.

Although citywide percentages of chikungunya cases may appear low under moderate VHRs, outbreaks often manifest heterogeneously, with transmission concentrated in specific areas [16, 42]. This localized clustering can result in substantially higher attack rates within certain neighbourhoods, leading to a substantial disease burden. Although our model did not explicitly evaluate variable VHRs across urban Miami, it is known that the VHR varies across urban settings due to factors such as human population density, housing quality, and local vector control efforts [43–47]. Notably, higher VHRs are often associated with lower socioeconomic status, exacerbating health disparities [48]. Understanding this uneven spread is crucial because the impacts of an outbreak extend beyond immediate illness. More than 40% of infected individuals may develop chronic symptoms [8, 9], resulting in long-term disability and increased healthcare costs [8, 49]. Additionally, outbreaks disrupt productivity through lost workdays, medical expenses [2, 44], and economic consequences for investment, trade, and tourism [44]. Given this, decision-makers must consider the localized nature of CHIKV outbreaks when allocating resources and implementing targeted interventions to protect the most affected communities.

A responsive vaccination program could substantially reduce chikungunya cases during an outbreak, particularly for larger outbreaks, as shown in our study. In some model runs with smaller outbreaks, vaccination had no measurable impact, reflecting the challenge of initiating effective intervention when the outbreak trajectory is still uncertain. The coverage rate plays an important role in vaccine effectiveness, with larger outbreaks needing a higher coverage rate to catch up on transmission rates. Vaccination effectiveness depends on coverage, as more vaccinated individuals prevent the infection from spreading and further flatten the outbreak curve [50]. Our base case assumed a conservative 10% coverage rate, though real-world uptake during an outbreak may be higher. For example, 78% of the population in Florida received at least one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine within the first year, of which 37% received the vaccine within the first 30 days [51]. While chikungunya vaccine demand may be lower due to different disease dynamics and a lower perceived risk, this suggests a willingness to vaccinate in response to an outbreak.

Nevertheless, vaccine hesitancy and public perception remain important barriers that could reduce uptake, particularly if the outbreak is small or perceived as low risk. Additionally, vaccination programs require substantial resources, including costs related to vaccine procurement, distribution, and administration. Scenario analysis showed that delaying the start of vaccination – initiating after 100 reported cases instead of 20 – resulted in significantly larger outbreaks, underscoring the importance of a timely response. Targeted vaccination in high-risk neighbourhoods, where outbreaks tend to cluster, may enhance cost-effectiveness by focusing resources on areas with greatest transmission risk. Given that chikungunya outbreaks are often localized, targeting vaccination efforts in specific high-risk neighbourhoods may increase local coverage rates and optimize resource use, improving both the efficiency and impact of the response. Careful planning is therefore essential to balance vaccine acceptance, resource allocation, and outbreak control to optimize the impact of reactive vaccination efforts.

Our study has several limitations. Climate change impacts, while their role in CHIKV transmission is well-established [4, 5], were not explicitly modelled as the focus was on the current situation rather than hypothesizing future scenarios. Some model parameters, such as biting rate and transmission probabilities, can vary with temperature, but were held constant based on robust data for model simplicity. Also, our model does not explicitly include density-dependent constraints on mosquito population growth, such as carrying capacity. Instead, we opted for a female mosquito population threshold to prevent unrealistically high mosquito numbers. Future studies could incorporate additional details such as these to improve precision and assess outbreak potential under varying conditions. Additionally, our model was not calibrated using real-world outbreak data. In the absence of prior large-scale CHIKV outbreaks in Miami or comparable U.S. cities, opportunities for direct model validation are limited. To address this, we used a scenario-based, stochastic approach and explored different VHRs to reflect uncertainty and highlight the range of potential outcomes. Additionally, a convergence analysis informed on the number of runs needed for stable estimates of key outcomes. While this lack of empirical validation may limit the precision of our projections for direct public health planning, our findings still provide valuable directional insights for preparedness efforts and highlight key factors influencing outbreak dynamics. Model calibration and validation using empirical data remain a priority for future work should larger outbreaks occur. Our model was developed in Microsoft Excel, which naturally constrained runtime and scalability. However, this choice also ensured transparency and accessibility. For our study aims, exploring outbreak dynamics and vaccination strategies, Excel provides a practical and user-friendly platform. We used daily temperature data to generate monthly averages for alignment with our time steps, which may obscure short-term fluctuations like cold snaps that affect mosquito survival. Also, we did not distinguish between symptomatic and asymptomatic cases and future research could incorporate this, especially once the model is validated against real-world data and more detailed epidemiological inputs become available. Furthermore, we assumed that vaccination coverage is applied homogeneously across the entire population of urban Miami. However, in real-world outbreak response, vaccination strategies can be more spatially targeted. If early surveillance detects an outbreak confined to specific areas, achieving high local vaccination coverage could achieve similar transmission reductions with fewer doses. Prioritizing high-risk groups, such as older adults or individuals with comorbidities, could further enhance the impact of vaccination campaigns from a morbidity or disability averted perspective.

An important consideration for future research is determining the optimal timing of vaccination during an outbreak. For instance, if a small outbreak occurs in March, early intervention with vaccination could potentially prevent a larger outbreak in July. Additionally, earlier vaccination at lower case thresholds (e.g., when cases reach 5 or 10) could alter outbreak dynamics substantially. This highlights the need for improved diagnostics in humans to enable timely detection and response. Future research incorporating spatial heterogeneity, adaptive vaccination strategies, and early intervention thresholds could improve outbreak preparedness and response planning.

Conclusion

Our modeling highlights the substantial risk of a CHIKV outbreak in Miami and the critical role of vector control and emergency vaccination in mitigating its impact. This scenario-based framework provides forward-looking insights into outbreak dynamics and the potential benefits of emergency vaccination. Targeted interventions in high-risk areas, along with proactive surveillance and rapid response strategies, could be especially effective. As CHIKV outbreaks are expected to become more frequent, these findings support early preparedness and public health planning in Miami and other subtropical regions at risk.
