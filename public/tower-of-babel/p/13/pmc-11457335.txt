Analyzing risk factors and constructing a predictive model for superficial esophageal carcinoma with submucosal infiltration exceeding 200 micrometers
Authors: Yutong Cui, Zichen Luo, Xiaobo Wang, Shiqi Liang, Guangbing Hu, Xinrui Chen, Ji Zuo, Lu Zhou, Haiyang Guo, Xianfei Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSubmucosal infiltration of less than 200 μm is considered an indication for endoscopic surgery in cases of superficial esophageal cancer and precancerous lesions. This study aims to identify the risk factors associated with submucosal infiltration exceeding 200 micrometers in early esophageal cancer and precancerous lesions, as well as to establish and validate an accompanying predictive model.MethodsRisk factors were identified through least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and multivariate logistic regression. Various machine learning (ML) classification models were tested to develop and evaluate the most effective predictive model, with Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) employed for model visualization.ResultsPredictive factors for early esophageal invasion into the submucosa included endoscopic ultrasonography or magnifying endoscopy> SM1(P<0.001,OR = 3.972,95%CI 2.161–7.478), esophageal wall thickening(P<0.001,OR = 12.924,95%CI,5.299–33.96), intake of pickled foods(P=0.04,OR = 1.837,95%CI,1.03–3.307), platelet-lymphocyte ratio(P<0.001,OR = 0.284,95%CI,0.137–0.556), tumor size(P<0.027,OR = 2.369,95%CI,1.128–5.267), the percentage of circumferential mucosal defect(P<0.001,OR = 5.286,95%CI,2.671–10.723), and preoperative pathological type(P<0.001,OR = 4.079,95%CI,2.254–7.476). The logistic regression model constructed from the identified risk factors was found to be the optimal model, demonstrating high efficacy with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.922 in the training set, 0.899 in the validation set, and 0.850 in the test set.ConclusionA logistic regression model complemented by SHAP visualizations effectively identifies early esophageal cancer reaching 200 micrometers into the submucosa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03442-1.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer represents a major global health challenge, ranking as the sixth leading cause of cancer-related deaths and accounting for 3.2% of all cancer fatalities annually [1]. In recent years, the incidence has experienced a slight decline [2]. surgical interventions remain common but are often associated with high costs, prolonged recovery, significant trauma, extended hospital stays, and risk of complications. Advances in endoscopic diagnosis and treatment have markedly improved survival rates for early-stage esophageal cancer [3, 4]. Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection (ESD) is now favored due to its cost-effectiveness, rapid recovery, minimal trauma, and shorter hospital stay [5, 6]. Currently, clinical guidelines in China, the United States, and Europe recommend the use of ESD for patients with T1a esophageal cancer without lymph node metastasis. However, an increasing number of studies indicate that ESD can also be effective for patients with T1b cancer, albeit with the need for strict screening protocols [7, 8]. Adhering solely to existing guidelines may lead to overtreatment in certain patients, causing distress and wasting medical resources. Japanese guidelines first endorse ESD for superficial submucosal cancers (SM1, depth ≤ 200 μm) when performed by skilled endoscopists [9]. Despite these advances, preoperative staging, especially for submucosal tumors, can be inaccurate. Our study found that 6.2% of patients required additional surgical interventions post-ESD due to deeper tumor infiltration than initially diagnosed, causing considerable patient distress. Similar findings have been reported elsewhere, where 23 patients needed esophagectomy after ESD despite clear margins, due to undetected submucosal infiltration [10]. In a multicenter study, 66.6% (20 out of 30) of patients who underwent additional surgery following endoscopic non-curative excision did so due to postoperative infiltration into the submucosa [11].

This study seeks to establish the most effective diagnostic prediction model for early esophageal cancer by analyzing patients’ clinical data. The objective is to enhance early treatment strategies, improve the accuracy of invasive depth assessment, and reduce the risks of both overtreatment and undertreatment.

Materials and methods

Inclusion criteria

(1) For preoperative assessment, endoscopic ultrasound, contrast-enhanced chest and abdominal CT, or lymph node biopsy should be utilized to exclude peripheral lymph node involvement and distant metastasis. (2) Postoperative histopathological examination confirmed high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (Tis) or mucosal/submucosal invasion (T1 stage) according to World Health Organization criteria [12].

Exclusion criteria

(1) Histopathological diagnosis of esophageal adenocarcinoma, papilloma, or spindle cell tumor following ESD. (2) Missing data prevalence not exceeding 15%. (3) No history of malignant tumors.

Depth of infiltration classification

Following the 2022 Japan Esophageal Cancer Guidelines [9], the depth of tumor infiltration assessed in postoperative biopsies was categorized into groups based on the extension to one-third of the submucosa (200 micrometers).

Study variables

The investigation included 30 variables, Collecting a range of clinical and histopathological factors pertinent to the prognosis and management of esophageal lesions.

encompassing clinical: Age, gender, Preoperative neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and platelet-neutrophil ratio (PNR), BMI, hypertension, heart disdase, diabetes, Chronickidney disease, COPD, Family history, Endoscopic ultrasonography or magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (EUS OR Me-NBI, EOM), esophageal wall thickens, esophageal stricture, Tumor, enhancement, Lesion location, Tumor size, Percentage of the circumferential mucosal defect (PCMD), Depth of Infiltration, Paris classification, Preoperative pathological type( PPT), Depth of infiltration.

lifestyle data: Long-term smoking, long-term drinking, pickled food, high-temperature food intake, fried food, fruit and vegetable intake, Oral hygiene, eating speed.

ESD indications

ESD is indicated for patients with infiltration ≤ SM1 and no lymph node metastasis. For lesions with infiltration greater than SM1 but not exceeding the submucosal layer (T1), ESD may be considered following evaluation by experienced endoscopists and obtaining informed consent from patients and their families, provided there is no lymph node invasion. However, pathological biopsy results should be closely monitored, and more rigorous follow-up is recommended.

Definitions

Lesion location

Lesions within the esophagus were classified based on the latest sectional criteria into four regions [13]: (1) Cervical esophagus, 15–20 cm from the upper incisor. (2) Upper thoracic esophagus, 20–25 cm from the upper incisor. (3) Middle thoracic esophagus, 25–30 cm from the upper incisor. (4) Lower thoracic esophagus, 30–40 cm from the upper incisor.

Depth of tissue invasion

According to the 2002 Paris Criteria for Gastrointestinal Tumors [14]. depth of lesion infiltration is classified as M (mucosal layer) and SM (submucosal layer). The M category includes M1 (epithelium), M2 (lamina propria), and M3 (muscularis mucosae). The SM category is divided into SM1 (upper third), SM2 (middle third), and SM3 (lower third) of the submucosa.

Lifestyle assessment

Lifestyle factors are assessed using the Liu and Lei scoring method [15, 16]. focusing on dietary habits (e.g., protein intake, fruits and vegetables, pickled and fried foods), oral hygiene (e.g., teeth brushing frequency), and hot water intake, Frequency is classified as occasional for ≤ 3 times per week and frequent for > 3 times per week. Eating speed are categorized as normal (20–40 min), fast (< 20 min), or slow (> 40 min). Hot water intake is gauged based on a self-perceived temperature threshold. Smoking or alcohol consumption exceeding three months is defined as long-term.

Esophageal stricture

Presence of esophageal stricture is indicated by fluid retention observed above the esophagus on CT scans.

Esophageal wall thickness

Determined via CT imaging, esophageal wall thickness is categorized as slight (5–7 mm) or significant (> 7 mm).

Disease definition

Cardiac disease is defined as an ejection fraction of less than 50%. Chronic kidney disease is characterized by a persistent decline in the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) to less than 60 mL/min·1.73 m² for more than three months. A family history is defined as having esophageal cancer or precancerous lesions in direct relatives within the past three generations. Hypertension is defined as three blood pressure measurements in a resting state, all exceeding 140/90 mmHg. The diagnostic criteria for diabetes include: Fasting Blood Glucose: ≥ 7.0 mmol/L. Postprandial Blood Glucose (2 h after a glucose load): ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, assessed via oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Random Blood Glucose: ≥ 11.1 mmol/L with typical symptoms of diabetes. HbA1c: ≥ 6.5%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of variables

In analyzing both the test and training datasets, continuous variables were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR), with comparisons conducted via the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and assessed using Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as bilateral p-values less than 0.05. Analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.3 with the gtsummary package version 1.7.2, and Python analyses utilized the scikit-learn package version 1.1.3.

Model development and validation

Predictive models were established by identifying characteristic factors from independent variables, with patients segmented into training and experimental groups. Multiple classification models were applied to evaluate the significance of each index within the training set, testing various model configurations, and select best model. A SHAP model was also developed for visual interpretation of model outputs. The process involved: (1). Data Processing: Due to the randomness of missing data, imputation was performed using K Nearest Neighbors (KNN) imputation in Python (scikit-learn = 1.1.3). Optimal clinical thresholds for continuous variables were determined through ROC curve analysis(Age, PLR, NLR, PNR, BMI, Tumor size, PCMD). For example, age was categorized into ≥ 71 and < 71 years based on a threshold of 71 years. This method was consistently applied to all relevant continuous variables to convert them into categorical forms. (2). Data Segmentation: Data were randomly divided into a 70% training set (n = 282) and a 30% validation set (n = 122) using R software (gtsummary: 1.7.2). (3). Feature Selection: LASSO regression was employed to mitigate collinearity, conducted in R (glmnet: 4.1.8), followed by multifactor logistic regression (R version: 4.2.3, with packages pROC = 1.18.5, rms = 6.7.1, forestploter = 1.1.1, dplyr = 1.1.4, MASS = 7.3.60), selecting variables with p-values < 0.05. The LASSO approach effectively shrank variable coefficients, reducing the risk of overfitting and addressing collinearity issues. Multifactor logistic regression further controlled for potential confounding factors. (4). Multi-Model Classification Analysis: A comprehensive classification analysis was conducted using an array of machine learning models, including eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Logistic Regression, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Random Forest Classifier, Adaptive Boostint (AdBoost), K-Nearest-Neighbors (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), and Gaussian Naïve Bayes(GNB). The model parameters were iteratively trained and tested across 10 repeated samples to evaluate the significance of various indicators. Model performance was assessed using Receiver Operating Curve (ROC), Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), and calibration curves to identify the optimal model. A Precision-Recall (PR) curve, generated using Python (scikit-learn = 1.1.3), supplemented these evaluations by providing additional insights into model accuracy and recall [17]. (5). Model Training, Testing, and Validation: The best-performing model was rigorously trained, tested, and validated, with the training phase incorporating 5-fold cross-validation. The evaluation extended to the test set to ensure robustness. Learning curves were plotted using Python (scikit-learn = 1.1.3) to assess the adaptability and stability of the model across both training and validation phases. (6). SHAP interpretation: The contribution of individual features to the predicted outcomes was quantified using SHAP values (Python, shap version 0.43.0). This analysis facilitated the visualization of feature importance and their impact on the model, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the results. Additionally, to illustrate the practical utility of the model in clinical settings, three representative patient cases were detailed, showcasing the model’s application in real-world scenarios.

The study included 304 endoscopic and 100 surgical interventions for treating these lesions. Follow-up evaluations were conducted 1 to 3 months after surgery. Of these, 112 patients exhibited infiltration beyond SM1, while 292 patients had infiltration equal to or less than SM1. The baseline characteristics of these groups are presented in Table 1. The p-values, which exceed 0.05, indicate that the data partitioning is stable.

Identification risk factors of infiltrating beyond 200 μm into the submucosa

LASSO regression analysis

To identify risk factors associated with penetration beyond 200 μm into the submucosa, LASSO regression was utilized. The coefficient profile and cross-validation curve for this analysis are shown in Fig. 1(a) and Fig. 1(b), respectively. The standard error for the optimal lambda in the minimum distance calculation was 0.048. Significant variables identified included PLR, pickled food intake, eating speed, EOM, esophageal wall thickening, esophageal stricture, tumor size, PCMD, and PPT.

Model evaluation and comparative analysis

Various machine learning models were employed for data sample classification. Parameter configurations for each model are detailed in Tables 3 and 4 (supplementary materials), Results indicated that XGBoost, RandomForest, and LightGBM performed best in the training set, while Logistic Regression excelled in the validation set (Fig. 2a and b). Note that AUC measures predictive accuracy but does not indicate clinical applicability or comparative superiority [18, 19]. The calibration curve evaluated the predictive accuracy of the Logistic Regression model (Fig. 2c). DCA showed that Logistic Regression and GaussianNB had greater clinical practicability (Fig. 2d). Additionally, Logistic Regression showed superior performance in both training and validation sets in PR curves, with the highest AP value in the validation set (Fig. 2e and f). These analyses suggest that Logistic Regression may be the optimal model for clinical application.

Optimal model construction and evaluation

Five-fold cross-validation of the training set produced an average AUC of 0.922 (95% CI: 0.858–0.987). For the validation set, the average AUC was 0.899 (95% CI: 0.750-1.000), and the final model achieved an average AUC of 0.850 (95% CI: 0.802–0.898) in the test set (Fig. 3a and b, and 3c). Since the AUC in the validation set did not exceed that of the test set by more than 10%, the model fitting was considered successful. The positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) are 0.656 (95% CI, 0.624–0.688) and 0.898 (95% CI, 0.887–0.908) in the training set, respectively, while in the validation set, they are 0.604 (95% CI, 0.559–0.648) and 0.884 (95% CI, 0.852–0.916). Sensitivity and specificity in the training set are 0.799 (95% CI, 0.777–0.821) and 0.820 (95% CI, 0.794–0.845), respectively; in the validation set, they are 0.847 (95% CI, 0.804–0.889) and 0.767 (95% CI, 0.735–0.798). The stability and fit between the training and validation sets were confirmed by the learning curve (Fig. 3d). Therefore, the logistic model was deemed suitable for the classification task of this dataset.

Discussion

In recent years, endoscopic early diagnosis and treatment have emerged as predominant strategies for managing superficial squamous cell carcinoma and precancerous lesions of the esophagus, leading to significant clinical advancements [3, 4, 20]. However, guidelines for ESD exhibit notable variability. Recent research highlights a critical issue with stage T1b esophageal cancer, demonstrating that endoscopic treatment in T1b patients without lymph node metastasis can be as effective as surgical approaches [7, 8], especially for well-selected patients with shallow submucosal infiltration and favorable histological characteristics [21]. According to the 2022 Japan Esophageal Cancer Guidelines, ESD is recommended for superficial submucosal esophageal squamous cell carcinoma with SM1(defined as ≤ 200 μm) [9]. Nevertheless, preoperative assessment of invasion depth in early-stage esophageal cancer, particularly in submucosal tumors, remains a significant challenge. In our study, approximately 6.2% of patients initially treated endoscopically required subsequent surgical intervention after postoperative pathology revealed invasion depths beyond SM1. Consequently, this study aims to improve the accuracy of diagnosing early esophageal cancer invasion depth by collecting and analyzing clinical data from patients with early-stage esophageal cancer.

Utilizing LASSO and multiple logistic regression analyses, our study identified seven clinical variables from an initial set of 30 to evaluate the risk of submucosal invasion exceeding 200 μm in patients with early esophageal cancer. The study identified EOM, esophageal wall thickening, pickled food intake, PLR, tumor size, PCMD, and PPT as significant risk factors for invasion depths beyond SM1. Ding Dandan et al. highlight a notable correlation between tumor size and the depth of esophageal cancer invasion, noting that an increase in tumor length is associated with a higher likelihood of exceeding SM1 [22], This finding is consistent with our research(P=0.027, OR = 2.369,95%CI,1.128–5.267) and aligns with established clinical practice. Historically, magnifying endoscopy and EUS were the primary methods for assessing cancer invasion depth. When both Me-NBI and EUS indicated deep mucosal invasion, histopathology confirmed a mucosal cancer incidence of 94%. Conversely, when either Me-NBI or EUS suggested non-mucosal depth invasion, the incidence of mucosal cancer decreased to 21% [23]. Several studies have demonstrated the efficacy of EOM in diagnosing the depth of esophageal cancer invasion, but its accuracy is highly dependent on the endoscopist’s experience, with sensitivity decreasing to 69% among less experienced practitioners [24–26], Our research also corroborates EOM’s effectiveness (P < 0.01, OR = 3.972, 95% CI: 2.161–7.478). Additionally, some studies have suggested that incorporating EUS following either magnifying or non-magnifying endoscopy does not significantly reduce overdiagnosis or improve the accuracy of determining cancer invasion depth [27].

Previous research has utilized inflammatory markers such as NLR, PLR, and LMR to predict tumor stage, differentiation, invasion, and prognosis in various cancers. Studies have demonstrated that a high NLR is associated with poor prognosis in esophageal cancer [28–30] and Our analysis indicates that PLR is diagnostically significant in determining the depth of esophageal cancer invasion (P < 0.01; OR = 0.284; 95% CI: 0.137–0.556), which is consistent with the findings of Ding [23]. The inflammatory microenvironment supports tumor progression through mechanisms such as growth promotion, invasion, angiogenesis, immune evasion, chemotherapy resistance, and metastasis [30]. The antitumor effects are primarily driven by a lymphocyte-dependent cellular immune response [31], In esophageal cancer, research indicates that the immunosuppressive environment and persistent antigen stimulation contribute to T cell exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment, nd is associated with elevated expression of C0-CD8 [32]. Reduced lymphocyte counts are linked to tumor immune escape, he invasive region of esophageal cancer contains significantly fewer T cells compared to other tumor regions, This occurs because cancer cells lose the ability to present antigens as a result of the downregulation of MHC class I [33]. Platelets can deliver a variety of pro-angiogenic factors to tumors and stimulate tumor cells to express these factors [34], Platelets have long been recognized as a primary source of vascular endothelial growth factor, platelet-derived growth factor, and basic fibroblast growth factor, all of which contribute to tumor growth [35, 36]. Therefore, PLR serves as a predictor of the depth of esophageal cancer invasion; however, its effectiveness requires validation through larger clinical studies and external assessments.

Several clinical factors may influence the development of esophageal carcinoma. Previous studies have identified a link between the consumption of pickled vegetables and salted meats—dietary habits prevalent in high-risk areas—and the onset of esophageal carcinoma [37, 38] This correlation is associated with P450 2C19 (CYP2C19) gene polymorphisms [39] and the presence of N-nitroso compounds(NOC) [40]. Our multivariate analysis demonstrated a significant association between the consumption of preserved foods and early esophageal cancer invasion depth (P = 0.04, OR = 1.837, 95% CI: 1.03–3.307). However, no significant correlations were observed with other dietary or lifestyle factors, including high-protein diets, fruit and vegetable intake, high-temperature diets, fried foods, eating speed, long-term smoking, or alcohol consumption (P > 0.05). CYP2C19 plays a role in the detoxification and inactivation of potential carcinogens [41], as well as the bioactivation of specific environmental precarcinogens, leading to the production of reactive DNA-binding metabolites [42–44], Nitrates present in preserved foods are absorbed by the body through the intestines. The subsequent decomposition of these nitrates into nitrosamines enhances the susceptibility of CYP2C19 to risk gene mutations (GA/AA) [39]. Excessive salt intake can damage esophageal tissue, while frequent consumption of highly acidic pickles may cause mucosal inflammation [45], potentially accelerating the progression of esophageal cancer or masking clinical symptoms of swallowing obstruction, thereby delaying detection until more advanced stages. Therefore, it is recommended to reduce the intake of preserved foods in high-risk areas for esophageal cancer to decrease its incidence.

Endoscopic examination is superior to contrast-enhanced CT for diagnosing early esophageal cancer, especially for primary tumors, where contrast-enhanced CT has an accuracy of only 51.4% [46]. Despite these limitations, contrast-enhanced CT remains the primary method for the initial clinical detection of esophageal cancer. A wall thickness greater than 5 mm suggests a potential tumor, while a thickness exceeding 7 mm indicates a higher likelihood of malignancy [47]. Our study is the first to incorporate imaging data into a multifactorial analysis, identifying esophageal wall thickening (> 7 mm) as a risk factor for invasion depth exceeding SM1(P<0.001,OR = 12.924,95%CI,5.299–33.96). Further clinical research is needed to validate its accuracy and efficacy.

The population in northeastern Sichuan exhibits a strong preference for pickled vegetables and resides in a region with a high incidence of esophageal cancer. Consequently, this study is representative and has the potential for broader application to additional populations and multicenter research. Despite utilizing various clinical experiences to predict invasion depths exceeding 200 μm in T1 esophageal cancer, clinicians often rely on personal judgment rather than a comprehensive predictive model. This study evaluated eight machine learning models, identifying logistic regression as the most effective. We employed 5-fold cross-validation, observing consistent results across repeated analyses of both training and validation sets, with an AUC of 0.922 in the training set and 0.899 in the validation set. The model’s performance was assessed using ROC curves, DCA, sensitivity, NPV, PPV, calibration curves, and PR curves. To visually represent the clinicians’ predictions, the model was explained using the SHAP method.

However, this study has several limitations. First, as a single-center, retrospective study with a small sample size, it requires validation through large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies to confirm its clinical applicability. Secondly, the assessment of patients’ lifestyle habits and other indicators involves a degree of subjectivity, necessitating further quantitative analysis to establish more precise criteria. If prospective studies are conducted in the future, wearable health monitoring devices could provide detailed recordings of daily life, facilitating easier evaluation. Moreover, random partitioning introduces uncertainty that may lead to potential errors; Therefore, employing more precise methods, such as stratified sampling, may be essential for enhancing the reliability of the model. Finally, the use of ROC curve thresholds may not always capture the clinically most meaningful thresholds and may oversimplify the nuances of the data. Thus, exploring alternative approaches is necessary.

Conclusions

In summary, a machine learning-based predictive model was developed, with logistic regression demonstrating the best performance. The SHAP approach was employed for individualized risk assessment to address the lack of established theoretical prediction models for diagnosing early esophageal cancer. This method can assist clinicians in tailoring treatments to individual patient risks, potentially reducing unnecessary medical burdens resulting from diagnostic errors.
