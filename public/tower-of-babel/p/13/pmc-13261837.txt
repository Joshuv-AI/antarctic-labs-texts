Analysis of the effects and mechanisms of carbon emission trading policies on the risk-taking behavior of high-carbon enterprises
Authors: Jiali Tian, Zhipeng Wu, Jinwei Wang, Jiachao Peng, Huiyue Luo, Shuke Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe carbon emissions trading scheme serves as a core mechanism in China’s climate policy, playing a crucial role in advancing the nation’s dual carbon goals. Examining the impact of this strategy on risk-taking behavior in high-carbon enterprises and its underlying mechanisms has substantial theoretical and practical significance for attaining integrated environmental and economic development. This research utilizes a difference-in-differences methodology to analyze the effect of policy on risk-taking behavior in high-carbon organizations by matching panel data from publicly listed enterprises in China’s Shanghai and Shenzhen A-share markets from 2008 to 2024. Research indicates that carbon emissions trading policy substantially diminishes risk-taking behaviors in high-carbon enterprises. Heterogeneous tests reveal that the most significant suppression effects are observed in low-competition enterprises, state-owned enterprises, over-invested enterprises, declining enterprises, and enterprises located in western and eastern areas, whereas high-carbon enterprises in the central region have a promotion impact. Mechanism analysis indicates that the policy exacerbates suppression by limiting management risk appetites and reinforcing finance constraints . These findings provide empirical evidence regarding market-based incentive mechanisms for emissions reduction in China. By assessing the influence of carbon emissions trading policy on risk-taking behaviors in high-carbon enterprises, they establish a foundation for the transformation and advancement of these enterprises.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-026-00427-3.Keywords: Carbon Emission Trading Policy, High-Carbon Industries, Risk-Taking Level, Difference-in-Differences Model

Introduction

With the pursuit of carbon neutrality gaining global momentum, carbon emissions trading has established itself as a pivotal market mechanism for cutting emissions and a key driver of national low-carbon transitions [1]. The fundamental method internalizes the external costs of carbon emissions by establishing total emission ceilings and allowing enterprises to trade carbon emission allowances, thus creating incentives for reduction while imposing cost constraints [2]. The European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) operates on an international scale. Subsequent to its 2005 inception, nations such as Japan, the United States, South Korea, and Australia have implemented their respective national carbon emissions trading policies. The year 2011 marked the beginning of China’s pilot carbon market, which was first launched in seven designated regions. In August 2025, China released the “Opinions of the General Office of the CPC Central Committee and the General Office of the State Council on Promoting Green and Low-Carbon Transformation and Strengthening the National Carbon Market,” which established the objective of “achieving fundamental coverage of the national carbon emissions trading market for principal emitting industries in the industrial sector by 2027,” indicating the carbon market’s evolution from pilot initiatives to comprehensive national coordination. Eight high-carbon industries—petrochemicals, chemicals, building materials, steel, non-ferrous metals, papermaking, electric power, and aviation—are facing unparalleled systemic pressure [3]. This pressure forces them to transform their production methods, technological pathways [4], and energy structures because of their substantial energy consumption and significant carbon dioxide emissions [5]. Market-driven emission reduction strategies—such as carbon quota distribution, fluctuations in carbon pricing, and regulatory compliance—transform business cost frameworks and significantly modify their risk landscapes. Carbon asset price volatility may induce operational risks, investments in low-carbon transitions may heighten financial uncertainty, and adherence to policy compliance may constrict profit margins in conventional corporate operations. Therefore, examining how carbon trading policies affect the risk-bearing capacity of high-carbon enterprises is essential to understand their transformation decisions and the policy outcomes.

Currently, the global carbon market is undergoing rapid expansion and deep evolution. In terms of its current status, the carbon market has become a core climate policy tool, with over 75 carbon pricing mechanisms in operation worldwide, including mandatory emissions trading systems (ETS) and carbon taxes, as well as voluntary carbon markets (VCMs). The share of global greenhouse gas emissions covered by these mechanisms continues to grow. Since China launched its pilot programs in 2011, the development of its carbon market has been rapid, with regional carbon markets established in pilot provinces and cities such as Beijing and Tianjin. In 2021, China initiated the construction of a national carbon market, becoming the largest carbon market in the world in terms of the scope of greenhouse gas emissions covered. On the theoretical front, the design of carbon markets is primarily based on Coase’s theorem, which aims to achieve cost-optimal emissions reduction through market transactions by clarifying the property rights of emissions allowances [6]. However, academic views on this issue are sharply divided. Some scholars argue that carbon markets can effectively discover prices, incentivize technological innovation, and reduce the overall societal cost of emissions reduction [7]. However, other scholars with differing views point out that the practical operation of carbon markets has fallen short of theoretical expectations, with many flaws [8]. In practice, an over-reliance on government allocation of initial property rights and market design has led to high transaction costs, among other issues. Therefore, focusing the research perspective on the corporate level, particularly examining how high-carbon enterprises adjust their risk perceptions, willingness to bear risks, and strategic decisions under carbon constraints, becomes a key link connecting macro policies with micro-level emission reduction behaviors, and is crucial for understanding the effectiveness of policies.

By introducing a market price on carbon, the emissions trading system fundamentally reshapes how enterprises perceive risk and catalyzes their shift toward low-carbon practices. This restructuring is evident in financial [9], operational [10], and reputational aspects [11]. This policy exacerbates market risk by increasing internal compliance costs and transmitting carbon price volatility [12], thereby amplifying stock price sensitivity and altering valuation frameworks, especially in high-emission sectors [13, 14]. The policy’s design mechanisms offer new operational risks in both operational and reputational dimensions, exacerbating compliance complexity and uncertainty in long-term strategic planning [15]. Carbon emission transparency improves environmental performance [16, 17], whereas poor business performance poses risks of reputational harm, market exclusion, and stricter financing conditions, ultimately resulting in concrete economic losses [18]. A review of the literature demonstrates the growing role of carbon emissions trading policy as a strategic imperative in corporate risk management. Nevertheless, current research mostly emphasizes the particular manifestations of corporate risk, neglecting the critical aspect of risk-taking, which is essential in strategic decision-making. A thorough investigation of changes in risk preferences among high-carbon enterprises and their underlying causes is notably absent.

Risk-taking, a fundamental aspect of corporate strategic decision-making, signifies an enterprise’s readiness and ability to accept uncertainty and invest resources in high-risk initiatives to achieve long-term benefits. It is shaped by various aspects, including corporate governance, ownership structure, and managerial attributes [19–21]. Prudent risk-taking is fundamental to enterprise innovation and value enhancement [22, 23]. In contrast to industrialized economies, Chinese enterprises demonstrate comparatively low levels of risk-taking [24], hindering the low-carbon transition process. In terms of research content, existing studies have primarily focused on the banking and financial industries, with limited attention given to enterprises in other sectors [25]. Moreover, most studies have examined these issues from a single perspective, such as corporate governance, executive characteristics, or macroeconomic policies, lacking a systematic analytical framework [26, 27]. The interactive mechanisms between various variables remain unclear. Additionally, there is still no consensus in academia regarding whether risk-taking ultimately enhances or damages enterprise value. In policy research, scholars have predominantly concentrated on conventional restrictions, including environmental legislation, green credit, and environmental taxes, identifying varied effects on risk-taking. For example, green credit promotes risk-taking [28], whereas environmental taxes mitigate it [29, 30]. Investigations into carbon trading are few and yield inconclusive results. Certain research indicates that carbon markets promote corporate risk-taking via a “positive expectation effect” [31], but do not elucidate the response mechanisms of high-carbon enterprises—a critical subset—or disclose variations in policy impact pathways.

In this study, the “risk-taking of high-carbon enterprises” does not simply refer to risk exposure, but rather to firms’ willingness to allocate resources to uncertain but potentially high-return investments and strategic choices—such as technological upgrading, green innovation, and business transformation—under the constraints of carbon emissions trading. This form of risk-taking is crucial not only for firms’ long-term value creation through proactive transition, but also for determining whether carbon trading policies can achieve environmental objectives without excessively suppressing corporate innovation incentives.

Current studies on carbon emissions trading policy and risk-bearing reveal three limitations: First, from a research standpoint, investigations into the micro-level effects of carbon emissions trading policy primarily emphasize corporate environmental performance or conventional economic performance, overlooking their influence on the fundamental strategic behaviors of high-carbon enterprises. Furthermore, regarding mechanism identification, current studies insufficiently examine the precise pathways through which policies affect corporate risk decisions. Although certain studies examine direct cost implications or innovation incentives, they fail to provide a comprehensive analysis of risk behaviors in high-carbon enterprises. Third, concerning theoretical frameworks, current research inadequately synthesizes various policy impact paths, leading to a disjunction between the viewpoints of “environmental benefits” and “economic benefits.”

The potential contributions of this work are threefold: First, by concentrating on principal carbon emitters, it elucidates the carbon trading policy’s restraining impact on risk-taking behaviors of high-carbon enterprises. The heterogeneous research elucidates the varying impacts, providing a novel viewpoint on comprehending the micro-level consequences of carbon emissions trading policy. Second, it integrates risk-taking into the research framework of high-carbon enterprise transformation, elucidating a dual-path mechanism by which carbon trading policies impose constraints through “management risk preferences” and “financing limitations,” surpassing the conventional dualistic environmental-economic viewpoint. Third, it empirically examines the counter-modulating functions of green innovation and institutional investor ownership within policy transmission channels, thereby enhancing the literature on the implications of carbon trading policies and offering insights into risk behaviors in high-carbon enterprises.

This analysis utilizes panel data from 211 high-carbon enterprises listed on China’s Shanghai and Shenzhen A-share exchanges from 2008 to 2024, addressing the identified deficiencies. The difference-in-differences model is employed to create a quasi-natural experiment that assesses enterprise risk-taking levels via earnings volatility. This method rigorously assesses the effects and processes of carbon trading regulations on the risk-taking behavior of high-carbon enterprises. Specifically, a mechanism analysis was conducted using management risk appetite and financing constraints, while a moderation effect analysis was performed using green innovation and institutional investors’ shareholding. Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

In the context of the carbon emissions trading policy, the risk-taking behavior of high-carbon enterprises exhibits distinct institutional and contextual characteristics. Generally, corporate risk-taking refers to firms’ propensity to engage in investment and strategic decisions with high uncertainty but potentially high returns, typically manifested in R&D investment, capital expenditure, technological upgrading, and strategic transformation. For high-carbon enterprises, risk-taking behavior is shaped not only by conventional factors such as market competition, resource endowments, and internal governance, but is also deeply embedded in the regulatory environment created by environmental policies.

By internalizing carbon emission costs through market-based mechanisms, the carbon emissions trading policy systematically reshapes the cost structure, expected returns, and financing conditions of high-carbon enterprises, thereby altering their risk decision-making process. On the one hand, emission constraints and rising compliance costs increase operational uncertainty and may compress the expected returns of high-risk investments, discouraging firms from undertaking aggressive risk-taking activities. On the other hand, price signals and transition pressure generated by the policy may, under certain conditions, incentivize firms to engage in technological innovation and strategic adjustment, prompting them to assume necessary risks to achieve long-term sustainability. Consequently, the risk-taking behavior of high-carbon enterprises reflects a comprehensive decision regarding transformation paths, investment intensity, and strategic choices under environmental constraints.

Based on this conceptualization, this study defines the risk-taking of high-carbon enterprises as their overall engagement in uncertain investment and strategic activities under the constraints of the carbon emissions trading policy. Building on this foundation, the study examines how the policy systematically affects risk-taking by influencing managerial risk preference, financing constraints, and firms’ internal and external governance environments, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the subsequent development of research hypotheses and empirical analysis.

Carbon emission trading policies and risk tolerance of high-carbon enterprises

Carbon emission trading policy predominantly enforces emission reduction limitations and incentives on businesses via cap-and-trade systems [32, 33], thereby internalizing the environmental externalities of carbon emissions into operational expenses [34].

This profoundly modifies the cost-benefit analysis framework of high-carbon enterprises. The Porter Hypothesis argues that stringent yet well-designed environmental policies can generate innovation benefits for firms that are sufficient to cover some or all of their compliance expenditures [35]. Market-based carbon trading mechanisms delineate carbon emission rights as tradable assets, thus creating new avenues for high-carbon enterprises to secure direct economic advantages. This essentially offers high-carbon enterprises a genuine opportunity for low-carbon investment. Stable carbon price signals provided by policy can markedly diminish the policy uncertainty encountered by high-carbon enterprises when investing in low-carbon technologies and novel business sectors [36], allowing management to more accurately evaluate the prospective value of long-term green investments. Concurrently, to evade the predicament of diminishing profit margins in conventional high-carbon operations and to secure first-mover advantages in nascent green markets, management is strongly motivated to undertake more strategic risks. This fosters proactive investment in technical innovation and corporate change, leading to an elevated risk-taking profile. Consequently, this research posits the subsequent hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1

a : The enactment of carbon emissions trading policy markedly elevates the risk-taking behavior of high-carbon enterprises.

Agency theory posits that there are intrinsic disparities in risk preferences between management and shareholders [37]. Compliance costs mandated by policy directly diminish the earnings of high-carbon enterprises, while fluctuations in carbon pricing intensify performance uncertainty [38]. To alleviate personal career risks associated with profit decreases or investment failures, management typically implements conservative operational measures [39], preemptively reducing expenditures on R&D and innovation, which are marked by prolonged cycles and significant uncertainty. Moreover, resource constraint theory posits that carbon emissions trading policy exacerbates financial limitations for high-carbon enterprises [40]. On the one hand, acquiring carbon allowances reallocates financial resources; on the other, the regulation underscores the transformation risks associated with high-carbon enterprises, potentially constricting external funding avenues and elevating expenses. Faced with concurrent internal and external finance limitations, high-carbon enterprises will be forced to prioritize survival costs, progressively diminishing high-risk strategic investments, which will ultimately result in a reduction in overall risk-taking levels. Consequently, this research posits the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1

b : The enactment of carbon emissions trading policy markedly diminishes the risk-taking behavior of high-carbon enterprises.

The transmission mechanism of carbon emissions trading policies on corporate risk-taking

Carbon emissions trading policies mitigate the risk appetites of high-carbon enterprise management by including regulatory risk and career concern mechanisms. The compliance requirements and penalty frameworks instituted by these regulations elevate management’s career risk [41]. In this context, risk-averse managers, influenced by agency theory, frequently prioritize personal job security over maximizing shareholder value, favoring conservative strategies [42] and intentionally shunning uncertain investments that could heighten compliance risks or profit volatility. Secondly, variations in carbon pricing and policy unpredictability considerably enhance the intricacy and vagueness of long-term investment assessments [43, 44]. Management encounters difficulties in precisely forecasting project returns. Behavioral choice theory posits that humans demonstrate a tendency to avoid ambiguity in circumstances characterized by excessive uncertainty. As a result, management often retains funds, reduces discretionary expenditures, and delays irreversible investments to ensure operational stability, creating a risk-averse cognitive framework that ultimately leads to diminished risk-taking at the corporate level. Consequently, this research posits the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2

a : Carbon emissions trading policy diminishes risk-taking behaviors in high-carbon enterprises by constraining management risk appetite.

Carbon emissions trading policy exacerbates financial limitations for high-carbon enterprises via two mechanisms: resource crowding-out effects and signaling impacts, consequently constraining their risk-taking ability. The policy directly elevates operational cash flow pressure for high-carbon enterprises. Acquiring carbon allowances to address quota deficiencies leads to immediate monetary expenditures, thus displacing internal resources that could be used towards strategic investments. This cash constraint compels enterprises to prioritize essential survival expenses and regulatory charges, at the expense of more speculative long-term investments. Secondly, carbon emissions trading policy exacerbates information asymmetry and risk reevaluation in external capital markets [45]. The intricate nature of carbon data reporting and apprehensions regarding disclosure quality intensify information opacity between high-carbon enterprises and their creditors. Conversely, classification as a pivotal emission-regulated organization in the carbon market conveys significant indications regarding corporate transition risks and prospective regulatory expenses. Financial organizations integrate climate change risks into credit assessment frameworks [46], necessitating elevated risk premiums or the reduction of credit lines. Furthermore, in markets increasingly oriented towards ESG, the “asset discount” linked to high-carbon assets may further limit their equity financing avenues [47]. In this context, internal resource depletion and constricting external funding circumstances create a detrimental cycle, ultimately diminishing overall risk-bearing capability. Consequently, this research posits the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2

b : Carbon emissions trading policy diminishes the risk exposure of high-carbon enterprises by reinforcing financial constraints.

The moderating effect of carbon emissions trading policies on corporate risk-taking

According to the resource-based view and dynamic capability theory, green innovation establishes risk-buffering mechanisms for high-carbon enterprises through three paths, thereby alleviating the constraints on risk-taking imposed by carbon emission trading regulations. Initially, green innovation markedly diminishes carbon emissions per output unit and compliance expenses by enhancing energy efficiency and advancing low-carbon technology [48]. The “innovation compensation effect” successfully mitigates policy-induced financial pressures, thus safeguarding financial capacity for high-risk ventures. Secondly, green innovation compels enterprises to create new product lines and business models [49], establishing a “second growth curve” that operates independently of conventional high-carbon activities. This diminishes dependence on businesses susceptible to policy changes, allowing enterprises to sustain their strategic risk-taking ability despite policy limitations. Ultimately, the results of green innovation indicate a favorable shift in the market [50], promoting policy endorsement and market acknowledgment. This enhances enterprise financing conditions and mitigates external financial constraints. These paths allow high-carbon enterprises with advanced green innovation to convert policy restrictions into developmental opportunities, maintaining their strategic commitment to high-risk, high-return investments. Consequently, this research posits the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3

a : Green innovation will diminish the suppressive impact of carbon emissions trading policy on the risk-taking behaviors of high-carbon enterprises. The greater the degree of green innovation, the lesser the policy’s inhibitory influence on risk-taking.

Agency and signaling theory suggest that institutional investors strengthen the suppressive effect of carbon emissions trading policy on corporate risk-taking in high-carbon enterprises by operating through two distinct channels. Institutional investors, motivated by the need for capital security and consistent returns, increase oversight on management by implementing stringent performance evaluation and risk control systems [51]. Following policy implementation, to mitigate performance volatility during the transition that may prompt institutional investors to withdraw, management emphasizes risk-averse compliance strategies—acquiring allowances instead of investing in high-risk emission reduction research and development—thereby constricting the strategic risk-taking capacity of high-carbon enterprises. Conversely, the analytical expertise of institutional investors allows them to adeptly discern transition risks arising from policy shocks [52]. Their portfolio adjustment actions convey adverse signals to the market, resulting in stricter financing conditions and elevated capital costs, thus establishing a market transmission chain of investment contraction. Institutional investors’ performance assessment methods and risk appetites predominantly encourage short-term conservatism, exacerbating risk-averse signals and heightening legislative restrictions on the risk-taking tendencies of high-carbon enterprises. Consequently, this research posits the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3

b : Institutional investors’ shareholding will enhance the mitigating effect of carbon emissions trading policy on risk-taking behaviors in high-carbon enterprises. The greater the ownership by institutional investors, the more pronounced the policy’s suppressive influence on risk-taking.

Variable Definitions

Instrumental variables

Management risk appetite () and financial constraints () are essential mechanisms by which carbon emissions trading policy affects risk-taking behaviors in high-carbon enterprises. This research utilizes Management risk appetite () and financial constraints () as instrumental variables, referencing prior research [57, 58]. Principal component analysis is utilized to derive the principal component that signifies executive risk preference levels for thorough assessment. Six indicators are chosen across five dimensions—asset structure, debt-paying capacity, profit structure, profit distribution, and cash flow—to assess executive risk preferences: the proportion of risky assets to total assets, debt-to-asset ratio, core profit ratio, retained earnings ratio, self-financing ratio, and capital expenditure ratio. Elevated values signify increased risk tolerance among corporate executives. This research further proposes the SA Index as a proxy for corporate financial constraints, acknowledging that the financing capacity of high-carbon enterprises is essential for supporting high-risk innovation activities. An elevated SA Index value signifies enhanced funding restrictions and heightened financial risk exposure for the enterprise.

Moderating variables

Green innovation () and the ratio of institutional investor' shareholding () function as critical moderating variables between carbon emissions trading policy and the risk-taking behaviors of high-carbon enterprises. Utilizing previous research, we use green innovation () and institutional investors’ shareholding () as moderating factors to investigate the moderating routes of carbon emissions trading pilot policies on the risk-taking levels of high-carbon enterprises. Green innovation is quantified by the volume of green patent applications submitted by high-carbon enterprises [59], where elevated figures signify enhanced levels of green innovation. The institutional investors’ shareholding () is defined as the proportion of shares held by institutional investors over the long term in relation to total equity. A greater value signifies more stringent market regulation imposed on high-carbon enterprises.

Data sources and processing

To ensure consistency in accounting standards and facilitate the comparability of research samples and the validity of conclusions, the research sample consists of 211 publicly traded companies in high-carbon industries, drawn from the Shanghai and Shenzhen A-share markets between 2008 and 2024. High-carbon listed enterprises before 2008, ST and *ST high-carbon listed enterprises, and high-carbon enterprises with incomplete data were removed. High-carbon industries were identified in accordance with the National Development and Reform Commission’s Notice on Effectively Implementing Key Tasks for the Launch of the National Carbon Emission Trading Market (NDRC Climate Office [2016] No. 57), encompassing eight pilot sectors: petrochemicals, chemicals, building materials, steel, non-ferrous metals, papermaking, electric power, and aviation. This research encompasses nine industries as the research sample, given the mining sectors significant pollution and energy consumption attributes. Enterprise green patent data were obtained from the China National Intellectual Property Administration, utilizing the IPC categorization list for green patents published by the World Intellectual Property Organization. The authors collected, structured, and analyzed data on the corporate risk-taking levels, Lerner index, management risk appetite, enterprise life cycle, and investment efficiency of high-carbon enterprises utilizing the Guotai An database. Sales price, marginal cost, sales revenue growth rate, retained earnings, capital expenditure, enterprise age, Tobin’s Q value, gearing ratio, cash flow, years in operation, asset size, and stock return data were collected from the Guotai An database and the Chinese Research Data Service Platform (CNRDS). Linear interpolation was utilized to address partial data gaps and fill in missing information. To alleviate the influence of outliers, control variables, instrumental factors, and moderator variables were subjected to log transformation and 1% bilateral clipping. Additionally, to improve the visibility of the link between the dependent and independent variables, the dependent variables in the regression formula were scaled by multiplying by 100 and presented in percentage format. Parallel trend test

The parallel trends test is a prerequisite for applying the Difference-in-Differences (DID) model. A violation of this assumption means the DID estimator will be inconsistent. This research evaluates the parallel trend assumption. Utilizing existing research [60], we conduct event analysis by incorporating virtual events before and during policy implementation to investigate similar trends between the treatment and control groups. The formula is created using the year prior to policy adoption as the baseline for event analysis.

In the formula (5), the variablerepresents the year dummy variable, which interacts with the treatment group dummy variable () to form a new interaction term. This newly constructed term is then incorporated into the econometric formula (5) for re-estimation. This paper tests the parallel trend hypothesis by utilizing data from the five periods preceding the event to compute the baseline mean, subsequently mean-adjusting the regression coefficients and their confidence intervals for all observation periods, including the six periods following the event, to mitigate the influence of the prior trend. Figure 3 illustrates the results, revealing that the coefficients of the interaction terms prior to the policy’s implementation are all statistically insignificant, thereby validating the common trend assumption. Following the policy’s implementation, the risk-taking levels of high-carbon enterprises within the pilot area decline annually, robustly substantiating the carbon emissions trading policy’s inhibitory effect on these enterprises’ risk-taking behavior, which successfully passes the parallel trend test.

Placebo test

The difference-in-differences () model employs a placebo test to do counterfactual assessments by simulating alternative policy time points or treatment groups to exclude the influence of non-policy elements. The baseline regression formula (1) is employed to estimate the samples and analyze the statistical distribution properties of the obtained coefficients and their p-values.

Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, and Chongqing, which are the four highest-level administrative divisions in China known as direct-controlled municipalities, have markedly superior levels of carbon market growth and economic advancement compared to typical cities [62]. Given the distinctive policy resource allocation, advantages in industrial upgrading, and risk mitigation capabilities of municipalities, these factors could act as confounding variables, potentially biasing the estimated causal effect of the carbon emissions trading policy on corporate risk-taking. To eliminate any estimate bias, this research omits the samples from the four municipalities and re-executes the regression analysis utilizing the baseline regression formula (1). Column (2) of Table 4 presents the estimation results following the exclusion of certain municipalities. The coefficient is significantly negative at the 5% level, indicating that the inhibitory effect of carbon trading policy on the risk-taking behavior of high-carbon enterprises remains robust even after eliminating the influence of cities with substantial resource endowments.

Heterogeneous tests

To reveal the structural characteristics of policy effects, this paper constructs a multidimensional heterogeneous analysis framework, focusing on five key aspects: the market environment in which enterprises operate, internal governance structure, resource allocation efficiency, development stage, and regional policy environment. Specifically, this paper examines the heterogeneous of policy effects across enterprises in different competition levels, ownership structures, investment efficiency, enterprise life cycle stages, and regions, providing empirical evidence for subsequent mechanism exploration and differentiated policy design.

Variations in the level of competition

This research utilizes the Lerner index to assess the level of market rivalry, as per the industry, relying on the market mechanism for orderly competition [64]. The Lerner index is calculated by determining the difference between the sales price and marginal cost of high-carbon enterprises, and then dividing this difference by the sales price. The Lerner index is calculated by determining the difference between the weighted average sales price of all enterprises in the industry and the weighted average marginal cost of all enterprises, and then dividing this difference by the weighted average sales price of all enterprises in the industry. The median Lerner index serves as a criterion for differentiating the level of competition within each industry: it is lower than the industry median for low-competitive enterprises and higher than the industry median for high-competitive enterprises. The inhibitory effect of carbon emissions trading policy is evaluated based on the aforementioned categorization criteria to determine any variance in competitive intensity, with the group regression findings presented in columns (1)-(2) of Table 5. The findings indicate that only the regression coefficients for low-competitive enterprises are significantly negative at the 1% level, whereas the coefficients for high-competitive enterprises are positive but not statistically significant.

State-owned enterprises are more predisposed to adhere to national policy directives, incorporating political and social responsibilities into their business objectives [66]. These enterprises typically take a proactive stance toward emission reduction policies, aligning their operations with national environmental goals. By doing so, they mitigate risky behaviors in production and operations. This is in stark contrast to non-state-owned enterprises, where the impact of such policies is less pronounced. Non-state-owned enterprises are more influenced by market factors, and their responses to policy directives are often driven by profit-maximizing incentives, leading to less proactive engagement with emission reduction efforts. Variations in investment efficiency

The market resource allocation model identifies substantial behavioral disparities among enterprises with varying investment efficiencies in relation to environmental regulation. This paper references existing research [67] and utilizes the enterprise Tobin’s Q value, gearing ratio, cash flow, years in operation, asset size, and stock return to develop the model for assessing the investment efficiency of high-carbon enterprises. The findings regarding investment efficiency are presented. High-carbon enterprises yielding favorable outcomes are classified as over-investment samples, whereas those with negative outcomes are categorized as under-investment samples, followed by related regression analyses. As shown in columns (5)-(6) of Table 5, the coefficient for overinvested enterprises is negative and statistically significant at the 10% level. In contrast, the coefficient for underinvested enterprises, though negative at −0.264, is statistically insignificant.

The divergence in responses between overinvested enterprises and underinvested enterprises can be explained by differing agency issues. Overinvested enterprises typically face significant agency problems, such as managerial overreach and inefficiencies, where policy shocks serve to mitigate these issues by strengthening external constraints on investment decisions. The carbon emissions trading policy, in this case, acts as an external force to curb inefficient investment practices. In contrast, underinvested enterprises face intrinsic resource limitations and often operate at the margins, where their risk-taking propensity is already relatively low. Consequently, the policy intervention has a minimal impact, as these enterprises do not have the resources to undertake high-risk projects even without the external constraints. Figure 5 (c1) and (c2) illustrate the parallel trend test for overinvested and underinvested enterprises, respectively. It is evident that overinvested high-carbon enterprises experience significant policy impacts in the long term, whereas underinvested high-carbon enterprises do not meet the criteria for the policy effect test.

Conclusions and recommendations

This research examines 211 publicly traded enterprises in China’s Shanghai and Shenzhen A-shares within the high-carbon sector from 2008 to 2024. It employs the volatility (i.e., standard deviation) of the return on assets (ROA) of high-carbon enterprises to quantify their risk-taking behavior, which serves as the explanatory variable for this research. This paper selects carbon emissions trading policy as a quasi-natural experimental subject, develops a difference-in-differences model, assesses its dynamic effects using a parallel trend test, and quantitatively analyzes the impact of carbon emissions trading policy on the risk-taking behavior of high-carbon enterprises. The carbon emissions trading policy markedly diminishes risk-taking behaviors in high-carbon enterprises, with results consistently validated through robustness tests, including the placebo test, alterations in sample intervals, exclusion of municipal samples, removal of contemporaneous policy interferences, substitution of the explanatory variable, propensity score matching, and one-period lagging. This research reveals through heterogeneous analysis that the carbon emissions trading policy exerts the most pronounced suppression effects on low-competition enterprises, state-owned enterprises, overinvested enterprises, declining enterprises, and enterprises located in western and eastern regions, whereas high-carbon enterprises in the central region have a promotion impact. Subsequent mechanism analysis indicates that the carbon emissions trading policy markedly amplifies its suppression by limiting management risk appetites and reinforcing financing constraints. Subsequent analysis indicates that green innovation alleviates the constraining impact of carbon emissions trading policy on risk-taking behaviors in high-carbon enterprises, but institutional investors’ shareholding amplifies this effect.

Based on the above, this paper proposes the following recommendations.

Initially, a differentiated and detailed regulatory framework should be constructed at the policy optimisation level. The research revealed that the carbon emissions trading policy has a particularly pronounced inhibitory effect on low-competition enterprises, state-owned enterprises, recessionary enterprises, and high-carbon enterprises in both the eastern and western regions. The policy’s implementation necessitates the avoidance of a “one-size-fits-all” approach. It is advisable to establish an innovation quota compensation mechanism for high-carbon enterprises with limited adjustment capacity, dynamically correlating their green R&D investments with quota allocation. For high-carbon enterprises with significant suppressive effects, a special transitional quota pool should be established to stabilize their survival space. Concurrently, a white list system for transforming enterprises should be instituted, offering specialised credit support for technological transformation in declining enterprises. Additionally, differentiated risk tolerance standards should be implemented in the eastern and western regions, permitting leading transforming enterprises to moderately elevate their risk-bearing thresholds, thereby preserving environmental benefits while preventing excess.

Secondly, at the corporate governance level, it is essential to offer targeted finance assistance to enterprises undergoing technological transition. Secondly, at the corporate governance level, it is essential to establish an incentive-compatible internal development system. Considering the transmission mechanism of the inhibitory effects of carbon emissions trading policies, which dampen management’s risk appetite and exacerbate financial constraints, it is recommended to integrate the efficacy of low-carbon innovation into the management evaluation framework of high-carbon enterprises. Additionally, a green innovation fault-tolerance mechanism should be established to bolster management’s willingness to undertake risks. Long-term equity incentives, aligned with the transformation project cycle, should be set up, deeply linking management compensation with long-term carbon performance. Concurrently, we must cultivate innovative financial instruments, such as pledge financing for anticipated returns on carbon quotas, to alleviate the liquidity constraints faced by high-carbon enterprises during the transition phase. Additionally, we should encourage financial institutions to formulate financial classification standards for the transition and diminish the financing costs of high-quality transition projects through precise risk pricing, thereby stimulating the intrinsic capacity for transformation within high-carbon enterprises through diverse strategies.

Thirdly, at the level of system synergy, it is essential to enhance the regulatory function of critical variables. Given the substantial moderating influence of green innovation and the shareholding ratio of institutional investors on the relationship between carbon emissions trading policy and the risk-taking behaviour of high-carbon enterprises, it is recommended that the government establish a green technology-innovation risk compensation fund to offer R&D insurance for pioneering low-carbon technologies; encourage institutional investors to adhere to responsible investment principles, incorporating the effectiveness of high-carbon enterprise transformation into their investment decision frameworks; and disseminate the experiences and exemplary cases of the central region’s carbon emissions trading policy to advance its implementation. It is recommended that regulatory agencies revise guidelines for institutional investor due diligence management to prevent them from overly avoiding short-term risks, which could hinder long-term transformation investments in enterprises. The policy experience and standard instances of carbon emissions trading in the central area will facilitate the transition of the policy impact from restriction to incentives, finally establishing a virtuous cycle of policy guidance and market support.

It is imperative to emphasise that, being the predominant source of energy consumption and carbon emissions within the national economy, the alteration in risk-taking behaviour of high-carbon enterprises exhibits a systematic demonstrative effect and transmission value over the entire market. High-carbon enterprises, typically positioned upstream in the industrial chain, can convert regulatory pressure into transformative momentum for the entire industrial chain via supply chain transmission, technology diffusion, and innovative finance models. Consequently, the investigation into the risk-taking behaviour of high-carbon enterprises can elucidate the direct effects of environmental regulation on principal entities, while also offering significant insights into the risk decision-making principles of carbon emissions trading policy across the entire enterprise system. This research aims to furnish theoretical foundations and practical strategies for achieving a holistic green transformation of the economy, ultimately facilitating the alignment of pollution reduction, carbon reduction, and high-quality development.

We collected, structured, and analyzed data on the corporate risk-taking levels, management risk appetite, and investment efficiency of high-carbon enterprises utilizing the Guotai An database. Additional data were obtained from the Guotai An database and the Chinese Research Data Service Platform (CNRDS). The data collected and organized by us is presented in the additional files.
