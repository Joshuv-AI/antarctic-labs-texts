Assessment of antibiotic stewardship knowledge, attitudes, and prescribing preparedness among medical, pharmacy, and dental students in Kuwait
Authors: Sarah Salah Alghanem, Ahmad Taqi, Shekha Waleed Alzemami, Abdelmoneim Awad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial resistance is a critical global health challenge, and health professional education plays a key role in promoting responsible antibiotic use. Adequate training in antibiotic stewardship is crucial to ensure that future healthcare providers are well-prepared for effective infection management. This study assessed antibiotic-related knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported prescribing preparedness among health sciences students in Kuwait, aiming to identify educational gaps and inform curricular reform.MethodsA cross-sectional survey study was conducted among senior undergraduate students from the colleges of medicine, dentistry, and pharmacy at the Health Sciences Center in Kuwait between February and June 2023. A self-administered questionnaire was used to assess antibiotic knowledge, attitudes toward appropriate use, and perceived preparedness to prescribe. Descriptive, comparative, and correlation analyses were performed.ResultsOf the 596 eligible students, 363 completed the questionnaire (response rate: 60.9%). The majority of respondents were female (94.8%). The median [IQR] knowledge score was 18 [5] out of 29 (62.1% [IQR = 17.2]), reflecting moderate overall knowledge. Nearly half of the participants (n = 177; 48.8%, 95% CI: 43.5–54.0) demonstrated moderate overall knowledge, while 42.4% (n = 154; 95% CI: 37.3–47.7) scored low, and only 8.8% (n = 32; 95% CI: 6.2–12.3) achieved high scores. Domain-specific scores analysis revealed a notable pattern: while students performed well in general antibiotic knowledge (100% [IQR = 0]), substantial gaps were identified in the areas of antibiotic resistance (55.6% [IQR = 22.2]) and clinical application (53.8% [IQR = 30.8]). Attitudes toward appropriate antibiotic use were generally positive (median: 4 out of 5 [IQR = 2]), yet perceived preparedness to prescribe was low (50.0% [IQR = 75.0]). The overall median [IQR] preparedness score was 50.0% [75.0], indicating a low level of preparedness. The three main clinical application gaps were reported in specimen selection (33.9%), interpreting resistance mechanisms (36.6%), and distinguishing flora from pathogens (39.1%). Medical and pharmacy students scored significantly higher than dental students in both knowledge and preparedness (p < 0.05). Knowledge was positively correlated with preparedness (Spearman’s r = 0.342; p < 0.001).ConclusionsDespite moderate general knowledge and positive attitudes, students exhibited significant deficiencies in antibiotic resistance and clinical application, alongside low levels of self-reported prescribing preparedness. These findings highlight the curricular reform priority need for targeted, competency-based, and interprofessional educational interventions to strengthen antibiotic stewardship training and support responsible prescribing practices among future healthcare professionals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09181-z.

Background

Antibiotics are among the most frequently prescribed medications globally and are essential for managing infectious diseases (IDs). However, their widespread and often inappropriate use accelerates antimicrobial resistance (AMR), leading to treatment failures, adverse drug reactions, and the spread of resistant pathogens [1]. AMR is estimated to directly cause 1.27 million deaths annually and contributes to an additional 4.95 million deaths worldwide [2]. Projections indicate major economic consequences by 2050, with substantial declines in global gross domestic product. Kuwait faces similar challenges, with national data indicating rising resistance rates and an urgent need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) and locally tailored prescribing guidelines [3, 4]Inappropriate antibiotic prescribing arises from systemic and individual-level factors, including insufficient undergraduate training in IDs and rational antibiotic use, limited access to clinical guidelines, and variable exposure to antibiotic AMS [5, 6]. These gaps can undermine prescriber confidence in real-world clinical decision-making [7, 8]. Regional studies also report similar deficiencies, highlighting needs related to policy awareness and more applied, practice-oriented teaching approaches [9].

Students at Kuwait University’s Health Sciences Center (HSC) represent the future healthcare workforce, and their knowledge, attitudes, and perceived preparedness are central to promoting safe antibiotic practices. The HSC serves as the country’s primary academic hub for health education and research, encompassing Medicine, Dentistry, Pharmacy, Allied Health Sciences, and Public Health, with a strong emphasis on evidence-based, patient-centered care and interprofessional training. As antibiotics are prescription-only in Kuwait, undergraduate education in prudent prescribing and AMS is directly aligned with national health policy goals [3, 4].

Addressing AMR challenges requires a proactive educational approach that equips future healthcare professionals with the competencies necessary for responsible antibiotic use. Although students generally recognize AMR as a global public health threat and understand that antibiotics target bacterial infections [10–12], substantial misconceptions persist, particularly the belief that antibiotics are effective for viral illnesses such as the common cold and influenza. These gaps can translate into inappropriate behaviors, including self-prescribing and overconfidence in clinical decision-making. Comparative studies suggest that medical students typically demonstrate stronger knowledge than their non-medical counterparts [13–15]; paradoxically, greater knowledge has sometimes been associated with higher rates of self-medication [16]. Recent regional findings also reveal discipline‑specific variability, such as higher AMS knowledge among pharmacy students compared with medical students [17]. Collectively, these findings highlight persistent gaps in knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to antibiotic use and underscore the urgent need for targeted educational interventions to strengthen stewardship competencies. Consistent with this, studies across multiple countries and healthcare disciplines report inconsistent and heterogeneous findings on students’ knowledge of antibiotic use and resistance [9, 15, 17–21]. Such variability reinforces the importance of context‑specific curricula and the need to examine preparedness alongside knowledge to better understand the readiness of future prescribers.

Within this context, the present study assessed antibiotic-related knowledge, attitudes, and prescribing preparedness among senior medicine, pharmacy, and dentistry students at Kuwait University’s HSC. This integrated approach combines domain-specific knowledge with self-reported preparedness, an outcome rarely examined in regional studies. By identifying discipline-specific knowledge gaps and their correlation with preparedness, the findings will help inform targeted curricular enhancements and support national AMS initiatives. To our knowledge, no previous study in Kuwait or the region has explored interprofessional preparedness among healthcare students.

Methods

Study design and population

A cross-sectional, institutional-based study was conducted among undergraduate students at Kuwait University, the sole institution in Kuwait to date offering professional programs in medicine, pharmacy, and dentistry. Kuwait University is the first governmental university in the country, established in 1966, and comprises 16 colleges covering a wide range of academic disciplines. The inclusion criteria encompassed students enrolled in the advanced years of their training: years 5–7 in medicine and dentistry, and years 4–7 in pharmacy. These cohorts were chosen because students at these levels are expected to possess adequate clinical exposure and foundational knowledge pertinent to antibiotic stewardship. Students in earlier years were excluded. All eligible students across the three colleges (n = 596) were invited to participate in a convenience sampling strategy, and 363 completed the survey independently between February 1 and June 30, 2023. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ministry of Health Research Ethical Committee, Kuwait (Reference No. MoH/REC/132/2022). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study questionnaire

The survey was adapted from previously validated questionnaires used in similar educational contexts [9, 19]. It was reviewed by three experts in infectious diseases pharmacotherapy, including one with biostatistics expertise, to ensure content validity and methodological rigor. To ensure clarity and contextual relevance, the adapted questionnaire was pretested with 10 students from each college (medicine, pharmacy, dentistry) representing various eligible academic years. Feedback focused on question clarity and cultural appropriateness. Minor wording adjustments were made based on this feedback, and all pilot participants confirmed that the questionnaire was clear and easy to complete. The final version of the questionnaire, which was administered in English for data collection, included five domains. The first consisted of 3 items regarding the participant sex, college, and year of study. The second domain included 29 items to assess participants’ knowledge about antibiotics (7 items), antibiotic resistance (9 items), and antibiotic use in various clinical scenarios (13 items). Items assessing antimicrobial resistance mechanisms and clinical scenarios were designed to evaluate foundational stewardship principles rather than nuanced clinical judgment, with correct responses aligned to internationally recognized guidance from the WHO, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the Infectious Diseases Society of America, and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing. The third domain consisted of 5 items to explore the participants’ attitudes toward antibiotic use. The fourth domain included 8 items to assess the participants’ preparedness for antibiotic prescribing. The final domain consisted of 6 items regarding participant’s ’ perceptions of the most effective level to tackle antibiotic resistance, awareness of initiatives in Kuwait focusing on antibiotic awareness and resistance, awareness of whether Kuwait has a national action plan on antibiotic resistance, having taught prudent antibiotic usage, as well as the management of infections encompassing diagnosis and antibiotic treatment, within their undergraduate curriculum, and desired areas for further education.

Data collection

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire distributed in paper format. Participants completed and returned the questionnaires within one to two weeks of distribution. Participation was entirely voluntary and anonymous. All students were assured of the confidentiality of their responses and provided written informed consent before participation.

Reliability, scoring, and categorization of measures

Internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) was assessed for the knowledge, attitudes, and preparedness domains. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated using the full study sample (n = 363), as the pilot sample was used solely to assess clarity and feasibility rather than reliability. The knowledge domain (29 items) showed good reliability (α = 0.76), the preparedness domain (8 items) showed high reliability (α = 0.85), and the attitudes domain (5 items) showed acceptable reliability (α = 0.62). Although the attitudes domain yielded an α of 0.62, this level is acceptable for exploratory survey research, particularly when a small number of items capture heterogeneous attitudinal constructs, and is consistent with established methodological recommendations in the psychometric literature [22].

Knowledge items were scored 1 for correct responses and 0 for incorrect or unsure responses. Subdomain percent scores were calculated for general (7 items), resistance (9 items), and clinical use (13 items), as well as an overall percent score (29 items), using the formula: Percent score = (number of correct/maximum) × 100. Scores were categorized using modified Bloom’s cut-offs: low < 60%, moderate 60–79%, high ≥ 80% [23–26].

The attitudes score was treated as a continuous variable by summing appropriate responses across five items (range 0–5). For the item on antibiotic use frequency in the past year, responses were coded as < 3 times/year = 1 and ≥ 3 times/year = 0. For the remaining four items, “No” = 1 and “Yes” = 0, reflecting alignment with appropriate attitudes. Total scores were categorized as negative (0–3) or positive (4–5), consistent with Bloom’s principles and common practice in KAP studies [23–26].

Preparedness for antibiotic prescribing was treated as a continuous variable by summing affirmative (“Yes”) responses across eight items (range 0–8). The percent score was calculated as: Percent score = (Sum/8) × 100. Scores were categorized using modified Bloom’s thresholds: low < 60%, intermediate 60–79%, high ≥ 80% [23–26].

Statistical analysis

The data were entered into the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29), and subsequent descriptive, comparative, and correlation analyses were performed. Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were employed to assess the normality of the data about knowledge, attitudes, and preparedness scores. The findings indicated non-normal distributions; therefore, results were presented as medians (interquartile ranges - IQR). Percentages were presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Given the non-normal distribution of the data, nonparametric tests were employed for comparative analysis. The Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized to compare the overall scores of knowledge, attitude, and preparedness across different colleges (medicine, dentistry, and pharmacy). Subsequently, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Dunn-Bonferroni approach. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust significance values for multiple tests, with adjusted p-values reported. To assess discrepancies in overall scores of knowledge, attitude, and preparedness between the middle-year (4th and 5th years) and final years (6th and 7th years), the Mann–Whitney test was employed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Furthermore, correlational analysis using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (Spearman’s r) was conducted to examine the association between respondents’ overall knowledge about antibiotics and their preparedness for antibiotic prescribing, as well as their attitudes towards antibiotic use and preparedness for antibiotic prescribing. A p-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Medical and pharmacy students comprised nearly equal proportions of the sample (45.7% and 45.5%, respectively), with the remainder representing dental students. Overall knowledge about antibiotics

The overall median [IQR] knowledge score was 18 [5] out of 29 items, corresponding to a median percentage score of 62.1% [17.2], reflecting a moderate level of knowledge. Nearly half of the participants (n = 177; 48.8%, 95% CI: 43.5–54.0) demonstrated moderate knowledge, while 42.4% (n = 154; 95% CI: 37.3–47.7) scored low, and only 8.8% (n = 32; 95% CI: 6.2–12.3) achieved high scores. Medical and pharmacy students had significantly higher overall knowledge scores than dental students (mean ranks: 197.9 and 179.4 vs. 113.2; p < 0.001 and p = 0.003, respectively). Final-year students also scored significantly higher than middle-year students (mean rank: 198.7 vs. 169.4; p = 0.008). Preparedness for antibiotic prescribing/ clinical decision-making readiness for non-prescribers

Table 6 presents detailed insights into participants’ responses to items assessing their preparedness for antibiotic prescribing/clinical decision‑making readiness for non‑prescribers. Preparedness/clinical decision-making readiness was higher for general prescribing tasks, such as switching from IV to oral therapy (55.4%), deciding whether to prescribe an antibiotic (52.1%), and knowing the appropriate dose (51.5%). In contrast, competencies that depend on microbiology and diagnostic stewardship were notably lower: selecting the correct and relevant specimen (33.9%), understanding resistance mechanisms from a microbiology report (36.6%), and distinguishing normal flora from a true pathogen (39.1%). These three lowest-scoring items are identified as priority curricular gaps warranting targeted instructional interventions.

The overall median [IQR] preparedness/clinical decision-making readiness score was 50.0% [75.0], indicating a low level of preparedness/clinical decision-making readiness. A substantial proportion of participants (n = 217; 59.8%; 95% CI: 54.5–64.8) scored in the low category (< 60%), while 22.3% (n = 81; 95% CI: 18.2–27.0) scored in the moderate range (60%–79%), and 17.9% (n = 65; 95% CI: 14.2–22.3) were classified as highly prepared (≥ 80%). A significant positive correlation was found between overall knowledge and preparedness (Spearman’s r = 0.342, p < 0.001), indicating that higher knowledge was associated with greater preparedness. However, no significant correlation was found between attitudes and preparedness (Spearman’s r = 0.097; p = 0.064), suggesting that attitudes did not strongly influence preparedness levels in this context. Preparedness/clinical decision-making readiness was significantly higher among pharmacy (mean rank: 199.8) and medical students (mean rank: 181.5) compared to dental students (mean rank: 92.8) (p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed between final-year (mean rank: 181.6) and middle-year students (mean rank: 182.3; p = 0.943). Awareness of AMR campaigns and training

Perceived level of action on AMR

Most participants (n = 257; 70.8%, 95% CI: 65.8–75.4) believed that addressing antibiotic resistance requires a comprehensive approach across all levels. This was followed by a preference for individual-level interventions among prescribers (n = 98; 27.0%; 95% CI: 22.6–31.9), regional/national/global levels; (n = 79; 21.8%; 95% CI: 17.7–26.4), and environmental/animal health domains (n = 54; 14.9%; 95% CI: 11.5–19.1). A small proportion (n = 39; 10.7%, 95% CI: 7.8–14.5) were unsure of the most effective level of intervention.

Awareness of national initiatives on antibiotic awareness and resistance

The most commonly recognized local initiatives were conferences and events addressing antibiotic resistance (n = 189; 52.1%, 95% CI: 46.8–57.3) and media campaigns (n = 148; 40.8%, 95% CI: 35.7–46.0). One-quarter of respondents (n = 92; 25.3%, 95% CI: 21.0-30.2) reported being unaware of any such initiatives. Notably, more than half of the participants (n = 191; 52.6%, 95% CI: 47.3–57.8) were unsure whether Kuwait has a national action plan on AMR, while 37.2% (n = 135; 95% CI: 32.2–42.4) believed no such plan exists. Only 10.2% (n = 37; 95% CI: 7.4–13.9) confirmed awareness of its existence.

Curriculum coverage and further training needs

A majority reported being taught prudent antibiotic use (n = 252; 69.4%, 95% CI: 64.3–74.1) and infection management, including diagnosis and treatment (n = 311; 85.7%, 95% CI: 81.6–89.0). When asked about preferred areas for further education, students most frequently selected antibiotic resistance (n = 169; 46.6%, 95% CI: 41.4–51.8), followed by appropriate antibiotic use (n = 142; 39.1%; 95% CI: 34.1–44.4), indications for antibiotics (n = 123; 33.9%; 95% CI: 29.1–39.0), antibiotic prescribing practices (n = 119; 32.8%, 95% CI: 28.0-37.9), and the interconnected health of humans, animals and the environment (n = 109; 30.0%; 95% CI: 25.4–35.1). A minority (n = 77; 21.2%, 95% CI: 17.2–25.9) had no desire for additional training on these topics.

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive assessment of antibiotic-related knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported prescribing preparedness among senior medical, pharmacy, and dental students in Kuwait. Although participants demonstrated moderate overall antibiotic knowledge, critical gaps were evident in areas such as antibiotic resistance and the clinical application of antibiotics – both essential for safe and effective prescribing. These gaps were mirrored by low levels of self-reported preparedness for antibiotic prescribing/ clinical decision-making readiness for non-prescribers, underscoring a disconnect between theoretical understanding and clinical readiness. In Kuwait, the scope of practice restricts antibiotic prescribing to licensed physicians and dentists, while pharmacists are not authorized to prescribe these agents under existing regulatory frameworks. The findings provide valuable insights for shaping educational policy and curriculum development at national and regional levels. Amid the escalating global threat of AMR, developing robust competencies in AMS among future healthcare professionals represents an urgent educational priority.

The moderate level of overall antibiotic knowledge among HSC students in this study aligns with mid-range findings reported in global studies.Studies involving multiple health professions, including medicine, pharmacy, and dentistry, have demonstrated considerable variation in antibiotic knowledge, ranging from inadequate [9], moderate [18, 21], to good [17, 20]. These cross-national differences likely reflect variations in curriculum design, clinical exposure, program stage (preclinical vs. final year), assessment instruments, and the emphasis placed on antimicrobial stewardship within undergraduate training. A notable finding in the current study is that most students correctly distinguished between antibiotics and painkillers, reflecting a solid understanding of this fundamental concept. This contrasts with findings from studies among healthcare students in the region, where notable misconceptions persist regarding the role of antibiotics, including their use for symptom relief and non-bacterial conditions (e.g., sore throat), indicating gaps in basic conceptual knowledge [17, 27]. The present results also align with findings from two systematic reviews reporting widespread educational gaps among medical students in IDs and antibiotic use [13, 28]. One review highlighted a deficiency in managing common infections, such as upper respiratory tract infections [28]. This observation underscores the global challenge of equipping future healthcare providers with competencies for evidence-based antibiotic prescribing. In this study, although students demonstrated strong general knowledge about antibiotics, their limited understanding of antibiotic resistance mechanisms and weak clinical application skills indicate a curricular imbalance. This discrepancy suggests that, although theoretical instruction is adequate, the translation of knowledge into practical, evidence-based clinical decision-making remains insufficient. Similar gaps in applied antibiotic knowledge have been reported in Australia and India [15, 29]. These findings underscore the urgent need to integrate structured, competency-based AMS education across undergraduate health professions curricula. The incorporation of mandatory stewardship modules, interprofessional simulations, and microbiology–clinical integration blocks, along with interprofessional prescribing workshops into health sciences curricula may effectively enhance students’ preparedness for evidence-based, real-world prescribing. The low performance in antibiotic resistance knowledge is particularly concerning, given the accelerating threat of AMR globally and in Kuwait. A multi-country survey of pharmacy students across seven Middle Eastern countries found substantial variability. Although Egyptian students demonstrated the highest knowledge about AMR, Kuwaiti students scored the lowest [27]. These disparities underscore the need for standardized antimicrobial education frameworks, as advocated by the Infectious Diseases Society of America [30].

A particularly concerning finding was the low level of self-reported preparedness for antibiotic prescribing/clinical decision‑making readiness for non‑prescribers. Although students expressed positive attitudes toward appropriate antibiotic use, their preparedness for prescribing/clinical decision-making readiness remained limited. The lowest-rated competencies were correct specimen selection, interpretation of resistance mechanisms, and distinguishing normal flora from true pathogens, representing priority curricular gaps. These deficiencies have direct clinical implications: incorrect specimen selection reduces diagnostic yield and delays or misdirects therapy; limited understanding of resistance mechanisms compromises antibiotic coverage and de-escalation; and misclassification of flora drives overtreatment, broad-spectrum use, and increased risk of Clostridioides difficile infection. These findings underscore the need for targeted instruction and assessment focused on real-world diagnostic stewardship, structured microbiology report interpretation, and a resistance mechanisms primer linking common mechanisms to antibiotic classes and local antibiograms. The results also demonstrate that favorable attitudes do not necessarily translate into prescribing confidence or competence. Variability in preparedness is not unique to Kuwait. European studies have reported similar disparities, often attributed to national differences in the multidrug-resistant organism prevalence. In these contexts, students often perceive ID management as more complex and challenging [31], promoting growing calls for a global consensus on minimum standards for AMS education. The positive correlation between antibiotic knowledge and perceived preparedness in this study underscores the need to strengthen undergraduate antimicrobial education. Current recommendations advocate integrating internationally recognized AMS competencies into pharmacy and other health professional curricula, reinforced through postgraduate and continuing education, in line with FIP guidance on AMR and stewardship education [32]. Embedding these core competencies across the professional education continuum is essential to ensure that future healthcare providers are equipped to manage AMR effectively and fulfill stewardship responsibilities in diverse clinical settings [17, 33].

Notably, most students reported having received instruction on prudent antibiotic use and infection management. However, they identified antibiotic resistance and appropriate antibiotic use as the top areas requiring further education, reflecting their actual knowledge deficits. These self-reported needs corroborate the quantitative findings and support the implementation of a more targeted, integrated approach to AMS education.

Encouragingly, most students recognized the importance of a comprehensive, multi-level approach to addressing AMR, reflecting awareness of its complexity and the need for coordinated efforts at individual, institutional, national, and international levels. However, only 10.2% reported awareness of Kuwait’s national AMR action plan, 52.6% were unsure, and 37.2% indicated no awareness. This distinction suggests that many students do not lack knowledge per se but have limited exposure to or uncertainty about national initiatives. These findings reveal a disconnect between public health strategies and undergraduate education, underscoring a critical gap in curricular alignment. Bridging this gap requires educational strategies that extend beyond foundational antibiotic content to explicitly incorporate national and global AMR policies. Integrating such content may enhance the relevance and retention of AMS training while fostering professional responsibility and engagement with health policy among future healthcare providers.

Despite growing recognition of the importance of interprofessional collaboration in AMS, few regional studies have adopted such a comprehensive approach. This study builds on and contrasts with previous research reporting highly variable antibiotic knowledge among students across disciplines and countries. By examining discipline-specific differences in knowledge and prescribing preparedness, this study provides critical insights to guide the development of targeted, team-based educational interventions. These findings align with global priorities outlined by the WHO and other international bodies, which emphasize the urgent need to strengthen AMR-related competencies across the healthcare workforce. This study contributes regionally grounded evidence to these global efforts.

In this study, female participants accounted for 94.8% of the sample, introducing a potential gender bias in interpreting preparedness and attitude scores. Evidence on gender differences in confidence regarding antibiotic prescribing is inconsistent. Ritchie et al. reported no significant differences in attitudes between male and female students, although female students expressed slightly higher confidence [15]. Conversely, Weier et al. found that male students reported greater confidence in antimicrobial knowledge [29]. These discrepancies may partly reflect variations in gender representation, as female students typically comprised 58.5% − 62% of participants [15, 28]. Methodological differences and sample imbalance limit a definitive conclusion regarding the impact of gender on prescribing confidence. Future research should aim for gender-balanced samples or apply adjusted analyses to clarify whether gender influences preparedness and attitudes AMS.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its interprofessional scope, encompassing senior students from colleges of medicine, pharmacy, and dentistry in Kuwait, where empirical data on antimicrobial education remain scarce. The overall finding of moderate antibiotic knowledge aligns with reports from neighboring Gulf countries; however, the domain-specific analysis provides novel insights into concentrated knowledge deficits. Notably, the inclusion of prescribing preparedness, a rarely assessed outcome, adds practical value by linking theoretical understanding to real-world clinical application. Additional strengths include the use of a validated, pretested survey adapted to the local context, and the assessment of students’ awareness of national AMR policies and their preferences for future training. Collectively, these findings provide actionable guidance for curriculum development and underscore the need for contextually relevant, multidisciplinary approaches to antimicrobial education in Kuwait.

This study has some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design captures students’ knowledge, attitudes, and preparedness at a single time point, without assessing longitudinal changes. Second, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and social desirability biases, although anonymity likely mitigated this effect. Third, self-reported preparedness may not fully reflect actual prescribing competence; future studies should incorporate objective assessments, such as Objective Structure Competency Exam or prescribing simulations. Fourth, with a response rate of 60.9%, non-response bias cannot be excluded, as non-participants may differ in knowledge or attitudes. Nevertheless, the response rate falls within acceptable ranges for educational research, and the absence of missing data strengthens the reliability of the findings. Fifth, the gender distribution mirrors the composition of dentistry and pharmacy students but underrepresents male students in medicine (approximately 22% male), potentially limiting the generalizability across all medical students. Sixth, the bivariate analysis does not fully disentangle college and year effects; therefore, observed differences may reflect both group composition and educational stage. Finally, although the findings are generalizable to Kuwait’s sole health sciences center, their applicability to other countries may be limited by contextual differences in training structures.

Conclusions

Despite moderate overall antibiotic knowledge and positive attitudes, HSC students exhibited critical gaps in antibiotic resistance understanding and clinical application areas essential for safe and effective prescribing, reflected in low self-reported prescribing preparedness. These findings underscore the urgent need for integrated, competency-based, and interprofessional educational reforms. Embedding AMS principles into undergraduate training, particularly through case-based and experiential learning, may help bridge the knowledge-preparedness gap and better equip future healthcare professionals to support national and global AMR efforts.
