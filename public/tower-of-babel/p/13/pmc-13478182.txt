Cryo-EM structure of African horse sickness virus VP2 receptor-binding protein enables nanoparticle vaccine design
Authors: Ane Martínez-Castillo, Andrea Aebischer, Philippine Toneatti, Lifei Fu, Damien Vitour, Corinne Sailleau, Bernd Hoffmann, Kati Franzke, Michael Eschbaumer, Saskia Weber, Eva Calvo Pinilla, Javier Ortego, David Gil-Cartón, Emmanuel Bréard, Stéphan Zientara, Jeroen Kortekaas, Martin Beer, Nicola GA Abrescia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
African horse sickness virus (AHSV) is a lethal equine pathogen with no licensed vaccine other than vaccines containing attenuated virus, which pose safety risks. Endemic to sub-Saharan Africa, AHSV has caused epizootics in Spain and Portugal, Cyprus, Morocco, the Middle East, India and Pakistan and, most recently, Thailand. Here, we resolve the 3.11 Å cryo-EM structure of full-length VP2 from AHSV serotype 4, adopting its native triskelion architecture and shedding light on an α-helical domain anchoring the triskelion core, which is absent in other structurally characterized orbiviruses. Structure-guided mapping identified a subdomain of VP2 as a key target of neutralizing antibodies. Displayed on nanoparticles using the SpyCatcher/SpyTag technology, the domain conferred complete protection from clinical disease after viral challenge infection in mice and elicited robust and long-lasting immune responses in horses, the target species of AHSV. These findings provide a structural blueprint for the next generation of recombinant vaccines against AHSV and related orbiviruses.

Introduction

African horse sickness (AHS) is the most lethal of all viral infections of horses, causing up to 80–90% mortality in naive animals. Donkeys and mules develop mild clinical signs, whereas zebras are resistant and function as major amplifying hosts. The causative agent is African horse sickness virus (AHSV), an arbovirus transmitted by Culicoides biting midges1. The virus belongs to the orbivirus genus (Order Reovirales, family Sedoreoviridae), along with other animal pathogens such as bluetongue virus (BTV) and epizootic hemorrhagic disease virus (EHDV). This genus is characterized by a double-stranded RNA genome divided into 10 linear segments that encode five non-structural proteins (NS1–NS3, NS3a, NS4) and 7 structural viral proteins (VP1–VP7). The viral genome is packaged in a double-layered capsid. The outer capsid is composed of 60 trimers of VP2 arranged as triskelion-shaped spikes and 120 trimers of VP5 organized into globular domains2. The inner capsid comprises 260 trimers of VP7 (T = 13 lattice) and 60 dimers of VP3 (T = 2 lattice)3. The polymerase (VP1), the capping enzyme (VP4), and helicase (VP6) are located at the 5-fold axes, forming the transcriptase complex4. Nine distinct serotypes of AHSV have been identified based on seroneutralization. VP2, encoded by segment 2, is the virus receptor-binding protein and dominant target of neutralizing antibodies and thereby determines the virus serotype5,6. Currently, the only commercially available AHS vaccine that is applied in South Africa, Namibia, and other African countries is based on attenuated viruses7. However, this vaccine poses risks, including residual virulence and the potential for reversion, and has limited serotype coverage, as serotypes 5 and 9 are not included. This has prompted the development of safer vaccine candidates, including those based on recombinant proteins and viral vectors8. Importantly, full-length soluble VP2 has been shown to be capable of inducing protective immunity in both mice and horses, underscoring its potential as a vaccine antigen9–15. Although full-length VP2 can be expressed in soluble form in insect cells, it is not secreted into the supernatant, requiring the use of crude cell lysates for immunization or purification from these lysates. While expression of proteins without secretion may facilitate the collection of relatively high antigen yield, purification from cell lysates is cumbersome in industrial settings. Progress in structure-guided antigen design had been hindered by the lack of high-resolution structural data, with only a 14 Å structure of AHSV previously available16.

Here, we determine the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the full-length AHSV serotype 4 (AHSV-4) VP2 trimer at 3.11 Å resolution. We define the molecular organization of the viral attachment protein and identify the molecular interactions underlying the native triskelion architecture, including a largely α-helical domain that contributes to anchoring the triskelion center—structurally unique to AHSV VP2 and absent in homologous proteins from other orbiviruses such as BTV, the only orbivirus structurally characterized to date17. Guided by this structure, we designed two AHSV-4 VP2 subdomains, of 280 (DI) and 311 (DII) amino acids in length, respectively, which were expected to contain major neutralizing epitopes. To further improve their immunogenicity, the DI and DII antigens with C-terminal SpyTag003 (ST) were displayed on SpyCatcher003-mi3 (SC-mi3) nanoparticles using the SpyCatcher/SpyTag technology18. The DI-mi3 and DII-mi3 nanoparticles were characterized by SDS-PAGE, western blot analysis, dynamic light scattering, and, for DI-mi3, cryo-EM.

We show that DI-mi3, selected based on its higher yield and equivalent immunogenicity, induces complete protection against clinical disease in virus-challenged mice and elicits robust and long-lasting immune responses in rabbits and in horses.

Together, these results show that the designed antigens represent promising prototype vaccines for efficacy studies in horses and demonstrate the value of structure-guided vaccine design informed by cryo-EM analysis.

Results

Overall VP2 structure

To elucidate the structure of full-length VP2, we made use of a Modified Vaccinia Ankara (MVA-T7)-based expression system. Purified full-length VP2 of AHSV-4, which forms a trimer in solution, was resolved by single-particle cryo-EM to an overall 3.11 Å resolution, based on the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) 0.143 criterion (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Initial data collection and image processing uncovered a preferential orientation issue, which was mitigated by collecting over 18,000 tilted movies, yielding a final interpretable three-dimensional map (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1c). The VP2 trimer adopts a triskelion-like architecture, measuring ~180 Å in diameter and ~100 Å in height (Fig. 1a). We fitted this trimeric structure into the corresponding VP2 density of the previously resolved 14 Å resolution cryo‑EM map of AHSV‑4 (ref. 16), where it is positioned on the pseudo-three‑fold symmetry axes adjacent to the icosahedral five‑fold axes, between three VP5 trimers and overlying four VP7 trimers (Fig. 1b). This close agreement indicates that the determined 3D structure of AHSV‑4 VP2 trimers represents the native viral attachment complex on the virion. Each VP2 is composed of four domains: the hub (A2–S141, R940–E1060), the body (Q142–M257, L534–P939), the tip (Y258–K321, Y449–E533) and the central domain (D329–Q442) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2). In the context of the virion, the central and tip domains form the most exposed regions, projecting furthest from the surface (Fig. 1b, bottom right). The hub domain includes the N- and C-termini, overall adopting an 11-stranded lectin-like β-sandwich with five small external helices interfacing with the body domain (Fig. 1c). The body domain is predominantly α-helical, flanked by a five-stranded antiparallel β-sheet and a series of connecting loops that lead to the tip domain. The tip domain is positioned laterally on top of the body domain and is composed of seven α-helices. Overall, this domain shows a weaker density than the other domains, suggesting inherent flexibility. The loops connecting to the central domain (residues K322–D328 and T443–K448), which extend toward the interior of the VP2 trimer, are disordered. Although the central domain is ordered and composed of four α-helices (α9- α12; Fig. 1c), it is resolved at lower resolution, consistent with increased conformational dynamics as supported by local resolution analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Disorder prediction of the sequence spanning the tip and central domains revealed an intrinsic propensity for structural disorder, which in AHSV serotype 4 corresponds to ~22%. Across other serotypes, the predicted disorder ranged between 14% and 34% (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The VP2 trimer is primarily stabilized by interactions between the hub and central domains of adjacent monomers. Electrostatic potential mapping showed two regions with marked opposite charges. A negatively charged patch on the monomer surface, flanking the central domain, delineates the docking site for central domain interactions within the trimeric assembly (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 4a, left). In contrast, a positively charged region at the base of the trimer is likely to mediate interactions with the underlying viral capsid, positioning the VP2 trimer directly above a VP7 trimer in the intact virion (Supplementary Fig. 4a, right; Fig. 1b, top right). The total buried interface between two VP2 monomers covers ~1800 Å², with ~900 Å² contributed by hub–hub interactions (Fig. 2a). The remaining surface involves the central domain of one VP2 engaging both the hub and central domains of its clockwise neighbor (Fig. 2b). At the molecular level, hub–hub interactions include one putative hydrogen bond, three salt bridges, and ~50 non-bonded contacts (Fig. 2a), while hub–central and central–central interactions contribute five salt bridges, four hydrogen bonds, and ~100 non-bonded contacts (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 4b).Fig. 1Atomic model of the VP2 trimer of AHSV serotype 4.a Left, schematic representation of the AHSV particle with major structural proteins indicated as in the key and the segmented genome shown in the interior (adapted from ViralZone web resource; www.expasy.org/viralzone/). Comparative sequence–structure analysis of BTV-1 and AHSV-4 VP2

Sequence alignment between the BTV-1 VP2 (UNP B7U676), which is pathogenic to ruminant livestock, and AHSV-4 shows a sequence similarity of 33.4% and an identity of 19.3%. Despite this relatively low sequence homology, most of the VP2 structure has been evolutionarily conserved. Structural superimposition of the AHSV-4 VP2 atomic model onto BTV-1 VP2 (PDB code 8W1O; ref. 17) yielded a root-mean-square-deviation (RMSD) of 3.1 Å across 581 aligned Cα atoms (of 961 residues), indicating that the main structural difference lies in the central domain—absent in BTV-1 VP2 but conserved across all AHSV serotypes (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 5). Superimposition of the individual AHSV-4 VP2 domains onto full-length BTV-1 VP2 showed that the hub domain is the most conserved region (238 of 261 Cα atoms aligned; RMSD = 2.0 Å), followed by the tip (118 of 149; RMSD = 2.7 Å) and body (349 of 524; RMSD = 3.2 Å) domains. Using Alphafold319, we additionally generated VP2 models for other orbiviruses, including the epizootic hemorrhagic disease virus‑1 (EHDV‑1), Changuinola virus (CGLV), and Peruvian horse sickness virus‑1 (PHSV‑1). These predicted structures more closely resemble BTV‑1 VP2, as they lack the helical motif of the central domain observed in AHSV-4 VP2 (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Notably, AHSV-4 VP2 possesses two distinct α-helices, α3 and α4 (residues 142–191), scaffolding the body domain externally, which are not present in BTV-1 VP2. Three hydrogen bonds anchor the two helices to the hub domain (R146–E708, R154–D719, and R175–D677). While the tip domains exhibit overall conservation of their α-helical structure, their orientations differ significantly relative to the hub and body domains, owing to their flexible attachment to the hub domain. Consequently, the interface energy of the AHSV-4 VP2 trimer, estimated using the PISA software (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/pisa/), shows an average ΔG of −12.3 kcal/mol (and as aforementioned an average interface area of ~1800 Å2)20. In contrast, the BTV-1 VP2 trimer has an average ΔG of −6.3 kcal/mol and an interface area of about 970 Å², indicating greater stability of the AHSV-4 VP2 trimer relative to that of BTV-1. Remarkably, three cysteines and one histidine—residues implicated in forming a zinc finger cluster in the cryo-EM structure of BTV-1 VP2 (ref. 2)—are conserved across all BTV and AHSV serotypes, though with a key positional difference. In BTV, the coordinating histidine (H164) is located two residues downstream of the adjacent cysteine (C162), whereas in AHSV-4 VP2 the equivalent histidine (H200) is positioned three residues downstream of the corresponding cysteine (C197) (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Thus, the relative spacing between these coordinating residues differs by one residue and, together with the insertion of α3 and α4 helices, marginally alters the spatial configuration of the putative zinc-binding motif. Consistent with this, the cryo-EM density of AHSV-4 VP2 supports the coordination of a Zn²⁺ ion by C197, C734, and C956, with weaker—but present—density for H200 (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Although the identity of the coordinated metal ion could not be unambiguously determined from the cryo-EM map alone due to limited local resolution, X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis of the VP2 sample confirmed the presence of Zn²⁺ in solution, alongside other divalent metals (Supplementary Fig. 8). The observed coordination geometry, together with prior structural data, supports its assignment as a zinc ion. Interestingly, in AHSV-4 VP2, the zinc finger cluster precedes the α3–α4 insertion at the base of the triskelion, a motif absent in BTV-1 VP2 (Supplementary Fig. 7b).

Full-length VP2 vaccine confers protection in IFNAR−/− mice

The recombinant VP2 used for structural studies was also assessed for its ability to elicit neutralizing antibodies and to protect highly susceptible IFNAR−/− mice from lethal AHSV infection5,21. By day 42 after immunization, all vaccinated mice had developed neutralizing antibodies against AHSV-4, with serum titers ranging from 1:30 to 1:640, irrespective of whether they received 10 µg or 25 µg of VP2 (formulated with ISA 71 adjuvant) (Fig. 3a). Following viral challenge infection at day 49, neutralizing titers increased markedly by day 63, reaching between 1:120 and 1:1280, with a statistically significant rise observed in the group receiving a dose of 25 µg. All vaccinated mice were fully protected from clinical signs after the lethal AHSV-4 challenge. In contrast, both unvaccinated IFNAR−/− control mice succumbed to infection at 8 or 9 days post-challenge. VP2 DI antigen selection and biophysical characterization

The cryo-EM structure of native-like VP2 provided a complete view of domain boundaries and their arrangement within the physiological trimer, revealing the fold and the spatial arrangement of previously predicted immunogenic neutralizing epitopes located within the most variable regions across AHSV serotypes22–24. The AHSV-4 VP2 atomic model enabled us to place the sequences of the remaining AHSV serotypes into their corresponding domains given the high sequence conservation of the helices composing the tip and central domains (Supplementary Fig. 5). Guided by the structural mapping of these variable, immunogenic regions onto the 3D structure, we designed two subdomains of AHSV-4 VP2 differing by 31 amino acids at their N-terminal end to facilitate large-scale production of vaccine candidates. Subdomain I (DI) spans residues G250 to I530, while subdomain II (DII) comprises residues P219 to I530. Both constructs encompass the tip and the central domains (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 5). DI and DII were expressed in mammalian Expi293 cells, and their antigenicity was evaluated in an immunization trial with IFNAR−/− mice (see below). As the larger DII domain conferred no advantage in immunogenicity and was expressed at lower yields, DI was selected for all subsequent experiments and analyses. DII was used only for comparison in selected assays. To assess whether the recombinant DI fragment expressed in a mammalian host cell system was folded according to the resolved full-length VP2 structure (Fig. 3b, top left), we carried out circular dichroism (CD) analysis. The CD spectrum indicated that the DI (including the linker, SpyTag, and StrepTag) is predominantly α-helical, with an estimated α-helical content of ~50% (Fig. 3b, right). Considering that the linker and tags are unlikely to adopt an α-helical conformation, this value corresponds to ~90% of the DI sequence, consistent with the subdomain being properly folded. Thermal stability assays further demonstrated that the DI fragment remains stable up to about 47 °C (Fig. 3b, bottom left), a temperature well above standard working conditions for vaccine production.

Non-native glycosylation increases AHSV-4 VP2 DI yield

Expressed with an N-terminally fused Igκ signal sequence in mammalian Expi293 cells, the recombinant AHSV-4 VP2 subdomains DI and DII, fused to a C-terminal SpyTag (ST), were efficiently released in the supernatant. However, migration in SDS-PAGE gels indicated that DI-ST and DII-ST were glycosylated. Considering that VP2 is present in the virion and is not glycosylated, it was important to assess the potential consequences of this non-native glycosylation. DI contains three potential N-glycosylation sites at positions N292, N301, and N498 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetNGlyc-1.0/). PNGase F digestion confirmed the presence of glycans upon expression in Expi293 cells (Fig. 3c). Glycopeptide analysis of the same sample further unraveled differences in the proportion of carbohydrate species at each site, with complex sugars predominating over high-mannose and hybrid species and N498 showing the largest species variability (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 9). The glycosylation of the VP2 DI subdomain (as well as DII) results from glycosyltransferase access to the Asn-X-Ser/Thr (X can be any amino acid except for proline) recognition sequons (Fig. 3d, top left). Sequence comparisons of VP2 from serotypes 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, and 9, which span the full phylogenetic diversity of AHSV, showed that the Asn residues at positions 292 and 301 in serotype 4 are not conserved across all the other serotypes, whereas the Asn site at position 498 is conserved in serotypes 5 and 9 (Supplementary Fig. 5).

To investigate the impact of glycosylation on protein expression, we expressed the wild-type VP2 DI domain in comparison to a mutated variant lacking all three glycosylation sites (Asn → Ala or Ser substitutions). Interestingly, the non-glycosylated mutant (mut-DI) was expressed at markedly lower yields and was only partially secreted into the supernatant (Fig. 3c).

Non-native glycosylation does not alter VP2 DI humoral response in rabbits

To compare the immunogenicity and functionality of the artificially glycosylated and non-glycosylated DI antigens, we immunized rabbits with each formulation. Four rabbits received three doses of 25 µg of the respective DI proteins displayed on mi3 nanoparticles. We collected serum samples before each booster and 4 weeks after the final immunization, and analyzed them by serum neutralization tests (SNT). Multivalent SC-mi3 display of DI or DII elicits protective antibody responses in mice

We evaluated the immunogenicity of the DI and DII domains, either as uncoupled subunits or displayed on mi3 nanoparticles, in a vaccination and challenge model using IFNAR−/− mice. Mice received two immunizations at 3-week intervals with 20 µg of the corresponding antigen. Serum analysis showed that uncoupled DI or DII induced neutralizing antibodies in four of six mice, whereas all animals vaccinated with DI-mi3 or DII-mi3 developed neutralizing antibodies with significantly higher titers than those immunized with the unconjugated proteins (Fig. 5b).

Following challenge with virulent AHSV-4, one of three unvaccinated control mice and one mouse vaccinated with unconjugated DI developed clinical signs and were euthanized 10 days postinfection. RT-qPCR analysis of samples collected 55 days after challenge revealed viral replication in control mice (Ct values, 24–26) and showed that immunization with uncoupled DI or DII did not prevent challenge virus replication. In contrast, mice vaccinated with DI-mi3 or DII-mi3 showed only trace levels of viral RNA in two animals (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 10). Because DI-mi3 and DII-mi3 conferred comparable protection and DI yielded higher expression levels, we selected DI-mi3 for the subsequent immunization study in horses.

SC-mi3 decorated with DI elicits neutralizing antibodies in horses

To evaluate the ability of DI-mi3 to elicit neutralizing antibodies in the target species, we immunized horses with three antigen doses. Three groups of four horses received two vaccinations on days 0 and 35 with either 25, 50, or 100 µg of antigen per dose. We detected no neutralizing antibodies in any group before day 35, regardless of dose. In contrast, all animals developed neutralizing antibodies between days 38 and 100, with peak titers occurring 7 to 15 days after the booster vaccination (Fig. 5d).

Overall, antibody responses varied widely across individuals, independent of dose. Two horses that received 100 µg doses showed the lowest neutralizing titers. Notably, 25 µg of antigen elicited neutralizing antibodies as effectively as 50 or 100 µg. One year after the prime immunization, we administered a booster vaccination with the same antigen dose they received for the first and second applications. All animals subsequently developed high neutralizing antibody titers (1:10 to 1:1000), peaking 7–10 days after vaccination (Fig. 5e). Horses immunized with 25 or 50 µg exhibited higher titers than those receiving 100 µg. Neutralizing antibodies remained detectable in all groups 100 days after the annual booster vaccination (1:30 to 1:320).

Discussion

AHSV has entered Europe on three documented occasions, attributed to windborne infected midges, but sustained by competent indigenous Culicoides. With climate change driving the northward spread of the major vector, Culicoides imicola, the risk of future incursions into Europe and beyond is rising26. Recent introduction of related orbiviruses to Central and Western Europe, such as BTV-3 and EHDV-8, underscores the risk and the need for preparedness. Although used in some endemic regions, the attenuated vaccine, which has important limitations, remains unlicensed outside Africa. To guide the design of antigenic domains for a subunit-nanoparticle vaccine, we determined the cryo-EM structure of the native, full-length AHSV-4 VP2 protein in its functional trimeric form—a structure that had long eluded characterization. The VP2 triskelion architecture serves a dual role, acting as a flexible multivalent receptor‑attachment protein and, together with VP5 trimers, forming an outer cage that protects the underlying viral core particle16. Comparing the AHSV-4 VP2 structure with the previously resolved BTV-1 VP2 revealed significant differences. This includes a distinct central α-helical insertion that contributes to stabilize the triskelion-shaped trimer and results in an orientation of the tip domain that is distinct from that of BTV VP2 (relative to the hub and body domains), a region implicated in receptor engagement and immune recognition. The presence of a zinc finger motif in AHSV VP2 in a similar structural position to that previously described in BTV VP2, which was proposed to mediate pH-sensing in the virus-cell entry process (e.g., VP2 dynamics and detachment in the early endosome during infection)2,27, indicates a conserved molecular mechanism of entry among orbiviruses. Although the functional role of this zinc-binding motif for AHSV remains to be experimentally validated, disruption of the zinc cluster likely increases the flexibility of the extended helix–turn–helix motif (α3–α4), a structural element that is absent in BTV VP2 and helps in scaffolding the triskelion exterior. This flexibility may be required to facilitate triskelion unfolding, which is more tightly stabilized than in BTV. Both the central and tip domains, which correspond to the most variable region across AHSV serotypes22–24, are surface-exposed and conformationally dynamic relative to the rest of the molecule, supporting that they are targets for neutralizing antibodies. We therefore aimed to express soluble subdomains of AHSV-4 VP2, which are secreted into the supernatant by targeting the proteins to the secretory pathway.

Unlike recombinant full-length VP2, which induces protective antibodies but is poorly expressed and not secreted, thereby limiting large-scale production in industrial settings, our structural insights enabled the rational design of soluble VP2 subdomains (DI and DII) for display on mi3 nanoparticles. The designed antigens preserved native-like folding, as demonstrated by biophysical analyses, and maintained epitope presentation, as proved by immunogenicity data.

Characterization of DI and DII purified from supernatants by SDS-PAGE gels suggested that both domains contained glycan moieties. Specifically, DI and DII were found to contain three potential N-linked glycosylation sites, which gave rise to heterogeneous glycan profiles. It is important to note that VP2 is naturally synthesized by free ribosomes and is thus not glycosylated when present on the AHSV virion. The inclusion of a signal peptide in our expression construct directs the VP2 subdomain into the secretory pathway, resulting in N‑linked glycosylation. N‑glycosylation can promote efficient protein folding and quality control in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), enhance protein stability by limiting proteasomal degradation, and facilitate ER‑to‑Golgi trafficking and secretion. Thus, the increased expression and secretion observed for the glycosylated VP2 subdomain are likely due to improved folding efficiency and stability within the host secretory pathway. This may involve engagement of the ER lectin chaperone system, such as calreticulin and/or calnexin, which is known to stabilize folding intermediates of N-glycosylated proteins and reduce premature degradation. In line with this interpretation, the presence of N-linked glycans enhanced yields of soluble DI and DII, supporting their role as expression enhancers rather than immunogenic modifiers28,29. Glycan presence has been reported to increase immunogenicity in some contexts, for example, via lectin receptor binding30, but we did not observe such an effect when analyzing antibody responses induced in rabbits immunized with mi3 displaying either glycosylated or non-glycosylated DI. Importantly, structural mapping showed that the glycosylation sites are located in regions buried within the native VP2 trimer, minimizing the risk of epitope masking. Accordingly, mutagenesis of all three N-linked glycosylation sites did not affect neutralization responses.

Unconjugated DI and DII domains provided only partial protection against clinical signs after AHSV infection and elicited modest neutralizing antibody titers in IFNAR−/− mice. In contrast, display of these antigens on mi3 nanoparticles markedly enhanced immunogenicity, inducing robust neutralizing antibody responses in both mice and rabbits and conferring complete protection against lethal challenge in mice. Cryo-EM analysis of DI-mi3 nanoparticles revealed a well-resolved core with diffuse density extending from the particle surface, consistent with conformational heterogeneity arising from the flexible linkage, as reported for related systems31. Importantly, such structural heterogeneity may favor immune recognition by increasing epitope accessibility and presentation. Collectively, studies in these small animal models provided a critical first step in evaluating vaccine candidates for immunogenicity and efficacy. Based on these promising results, we subsequently assessed the immunogenic potential of DI-mi3 in horses, the primary target species of AHSV. We showed that different antigen doses (25–100 µg) triggered neutralizing antibody responses following two injections with markedly raised titers (>1:1000 in most cases) after an annual single-shot booster immunization. Compared to other AHSV vaccine approaches, including licensed attenuated vaccines as well as inactivated and DISA-DIVA strategies that are still under development, DI-mi3 elicits strong neutralizing antibody titers while avoiding the safety concerns, slow onset of protection, and regulatory limitations posed by current options32,33.

These robust antibody responses in horses, together with the protective efficacy observed in mice, indicate that DI-mi3 formulated with appropriate adjuvants represents a promising prototype for next-generation AHSV subunit vaccines (Fig. 6). Ethics statement

Animal sample sizes were selected following the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement). The number of animals per group (4 for horses, 5 or 6 for mice) was determined by a power calculation based on a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test (α = 0.05; power = or >80%), designed to detect a difference of at least 1 log10 in viral load or antibody titer between groups. For rabbits, 2 animals per group were selected based on standard practice for immunogenicity experiments at the FLI. All animal studies were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations. The VP2 vaccination study in mice was approved by the French Ministry of Research (APAFIS#30145-2021061418428913 v2). The study with rabbits was approved by the relevant animal ethics committee at the State Office of Agriculture, Food Safety and Fisheries of Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, Rostock, Germany (permission number LALLF M-V/TSD/7221.3-2-003/23-1). Vaccination studies using nanoparticles decorated with the VP2 DI antigen in mice and horses complied with French national regulations for animal experimentation and were approved by the Service de la performance, du financement et de la contractualisation avec les organisms de recherche, France (APAFIS: #47580-2024021609415072 v4 and v5).

Production and purification of AHSV-4 full-length VP2

Recombinant full-length VP2 was produced as described previously with minor modifications34. The VP2 coding sequence from the reference strain (GenBank KM820850/UniProt A0A0B5GT51), fused at the N-terminus to a FLAG tag, was cloned into pDONR207 via Gateway® technology and transferred to pVote-FGW by LR Clonase™ recombination. BSR cells (Accession No Cellosaurus: CVCL_C8A1)35 were co-infected with MVA-T7 virus and transfected with the VP2-FLAG plasmid. VP2 expression was verified by western blot analysis using an anti-FLAG antibody according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ANTI-FLAG® M2 antibody, Cat. No F3165; Sigma-Aldrich); a positive construct was selected, and recombinant MVA expressing VP2-FLAG was generated by coumermycin counter-selection. For protein production, confluent BSR monolayers in T75 flasks were infected with recombinant MVA. After 1 h of adsorption, medium containing 100 µM IPTG was added and cells incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Cells were washed with PBS plus protease inhibitors, harvested by scraping, and stored at −20 °C. Lysates were prepared by freeze-thaw cycles, vortexing, and centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Clarified lysates were incubated with anti-FLAG M2 agarose resin, and VP2 protein was eluted with 3× FLAG peptide in 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4. Purity and identity were confirmed by SDS-PAGE, Coomassie staining, and western blot analysis using an anti-FLAG antibody. Purified VP2 was stored at −20 °C until use.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection for AHSV-4 VP2

Recombinant VP2 protein (C-terminal FLAG tag) was initially vitrified using a Leica EM GP2 plunge freezer (Leica Microsystems). Aliquots of 3 µL VP2 (0.1 mg/mL) were applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil R2/2 Cu 200 mesh grids (Cat. No N1-c16nCu20-01) at >90% humidity and 8 °C. Grids were screened in-house for particle distribution and vitrification quality. Preliminary cryo-EM data showed preferential orientation, resulting in an uneven angular distribution and limiting 3D reconstruction quality. To reduce orientation bias, VP2 was pegylated following the described protocols36. Briefly, VP2 (1.4 mg/mL) was buffer-exchanged into 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.6), 150 mM NaCl using a 10 kDa Vivaspin concentrator, incubated with 32 mM PEG8 (40 µL total volume), then buffer-exchanged into 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl using Bio-Spin 6 desalting columns. Pegylated VP2 (0.14 mg/mL) was vitrified on glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 Cu 300 mesh (Cat. No N1-C14nCu30-01) or UltrAuFoil R2/2 Au 200 mesh grids (Cat. No N1-A16nAu20-01) using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Initial data were collected at 0° tilt but showed a persistent preferential orientation for both unmodified and pegylated and pegylated samples. Subsequent datasets were acquired with +30° (4007 movies) and -30° (14,118 movies) stage tilts to increase the diversity of particle orientations and improve angular coverage. Imaging was performed on a Titan Krios G4 microscope (300 kV) equipped with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan Inc.). Automated acquisition used EPU 2 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 130,000× magnification (pixel size 0.6465 Å). Movies comprised 50 frames with a total dose of 52.7 e⁻/Å² and defocus range −0.80 to −2.6 µm. In total, 18,125 movies were collected (Supplementary Table 1).

VP2 image processing and 3D reconstruction

All data processing was conducted using cryoSPARC v437. Movies were motion-corrected, and the contrast transfer function was estimated within cryoSPARC. Particles were initially picked using the blob picker, followed by template-based picking from a small batch of particles with a 250 Å particle diameter. Selected particles were extracted in binned form with a box size of 256 pixels, yielding a total of 4,772,633 particles. Several rounds of 2D classification were performed to clean the particle set. An ab initio density map was generated using 7205 particles with C3 symmetry, which served as the starting model for initial homogenous refinements. The final map was obtained via non-uniform refinement with 381,372 particles, applying C3 symmetry, per-particle defocus, and anisotropic magnification fitting (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1c). The resolution, determined by the gold-standard FSC, was 3.11 Å (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Tilted data collection improved particle orientation coverage, yielding an informative and interpretable final map, though residual anisotropy remained (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Half-maps were also processed with spIsoNET v1, generating a slightly improved map used for cross-comparison during model building38.

VP2 model building, refinement, and analysis

To interpret the map, we combined the ModelAngelo-built model into the density with the VP2 predicted model from AlphaFold3 (refs. 19,39). The fragmented model generated by ModelAngelo was used as a reference to manually place the domains of the predicted model into the density map using the graphic software COOT v.0.9.8.7 and to adjust and rebuild regions with weaker density, such as the central and tip domains and some of the connecting loops, resulting in the correct protein register40. Model refinement was carried out using Phenix v1.20.1-4487 (ref. 41). The VP2 molecules forming the trimer were refined in real space within the boxed map using non-crystallographic symmetry, secondary-structure, rotamer, and Ramachandran restraints, with target bond and angle RMSD of 0.01 Å and of 0.8°, respectively, yielding a model with satisfactory geometry and model-to-map fit (Supplementary Table 1).

Sequence and structural comparisons between BTV-1 VP2 and AHSV-4 VP2

Sequence alignment between BTV-1 VP2 (UniProt accession B7U676) and AHSV-4 VP2 was performed using Emboss-Needle (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/psa/emboss_needle). Multiple sequence alignments of all AHSV serotypes (1–9) were generated with CLUSTAL-OMEGA (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo) and visualized using ESPript 3 (ref. 42). Structural superimposition of BTV-1 VP2 (PDB code 8W1O) onto AHSV-4 VP2 was carried out using Secondary Structure Matching in COOT. Analysis of energy, contact interactions, and interface areas for the AHSV-4 and BTV-1 VP2 trimers was conducted with PISA and PDBsum20,43. As BTV-1 VP2 was deposited as a monomer, three copies were fitted into the corresponding VP2 region of the BTV-1 cryo-EM density map (EMD-43727 [https://www.ebi.ac.uk/emdb/EMD-43727])17 using COOT, followed by real-space refinement in Phenix to reconstruct the physiological trimer.

Figures displaying molecular structures and density maps were generated either with ChimeraX44 or PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0, Schrödinger, LLC).

X-ray fluorescence of full-length VP2 and circular dichroism of VP2 DI antigen

To assess the presence of metal ions in the purified full-length VP2 sample, XRF analysis was performed at beamline ID30B of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF, Grenoble, France). Biochemical and mass spectrometry characterization of N-glycosylation sites and generation of carbohydrate-deficient VP2 DI

The presence of N-linked glycans on purified AHSV-4 VP2 DI and DII antigens was assessed by PNGase F treatment following the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat. No P0704S; New England Biolabs). For site-specific glycosylation analysis, LC-MS/MS was performed. The DI antigen (two biological replicates) was digested with trypsin or chymotrypsin using a filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol with minor modifications48. Enzyme-to-protein ratio was 1:20, incubated overnight at 37 °C, then the samples were dried in a speedvac (RVC2 25; Christ) and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid. Peptides were desalted using Omix C18 stage (10 µl) tips (Cat. No A5700310K; Agilent) and analyzed by LC-ESI-MS/MS. Mass spectrometry was conducted on a timsTOF Pro with PASEF (Bruker Daltonics) coupled to an Evosep ONE LC system, using a 30 samples-per-day protocol. Two acquisition methods were applied: a standard method for non-glycosylated peptides and a stepped-collision energy method optimized for glycopeptides49. Samples were analyzed in analytical triplicate with both methods. Data processing and glycopeptide identification were performed using the BYOS suite (Protein Metrics), searching against the VP2 DI sequence with precursor and fragment mass tolerances of 20 ppm and 0.05 Da, respectively. A mammalian glycan database of 182 compositions was used, and peptides scoring ≥200 identified in at least three replicates from one acquisition method were retained for further analysis.

Evaluation of full-length AHSV-4 VP2 immunogenicity in mice

The immunogenicity of full-length AHSV-4 VP2 was evaluated in 129/Sv IFNAR−/− knockout mice. Mice were housed at the ANSES Laboratory for Animal Health (Maisons-Alfort, France) under standard conditions, with a 12-h light/dark cycle, room temperature of ~22 °C, and relative humidity maintained between 40–60%. Twelve IFNAR−/− mice (10–30 weeks old, female or male) were allocated into three groups: two groups of five mice immunized with either 10 µg or 25 µg of purified VP2 in 70% MONTANIDETM ISA 71 VG adjuvant (Cat. No 36514P; Seppic), and one group of two mice receiving PBS (challenge control). Vaccines were freshly prepared by mixing antigen with adjuvant using a Minilyser (MP Biomedicals), adjusted to 100 µl per dose with sterile PBS, and administered subcutaneously on days 0 and 21. Blood was collected on day 42. On day 49, all mice were challenged with 5 × 10⁵ TCID₅₀ of AHSV-4. Blood samples were taken on day 55 (for viral genome detection) and day 63 for serum neutralization test (SNT). For serotype-specific neutralizing antibodies against AHSV-4, two-fold serial dilutions of sera (in duplicate), starting at 1:10 (up to 1:640), were incubated with 100 TCID₅₀ of AHSV-4 in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) for 1 h at 37 °C. Vero C1008 cells (ATCC CRL-1586) were added (20,000/well in 100 µl MEM supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum and 2% sodium pyruvate), and plates were incubated for 5–7 days at 37 °C. The neutralization titer was defined as the log₂ of the reciprocal serum dilution resulting in 50% neutralization.

No L00350Y; GenScript), and confirmed to be <10 EU/ml. For conjugation, the purified DI-ST antigen was incubated with SC-mi3 nanoparticles at a molar ratio of 1:1.5 (antigen:SC-subunit) for 24 h at 4 °C in conjugation buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0). The SpyTag003/SpyCatcher003 reaction proceeded to completion, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE, western blot, and dynamic light scattering. For western blot staining, a StrepMAB-Classic HRP (Cat. No 2-1509-00; IBA Lifesciences) and a monoclonal anti-FLAG M2 antibody (Cat. No F3165; Sigma-Aldrich) were used following the manufacturer’s recommendation. The biochemical analyses showed no detectable amounts of unbound antigens in the final preparation; therefore, no additional purification steps were performed. Consequently, antigen loading is defined by the input stoichiometry and corresponds to an average occupancy per particle. Single-particle occupancy was not directly measured. All vaccine preparations were freshly assembled prior to use.

Evaluation of immunogenicity of nanoparticle-coupled glycosylated or non-glycosylated VP2 DI in rabbits

Glycosylated and non-glycosylated VP2 DI antigens were conjugated to SC-mi3 nanoparticles as described above. Conjugation efficiency and nanoparticle integrity were confirmed by SDS-PAGE, western blot, and dynamic light scattering. Rabbit immunizations were performed at the FLI animal facility (housing conditions: 12-h light/dark cycle, 15–21 °C, 45–60% relative humidity). Per group, two male and two female 3–4 months old New Zealand White rabbits (Crl:KBL(NZW), strain code 052; Charles River Laboratories, Germany) were randomly selected. The animals were immunized three times at 4-week intervals with 0.5 ml doses containing 25 µg antigen formulated with 10% (v/v) Polygen adjuvant (Cat. No MVP120121GLB-R012; Phibro Animal Health). Blood was collected prior to each booster and 4 weeks after the final immunization. Serum neutralization tests were performed according to World Organisation for Animal Health guidelines using 100 TCID₅₀/ml of AHSV-4, and the titers were determined after 7 days of incubation. The neutralization titer was defined as the log₂ of the reciprocal serum dilution resulting in 50% neutralization.

Evaluation of DI-mi3 and DII-mi3 immunogenicity in mice

Twenty-seven C57BL/6 IFNAR−/− knockout mice, aged approximately 18–41 weeks and housed at the ANSES Laboratory for Animal Health under the same conditions described above, were divided into five groups: four groups of six mice each received 20 µg of DI or DII, or the same amount of antigen coupled with mi3 nanoparticles while one group of three mice served as the challenge control (PBS only). Vaccine formulations were prepared as described above with 70% MONTANIDETM ISA 71 VG adjuvant (Cat. No 36514P; Seppic) and administered subcutaneously on days 0 and 21. Blood was collected on day 42. Mice were challenged on day 49 with 5 × 10⁵ TCID₅₀ of AHSV-4, and blood was collected on day 55 (for genome detection) and day 64 (for SNT).

AHSV genome detection in mice

To assess viral replication, total RNA was extracted from 10 µl of mouse blood collected 6 days post-challenge using the ID Gene™ Mag Universal Extraction Kit (IDvet) and eluted in 80 µl of RNase-free water. Five microliters of denatured RNA (95 °C, 3 min) were analyzed by RT-qPCR targeting segment 3 of the AHSV genome51.

Efficacy of mi3 particles decorated with AHSV-4 VP2 DI antigens in horses

To determine if the conjugated AHSV-4 DI candidate vaccine could elicit a humoral response in the target species, antigens were thawed and formulated with 2.1 mg of aluminum hydroxide gel (Alhydrogel® 2%, Croda Denmark A/S, Frederikssund, Denmark; product code AJV3010) and 12 hemolytic units of Saponin (Quillaja saponaria, Cat. No 0521; Schmittmann GmbH) in Tris buffer pH 7.4 (final volume per dose: 800 µl). Three groups of five horses were inoculated with the formulated nanoparticles containing 25, 50, or 100 µg of DI: four horses per group (1 Anglo-Arabian, 18 years old; 4 Trotters, 10, 6, 5, 6 years old and 7 Saddle horse, 9, 19, 12, 8, 17, 12, 11 years old) were vaccinated twice, with a 5-week interval. Animals were housed at the Institut Français du Cheval et de l’Équitation (La Jumenterie du Pin, France). Horses were maintained in groups in stables on a 100% forage diet for 5 days following vaccination and were subsequently kept at pasture. Vaccinations were performed by a veterinarian. Blood samples were collected by trained personnel under light restraint (halter holding) to minimize stress. Animals were monitored for 5 days following each inoculation. Monitoring included rectal temperature measurements taken twice daily and daily visual assessment for adverse effects, including abnormal posture or behavior (e.g., reduced appetite, lethargy, difficulty lowering the neck).

To evaluate immunogenicity, serum samples were collected on D0 (in 2024, prior to the first inoculation), D7, D15, D21, D28, D35, D38, D42, D50, D63, D72, D85, and D100 to assess the antibody response after the first and the second inoculation by SNT. After the annual boost in 2025, samples were collected on D0, D7, D10, D14, D21, D28, D42, D56, D70, D85, and D100 for analysis by SNT.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons of neutralizing antibody titers across two time points for two treatment groups (e.g., full-length VP2 at different doses and time points), a two-sided paired Student’s t-test was used. Neutralization titer and Ct differences between DI and DII antigens, either unconjugated or conjugated to mi3, were evaluated using a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test. For RT-qPCR analysis, undetectable viral RNA samples were assigned a Ct value of 40, corresponding to the maximum number of amplification cycles. For all statistical analyses, a significant difference was defined by a p value < 0.05.

Cryo-EM of DI-mi3 antigen vaccine and analysis

Three microliters at 0.66 ug/mL of SC003-mi3 nanoparticles conjugated with DI-SpyTag were deposited on Quantifoil UltrAuFoil R2/2 200 mesh grids and vitrified using a Leica EM GP2 plunge freezer. The grids were previously glow-discharged with the PELCO easiGLOW system for 45 s at 15 mA. The chamber conditions during the vitrification were 8 °C and 95% humidity. Cryo-EM movies (50 frames) were collected in the same TEM microscope as used for the VP2 target but at 0° tilt and at a nominal magnification of 105,000×, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.8238 Å and with an average defocus ranging from −0.8 to −2.5 µm. A total electron dose of 50.1 e⁻/Å² was applied per movie, and 10,044 movies were collected. Image processing was performed using the cryoSPARC pipeline, including extensive 2D classification steps with circular masks of varying radii applied to extracted, binned particles (final pixel size: 1.648 Å; box size: 256 pixels). The atomic model with PDB code 5KP9 was used as an initial template for 3D heterogeneous refinement in cryoSPARC, using both C1 and I1 symmetries. The final symmetrised map reached a resolution of ~3.5 Å, as estimated by the gold-standard FSC at 0.143 (Supplementary Table 2), while the C1 reconstruction resolved to ~4.8 Å. Final maps were interpreted using the atomic model from PDB code 5KP9, which was rigid-body fitted into the density using ChimeraX and further analyzed.
