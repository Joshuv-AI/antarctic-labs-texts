Descriptive analysis of the causes and outcomes of patients admitted to intensive care unit in Gaza during June 2024: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Ahmed Abed, Abd Al-Karim Sammour, Shadi Aljerjawi, Ruba Musallam, Mohannad Shaheen, Hani Sammour, Bashar N. Alzghoul, Moaath K. Mustafa Ali, Abdallah Y. Naser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Since the onset of the 2023 Israel–Gaza war, the healthcare system in the Gaza Strip has nearly collapsed. More than 70,937 casualties and 171,192 injuries have been reported, yet limited data exist on injury types and outcomes. Critical care services are essential during conflicts. This study aimed to determine causes of intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, associated injuries, complications, and outcomes in the Gaza Strip. We conducted a prospective cohort study of all adult and pediatric patients admitted to the ICUs at European Gaza Hospital and Shuhada Al-Aqsa Hospital between May 29 and June 30, 2024. Outcomes of interest were causes of admission, survival, types of complications, and length of stay. A total of 115 patients were admitted. Median age was 27 years (interquartile range 18–38), including 30 pediatric patients (26%). Thirty-five patients were female (30%). Trauma with or without burns accounted for 75 admissions (65%), while 27 (23.5%) had medical causes. Among trauma patients, 56 (75%) were from explosive injuries; traumatic brain injury was most common (30/75, 40%), including 10 of 21 pediatric trauma patients (47.6%). During ICU stay, 12 patients developed sepsis (10.6%), 10 developed septic shock (8.8%), and 12 developed multiorgan dysfunction (10.6%). Of 112 patients with known disposition, 64 were discharged and 48 died (43%). Median ICU survival was 6 days (95% confidence interval 5–17), and 17 days for pediatric patients (95% confidence interval 5–not calculable). Severe traumatic brain injury (33%) was the leading cause of death, followed by septic shock (22%). The high burden of trauma, complications, and mortality reflects the critical collapse of Gaza’s healthcare system. Women and children represented a substantial proportion of ICU patients, highlighting civilian vulnerability. Urgent international medical intervention, humanitarian support, and a permanent ceasefire are essential to prevent continued loss of life.

1. Introduction

Before the current 2023 Israel–Gaza war, the healthcare system in Gaza was characterized as brittle and was composed of 36 hospitals serving close to 2.2 million people.[1,2] The system was already strained by recurrent wars, limited funding, insufficient medical staffing, and a 17-year siege.[3,4] Since the war started in October 2023, the health system has been subject to severe stress because of the massive increase in the number of casualties and deaths.[1,5] As of December 23, 2025, the Ministry of Health has reported at least 70,937 fatalities and 171,192 injuries related to the war.[2] These estimates are believed largely to be accurate and conservative.[6] Moreover, when accounting for direct and indirect mortality due to the war, it was estimated that almost 186,000 or more deaths have occurred between October 2023 and July 2024, which represented 8.5% of the population of Gaza before the war.[7] Indirect mortality is likely now much higher, due to the length and the severity of the ongoing war. In addition, Gaza has experienced a significant increase in infectious epidemics,[8] including close to 992,000 acute respiratory infections, 575,000 acute watery diarrhea, and 107,000 cases of acute jaundice syndrome, as of August 2024.[9] Another factor contributing to increased morbidity and mortality in the Gaza Strip is the famine affecting both children and adults.[2,10,11] The ongoing war and its health consequences have led to a precipitous decline in life expectancy by more than 30 years, even under conservative estimates.[12]

The health system’s capacity to deal with this large influx of casualties has been limited due to damage to the infrastructure and a reduction in the number of healthcare workers. Out of 36 hospitals, 32 have been raided or damaged.[13] As of December 15, 2025, 18 hospitals are partially functional.[2] On several occasions, hospitals have undergone forced evacuation, compounding the suffering of patients.[2,14] As of December 15, 2025, 825 attacks impacted healthcare facilities, and at least 985 and 2000 healthcare workers were killed and injured, respectively.[2]

Although some literature has been published about the 2023 Israel–Gaza war,[12,15–21] little is known about the types of admissions to intensive care units (ICU) and injuries associated with admissions in the Gaza Strip. In this study, we aim to determine the causes and outcomes of admission to ICUs and types of trauma-related injuries encountered in the Gaza Strip after the onset of the 2023 Israel–Gaza war.

2. Methodology

2.1. Study design, setting, and participants

This multisite prospective cohort study was conducted in the ICUs of European Gaza Hospital (EGH) and Shuhada Al-Aqsa Hospital (SAH) in the Gaza Strip. The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Isra University. At the time of the study, no functioning local IRB or Helsinki Committee was available in Gaza; therefore, administrative approval for study conduct was obtained from the leadership of both EGH and SAH.

Verbal informed consent was obtained from patients who were able to provide consent. Written consent was not feasible due to emergency clinical conditions and severe logistical constraints during the study period, including scarcity of paper, lack of printing resources, and intermittent electricity availability. For patients who lacked decision-making capacity due to medical conditions, verbal consent was obtained from a family member or, if unavailable, from a close acquaintance. In cases where no surrogate decision-maker was available, the requirement for informed consent was waived. The full study protocol is provided as a Supplementary File, Supplemental Digital Content 1.

The study enrolled all patients admitted or already present in the ICU from May 29, 2024, through June 30, 2024. During the study period, there was only 1 ICU functioning in each hospital and therefore also managed pediatric patients when they needed intensive care. A significant number of casualties died in the emergency department (ED) upon arrival, and admissions to the ICU were triaged by ED and ICU physicians based on the patient’s likelihood to survive, with very severe cases being less likely to be admitted due to resource constraints. There were no formal criteria for ICU triage established. The exact numbers arriving at the ED before being admitted to ICU were not tracked.

The EGH is in Khan Younis, Gaza Strip, and contains 232 beds. During this current war, the capacity has increased to approximately 1000 beds.[22] The hospital was founded with a grant from United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East. During the study period, it was the main referral center for the southern areas in the Gaza Strip. In June 2024, the ICU was functioning at 21 beds and contained 21 mechanical ventilators. During 24-hour shifts, 3 physicians covered the ICU, who were residents most of the time. There was an intermittent presence of board-certified critical care specialists. Nurses used to cover 2 patients at any time during shifts.

The SAH is in Deir al-Balah, Gaza Strip, and contains 166 beds, but during the war, it increased to up to approximately 800 beds.[23] The hospital is managed by the Palestinian Ministry of Health. During the study period, it was the main referral center for central areas in the Gaza Strip. In June 2024, the medical ICU unit was functioning at 10 beds and contained 10 mechanical ventilators. During the study period, there were a total of 16 physicians, 8 were board-certified in the ICU (anesthesiology or pulmonary/ critical care) and 8 were not board-certified/ eligible (i.e., general practitioners). On any day, the ICU would be staffed by a total of 2 ICU physicians, 2 general practitioners, and 6 to 8 nurses.

2.2. Variables and outcomes

The study team collected the following variables: admitting hospital name, age, sex, baseline comorbidities (i.e., hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart disease), source of referral/ admission to ICU, date of admission to hospital and ICU, date of discharge from ICU and hospital, discharge destination from ICU, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) at admission.

Regarding the cause of admission, it was classified into trauma, burn, surgical reasons not related to trauma, and medical reasons. For trauma-related admissions, they were categorized based on military vs nonmilitary causes. Further details about trauma-related injuries were also collected.

Other variables included types and dates of treatments administered in the ICU: elective or emergent surgery, intubation, antibiotic administration, hemodialysis administration, and inotrope/vasopressor administration.

Outcomes and complications of ICU stay included ICU mortality, sepsis, septic shock, multiorgan dysfunction (MODS), bed sores, ventilator-associated or hospital-associated pneumonia, ICU delirium, and urinary tract infection. We also collected further details on types of infections managed, including the type of infection, date of occurrence, therapeutic approach, and clinical outcome.

2.3. Data sources

The data were collected using a form created in Google Forms and shared as an electronic link with the data collection team (form available as a Supplementary File, Supplemental Digital Content 1).[24] The data collection team was working in the ICU in the Gaza Strip and comprised 3 medical doctors, 2 medical students, 1 critical care nurse, and 1 board-certified critical care attending. The data collection sheet was created in May 2024, and after a pilot period of 3 days, data collection commenced on May 29, 2024. Patient data were collected dynamically and updated at the time of discharge from the ICU. Data sources included: directly from patients when feasible, family members or companions when available, surgical operative notes, and patient medical records written by medical doctors.

The data collection cutoff was mid-July 2024, and the data were downloaded and underwent cleaning. An attempt was made to obtain missing data; however, the EGH was evacuated on July 1, 2024.[25] Patients who were still in the ICU at the time of the EGH evacuation were considered lost to follow-up and censored in the analysis because of the inability to track the outcomes of those patients after their evacuation.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize variables. Continuous data were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or means with standard deviations (SDs). Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers and summarized as percentages. Mortality during the ICU stay was collected as a time-to-event, summarized using the Kaplan–Meier method, and compared using log-rank. The time was calculated from the time of admission to the ICU until the time of death in the ICU or discharge alive from the ICU. Median follow-up was determined by the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. The study included all patients admitted, and no sample size was calculated. The assumptions underlying the log-rank test and Kaplan–Meier analyses were verified. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P values < .05 were considered statistically significant. R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; 2024-06-14) statistical software was used for analysis. There was no funding for this study.

3. Results

3.1. Patient characteristics and admission details

Between May 29 and June 30, 2024, 57 patients (49.5%) were treated in the EGH ICU and 58 in the SAH ICU (50.5%). Patients of all ages were treated in the general ICU in both EGH and SAH. The average age of patients was 30.2 years (SD ± 16.8), and the median age was 27 years (IQR: 18–38, range: 3–80). There were 30 pediatric patients (≤ 18 years) admitted to the ICU (26%), with an average age of 12.7 years (SD ± 4.5) and a median age of 14 years (IQR: 11–17 years). Thirty-five patients were female (30%). Baseline hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease/ heart failure were seen in 7 (6%), 14 (12%), and 4 (3.5%) patients, respectively.

The source of admission to the ICU was 28.7% from outside, other than a hospital, 27% transfer from the regular nursing floor, 27% from the operating room, and 17.4% transfer from another hospital. The GCS at the time of admission was available in 60 patients. The median GCS was 10.5 (IQR: 7–15). The causes of admissions to both ICUs were trauma (n = 69 patients), medical (n = 27), surgical other than trauma/ burn (n = 13), combined trauma and burn (n = 5), and combined trauma and surgical other than trauma (n = 1). In the pediatric-age group, 7 admissions were for medical reasons, 2 for surgical other than trauma/ burn, and 21 were related to trauma or burn. 4. Discussion

In this study, we aimed to characterize the spectrum of injuries, assess their complexity, and evaluate clinical outcomes among patients requiring critical care in Gaza during the 2023 Israel–Gaza war. Most ICU admissions were due to trauma-related injuries, with a substantial proportion occurring in pediatric patients. The high mortality observed likely reflects both injury severity and limited healthcare resources. By providing this information to the international healthcare community, we aim to help guide understanding of the specific needs of Palestinian patients in Gaza and to inform future humanitarian and medical missions and assist in planning resources required to care for critically ill and injured patients.

There is limited data on the types of injuries that exist in the current Israel–Gaza war.[26–28] In a preprint publication, Alsalqawi and Villar studied the types of injuries of 110 consecutive patients who presented to the ED of SAH.[29] Similar to our study, they found that explosive injuries accounted for 86.4% of trauma injuries compared to 8.2% of gunshot injuries.[29] Additionally, women and children represented 34.6% and 24.6% of injured patients, which aligns with our findings.[29] Previous wars were associated with similar types of injuries and a similar distribution of war-related injuries among females and pediatric-age groups.[30] Females represent 49.3% of the population in Gaza, and the median age is 19.5 years.[31] Around 38.8% of the population is 14 years old or less. In our study, 30% of ICU admissions were females and 26% were children. Despite trauma-related injuries being more common in men, 45% of these injuries occurred in women, and trauma was the leading cause of admission in the pediatric-age group. We also found that the causes of injuries and administered therapies in the ICU were prevalent in both pediatric and adult groups. These findings indicate that military acts resulted in significant collateral damage to civilians. In a survey study of 78 international healthcare workers deployed to Gaza, 23,726 total injuries were reported; 81% were traumatic in nature and 19% were burn injuries, which is consistent with our findings.[28]

Our study found that most ICU admissions were due to trauma (65%), followed by medical reasons (23.5%). This differs from common causes of admission to ICUs globally, which vary but are commonly related to medical reasons, such as cardiovascular complications, respiratory failure, or intoxication.[32–34] We found that a significant number of patients required emergency or elective surgeries, indicating the complexity of care that patients needed during their ICU stay. The most common type of injury seen in patients admitted to the ICU was TBI, which can be associated with significant morbidity and mortality. TBI can vary from mild to severe and is associated with long-term neurodegenerative diseases if a patient survives acute complications.[35] Previous studies found that explosive injuries were the most common cause of TBI in war-related injuries.[35,36] Similar to our study, we found that missiles and explosive mechanisms accounted for the majority of trauma-related admissions.

The rates of ICU complications were broadly consistent with prior literature. Sepsis and septic shock were present in 10.6% and 8.8% of patients in the ICU in our study, which is consistent with other studies.[37] Similarly, the occurrence of MODS was comparable to previous reports.[38] Beyond injury severity, the high burden of sepsis likely reflects systemic constraints, including limited infection control capacity and shortages of essential equipment and supplies. Such factors may contribute to preventable sepsis-related mortality. Strengthening basic infrastructure is therefore critical to improving outcomes in these settings. Around 5% of patients developed ICU delirium, similar to rates reported elsewhere.[39] ICU mortality in this cohort was high compared to international benchmarks. However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of severe resource constraints and ICU triage practices during the study period. Because patients with extremely severe injuries and a very low likelihood of survival were often not admitted to the ICU due to limited bed capacity and staffing shortages, the reported mortality reflects outcomes among selected ICU-admitted patients rather than all critically injured patients. Consequently, the true mortality burden among critically ill and injured patients in Gaza is likely higher than observed in this cohort. The median survival in the pediatric-age group was 17 days compared to 6 in adults (P = .2). These survival estimates represent ICU survival among admitted patients only and should not be interpreted as hospital-wide or population-level survival. Furthermore, survival estimates in our study are subject to uncertainty because of the relatively small sample size, limited follow-up duration, and censoring resulting from patient transfer, discharge, or ICU evacuation. Although pediatric patients demonstrated longer survival, this must be interpreted in the context of severely limited rehabilitation and follow-up capacity. Survivors, particularly those with TBI, are at high risk of significant long-term neurological and functional disability. This will likely translate into a substantial and under-recognized societal and caregiving burden in the long term. The most common cause of death in our study was severe TBI. Despite variations in ICU case fatality rates by country and type of ICU, the case fatality in our study was higher compared to the international estimates, which range between 10 to 29%.[40–43] This indicates the severity of patient injuries, decreased medical capabilities of hospitals, insufficient medical staffing, and possible lack of medical expertise.

The elevated mortality observed in this cohort likely reflects not only injury severity but also profound strain on the healthcare system. Previous studies have documented workforce shortages, prolonged working hours, psychological distress, and direct exposure of healthcare workers to conflict-related harm, all of which may adversely affect healthcare delivery and patient outcomes.[2,44–46] The high mortality and complication burden observed in this study underscore the importance of protecting healthcare facilities and personnel during armed conflict. International organizations, including the World Health Organization and Global Health Cluster, have emphasized that attacks on healthcare infrastructure disrupt access to essential medical services and worsen patient outcomes.[47,48]

5. Limitations

Our study has limitations. The study was conducted in June 2024, and its findings cannot be generalized to the whole war duration, which started in October 2023. The number of deaths and injuries has slowed down over the war period, and hence, the proportion of trauma-related admissions likely varied over the last several months of the war.[49] During the study period, the bed occupancy of the EGH ICU was close to 70%, which was one of the lowest rates since the war started, according to on-ground physicians’ observation. In late June of 2024, Palestinians fled Eastern Khan Younis to Western Khan Younis, and with the concomitant opening of Nasser Hospital in Western Khan Younis, most critically ill and injured cases were diverted to Nasser Hospital.[50] On the other hand, bed occupancy in SAH was close to 100% for most of June 2024. Another reason the study’s generalizability is limited is because the EGH and SAH only served the critical care needs of the central and southern Gaza Strip, excluding the northern territories. The Netzarim Corridors separated Northern Gaza from the southern parts,[51] and during the study period, the Indonesian Hospital was the main hospital providing critical care for northern regions after Al-Shifa Hospital, the main referral hospital in the Gaza Strip, was largely destroyed.[52] We could not extend our study to northern Gaza, given the severity of the war there, insufficient medical staffing, and poor internet coverage. Another limitation is the ability of medical staff to differentiate between the different types of military-related injuries, as most medical staff have not received extensive military or forensic medicine training. This may have led to some misclassification in data. Despite that, the exposure of medical staff to recurrent wars in Gaza, the presence of general and trauma surgeons, and the protracted wars have increased their experience in identifying and treating these injuries. Moreover, some data are missing, including the GCS, which was not possible to collect retrospectively. The intermittent internet coverage and the evacuation order of EGH in early July limited our ability to collect missing data or extend the study period. Because of the limited resources and staff availability, patients with very severe injuries and a low likelihood of survival were not admitted to the ICU, and hence, this likely skewed the patient population toward those with better prognoses, introducing selection bias and underestimating the true mortality rate. This could limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly in relation to the full spectrum of critically ill patients typically seen during mass casualty events. One of the major challenges in the study design was the absence of a functioning local IRB at the time, along with substantial barriers to obtaining informed consent. Many patients were intubated or critically injured, and in some cases, no family members were available to provide consent due to displacement or death related to the military conflict; several patients were the sole survivors of their immediate families. In this context, informed consent was obtained verbally whenever possible from patients or, when they lacked decision-making capacity, from available surrogates. When no surrogate decision-maker was available, the requirement for consent was waived in accordance with IRB guidance. To address these constraints, investigators analyzed data in aggregate and ensured that no patient identifiers were included. The Isra University IRB reviewed and approved this approach. Another limitation is that we were also not able to follow patients after their ICU stay due to limited staff availability.

6. Conclusion

This study characterizes the types of injuries encountered in ICUs across Gaza hospitals during the 2023 Israel–Gaza war and highlights the poor clinical outcomes observed among critically ill patients. In doing so, it elucidates the profound challenges faced by Gaza’s healthcare system during a prolonged period of armed conflict. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining access to healthcare services, protecting healthcare facilities and personnel, and ensuring sustained humanitarian and medical support for civilians affected by armed conflict. Our study reflects the state of the healthcare system as of June 2024; since that time, conditions have continued to deteriorate, with widespread damage to healthcare infrastructure, loss of healthcare personnel, and extensive destruction of the broader social fabric. We urge international agencies and the global medical community to support efforts that preserve healthcare infrastructure, expand access to critical care services, and facilitate the delivery of humanitarian and medical assistance to affected populations. Immediate and decisive international action is imperative to prevent further catastrophic loss of human life.
