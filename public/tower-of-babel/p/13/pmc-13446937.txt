Rare Case of Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma in an Adolescent with Multiple Congenital Anomalies and Homozygous USH2A Mutation
Authors: Arvind Krishnamurthy, Nandhini Vighneshwar, Gurushankari Balakrishnan, Venkatraman Radhakrishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 16-year-old boy from consanguineous parents, carrying a homozygous USH2A mutation and multiple congenital anomalies, presented with distal esophageal squamous cell carcinoma – likely the first such case documented. This report underscores the value of genetic testing and multidisciplinary management, demonstrating feasible surgery-only treatment in a frail adolescent with an expanded USH2A phenotype.

INTRODUCTION

Esophageal carcinoma ranks as the sixth leading cause of cancer-related death globally, with incidence peaking between 35 and 64 years.[1] Its occurrence in children and adolescents is extraordinarily rare, with only isolated case reports documented.[2] In pediatric series, progressive dysphagia occurs in nearly 90% of patients, identifiable risk factors in one-third, and metastatic disease at diagnosis in almost half, contributing to dismal prognosis.[2] Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) predominates in younger patients and may involve any esophageal segment, unlike the distal predilection of adenocarcinoma in adults. No dedicated management guidelines exist for pediatric esophageal carcinoma; treatment is extrapolated from adult protocols and individualized according to stage, incorporating surgery, chemotherapy, and/or radiotherapy.[2] Outcomes remain poor due to delayed diagnosis and advanced presentation. The aim of this case report is to describe the first documented instance of esophageal SCC in an adolescent with a homozygous USH2A mutation and multiple congenital anomalies, highlight the expanded phenotypic spectrum of USH2A-associated disease, and discuss the benefits of genetic testing in informing multidisciplinary, tailored management strategies for such rare presentations.

CASE REPORT

A 16-year-old boy, born to second-degree consanguineous parents, presented with 2 weeks of progressive dysphagia, worse for solids, accompanied by regurgitation and vomiting. He was the second child; an elder sibling was healthy, and no family history of congenital anomalies or malignancy existed. Past history included left nephrectomy at 15 days for multicystic renal dysplasia, diagnosis of epidermolysis bullosa and ichthyosis at 5 months, recurrent acute glomerulonephritis with hypocomplementemia at 3 years, and bilateral severe sensorineural hearing loss confirmed by brainstem evoked response audiometry, resulting in deafness and mutism. He received enalapril, calcium, and ranitidine. At 12 years, lipoid proteinosis was diagnosed and treated for 4 years.

On examination, height was 140 cm and weight 25 kg (below the third percentile). Findings included ankyloglossia, poor dentition with malformed teeth, dry scaly skin, and premature hair graying. No ocular, nasal, or aural deformities were noted; lymphadenopathy was absent. The chest was normal. The abdomen showed distension and a left subcostal scar from nephrectomy; no organomegaly. Genitalia were normal. Neurological examination revealed a normal calvarium, spontaneous limb movements, mutism, and monosyllabic communication. Developmental and speech delays were attributed to profound hearing loss, though broader syndromic neurological involvement remained possible.

Routine blood tests, including hemogram, renal, and liver function, were normal. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a circumferential obstructing growth at 30 cm from the incisors, precluding further passage. Biopsy confirmed moderately differentiated SCC (grade 2). Positron emission tomography–computed tomography demonstrated distal esophageal thickening with hypermetabolism extending to the gastroesophageal junction, without mediastinal nodes or metastases (cT3N0M0) [Figure 1].

Positron emission tomography–computed tomography images of the thorax and upper abdomen demonstrating irregular thickening due to growth in the distal esophagus

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy was deferred due to single-kidney status, recurrent glomerulonephritis, and frailty. Upfront open transhiatal esophagectomy with gastric conduit was performed, which entailed the removal of the esophagus without opening the chest. This was performed through an upper abdominal incision and a left neck incision. The entire esophagus, including the tumor-containing distal portion, is bluntly dissected from both ends through the widened diaphragmatic hiatus and neck and transacted with a 10-cm proximal and a 5-cm distal margin. The stomach tube (gastric conduit) is fashioned in the abdomen and is pulled up through the posterior mediastinum to the neck, where it is anastomosed to the remaining cervical esophagus, restoring continuity [Figure 2]. Postoperative pulmonary infection was treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics. Oral feeds began on day 6; discharge occurred the next day. Histopathology confirmed SCC, pT3N1, with R0 resection. Multidisciplinary tumor board comprising pediatric surgeons, surgical oncologists, medical oncologists, and radiation oncologists, among other recommended omitting adjuvant therapy, favoring surveillance given R0 margins and syndromic frailty. However, the patient was lost to follow-up after 3 months and reportedly succumbed to his cancer five and a half months post his surgery.

DISCUSSION

This case documents esophageal SCC in a syndromic adolescent with homozygous USH2A mutation and multisystem anomalies, highlighting diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in pediatric esophageal carcinoma. Fewer than 100 cases u nder age 21 are reported worldwide.[2] Progressive dysphagia is nearly universal; one-third have risk factors (caustic ingestion and tobacco) and half present with metastases, yielding a median survival of 9–14 months.[2]

Usher syndrome type 2A, due to biallelic USH2A mutations, classically features sensorineural hearing loss and retinitis pigmentosa.[3] Our patient lacked retinopathy but displayed renal, cutaneous, and oral anomalies, indicating phenotypic expansion.[4] USH2A encodes usherin, critical for basement membrane integrity; truncating mutations correlate with severe phenotypes.[4] Extraocular manifestations are uncommon but plausible given overlapping extracellular matrix roles with epidermolysis bullosa (dermoepidermal junction genes) and lipoid proteinosis (ECM1 mutations).[5] Consanguinity supports recessive inheritance; renal involvement suggests basement membrane defects possibly involving USH2A, ECM1, or COL4A networks.

No direct USH2A–esophageal cancer link exists, but epithelial fragility from matrix dysfunction could promote chronic irritation and carcinogenesis, especially with mucosal anomalies like ankyloglossia. To further explain this in a mechanistic manner, truncating mutations, like the homozygous frameshift deletion identified in this syndrome (chr1: G.216418642del), could potentially lead to epithelial fragility akin to that in lipoid proteinosis (as seen in the patient), where weakened barriers increase the susceptibility to chronic mechanical or chemical irritation from food bolus, regurgitation, or gastroesophageal reflux. Over time, this could promote inflammation, metaplasia, and carcinogenesis through pathways involving DNA damage accumulation or impaired repair, similar to how basement membrane defects in syndromes like tylosis (Howel–Evans syndrome) which predisposes to esophageal SCC without direct oncogenic mutations. Further, the potential iatrogenic contributions from the patient’s long-term medications (enalapril, calcium supplements, and ranitidine) might have synergistically heightened the mucosal vulnerability, contributing to the early onset of SCC without traditional risk factors in the context of USH2A-related epithelial fragility. Known syndromes predisposing to esophageal carcinoma (tylosis, Fanconi anemia, and Bloom syndrome) involve DNA repair or epithelial integrity; USH2A may represent an unrecognized contributor in pediatric cases lacking environmental triggers.

Management lacks pediatric guidelines; adult protocols are adapted.[2] Multimodal therapy improves survival when complete resection is combined with platinum-based chemotherapy, though radiation is often avoided due to growth concerns. Our patient’s single kidney and frailty precluded neoadjuvant chemotherapy; upfront transhiatal esophagectomy achieved R0 resection despite pT3N1 staging. Postoperative infection resolved conservatively. Adjuvant therapy was omitted to minimize toxicity, with surveillance planned. This approach underscores individualized care in syndromic patients. A multidisciplinary approach and international registries, as established for other rare pediatric tumors, are essential to standardize care and improve understanding.

In conclusion, pediatric esophageal carcinoma remains a diagnostic rarity, particularly in syndromic adolescents. This report highlights the role of genetic mutations like USH2A in multisystem involvement and advocates for multidisciplinary, tailored management to optimize outcomes in such complex cases.
