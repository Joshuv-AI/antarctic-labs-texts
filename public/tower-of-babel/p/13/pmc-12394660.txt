Regulation of divergent epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition responses via the CDK4/6-USP51 pathway through ZEB1 protein stabilization
Authors: Yuta Sakamoto, Masataka Takahashi, Hajime Nishimura, Kazuhide Watanabe, Harukazu Suzuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a cellular process important for numerous developmental processes, wound healing and cancer progression. In the context of heterogenous nature of cancer, EMT occurs at the forefront of tumor invasion, although the precise molecular mechanisms governing its spatial dynamics remain unclear. This study aimed to explore the diverse responses to EMT in monolayer cell cultures of MCF10A epithelial cell with induction of Zinc-finger E-box binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1), an EMT-inducing transcription factor. ZEB1-induced MCF10A cells exhibited EMT in space- and cell density-dependent manner. The expression of ZEB1 was modulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. We found that CDK4/6 and USP51 enhanced ZEB1 protein stability via deubiquitination. Moreover, suppressing USP51 and CDK4/6 activity attenuated cell migration, a characteristic of the mesenchymal phenotype, in ZEB1-induced MCF10A cells. Finally, we demonstrated that CDK4/6 kinase activity is important for cell migration as well as stabilizing ZEB1 in the mesenchymal breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231. These insights could pave the way for developing more targeted and effective therapies targeting at ZEB1 and EMT in advanced cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17310-9.

Introduction

Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a cellular process, in which epithelial cells lose their properties and acquire mesenchymal traits, which enhance cell motility. EMT is a well-known driver of tumor progression that facilitates invasion and metastasis1,2. Typically, peripheral tumor cells are more prone to undergo EMT, whereas those located at the center tend to retain an epithelial phenotype3. The heterogeneous EMT response observed in clinical tumors can be replicated in two-dimensional culture systems. For instance, in mammary epithelial cell lines (such as MCF10A and primary human mammary epithelial cells), cells at the center of epithelial colonies exhibit greater resistance to spontaneous or TGF-β-induced EMT4. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these heterogeneous EMT responses are remain poorly understood.

Zinc-finger E-box binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1) is a key transcription factor in EMT, with its expression closely linked to the EMT phenotype5. ZEB1 functions though two distinct modes: it represses epithelial gene expression by recruiting co-repressors such as CtBP6, and also activates tumor-promoting genes by cooperating with JUN, an AP-1 component, and YAP, a Hippo pathway factor7. Moreover, ZEB1 is crucial for cancer progression, contributing to cell migration, stemness, and various cancer-related functions8. Elevated ZEB1 expression has been detected in cells at the invasive front of pancreatic tumors and lung adenocarcinoma9,10, indicating that it plays a role in the divergent EMT responses. Therefore, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms governing the expression of ZEB1, particularly within the spatial context of epithelial cells, remain crucial.

The transcriptional regulation of ZEB1 by pathways such as TGF-β and NF-κB is well-documented, and post-transcriptional regulation by miR-200 family and miR-551b pathways are well established11,12. However, the post-translational regulation of ZEB1 remains poorly understood. This study investigated the role of post-translational regulation of ZEB1 protein in the context-dependent regulation of EMT using two-dimensional epithelial cultures.

Results

ZEB1 expression is post-translationally regulated in a confluency-dependent manner

To investigate the impact of ZEB1 on EMT phenotypes in distinct regions of epithelial colonies, we established a MCF10A cell clone to stably and uniformly express ZEB1 upon doxycycline (DOX) stimulation. First, we investigated the effect of ZEB1 expression on EMT under dispersed (normal culture) and colony-forming (colony culture) conditions. Under normal culture conditions, ZEB1 induction resulted in a uniform transition to a mesenchymal-like morphology and an E-to-M switch in the EMT reporter (normal culture in Fig. 1A and B; see “EMT reporter system and Flow cytometry analysis of Materials and Methods” for details). Conversely, under colony culture conditions, ZEB1 induction led to a mesenchymal-like morphology and the expression of vimentin in cells in the outer, while inner maintained an epithelial-like appearance and E-cadherin expression. The result of flow cytometry revealed that ZEB1 induction elicited a heterogeneous response in the colonies, resulting in mixed epithelial and mesenchymal phenotypes (colony culture in Fig. 1A and B). We further examined the transcriptional changes in EMT marker genes in the inner and outer fractions of the colonies following DOX stimulation. The epithelial markers CDH1, CRB3, and EPCAM were predominantly expressed in the inner regions, whereas the mesenchymal markers CDH2, FN1, and VIM were predominantly expressed in outer regions (Fig. 1C). Notably, ZEB1 protein induction was rarely observed in inner cells exhibiting epithelial traits but was detected more frequently in outer cells (Fig. 1D). Additionally, genes directly influenced by ZEB1 were categorized as ZEB1/YAP/JUN-activated or ZEB1-suppressed7, with the former being upregulated in outer cells and the latter downregulated (Fig. 1E). In contrast, quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) results indicated that the exogenous ZEB1 mRNA levels tends to show only small differences between the inner and outer fractions (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these findings suggest that ZEB1 is post-translationally regulated in the inner and outer cells of epithelial colonies.

To understand the cellular contexts underlying the heterogeneous ZEB1 expression, we examined the impact of cellular density, which differs between the inner and outer regions of epithelial colonies. Real-time imaging analysis of exogenously expressed ZEB1 protein revealed that ZEB1 was detected and a mesenchymal-like morphology was induced under sparse condition, whereas ZEB1 protein levels were suppressed and epithelial-like morphology was retained in confluent conditions (Fig. 1G). Western blot analysis showed that ZEB1 protein expression correlated with cellular confluency, being higher under sparse conditions and lower under confluent conditions in ZEB1-induced MCF10A cells (Fig. 1H). Additionally, the expression levels of the EMT markers E-cadherin and vimentin reflected these changes (Fig. 1H). In contrast, RT-qPCR analysis revealed small differences in induced exogenous ZEB1 mRNA levels between the sparsely and confluently cultured cells (Fig. 1I). These findings indicated that ZEB1 expression is regulated at the protein level by cellular confluency.

ZEB1 is regulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway

We subsequently investigated how the ZEB1 protein is regulated by cell confluency at the molecular level. First, we analyzed the half-life of ZEB1 protein under sparse/confluent conditions following treatment with cycloheximide (CHX), a protein synthesis inhibitor. We found that the half-life of ZEB1 was significantly longer under sparse conditions (Fig. 2A). The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway plays a key role in regulating protein stability13. To determine whether this pathway is involved in regulating ZEB1 stability, we analyzed the impact of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 on ZEB1 protein levels under high cell confluence conditions. MG132 treatment led to a dose-dependent increase in ZEB1 protein expression, suggesting that ZEB1 undergoes rapid proteasome degradation in a high-cell-density environment (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, ubiquitinated ZEB1 protein levels were increased under confluent conditions compared to those under sparse cell density (Fig. 2C). These findings indicate that the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway actively promotes ZEB1 protein degradation in a high-cell-density environment.

CDK4/6 and USP51 enhance ZEB1 protein stabilization

Our findings indicate that ubiquitination plays a significant role in regulating ZEB1 protein expression. Previous studies have suggested that ZEB1 protein stability is modulated by deubiquitination through the CDK4/6-USP51 pathway in cancer cells14. Therefore, we hypothesized that this pathway might suppress ZEB1 ubiquitination under sparse conditions. To test this hypothesis, we characterized the relative activity of CDK4/6 in relation to cell density using western blot analysis (Fig. 3A). The phosphorylation level of retinoblastoma protein (RB) (S807/S811), an indicator of CDK4/6 activity, was significantly higher under sparse conditions. Thus, these data demonstrated that CDK4/6 activity is dependent on cell density, with increased activation occurring in a low-cell-density environment.

To investigate involvement of the deubiquitinase USP51 in ZEB1 protein stability, we established a conditional shRNA knockdown system for USP51 (Fig. 3B). USP51 knockdown resulted in decreased ZEB1 protein expression levels even under sparse conditions (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, USP51 depletion accelerated ZEB1 protein degradation even in a low-cell-density environment (Fig. 3D). The increase in ubiquitinated ZEB1 protein levels following USP51 suppression further supported the notion that USP51 is crucial for modulating ZEB1 protein stability through deubiquitination processes (Fig. 3E). Additionally, co-immunoprecipitation assays indicated a physical interaction between USP51 and ZEB1 under sparse conditions (Supplementary Fig. S1), suggesting that USP51 directly interacted with and deubiquitinated ZEB1 protein.

These findings were further corroborated by the observation that treatment with the highly selective CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib resulted in decreased ZEB1 protein expression under sparse conditions (Fig. 3F). Additionally, palbociclib treatment shortened the half-life of ZEB1 protein (Fig. 3G) and facilitated its ubiquitination (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these findings suggest that stabilization of the ZEB1 protein is regulated through the coordinated actions of the CDK4/6 kinase and the deubiquitinase USP51. Specifically, these data indicates that active CDK4/6 signaling under low-cell-density conditions promotes the deubiquitination and stabilization of ZEB1 protein by USP51. Conversely, inhibition of CDK4/6 activity leads to increased ubiquitination and the subsequent proteasomal degradation of ZEB1.

CDK4/6 and USP51 regulate EMT phenotypes in normal epithelial cells

To gain deeper mechanistic insights into the interplay between USP51 or CDK4/6 inhibition and the regulation of EMT phenotypes in ZEB1-induced MCF10A cells, we utilized real-time live-cell imaging using the IncuCyte system. This approach allowed us to observe and quantify the migratory behavior of ZEB1-induced MCF10A cells under various experimental conditions. Specifically, we investigated the effects of suppressing USP51 expression via shRNA-mediated knockdown or inhibiting CDK4/6 activity using the selective inhibitor palbociclib. A significant decrease in the migration speed of MCF10A cells was observed when either USP51 or CDK4/6 was inhibited even under sparse culture conditions (Fig. 4A and B, respectively). Perturbation of USP51 and CDK4/6 activity led to a marked increase in E-cadherin and an accompanying decrease in vimentin under DOX stimulation (Fig. 4C and D, respectively). These results indicate that the active CDK4/6-USP51 pathway promotes a more mesenchymal, migratory phenotype in MCF10A cells under low-cell-density conditions, whereas inhibiting this pathway suppresses EMT-related behaviors.

CDK4/6 enhances ZEB1 protein stability and cell migration in the mesenchymal breast cancer cell line

To further investigate the involvement of the CDK4/6 signaling pathway in regulating ZEB1 and associated EMT traits in cancer cells, we evaluated the effect of the selective CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib on EMT phenotypes in the aggressive breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231. MDA-MB-231 cells exhibit a highly mesenchymal phenotype and express elevated ZEB1 protein expression7. Palbociclib treatment reduced ZEB1 protein levels in a dose-dependent manner in MDA-MB-231. Consistent with reduction of ZEB1 protein, the phosphorylation of Rb protein was also decreased in a dose-dependent manner, while the overall expression of CDK4/6 remained unchanged (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, palbociclib treatment shortened the half-life of ZEB1 (Fig. 5B). Functionally, palbociclib-mediated inhibition of CDK4/6 activity decreased the healing rate of scratch wounds and the movement speed of MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 5C and D, respectively), both of which are characteristic of EMT-associated phenotypes. Collectively, these findings indicate the involvement of the CDK4/6 signaling pathway in maintaining EMT characteristics, potentially by modulating ZEB1 protein levels, in highly malignant cancer cells. These data suggests that the active CDK4/6 signaling may plays a crucial role in sustaining the mesenchymal phenotype and migratory behavior of aggressive cancer cells by stabilizing ZEB1.

Discussion

EMT plays important roles in a variety of developmental processes, wound healing and, cancer progression1. Although EMT phenotypes are divergent within a tumor mass or an epithelial colony, the molecular mechanisms underlying the spatial regulation of EMT are still not fully understood. This represents a fundamental challenge in current biology and is crucial for developing innovative, effective cancer therapies targeting EMT. Herein, our study sheds light on understanding the molecular mechanism of space- and cell density-dependent manner though regulating ZEB1 in mammary epithelial cells. ZEB1-induced MCF10A cells revealed EMT in space- and cell density-dependent manner where induced ZEB1 was less affected from the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway in outer fractions of the colonies and in the sparse condition. Moreover, we found that ZEB1 stability was enhanced by CDK4/6 and USP51 via deubiquitination. Furthermore, the blockade of USP51 and CDK4/6 activity suppressed cell migration, a feature of the mesenchymal phenotype, in ZEB1-induced MCF10A cells. Finally, CDK4/6 kinase activity was found to be important for ZEB1 protein stabilization and cell migration in the mesenchymal breast cancer cell line, MDA-MB-231.

Our findings suggest that, within the center of epithelial colonies, ZEB1 protein are maintained at low levels via ubiquitin-mediated degradation, which preserves the epithelial identity of inner cells. Conversely, this ubiquitination mechanism is less stringent in the cells located at the peripheral edges. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report the mechanisms by which ZEB1 is spatially regulated at the protein level. Given that ZEB1 is regulated by transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms in various cellular cues, including TGF-β and the miR-200 family, in several epithelial cells11,12, the mechanisms for the acquisition of EMT resistance and the maintenance of epithelial properties might be multiple steps of regulation of ZEB1 expression. We identified that the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway as a key factor in regulating ZEB1 expression. However, E3 ubiquitin ligases, responsible for degrading ZEB1 in MCF10A cells, remain unknown. Several studies have shown that E3 ubiquitin ligases SIAH1 and FBXO45 are involved in ZEB1 degradation in breast cancer cell lines15,16. Further investigation is needed to fully understand the precise mechanisms underlying ZEB1 ubiquitination in MCF10A cells.

CDK4/6 induces the post-translational phosphorylation of USP51 at serine 26 in breast cancer cells, significantly promoting to the acquisition of deubiquitinating activity of USP5114. In this context, our results confirmed that inhibiting CDK4/6 and USP51 decreased ZEB1 protein stability. However, the relationship between CDK4/6-USP51 and ZEB1 remains unclear. On one hand, it is possible that USP51 and CDK4/6 act independently to stabilize ZEB1 protein. Moreover, while this study primarily addresses the deubiquitination of ZEB1, it is noteworthy that other post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation and acetylation, have also been implicated in ZEB1 protein stabilization17,18. Future research should explore how these additional post-translational modifications contribute to ZEB1 stabilization.

The difference in cell density affects various signaling pathways19. It has been well established that CDK4/6 activity is suppressed in a high-cell-density environment, in which CDK4/6 inhibitory protein expression is increased20,21. Our studies also demonstrated that CDK4/6 activity decreased under confluent conditions compared to sparse conditions. Thus, the deubiquitination-mediated stabilization of ZEB1 via the CDK4/6-USP51 pathway are considered to be reduced in a high-cell-density environments. On the other hand, the result of western blotting showed that USP51 protein expression did not differ between sparse and confluent conditions. Given that CDK4/6 has a pivotal role in the activation of enzymatic function of USP5114, we envision that the enzymic activity of USP51, as well as CDK4/6 activity, is reduced in a high-cell-density environments.

Dysregulation of ubiquitination in cancer cells is critical for promoting cell survival22. Several studies have shown that USP51 is associated with chemoresistance, stemness, and poorer survival rates23,24. Given that USP51 plays a significant role in these processes, targeting this enzyme may affect cancer progression by modulating ZEB1. As a deubiquitinase, USP51 can potentially be inhibited by small molecules, providing a new therapeutic strategy to combat the poor prognosis and treatment resistance often observed in cancer. Moreover, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) exhibits a worse prognosis than other subtypes, primarily because of the lack of effective therapeutic agents to improve survival rates25. CDK4/6 inhibitors treat the therapy of estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer by primarily targeting the CDK4/6-Rb signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing tumor shrinkage26. We demonstrated that the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib significantly promoted ZEB1 degradation and suppressed cell migration in MDA-MB-231 cells, which are representative of the triple-negative subtype. Since TNBC with elevated ZEB1 expression is particularly aggressive, our findings suggest that CDK4/6 inhibitors may serve as promising therapeutic agents for managing TNBC and other highly malignant tumors.

While this study analyzed the spatial and cell density-dependent regulation of ZEB1 in EMT using 2D culture, we recognize its limitations in fully elucidating EMT dynamics under more physiological conditions. Therefore, with an eye toward clinical application, we envision that more physiological environment approaches utilizing 3D culture models (e.g., spheroids) and in vivo experiments will be indispensable in future studies.

In conclusion, our research indicates that ZEB1 is regulated at the protein level by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway in spatial- and cell density-dependent manner. Furthermore, we highlighted the critical roles of USP51 and CDK4/6 in maintaining the stability of ZEB1 through deubiquitination, which are illustrated in Fig. 6. These insights will enhance our understanding of divergent responses to EMT and contribute to the development of more effective cancer treatments that target both EMT and ZEB1.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

MCF10A and MDA-MB-231 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). MCF10A cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA) supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated horse serum (Invitrogen), EGF (20 ng/mL, PEPROTECH, Cranbury, USA), hydrocortisone (0.5 mg/mL, FUJIFILM Wako), insulin (0.023 IU/mL, FUJIFILM Wako), penicillin (100 IU/mL) and streptomycin (100 mg/mL, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), and cholera Toxin (100 ng/mL, FUJIFILM Wako). MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in DMEM + high glucose medium (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (SIGMA), and penicillin (100 IU/mL) and streptomycin (100 mg/mL). Cell culture was performed at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

Plasmid construction

pSLIK-hygro was obtained from Addgene (Massachusetts, USA). To generation of the Tet-on ZEB1 fusion protein expression system, full-length mouse ZEB1 cDNA was subcloned into the pENTRE vector, and a green fluorescent protein (GFP) or FLAG tag was added to the C-terminus of ZEB1 (to generate pENTRE ZEB1-GFP or ZEB1-FLAG-tag, respectively). Subsequently, the Gateway LR Clonase II Enzyme mix (Invitrogen) was used to transfer the ZEB1-GFP or ZEB1-FLAG from the pENTRE ZEB1-GFP or ZEB1-FLAG vector, respectively, into the pSLIK-hygro vector.

Lentivirus production and stable cell line generation.

Lentiviral particles were produced using an LV-MAX Lentiviral Production System (Gibco, Massachusetts, USA). The human HEK293F cell line was cultured in LV-MAX Production Medium (Gibco) at 37 °C with 8% CO2. The day prior to transfection, the HEK293F cells were seeded in a 250 mL flask to reach high confluence. The following day, ViraPower packaging mix (1.38 µg, Invitrogen) and the lentiviral vector plasmid (920 µg) were co-transfected into the HEK293F cells using the LV-MAX Transfection Reagent (Gibco), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 6 h post-transfection, the enhancer solution was added to the HEK293F cells. At 48 h post-transfection, the viral supernatant was collected.

To obtain stable tetracycline-inducible (Tet-on) ZEB1-GFP or ZEB1-FLAG fusion protein expressing MCF10A cells, the viral supernatant was added to MCF10A cells, followed by selection with hygromycin (FUJIFILM Wako) at 48 h post-transduction. Monoclonal cell lines were established using serial dilution cloning.

Colony production

Monoclonal Tet-on ZEB1-expressing MCF10A cells were cultured in cloning rings (Merck, Stockholm, Switzerland) placed at the center of 6-well plates. After 24 h, the cloning rings were removed, and the MCF10A culture medium was added to the plates. The cells were then treated with doxycycline (DOX, Takara) to induce ZEB1 expression. DOX was replenished every 48–72 h. Cell morphology and GFP fluorescence were measure using an IncuCyte live-cell imaging system (Sartorius Japan, Tokyo, Japan). After 5 days of DOX treatment, the inner and outer regions of the cell colonies were collected separately by scraping using a cell lifter.

EMT reporter system and flow cytometry analysis

The generation of EMT reporter-expressing MCF10A cells has been previously described27. The EMT reporter system utilizes the CDH1 (E-cadherin) promoter to drive eGFP expression and the VIM (vimentin) promoter to drive mCherry expression, enabling monitoring of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) status. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a BD FACSAria II instrument and FACS Diva 6.0 software. Dead cells and doublets were excluded from the analysis.

Western blot analysis

To prepare total cell lysates, cells were washed with (D)-PBS (Nacalai Tesque) and then lysed in RIPA buffer (Nacalai Tesque). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay (Takara Bio, Saga, Japan). Total cell lysates were subjected to 4–12% SDS-PAGE (Invitrogen) and transferred to a PVDF membrane using iBlot2 (Invitrogen). The membranes were blocked with Bullet Blocking One for Western Blotting (Nacalai Tesque) for 15 min at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies followed by incubation with HRP-linked secondary antibodies. Chemiluminescence signals were detected using ECL Prime Western blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva), and blot images were captured using SOLO.7 S.EDGE (M&S Instruments, Osaka, Japan). The following anti-bodies were used for protein detection. The following anti-bodies were used for protein detection: ZEB1 (Abcam, ab203829, 1:5,000), b-actin (Merck, A3864-200UL, 1:10,000), Rb (Abcam, ab32513, 1:5,000), Anti-phospho-Rb(S807/S811) (Cell Signaling Technology, D20B12, 1:5,000), CDK4 (Cell Signaling Technology, 12790 T, 1:5,000), CDK6 (Abcam, ab9337, 1:5,000), E-cadherin (BD Biosciences, 610182, 1:5,000), Vimentin (Cell Signaling Technology, D21H3, 1:5,000), USP51 (Atlas antibody, HPA001942-100UL, 1:5,000), Anti-Rabbit HRP-Linked Whole Ab Donkey (Cytiva, NA934V, 1:10,000), Anti-mouse HRP-Linked Whole Ab Donkey (Cytiva, NA931V, 1:10,000).

Small compound Inhibition of CDK4/6

To evaluate the impact of CDK4/6 inhibition, MCF10A cells were treated with the selective CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib (Selleck Chemicals, Houston, USA). Cells were seeded in 100 mm dishes and, after 24 h, were cultured in media supplemented with palbociclib for 48 h.

Cycloheximide (CHX) phase assay

ZEB1 stability was assessed using a CHX chase assay. MCF10A cells were treated with the protein synthesis inhibitor CHX (25 µg/mL; FUJIFILM Wako) and harvested at the indicated time points. MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 60 mm dishes. After 24 h of culture, MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with palbociclib (10 µM) and CHX (100 µg/mL) and were harvested at the indicated time points. ZEB1 protein levels were determined using western blot analysis. ZEB1 protein signals were normalized to the internal loading control, β-actin, to account for any differences in the samples. Moreover, this CHX chase assay enabled the quantification of the ZEB1 protein half-life under the given specified experimental conditions.

Inducible knockdown of USP51

To investigate the functional role of the deubiquitinase USP51, we employed an IPTG-inducible shRNA knockdown system. Lentiviral vectors containing IPTG-inducible shRNA targeting USP51 were purchased from Vector Builder Japan (Kanagawa, Japan) and used to generate stably transduced MCF10A cell lines. For USP51 knockdown, the MCF10A cells harboring the IPTG-inducible shRNA construct were cultured in the presence of 2 mM IPTG (Nacalai Tesque) for 96 h. The knockdown efficiency was subsequently evaluated using quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis. The sequences of the shRNA targeting USP51 and the non-targeting control shRNA (shCtrl) are listed below. Ctrl 5′-CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCGCTCGAGCGAGGGCGACTTAACCTTAGG-3′, USP51 5′-GCATCTGATATGGATCCATGCCTCGAGGCATGGATCCATATCAGATGC-3′.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the NucleoSpin RNA Plus kit (Takara Bio). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNase H Plus) (Takara Bio) on a thermal cycler. Gene expression was normalized to the endogenous controls GAPDH or ACTB using the ΔΔCt method. The RT-qPCR primers used are listed in Table 1.

Immunoprecipitation assay

Immunoprecipitation assays were performed using an immunoprecipitation kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). Briefly, total cell lysates were incubated with the appropriate antibodies (FLAG (Merck, F3165-1MG), Ubiquitin (Santa Cruz, sc-8017)) overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. The following day, Protein A/G Sepharose beads were added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 4 h at 4 °C. The beads were then washed, and the precipitated proteins were eluted by heating at 95 °C in NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cell tracing analysis

To analyze cell migration, the cells were transduced with a GFP-expressing lentiviruses. GFP-labeled cells were then seeded, and their movements were monitored and recorded for 48 h using the IncuCyte live-cell imaging system. The cell trajectories under each experimental condition were analyzed using the Fiji package TrackMate and the R package cellTrackR.

Wound healing assay

The impact of CDK4/6 inhibition on the migratory behavior of MDA-MB-231 cells was assessed using a wound healing assay. The cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with palbociclib (10 µM) for 48 h. Scratch wounds were created using a Wound Maker (ESSEN BIOSCIENCE), and wound closure was measured hourly over time using the IncuCyte live-cell imaging system with a 10× objective.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical environment (version 4.2.3). An unpaired two-tailed t‐test was used for quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR), cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay, wound healing assay and image quantification. These experiments were repeated at least three times. A Mann–Whitney U test was employed for cell tracking. The error bars represent mean ± standard deviation.
