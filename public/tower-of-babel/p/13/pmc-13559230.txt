Histological and transcriptomic changes associated with matrix metalloproteinase 9 upregulation in rat esophageal fistula
Authors: Lipeng Li, Yihao Sun, Xueyong Feng, Chusheng Huang, Yuxuan Wang, Guoyong Chen, Lei Xian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal fistula (EF) is a serious complication associated with high morbidity, yet the local tissue changes that drive persistent fistula formation and failed healing remain poorly understood. This study established a surgically induced rat model of EF and characterized its histological and transcriptomic features, with particular attention to molecules potentially involved in inflammatory remodeling.MethodsFifty male Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly assigned to an EF group or a sham-operated control group (25 per group). EF was induced by cervical esophageal incision followed by indwelling tube placement. Model establishment was confirmed by gross inspection and methylene blue leakage testing. Histopathological changes were evaluated using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and elastic van Gieson (EVG) staining, together with immunohistochemistry (IHC) for CD31, CD34, cytokeratin 5/6 (CK5/6), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9). Whole-transcriptome sequencing was performed on EF tissue and normal esophageal tissue from three rats per group, followed by differential expression and enrichment analyses. Selected candidate genes were further validated by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and western blotting.ResultsThe rat EF model was successfully established and showed full-thickness esophageal defects with persistent luminal communication. Histologically, EF tissue exhibited marked architectural disruption, dense inflammatory cell infiltration, muscular layer interruption, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and selective loss of elastic fibers. IHC demonstrated increased CD31- and CD34-positive vascular signals, expansion of CK5/6-positive basal-like epithelium, and accumulation of α-SMA-positive myofibroblasts in EF tissue. Transcriptomic profiling identified 1,550 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 1,128 upregulated and 422 downregulated genes. Enrichment analyses showed strong activation of immune-inflammatory and matrix-remodeling pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling, interleukin-17 (IL-17) signaling, nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, leukocyte transendothelial migration, and ECM-receptor interaction. RT-qPCR confirmed significant upregulation of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 6 (CXCL6) and MMP9 at the messenger RNA (mRNA) level in EF tissue, and increased MMP9 protein expression was further verified by western blotting and IHC.ConclusionsRat EF is characterized by persistent inflammatory activation, abnormal stromal remodeling, selective elastic fiber loss, and incomplete structural restoration. MMP9 is consistently upregulated in this pathological microenvironment and may be associated with impaired healing in EF. This model provides a practical platform for future mechanistic studies of EF.Keywords: Esophageal fistula (EF), rat model, transcriptomics, extracellular matrix remodeling (ECM remodeling), impaired healing

Highlight box

Key findings

• A surgically induced rat model of esophageal fistula (EF) showed persistent inflammatory activation, extracellular matrix remodeling, selective elastic fiber loss, and incomplete tissue restoration. Matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) was consistently upregulated and was validated at both the messenger RNA and protein levels.

What is known and what is new?

• EF is a serious complication with high morbidity, but the local tissue mechanisms underlying fistula persistence and failed healing remain poorly defined. Current management is still focused mainly on closure and infection control.

• This study provides a combined histologic and transcriptomic characterization of EF tissue and shows that the lesion represents an active nonhealing microenvironment rather than a simple structural defect.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings support a shift from viewing EF as a purely mechanical problem to studying it as a biologically dysregulated wound. Future work should focus on inflammatory-proteolytic pathways, particularly MMP9-related mechanisms, as potential targets for improving tissue repair.

Introduction

Esophageal fistula (EF) is defined as an abnormal communication between the esophagus and adjacent organs or tissues, including the trachea, pleural cavity, and pericardium, and represents a group of severe clinical conditions (1-4). EF may arise from diverse etiologies, including malignant tumors, radiotherapy, foreign body injury, caustic burns, postoperative complications, and infection. Once established, EF can lead to sepsis, malnutrition, and recurrent pulmonary infections, all of which adversely affect prognosis and quality of life. Although advances in endoscopic techniques, such as stenting, negative pressure drainage, and over-the-scope clipping, together with multidisciplinary perioperative management, have improved clinical care, EF remains associated with substantial mortality, and effective mechanism-based treatment strategies and early predictive biomarkers are still lacking (4,5).

Current management of EF still relies mainly on technical closure and control of local contamination. However, much less is known about the tissue-level events that determine whether esophageal injury resolves or progresses to a persistent fistulous tract. In particular, the roles of inflammation, proteolysis, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling in the fistula microenvironment have not been fully clarified. Evidence from other fistulizing disorders, especially Crohn’s disease, suggests that persistent inflammatory cell recruitment, defective epithelial restitution, and matrix degradation may act together to promote fistula formation and persistence, suggesting that related processes may also contribute to EF (6-8).

The ECM forms the structural scaffold of the esophageal wall and is critical for tissue integrity during injury and repair. In addition to collagen, elastic fibers help maintain wall compliance and recoil, so their loss may have important mechanical consequences. Selective elastic fiber injury may weaken the esophageal wall, alter local biomechanical properties, and make durable defect closure more difficult (9,10). Because clinical EF is etiologically heterogeneous and representative tissue samples are often difficult to obtain, a reproducible animal model provides a practical platform for examining these changes under controlled conditions. In the present study, we established a surgically induced rat model of EF and combined histological staining, quantitative immunohistochemistry (IHC), and whole-transcriptome sequencing to characterize the local pathological microenvironment. We also examined transcriptome-derived candidate molecules, with particular attention to C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 6 (CXCL6) and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9). Using this model, we aimed to characterize the histological and transcriptomic features of EF and to identify molecules potentially associated with impaired healing. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1026/rc).

Methods

Animals

A total of 50 healthy male Sprague-Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 200–250 g) were used in this study and randomly assigned to the model group (Group M, n=25) or control group (Group N, n=25). The rats were obtained from Guangdong Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Foshan, China) and housed in the Animal Laboratory Centre of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (12-h light/dark cycle, ad libitum access to standard chow and water prior to surgery). Established guidelines for the humane treatment and care of laboratory animals were strictly followed by our team. All procedures and housing conditions of the rats were in accordance with the prescribed standards of Guangxi Medical University. The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University [No. 2023-IACUC(001)], in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and the guidelines of the Ministry of Science and Technology, which emphasized the humane treatment of laboratory animals. A protocol was prepared before the study without registration.

Construction of the EF model and control procedures

Group M: Rats were placed in the supine position and anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol (400 mg/kg) (11). The neck hair was shaved. A 10 mL subcutaneous injection of 0.9% NaCl solution was administered to prevent postoperative dehydration. After a midline cervical skin incision was made, the tissues were dissected layer by layer until the esophagus was fully exposed. The anterior wall of the esophagus was then incised, and a plastic tube [polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) material, 4 cm in length, outer diameter 1.2 mm, inner diameter 0.8 mm] was inserted. The incision was subsequently closed in layers to secure the plastic tube in place. Group N: the trachea was exposed and the connective tissue surrounding the esophagus was dissected in the same manner, after which the incision was closed in layers without esophageal incision or tube placement.

On the seventh postoperative day, the indwelling plastic tube was removed. Methylene blue solution was slowly injected through a gastric tube, and the neck incision was observed for blue dye leakage. The appearance of blue dye at the cervical incision indicated successful establishment of a patent EF tract. After model evaluation, rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg). Death was confirmed by the absence of spontaneous respiration, heartbeat, pedal withdrawal reflex, and corneal reflex before tissue collection (12).

Sampling and histologic evaluation

After euthanasia was confirmed, rats in the M group underwent surgical resection of intact esophageal tissue 1 cm above and below the fistula center. Rats in the N group had normal esophageal tissue excised from the same regions, with blood flushed out using 0.9% saline. For histological evaluation, 5-µm sections were prepared from tissue surrounding the fistula center. Esophageal tissue from both groups underwent microscopic examination using standard hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining (13), Masson’s trichrome staining, and elastic van Gieson (EVG) staining. Overall morphology and staining patterns were assessed at 40× and 200× magnification.

RNA sequencing and functional analysis

Samples for transcriptomic analysis were collected from fistula tissues of three rats in the model group (Group M) and from normal esophageal tissues of three rats in the control group (Group N). Total RNA was extracted using the TM miRNA extraction kit (#TR205-200) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) of 6 or higher were used for library preparation. Total RNA was further purified with the RNAClean XP Kit (Cat# A63987, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and the RNase-Free DNase Set (Cat# 79254, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany).

Sequencing libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Ribo-off rRNA Depletion Kit (Human/Mouse/Rat; Vazyme, Nanjing, China, Cat# N406-02) and the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, Cat# NR604-02). Library size distribution was evaluated on an Agilent 4200 TapeStation and showed a peak size of approximately 380 bp. Library concentrations were quantified with a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer. Qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in paired-end 150-bp mode. Approximately 9.60–11.83 Gb of raw data were generated per sample, and all samples met the sequencing quality criterion of Q20 ≥90%.

Raw reads were processed using Seqtk to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases at the 3' end (Q<20), reads shorter than 25 bp, and rRNA reads. Clean reads were aligned to the rat reference genome (Rattus norvegicus, Rnor_6.0) using HISAT2 (version 2.0.4) with default parameters. Clean read ratios ranged from 89.04% to 92.97%, and overall mapping ratios ranged from 84.05% to 90.27%. Detailed RNA-seq sequencing quality, preprocessing, and mapping statistics for each sample are provided in Table S1. Gene expression levels were estimated using StringTie (version 1.3.0), and fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values were used for expression visualization. Differential expression analysis between Group M and Group N was performed with the edgeR package using trimmed mean of M values (TMM) normalization. Genes with a q value ≤0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) ≥2 were considered differentially expressed. The complete list of DEGs is provided in table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/jtd-2026-1026-1.xlsx. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were then performed for the DEGs. Given the limited sample size (n=3 per group), transcriptome-wide findings were considered exploratory and were further assessed by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) validation of selected candidate genes.

RT-qPCR

To validate the transcriptomic sequencing results, total RNA was isolated from approximately 50 mg of rat EF tissue or control esophageal tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with oligo(dT) primers.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). Each 20 µL reaction mixture contained 1X SYBR Green Master Mix, 10 µM of each forward and reverse primer, and 2 µL of diluted cDNA template. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 15 sec and 60 ℃ for 1 min. A melt curve analysis was conducted at the end of each run to confirm the specificity of the amplification reactions. All reactions were performed in triplicate for each biological sample.

The expression of the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene was used as an endogenous control to normalize the expression levels of the target genes. The relative messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels were calculated using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used for the target genes (CXCL6, MMP9, etc.) and the reference gene (GAPDH) are listed in Table S2.

IHC

Tissue processing and sectioning

Following surgical excision, esophageal tissue samples from both the model (Group M) and control (Group N) rats were fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections were cut at a thickness of 4 µm using a rotary microtome (RM2016, Leica, Germany) and mounted on adhesive glass slides.

Immunohistochemical staining

The immunohistochemical staining was performed following a standard protocol. In brief, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. For antigen retrieval, the slides were immersed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0, Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat# GA2307051) and heated using a microwave oven. Subsequently, endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide (Servicebio, Cat# G1204) for 25 minutes at room temperature. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Servicebio, Cat# GC305010) for 20 minutes to reduce non-specific binding.

The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the following primary antibodies diluted in PBS: rabbit anti-CD31 (1:1,000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK, Cat# ab182981), rabbit anti-CD34 (1:100, Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# AF1387), rabbit anti-cytokeratin 5/6 (CK5/6, 1:200, Huaan Biotechnology, Hangzhou, China, Cat# ER1901-03), rabbit anti-α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, 1:100, Affinity, Changzhou, China, Cat# AF1032), and rabbit anti-MMP9 (1:100, Huaan Biotechnology, Cat# ET1704-69). After washing, the sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:100, Servicebio, Cat# GB22303) for 30 minutes at 37 ℃.

Antigen visualization was achieved by treating the sections with a 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (ZLI-9018, Beijing Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction was monitored under a microscope and stopped by immersion in distilled water. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral balsam.

Image acquisition and quantitative analysis

Stained sections were examined and imaged using a BA400 digital microscope (Motic, Xiamen, China). For each sample, three non-overlapping fields of view were captured at 400× magnification. The digital images were then analyzed using the Halo image analysis platform (v101, Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM, USA) to quantify the percentage of DAB-positive area (% positive area), which represents the expression level of each target protein.

Western blot analysis of MMP9

For western blot analysis, esophageal tissues from three rats in Group M and three rats in Group N were collected for protein extraction. Total protein was extracted using lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, and protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with rabbit anti-MMP9 antibody (1:2,000, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA, Cat# 10375-2-AP) and rabbit anti-GAPDH antibody (1:200,000, Abclonal, Woburn, MA, USA, Cat# AC033) as the loading control. After washing, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and quantified by densitometric analysis. Relative MMP9 protein expression was normalized to GAPDH.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R software (version 4.2.0) were used for statistical analysis and data visualization. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Mann-Whitney U-test was used when appropriate for non-normally distributed data. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used for sample correlation analysis. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, ns indicates no significant difference, * indicates P<0.05, ** indicates P<0.01, and *** indicates P<0.001. Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated using the EnhancedVolcano and ComplexHeatmap packages in R (14), and scatterplots and principal component analysis plots were generated using ggplot2. Differential expression analysis for RNA-seq data was performed using the edgeR package with TMM normalization, as described in the “RNA sequencing and functional analysis” section.

Results

Establishment and macroscopic validation of the rat EF model

All 50 rats (25 per group) survived the surgical procedures without intra-operative or early postoperative mortality. In the model group (M), EF was created by cervical esophageal incision with indwelling tube placement, whereas sham-operated rats (N) underwent cervical exposure and closure without esophageal transection. On postoperative day 7, M rats exhibited neck swelling with localized ulceration and visible cutaneous openings, while N rats showed well-healed incisions without skin disruption (Figure 1A). Upon re-exploration, the esophageal wall in M rats showed full-thickness defects. Injection of methylene blue through a gastric tube resulted in blue dye extravasation from the cervical wound, confirming a patent communication between the esophageal lumen and the skin surface (Figure 1B).

Histopathological characterization of esophageal tissues

On H&E staining, control esophagi displayed an intact concentric architecture with preserved mucosa, submucosa and muscularis, and only minimal lymphocytic infiltration. In contrast, M rats showed discontinuity of the esophageal ring corresponding to the fistula tract, with marked disruption of the muscularis layer and dense inflammatory cell infiltration. At higher magnification, epithelial hyperplasia and inflammatory cell accumulation at the defect margins were evident (Figure 2A).

Masson’s trichrome staining in N rats revealed a continuous ring of blue-stained collagen in the submucosa and muscularis with preserved circumferential muscle bundles. In M rats, only sparse blue collagen fibers were observed around the fistula, accompanied by interruption of the muscularis (Figure 2B). EVG staining further refined these changes: in N rats, the submucosa contained regular, densely packed blue elastic fibers with abundant red-stained collagen in the muscularis, whereas in M rats, submucosal elastic fibers were markedly reduced, particularly in the fistula region, and muscle continuity was lost (Figure 2C). Together, these findings indicate prominent ECM remodeling with selective depletion of elastic fibers and structural failure of the esophageal wall in the fistula group.

Transcriptomic profiling of fistula versus normal esophageal tissue

To define the transcriptomic features of EF, RNA sequencing was performed on fistula tissue from three model rats and normal cervical esophageal tissue from three control rats. Using a cutoff of q value ≤0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥2, 1,550 DEGs were identified, including 1,128 upregulated and 422 downregulated genes in EF tissue (Figure 3A). A complete list of DEGs is provided in table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/jtd-2026-1026-1.xlsx. Heatmap analysis of the top 50 DEGs clearly separated fistula samples from normal controls, indicating extensive transcriptional remodeling in EF tissue (Figure 3B). Enrichment analysis showed that these DEGs were primarily associated with immune and inflammatory responses. GO terms were significantly enriched for cytokine production, immune response-regulating signaling, lymphocyte activation, adaptive immune response, leukocyte migration, leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, and regulation of inflammatory responses (Figure 3C). KEGG pathway analysis similarly revealed significant enrichment in cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling, cell adhesion molecules, phagosome, NOD-like receptor signaling, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling, interleukin-17 (IL-17) signaling, nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, leukocyte transendothelial migration, Toll-like receptor signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, and complement and coagulation cascades (Figure 3D). Overall, these data show that EF tissue is characterized by prominent immune and inflammatory activation, accompanied by increased leukocyte recruitment and ECM remodeling. Notably, several members of the MMP and CXCL families, including MMP8, MMP9, MMP12, MMP13, CXCL2, and CXCL6, were among the upregulated genes identified in EF tissue.

Immunohistochemical characterization of the fistula microenvironment

To further delineate the cellular composition of the fistula niche, we quantified key markers by IHC. CD31 and CD34 were used to label vascular endothelial and progenitor cells, CK5/6 to identify basal-like squamous epithelium, and α-SMA to detect activated myofibroblasts (Figure 4). Quantitative image analysis using HALO revealed that the percentage of CD31-positive area increased from 0.4882%±0.1719% in N rats to 9.4901%±1.8366% in M rats (P<0.001), and CD34-positive area increased from 4.7399%±1.5850% to 14.5720%±5.1159% (P<0.01), indicating robust neovascularization in fistula tissue. Similarly, CK5/6-positive epithelial area increased from 9.7131%±2.8638% in N rats to 20.2551%±3.1806% in M rats (P<0.001), consistent with marked expansion and activation of basal-like squamous epithelium at the fistula margin. α-SMA-positive area rose from 1.1723%±0.6599% to 8.5151%±2.0108% (P<0.001), reflecting accumulation of activated myofibroblasts around the tract (Table S3). Collectively, these changes depict a highly active microenvironment characterized by pathological angiogenesis, basal-like epithelial hyperplasia and fibro-myofibroblast activation in the EF group.

RT-qPCR validation of candidate chemokines and MMPs

Based on the RNA-seq results, we selected members of the CXCL chemokine family and MMP family for RT-qPCR validation in an independent cohort of animals. CXCL6 and MMP9 mRNA levels were significantly upregulated in EF tissues compared with normal esophagus (P<0.001), in line with the transcriptomic data, and showed the greatest fold-changes among the candidates tested (Figure 5). In contrast, MMP8, MMP12, MMP13 and CXCL2 exhibited expression patterns that were not fully concordant with RNA-seq, which may reflect the limited sample size and biological variability across animals.

Upregulation of MMP9 protein in EF tissue

To validate MMP9 expression at the protein level, western blotting and IHC were performed in EF and normal esophageal tissues. Western blot analysis showed that MMP9 protein levels were increased in fistula tissue relative to normal controls, and quantification of band intensity confirmed a significantly higher MMP9/GAPDH ratio in the fistula group (Figure 6A,6B, P<0.05). In parallel, immunohistochemical staining revealed weak MMP9 expression in normal esophageal tissue but markedly stronger staining in fistula tissue (Figure 6C). Consistently, semiquantitative analysis demonstrated significantly increased MMP9 immunoreactivity in the fistula group compared with the normal group (Figure 6D, P<0.001). Together, these results confirm that MMP9 is significantly upregulated at the protein level in EF tissue.

Discussion

EF remains a difficult clinical problem because persistent contamination, local infection, impaired oral intake, and malnutrition can all interfere with durable tissue healing. In practice, management often requires nutritional support, drainage, endoscopic closure or stenting, and, in selected cases, surgical repair or reconstruction (15-17). Even with these approaches, treatment can be prolonged and outcomes remain unsatisfactory in severe cases, especially when diagnosis is delayed or local sepsis has already developed (16,18). This clinical difficulty makes experimental models particularly valuable, as they provide an opportunity to examine the local tissue events that accompany persistent fistula formation and failed repair under controlled conditions.

Animal models relevant to EF differ substantially in purpose and experimental utility. Adriamycin-induced rat models have been useful for studying congenital esophageal atresia and tracheoesophageal fistula, but they mainly reproduce developmental foregut malformations rather than acquired post-injury, nonhealing fistulas (19). Magnetic compression models in rabbits and dogs can generate relatively standardized fistulas with less operative trauma, although they require specialized devices and a period for fistula maturation (20). Large-animal surgical models, including porcine fistula models, provide a translational platform for testing experimental therapies (21), but they are more resource-intensive and less practical for high-throughput tissue-based studies. In contrast, surgically induced small-animal models allow direct lesion creation, precise control over fistula location, and serial tissue harvest for histologic and molecular analyses. Within this context, our rat model, based on cervical esophageal incision with indwelling tube placement, offers a practical platform for investigating the pathological microenvironment of acquired EF. Its main advantages include technical simplicity, convenient tissue collection, and compatibility with histology, IHC, transcriptomic profiling, and future intervention-based studies. Nevertheless, it remains an acute experimental injury model and does not fully capture the biological and clinical heterogeneity of human EF.

Our findings suggest that EF tissue is more than a simple structural defect of the esophageal wall. Rather, it appears to represent a persistent nonhealing microenvironment characterized by inflammatory activation, stromal remodeling, and defective repair. Consistent with this impression, transcriptomic analysis showed enrichment of pathways related to cytokine production, leukocyte migration, chemokine signaling, TNF, IL-17, NF-κB, and Toll-like receptor signaling, together with ECM-associated programs such as ECM-receptor interaction and complement/coagulation cascades. These molecular changes were accompanied by histopathologic evidence of active remodeling, including increased CD31/CD34-positive vascular signals, expansion of CK5/6-positive basal-like epithelium, and accumulation of α-SMA-positive myofibroblasts in fistula tissue (22). This pattern is consistent with a chronic injury state in which inflammation persists while tissue restoration remains incomplete, a pattern also seen in other nonhealing wounds and fistulizing disorders (23-25).

Another notable histologic feature of our model was the concurrent loss of elastic fibers, α-SMA-positive stromal activation, and expansion of CK5/6-positive basal-like epithelium around the fistula tract. Such changes argue against orderly tissue repair. Myofibroblasts are central to wound contraction and matrix remodeling, whereas defective re-epithelialization is a hallmark of chronic nonhealing wounds (25,26). Elastic fibers are likewise important for tissue recoil and mechanical stability, yet their regeneration after injury is limited (10). Together, these observations suggest that EF persistence reflects a maladaptive repair state marked by ongoing matrix disruption and incomplete epithelial restitution, rather than a purely structural defect (24).

Within this broader setting of inflammation and matrix remodeling, CXCL6 and MMP9 emerged as notable candidates, although the level of supporting evidence differed between them. CXCL6, a neutrophil-associated chemokine, is biologically relevant in EF because it may contribute to inflammatory cell recruitment and amplification of local inflammatory responses. In our study, CXCL6 was consistently upregulated at the transcript level in both RNA-seq and RT-qPCR analyses. However, unlike MMP9, CXCL6 could not be reliably validated at the protein level. Two different commercial antibodies were tested, but neither produced reproducible or interpretable western blot or immunohistochemical results in rat esophageal tissue. This discrepancy may reflect low local protein abundance, rapid secretion and diffusion, post-transcriptional regulation, epitope masking, or limited antibody compatibility with rat tissue under our experimental conditions.

MMP9 was one of the most compelling molecules identified in this study. As a gelatinase with broad substrate specificity, MMP9 contributes not only to ECM turnover, but also to the regulation of cytokine and chemokine activity, placing it at the intersection of tissue remodeling and inflammation (27,28). In normal repair, transient MMP9 activity may support cell migration and matrix reorganization. When expression is sustained or excessive, however, MMP9 has more often been linked to pathological proteolysis, basement membrane disruption, and impaired healing in chronic wounds (27,29,30). Liu et al. reported that elevated wound-fluid MMP9 levels, as well as higher MMP9-to-TIMP-1 ratios, were associated with poor healing outcomes (30). Experimental work has likewise suggested that excess MMP9 can delay wound closure by disturbing basement membrane integrity and interfering with re-epithelialization (31).

Our data are consistent with this interpretation. In EF tissue, MMP9 was consistently upregulated at the transcript level and was further confirmed at the protein level by both western blotting and IHC. Its increased expression fits the broader tissue phenotype observed in our model, namely persistent inflammation, stromal activation, ECM remodeling, and incomplete structural restoration. MMP9 is also capable of contributing to elastin degradation under pathological conditions, which makes its upregulation particularly noteworthy in light of the selective loss of elastic fibers around the fistula tract (32). That said, our data remain associative rather than definitive. We did not measure MMP9 enzymatic activity, identify its cellular source, or test whether its inhibition could alter fistula persistence or matrix injury. Thus, the present findings identify MMP9 as a candidate molecule associated with the proteolytic, nonhealing microenvironment of EF, while its precise functional role remains to be determined.

The 7-day observation window should therefore be interpreted as an early stage of fistula formation rather than a complete representation of chronic fistula persistence. At this time point, the histological and transcriptomic findings are likely to reflect acute-to-subacute inflammatory activation, early ECM disruption, angiogenic responses, and initial stromal remodeling around the newly established fistula tract. In clinical settings, however, esophageal and tracheoesophageal fistulas often require prolonged multidisciplinary management and may evolve as complex nonhealing defects, with persistent contamination, local inflammation, tissue fibrosis, epithelialization of the tract, and delayed closure or long-term persistence (4,6,8,20,21). Therefore, the day-7 transcriptomic profile in the present model should be viewed as an early inflammatory-proteolytic signature and may not fully capture the later biological programs that determine durable healing or chronic fistula failure. Future studies using serial time points are needed to distinguish early inflammatory events from subsequent fibrotic remodeling and to determine whether MMP9 remains persistently upregulated during fistula maturation or nonhealing.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this model represents an acute surgically induced injury in otherwise healthy rats and cannot fully reproduce the etiological heterogeneity or chronic course of human EF. Second, tissue analysis was performed at a single postoperative time point; therefore, the dynamic transition from early inflammation and matrix degradation to later fibrotic remodeling, tract maturation, and fistula closure or persistence could not be evaluated. Third, the transcriptomic analysis was based on a limited sample size and should be considered exploratory. In addition, although MMP9 was validated at both the mRNA and protein levels, its enzymatic activity, cellular source, and functional role were not assessed. CXCL6 was upregulated at the transcript level, but protein-level validation was unsuccessful despite testing two commercial antibodies. Future studies should include serial time-point analyses and functional experiments to clarify the roles of CXCL6- and MMP9-related inflammatory-proteolytic pathways in EF persistence.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we established a surgically induced rat model of EF and found that EF tissue is characterized by persistent inflammatory activation, abnormal stromal remodeling, selective elastic fiber loss, and incomplete structural restoration. Transcriptomic profiling revealed broad immune- and ECM-related alterations and identified CXCL6 and MMP9 as notable dysregulated candidates. Among these, MMP9 was further validated at the protein level by western blotting and IHC. Overall, our findings suggest that EF represents a nonhealing pathological microenvironment rather than a simple structural defect, and support MMP9 as a candidate molecule associated with this process. This model provides a practical experimental platform for future studies of impaired healing and related mechanisms in EF.
