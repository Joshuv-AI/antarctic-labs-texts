JDS Communications H5N1 special issue: The importance of collaboration
Authors: Jessica AA McArt
Published: 2024

In early 2024, I was on sabbatical in Melbourne, Australia. I had heard murmurings of a strange disease affecting dairy cows in Texas, but that was far from my mind and geographic location. Then, on March 25, I received 2 emails in short succession—1 from the New York State Department of Agriculture and Markets sent to United States Department of Agriculture–accredited veterinarians, and 1 from the American Association of Bovine Practitioners—reporting that highly pathogenic avian influenza, specifically H5N1, had been confirmed in the recent disease outbreak in dairy cows in Texas and Kansas. That wasn't on my Bingo card.

I am thrilled to introduce our first-ever JDS Communications special issue on a hot topic: Highly pathogenic avian influenza, H5N1, in dairy cattle. This small issue includes an editorial from a veterinarian intimately involved in the initial stages of the outbreak and 3 invited articles addressing current knowledge on the virus' host range, the epidemiological and clinical aspects of the disease in dairy cattle, and its effects on dairy products. These articles provide real-time knowledge of what is known at the time of acceptance (August 2024). I would like to thank the authors for their willingness to share their experiences and current knowledge, and the reviewers who quickly provided their valuable comments.

These articles clearly show the importance of collaboration and information transfer during epidemics. During this outbreak and in the time that has followed, as we continue to learn about H5N1 in dairy cows, the impact on other species (including humans), and how best to reduce its spread, I have spoken with several scientific, veterinary, and governmental colleagues and now reflect on a perceived lack of information sharing between groups. This hesitancy to share data negatively affects us all, from the dairy producer and their veterinarian trying to prevent disease spread to cows and farm employees to the dairy scientist trying to establish disease physiology, and the virologist assessing host preference to the consumer wondering whether their milk is safe to drink. Unfortunately, this reluctance to disseminate information is not an isolated event.

As a dairy scientist and bovine veterinarian, I often wear multiple hats, depending on the situation. Interestingly, when I attend the American Dairy Science Association Annual Meeting, I find myself devaluing my experience as a bovine practitioner because clinical experience is often downplayed by scientists. Conversely, when I attend the American Association of Bovine Practitioners' Annual Conference, I sometimes find myself categorized as a “clipboard vet” because I spend less time doing clinical work than full-time practitioners, given my research program. Veterinarians are trained in anatomy, physiology, virology, bacteriology, clinical disease, and many other medical specialties. We have an intimate knowledge of disease processes and understand how these diseases present clinically. Likewise, dairy scientists are trained in physiology, nutrition, statistics, study design, and many other scientific areas. We understand the scientific process, can design hypothesis-driven studies to answer specific questions, and interpret data within known limitations. Although it is human nature to put things into familiar boxes, it is the communication among boxes that often drives knowledge forward, especially in unknown situations. This is the reason why we collaborate on grants—the combined knowledge of experts exceeds their individual sum.

The importance of collaboration will only increase in consequence as time proceeds. As dairy scientists, it is imperative that we have a seat at the table and welcome others to the conversation. It is in this spirit that we publish this hot topic special issue.

Cheers,
