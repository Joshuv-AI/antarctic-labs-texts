Social anxiety and the positive valence system: A scoping review
Authors: Franklin Schneier, Isaac Deitz-Green, Arsen Seitov, Liza Handler, Margaret Satchwell, Charles T Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial anxiety disorder (SAD) is defined by negative valence symptoms, such as fear and anxiety related to perceived social threats, however, a growing literature has also identified positive valence system (PVS) impairments. PVS deficits in approach motivation, positive affect, and reward sensitivity may contribute to limitations in efficacy of existing treatments that primarily target negative valence system features. This scoping review describes the literature assessing PVS-targeted treatments of SAD and associations of PVS measures with social anxiety (SA) or SAD across units of analysis, from self-report to neuroimaging. Following screening, 192 peer-reviewed articles were included. Findings were most robust for association of SA and SAD with lower self-reported positive affect and reward sensitivity. Studies utilizing reward tasks reported some divergence of outcomes, with SA and SAD being associated with greater behavioral and striatal reactivity to social and monetary rewards in youth, but decreased reactivity specific to social rewards in adults. Some studies of reward learning found an association of SAD and SA with deficits in learning from and recalling social rewards, though neural outcomes were mixed. Most studies of novel PVS-focused treatments have used nonsocial rewards to motivate behavioral change, with fewer studies exploring direct enhancement of PVS functions. Inconsistencies in findings may relate to small sample sizes and heterogeneous methods. Further clarification of behavioral and neural mechanisms of PVS deficits is needed, but existing evidence supports increased development and testing of treatments to leverage and enhance PVS function in SAD.Keywords: Social anxiety disorder, Social anxiety, Positive valence system, Reward, Motivation, Research Domain Criteria, Positive affect

Introduction

Social anxiety disorder (SAD)

Social anxiety disorder (SAD; also known as social phobia) is one of the most common psychiatric disorders, with a 5–13% lifetime prevalence and high chronicity and impairment [1]. SAD is defined clinically by features centered around negative affect in relation to threat: excessive fear or anxiety about social situations in which one might be scrutinized, fear of negative evaluation of behavior or of displayed symptoms of anxiety, and intense fear and/or avoidance of social situations [2].

First-line psychotherapeutic and pharmacological treatments for SAD prioritize targeting these symptoms of fear, anxiety, and avoidance. Outcome assessments in clinical trials for SAD commonly utilize measures, such as the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale, that explicitly assess fear/anxiety and avoidance related to social and performance situations [3]. Cognitive behavioral therapy for SAD was designed to diminish negative thoughts using cognitive procedures and to reduce avoidance and anxiety symptoms through behavioral exposures that engage habituation or extinction processes. Serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) have been shown to induce similar improvements in anxiety and avoidance via impacts on fear neurocircuitry, such as down-regulation of amygdala reactivity [4]. Although these treatments have demonstrated efficacy for reduction of SAD symptoms, they fail to benefit a substantial subset of patients and are only partially effective for most others, with remission rates under 50% [5], [6].

Positive and negative valence systems in SAD

Anxiety in SAD typically occurs during approach-avoidance conflict, in which the individual both desires and fears social engagement. Although negative affective features of fear and avoidance are defining elements of SAD diagnostic criteria, absent from these criteria is impairment in positive affective features such as motivation for, approach toward, and enjoyment of social situations. Impaired social motivation is more commonly associated with depression or schizoid personality, yet some individuals with social anxiety (SA) also report low desire and pleasure related to social interactions [7]. Early research into affect in anxiety disorders identified SAD as the anxiety disorder with greatest deficits in positive affect (PA) [8]. Primary deficits in pleasure or motivation might contribute to SA and SAD (SA/SAD) by interfering with engagement of effective social behaviors (e.g., social approach and responsiveness) [9], [10]. Alternatively, motivational deficits might follow perceived negative social experiences [11]. One possible contributor to limitations of first-line SAD treatments is that these treatments do not adequately impact these deficits, as CBT does not primarily focus on enhancing motivation or pleasure, and SSRIs have been observed to sometimes reduce motivation [12]. Despite the importance of approach motivation and behavior to a full understanding of SAD, these features have been infrequently assessed and targeted by treatments.

Distinct affective and brain systems underlie functions related to approach motivation vs. fear and avoidance [13], [14]. These are termed the positive valence system (PVS) and negative valence system (NVS), respectively, within the National Institute of Mental Health’s Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework [15], which aims to stimulate translational research on mechanisms of psychiatric disorders by describing domains that exemplify types of neurobehavioral functioning. Within each domain, different psychological/biological dimensions that span a full range of functioning, from healthy to pathological variants, can be described across a range of units of analysis, from genes to self-reports. The PVS domain in RDoC includes three dimensional constructs: 1) Reward responsiveness, which includes reward anticipation, initial response to reward, and reward satiation. 2) Reward learning, composed of probabilistic and reinforcement learning, reward prediction error, and habit. New learning occurs as expectations are modified by experiences (prediction errors), but this process may be impaired, for example, by inaccurate perceptions of outcomes of social experiences. 3) Reward valuation, which includes reward probability, delay, and effort.

Previous reviews have examined the relationship of neural circuits overall to anxiety disorders [16], and the relationship of the PVS to mood and anxiety disorders generally [17]. Other recent reviews noted that deficits in reward processing across depression, anxiety and trauma predict poor long term outcomes [18], and across anxiety disorders, psychotherapies improved negative affect (g= −0.90; 95%CI [-1.19, −0.61]) to a greater extent than they improved PA (g = 0.27; 95%CI [0.05, 0.59]), Z = -5.26, p < .001) [19]. A review of PVS function in youth anxiety found that behaviorally inhibited youth had elevated neural reactivity to reward anticipation, and youth with SAD had blunted PA [20]. An early meta-analysis by Kashdan [21] of SA studies with self-report measures found negative correlations of SA with PA (K= 19, N = 2976, r = -.36, P < .001) and curiosity (K= 15, N = 2091, r = -.24, P < .001). There have been no comprehensive reviews, however, of the literature assessing PVS-targeted treatments of SAD or associations of PVS measures with SA/SAD across the lifespan using the full range of units of analysis.

Current study

This review aims to assess the status of current human research literature on the relationship of the PVS to the development, severity, maintenance, and treatment of trait SA and SAD. A scoping review structure was chosen because existing research on the PVS and SA is limited and utilizes heterogeneous measures and paradigms. This review aims to map approaches and findings in this area, consider clinical implications, and highlight areas in which more research is most needed.

Methods

Overview

This protocol followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines and was registered with the Open Science Framework on May 3, 2025 [22].

Article selection

Screening procedures

Scientific literature was screened for papers that included predictive measures, interventions or outcome measures related to the PVS in persons with SAD or elevated levels of SA, or in association with SA levels. The search included the following terms, intended to cast a wide net for papers with findings specific to the PVS: ("Social Anxiety" OR "Social Phobia" OR "Social Anxiety Disorder") AND ("Positive Valence" OR "Reward*" OR "Probabilistic Learning" OR "Reinforcement Learning" OR "Habit" OR "Delay" OR "Effort" OR "Motivat*" OR "Behavioral Activat*" OR "Positive Affect" OR "Positive Emotion" OR “Sociability” OR "Social Connect*" OR “Extraversion”). This query was run on PubMed, PsycNET, and Cochrane Library. On PubMed, the following filter was used: “English.” On PsycNET, the filter “Peer Reviewed Journals” was used. On the Cochrane Register of Controlled Trials, no additional relevant filters were available. The search was run in May 2025 and pulled 724 articles from PubMed, 197 articles from PsycNET and 207 articles from the Cochrane Register of Controlled Trials.

Search results were screened as follows: 1) Four authors reviewed several articles, and then the full research team discussed findings and discrepancies to sharpen inclusion criteria. 2) Two authors then independently screened all titles and abstracts and discussed discrepancies. 3) For all articles preliminarily accepted at screening by at least one author, two authors (F.S. and C.T.) then independently reviewed titles and abstracts, and when necessary full papers, to generate lists of included papers. For all articles preliminarily accepted by neither author at screening, one additional author re-screened abstracts, and for articles deemed possibly eligible at re-screening, two authors (F.S. and C.T.) then independently reviewed articles for inclusion. 4) The full research team then met to resolve disagreements or discrepancies. When review of a paper revealed other possibly-relevant papers, these were screened using the above process. The online tool Rayyan [23] was used to deduplicate articles, document individual screening decisions while keeping reviewers blind to others’ assessments, and compare inclusion/exclusion judgments. Inclusion criteria and boundary issues

Inclusion was based on the following criteria: Articles must have 1) examined individuals with SAD or elevated SA, or analyzed SA severity; 2) included a PVS-specific outcome or an intervention directed at the PVS; 3) analyzed a PVS-specific outcome or a PVS-directed intervention in relation to SA/SAD; or analyzed a PVS-specific outcome in relation to treatment of SA/SAD; 4) been an empirical study published in a journal after peer review; and 5) had full text available in English.

In reviewing articles for inclusion, several issues required consideration in respect to defining which measures should be conceptualized as assessing the PVS. One issue is the interface of the PVS with another RDoC domain, Systems for Social Processes, and in particular its construct of Affiliation and Attachment, which RDoC notes to be moderated by social information processing and social motivation. We included papers with assessments of PVS constructs related to reward and motivation in social contexts, but not those with social assessments that did not specifically assess PVS-related constructs (e.g., for studies of extraversion, this led to inclusion of studies analyzing its PVS-related facets of positive emotion and excitement-seeking, but not studies whose analyses pooled all facets of extraversion, which includes four facets that are not explicitly reward-related).

Additional areas of discrimination included literature on fear of positive evaluation [25]. We did not include studies limited to this construct because its focus on fear responses makes it most relevant to NVS function rather than PVS-related constructs. Other papers were not included because rather than analyzing a PVS construct they reported only a PVS-NVS difference measure (e.g., magnitude of response to reward – magnitude of response to punishment) in relationship to SA/SAD, such that the specific association with the PVS could not be disentangled from association with the NVS. Other papers were not included because they did not analyze PVS results in relation to SA/SAD, but only in relation to a larger heterogeneous sample.

Data extraction

Two authors independently charted data from every article using a data extraction template adapted from Cochrane [26]. The research team then discussed the extracted data and resolved inconsistencies. Data was extracted on article characteristics (e.g., year of publication), sample characteristics (e.g., demographics), study design characteristics (e.g., observational, randomized controlled trial, etc.), methods of diagnosing SAD and/or assessing SA severity, methods of assessing the PVS, intervention type if present, and key findings. Tables summarizing extracted characteristics of each article by level of analysis are in Appendix A. Supplementary material.

We considered organizing studies primarily by RDoC subdomain (reward anticipation, initial response to reward, etc.), however, many of the extracted studies did not fit neatly into RDoC constructs. Many studies did not utilize measures that are specified in RDoC, and many of the utilized measures could be argued to relate to multiple RDoC constructs. We therefore chose to maximize reliability of the organizational structure, by organizing the overall grouping of studies by level of analysis and methods, but within each group to identify subgroups of studies (e.g., studies assessing reward anticipation with the monetary incentive delay task) that particularly matched an RDoC subconstruct and to interpret these separately.

Results

Overview

192 publications met inclusion and exclusion criteria for this review. See Fig. 2 for number of publications by year of publication.

Articles by year of publication.

One hundred and thirty-one publications studied adults, 73 undergraduates, and 22 children or adolescents. PVS constructs were measured by self-report in 129 studies, by behavioral performance in 32, and by neural or genetic assessments in 34 (some publications included multiple samples and levels of analysis). PVS-targeted treatments were studied in 16 publications, and PVS-related outcomes of other treatments were studied in 11. Below we describe the literature organized by level of analysis of PVS constructs.

Association of SA and SAD with positive valence measures, by level of analysis

Self-report measures

The most-commonly studied PVS feature has been PA, assessed by the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) [27] and other self-report measures. The PANAS is listed in RDoC as a self-report state measure of the Reward Responsiveness subconstruct Initial Response to Reward; however, reviewed studies included those assessing PA as a state or trait. Low PA was among the first PVS deficits to be reported in SA/SAD, pre-dating the development of RDoC [see Kashdan [21] for review]. Of the 67 publications identified in this search as assessing PA, 28 utilized the PANAS. Twenty-five of the PA studies compared persons with diagnosed SAD to a healthy control group, six compared samples selected for high SA and low SA, and 36 reported within-group analyses of a sample that was not selected for SA/SAD. The vast majority of these studies found SA, high SA, or SAD to be associated with lower PA.

SA and SAD were found to be associated with blunted trait and momentary PA in general and with PA in response to a variety of specific rewarding stimuli, including receipt of favors [28], interactions with acquaintances [29], pleasant social and nonsocial stimuli [30], positive social feedback [31], and overinclusion in a cyberball paradigm (implying social acceptance) [32]. SA/SAD was also associated with lower affective empathy for positive emotions [33], and with greater dampening and reduced savoring of PA [e.g., [34]]. However, SA may also amplify PA related to certain interpersonal events that are experienced as highly positive [29], [35]. While the PANAS has been noted to primarily measure “activated PA,” associated with drive to seek reward, PA states related to relaxation or contentment may be more decreased in SAD [30]. Other self-reported constructs related to diminished PA and associated with SA/SAD include greater anhedonia and lower levels of trait positive emotionality, positivity, happiness, flow, connectedness, pleasure, and pleasantness.

After PA, the next most-common PVS-related construct assessed by self-report was reward sensitivity or dependence, the tendency to be responsive to rewards. Our search identified 24 studies best categorized as assessing trait reward sensitivity or dependence, nine of which used the Behavioral Activation System Scale (BAS) of the Behavioral Inhibition System/Behavioral Activation System Scales [36]. The BAS was designed to assess reward motivation, with three subscales assessing Reward Responsiveness, Drive, and Fun-Seeking. RDoC lists the Drive subscale of the BAS as a self-report measure of the Reward Valuation subconstruct of Effort, but most BAS studies do not report subscale scores, and the relationship of the BAS total score or other reward sensitivity self-report measures to specific RDoC subconstructs has not been well-established, although the “fun seeking” subscale of the BAS suggests overlap with novelty seeking measures considered below. Another common measure of self-reported reward sensitivity was the Sensitivity to Reward subscale of the Sensitivity to Punishment and Sensitivity to Reward Questionnaire [37], used in five studies. Of these 24 studies, 17 reported reward dependence to be negatively associated with SA or SAD, with mixed results in the other seven. Two studies that separately assessed associations with social interaction anxiety and social performance anxiety found a significant negative association only with the former. [38], [39].

Twenty-five publications reported findings for self-reported novelty seeking and related features (e.g., risk taking, novelty seeking, curiosity, monotony avoidance, and the excitement seeking facet of extraversion). Mood enhancement motives for alcohol or cannabis use were assessed in 11 studies, of which only four found these motives to be positively associated with SA/SAD. SA/SAD was negatively associated with novelty seeking in seven studies, and five reported mixed results. Some studies, however, suggest that a subgroup of individuals with high SA or SAD evidence the reverse association, with increased reward sensitivity, risk-taking and substance use problems [e.g., [40]]. Other self-report measures included assessment of cognitive processes related to PVS constructs, such as predictions of positive social events, and positive reactions to and interpretations of positive social events, with most finding negative associations with SA/SAD across nine reports [e.g., [41], [42]].

Behavioral measures

We identified fewer studies (k = 32) that utilized tasks to objectively assess behavioral manifestations of PVS function and their relationship to SA. Nineteen behavioral reports included adults, 11 included undergraduates, and three included children or adolescents. Seven of these behavioral studies examined the relationship of SA/SAD to biased memory for and interpretation of positively valenced social stimuli and feedback, of which three reported SA/SAD to be associated with impairment in these domains. For example, after giving a speech, SAD and high SA groups evidenced greater deterioration of their memory for positive feedback over time [43], [44], [45]. Learning and memory processes are related to elements of the RDoC constructs of Reward Learning and Reward Responsiveness.

Some behavioral tasks have been designed to interrogate specific RDoC subconstructs within the PVS system. For example, a variety of probabilistic reward learning/decision-making tasks, have assessed reward anticipation, receipt, prediction error, and/or reward learning using monetary or social rewards. Four of these studies that utilized social rewards, such as viewing positive social feedback (i.e., happy faces) or being favored in a cyberball game, found mixed results for behavioral responses. Six behavioral task studies that utilized monetary rewards had largely negative results. Other behavioral tasks assessed attention to and processing of positively-valenced social stimuli, with mixed results.

Other studies with behavioral task outcomes included four utilizing a non-social delay discounting task to assess the RDoC subconstruct Delay, within the construct of Reward Valuation. Two of these studies found high SA and SAD to have a greater preference for immediate rewards than control groups [46], [47], and two studies found largely no differences in impulsive decision-making [48], [49]. Another behavioral study addressed the RDoC subconstruct Effort, within the construct of Reward Valuation, finding in adolescents that greater SA was associated with decreased effort to obtain social feedback from high status peers, a risk-averse pattern [50].

Neural measures

Most neural assessments of the PVS in relation to SA/SAD have utilized functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI, k = 16) or electroencephalography with evoked response potentials (EEG/ERP, k = 11), often in conjunction with behavioral tasks designed to interrogate elements of the PVS. Of these studies, 21 included adults, 12 included children or adolescents, and two included undergraduates.

fMRI

Among fMRI studies, five utilized a monetary incentive delay task, in which timely response time to a target triggers reward, to assess the RDoC subconstruct of Reward Anticipation. RDoC recognizes eight brain regions engaged with reward responsiveness, primarily involving mesolimbic dopamine system projections from ventral tegmentum to striatum and cortical regions. In three of these monetary incentive delay task studies with samples aged 20 or younger, SA was associated with greater striatal activation to monetary reward anticipation [51], [52], [53], but this finding was absent in two studies of adults [54], [55].

The two fMRI studies using the monetary incentive delay task in adults also included a social incentive delay task that utilized social stimuli (e.g., positive faces) instead of monetary rewards. These studies found decreased nucleus accumbens activation to anticipation of positive social stimuli in participants with SAD. Other fMRI studies have reported that among adults with SAD, striatal response is also blunted to social feedback [56] and specifically to social reward under conditions of being observed [57]. However, striatal activation was increased in adolescents/young adults (ages 16–25) with greater SA who experienced greater relatedness during a social task [58]. Another study, of responses to unexpected social reward from high value peers, found that heightened striatal activity and negative frontal-striatal functional connectivity occurred in socially anxious youth (ages 8–17) but not adults [59], yet in a study by the same research team using a different paradigm, neural response to unpredicted positive evaluations was decreased in youth (ages 8–18) [60]. fMRI studies of resting state functional connectivity have reported greater variability in functional connectivity of reward regions in SAD [61], and decreased striato-frontal connectivity [62].

Overall, this fMRI literature suggests that SA/SAD is associated with altered reward processing in the mesolimbic dopamine pathway, with blunting of responses to social reward but not monetary reward in most studies of adults, but normal or increased sensitivity to both nonsocial and social rewards in children, adolescents, and young adults. There is substantial variability in results, however, across different samples and experimental paradigms.

Evoked response potential (ERP)

As in the fMRI literature, ERP studies have focused on anticipation of and responses to either monetary or social rewards in a variety of tasks, and monetary reward task studies have reported SA and SAD to be associated with elevated neural responses primarily in children and adolescents. Three monetary reward studies found that adolescents (age 12–18) with greater SA had greater activation during reward anticipation (stimulus preceding negativity) and during reward processing (feedback related negativity) [63], and children (aged 8–10) had enhanced activation (feedback related negativity) following receipt of positive feedback of monetary reward [64]. However, SA was not associated with ERP response to reward in another study of adolescents (aged 16–21) [65]. Three studies of adults did not find differences in ERP responses to monetary reward, except under the condition of being observed [66], [67], [68].

ERP studies assessing the relationship of SA/SAD to social rewards have had mixed results. SAD was associated with blunting of P2 following overinclusion in cyberball in adults [32], but several other studies in children (ages 16–21) [65] and in adults [28], [67], [69] reported no significant findings for SA/SAD association with ERP responses to social reward.

Other neural measures

Single-photon emission tomography (SPECT) and positron emission tomography (PET) neuroreceptor imaging studies of striatal dopamine receptors that are critical for reward processes have reported mixed results, with findings of low striatal D2 receptor availability in SAD [70] and in SAD comorbid with obsessive-compulsive disorder [71], but not replicated in a larger SAD sample [72]. There have been negative findings for dopamine release differences [72], and dopamine transporter binding in reward regions has been found to be both decreased [73] and elevated in SAD [74]. Also, using neuromelanin-sensitive MRI, decreased signal intensity measuring dopamine in the midbrain was recently associated with severity of SA in adolescents [75]. Among genomic studies, a small case-control study did not find evidence for dopamine system genes to be associated with SAD [76].

Treatment studies

Non-PVS-targeted treatments with PV assessments

This review identified only 11 studies that reported the association of any PVS features or outcomes with an established non-PVS-targeted treatment for SAD. Although SSRI treatment has been critiqued for its possible negative impact on motivation [12], the one SSRI trial that reported a PVS-related outcome found significant increases in PA after open label SSRI treatment [77]. Among PET and SPECT neuroreceptor imaging studies, two found increases in striatal dopamine transporter binding post-SSRI and also post-CBT, with or without placebo [78], [79]. Changes in dopamine transporter binding were not associated with degree of clinical improvement on SSRI alone [78] or for CBT+SSRI [79], but they were associated with change for CBT + placebo [79]. Another study found that striatal dopamine transporter binding decreased during “overt” SSRI treatment but increased in a group that was similarly treated but told they were getting placebo, suggesting that expectancy effects alone may modulate the dopaminergic reward system [80].

Several randomized controlled trials have suggested that CBT can impact self-report and behavioral PVS-related outcomes in SAD. Two studies reported increased PA [81] and positive emotions [82] post-treatment, and increases in positive empathy mediated improvement in CBT but not in mindfulness-based stress reduction [83]. Behavioral therapies led to increased reward dependence (but not novelty seeking) assessed with the Tridimensional Personality Questionnaire, though change did not correlate with improvement in SA [84]. Additionally, attention bias to happy faces and positive words was reported to increase more post-CBT relative to waitlist, and these changes correlated with improvement in symptoms [85].

PVS-targeted treatments or interventions

Several interventions have leveraged preserved PVS sensitivity to non-social rewards in adults with SA/SAD to motivate behavioral change. The single most-studied approach has been gaze-contingent music reward therapy, a computer-based training that uses rewarding music, triggered by visual attention assessed via eye tracking, to reinforce training of attention away from social threat stimuli and toward neutral stimuli. In randomized controlled trials it was found superior to a placebo form of attention training [86] and equivalent to SSRI treatment for improving SAD symptoms [87]. Most recently, however, another gaze-contingent music reward therapy randomized controlled trial reported that training attention between non-social, non-valenced stimuli led to equivalent improvement as training away from social threat, suggesting that enhancement of attention control in general may be the key mechanism, rather than shifting of attention specifically away from social threat [88]. Other approaches include use of monetary reward to reinforce attention bias modification away from threat with a dot probe task [89], and the addition of a values-focus in the selection of exposure targets in a single session of behavior therapy [90]. Both approaches enhanced symptom-based outcomes in randomized controlled trials.

Other behavioral interventions have been designed to directly enhance PVS function related to social cues or behaviors, such as by training attention toward positive social stimuli, training approach behaviors, or assigning other social behaviors that engage the PVS. Computer-based approach training, randomized to either positive or neutral pictures, improved social engagement and motivation in high SA students [91], but in a subsequent study of SAD patients, social approach, connectedness, social anxiety and anhedonia improved equally in active and control conditions [92]. In a high SA sample, instruction to engage in kind acts increased PA and relationship satisfaction while decreasing social avoidance, relative to behavioral experiments or monitoring conditions [93]. In two studies of unselected samples, training of attention toward positive social-evaluative words was less effective in participants with greater SA [94], but SA and depression symptoms improved after a one-session intervention that encouraged seeking, savoring, and attending to PA [95].

Medications that specifically target mesolimbic dopaminergic reward circuitry have not yet been studied as treatments for SAD, but some medications that have appeared efficacious for SAD in clinical trials, such as monoamine oxidase inhibitors and ketamine, are known to increase dopaminergic transmission and reward function as one of their multiple neural effects [96], [97]. Oxytocin has been observed to enhance the “liking” of social rewards [98], and in one SAD study it enhanced motivation for social rewards, though only among participants with lower anxiety [99]. In another study, oxytocin was administered prior to CBT sessions, and it enhanced positive self-evaluations of performance but did not improve SAD symptoms overall [100].

Discussion

This review identified a growing body of research into the association of self-report, behavioral, and neural PVS measures in human subjects with SA/SAD. A smaller literature has assessed PVS outcomes of established treatments for SAD, and tested treatments that more-directly target PVS deficits in this population. Overall, these studies suggest that SA and SAD are associated with differences (and most commonly decreases) in engagement of the PVS, across units of analysis. These findings have been demonstrated across samples with SAD and elevated SA, and in unselected samples, supporting the RDoC conceptualization of these constructs existing on a continuum through healthy and clinical populations.

Studies have consistently found SA and SAD to be associated with lower self-reported state and trait positive affect (PA), measured using the PANAS and a variety of other assessments, as noted in prior meta-analyses [21]. These decreases are reported across children, adolescents, and adults within a variety of contexts, are specific to SAD relative to other anxiety disorders, and are not fully accounted for by levels of depression. SA and SAD are further associated with decreases in self-reported anticipation of PA and savoring of PA, and these findings of deficits in PA processing over time are supported by behavioral studies showing impairment in recollection of positive feedback. Low PA has been established to be present in response to social rewards, but also to nonsocial rewards in some studies. Despite these deficits, individuals with SAD may also experience more intense PA in response to meaningful social encounters [29], [35]. RDoC includes PA as a measure of “initial response to reward,” however, anticipatory PA may relate to the RDoC subdomain of “reward anticipation,” and this large literature using assessments of PA as both state and trait suggests that low PA is also a more pervasive phenomenon of hedonic tone, which is not currently recognized in RDoC.

For the RDoC construct of reward anticipation, a more complex relationship with SA/SAD is emerging from behavioral, fMRI, and ERP measures of behavioral reward tasks that have been developed to specifically assess this domain. Many studies using objective outcomes of behavioral tasks have reported SA- and SAD-related decreases in reward anticipation (and initial response to reward), but other patterns have emerged particularly for neural outcomes related to monetary vs. social rewards, and pediatric vs. adult samples. Notably, in children and adolescents, striatal activation to reward anticipation was increased in association with SA/SAD in four fMRI studies of monetary rewards, two ERP studies of monetary rewards and other fMRI studies using social rewards. In adults, however, fMRI studies utilizing both monetary and social rewards have reported SA/SAD to be associated with no differences in relation to monetary rewards but decreased striatal activation in anticipation of social rewards, and several ERP studies have also reported decreased reward processing in anticipation of social rewards. The reduced likelihood of predicting rewarding events in SA/SAD [41], [42] also appears limited to social events [101].

The overall trend for reward anticipation in SAD and high SA adult samples suggests normal anticipation of monetary reward but decreased activation in anticipation of social rewards. This difference could relate to a variety of influences, including selective devaluation of social rewards or even paradoxical aversion and engagement of the NVS to social cues like happy faces that are conventionally considered rewarding. In regard to the differences in PVS function in children versus adults with SA/SAD, Richey and colleagues [11] have proposed a compelling developmental theory, which proposes that children with SAD evidence social sensitivity to reward in late childhood/early adolescence. A subset then transitions to social-motivational deficits during adolescence, a critical period of normative increased sensitivity to social context. Chronic social stress from aversive social interactions then leads to social anhedonia in later adolescence and adulthood.

Reward learning has similarly been assessed with a variety of learning tasks designed to assess this RDoC domain using behavioral and neural outcomes. Here, the results have been less consistent, with various studies finding SA/SAD to be associated with deficits, increases, or no differences in reward learning measures across behavioral, fMRI, and ERP measures. These differences may be due in part to substantial heterogeneity in employed tasks and analysis approaches. However, differences may also relate to sample age, as discussed above, with younger SA/SAD groups tending to show heightened PVS activity that is more commonly found to be diminished in adult samples. Deficits in learning from social rewards in adults with SA/SAD may be related to deficits in attention to social reward stimuli, interpretation biases, and diminishing recall of positive events over time, though paradigms assessing attention, interpretation, and recall of rewarding stimuli are not currently specified within the RDoC PVS domain. Overall, these findings support the importance of differences in reward processing as an element of PVS dysfunction in SAD.

Self-report assessments related to the RDoC domain of reward sensitivity show SA and SAD to be associated with deficits in reward motivation. A limitation of most existing self-report measures, however, is that they do not clearly distinguish RDoC subdomains such as reward probability and effort. Only a few studies have begun to assess such domains with behaviorally specific tasks, such as delay discounting or effort tasks. Approach-avoidance tasks are a promising behavioral approach to reward motivation that relates to the experience of conflicting motivations in SA/SAD; however, their ecological relevance for understanding how people navigate real world social contexts remains to be established. This limitation extends to any behavioral paradigm based upon responding to passive social cues that may not generalize to the dynamic transactional contexts of daily life [102].

At the molecular and genomic levels of analysis, measures of striatal dopamine and related genes in mostly small samples have produced mixed results, though with some evidence of decreased dopamine transmission in brain reward circuits.

The increasing evidence for PVS deficits has clinical implications for use of established treatments and development of novel treatments for SAD. While SSRI and CBT treatments have middling efficacy and are not designed to directly address PVS deficits, their limitations have motivated research into PVS function in SA/SAD. Few studies have directly assessed the impact of SSRIs and CBT on PVS-related outcomes, but existing studies suggest that both SSRIs and CBT may improve PVS deficits, if not to the same extent as they improve NVS symptoms [19]. Novel treatments that target the PVS, however, hold promise for further enhancing clinical outcomes. Two general categories of PVS-related behavioral approaches that have been studied to date are: 1) treatments that harness the PVS to reward and motivate change in behaviors that are not PVS-related, such as attention bias to social threat, and 2) treatments that aim to directly enhance PVS function. There are promising results for treatment in the first category, and very preliminary findings in SA/SAD for treatments aiming to directly enhance PVS function. Given the growing evidence base for PVS dysfunction in SA/SAD, the time is ripe for expanding the development of PVS-focused behavioral treatments and for further exploration of medications and other treatments that might enhance brain reward system function.

Limitations of our approach

Although the PVS focus of this review necessarily limited inclusion to studies that isolated PVS outcomes, a more complete understanding of the impacts of PVS function will require assessing dynamic interactions with the NVS. For example, the impact of PA on social connectedness and on response to social stressors has been shown to be attenuated at higher levels of anxiety or negative affect [103], [104]. Low approach behavior toward a potentially rewarding stimulus (e.g., happy face) may be mediated by both low valuation of anticipated reward and by elevated fear of potential negative evaluation, even though the stimulus is conventionally positive. Even explicitly positive evaluations may activate the NVS in SAD (e.g., evoking anticipation of increased expectations that will ultimately lead to negative evaluations), as has been described in the literature on fears of positive evaluation in SAD [25].

This review’s focus on the PVS also resulted in exclusion of studies that did not distinguish reward-related social functions from Systems for Social Processes -- another RDoC domain. However, interactions of social-specific functions with the PVS are surely important for fully understanding PVS function in SA/SAD, especially given the evidence from some studies that social anhedonia can develop in the presence of preserved hedonic function for non-social rewards.

Similarly, while the relationship of SA/SAD to the PVS has been shown to differ from that of other anxiety disorders and to persist after controlling for depression, it was beyond the scope of this paper to include studies that assessed PVS function across disorders. PVS function is a transdiagnostic phenomenon, however, and PVS function and treatment across disorders also have relevance for SAD. And while preclinical studies have influenced the development of PVS constructs, we have limited this review to human studies.

This review considered studies by level of analysis (e.g. self-report, behavioral, neural), assessment method (e.g. social or nonsocial rewarding stimuli, in the context of a task), and sample characteristics (e.g. adult or youth), aiming to identify groups of studies that clearly shared methods without being too small to support meaningful conclusions. Nevertheless, it is possible that heterogeneity of methods within groups of studies obscured findings that might be specific to more-narrowly defined subgroups. For example, the term 'social reward' has been used in the literature and in this review to refer to a variety of social stimuli, ranging from cues that represent the real or implied presence of other people (e.g., pictures of smiling faces, positive interpersonal feedback) to behavioral exchanges with other people (e.g., positively communicating with others). Although sharing positive valence, these different kinds of social stimuli may elicit somewhat different neuropsychological processes. Additionally, while the review has focused on PVS associations with SA/SAD as a whole, subgroups within SAD have been addressed by a few studies. For example, evidence suggests a SAD subgroup with increased reward sensitivity, risk-taking and substance use problems [e.g, [40]], and SAD limited to performance fears may have different associations with the PVS [38], [39].

Future directions

Several findings here suggest directions for future research. First, the substantial body of evidence for PVS deficits associated with SA/SAD contrasts with the limited literature on the impact of existing treatments on PVS function and on development of novel treatments that engage the PVS. Treatment research in SAD should routinely incorporate PVS measures as predictors and outcomes, to enhance understanding of the breadth of treatment effects. Simple measures such as self-report of PA should be a feature of any clinical trial in SAD, and more sophisticated behavioral or neural assessments of reward-related attention, memory, anticipation should be considered for inclusion as potential moderators and mediators of outcome in PVS-directed and non-PVS-directed treatments alike. Findings of preserved responsivity to nonsocial rewards in adults with SA/SAD support ongoing research into treatments that leverage responsivity to nonsocial rewards to motivate behavioral change in SAD. Behavioral and pharmacological interventions that aim to directly enhance PVS function in SAD have been little-studied but deserve further research attention.

Findings here also extend earlier observations that heightened reward sensitivity associated with SA/SAD in childhood and adolescence may be a precursor to the decreased reactivity to social rewards that is seen in adults with SAD. Longitudinal research is needed to shed more light on mechanisms of this progression from reward sensitivity to blunting. These findings also highlight the need to respect differences in childhood and adult SAD that may require different treatment approaches. Research should investigate whether age-related or individual differences in responsivity to social reward could predict treatment responses to conventional or PVS-focused interventions in SAD.

While a preponderance of evidence supports the existence of PVS dysfunction in SA/SAD, this review also identified substantial variability in outcomes across individual studies. One source of this variability may be the marked heterogeneity in methods for assessing PVS constructs, especially in regard to behavioral and neural measures utilizing tasks to engage the PVS. As discussed above, specific positively valenced stimuli presented in particular contexts may elicit specific neurobehavioral processes. Existing tools for measuring PVS-related constructs in humans also vary widely in how specifically they may relate to RDoC subdomains. Many of the constructs and assessment methods utilized in this literature either predate the development of RDoC or are grounded in other descriptive models. This review identified a need for novel measures and tasks that more specifically interrogate particular PVS subdomains, especially in regard to self-report measures that might more-specifically probe subdomains of reward anticipation, initial response to reward, reward satiation, and reward learning and valuation. Behavioral tasks targeting RDoC subdomains of reward delay and efforts have begun to be employed, but only by a few studies. Conversely, behavioral studies have identified deficits in attention to reward stimuli, interpretation biases, and diminishing recall of positive events over time, features that will deserve consideration in future revisions to RDoC PVS subdomains.

As research establishing the relationship of the PVS with SA/SAD matures, it will be important to better understand the influence of other relevant domains of function, such as systems for social processes and the NVS, as well as other relevant sample characteristics, such as comorbidity, fears of positive evaluation, and sociocultural factors. The distinction between preserved responses to nonsocial rewards and impaired responses to social reward in adult SA/SAD highlights the likely importance of social processes in development of this gap. And despite evidence for distinct neurobehavioral systems governing the PVS and NVS, interactions between these systems surely contribute to symptoms and functioning in SAD, as demonstrated in studies utilizing complex tasks that engage both systems in decisions involving reward vs. punishment or approach vs. avoidance.

Finally, while there is clinical value in understanding associations of the PVS with a specific mental disorder, a more complete understanding of the clinical impact of PVS function will need to utilize transdiagnostic samples and clinical characteristics beyond SA/SAD.
