Organ-Specific Migration License (OSML) theory: a novel paradigm for spatiotemporal regulation and intervention of cross-organ immune cell migration in tumor immune responses
Authors: Jiaming Cui, Jingru Han, Yang Dai, Shengqiang Li, Xinyu Wang, Ting Ge, Guixin He, Juyue Zhou, Li Yang, Yuanyuan Tian, Yingying Xie, Lefan Liu, Jinghui Du, Wentao Li, Jianchun Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe efficacy of antitumor immune responses depends not only on the activation and effector function of local immune cells but also on the dynamic interplay between the tumor microenvironment and systemic immunity. Emerging evidence indicates that the migration of immune cells from tumor sites to secondary lymphoid organs and the circulatory system is essential for translating localized immune activity into systemic protective responses. This migratory process displays marked organ-specific spatial heterogeneity and temporal variability, which complicate the prediction and modulation of systemic outcomes through local therapeutic strategies.We propose the concept of Organ-Specific Migration Licensing (OSML) to delineate the mechanisms and biological underpinnings that govern immune cell trafficking in tumor immunity. This framework integrates spatial heterogeneity, temporal dynamics, and intervention-induced reprogramming to clarify how organ-specific factors shape immune efficacy and resistance. Crucially, these three dimensions are not independent but intricately coupled, forming a dynamic regulatory system that determines whether immune cells receive “migration permission”. The OSML paradigm advances tumor immunotherapy from a focus on local modulation to one of systemic coordination, offering a conceptual and practical foundation for optimizing organ-targeted combination therapies.Keywords: Tumor immunity, Organ-Specific Migration Licensing, Spatial heterogeneity, Temporal dynamics, Immune cell migration, Microenvironment, Systemic immunity

Introduction

Tumor immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors and adoptive cell therapies, has achieved substantial progress across multiple cancer types. However, overall response rates remain limited due to the complexity and heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [1, 2]. The TME, comprising malignant cells, immune and stromal cells, vascular structures, and soluble factors, dynamically governs both local and systemic immune responses [1, 3]. Emerging evidence indicates that immune cell migration from tumor sites to secondary lymphoid organs and circulation is critical for converting local immune activation into systemic antitumor effects [4, 5]. Effective tumor elimination thus requires coordinated spatiotemporal migration of immune cells across distinct organs and microenvironments [4, 5].

Current research has largely focused on immune dynamics within the local TME, lacking a unified framework to explain cross-organ and spatiotemporal migration [6, 7]. In reality, immune cell migration is finely regulated not only by chemokines and cytokines but also by organ-specific microenvironments—including vascular architecture, extracellular matrix, metabolic conditions, and neuroimmune interactions—that collectively determine whether cells receive “migration permission” to exit primary sites, enter circulatory pathways, and reach target organs [5, 8]. Organ-specific microenvironments thus emerge as pivotal determinants of migratory capacity, immunotherapy resistance, and therapeutic variability. For instance, the lung, liver, and brain selectively modulate immune cell recruitment and homing through distinct chemokine networks, adhesion molecules, and metabolic cues [4, 8, 9].

Against this background, we propose the Organ-Specific Migration Licensing (OSML) theory, which systematically delineates the spatial heterogeneity, temporal dynamics, and intervention-induced reprogramming of immune cell migration across organs (Fig. 1). The OSML framework integrates these three dimensions to govern immune cell trafficking. Crucially, these three dimensions are not independent but intricately coupled: spatial heterogeneity establishes the baseline “license” threshold unique to each organ; temporal dynamics reflect how this threshold evolves during disease progression or therapy; and intervention-induced reprogramming represents the active manipulation of these thresholds to restore or enhance immune surveillance. Together, they form a dynamic and integrated regulatory system that governs cross-organ immune cell trafficking. By integrating multi-dimensional technologies—such as single-cell omics, spatial transcriptomics, and in vivo imaging—this framework aims to elucidate how organ-specific factors shape immune cell trafficking and therapeutic efficacy [10, 11]. The OSML framework offers a new theoretical foundation and mechanistic understanding of how organ-specific factors shape immune cell trafficking and therapeutic efficacy. This paradigm provides a basis for developing precision immunotherapy strategies that extend beyond local immune activation toward systemic coordination, with the aim of achieving broader and more durable clinical benefit.

OSML theory: a three-dimensional integrated framework for permitting immune cell cross-organ migration

Regulation of immune cell migration permission by organ-specific microenvironments

Defining migration license and its regulatory dimensions

A precise definition of “migration license” is required to distinguish it from conventional concepts of immune cell recruitment. In standard immunology, immune cell recruitment refers to the process by which cells are directed to a site of inflammation or tumor via chemokine gradients and adhesion molecules [12, 13]. Recruitment is often viewed as a binary event: a cell either migrates along the gradient to reach the target tissue or it does not [14, 15].

In contrast, “migration license” as proposed in the OSML framework is a multilayered regulatory concept that determines not only whether a cell can enter a given organ, but also whether it can persist, function, and exit. A license is granted only when three conditions are met: (1) physical permissiveness—the vascular and stromal architecture must allow transmigration; (2) chemical permissiveness—the local chemokine and cytokine milieu must provide directional cues without trapping or exhausting the cell; and (3) functional permissiveness—the metabolic and immunosuppressive environment must support rather than suppress effector functions.

Thus, while recruitment is a component of migration license, the license concept encompasses a broader, dynamic regulation that integrates spatial barriers, temporal changes, and functional outcomes. This distinction is critical for understanding why immune cells may successfully infiltrate a tumor yet fail to exert antitumor effects—they may possess “entry license” but lack “function license” [16, 17]. The OSML framework aims to capture this multilayered regulation and provide a roadmap for interventions that restore full migratory and functional capacity.

The OSML framework posits that immune cell migration permission is determined by the interplay of three coupled dimensions. The spatial dimension, which is the focus of this section, establishes organ-specific baseline thresholds through distinct microenvironmental components, vascular structures, and immunosuppressive landscapes. These spatial blueprints are not static; they evolve over time under the influence of tumor progression and therapeutic interventions. Understanding how spatial and temporal factors converge is essential for designing effective reprogramming strategies. This tripartite coupling distinguishes the OSML framework from static, single-factor models of immune recruitment.

Expression of microenvironment components and migration permission molecules

Organ-specific microenvironments critically determine the cross-organ migratory capacity of immune cells through variations in stromal, endothelial, and immunosuppressive cells. Within the TME, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) secrete chemokines such as CXCL12 and CCL21, regulating T-cell and dendritic-cell migration [18, 19]. For example, using a murine pancreatic cancer model (KPC mice), Feig et al. demonstrated that CAF-derived CXCL12 directly restricts T-cell infiltration, and treatment with the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 significantly increased CD8 + T-cell accumulation within tumors, leading to reduced tumor growth [20]. Endothelial cells modulate transendothelial migration by altering adhesion molecules like ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 [21, 22]. Studies using the light conversion probe mEos4b and CRISPR/Cas9 demonstrated that endothelial cells actively induce T cell adhesion and transendothelial migration by forming “hot spots” for ICAM-1 heterogeneous expression; therefore, blocking ICAM-1 with monoclonal antibodies can disrupt this migration pattern and reduce T cell infiltration [23]. In multiple myeloma, stromal remodeling disrupts adhesion molecule expression, facilitating tumor dissemination and immune evasion [24].

Key chemotactic molecules exhibit pronounced organ-specific heterogeneity. The CXCL12–CXCR4 axis, for instance, can form a “chemokine barrier” that restricts effector T-cell infiltration into tumors [25, 26]. Carter et al. found by single cell and spatial analysis that CXCL12+ myofibroblasts interact with CXCR4+ lymphocytes to mediate T cell rejection in fibrolamellar carcinoma; CXCR4 inhibition is sufficient to mobilize stromal T cells into tumor parenchyma, and its combination with PD-1 blockade further synergistically enhances antitumor cytotoxicity [27]. CCL21 in lymphoid organs and tumors directs dendritic-cell and T-cell homing [28]. In a murine model utilizing C57BL inbred mice bearing pancreatic cancer allograft tumors, it was demonstrated that CCL21 overexpression induced recruitment of naive T cells and dendritic cells, leading to tumor growth suppression [29]. Endothelial adhesion molecules determine immune cell extravasation, and their expression is regulated by inflammatory cytokines, tumor-derived factors, and metabolic conditions [21, 22, 30, 31]. Immunosuppressive cells, such as tumor-associated macrophages secreting TGF-β, further reinforce barriers to effector T-cell migration [32, 33]. Thus, the dynamic composition of the microenvironment and spatiotemporal regulation of migration-permitting molecules jointly govern interorgan immune cell trafficking.

Spatial distribution of local immunosuppressive factors

Immunosuppressive factors such as TGF-β and IL-10 exhibit marked spatial heterogeneity across tumors and host organs, profoundly influencing immune cell migration. TGF-β is highly expressed in glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer, and cervical cancer, with localized overexpression promoting metabolic reprogramming and immune suppression that limit effector T-cell infiltration [34]. Mucciolo et al. used pancreatic carcinoid and mouse models to reveal that TGF-β activates EGFR/ERBB2 signaling through induction of autocrine amphiregulin, drives myofibroblast cancer-associated fibroblast heterogeneity and promotes its metastatic function [35]. In cervical cancer metastasis, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses reveal that TGF-β signaling drives epithelial–mesenchymal transition and cooperates with neutrophils and CAFs to create immunosuppressive niches that impair immune cell migration [36].

IL-10 also shows spatial heterogeneity; in breast cancer, Factor H–induced macrophages expressing high IL-10 and low HLA-DR co-localize with tumor cells, reducing T-cell activation [37]. Peura et al. used a primary patient-derived graft culture model (PDEC) to show that soft stroma in the tumor microenvironment drives tumor cell autosecretion of IL-10 and induces macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype by activating the COX-FGF2 signaling pathway. These IL-10-high M2-like macrophages co-localize with tumor cells in local tumor regions and lead to a decrease in CD8 + T cells, thereby promoting local immune tolerance [38]. CAF-secreted IL-6 and TGF-β establish localized high-concentration zones that hinder dendritic-cell and T-cell migration [39, 40].

Spatial heterogeneity further manifests as regional variation in immune infiltration. In pancreatic cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma, immunosuppressive cells (M2 macrophages, Tregs, MDSCs) accumulate in tumor cores, forming “immune cold zones,” while effector CD8+ T cells and cDC1 dendritic cells are confined to the periphery [41, 42]. In a study of human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), spatial resolution using quantitative multiplex immunofluorescence revealed that M2 polarized macrophages were enriched within the tumor and that their spatial proximity to tumor cells was significantly associated with poor patient outcomes [43]. Additionally, spatial variability in the tumor-associated microbiome creates metabolic gradients that modulate immunosuppressive factor distribution [44]. Together, these spatially heterogeneous factors create organ-specific “migration-permission” barriers.

Organ-specific vascular and lymphatic structures

Liver, lung, and brain possess distinct vascular and lymphatic architectures that regulate immune cell migration. Hepatic vessels and lymphatics influence migratory routes; tissue-clearing and 3D imaging have enabled detailed visualization of their remodeling within the TME [45, 46]. In lungs, inflammation induces lymphatic dilation and neogenesis, enhancing immune cell trafficking to hilar lymph nodes [47]. In the central nervous system, absence of conventional lymphatic vessels in the parenchyma, with lymphoid structures restricted to meninges, creates anatomical constraints that contribute to immune privilege [48, 49]. Conversely, within the skin, functional lymphatic vessels are essential for priming adaptive immunity, as their presence is necessary to induce local and systemic immune responses against challenges such as melanoma [50]. Intriguingly, in established tumors, the role of lymphatics becomes more complex: while often associated with immunosuppression, tumor-associated lymphatic expansion can paradoxically enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy by facilitating the recruitment and activation of antitumor T cells [51].

Beyond the fixed architectural differences, the cellular composition of these vascular and lymphatic niches also varies. Notably, the lymphatic marker LYVE-1 is not exclusively expressed on lymphatic endothelial cells; it is also found on a subset of macrophages, particularly those involved in tissue remodeling and lymphangiogenesis [52]. These LYVE-1 + macrophages are pivotal regulators of lymphangiogenesis, capable of dynamically modulating the formation, remodeling, and function of lymphatic vessels through multiple mechanisms [53]. In the tumor context, such macrophages may facilitate lymphatic expansion, thereby influencing immune cell trafficking and metastatic dissemination [54]. Understanding the dual role of LYVE-1 + macrophages in both promoting lymphangiogenesis and modulating immune responses is essential for comprehending organ-specific migration barriers.

Vascular endothelial activation and tight junction protein expression are key determinants of immune extravasation. Endothelial phenotypes vary across organs: brain endothelial cells express high levels of claudins and VE-cadherin, establishing the blood–brain barrier and restricting immune passage, whereas intestinal endothelium is more permeable, supporting surveillance [55–58]. Lymphatic endothelial cells express VEGFR-3, LYVE-1, and podoplanin, maintaining vessel integrity and governing immune cell entry/egress [57, 59]. Thus, organ-specific vascular and lymphatic structures modulate immune trafficking through local chemokine gradients, adhesion molecules, and endothelial activation [60–62](Fig. 2).

Temporal dynamics of immune cell migration permission

Building on the spatial landscape described above, this section addresses the temporal dynamics that modulate migration permission. The spatial baseline thresholds are not static; they evolve during tumor progression, therapy, and immune memory formation [7]. This temporal dimension dynamically determines whether immune cells retain their ability to infiltrate and function within a given organ, and whether new immune subsets gain access as the microenvironment evolves [63]. The OSML framework posits that effective immune surveillance requires continuous tracking of these temporal shifts and adapting interventions accordingly [64].

Tumor progression stages and changes in migration permission

Tumor progression dynamically alters the TME’s capacity to regulate immune cell migration. In early-stage tumors, elevated expression of chemokines (CXCL9, CXCL10) and adhesion molecules (ICAM-1, VCAM-1) promotes effector immune cell infiltration, enhancing antitumor responses [65–68]. Specifically, transcriptomic analysis, single-cell sequencing and CCR1 knockout mouse models demonstrated that CCR1 on macrophages upregulated the expression of CXCL9 and CXCL10 in gastric cancer by activating NF-κB/MAPK pathway, thereby promoting CD8 + T cell recruitment to tumor sites [69]. As tumors advance, the TME acquires immunosuppressive characteristics: infiltration by tumor cells, CAFs, and immunosuppressive cells leads to reprogramming of chemokine networks and adhesion molecule expression, impeding effector recruitment while favoring immunosuppressive populations [70–72].

These shifts involve upregulation of CCL2 and CXCL12, which recruit regulatory T cells and MDSCs, and downregulation of CXCL9/10, restricting CD8+ T-cell infiltration [65, 70]. Tumor and stromal cells further remodel migration via exosomes, matrix metalloproteinases, and extracellular matrix (ECM) restructuring [68, 73, 74]. Consequently, immunosuppressive cells accumulate while effector T cells and dendritic cells decline due to migration barriers and exhaustion [75, 76]. Longitudinal labeling shows continuous rapid recruitment of regulatory T cells dependent on integrins CD11a and CD49d; inhibiting these integrins reduces tumor burden, highlighting the importance of dynamic migration-regulating mechanisms [75]. Thus, the evolving molecular networks and immune subset composition define antitumor immune strength (Fig. 3).

Dynamic evolution of migration permission timing in tumor progression and immunotherapy

In early-stage tumors, migration permission is “on”: CXCL9/10 recruit effector T cells, enabling dense intratumoral infiltration and elevated T-cell activity (green line). In advanced tumors, permission switches “off”: immunosuppressive factors (CXCL12, TGF-β) create a biochemical barrier, inducing T-cell exhaustion and restricting infiltration, with suppression peaking (red line) as effector T cells decline. Following immune checkpoint blockade (e.g., anti–PD-1), permission is reprogrammed: inhibitory signaling is blocked, restoring T-cell infiltration and immune activity, as reflected by recovered effector T-cell levels and concurrent suppression decline.

Temporal response of migration permission under immunotherapy intervention

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and CAR-T cell therapy rely on effective immune cell migration and spatiotemporal regulation within the TME. ICIs (anti–PD-1/PD-L1, anti–CTLA-4) restore effector T-cell activation and modulate migration-permitting molecules, increasing tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and reshaping their spatial distribution. For example, multimodal imaging studies demonstrated that anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 combination therapy dynamically modulates immune cell trafficking and tumor invasion, with enhanced cytotoxic activity observed prior to tumor volume reduction, visually revealing adaptive feedback of migration pathways [77]. After ICI therapy, T cells at the tumor periphery and core exhibit distinct migratory behaviors, with enhanced core infiltration reflecting adaptive feedback of migration pathways [78].

Immunotherapy induces phased alterations in migration-permitting molecules. For instance, a nanoglycocluster that is triggered by hypoxia-upregulated MMP-2 to self-assemble in situ can efficiently repolarize hypoxic TAMs towards an M1 phenotype via multivalent engagement with mannose receptors, enabling spatiotemporal-restricted initiation of antitumor immunity [79], which exemplifies how nanomedicine-based modulation of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) enables spatiotemporal control, promoting selective immune cell recruitment while minimizing toxicity [80, 81]. Single-cell analyses reveal oscillatory changes in chemokine and receptor expression, influencing sustained trafficking and functional persistence [82].

CAR-T cell efficacy depends on in vivo expansion and tumor localization. Migration-permitting molecule expression in CAR-T cells is regulated by ex vivo activation and microenvironmental cues; genetic disruption of immune checkpoints enhances persistence and infiltration [83]. Noninvasive imaging studies, such as ICOS-immunoPET, have directly validated that therapeutic efficacy correlates with CAR-T cell localization and expansion within specific tumor sites [84]. Nanomedicine approaches further augment tumor targeting and spatiotemporal control of migration [81]. Hence, immunotherapy dynamically modulates migration-permitting molecules to govern immune-cell recruitment and infiltration, shaping therapeutic response.

Spatiotemporal coupling of immune memory formation and migration permission

Immune memory formation and maintenance depend on dynamic migration and tissue colonization of memory T and B cells. Memory T cells comprise circulating (blood, lymph nodes, spleen, bone marrow) and tissue-resident (TRM) subsets. Circulating cells enable rapid systemic responses [85]; TRMs persist in barrier tissues (skin, lung, intestine) and provide localized protection [86, 87]. In the critical barrier of the upper respiratory tract (nasal cavity and nasopharynx), the researchers directly confirmed the existence of stable and functionally differentiated tissue-dwelling memory T cells and B cells pools, which constitute the first line of defense against pathogen invasion, through longitudinal sampling combined with single-cell analysis techniques [88]. Single-cell sequencing reveals TRM clonal diversity, localized expansion, and functional heterogeneity across organs, with superior homing capacity. TRM activation also promotes dendritic-cell migration to draining lymph nodes, coordinating local and systemic responses [89].

For B cells, memory B cells and plasma cells establish durable humoral immunity. Bone marrow serves as a principal reservoir for long-lived memory B cells and plasma cells [90]. Longitudinal B-cell receptor sequencing in healthy individuals has quantitatively demonstrated that memory B cell clones can persist for decades, with an estimated half-life of about 10 years, and continuously replenish the antibody-secreting cell pool [91]. During germinal center reactions, B cells undergo affinity maturation and differentiation; subsets migrate to bone marrow, spleen, or peripheral tissues, forming stable niches [92]. Upon re-encounter, memory B cells rapidly differentiate into plasma cells, producing high-affinity antibodies [90].

Memory cell migration is regulated by chemotactic cues, local microenvironment, inflammation, and immune checkpoints. The CXCL16–CXCR6 axis recruits and retains TRMs [93]; CCR7 influences T-cell migration and is clinically relevant in graft-versus-host disease [94]. In tumor immunity, TRM persistence and rapid reactivation suppress recurrence and metastasis, while circulating memory cells maintain systemic surveillance [85, 95].

Mechanisms of spatial heterogeneity in inter-organ migration permission

The preceding discussion has detailed how spatial heterogeneity establishes baseline thresholds and how temporal dynamics modulate them. However, the OSML framework emphasizes that these dimensions are not additive but interactive. For example, the spatial architecture of an organ (e.g., dense ECM in pancreatic cancer) may become more restrictive over time as tumors progress, while the same tumor may simultaneously alter its chemokine profile to recruit immunosuppressive cells [96]. Thus, the migration license at any given moment is the product of a continuous interplay between spatial constraints and temporal evolution. Interventions designed to reprogram migration permission must therefore target both the static architecture and the dynamic signaling that sustains it [97].

Molecular basis of migration permission thresholds across different organs

Immune cells exhibit organ-specific molecular requirements for migration, defined by a “migratory permissive threshold.” Inter-organ migration depends not only on chemokine–receptor expression (e.g., CCL/CXCL families, CCR/CXCR) but also on downstream signaling pathways [5, 70]. Organ-specific chemokine–receptor pairs influence cell polarity, adhesion, and cytoskeletal remodeling via pathways such as phosphoinositide signaling [98] and Rho GTPases (Rac1, RhoA) [99, 100]. The central role of these signaling modules in immune cell migration has been extensively reviewed [101]. For example, studies have shown that lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) regulates T cell migration and function by activating RhoA GTase in T cells, disrupting cytoskeletal rearrangement and immune synapse formation [102]. Integrin–ligand interactions and adhesion molecule expression further determine barrier traverasal and tissue residence [103]; for instance, within the thymic niche, the mechanical properties of the extracellular matrix, sensed through integrins such as α4β1, directly instruct hematopoietic stem cells to differentiate into T cells and reside in that niche or adopt alternative fates [104].

Broader physiological factors, including circadian rhythms, metabolic states, and neural regulation, also shape migration thresholds. Circadian oscillations modulate chemokine and receptor expression, affecting trafficking over time [5, 105]. Studies have shown that in tumor immunotherapy, targeting HIF-1α-BMAL1 axis by nanomaterials to restore circadian rhythm can up-regulate core clock gene BMAL1, thereby enhancing antigen processing and immune activation functions of dendritic cells [106]. This molecular regulation underlies immune homeostasis and provides targets for therapeutic manipulation.

Importantly, the principles of organ-specific migration licensing extend beyond conventional T cells to other immune lineages, including innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) and type 2 immune effectors. ILCs, which mirror T helper subsets in cytokine production, exhibit tissue-resident and migratory behaviors that are tightly regulated by organ-specific cues. For instance, ILC2s are enriched in barrier tissues such as the lung and gut, where they respond to local alarmins and lipid mediators, and can migrate to distal sites upon inflammatory stimulation [107]. Their survival and function are further modulated by the local metabolic environment; for example, in the tumor microenvironment, lactate derived from tumor cells can suppress the function and survival of ILC2s [108]. Similarly, type 2 immune cells (e.g., Th2 cells, eosinophils) display context-dependent roles in tumor immunity: they can either promote tumor progression through immunosuppression or contribute to antitumor responses by recruiting eosinophils and modulating tissue remodeling [109, 110]. These observations underscore that the OSML framework must account for the full spectrum of immune cells, as their migration permission thresholds are shaped by organ-specific molecular, metabolic, and structural factors. Therapeutic strategies aimed at reprogramming migration permission should therefore consider the heterogeneity of immune subsets and their dual potential in different tumor microenvironments. Advanced 3D models, such as organoid–immune cell co-cultures [111], provide valuable platforms to dissect the spatiotemporal dynamics of these cells and guide the development of combination immunotherapies that harness both type 1 and type 2 immunity in an organ-specific manner.

Spatial coupling barriers between local and systemic immunity

Translating local immune activation into systemic protection is a key challenge in immunotherapy. Immune activation within the TME often remains confined due to mechanisms that restrict immune cell migration between tumors and secondary lymphoid organs. Spatially diverse chemokine and cytokine patterns direct immune cells to specific microdomains but can entrap them within the tumor, preventing egress into circulation or lymphoid tissues, thereby impairing antigen presentation and long-term immune memory formation [112]. Physical barriers—aberrant vasculature, dense stroma, elevated interstitial pressure—further impede immune cell movement and redistribution, hindering effector T-cell recirculation and dendritic-cell trafficking of antigens to lymphoid organs [113]. In an orthotopic pancreatic cancer model using organoids derived from a KPC (KrasLSL-G12D; p53LSL-R172H; Pdx1-Cre) mouse, local tumor ablation with irreversible electroporation (IRE) induced tumor regression and generated neoantigen-specific immune responses, but the systemic antitumor T-cell response elicited by IRE alone was insufficient to effectively control metastatic disease progression [114]. Even local interventions like ablation may transiently stimulate responses, but systemic immunity fails without effective spatial coupling. Elucidating the molecular and structural nodes that enable local-to-systemic immune communication is critical for refining immunotherapy.

Biophysical barriers to cross-organ immune cell migration

Immune cells encounter biophysical barriers during cross-organ migration. Specialized anatomical structures—such as the blood–brain barrier (tight endothelial junctions, basement membrane, astrocytic endfeet) and hepatic sinusoidal endothelium (fenestrated pores)—exert selective permeability, restricting cellular passage [115]. The skin’s multilayered keratinocytes also provide a robust defense [115].

Beyond structure, ECM density and interstitial pressure critically influence migration. Dense ECM increases mechanical resistance, impeding motility; elevated interstitial pressure alters fluid dynamics, affecting directionality and efficiency. Aquaporins regulate water permeability, indirectly modulating immune cell distribution [115]; for instance, in allergic responses, they facilitate water and solute transport across barriers in organs like the skin and lungs, influencing processes such as edema, mucus production, and immune cell migration [116]. Cytoskeletal remodeling, cell–matrix adhesion, and mechanical properties determine barrier traversal. For instance, the Rho-associated kinase (ROCK) signaling modulates Langerhans cell morphology and migration; ROCK inhibition impairs migration [117]. Engineered biomaterials (e.g., hydrogels) can restore or modify barriers to enhance immune cell mobility by mimicking native ECM properties [118], such as by mimicking the electrostatic binding properties of native ECM to adsorb inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, thereby regulating regional immunity and promoting tissue repair [119]. Understanding these barriers and their regulatory pathways is essential for improving immune cell trafficking.

Strategies and mechanisms for reprogramming migration permission

The OSML framework’s emphasis on coupling between spatial and temporal dimensions implies that effective reprogramming cannot target a single factor in isolation. Rather, interventions must simultaneously address the physical architecture, the chemical milieu, and the temporal dynamics that sustain immunosuppression. The strategies discussed in the following subsections target each dimension, but their true power lies in combination—for example, normalizing tumor vasculature while blocking checkpoint molecules to restore effector cell infiltration and function.

Interventions targeting chemokine–receptor axes

Chemokine–receptor interactions centrally regulate immune cell trafficking and shape the TME. Pharmacologic modulation of these axes is a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapy. The CXCR4–CXCL12 axis, expressed on tumor and stromal cells, promotes tumor migration and immune evasion. CXCR4 antagonists (e.g., AMD3100) block immunosuppressive cell recruitment and facilitate effector T-cell infiltration, enhancing ICI activity in preclinical models [120, 121]. Similarly, the CCR7–CCL21 axis governs lymph node homing and immune cell localization but is also implicated in tumor metastasis and immune regulation. Targeting this pathway may suppress lymphatic dissemination of tumor cells and augment antitumor immunity [122]. Notably, preclinical studies in hepatocellular carcinoma have shown that exogenous VEGF-C treatment enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy by activating the CCL21/CCR7 axis and lymphatic angiogenesis to drive tertiary lymphoid structure formation [123]. Engineering T cells to express exogenous chemokine receptors (CXCR6, CCR2) improves tumor trafficking and adoptive cell therapy efficacy [124, 125]. Clinical trials of CXCR4-targeting agents show reduced tumor burden and enhanced immune infiltration in some patients, though efficacy is limited by chemokine network redundancy and TME plasticity [121, 126].

The CCR7–CCL21 axis governs lymph node homing and is implicated in metastasis; targeting it may suppress lymphatic dissemination and remodel the immune microenvironment [122, 127]. Supporting this notion, in a chronic murine heart transplantation model, a lymph node-targeted delivery system decorated with a CCL21-specific aptamer was shown to locally promote endogenous Treg generation and reduce Teff proportions, effectively reshaping the local immune microenvironment [128]. Challenges include pathway redundancy, receptor diversity, and off-target effects, prompting research into multitarget strategies and combination with ICIs [126, 129]. Collectively, targeting chemokine axes offers a foundation for reprogramming immune cell migration and improving immunotherapy.

Microenvironment remodeling and immune cell migration permission

Modulating the TME can enhance immune cell migration permissiveness. TGF-β restricts effector T-cell infiltration; combining anti-TGF-β with PD-1 blockade increases CD8+ T-cell and NK-cell infiltration in “cold” tumors like neuroblastoma [130]. Abnormal tumor vasculature limits immune entry; anti-angiogenic or vascular normalization strategies improve vessel architecture, reduce hypoxia, and lower pressure, creating permissive pathways [131, 132]. Supporting this concept, research in hepatocellular carcinoma models demonstrated that pharmacological stabilization of CCL16 using the DPP4 inhibitor sitagliptin normalized tumor vasculature via a CCL16-ICAM1-JAK2-STAT6-IL24 axis in macrophages, which enhanced immune cell infiltration and synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy [133]. Nanoplatforms promoting perivascular coverage achieve normalization, enhancing infiltration of NK cells, M1 macrophages, and CD8+ T cells, converting “cold” to “hot” tumors [132].

Matrix remodeling also regulates migration. CAFs alter ECM composition, forming physical barriers that restrict T-cell infiltration; they also secrete immunosuppressive factors and modulate immune checkpoints [134]. Demonstrated in EMT6 and MC38 subcutaneous xenograft models, the CAF-targeting dye IR-780 inhibited extracellular matrix protein deposition, broke the T-cell barrier, increased tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte proportion, and enhanced anti-PD-L1 therapeutic efficacy [135]. ECM components have bidirectional effects: collagen VI promotes CD8+ T-cell dysfunction, while collagen I enhances activity, illustrating dynamic modulation of migration and function [136]. Targeting ECM remodeling, e.g., inhibiting pro-immunosuppressive components or signaling pathways, is a promising strategy [137]. Supporting this, in a metastatic breast carcinoma model, inhibiting chemotherapy-induced LOX activity in CD8 + T cells blocked pro-metastatic pulmonary ECM remodeling [138]. Combining anti-angiogenesis with ECM-targeted therapies synergistically improves infiltration [139]. Consistent with this combinatorial strategy, in the E0771 breast cancer model, targeting the fibronectin-organizing integrin α5β1 was shown to reduce fibronectin fibril formation, enhance CD8 + T cell transendothelial migration, increase vascular permeability, and thereby synergistically improve the efficacy of PD-L1 blockade [140].

It is important to note that CAFs are not uniformly pro-tumorigenic; certain CAF subsets can restrain tumor growth by promoting immune cell infiltration or by directly inhibiting tumor cells [141]. As demonstrated by the in vivo engineering of FAP-targeting CAR T cells via targeted lipid nanoparticle delivery of mRNA, which depletes FAP-expressing CAFs, disrupts the fibrotic tumor stroma, and enhances immune cell infiltration to restrain pancreatic tumor growth [142]. Additionally, some CAFs produce chemokines that recruit effector T cells into the tumor microenvironment [143]. The functional heterogeneity of CAFs underscores the need for context-dependent targeting in immunotherapy. Understanding the specific CAF subsets that facilitate or impede immune cell migration is crucial for designing effective OSML-based interventions.

Emerging nanotechnologies further regulate vascular and stromal components, optimizing immune cell migration and function [144]. In summary, multi-level microenvironment remodeling enhances migration permissiveness and immunotherapy outcomes.

Regulation of migration permissibility by nanomedicines and delivery systems

Nanomedicines enable precise modulation of organ-specific migration-permitting molecules. Cell membrane–coated nanoparticles mimic source-cell migratory properties for targeted delivery. For instance, osteoblast-membrane-coated mesoporous silica nanoparticles exhibit bone-directed migration [145]; macrophage- or platelet-membrane-coated nanoparticles home to tumors or inflammation sites [146, 147]. Functionalized nanoparticles regulate integrins and chemokine receptors; a direct demonstration of this is provided by anti-CD61 monoclonal antibody-functionalized carbon-encapsulated iron nanoparticles, which exhibit specific binding to integrin αVβ3 on murine glioma cells for targeted MR imaging [148]. Ferritin/transferrin-modified protein nanoparticles promote monocyte transendothelial migration via integrin upregulation [149].

Stimulus-responsive “smart” delivery systems provide spatiotemporal control. pH-sensitive nanoparticles deliver CXCR4 antagonists or siRNA to the TME, promoting effector T-cell infiltration [150, 151]. External-field–driven platforms (magnetic nanoparticles, nanorobots) enable remote navigation and organ-specific accumulation, e.g., magnetically controlled DCR3 nanoparticles target liver cancer cells [152], as further evidenced by a bioengineered tumor-on-chip model that employed calibrated magnetic fields to guide and enhance the retention of chitosan-coated magnetic drug carriers within a perfusable 3D breast tumor microenvironment under physiologically relevant shear stress [153]. Bio-inspired nanoplatforms achieve selective homing via surface glycosylation and integrin expression [154, 155] (Fig. 4). Integration with omics and imaging technologies may further enable real-time monitoring and personalized modulation of immune cell migration.

Strategies for reprogramming migration permissibility. A Core Layer (Direct Targeting): Modulate immune cell surface receptors using antibodies or small-molecule inhibitors to relieve migration-inhibitory signals, restoring baseline T-cell function. B Intermediate Layer (Microenvironment Remodeling): Reshape physical and chemical barriers within the TME via vascular normalization and ECM degradation, creating permissive pathways for immune cell infiltration. C Outer Layer (Systemic Intervention): Integrate advanced biotechnologies with systemic strategies—including smart delivery platforms, cell engineering, and global modulation—to enable precise and synergistic reprogramming of migration licensing. ECM, extracellular matrix; TME, tumor microenvironment; LDHA, lactate dehydrogenase A; CAR-T cell, chimeric antigen receptor T cell.

Integrated model and clinical significance of the OSML framework

Construction of the three-dimensional integrated OSML model

The OSML three-dimensional model unites spatial heterogeneity, temporal dynamics, and intervention-mediated reprogramming into a cohesive framework where each dimension dynamically influences the others. Spatial heterogeneity defines the anatomical and molecular landscape that sets the initial migration threshold. Temporal dynamics capture how this threshold shifts during tumor progression, immune memory formation, or therapy-induced remodeling. Intervention-mediated reprogramming actively resets these thresholds by targeting specific nodes within the spatial–temporal network. Thus, the OSML model posits that effective modulation of immune cell migration requires simultaneous consideration of where (spatial), when (temporal), and how (intervention) to intervene—a principle that distinguishes it from static, single-factor models of immune recruitment. Spatial heterogeneity refers to immune cell distribution and functional differences across organs and tissues. Technologies like 3D bioprinting and organ-on-a-chip enable in vitro reconstruction of tissue architectures to simulate immune cell migration and TME interactions [156]; complementarily, computational models integrating spatial multi-omics data can reconstruct the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment in silico to predict immunotherapy responses [157]. Temporal dynamics capture evolving immune cell migration during tumor progression, therapy, and microenvironment remodeling; microfluidic and organ-on-a-chip platforms allow real-time monitoring of immune–tumor–endothelial interactions [158]. A prime illustration is provided by a study where a dual-sized microparticle system was developed to deliver disease-specific antigens and defined immunomodulatory factors in a mouse model of multiple sclerosis; this approach fundamentally validated the design principle that to effectively mimic disease-relevant recruitment and interactions on such platforms, co-delivery of specific factors for immune cell recruitment—such as the chemokine SDF-1 (CXCL12)—is indispensable for orchestrating targeted cellular movement and modulating disease outcomes [159]. Intervention-mediated reprogramming actively modulates migration via gene editing, biomaterials, and targeted drug delivery; high-resolution 3D bioprinting facilitates precise control of cellular distribution and microenvironmental components [156]. By integrating these three dimensions, the OSML model provides a comprehensive framework to explain dynamic tumor immunity regulation.

Role of OSML in immunotherapy resistance mechanisms

Impaired inter-organ immune cell migration is a key contributor to immunotherapy resistance. Within the TME, TAMs secrete TGF-β and IL-10, promoting immune escape and enhancing tumor invasiveness [160]. Specifically, M2-type TAMs secrete IL-10 and TGF-β to inhibit cytotoxic T lymphocytes while expanding immunosuppressive regulatory T (Treg) cells, thereby establishing a key mechanism for immune evasion within the tumor microenvironment [161]. MFAP2 + CAFs interact with immune cells via macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), inducing CD8 + T-cell dysfunction and M2 macrophage polarization, establishing an immunosuppressive niche [162]. Tumor cells modulate chemokine-receptor expression to restrict effector T-cell entry, diminishing ICI efficacy [163, 164]. They also interfere via exosomes, metabolic byproducts, and epigenetic modifications; e.g., aberrant PI3K-mTOR activation promotes proliferation and migration while modulating immune visibility via EZH2, contributing to ICI resistance [165, 166].

The OSML model guides strategies to overcome resistance. Targeted modulation of migration-related molecules (chemokine axes, EZH2, PI3K-mTOR, LDHA) can enhance inter-organ migration and restore effector infiltration [165, 166]. OSML-based combination immunotherapy involves:

Organ-specific immunomodulation

select agents based on organ immune microenvironment (e.g., Treg inhibitors, ICIs, localized activators) [167], as demonstrated by strategies utilizing pharmacological induction of intratumoral high endothelial venules and tertiary lymphoid structures to reshape the tumor immune contexture, which synergizes with immune checkpoint inhibitors to enhance antitumor efficacy [168].

Precision delivery

nanomedicine carriers target drugs to specific organs, maximizing efficacy and minimizing toxicity [169, 170]. This is exemplified by a tumor-specific strategy where, in orthotopic HCC models, dual pH-responsive nanoparticles enabling T-cell hitchhiking and co-delivery of aPD-1/MDK-siRNA reshape the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, achieving significant therapeutic efficacy with minimal side effects [151].

Migration-informed strategies

account for immune and tumor cell migration pathways to achieve coordinated activation across organs [171].

Dynamic monitoring

track immune cell distribution and function in real time to adjust strategies [172]. By employing Kaede photoconvertible mice and single-cell genomics to map the migratory trajectories of immunocytes from the colon to peripheral sites, this research provides the essential spatial-temporal data required to tailor immunomodulatory strategies for systemic inflammation [173].

Integrated multimodal approaches

combine local radiotherapy, targeted therapies, etc., to remodel the TME [174], a strategy evidenced by the synergy between hypofractionated radiotherapy and a fibroblast activation protein (FAP)-targeted CD40 agonist in a syngeneic orthotopic murine HPV-positive head and neck cancer model, where radiotherapy-induced TME remodeling was essential for optimal activation of the CD8 + T-cell-cDC1 axis and durable complete responses [175].

These multidimensional strategies enable personalized, dynamic interventions to maximize antitumor immunity while minimizing adverse effects.

Challenges and opportunities in the clinical translation of OSML

Clinical translation of organ-specific combination immunotherapy faces challenges: tumor microenvironment heterogeneity across organs causes variable responses; metastatic lesions at different sites may respond divergently to the same therapy, requiring tailored strategies [176, 177], as evidenced by spatial transcriptomic profiling which revealed that metastasis-specific immune niches, not primary-tumor characteristics, dictate response to immune checkpoint inhibitors in paired primary and metastatic renal cell carcinoma lesions [178]. Efficient targeted delivery to specific organs remains a hurdle; nanomaterials and smart delivery platforms show promise but need rigorous validation of safety, pharmacokinetics, and long-term efficacy [169, 170]. Organ-specific adverse effects (e.g., immune-related hepatitis, pneumonitis) constrain application, necessitating balance between efficacy and toxicity [179, 180].

Conversely, advances in single-cell omics, spatial multi-omics, and transcriptomics enable precise characterization of organ-specific immune compositions, providing a framework for personalized therapy [171, 172]. Emerging technologies like AI and organ-on-a-chip can accelerate drug screening and predict efficacy, enhancing translation efficiency [171, 181]; for example, the integration of AI for automated data analysis and design optimization with human organ-mimicking OoC platforms creates a closed-loop system that streamlines the drug evaluation pipeline [182]. Interdisciplinary collaboration across immunology, bioengineering, and clinical oncology will be essential to advance OSML-based immunotherapies, enabling precise dynamic modulation of immune responses in multi-organ metastatic tumors and improving patient outcomes.

Recent research advances and future directions

Applications of single-cell omics and spatial transcriptomics in OSML research

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics enable high-resolution characterization of immune cell heterogeneity and spatiotemporal migration within the TME. For instance, integrating 10X Visium and Nanostring CosMx in head and neck cancer revealed distinct myeloid–T-cell spatial interactions in ICB responders versus non-responders, confirming the CXCL9/10–CXCR3 axis role and enabling construction of ligand–receptor maps [183]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, multi-omics integration (whole-exome, transcriptome, spatial proteomics) showed spatial heterogeneity in immune-regulatory gene expression between primary tumors and metastases, aiding precise subtyping [184, 185].

Multi-omics integration further elucidates OSML networks. Studies in gastric, liver, and breast cancers revealed spatial distribution and functional heterogeneity of CAF subtypes, myeloid cells, and T cells linked to immunotherapy resistance [186–188]. In breast cancer, single-cell transcriptomics and genetic depletion models demonstrate that cancer-associated fibroblasts recruit monocytes via the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis and reprogram them into immunosuppressive lipid-associated macrophages which impair T cell function, directly linking this spatial cellular crosstalk to resistance against immune checkpoint blockade [189]. In renal cell carcinoma and glioblastoma, spatial maps identified interaction patterns between pro-inflammatory macrophages and CD8+ T cells, and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) expression spatial characteristics, offering predictive insights and therapeutic targets [190, 191]. Collectively, these technologies propel tumor immunology toward spatiotemporal precision.

Breakthroughs in animal models and preclinical research

Innovative animal models and preclinical platforms have advanced OSML validation. Traditional syngeneic mouse xenografts are limited in recapitulating human TME complexity [192, 193]; humanized and patient-derived xenograft models improve translational relevance [193]. Specifically, restoring NK cell reconstitution and infiltration in HCC patient-derived xenografts established in humanized mice via AAV-hIL15 enabled the evaluation of NK cell-dependent efficacy of combined PD-1 and TIGIT blockade [194]. Integrating multi-organ co-modeling, dynamic imaging, and lineage tracing enables real-time monitoring of immune cell migration trajectories and functional evolution across organs [75, 195].

Organoid and organ-on-a-chip systems can reconstruct the patient-specific TME under controlled hydrodynamics to interrogate cellular interplay and therapy testing [196].In vitro systems—3D tumor models, organoids, organ-on-a-chip—reconstruct TME in microfluidic environments, incorporating tumor, immune, and stromal cells to simulate migration, infiltration, and cytotoxicity.

These models vary in complexity and physiological relevance. Spheroid cultures, often generated from tumor cell lines or patient-derived cells, provide a simple three-dimensional architecture that recapitulates nutrient and oxygen gradients, enabling the study of immune cell penetration into avascular tumor regions [197]. More advanced organoid models incorporate multiple cell types within an extracellular matrix scaffold, better mimicking the native tissue architecture [198]. For example, Wagner and Koyasu developed a co-culture system using 3D tumor spheroids and immune cells, which provides a controlled in vitro model to study ILC–tumor cell interactions [111]. Such organoid-based platforms can be further integrated with microfluidic technology to incorporate perfusable vasculature and dynamic flow, offering a more accurate representation of the in vivo microenvironment.

Microfluidic organ-on-a-chip devices enable precise control of fluid shear stress, chemical gradients, and mechanical forces, making them particularly suited for studying immune cell trafficking under physiologically relevant conditions. Multi-organ-on-a-chip replicates NK-cell migration across endothelium under shear stress, infiltration into tumor stroma, and killing, providing biomimetic platforms for drug screening [199, 200]. These models elucidate inter-organ migration during metastasis and organ-specific modulation of immune function [201, 202].

These models reinforce OSML theory and inform therapeutic optimization. Targeting migration-related molecules (integrins, chemokines) in animal models enhances immune infiltration and cytotoxicity [75, 203]; specifically, silencing the m6A methyltransferase METTL5 in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma models upregulates the CXCL16 chemokine to enhance CD8 + T cell infiltration and cytotoxic function [204]. High-throughput screening using organoids and chips accelerates evaluation of candidate drugs on migration and cross-organ responses, expediting clinical translation [199, 200].

Application prospects of OSML theory in novel immunotherapy development

OSML theory provides a new paradigm emphasizing spatiotemporal regulation of immune responses, shifting from localized interventions to coordinated multi-organ approaches. Patient-specific immune cell migration pathways can guide personalized regimens; modeling immune cell flows between TME and organs (lymph nodes, liver, bone marrow) enables evaluation of systemic effects and adverse reactions, optimizing strategies [171].

OSML also informs TME reprogramming and systemic immune activation. The TME drives immune evasion and therapy resistance, with immune cell distribution regulated by complex networks. Leveraging OSML, advanced in vitro platforms (organ-on-a-chip, multi-organ-on-a-chip) model immune cell migration, activation, and interactions across multiple organs, providing robust tools for drug screening, efficacy prediction, and safety assessment [205–207]. Using patient-derived cells in such systems can accelerate personalized immunotherapy translation.

Integration with multi-omics and AI enables high-throughput monitoring of immune cell migration and TME dynamics, offering insights into immune escape and drug resistance. OSML-guided strategies may facilitate next-generation immunotherapies with precise spatiotemporal control, optimizing combination regimens and improving patient outcomes.

Toward a quantitative and predictive OSML framework

While the OSML theory provides a conceptual foundation for understanding organ-specific immune cell migration, its translation into a predictive framework requires integration with quantitative computational modeling and multi-omics data. Recent advances in computational immuno-oncology have demonstrated the power of mathematical models to simulate tumor-immune dynamics and predict therapeutic responses [208, 209]. These approaches can be adapted to validate and refine the OSML hypothesis.

Knowledge-based mechanistic models offer one avenue for testing OSML principles. Agent-based models (ABMs), which simulate the behavior of individual cells in response to their microenvironment, are particularly suited for studying immune cell migration [210, 211]. For example, ABMs have been used to investigate how extracellular matrix (ECM) density, such as collagen concentration, influences cancer cell migration in hypoxic microenvironments [212], and to predict how cells sense and transduce microenvironmental signals, such as pressure gradients, into migration decisions across varying geometries [213]. By incorporating organ-specific parameters—such as vascular architecture, ECM density, and chemokine profiles—ABMs could predict how altering specific “license” parameters affects T-cell trafficking across different organs. Similarly, delay differential equation (DDE) models have been used to represent biologically realistic time delays inherent in immune cell activation and migration [214], providing a mathematical framework to quantify the temporal dimension of OSML.

Data-driven machine learning approaches, when integrated with spatial omics data, offer complementary tools to identify organ-specific “migration permission signatures.” Recent studies have demonstrated that graph neural networks (GNNs) trained on spatial transcriptomics data can effectively predict immunotherapy response by modeling the spatial organization and cell–cell interactions within the tumor microenvironment [215, 216]. For instance, a deep learning framework integrating spatial proteomics and transcriptomics successfully identified an immunotherapy resistance niche in hepatocellular carcinoma and predicted treatment outcomes with high accuracy [217]. The mosna Python package enables systematic analysis of spatial omics data to discover cell interaction patterns predictive of immunotherapy response [218]. These methods could be extended to learn the spatial rules governing immune cell migration permission in an organ-specific manner.

Hybrid approaches that combine mechanistic modeling with machine learning represent a promising frontier [219]. For example, integrating ABMs with patient-derived organoid and organ-on-chip platforms could enable iterative refinement and validation of OSML predictions [220]. Ultimately, a quantitative OSML model could guide the design of combination immunotherapies by predicting which organ-specific barriers to target, at what time point, and with which intervention—moving beyond empirical trial-and-error toward precision immuno-engineering.

Conclusion

Tumor immunotherapy has advanced remarkably, but therapeutic heterogeneity and immune resistance limit clinical benefit. Effective antitumor immunity is systemic, depending on dynamic migration, spatial distribution, and functional coordination of immune cells across organs. OSML theory provides a framework highlighting spatiotemporal regulation of inter-organ immune cell migration as central to overcoming bottlenecks.

OSML posits that immune cells require “migration permits” to traverse organ microenvironments, regulated by complex signaling networks. TME heterogeneity, inter-tissue barriers, and organ-specific molecular expression impede migration and function, rendering single-target interventions often ineffective.

Understanding OSML networks elucidates the “spatial game” between tumors and the immune system, guiding rational design of organ-specific combination immunotherapies. Modulating migration-permitting molecules, targeting organ-specific signals, or integrating systemic and local interventions may enhance immune homing and antitumor activity, improving efficacy and safety.

Although OSML mechanisms are still being unraveled, advances in single-cell omics, spatial transcriptomics, and multi-omics integration promise high-resolution mapping of migration networks. Combining OSML insights with patient-derived data, animal models, and AI may accelerate translation.

The OSML framework enriches tumor immunology, offering strategies to overcome resistance and heterogeneity. Multidisciplinary collaboration will align mechanistic insights with clinical needs. Integrating OSML-based approaches with organ-specific combination immunotherapies may achieve more durable and broadly applicable clinical benefits for cancer patients.
