Dearomative Nucleophilic Addition of Lithium Enolates to Electron‐Poor Indoles
Authors: Mélanie Roseau, Matthieu Hédouin, Catherine Fressigné, Julien Legros, Isabelle Chataigner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dearomatization of electron‐poor indoles was investigated through the addition of lithiated ester or ketone enolates. Nitro‐substituted indoles undergo 1,4‐addition to afford the dearomatized products, after smooth hydrolysis. Indoles bearing an α‐ketoamide group show a more complex reactivity, undergoing either 1,2‐ or 1,4‐addition, depending on the steric hindrance of the amide group. The regioselectivity of the latter is buttressed by DFT calculations.Keywords: 1,2‐addition; 1,4‐addition; dearomatization; electron‐depleted arenes

Introduction

Dearomatization reactions are useful tools in organic synthesis, enabling the transformation of simple and flat molecules into functionalized 3D scaffolds (See for instance).[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] Since electron‐deficient aromatic compounds are easily accessible, through SEAr reactions (electrophilic aromatic substitution) for example, their involvement in dearomatization reactions is highly appealing. In general, when electron‐deficient aromatics interact with nucleophilic species, they undergo nucleophilic aromatic substitutions (SNAr), which involve an addition/elimination sequence that restores the aromaticity of the original ring (See for instance).[ 6 ] In some cases, the dearomatized Meisenheimer intermediate resulting from the addition step can be intramolecularly trapped, in a formal cycloaddition process, resulting in a dearomatized cycloadduct (see for instance).[ 7 ] Intermolecular trapping of this intermediate is generally more challenging due to the favored competitive retro‐addition process, which leads to the recovery of the starting materials.

Dearomatization of electron‐poor indoles such as 3‐nitroindoles has been largely explored recently, with most processes involving (formal) cycloaddition reactions in the presence of electron‐rich dienes, 1,3‐ or 1,4‐dipoles (for reviews, see for instance).[ 8 , 9 ] In general, a stepwise process occurs, with an initial reversible addition step involving the formation of a C—C bond between the electron‐rich species and the aromatic carbon atom at position 2. In such cases, the intramolecular trapping of the Meisenheimer nitronate intermediate is key to push the reaction forward (Scheme 1,a). In addition to these processes, it has been shown that stable enamines add at position 2 of 3‐nitroindoles, with an intramolecular proton trap (in a formal ene reaction) playing a key role in generating the indoline in this case.[ 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ] In other few examples, it has been shown that other polar organometallic carbon nucleophiles add to 3‐nitroindoles at position 2.[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] The formation of a nitroindoline is then possible by acidic work‐up. In these reactions, however, the retro elimination process or other secondary reactions often interfere, altering both the yield and the reproducibility of the results.

Formal cycloadditions and addition of enolates involving 3‐nitroindoles.

Enolates are powerful nucleophiles that enable the formation of new C—C bonds while introducing a functional group of synthetic value. We have previously shown that enolates derived from 4‐methoxybut‐3‐en‐2‐one can efficiently add to 3‐nitroindoles, leading after intramolecular trapping to the corresponding formal (4 + 2) cycloadducts (Scheme 1,a).[ 18 ] Recently, we also performed enolate additions to 3‐nitroindoles, in a different context, with highly nucleophilic enolates derived from malonates in order to evaluate the electrophilic potential of these nitrohetarenes (Scheme 1,b).[ 19 ] Notably, this study highlighted the reversibility of the enolate addition to 3‐nitroindoles. In a related example, Gribble had reported the addition of the enolate derived from cyclohexanone and diethyl malonate to 3‐nitroindole (Scheme 1,b).[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 20 ]

In light of these preliminary investigations and building on our previous studies of (cyclo)additions involving nitroarenes,[ 10 , 18 , 19 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 ] we now report the dearomatization of electron‐depleted indoles by addition of simple lithium enolates leading to novel dearomatized and functionalized compounds after smooth intermolecular hydrolysis (Scheme 1,c).

Results and Discussion

We began our study using 3‐nitro‐N‐tosylindole 1a [ 20 ] as a model electrophilic heteroaromatic compound and nbutylacetate 2a under conventional conditions, with lithium diisopropylamide (LDA) as the base, in THF at −78 °C (Table 1 , entry 1). After smooth hydrolysis with an aqueous saturated ammonium chloride solution at 0 °C, we were pleased to observe the formation of 3a, isolated as a single diastereomer, with the added chain and nitro group in a trans configuration.[ 18 ] The adduct 3a was isolated in a good 74% yield.[ 28 ] Noteworthy, when the crude product obtained after extraction was left exposed to air before purification by silica gel chromatography, it gradually converted into ketone 4a.[ 29 ] This transformation likely proceeded via the formation of the nitronate derivative of 3a, facilitated by the basic diisopropylamine still present in the reaction medium, followed by the addition of trace amounts of water, similar to a Nef oxidation reaction. Next, we explored other parameters that could influence the reaction outcome. Using a higher excess of enolate (2.4 equivalents) resulted in a much lower yield of 12%, a complex reaction mixture being obtained (entry 2). Preforming the enolate anion for 15 min was essential, as simultaneous introduction of the reactants led to a significant decrease in yield (entry 3). The method of adding the electrophile, whether by cannulation or dropwise, did not notably affect the yield (compare entries 1 and 4). Switching to water hydrolysis instead of acidic hydrolysis proved detrimental, leading to a lower yield and the formation of keto adduct 4a as a side product (entry 5). Attempts to trap the nitronate intermediate with methyl iodide as an electrophile were unsuccessful, and the same protonated adduct 3a was isolated (entry 6). Finally, for comparison, the trimethylsilyl enol ether of 2a was reacted with the same nitroindole in dichloromethane at room temperature (entry 7). This led to a lower isolated yield of 65%, confirming the relevance of using the lithium enolate in the reaction. Note that on a stereochemical point of view, the trans isomer is selectively recovered in all these cases, suggesting a perfectly controlled reprotonation of the nitronate resulting from the addition, as discussed previously.[ 18 , 28 ]

With these conditions in hand, we then evaluated the scope of this process with the variation of the nitro substituted indoles and the enolate species (Scheme 2 ). Reacting indole 1a, with nbutyl or ethyl ester enolates furnished the adducts 3a and 3b in similar isolated yields and the same complete diastereoselectivity. The ketone enolate derived from acetophenone could add on 1a, leading to the corresponding adduct 3c isolated in a slightly lower 65% yield. Substitution of the 6‐membered ring of the 3‐nitroindole by bromo or nitro groups were tolerated, furnishing the corresponding adducts 3d, 3e, and 3f in 78, 48% and 55% yield respectively. Switching from a sulfonamide protected indole to a carbamate one led to a slight yield decrease, 3g being isolated in a reduced 50% yield. The use of another nitroheteroaromatic with the electrophilic 7‐aza‐3‐nitroindole protected as a Boc carbamate proved even less efficient furnishing the corresponding adduct 3h in only 20% yield, probably because of the higher sensitivity of the adducts in the operating conditions. Reacting of the corresponding N‐sulfonyl protected azaindole proved more efficient, leading to adduct 3i isolated in 50% yield, confirming the preference for a sulfonyl protecting group on the heteroaromtic nitrogen atom.

Dearomatizing addition of enolates to 3‐nitroheteroarenes.

To enlarge the scope of this reaction, we envisaged the dearomatization reaction with more challenging nitroarenes such as 3‐nitropyridine (5). We had previously shown that the addition of the enolate derived from 4‐methoxybut‐3‐en‐2‐one required the use of a deaggregating agent for this nitroarene, with HMPA being the best one.[ 18 ] Reacting 5 in the absence of additive did not lead to any adduct. Here again, addition of HMPA was essential, probably to deaggregate the lithium enolate cluster and promote the reaction with this compound. The process then led to a mixture of 3 regioisomers 6:7:8, depending on the addition site (Scheme 3 ), formed in a ≈2:3:1 ratio. They could be separated by preparative reverse phase chromatography and isolated in 29, 34% and 13% yields, respectively (76% overall yield).

Dearomatizing addition of ester enolate to 3‐nitropyridine (5).

The dearomatization process was then applied to a different class of electron‐depleted indoles, which featured a ketoamide group in place of the nitro group at position 3. Although the ketoamide is much less electron‐withdrawing than the nitro group, it remains a strongly attracting substituent. As a result, 3‐ketoamide indoles were shown to be sufficiently electron‐depleted to undergo reactions with the electron‐rich Danishefsky's diene in (4 + 2) cycloadditions under high‐pressure conditions.[ 30 , 31 , 32 ] Depending on the substitution on the nitrogen atom of the ketoamide group, the Diels–Alder reaction can occur either at the C2=C3 aromatic double bond of the indole dienophile (with tertiary ketoamides) or at the C=O keto group of the substituent (with secondary ketoamides).[ 31 ]

With the N,N‐diethyl ketoamidic indole 9a used as a model compound and nbutylacetate 2a under the conditions described above, we noticed the complete consumption of the initial heteroaromatic substrate, showing its electrophilic character. The formation of two compounds 10a and 11a was observed, resulting from a diastereoselective dearomative 1,4‐addition addition at position C2 of the indole, or from a 1,2‐addition at the keto group of the substituent, respectively (Table 2 , entry 1). Anticipating that steric hindrance of the amide moiety would have an impact on the chemoselectivity of the process, we checked the reactivity of the N,N‐dicyclohexyl ketoamide 9b. With this more sterically hindered indole, a switch in the chemoselectivity of the process was indeed observed with the preferred formation of the dearomatized adduct 10b, isolated in a 43% yield (Entry 2). The N,N‐diisopropyl ketoamide 9c behaved similarly with the preferred formation of the adduct 10c, isolated this time in a higher 60% yield (entry 3). The influence of the protecting group on the nitrogen atom of the indole was next studied, and this showed that the carbamate protection was better for the chemoselectivity of the process (compare entries 3 with 4 and 5), leading to the exclusive formation of the trans adducts 10d and 10e in this case. The Boc protected substrate 9e led to the best isolated yield in 10e (82%), probably because a more rigid conformation pushing the hindered OtBu group away from the indolic C2 atom.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were then carried out to better understand of the regioselectivity of these nucleophilic additions on electron‐poor indoles. Geometry optimizations were carried out at the M062X/6‐31++G(d,p) level of theory with a SMD solvation model (THF) (see Supporting Information (S.I.) for details). We first compared the electrophilic reactivity of indole derivatives bearing a similar Ts moiety at position 1 and different electron‐withdrawing groups at position 3 (Table 3 ). Computing frontier molecular orbitals, revealed that the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy for the nitroindole 1a was expectedly lower than for the ketoamidic indoles 9 (−1.62 vs. −1.18 eV, entries 1–3), in line with the more electron‐withdrawing character of the nitro group. Similarly, the global electrophilicity parameter (ω) as defined by Parr et al. in the context of conceptual DFT,[ 33 ] was higher for the nitro derivative (1.82 vs 1.51, entries 1–3), suggesting that compound 1a is more reactive than 9 toward nucleophiles such as lithium enolates. Then, we compared the local electrophilic character of these compounds, by estimating a local electrophilicity value over atoms (f+)[ 34 ] defined within the context of the atoms‐in‐molecules theory.[ 35 ] All these calculations have been performed with the TopChem2 package.[ 36 ] This revealed that the C2 center is the most electrophilic one, in line with the observed reactivity. Here again, the electrophilicity of the nitrated indoles 1a is higher than the ketoamide ones (0.166 vs. 0.109, entries 1–3). The electrophilicity at the keto position for compounds 9e and 9c was lower than at C2, and almost identical whatever the alkyl substituents on the amide moiety (compare entries 2 and 3). The different regioselectivities observed for the enolate additions depending on the alkyl substituent on the nitrogen can thus not be fully accounted with these values.

These calculations run on the sole substrates do not take into account the interaction of the indole derivative with the nucleophilic enolate species. To move a step forward, we compared the reaction paths, considering both attacks at the C2 and C=O carbon atoms of indoles 9. We used as a model methyl acetate lithium enolate species 15 on one hand and N‐mesyl‐3‐ketoamidic indole 9g (dimethylamide) and 9h (diisopropylamide) on the other. Various conformations of the ketoamide moieties and enolate approach were evaluated (see S.I. for details); the results presented below correspond to the most favorable TSs. Notably, in each case, the favored approach was associated to a coordination of the lithium cation by the oxygen atom of the amide moiety. This required a conformational change of the initial ketoamide, triggered by rotation around the C2—C(O) bond. The s‐cis/s‐trans conformer (9‐conformer) was around 1 kcal.mol−1 less stable than the s‐trans/s‐trans one (9) (0.6 and 1.4 kcal.mol−1 for dimethyl and diisopropyl indoles, respectively) (Scheme 4 ). This coordination expectedly involved the oxygen atom displaying the most nucleophilic character as shown by the local nucleophilic character estimated on this atom (f‐) for compounds 9f and 9c (Table 3). In both C2 and C(O) attack scenarios, the lithium atom was coordinated to the two oxygen atoms from the enolate and the amide, in a quasi‐linear arrangement consistent with a dynamical extra coordination by two THF molecules in the experimental systems.[ 37 ]

Energy profiles for the C2 and CO additions of enolate 15 to indoles 9

With the dimethyl ketoamidic indole 9g, the most favored pathway involved the enolate attack at the C2 center (ΔG(C2) ‡ = 2.2 kcal.mol−1, Scheme 4, red path). The difference between the attacks at C2 and carbonyl C(O) proved small (ΔΔG‡ = 1.3 kcal.mol−1), in line with the low chemoselectivity observed experimentally with the diethylketoamide substrate 9b (Table 2, entry 1). If the preference for the CO attack is not fully rendered here, probably because of the significant error range in the absence of an explicit solvation of the lithium cation, the trend for a low chemoselectivity is given. Switching to the more sterically hindered diisopropylketoamide indole 9h has little effect on the TS energy barrier for the attack at C2 (ΔG(C2) ‡ = 2.3 kcal.mol−1, Scheme 4, blue path). In contrast, the attack at carbonyl C(O) was much less favored, (ΔG(C2) ‡ = 5.4 kcal.mol−1, Scheme 4), suggesting a higher preference for the formation of the C2 adduct when diisoprolyl substituted indoles are involved, in line with experimental results (Table 2, entries 3–6). Thus, the steric hindrance around the keto group of the ketoamide seems to be the key factor, here. In all cases, compared with the separated substrates, the Van der Waals precomplexes associating the two species (indole and enolate) proved lower in energy than the separated substrates, because of a stabilizing extra coordination between the lithium atom of the lithiated enolate species and the oxygen atom of the indole amide moiety. The additions were exergonic in all cases. The exergonicity was much bigger for the C2‐attack (ΔGr(C2) ≈ −25.0 kcal.mol−1, ΔΔGr (C2 vs CO) > 18 kcal.mol−1). This can be attributed to a higher stabilization of the C2‐adduct, by delocalization of the negative charge of the new enolate anionic species generated on the keto group at position 3, while the negative charge of the C(O) adduct remained localized on the oxygen atom. From a thermodynamical point of view, attack at C2 was preferred whatever the amide's substituents on the indole substrate.

The C—C forming bond in the transition state involving the carbonyl C(O) addition was shorter than that in the transition state involving C2 addition (≈2.2 and ≈2.4 Å for the C(O) and C2 TSs, respectively), indicating later transition states, in accordance with the higher activation barriers. In line with this observation, the pyramidalization of the reacting carbon atom in the transition state corresponding to the C2 addition was slightly more marked than in the one corresponding to the C(O) attack (sum of the three angles around the reacting carbon atom (NC2C3,C3C2H and HC2N) of ≈356° and (C3CO, OCC(O), and (O)CCC3) of ≈354°). Notably, the indole deformation in the C(O) TS was more important than in the C2 one and proved bigger when the steric hindrance of the amide increases (see S.I. for more details).

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results described herein show that nitro substituted (hetero)arenes can react with enolates derived from esters or ketones in a Michael 1,4‐additions to selectively provide the trans diastereoisomer in good to excellent yields. The hydrolysis step is crucial to provide the dearomatized compound and limit the competitive Nef mechanism. 3‐Ketoamidic indole can also react in these enolate addition reactions. A competitive 1,2‐addition on the keto group is possible in this case, but this side‐reaction can be suppressed resorting to sterically hindered amides. Consistent with the experimental results, DFT calculations indicate that the steric hindrance of the indole ketoamide plays a crucial role in chemoselectivity. Additionally, the deformation of the indole required to reach the transition state geometries helps explain the significant impact of steric hindrance on the CO additions.
