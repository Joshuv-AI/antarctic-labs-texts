Improvement of fertility in female mice by treatment with novel telomerase-increasing compounds
Authors: Marina Abramov, Iris Har-Vardi, Lee Assenheim, Sylvia Tsory, Elad Bar Gil, Esther Priel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharmaceutical approaches to enhance female fertility are especially important for women who delay childbearing and experience age-related fertility decline. Previous studies have highlighted the potential role of telomerase in female reproductive health and fertility. Infertility in fourth-generation telomerase knockout mice, which have critically shortened telomeres, highlights the importance of both telomere length and telomerase in reproductive health. Increasing telomerase levels in the ovary shows promise for enhancing fertility by influencing folliculogenesis, oocyte maturation, and ovulation. AGS compounds, designed by us, transiently increase telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) expression and telomerase activity in vitro and in vivo. We therefore examined, for the first time, the effects of AGS compounds on female fertility.MethodsYoung (3–5 months), older (12 months), and TERT-knockout (TERT-KO) female mice were treated with subcutaneous injections of AGS compounds. We then evaluated the effects on ovarian TERT levels, folliculogenesis, ovulation, blood hormone levels, number of embryos and offspring, fertility-related gene expression, and key signaling pathways.ResultsIn both age groups, treatment with AGS increased TERT mRNA levels and telomerase activity in the mouse ovary, leading to faster oocyte maturation and ovulation, as well as higher expression of fertility-related genes. Treatment also elevated plasma levels of progesterone, estrogen, and gonadotropins. Consequently, the number of embryos and offspring increased, with all showing normal development and growth. However, in telomerase-knockout (TERT-KO) mice, AGS treatment did not affect ovarian stage or the expression of certain genes, indicating a TERT-dependent mechanism. Additionally, AGS exert their effects partially through TERT-dependent activation of the Wnt-β-catenin pathway.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that transient telomerase activation in the ovary through pharmaceutical compounds may improve female fertility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12958-026-01544-x.Keywords: Telomerase, AGS compounds, Ovulation, Folliculogenesis, Female fertility, TERT, Reproductive health

Background

A woman’s age is one of the major determinants of fertility. With advancing age, women experience depletion of the ovarian follicular pool, irregular ovulation, and a higher incidence of meiotic errors in oocytes [1]. In addition to these well-known age-related changes, telomere shortening and reduced telomerase activity have also been implicated in the decline of female fertility [2–5].

Telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein (RNP) enzyme complex that protects the ends of the double-stranded DNA by adding hexanucleotide (TTAGGG) repeats to the chromosome ends. It counteracts the progressive telomere shortening that occurs during DNA replication. In the absence of telomerase, telomeres eventually reach a critical length, triggering cellular senescence or apoptosis.

The enzyme consists mainly of a catalytic subunit, telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), and an RNA component (TERC) that provides the template for telomeric DNA synthesis [6–8]. Unlike human tissues, most mouse tissues exhibit active telomerase. However, in both species, telomerase is expressed in germ cells, such as granulosa cells (GCs), stem cells, and cancer cells. Beyond its canonical role in telomere elongation, TERT exerts several non-canonical functions, including protection from oxidative stress [9–12], facilitation of nuclear DNA repair and resistance to apoptosis [13], and regulation of gene expression [14–16]. Experimental activation of telomerase, either through gene therapy or small-molecule activators such as TA-65, has been shown to extend health span in adult mice by re-elongating short telomeres [17, 18].

The crucial role of telomerase in female fertility is evident in granulosa cell proliferation, which relies on proper telomere maintenance; reduced telomerase activity correlates with depletion of primordial follicles [19]. Furthermore, reduced telomerase activity in GCs is associated with apoptosis and follicular atresia [20], and oocyte telomeres have been shown to shorten with age [21, 22]. Similarly, mice deficient in the TERC component of telomerase become infertile by the fourth generation [23, 24]. Collectively, these findings suggest that both telomere integrity and telomerase activity are essential for folliculogenesis and normal ovulation.

Novel telomerase-increasing compounds (AGS), synthesized in our laboratory, transiently increase TERT expression and telomerase activity in human and mouse cells [10, 25]. These compounds act at the transcriptional level by de novo enhancing TERT mRNA levels, which is followed by an increase in TERT protein levels and telomerase activity [10, 25, 26]. Treatment of human granulosa cells (GCs) from women undergoing in vitro fertilization (IVF) significantly increased telomerase expression following AGS exposure [27]. In vivo administration of AGS compounds increased TERT mRNA and telomerase activity in the brains of healthy mice, in a mouse model of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and in an in vitro model of Alzheimer’s disease [25, 26]. AGS compounds protect cells from oxidative stress and other forms of damage in a telomerase-dependent manner, delay the progression of ALS in a human superoxide dismutase 1 (hSOD1) transgenic mouse model, and contribute to the proper functioning of diseased human coronary blood vessels [10, 11, 25]. We also demonstrated that elevating TERT levels in GCs (either through exogenous TERT gene expression or AGS treatment) modulated steroidogenesis and increased the production of steroid hormones such as estrogen in both human and mouse cells [27]. Long-term administration of AGS at various doses in mice produced no apparent toxicity. Animals maintained normal behavior and health over 12 months, and post-mortem examinations revealed no visible tumors [25]. Furthermore, we showed that AGS compounds did not alter the differentiation capacity to the various lineages of human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSC), did not alter the population doubling and did not cause chromosomal aberrations [10].

In the present study, we investigated the effects of AGS treatment on TERT expression and telomerase activity in the ovaries of young and aged female mice, and evaluated its impact on folliculogenesis, oocyte maturation, and ovulation. The results demonstrated increased TERT mRNA levels and telomerase activity in the ovaries following AGS treatment, along with accelerated estrous cycles, enhanced folliculogenesis and ovulation, increased steroid hormone production, and fertility-related gene expression. Consequently, the number of embryos and offspring increased, with all of them showing normal development comparable to controls. No effect was observed in TERT-knockout females, confirming that TERT mediates the fertility-enhancing effects of AGS treatment.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee at Ben-Gurion University and conducted in accordance with the guidelines and regulations (approval numbers IL-07-06-14 and IL-11-09-2018B).

Hsd: ICR (CD-1®) female mice were purchased from Envigo RMS (Israel). Heterozygous TERT (+/–) mice and wild-type B6.129 S-Terttm1Yjc/J controls [28], were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (JAX #005423) to establish the TERT-KO colony (generation 1, 2, 3; G1–G3). Mice were housed under standard conditions (25 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. Females aged 10 days to 12 months were used.

Vaginal smear cytology

Vaginal smear cytology was performed as previously described [29]. Cells were gently collected with double-distilled water, air-dried on glass slides, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Each estrous stage was identified by cell composition [30, 31]: proestrus-predominantly nucleated epithelial cells; estrus-cornified epithelial cells; metestrus-mixed leukocytes and epithelial cells; diestrus-mostly leukocytes.

Treatment with AGS compounds

The chemical structures of AGS compounds (AGS-499, AGS-500, AGS-534) are shown in Figure S1. Mice received a single subcutaneous injection (6 mg/kg dissolved in 0.5% DMSO/PBS, total volume 100 µL) of a single AGS compound (AGS-499, AGS-500, or AGS-534, administered individually) or 0.5% DMSO vehicle as previously described [25]. The compounds were initially dissolved in 100% DMSO and subsequently diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 0.5% DMSO, as pure DMSO cannot be administered to animals. After 12 h, mice were euthanized and ovaries and, where indicated, pituitary glands were collected in ice-cold PBS for analysis. The dose of 6 mg/kg and the 12-hour post-treatment time point was selected based on our previously published time-course data demonstrating peak TERT protein and telomerase activity at this interval and dosage following subcutaneous AGS injection in mice [25].

Preparation of protein extract

Ovaries were homogenized in PBS, centrifuged (1200 rpm, 4 °C, 10 min), and resuspended in CHAPS buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.0, 1 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM PMSF, 0.5% CHAPS, 10% glycerol). After 30 min on ice, lysates were centrifuged (13,500 rpm, 4 °C, 30 min), and supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was measured using a Bio-Rad protein assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Germany).

Telomerase repeat amplification protocol (TRAP) assay

Telomerase activity in the mouse ovaries was evaluated by our slight modification of the TRAP assay [32]. The assay contained three main stages: Telomerase reaction, PCR reaction, and high-resolution agarose mini-gel electrophoresis analysis. Protein extract (0.5 µg/µl) was incubated with 1µl of 10X TRAP assay reaction mix: (20mM Tris-HCl pH 8.2, 63mM KCl, 1.5mM MgCl2, 1mM EDTA, 0.1 mg/mL BSA and 0.05% Tween 20) and 0.1 µg telomerase substrate (TS) primer (5’-AATCCGTCGAGCAGAGTT-3’, 0.1 µg/µl), 2.5mM dNTP’s and UPW to a final volume of 10 µl for 45 min in a 30˚C water bath. The telomerase reaction products were then amplified by PCR using a reaction mix containing 0.1 µg ACX primer (5’-GCGCGGCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCTAACC-3’, 0.2 µg/µl), 1.25 µl Titanium Taq polymerase buffer x10 and 0.25 µl Titanium Taq-polymerase x50. Internal standard primer was used as a control: 0.5 × 10− 15 µg IS primer: (5’AATCCGTCGAGCAGAGTTAAAAGGCCGAGAAGCGAT-3’, 1 × 10− 15 µg/µl) and 0.025 µg ISR primer (5’-ATCGCTTCTCGGCCTTTT-3’, 0.05 µg/µl). PCR cycles 34, as previously described [32] were used for the detection of the products, which were separated on a 4.5% high-resolution agarose mini-gel and detected with Gel-Red solution (X10000 stock solution diluted to X3 working solution with 0.01 M NaCl) for 20 min. The gels were filmed using a UV trans-illuminator digital camera system at 302 nm wavelength. All assays included a CHAPS buffer as a negative control and protein extract from glioblastoma cells as a positive control.

Quantification of telomerase activity

TRAP assay products were quantified by densitometric analysis using the EZQuant software (EZQuant Ltd., Rehovot, Israel) and normalized to IS (Internal standard). We also used positive control (protein extract from cancer cells) in each experiment as a constant reference for evaluating the results. The data, which were normalized to the IS, were calculated as % from the positive control values in each experiment as follows: (Sample/positive control) x 100.

The reduction in telomerase activity was calculated as follows: (relative telomerase activity observed at 3 months / relative telomerase activity at 10 days) × 100. The 55% reduction was calculated as follows: (relative telomerase activity observed at 3 months / relative telomerase activity at 10 days) × 100.

Isolation of total RNA and cDNA preparation

Mice ovaries and pituitary glands were removed and homogenized in a Tri-reagent RNA extraction buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Rehovot, Israel), and RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Pittsburgh PA, USA). The RNA (1000ng) was converted to cDNA with the “Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit” (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Progesterone and estrogen levels in mouse serum

Blood samples were collected from the sacrificed mice, and after centrifugation (3800 rpm, 10 min), the serum was sent to the Clinical Endocrinology Laboratory at Soroka University Medical Center in Beer-Sheva, Israel, to measure progesterone levels.

Histological analysis

The mice ovaries were removed and fixed overnight with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated in increasing ethanol concentrations to xylene, and embedded in paraffin. A rotary microtome was used to produce 5 μm sections, which were mounted on Superfrost Plus slides. The sections were dried, heated at 37˚C overnight, and stored in a dry place. Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through decreasing concentrations of ethanol. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin-eosin or subjected to immunochemical and immunofluorescent assays.

Immunofluorescence

All sections were initially treated with antigen retrieval solution: the sections were heated for 5 min in 10 mM sodium citrate solution pH = 6. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubating the sections with 5% BSA. The primary antibodies used for immunofluorescence analysis were monoclonal mouse anti-PCNA, clone PC 10 sc-56 (Santa Cruz Dallas, Texas USA) diluted 1:200. Monoclonal mouse anti-SF-1 antibody ((A-1): sc-393592 (Santa Cruz Dallas, Texas USA) diluted 1:200. Polyclonal Rabbit anti-BDNF (# AGP-010, Alomone Labs, Israel) diluted 1:100. Sections were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the sections were washed with PBS and PBS containing 0.1% TWEEN 20 (Sigma). The secondary antibody used were anti-PCNA and anti-SF-1 anti-mouse antibody (cy3, diluted 1:200) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA). The secondary antibody used for anti-BDNF was anti-rabbit immunoglobulin (Alexa Fluor 488, diluted 1:200) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA). The secondary antibody was applied for 2 h at room temperature. The sections were washed with PBS, and then PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 was applied for 10 min. DAPI (Sigma) was applied for 10 min. Finally, the sections were washed and covered with water-based mounting medium. The sections were analyzed using the Panoramic Midi II scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary).

Rabbit monoclonal anti-TERT antibody (ab32020, Abcam, Cambridge MA, USA) diluted 1:400. Rabbit monoclonal anti-β-catenin antibody (β-Catenin (6B3) Rabbit mAb #9582, Cell Signaling technology, Danvers, MA, USA) diluted 1:100. Rabbit polyclonal anti-CREM antibody ((X-12): sc-440, Santa Cruz Dallas, Texas USA) diluted 1:100. The avidin–biotin–peroxidase complex (ABC) method was performed on the section. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked by incubating the sections in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min at room temperature, and the tissue samples were then heated in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) for 5 min. The section was then incubated with horse serum for one h at room temperature, followed by incubation with the primary anti-TERT antibody at a concentration of 1:400 overnight at 4 °C. The secondary antibody, a biotinylated horse anti-rabbit antibody, and Vectastain from the Universal ABC-HRP Kit (Vector Laboratories; Burlingame, CA, USA) were each applied for 10 min. Finally, the sections were incubated in 3’3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB Peroxidase Substrate, Vector Laboratories, and Burlingame, CA USA) for 2 min, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Negative controls were performed by replacing the primary antibody with non-immune serum.

Pairings between male and female mice

Female ICR mice were treated with a subcutaneous injection of AGS-499, AGS-500 and AGS-534 6 mg/kg or with DMSO 0.5%. Immediately following the injection, the female mouse was placed in the cage of the male mouse, and 14 days later, it was sacrificed, and the number of embryos in the uterus was determined.

Offspring monitoring

Three-month-old female mice (at the proestrus stage) were treated by a single subcutaneous injection with either AGS-500, AGS-534, or DMSO as a vehicle. Untreated mice were used as controls. Following the AGS treatments, female mice were immediately paired with age-matched males. The number of offspring was determined, and we followed the development of the progeny from 8 days of age until 30 days postnatally. Mouse tail length and weight were measured twice a week, and fur growth was visually evaluated. At the age of 30 days, mice were euthanized, and the hearts, spleens, ovaries, pituitary gland, and brains of the mice were removed and weighed. Each organ coefficient was calculated as organ coefficient=organ weight/body weight x100%.

Genotyping and mating to progressive generations of TERT knockout mice

Mice were placed in cages at temperatures of 22 ± 2 °C and 65% humidity. Food and water supply were made available, and a 12 h light/dark cycle was maintained. Heterozygous Tert+/− mice were mated to produce WT and G1 Tert−/− mice, then G1 Tert−/− were crossed to obtain G2 Tert−/− mice, which were crossed to generate G3 Tert−/− mice.

DNA isolation

DNA was extracted from AGS-treated and untreated mice ovaries using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN (Qiagen # 69504, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Pittsburgh PA, USA). Isolated DNA was used to assess telomere length.

Telomere length

The average telomere length of target genomic DNA samples from treated and untreated mice ovaries was evaluated by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) in a LightCycler480 II Real-Time PCR system (Roche Diagnostics International, Rotkreuz, Switzerland) using SYBR® Green assay kits (ScienCell’s Absolute Mouse Telomere Length Quantification qPCR Assay Kit [AHTLQ M8918], Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the recommendations of the manufacturers. For each DNA sample, two consecutive reactions were performed: for the telomere sequence and for a single-copy reference (SCR) gene. The SCR primer set recognizes and amplifies a 100-bp-long region on human chromosome 17, serving as a reference for data normalization. Each PCR reaction contained genomic DNA sample (1 ng), telomere primer, 2x qPCR master mix, and nuclease-free water. Reference genomic DNA was used as an internal control. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and template controls were included in each run. The PCR conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 10 min followed by 32 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 52 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 45 s. The average telomere length was calculated and measured by the ΔΔCt method, in which the telomere length is calculated relatively to the reference sample according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cells

LβT2-Gonadotrope cells derived from pituitary tumors in transgenic mice were kindly provided by the laboratory of Prof. Zvi Naor (Tel Aviv University) and were maintained in a 75 cm² flask in DMEM + 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, 1% L-Glutamine, and 10% FBS at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was assessed by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc test or Student’s t-test (GraphPad Prism 6.0, GraphPad Software). p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

AGS compounds increased TERT mRNA levels in the ovaries of young and old mice at all estrous cycle stages

In this study, we evaluated the effect of three AGS derivatives (AGS-499, AGS-500, AGS-534; Figure S1), which share a tri-aryl core but differ in R groups, on ovarian TERT expression and telomerase activity across estrous stages. To investigate whether TERT mRNA levels are influenced by the stage of the estrous cycle in mouse ovaries, we initially identified the estrous cycle stages (in 3-month-old mice), prior to sacrifice, using standard vaginal smear techniques (see Figure S2A-D). TERT mRNA levels were quantified by real-time PCR using specific mTERT primers. The results indicated that TERT mRNA levels in mouse ovaries remained relatively stable across various stages of the estrous cycle at the age of 3 months (Figure S2E). Telomerase activity in female mouse ovaries was assessed at different ages (10 days, 18 days, 1 month, and 3 months; n = 8 per group) using a modified TRAP assay [32]. Quantification was performed through densitometric analysis with EZquant software, following established protocols [10, 25, 33]. Telomerase activity showed a mild, non-significant 15% rise between day 10 and 18, then declined significantly, reaching 55% reduction at 3 months of age (Figure S3A, S3B).

In a previous study, we demonstrated the capability of AGS compounds, novel telomerase-increasing agents, to augment TERT mRNA levels, TERT protein levels, and telomerase activity in vivo in the brains of mice [25, 27]. Here, we extended this approach to the ovary, treating young (3–5 months) and aged (12 months) female mice with a single subcutaneous dose (6 mg/kg) of AGS-499/AGS-500/AGS-534, or vehicle (0.5% DMSO), or no treatment as control [25, 26]. Administration of AGS compounds led to increased TERT mRNA levels across all estrous cycle stages in young-adult mice (3–5 months old) (Fig. 1A-D) and at the estrus stage in older mice (12 months old) (Fig. 1E). In young adult mice, AGS treatment significantly upregulated TERT mRNA relative to vehicle: proestrus up to 1.52 ± 0.09-fold (AGS-534, p < 0.0001); metestrus up to 1.8 ± 0.39-fold (AGS-499, p < 0.0005); diestrus up to 1.55 ± 0.15-fold (AGS-500, p < 0.0005; Fig. 1A–C respectively; Table S1). Comparison of AGS effects at the estrus stage between young adult and old mice revealed a significantly greater increase in TERT mRNA levels in old mice. In young-adult mice 1.32 ± 0.06 fold, p < 0.0001 for AGS-499; 1.36 ± 0.1 fold, p < 0.0005 for AGS-500; 1.3 ± 0.05 fold, p < 0.0001 for AGS-534 (Fig. 1D and Table S1) while in old mice TERT increased by 2 ± 0.5 fold, p < 0.0001 for AGS-500 ; 2 ± 0.9 fold, p < 0.005 for AGS-534 (Fig. 1E and Table S1). TRAP assay confirmed proportional increases in telomerase activity following AGS treatment (a representative gel image is shown in Figure S4): proestrus up to 1.46 ± 0.09-fold (AGS-499, p < 0.05); metestrus up to 1.55 ± 0.02-fold (AGS-499, p < 0.001); estrus up to 1.25 ± 0.03-fold (AGS-534, p < 0.001); diestrus up to 1.38 ± 0.06-fold (AGS-500, p < 0.001; Fig. 1F–I respectively; Table S1).

AGS compounds increased ovarian size and weight at each stage of the estrous cycle and accelerated the estrous cycle

Mouse ovaries (3–5 months old) from the above-described experiments were weighed prior to the various analyses. We observed an increase (up to 22 ± 0.9%, p < 0.005 for AGS-500 treatment) in the size and weight of the ovaries following AGS treatment compared to control mice (untreated and DMSO) at all estrous stages (Fig. 1J and K). It should be noted that typically, without treatment, the difference between the weights of the ovaries at the proestrus stage and that at the diestrus stage was 39%.

To examine the biological effects of a single dose of AGS compounds on the estrous cycle of young-adult mice, we determined the estrous cycle stage prior and 12 h after the treatment. The results presented in Figure S5 (the composition of cells in the vaginal smears) and summarized in Table 1 showed that AGS treatment accelerated the estrous cycle since mice injected with a single dose of the AGS compounds in the proestrus (P) or estrus (E) stages were found to be in the metestrus stage (12 h post-treatment), while the control untreated or vehicle-treated mice were at the estrus stage (12 h post injection). No changes in the estrous cycle stage were observed (12 h post-treatment) when the compounds were injected at the diestrus or metestrus stages. Interestingly, examination of the effect of AGS treatment on the estrous cycle in old mice revealed an acceleration in the estrous cycle similarly to that observed in the young adult mice (Table 1).

A single AGS treatment did not affect the length of telomeres in the ovary

Studies have shown that in telomerase KO mice (TERC-/- or TERT-/-) telomere shortening occurs [37–39]. We evaluated the telomere length in the ovaries of TERT KO mice from the first generation (G1) to the third generation (G3) compared to wild-type mice at the same age. The results showed that the telomeres progressively shortened across TERT KO generations by 42% from 29.5 ± 2.9 kb for WT to 17.2 ± 1.5 kb, p < 0.05 for G3 (Fig. 6H). To determine whether AGS treatment (as administered in this study, namely a single injection and examination 12 h post-treatment) has any effect on telomere length, we analyzed the length of telomeres in the ovaries of AGS-treated WT mice and found that the UT, DMSO-treated and AGS-treated mice exhibited similar telomere length 12 h after treatment (33.4 ± 4, 32.3 ± 4. 31.8 ± 5 kb for UT, DMSO and AGS respectively (Fig. 6I).

The expression level of LH and FSH in the pituitary gland following AGS treatment was compatible with the ovarian state

The pituitary glands from the young-adult AGS-treated and untreated mice (described in Fig. 2) were also removed, and the levels of LH and FSH mRNA were determined by qRT-PCR (Fig. 7A, B and C). The results showed that the levels of LH and FSH mRNA in the pituitary gland following AGS treatment were compatible with the estrous stage of the ovary obtained 12 h post AGS treatment. Mice treated with AGS at the proestrus stage exhibited higher levels of LH mRNA by 1.24 fold (from 0.37 ± 0.05 RQ for UT to 0.46 ± 0.01 RQ for AGS, p < 0.05) and FSH by 1.16fold (from 0.025 ± 0.002 RQ for UT to 0.029 ± 0.002 RQ for AGS, p < 0.05), which was compatible with the ovarian state as seen in Fig. 2A. In mice treated with AGS at the estrus stage, the levels of LH and FSH mRNA were found to be significantly lower (by 3-fold) compared to those in control, untreated mice (Fig. 7B). This is compatible with the metestrus stage of the ovaries seen in AGS-treated mice (Fig. 2B). The FSH mRNA level was significantly higher (by 4-fold), and LH mRNA was slightly lower in AGS-treated mice at the metestrus stage, 12 h after treatment, compared to control, untreated mice (Fig. 7C). These data were consistent with the state of the ovaries which demonstrated the presence of many activated follicles in AGS treated mice (see Fig. 2C). We also examined the effects of AGS compounds on the expression of various steroidogenesis genes in the ovary, at each estrous stage, including LHR, FSHR, 3B-Hsd, and Cyp19A1 [40–42]. The data showed that 12 h after AGS treatment, the expression pattern of the steroidogenesis genes corresponded to the ovarian phase (follicular or luteal) (Figure S6). We also found that treatment of mouse pituitary cell culture (LβT2) with AGS compounds increased the levels of TERT and LH mRNA by AGS-499 6 h after treatment and by AGS-500 and AGS-534 12 h after treatment (Figure S7). The data demonstrates distinct time-dependent effects among the three compounds in these specific cells. AGS-499 increased TERT and LH levels after 6 h of treatment (Figure S7A and S7B), but its effects diminished after 12 h (Figure S7C and S7D). In contrast, AGS-500 and AGS-534 enhanced TERT and LH levels at the 12-hour mark (Figure S7C and S7D), showing no effects at 6 h. These data suggested that increasing TERT by AGS compounds enhanced steroidogenesis and the production of steroid hormones and gonadotropins, thereby accelerating the estrous cycle.

Treatment with AGS compounds induced the secretion of progesterone and estrogen

To determine whether the increase in the number of CL following AGS treatment produced functional CL that secrete progesterone, blood samples were taken 12 h post-treatment from the sacrificed mice, and the serum was analyzed for progesterone levels. The results showed a significant increase of 15-fold in the blood progesterone level in mice treated with AGS at the proestrus stage compared to DMSO or UT (from 2.6 ± 0.1ng/ml for UT and 1.74 ± 0.1ng/ml for DMSO to 39.2 ± 1 ng/ml for AGS, p < 0.05 (Fig. 7D). These data are consistent with the presence of numerous CL in AGS-treated ovaries and with the finding that a high level of progesterone secretion occurs during the beginning of the metestrus stage [43, 44]. Furthermore, we examined estrogen levels in blood samples following AGS treatment at the luteal phase (metestrus). Results showed that 12 h post-treatment, a significant increase in estrogen levels in the blood was detected (AGS-499 up to 1.26-fold, AGS-500 up to 1.9-fold, and AGS-534 up to 1.8-fold) (Fig. 7E).

Activation of telomerase by AGS compounds does not affect the ovarian reserve

To examine whether telomerase activation by AGS compounds caused an increase in the number of recruitment follicles and thus affects the ovarian reserve, we examined the effect of AGS on the expression of Amh and Foxo3a genes that participate in the regulation of primordial recruitment. AMH (secreted by the small antral follicles) correlates strongly with the ovarian reserve. Amh plays a role in maintaining quiescence by inhibiting the transition from primordial to primary follicles. Foxo3a is also important for maintaining the quiescence of primordial follicles and is required for the maintenance of the ovarian reserve in mice. Following AGS treatment, no change was observed in the mRNA levels of Amh and Foxo3a compared to the control (Figure S8A, S8B). The results suggested that AGS treatment did not affect the ovarian reserve in female mice.

We previously showed that treatment with the AGS compounds activates the Wnt-β-catenin signaling pathway in the hippocampus of AGS-treated mice [26]. While our data suggest that AGS can activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, whether this occurs directly downstream of TERT or through intermediate signaling events remains to be determined. Future studies employing pathway inhibitors or conditional knockout models will be necessary to delineate the precise molecular link between TERT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling in the ovary. Here, as shown in Fig. 9 (A, B, and G), we determined the activation of the Wnt-β-catenin pathway in the ovaries of the same mice described above, starting with the expression of β-catenin. Immunostaining analysis of the ovarian tissue using a specific anti-β-catenin antibody also revealed a significant increase of β-catenin protein staining in the granulosa cells of antral follicles and in the luteal granulosa cells in the CL of AGS-500 or AGS-534 treated mice compared to control mice (UT or DMSO) (Fig. 9G). To confirm that the Wnt-β-catenin pathway is activated, we examined the expression of cyclin D1, whose expression is elevated by the Wnt-β -catenin signaling pathway [51]. Indeed, the level of cyclin D1 mRNA in the ovaries of AGS-treated mice significantly increased compared to control mice (Fig. 9B). At proestrus and estrus stages AGS-500 increased cyclin D1 mRNA level by 183 ± 18% p < 0.005 and AGS-534 by 163 ± 18% p < 0.05. At the metestrus stage 12 h post-treatment, both derivatives of AGS significantly increased Cyclin D1 mRNA level by 150 ± 20% p < 0.05. We next examined the expression of steroidogenic factor-1 (Sf-1) which is involved in steroidogenesis regulation, is essential for proper reproduction [52–56] and its activity is modulated by β-catenin [48, 57–62]. Our results demonstrated that 12 h post-treatment with AGS-500, at the proestrus stage, the mRNA level of Sf-1 significantly decreased by 33%, and by 40% with AGS-534. 12 h post-treatment with AGS-500 at the estrus stage, Sf-1 mRNA level was significantly reduced by 39%, and by 21% with AGS-534, while 12 h post-treatment at the metestrus stage Sf-1 mRNA level significantly increased to 177 ± 31% p < 0.005 with AGS-500 and to 166 ± 17% p < 0.005 with AGS-534 compared to vehicle (Fig. 9C). We also examined the effect of AGS treatment on Sf-1 protein using immunofluorescence analysis with a specific anti-Sf-1 antibody. We observed the same results as with the level of Sf-1 mRNA. 12 h post-treatment with AGS, at the proestrus stage, a decrease in Sf-1 staining was observed compared to vehicle-treated or UT mice. As can be seen in Fig. 9E, Sf-1 protein staining is observed mainly in granulosa cells of ovarian follicles at different stages of development in UT or DMSO-treated mice, which indicates the follicular phase. However, 12 h post-AGS treatment, the ovaries exhibited the luteal phase, with more CL and less Sf-1 staining. Gonadotropins induce cAMP signals in granulosa cells, which regulate the transcriptional responses of multiple ovarian genes, including those encoding estrogen and aromatase, that can be modulated by Sf-1 [48, 57, 58]. cAMP regulates genes that encode transcription factors, such as the cAMP-responsive element modulator (CREM). CREM gene products may be a key mechanistic component for granulosa cell differentiation and proliferation [63–69]. We therefore determined the expression of CREM in mouse ovaries following AGS treatment. The results depicted in Fig. 9D revealed that the level of CREM mRNA significantly increased up to 150 ± 16% p < 0.005 in AGS-500-treated mice at all stages compared to control mice. 12 h post-treatment with AGS-534 showed a significant increase in CREM expression up to 216 ± 30 p < 0.005 at the proestrus stage, up to 132 ± 8% p < 0.005 at the estrus stage, and up to 241 ± 44% p < 0.05 at the metestrus stage. We also examined the effect of AGS treatment on CREM protein levels using immunostaining analysis with a specific anti-CREM antibody. The results showed a significant increase in CREM staining in the GC of the follicles and in the CL of AGS-treated mice compared to the control (Fig. 9F).

Discussion

Several studies have highlighted the significance of telomerase activity and telomere length in maintaining fertility. In TERT or TERC knockout mice, the absence of telomerase activity resulted in significantly shortened telomeres by the fourth generation, which was associated with infertility [70, 71]. When telomerase expression was reactivated in TERT-depleted mice through genetic manipulation, telomeres were elongated, and fertility was restored [70]. In this study, we showed that the activity of telomerase in the ovaries changed with age and declined by 55% at the age of 3 months. Indeed, it has been shown that relative telomerase activity in oocytes from reproductively aged females is significantly lower than that in oocytes from young females [21]. It was also demonstrated that small activated growing follicles contained high telomerase activity, which significantly declined in mature follicles [4]. The presence of active telomerase is essential in follicles since the loss of telomerase activity in any growing stage of the follicles leads to atresia [20] and female mice with telomerase deficiency exhibit a low size and weight of the ovary, low ovulated oocyte numbers, and poor embryonic development [72]. In human ovaries, the decline of telomerase activity with age has been attributed to primordial follicle depletion [19]. Therefore, we hypothesized that a controlled increase in telomerase expression in mouse ovaries may contribute to their fertility. We previously demonstrated that AGS compounds transiently increased the expression and activity of telomerase in various human and mouse cells (including granulosa cells) in vitro and in vivo in a dose and time-dependent manner [10, 25–27]. The increase in telomerase by these compounds protected various cell types (in vitro and in vivo) from oxidative stress [10, 25], delayed the onset and progression of neurodegenerative disease (ALS) [25], protected neurons from amyloid beta toxicity [26] and contributed to the re-establishment of a physiological mechanism of vasodilation in arterioles from human subjects with coronary artery disease [11]. We also showed that treatment with AGS compounds of GCs, derived from women who underwent IVF procedures, enhanced the production of estrogen and affected steroidogenesis in mouse cells [27]. In this study, we utilized three derivatives of the AGS compounds, which share the same three aryl structures but differ in the R groups (Figure S1). We used the three AGS compounds for several reasons: (1) to confirm the necessity of the three-aryl structure for the increase in TERT mRNA in vivo in the ovary (we observed similar results in cell culture), and (2) to investigate whether the biological effects of the three different AGS compounds on the ovary in vivo vary, as small molecules with different R groups may produce distinct biological outcomes. The AGS compounds were specifically synthesized for our study. At the outset, all three derivatives (AGS-499, AGS-500, and AGS-534) were used in parallel experiments to confirm that the shared tri-aryl core structure, rather than the specific R groups, was responsible for the observed increase in TERT mRNA and telomerase activity. Once it became evident that all three compounds produced broadly similar biological results with no significant differences among them, they were used interchangeably in subsequent experiments based on availability, given that the study was conducted over a period of seven years. In addition, in this study, a single dose of AGS compound was sufficient to establish the desired effect on female fertility, while in chronic diseases, like ALS, repeated daily doses were required to observe the delay in the onset and progression of the disease [25].

Here, we demonstrated for the first time that treatment of female mice (young and old) with any of the three derivatives of AGS compounds increased telomerase activity and TERT mRNA levels (up to 1.5-fold for telomerase activity and up to 2-fold for TERT mRNA levels) in the ovary 12 h after treatment. These compounds increased telomerase in the ovaries when injected at each of the estrous stages (proestrus, estrus, metestrus, and diestrus). No significant differences were observed in the increased levels of both TERT mRNA and telomerase activity (Fig. 1) or in TERT protein intensity (Fig. 4) among the three AGS compounds. This suggested that the three aryl structures play a crucial role in enhancing TERT mRNA levels and promoting telomerase activity. The effect of these telomerase-increasing compounds on female fertility was examined using various parameters, including their influence on the estrous cycle, folliculogenesis, oocyte maturation, and ovulation. We also assessed steroid hormone production and its regulation by gonadotropin gene expression and the participation of signaling pathways such as the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Additionally, we examined their effect on the number of embryos and offspring, as well as their development. It is worth noting that the three AGS compounds exhibited generally similar biological activity, with only slight, insignificant differences.

Mice that were at the proestrus or estrous stages at the time of AGS treatment were found to be, 12 h post-treatment, at the beginning or the end of the metestrus stage, while control mice (untreated or vehicle-treated) were at the estrus stage, suggesting that the estrous cycle was accelerated in the AGS treated mice by approximately 36 h. Examination of ovarian histology of treated and control (DMSO and untreated) mice showed that indeed the ovaries of AGS-treated mice contained many CL, which were formed from the ovarian follicles after ovulation and were typical of the metestrus stage. Therefore, since CLs are the consequence of ovulation, this indicated that AGS treatment likely accelerated the ovulation process when given at the pre-ovulatory stage. In addition, many activated follicles that showed proliferation of granulosa cells (as revealed by staining with the cell proliferation marker PCNA) were observed when mice were treated with AGS at proestrus/estrus or metestrus/diestrus stages. These activated follicles demonstrated an intense staining with anti-TERT antibody, indicating an increase in TERT protein level in the proliferating granulosa cells in the activated follicles following AGS treatments. Taken together, these data strongly suggest that AGS compounds enhance folliculogenesis, probably by increasing telomerase activity in the granulosa cells. Indeed, when TERT KO mice were similarly treated with the AGS compounds, no effect on the ovaries was detected, indicating that the effect of AGS treatment on accelerating the estrous cycle and enhancing folliculogenesis and ovulation is TERT-dependent. Notably, the absence of an estrous cycle stage change in mice treated at the metestrus or diestrus stages is entirely consistent with these findings and, in fact, reinforces the specificity of the AGS effect. AGS treatment accelerated the estrous cycle only when administered at the pre-ovulatory stages (proestrus and estrus), which are the stages at which follicular maturation and ovulation are actively occurring. At post-ovulatory stages (metestrus and diestrus), the biological context is not conducive to further ovulation-promoting effects, and therefore no stage advancement was expected or observed.

We examined the level of progesterone in the blood of various AGS-treated and untreated mice and observed that, 12 h post-AGS treatment, administered at the proestrus stage, a high blood progesterone level (up to 15-fold compared to the control) was detected. These data were compatible with the increase in the number of CL in the ovaries of AGS-treated mice and indicate that the CL formed by AGS are indeed active and can produce progesterone. Moreover, we found that the blood estrogen level increased following AGS treatment at the luteal phase. We also examined the expression of steroidogenic genes that regulate steroid hormone secretion in the ovary of AGS-treated mice. We found that FSHR, LHR, and key steroidogenic enzymes: 3B-HSD and CYP19A1 mRNA levels in the ovary were compatible with the stages of the ovaries and the estrous cycle stage of the AGS-treated or untreated mice (Figure S5). These data were also compatible with our previous study, which demonstrated the influence of increasing TERT in GCs on the enhancement of the steroidogenesis process [27]. Since folliculogenesis and ovulation are controlled by gonadotropins secreted from the pituitary gland, we examined these hormone levels following AGS treatment. We found that the mRNA level of either FSH or LH in the pituitary gland was also compatible with the stages of the ovaries and the estrous cycle stage of the AGS-treated or untreated mice. These results suggested that the enhancement of folliculogenesis and ovulation observed in the ovaries of AGS-treated mice was also confirmed by the expression pattern of gonadotropins LH/FSH genes in the pituitary gland. The ability of AGS compounds to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and transiently increase telomerase in the brain has been previously demonstrated [25]. It was therefore possible that the expression of TERT in the pituitary gland was influenced by AGS. Indeed, in a mouse pituitary cell culture (LβT2), we found that treatment of these cells with AGS increased the levels of LH (Figure S6). Therefore, it is possible that AGS compounds increased telomerase expression in both the ovaries and the pituitary gland, thereby enhancing folliculogenesis and ovulation by affecting the production of gonadotropins, steroidogenesis, and the proliferation of granulosa cells. To substantiate these findings, we examined the expression of genes that were suggested as markers for follicle development and oocyte maturation. Indeed, we demonstrated that the Rnf24 gene, which is involved in oocyte growth and maturation [73, 74], increased by up to 2-fold in AGS-treated mice compared to the control. The expression of the B4galt2 gene increased up to three-fold in AGS-treated mice, and this gene has been shown to be involved in the development of primordial follicles [75, 76]. The Rab23 gene expression increased up to 2-fold in AGS-treated mice, and this gene is associated with the development of follicles and oocyte growth [77]. The BTC gene, which is involved in the ovulation process [78], increased up to 2-fold in AGS-treated mice. The expression of the BDNF gene also increased by up to 2-fold in AGS-treated mice compared to the control. BDNF gene expression is positively correlated with the number of mature oocytes [79], and contributes to luteal granulosa cells’ survival in the active corpus luteum [80]. The level of BDNF protein was elevated, as evidenced by the increased staining intensity observed in the immunofluorescence (IF) procedure, particularly in the CL after AGS treatment, indicating that new CL are activated. These results strongly support the histology and immunofluorescent results, suggesting that the enhancement of folliculogenesis and ovulation following a single treatment with telomerase-increasing compounds is indeed accompanied by an increase in specific genes, whose encoded proteins are required for these processes. No effect on the expression of these genes was detected in TERT KO mice at the age of 3–5 months following AGS treatment, indicating that the effect of AGS treatment on gene expression depends on the presence of TERT. We did not investigate the impact of AGS compounds on gene expression in older TERT KO mice. However, since we did not observe any effects of AGS compounds on gene expression or ovarian stage in 3–5-month-old TERT KO mice, which still retain some ovarian activity, it is unlikely that such effects would be present in older TERT KO mice, where ovarian function has significantly declined.

It should be noted that BDNF has been reported to modulate telomerase activity in embryonic hippocampal neurons [81], and that downregulation of TERT expression in the rat model also exhibited downregulation of the hippocampal BDNF expression [82, 83]. Our data are compatible with these findings, showing that the expression of BDNF was significantly reduced in the ovaries of TERT+/- and TERT-/- compared to the WT. We also demonstrated that enhancement of TERT levels with AGS compounds in WT mice significantly increased BDNF levels but not in TERT KO mice. These data strongly suggested that increasing TERT expression and telomerase activity modulated and upregulated BDNF expression. Our data also reveal the importance of TERT in folliculogenesis and the ovulation process, as we have shown that the expression levels of these associated genes and proteins significantly decrease in TERT+/- and TERT-/- relative to WT.

It is known that BDNF is regulated by the Wnt/β-catenin signaling [84]. The involvement of Wnt signaling in steroidogenesis in the ovary was demonstrated by several studies [85–88]. We examined the Wnt signaling pathway following AGS treatment and found that the levels of both the β-catenin gene and protein are increased. Moreover, the cyclin D1 gene, known to be regulated by Wnt-β-catenin signaling [51, 89, 90], was also increased in AGS-treated mice compared to controls. Previous studies have demonstrated that the activation of β-catenin can expedite FSH-mediated effects in ovarian follicular cells [49, 50, 91]. Conditional deletion of β-catenin in mouse granulosa cells resulted in an impaired ability of FSH to stimulate CYP19A1 expression, consequently influencing estradiol production [86]. β-catenin is also linked with the LH-mediated production of progesterone from CL [88]. It was also revealed that there is an interaction between gonadotropin and WNT/CTNNB1 signaling in granulosa cells and in luteal cells [50]. Gonadotropin hormones activate the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway [63, 92]; therefore, we scrutinized one of the members of the CREB family, the cAMP-responsive element modulator (CREM), a transcription factor that binds to the cAMP-responsive element (CRE) [64–66, 93]. The results demonstrated that CREM mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated at all estrous stages following AGS treatment. Moreover, it was reported that Sf-1 cooperates with β-catenin to activate the transcription of 3βHSD, CYP19A1, and StAR gene expression, and that the interaction between β-catenin and Sf-1 is essential for the FSH and cAMP cascades [48, 57, 58]. Accordingly, we analyzed Sf-1 (steroidogenic factor 1) mRNA levels following AGS treatment at various stages. The results revealed that 12 h post-treatment with AGS compounds at the follicular phase, the expression of the Sf-1 gene and protein levels were reduced compared to UT or vehicle-treated mice. In contrast, 12 h post-treatment with AGS at the luteal phase, the expression levels of Sf-1 increased significantly compared to control mice. It is well known that steroidogenic factor-1 (Sf-1) plays a crucial role in the signaling pathway that regulates aromatase genes in granulosa cells during the follicular phase [94–100]. At the luteal stage, after the LH surge, Sf-1 protein rapidly decreases with the downregulation of aromatase [101–103]. Our results support the idea that the high level of Sf-1 observed in granulosa cells regulates CYP19A1 expression and E2 synthesis in response to FSH stimulation. Following AGS treatment, there is an acceleration of the luteal phase, and we observed a decrease in the amount of Sf-1, which is compatible with aromatase gene expression. This evidence further supports our results and substantiates the notion that telomerase-increasing compounds can accelerate folliculogenesis and the ovulation process with a single treatment. Although our study did not employ Sf-1 knockout models, the observed pattern of Sf-1 regulation (with decreased expression during the accelerated luteal transition and increased expression at post-ovulatory stages) is consistent with its established role downstream of β-catenin in mediating FSH and cAMP signaling cascades [60, 95, 97, 102]. Future experiments using Sf-1 knockout or conditional Sf-1-deficient granulosa cells would help to clarify whether the steroidogenic effects of AGS are mediated directly through Sf-1 or through parallel pathways. Importantly, the finding that AGS treatment did not alter the expression of AMH or Foxo3a (two well-established markers of primordial follicle quiescence and ovarian reserve) suggests that the fertility-enhancing effect is highly selective. AGS appears to promote the maturation and ovulation of follicles that have already been recruited into the growing pool, without triggering additional primordial follicle activation. This distinction is clinically meaningful, as it indicates that AGS does not deplete the ovarian reserve but rather optimizes the efficiency of the existing follicular cohort.

We suggest that the effect of AGS compounds on the ovary and female fertility is primarily due to the non-canonical role of TERT, as no difference was observed in telomere length in the ovaries following AGS treatment compared to the control mice under the study conditions (a single dose). However, it should be considered that the sensitivity of the method used for telomere length measurement after a single treatment may be limited, and other, more sensitive methods like FISH [17] might show a slight increase in telomere length. So, it is possible that an increase in telomere length also contributes to the AGS effects on the ovary. It is worth noting that we previously demonstrated that long-term treatment with AGS compounds in human mesenchymal stem cells significantly and consistently increased telomere length [10]. We assume that AGS compounds increased TERT in the ovaries and thus enhanced folliculogenesis and ovulation by affecting the production of gonadotropins, the steroidogenesis, and the proliferation of granulosa cells. The ovaries of the AGS-treated mice showed an increase in CL, indicating the ovulation of multiple oocytes. To examine the viability of these oocytes, we investigated their ability to be fertilized and develop into vital embryos and live offspring. AGS-treated female mice were mated with untreated male mice. The results clearly showed that the number of embryos in the uteri of these mice increased, suggesting that the oocytes, ovulated following AGS treatment, can be fertilized to produce embryos. Moreover, the average number of live offspring per mouse also increased in AGS-treated mice, up to 3 more live offspring. The growth and development of the offspring derived from AGS-treated mice were like those of the offspring derived from untreated mice.

Furthermore, no significant changes were detected in the weight, morphology, or presence of abnormal growths (such as tumors) in the various organs examined from these offspring.

Altogether, these results indicate that the AGS treatment of female mice did not affect the normal development of their offspring. In previous studies, we demonstrated that long-term treatments with AGS compounds were non-toxic and did not induce chromosomal aberrations [10, 25]. Examination of the various organs after long-term AGS treatment did not show visible tumors. Recent findings indicate that enhancing telomerase through gene therapy does not accelerate tumorigenesis in the context of K-Ras oncogene-induced lung cancer [104]. Here, we used a single AGS treatment, which transiently increased TERT mRNA and protein levels for a short period. Since TERT is not classified as an oncogene and is essential for maintaining genome stability, it can be inferred that the risk of developing cancer following a single AGS treatment is very low. The transient nature of AGS activity is further supported by pharmacokinetic considerations: although the formal half-life of AGS has not been determined in mice, data from other species indicate an approximate half-life of 3–4 h in rats. Consistent with this, our time-course data show that TERT expression returns to baseline levels by 48 h post-treatment in mice, confirming that the biological window of AGS activity is transient. Nevertheless, although the single-dose regimen used in this study showed no tumorigenic effects, long-term safety remains an important consideration for clinical translation.

We present a model outlining how increased TERT levels induced by AGS compounds can enhance the secretion of steroid hormones, stimulate cell proliferation, and facilitate oocyte maturation and ovulation (See Fig. 10). We suggest that increasing TERT expression in the ovary by AGS activates the Wnt signaling pathway. This activation leads to an increased expression of BDNF in the ovary. In addition, β-catenin can cooperate with Sf-1 to enhance FSH stimulation, thereby regulating CYP19A1 expression and E2 synthesis, or regulating 3BHSD expression and progesterone synthesis through the activation of the cAMP/PKA/CREB/CREM signaling pathway.

A proposed model for AGS activity in the ovary. Proposed mechanisms by which a transient increase in TERT expression in the ovary of mouse treated with a single dose of AGS compound can promote secretion of steroid hormones, cell proliferation, oocyte maturation and ovulation

Estrogen (E2) promotes the proliferation of granulosa and theca cells, facilitating follicular development and oocyte maturation. Theca cells are located outside the granulosa layers, separated by the follicular basement membrane. They produce androgens, which are converted to estrogens by the granulosa cells, thereby promoting further follicular growth and oocyte maturation. Also, estrogen and progesterone regulate LH production [105], which, in turn, induces growth factors (EGF, BDNF, and IGF-1), which are essential for oocyte maturation [106].

In addition, estrogen activates TERT transcription by binding to the ligand-activated estrogen receptor at the estrogen-responsive element (ERE) in the TERT promoter [107–109]. EGF is required for the upregulation of telomerase activity, which also requires ERK1/2 kinase signaling [110, 111]. Moreover, telomerase (TERT expression and activity) might be a significant mediator of BDNF through the activation of PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK1/2 pathways [81, 112]. Furthermore, the non-canonical roles of TERT, such as a pro-survival function resulting in increased resistance to DNA damage and decreased apoptosis, are also enhanced by the AGS compounds. All of this together promotes cell survival and proliferation of granulosa and theca cells in the ovary, thereby enhancing folliculogenesis and ovulation (Fig. 10).

Conclusion

We clearly showed that the observed effects of AGS compounds on the ovaries of young and old mice were mediated by TERT, and the noncanonical functions of TERT are involved. However, the possible effect of AGS on telomere elongation cannot be excluded. The data suggested that a transient increase in telomerase expression by the AGS compounds in the ovaries, at pre-ovulatory stages, increased ovulation, and the ovulated oocytes were vital and could be fertilized, producing vital offspring with normal growth and development. Additionally, increasing telomerase at post-ovulatory stages enhanced folliculogenesis. We showed that the acceleration in the estrous cycle is likely the consequence of the enhancement of the steroidogenesis process by increasing TERT expression and activation of Wnt and cAMP/PKA/CREB/CREM signaling pathways, which contribute to the proliferation of granulosa and theca cells in the ovary, thus enhancing folliculogenesis and the ovulation process, resulting in an increase in the number of embryos.

When evaluating the implications of this study for humans, it is essential to note that telomerase activity is generally low or absent in most human somatic cells, resulting in gradual telomere shortening. In contrast, mice exhibit broader expression of telomerase, with detectable activity even in somatic tissues and long telomeres. However, in both humans and mice, telomerase activity is consistently present in germ cells. The absence of telomerase activity in germ cells leads to shorter telomeres, thereby significantly impairing fertility in both species. We therefore conclude that a temporary increase in telomerase activity in the ovary, by pharmaceutical compounds like AGS, promoted ovulation and increased offspring number in both young and aged female mice, suggesting a potential role in improving female fertility.
