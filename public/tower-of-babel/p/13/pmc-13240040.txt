ECG and Anatomical Features in Patients of Left Coronary Cusp Ventricular Arrhythmias With R Wave in Lead I
Authors: Satoshi Hara, Hitoshi Hachiya, Naoyuki Miwa, Yasuaki Tsumagari, Yoshikazu Sato, Tadanori Nakata, Taiki Ishizawa, Mirei Setoguchi, Hikaru Shimosato, Shigeki Kusa, Tetsuo Sasano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundsVentricular arrhythmias (VAs) originating from the coronary cusps with R wave in lead I typically originate specifically from the right coronary cusp (RCC), but are occasionally of left coronary cusp (LCC) origin.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent successful initial catheter ablation for VA in either the LCC (N = 41) or RCC (N = 14). Twelve‐lead ECG during VA and during sinus rhythm were compared between LCC arrhythmia patients with an R wave in lead I during VA (R(I)‐wave group) and those without (non‐R(I)‐wave group). In LCC cases in whom CT was available, we measured the anatomical axis of the left ventricle in the frontal plane.ResultsAmong the 41 LCC patients, 9 (22.0%) showed an R wave in lead I in contrast to 100% of the 18 RCC patients. CT imaging was available for 22 LCC patients (53.7%) and revealed a modest correlation between electrocardiographic and anatomical axis (r = 0.64, p = 0.0014). The R(I)‐wave group had a significantly more leftward QRS axis during sinus rhythm (2.0° ± 29.6° vs 43.1° ± 31.1°, p = 0.001) and smaller anatomical axis on CT (14.4° ± 16.46° vs 35.8° ± 11.71°, p = 0.004). ROC analysis showed that a QRS axis < 26° best predicted R wave presence in LCC patients.ConclusionIn cases of VA suspected to originate from the aortic cusps with R wave in lead I, the presence of a leftward QRS axis during sinus rhythm suggests mapping should be extended to the LCC.Keywords: 12‐lead ECG, CT, left coronary cusp, ventricular arrhythmia

Introduction

Most ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) originating from the coronary cusps with R wave in lead I are reported from the right coronary cusp (RCC) [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, we sometimes experience VAs with R wave in lead I that originate from the left coronary cusp (LCC) [4]. This study aims to identify characteristics of cases in which LCC‐VAs exhibit an R wave in lead I to help with preoperative prediction regarding the origin of VAs.

Methods

Patient Population

We retrospectively analyzed consecutive VA patients who underwent initial ablation in the LCC that was successful between 2018 and 2024 in our institution. By successful, we mean clinical VAs were completely eliminated. In this population, we analyzed the difference between LCC‐VAs with and without R‐wave in lead I of the ECG. Presence of R‐wave in lead I in this study was defined as an R wave amplitude ≥ 0.15 mV or an Rs pattern with an R/(R + S) ratio ≥ 0.75 [3]. Additionally, we evaluated VA patients who underwent initial ablation in the RCC that was successful during the same period (RCC group). QRS morphology in lead I during VA and electrical axis during sinus rhythm were studied. We also evaluated the QRS morphology of pace mapping obtained from the LCC in the RCC group.

Cases in which successful ablation was achieved in the LCC and RCC junction (commissure) were excluded due to the lack of preservation of catheter orientation data and their small number.

Evaluation factors included baseline patient characteristics and 12‐lead ECG during sinus rhythm and VA. If CT imaging had been performed for any reason, correlations between anatomical axis and 12‐lead ECG findings were calculated in the LCC population.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Electrocardiogram Analysis

During sinus rhythm, we noted QRS morphology, electrical axis, and transition zone. During VA, we noted the transition zone [1, 5], QRS morphology in lead V1 [5], peak deflection index [6] (PDI), and the presence or absence of S waves in leads V5 and V6 [7].

CT Analysis

A retrospective analysis was performed on cases in which chest CT had been performed for any reason within 1 year before or after ablation.

We measured the angle from the cardiac base to the apex on CT images in the frontal plane at the depth of the inferior margin of the RCC and called it the anatomical axis (see Figure 1) [8, 9].

CT assessment of the anatomical axis. The anatomical axis was defined as the angle between the horizontal line (white line) and the line connecting the inferior margin of the right coronary cusp and left ventricular apex (yellow line) in the frontal plane.

The inferior margin of the RCC was selected because the His–Purkinje system distal to the AV node courses beneath this region [10]. We focused on this anatomical landmark for two reasons: (1) outflow tract VAs typically exhibit an activation vector converging toward the ventricular apex, and (2) the present study examined axis deviation during sinus rhythm, making this location the most appropriate reference point for measurement.

Since the apex may not lie in the same horizontal plane, we adjusted CT slices to locate the apex and measured the angle from the apex to the RCC base.

We used a frontal section so its plane would approximate the plane of Einthoven's triangle.

Electrophysiological Study and Catheter Ablation

The coronary cusps were mapped by ablation catheter or multipoint mapping catheter (HD‐Grid, Abbot, St Paul, MN; Pentaray, or Octaray, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA). Pace mapping was performed from the earliest activation site of the coronary cusp obtained by mapping. The ablation site was determined based on a comprehensive assessment of local activation time, morphology of unipolar electrogram, and pace mapping findings. Before radiofrequency (RF) application, coronary angiography was performed to confirm the anatomy of the coronary cusps and location of the left main coronary artery. If the CARTO mapping system was used, CARTO Sound was utilized to identify the location of each coronary cusp [5]. RF application was performed using a 3.5 or 4 mm tip open irrigated catheter or non‐irrigating catheter by operator choice with a maximum output of 30 W and maximum duration of 60 s.

Statistical Analysis

All continuous variables are summarized as mean ± SD and were compared using unpaired t‐test or as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared using χ 2 or Fisher's exact tests. Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to analyze correlation between two continuous variables. Receiver‐operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to obtain optimal cut‐off value. For all analyses, a two‐sided p value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance (JASP, version 0.19.3).

Results

Patient Grouping

A total of 41 patients had successful ablation at the LCC, 9 (22.0%) of whom displayed an R‐wave in lead I (R(I)‐wave group). The remaining 32 were called the non‐R(I)‐wave group. A total of 14 patients had successful ablation at the RCC (RCC group). Sample ECGs from patients from the three different groups are shown in Figures 2, 3, 4. No differences were observed between the R(I)‐ and non‐R(I)‐wave groups in baseline characteristics, although the R(I)‐group patients tended to be older (p = 0.1) (Table 1).

ECG Findings During Sinus Rhythm

The mean QRS axis in the R(I)‐wave group was 2.0° ± 29.6°, which was significantly more leftward compared to 43.1° ± 31.1° in the non‐R(I)‐wave group (p = 0.001) (Figure 5A). In contrast, the QRS axis during sinus rhythm was similar between the RCC group and non‐R(I)‐wave group (47.4° ± 28.1° vs. 43.1° ± 31.1°, p = 0.66), indicating that leftward axis is not the sole determinant of positive R wave in lead I. In addition, QRS morphology in Lead V1 in the RCC group demonstrated an rS pattern. The transition zone of the R(I)‐wave group during sinus rhythm was located between V2–3 and V4, while the transition zone of the non‐R(I)‐wave group during sinus rhythm was between V2 and V5–6 (Table 2). According to the ROC curve, area under the curve (AUC) for detecting R(I)‐wave group was 0.849 (95% CI 0.7250–0.973, sensitivity 81.2%, and specificity 88.9%) for QRS axis < 26° during sinus rhythm (Figure 5B).

Morphology of Ventricular Arrhythmia

In the R(I)‐wave group, the ratio of R wave amplitude in leads II/III during VA was 0.83 ± 0.13, which was smaller than 1.05 ± 0.15 in the non‐R(I)‐wave group (p < 0.001). The transition zone of the R(I)‐wave group during VA was located at V1, V1–2, V2–3, and V3–4 in 2, 3, 3, and 1 patients, respectively. In the non‐R(I)‐wave group, the transition zone during VA was located at V1, V1–2, V2, V2–3, V3, and V3‐4, in 10, 4, 1, 9, 1, and 7 patients, respectively.

In the R(I)‐wave group, QRS morphology in lead V1 consisted of an rS pattern in six patients and an R pattern in three patients. In the non–R(I)‐wave group, an rS pattern was observed in 23 patients, an Rs pattern in 4 patients, an R wave pattern in 3 patients, and an rsR pattern in 2 patients (Table 2). There were no differences in QRS width, PDI, and S wave in lead V5/6 between the R(I)‐wave group and non‐R(I)‐wave group.

Among the 14 patients of the RCC group, pace mapping was also performed from the LCC in 9 patients. Of these, three demonstrated an R wave in lead I. The QRS axes during sinus rhythm in those cases were −65°, −17°, and −2°, respectively.

Discussion

There are multiple methods for predicting LCC and RCC origin VA. One criterion that strongly suggests RCC origin is a positive R wave in lead I of the 12 lead ECG during VA. However, we sometimes come across patients with a positive R wave in lead I whose VA origin is the LCC. The focus of our study was to characterize LCC VA with positive R wave in lead I by comparison with LCC VA without positive R wave. We found 22% of LCC VA patients to have a positive R wave in lead I, and that they had a significantly leftward sinus beat QRS axis compared to non‐R wave patients. As might be expected, 100% of RCC‐VA patients had a positive R wave. We also found a significant positive correlation between QRS axis and the anatomical axis (the angle of the left ventricle from the horizontal axis in the frontal plane) in the LCC‐VA patients. In RCC origin cases, lead I positivity during sinus rhythm is expected because the RCC occupies the most rightward position of the outflow tract and the left ventricle. In contrast, when LCC origin VAs present with an R wave in lead I, this likely reflects a relative rightward shift of the ectopic focus. We consider this shift to be attributable to a more horizontal orientation of the left ventricle, which, in our study population, manifested as left axis deviation during sinus rhythm, that is, the positive R wave in RCC‐VA patients during VA likely comes from the rightward position of the RCC at the base of the heart, while the positive R wave in the subset of LCC‐VA patients who do have positive R wave in lead I arises from a more horizontally lying ventricle.

Electrocardiographic Features of VAs Originating From the RCC and LCC, Especially Lead I

In this study, all patients in the RCC group demonstrated an R wave in lead I during VA. Previous reports described R wave appearance in lead I during VA in cases where successful ablation was achieved at the RCC [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The proposed explanation for the presence of an R wave in lead I of RCC‐origin VAs is that at the peri‐pulmonary valve location, the left ventricular outflow tract including the aortic valve lies to the right of the right ventricular outflow tract, and the RCC is furthest to the right of the aortic cusps. Therefore, VA originating from the RCC generates a dominant right‐to‐left electrical vector, resulting in a pronounced positive deflection in Lead I. This ECG feature has been incorporated into algorithms used to identify the RCC as the site of origin for VAs, alongside criteria based on precordial lead characteristics during VA [3, 4].

Meanwhile, several cases of VAs originating from the LCC have also been reported to exhibit an R wave in lead I [4, 5]. However, to our knowledge, no studies have examined the mechanism responsible for the presence of an R wave in lead I in cases of VA originating from the LCC, nor have they characterized the clinical features of patients in whom this finding is observed.

Relationship Between the Electrocardiographic Electrical Axis and Cardiac Anatomical Orientation

Prior reports and consideration of anatomy suggest that a more horizontal cardiac orientation should result in a more leftward QRS axis on the ECG. We found a modest correlation between the anatomical axis and QRS axis, and the R(I)‐wave group demonstrated a more horizontal cardiac orientation and more leftward QRS axis than the non‐R(I)‐wave group. Aging also has been reported to shift the QRS axis leftward [11], but there was only a tendency toward older age in the R(I)‐wave group in the present study.

Ventricular Arrhythmias Originating From the Left Coronary Cusp Presenting With an R Wave in Lead I

In this study, patients with VAs originating from the LCC and exhibiting a positive deflection in lead I during their arrhythmia were found to have a leftward electrical axis during sinus rhythm. CT imaging also demonstrated a more horizontal cardiac orientation in those cases.

In cases where the electrical axis is oriented close to the normal axis, ventricular activation during VA tends to generate a stronger vertical vector, resulting in the absence of an R wave in lead I (Figure 7C). In contrast, in patients with a leftward shift of the electrical axis, the electrical propagation directed from right to left becomes more prominent (Figure 7F), thereby yielding positive components in lead I. This is consistent with our finding of a greater III/II R wave amplitude ratio during VA in the R(I)‐wave group compared to the non‐R(I)‐wave group. In the RCC group, all three patients who exhibited an R wave in lead I during LCC pace mapping had a sinus rhythm QRS axis of less than 26°. This finding supports our claim that left‐axis deviation during sinus rhythm contributes to the presence of an R wave in lead I.

Most algorithms for differentiating between RCC and LCC are based on QRS morphology during VAs [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 12], with no reference to QRS axis during sinus rhythm. As demonstrated in the present study, the QRS axis during sinus rhythm varies widely over patients. A leftward QRS axis can signify a more horizontal anatomical axis, which in turn can manifest in differing conduction patterns during VA and potentially hinder the applicability of conventional algorithms for detecting arrhythmia origin. Maeda et al. demonstrated that the angle between the aorta and the left ventricle changes with aging, resulting in characteristic alterations in pace‐mapping morphologies, particularly in the inferior leads [13]. Their analysis incorporated not only tachycardia ECG findings but also anatomic characteristics. In contrast, we believe that the electrical axis assessed in the present study reflects activation patterns influenced by factors beyond age, suggesting that it may serve as a more broadly applicable parameter.

In the present study, we had a high (22%) proportion of LCC VA exhibiting R wave in lead I, which likely helped us identify distinguishing characteristics. One possible explanation for the higher prevalence is the relatively older age of our study population compared to previous reports, which typically included patients around 45 years of age [1, 2, 3, 4].

Limitation

Because the complete ventricular activation pattern during VA was not mapped, the specific components contributing to a positive deflection in lead I could not be determined. Lead I was not consistently positive for some cases with a relatively horizontal QRS axis; the reason for this discrepancy is unclear.

Conclusion

In patients with a horizontal cardiac axis, an R wave may appear in lead I even in cases of VAs originating from the LCC. In patients with a leftward QRS axis, especially less than 26° during sinus rhythm, mapping should examine not only the RCC but also the LCC.
