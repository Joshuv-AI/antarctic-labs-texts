Is simulation always a safe learning environment? Anxiety and social anxiety symptoms in first-year medical students during simulated patient encounters
Authors: Maria Helena Favarato, Isabela Chagas Peres, Iolanda de Fátima Lopes Calvo Tibério
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterpersonal contact is essential for health professions. Simulation with actors in a safe, protected environment followed by feedback from teachers and simulated patients can positively impact anxiety symptoms related to social anxiety, which this project focuses on.ObjectivesTo assess social anxiety in medical students and their relationship with practical simulations during the first year, determining the prevalence of social anxiety; and to qualitatively analyze students’ narratives about simulations with simulated patients. MethodsSelf-administered questionnaires measured social anxiety (LSAS - Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale) and trait anxiety weeks before the experience, and monthly on simulation days, assessing LSAS and state anxiety. Free narratives on expectations (before) and experiences (on simulation day) were collected.ResultsThe mean baseline LSAS was 56.66 ± 26.71; mean trait anxiety was 49.03 ± 12.05. Expectations showed high motivation and recognition of the learning potential and personal development through simulations, but also anxiety, nervousness, self-doubt, and insecurity. On simulation days, 80 students gave 107 responses; mean LSAS was 51.95 ± 28.49, not significantly different from baseline (p=0.30). Mean state anxiety was 48.93 ± 10.75. Among 21 students with repeated responses, no significant change was found in state anxiety (p=0.66) or LSAS (p=0.09) over time. Qualitative analysis highlighted anxiety and performance insecurity, but also ambivalence, with reports of fear alongside pleasure and gratitude. Students saw simulations as opportunities for self-reflection and growth in a safe space to learn from mistakes.ConclusionHigh anxiety linked to simulations is a concern and should be addressed in simulation planning to ensure a psychologically safe learning environment. Social anxiety may hinder clinical performance and should be addressed in health professions education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09827-y.Keyword: Anxiety, Social phobia, Medical students, Undergraduate medical education, Patient simulation, Simulation training

Introduction

Clinical skills training for medical students through simulations represents an effective and safe option for developing communication skills, techniques, attitudes and knowledge [1–3]. In the field of individual health care, communication, leadership, needs assessment, planning and evaluation of care can be simulated, standardized and controlled in learning facilities [4–7]. In medical education, simulated patients are individuals trained to realistically portray patients or family members for teaching, assessment, or research purposes, allowing students to practice clinical and communication skills in a safe environment [6, 8–12]. Unlike standardized patients, simulated patients are not necessarily required to reproduce the same performance for all learners [6, 8–12]. Rather than following rigid scripts, actors receive detailed background information regarding symptoms, medical history, social context, and emotional aspects, enabling improvisation and authentic emotional responses during interactions with students [5, 6, 12–14]. They are encouraged to adapt their responses according to the situation and student performance, disclosing information only when appropriately explored. This flexible approach promotes empathy, communication, patient bonding, and awareness of patients’ emotional states, while also allowing broader feedback beyond checklist-based evaluations [15–18]. Simulated patient-based learning promotes realistic and emotionally engaging encounters, contributing to the development of communication skills, empathy, self-reflection, and patient-centered care. Studies also highlight that flexible interactions and immediate feedback from simulated patients enhance students’ confidence and emotional preparedness for challenging clinical encounters [7]. Repeated encounters with simulated patients progressively enhance clinical skills, supporting a dose–response relationship between the number of simulation sessions and learning outcomes. There is a strong association between simulation practice time and standardized learning outcomes [19, 20]. Progressive improvements in history taking, physical examination, and communication skills may be observed across sequential simulation scenarios during the academic year, particularly among senior students [21]. Furthermore, repeated contact with simulated situations with simulated patients reduces students’ anxiety about their performance and straightens their perception about professionalism [15–17]. High-fidelity simulation experiences have been shown to significantly reduce self-reported anxiety levels and increase confidence after participation, with repeated exposure further decreasing pre-simulation anxiety and enhancing psychological stability in clinical settings [22, 23]. A meta-analysis including 62 studies and 4,570 nursing students found that simulation was associated with a small reduction in anxiety and a moderate increase in self-confidence compared with conventional teaching methods [24]. Meta-regression further demonstrated a positive association between self-confidence and learning outcomes [24]. Specific simulation design characteristics, particularly prebriefing, sessions longer than 60 min, and high-fidelity simulations, were associated with greater anxiety reduction [25]. Simulation with standardized patients is perceived as authentic and provides a safe environment for practicing communication skills, which can help demystify patient interactions and reduce anticipatory anxiety [5, 6].

This type of activity, with constant feedback, allows students from the beginning of their training to become familiar with the fact that patients have feelings, impressions, experiences, beliefs and attitudes regarding health care [15, 17] and this experience and reflection enable the student to be able to have empathy, dialogue and negotiation skills when faced with different situations [6].

Social anxiety disorder is defined by at least 6 months of marked, persistent fears about social and performance situations where the individual may be scrutinized by others, which are either avoided or endured with intense anxiety [26]. The generalized subtype of social anxiety disorder is characterized by fear and avoidance of most social situations (speaking in small groups, socializing at parties, speaking to authority figures), accounting for approximately half of community cases and most treatment-seeking individuals. This represents the most disabling form of social anxiety disorder [27, 28]. Social anxiety disorder has been linked to significant functional and occupational impairments, a persistent and chronic course, an increased risk of co-occurring depression, and reduced social skills [28, 29]. It is a common medical condition that impacts quality of life and academic performance, affecting 2 to 16% of the general population [30, 31] but up to 60% of medical students [32, 33]. Mental health issues lead to negative effects on academic performance, social functioning, and career development [34, 35].

Central symptoms such as being “anxious at parties” and “nervous with authority figures,” as well as bridge symptoms like “nervous in informal gatherings” and “fatigue,” are particularly relevant for targeted intervention strategies [33].

The first year of medical school represents a period of heightened vulnerability. Students are adapting to a competitive academic environment, developing a new professional identity, and confronting performance-based evaluation for the first time [36].

Qualitative studies indicate that while simulation can initially trigger anxiety—particularly related to being observed and fear of making mistakes—these experiences also foster self-awareness and professional growth. The anxiety provoked is not directly correlated with reduced learning effectiveness, and, when managed appropriately, can serve as a gateway to deeper insight and resilience [6, 37].

Physiological studies confirm that simulation scenarios elicit measurable stress responses, but these are not necessarily detrimental and may be mitigated by repeated exposure and supportive debriefing [38, 39]. Overall, the literature supports the use of standardized patient simulations as a valuable tool for reducing anxiety symptoms and preparing medical students for real-world patient interactions.

Rationale and objectives

Clinical simulation with simulated patients has become a cornerstone of undergraduate medical education, particularly in the early years of training. Simulation is widely promoted as a psychologically safe learning environment that allows students to practice communication, clinical reasoning, and professional behavior without risk to real patients. However, the assumption that simulation is universally “safe” from a psychological standpoint remains insufficiently examined [40].

Although simulation is designed to provide structured exposure to clinical scenarios, it simultaneously introduces elements that may activate anxiety: being observed, performing in front of peers, interacting with unfamiliar actors, and receiving feedback. For students with elevated baseline anxiety or social anxiety symptoms, simulated patient encounters may represent a high-stakes social-evaluative situation rather than a safe rehearsal space [6, 37, 40].

At the same time, exposure-based learning theories suggest that repeated, structured, and supervised exposure to anxiety-provoking situations can reduce anxiety over time. Simulation may therefore function as a form of graded exposure, potentially decreasing both general anxiety and social anxiety symptoms through habituation, increased self-efficacy, and skills mastery [41–43]. However, empirical data examining this hypothesis remain limited and inconsistent, particularly when addressing social anxiety and first-year medical students.

Understanding whether simulated patient encounters reduce, maintain, or exacerbate anxiety is critical for educational planning and curricular design and to promote students’ well-being and learning equity. The hypothesis of this study is that repeated, structured, and supervised exposure to simulated patient encounters functions as a form of graded exposure capable of reducing anxiety symptoms as well as social anxiety among first-year medical students. Additionally, the study aims to determine whether simulated patient encounters may alternatively maintain or exacerbate anxiety symptoms, thereby clarifying their impact on student well-being and informing curricular design and educational planning.

Methods

This study employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods research design, integrating both quantitative and qualitative approaches to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomena under investigation as it enables comparison, corroboration, and complementarity between datasets collected simultaneously [44]. Data collection was longitudinal and prospective over the two first semesters of medical school at University of São Caetano do Sul, Sao Paulo, Brazil. The project was evaluated and approved by the local ethics committee (Research Ethics Committee of the Municipal University of São Caetano do Sul/ Comitê de Ética em Pesquisa da Universidade Municipal de São Caetano do Sul – CEP-USCS)- CAAE-CEP-USCS: 78494724.4.0000.5510- before the beginning of the research and there is alignment between the study protocol and the ethical principles advocated by the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the objectives and procedures of the study and provided written informed consent prior to their participation, in accordance with applicable ethical standards and regulations. Participation in the study was voluntary and anonymous; data collection preserved the identity of the individuals.

There are 120 students who enroll in the course every year, and all of them were invited in person in the classroom before their first simulation with simulated patients. After this first simulation, in the 8th week of the semester, the simulations occur monthly until the end of the fourth semester of the medical course. After the invitation, the Informed Consent Form was presented and those who agreed by signing it were considered participants in the study and asked to answer the questionnaires in a serial manner until the end of the second semester. At any time, if they so wished, they could leave the study, and students could also be included after the first simulations, without completing the first evaluation. (A) Weeks before the first simulation with an actor: Collection of demographic data: age, gender, race, and if the student has already been diagnosed or has undergone/is undergoing treatment for psychiatric illnesses. Application of the Liebowitz social anxiety scale (LSAS) translated into Portuguese and validated in the Brazilian population [45–47] and the trait anxiety scale (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory- STAI-Trait anxiety scale) [37, 48, 49]. An additional open-ended section was included in the questionnaire, allowing participants to freely express their expectations regarding the simulation activities as a free narrative text.

A widely used and reliable [50, 51] self-report measure for social anxiety disorder internationally is the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS), which consists in a 24-item semi-structured interview based on a two-factor model, with separate subscales evaluating fear and avoidance of situations involving social interaction and performance/observation by others. Respondents are asked to rate their fear/anxiety (LSAS-anxiety subscale) on a four-point scale ranging from 0 (none) to 3 (severe), and avoidance (LSAS-avoidance subscale) on a four-point scale ranging from 0 (never) to 3 (usually). The total score is obtained by adding the scores obtained on both subscales [30]. The calculation of the total score and the use of the fear and avoidance subscales is explained in the article by Rytwinski [50, 52]. The classification of social anxiety severity by the LSAS is based on cutoff points validated in different populations. Studies demonstrate that a total score equal to or greater than 30 on the LSAS presents a good balance between sensitivity and specificity for identifying cases of social anxiety disorder [50, 52, 53]. A Brazilian study suggested the cutoff of 32 points [48]. For the generalized subtype of social anxiety disorder, a cutoff point of 60 is recommended [50, 52, 53].

STAI aims to assess, through self-report, the presence and intensity of current anxiety symptoms, as well as a general tendency to experience anxiety. The instrument includes two subscales. The STAI contains 40 items, with 20 assigned to each of the S-Anxiety and T-Anxiety subscales. The Trait Anxiety Scale (T-Anxiety) assesses more stable characteristics related to anxiety proneness, such as general feelings of calmness, confidence, and security, measuring the frequency of general feelings. Each subscale yields scores ranging from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater levels of anxiety [50, 54]. STAI scores are commonly classified as “no or low anxiety” (20–37), “moderate anxiety” (38–44), and “high anxiety” (45–80) [55, 56].

(B) Immediately before each simulation with a simulated patient (2 to 3 per semester): Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale and State Anxiety Scale (State Anxiety Inventory-STAI-E) [49]. An additional open-ended section was included in the questionnaire, allowing participants to freely express their experiences regarding the simulation activities as a free narrative text.

The State Anxiety Scale (S-Anxiety) measures the individual’s current level of anxiety, asking how they feel “right now,” with items that capture subjective experiences of apprehension, tension, nervousness, worry, and physiological arousal of the autonomic nervous system. A threshold score of 39–40 on the S-Anxiety scale has been proposed as an indicator of clinically significant anxiety symptoms [50, 54]. Statistics involves descriptive analysis and parametric tests for comparisons. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Paired t-test was used to compare different moments of the same individual. The GraphPad Prism Software was used (2025 GraphPad Software) and SPSS v22 (IBM Corp., 2013) were used to calculate Cronbach’s alpha.

From the narratives about students’ expectations regarding the simulations in the first moment and the narratives about students’ experiences with the simulations collected across the period, Bardin’s content analysis [57, 58] was performed as a qualitative analysis.

Triangulation was conducted following a mixed-methods integration framework in order to examine the phenomenon of anxiety in simulation from complementary perspectives. Quantitative data (LSAS and STAI scores) provided measurable indicators of social, trait, and state anxiety, whereas qualitative narrative data offered contextualized and subjective insights into students’ experiences. A convergent parallel design was adopted, in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected during the same phase of the study, analyzed independently, and subsequently integrated for interpretation purposes [59].

Results

The study recruitment period was from April, 2024 to June, 2025.

Participants’ characteristics: 57 first year medical students answered the questionnaires before initiating the experience of simulated patients’ consultations. Of these, 44 (77.2%) were cis women, 12 (10.71%) were cis men and 1 (1.75%) identified as “other gender”. In terms of race, 47 (83.8%) self-declared as white, 7 (12.28%) as black and 2 (3.57%) as Asian. The mean age was 21.14 ± 4.4 years. 22 participants (38.6%) declared that they have been diagnosed with, be undergoing or having undergone mental health treatment. It is worth noting that 75.94% of medical students on our campus identify as women.

Quantitative results

Before any exposure to simulated patient encounters, the mean score on the LSAS was 56.66 ± 26.71. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The overall scale demonstrated good reliability (α = 0.89). When analyzed by subscales, internal consistency remained adequate, with α = 0.87 for the performance subscale and α = 0.84 for the social interaction subscale. Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of participants across the different LSAS categories. The distribution of students across LSAS classification strata is presented in Fig. 1(a); scores below 32 were considered indicative of absent or low social anxiety, whereas scores above 60 were classified as generalized social anxiety. In the social interaction anxiety subscale, the mean score was 28.91 ± 14.10, while in the performance/avoidance subscale the mean was 27.75 ± 14.31. The highest-scoring (most anxiety-provoking) situations were largely consistent across subscales. The five situations associated with the greatest anxiety were: acting, performing, or speaking in front of an audience; expressing disagreement or disapproval to individuals not well known to the participant; being the center of attention; working while being observed; and attempting to initiate a romantic interaction.

LSAS Classification distribution in (a) first evaluation and (b) on the day of simulations. Scores under 32 are considered as no or low social anxiety and over 60 as generalized social anxiety

On the days when simulations took place, 80 different students provided a total of 107 responses. Then, the mean LSAS score was 51.95 ± 28.49, which did not differ significantly from the pre-simulation LSAS mean (p = 0.30). The distribution of participants across LSAS categories is presented in Fig. 1(b). In the subsequent evolutive assessments, LSAS demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = 0.78).

The mean STAI of trait anxiety (T-anxiety) was 49.03 ± 12.05. Based on established cutoffs, 18 students were classified as having low trait anxiety (31.57%); 26 students were classified as having moderate trait anxiety (45.61%); 13 students were classified as having high trait anxiety (22.8%). Internal consistency of the trait anxiety scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The instrument demonstrated excellent reliability in the present sample (α = 0.93), indicating a high degree of internal consistency among the 20 items.

The mean STAI-State (S-anxiety) score on simulation days was 48.93 ± 10.75. Among respondents, 24 (22.4%) scored below 40, indicating absent or low state anxiety; 68 (63.6%) scored between 41 and 60, consistent with moderate anxiety; and 15 (13.9%) scored between 61 and 73 (maximum possible score = 80), reflecting high levels of state anxiety. The instrument demonstrated excellent reliability in the present sample (Cronbach α = 0.95), indicating a very high level of internal consistency among its items.

Twenty-one students submitted more than one response over time. When comparing their first with their last response, STAI-state mean was 48.10 ± 10.55 in their first evaluation and 49.05 ± 12.67 in their last, showing no significant difference over time (p = 0.66). LSAS score behavior over the course of the research was 41.19 ± 27.22 on their first response and 46.24 ± 27.81 in their last, not statistically significant (p = 0.09).

Triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings

The integration of quantitative and qualitative data reveals a coherent and complementary pattern regarding students’ experiences with simulated patient encounters. Quantitatively, participants demonstrated elevated levels of social anxiety (LSAS) prior to simulation exposure, as well as moderate to high levels of trait (STAI-T) and state anxiety (STAI-S). Importantly, anxiety levels remained stable over time, with no statistically significant reductions observed either on the day of simulation or among students who completed repeated assessments. These findings suggest that simulation exposure alone did not substantially attenuate anxiety levels within the study period.

In the qualitative essays, students consistently described ambivalent expectations and experiences: while expressing high motivation and recognizing simulation as a valuable opportunity for experiential learning and personal development, they also reported feelings of nervousness, insecurity, and self-doubt. Anxiety emerged as a central and recurring theme in their narratives, particularly in relation to performance, being observed, and fear of negative evaluation.

This convergence between data strands is particularly evident in the alignment between the LSAS performance-related items with the highest scores (e.g., speaking in front of an audience, being the center of attention, working while being observed) and the qualitative reports emphasizing performance pressure and fear of judgment. The quantitative prevalence of moderate state anxiety on simulation days further corroborates students’ subjective accounts of nervous anticipation and emotional activation prior to participation.

Discussion

In this study it was observed that there is an elevated prevalence of social anxiety. The high proportion of participating students with scores above 60 on the LSAS suggests high prevalence of generalized social anxiety disorder, a potentially debilitating condition [54]. Participants show as well high trait and simulation-related state anxiety. In the qualitative analysis of their narratives, the students who participated in this study expressed ambivalent feelings towards their experience with simulated patients. At the same time, they recognize the opportunity for self-reflection, receiving feedback and improving technical and interpersonal skills on a metacognitive exercise, anxiety permeates their experience.

From the students’ perspective, simulation represents an opportunity for experiential, hands-on learning within a secure, judgmentally-free and controlled environment [60]. However, it also exposes them to multiple stressors, including complex interpersonal interactions, unexpected emotional responses, critical incidents, and communication challenges—factors that may provoke anxiety, particularly among novice learners [60, 61]. A moderate degree of stress may be beneficial, as it can enhance motivation and engagement during scenario execution. Nevertheless, excessive anxiety and distress may compromise the attainment of learning objectives. Previous research indicates that some students perceive simulation scenarios as even more stressful than real clinical settings, partly due to the awareness of being observed and evaluated [60, 61]. Consequently, institutional support is essential to maximize the educational benefits of simulation. Learning to manage simulation-related anxiety may also foster coping skills transferable beyond the simulated environment [60, 61].

The elevated levels of trait and state anxiety, together with the high prevalence of social anxiety identified in this study, may have implications for future professional performance. Evidence suggests that higher anxiety levels are associated with poorer clinical competence and an increased likelihood of errors [22, 62].

In terms of the educational environment, simulation-based curricula, particularly with repeated experiences, have been shown to reduce anxiety and increase confidence among medical students in previous studies [22, 23, 63], with repeated simulation experiences leading to psychological stability and to competent medical treatment in real-life clinical settings [10]. After previous simulation experiences, students present less anxiety and depression and more self-confidence [22, 23, 61, 63]. However, this pattern was not observed in the present study, highlighting the need to critically re-evaluate aspects of the educational context. Structured debriefing and supportive faculty involvement allow students to reflect on their performance and learn from mistakes [22, 23, 64].

Psychological safety, a core component of high-quality learning, refers to learners feeling comfortable participating, speaking up, asking questions, and making mistakes without fear of embarrassment or negative consequences during simulation activities [60]. In simulations with simulated patients, threats to psychological safety may include performance pressure, increased task difficulty, and biased or harsh feedback from faculty or simulated patients. To promote a supportive learning environment, educators should establish clear learning objectives, encourage learning rather than perfection, allow time-outs when needed, minimize unconscious bias, and conduct constructive debriefings focused on strengths and opportunities for improvement [65–67]. With careful design and reflective delivery, the introductory phase of simulation (pre-briefing) might effectively prepare students, reduce anxiety, and promote psychological safety to improve learning experiences [68].

Educators bear responsibility for fostering ideal learning environments and should be perceived by students as facilitators of emotional coping in challenging and stressful situations. Key elements of such environments include physical comfort, mutual trust and respect, collaboration, freedom of expression, and acceptance of individual differences. Both pre-briefing and debriefing constitute strategic opportunities to build trust. Additionally, careful calibration of scenario difficulty is essential to sustain motivation driven by appropriate challenge while avoiding feelings of overwhelm or stagnation [60, 61].

The qualitative analysis highlights anxiety as a main theme related to their simulation experience. It is in line with previous results from observational studies and meta-analysis that addressed students’ perceptions in which, although students’ views of simulation were positive, anxiety arose as a key theme due to performing in front of educators/peers [37, 69] When comparing simulation with standardized patients with high-fidelity manikins simulation, anxiety levels are considered to be similar. Regarding standardized patients, anxiety could be related to realism and patient interactions [37, 40, 70, 71]. Other studies addressed severe and clinical-deteriorating scenarios [71, 72] and final years students [37], but ours was aimed primarily at health promotion.

Thus, this is the first study to evaluate the impact of simulated patients encounters in social anxiety. Another strength is that scenarios were designed for community health situations, with first-year students aiming to identify common and non-acute health needs. A weakness of this study is a low response rate to repeated evaluations, which may lead to selection bias.

Voluntary mental health surveys among medical students commonly achieve low response rates. Online questionnaires, particularly those requiring repeated participation, frequently yield lower engagement than anticipated [73, 74]. The demanding academic workload, emotional burden of medical training, and repeated exposure to institutional surveys may reduce adherence [73, 75]. Additionally, students experiencing higher anxiety levels may avoid participation due to emotional discomfort or concerns about privacy and confidentiality [74, 76], while others with greater interest in mental health topics may have been more inclined to respond [77], potentially introducing selection bias. The concomitance of depressive symptoms was not evaluated as it was not an objective of the study, but it is known that depressive patients also score highly in STAI scales [54, 78, 79].

In conclusion, high levels of anxiety associated with simulation are a tangible and significant phenomenon. Taken together, the triangulated findings suggest that anxiety in simulation contexts is both measurable and experientially salient. In contrast to what the literature review and the initial hypothesis might suggest- that repeated, structured, and supervised exposure to simulated patient encounters functions might be capable of reducing anxiety symptoms and social anxiety- among first-year medical students it appears to function as a dual phenomenon—simultaneously a potential facilitator of engagement and a barrier to optimal performance. The stability of anxiety levels across time, combined with the persistence of anxiety-related themes in students’ narratives, underscores the need for deliberate educational strategies aimed at enhancing psychological safety for learners and supporting adaptive coping mechanisms within simulation-based curricula. Anxiety management might therefore be integrated as a core component of simulation planning. Given its potential to hinder optimal clinical performance, social anxiety should also be addressed explicitly within health professions education, with emphasis on developing effective coping strategies.
