Characterisation of steady‐state pharmacokinetics and glucodynamics of once‐weekly insulin efsitora alfa in individuals with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Jennifer Leohr, Oliver Klein, Tim Heise, Eric Zijlstra, Juliana M Bue‐Valleskey, Subha Suriyapperuma, Ryan Jarrett, Douglas James, Micheal Case, David Waters, Edward Pratt, Helle Linnebjerg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo evaluate the steady‐state pharmacokinetics (PK) and glucodynamic effects of insulin efsitora alfa (efsitora) over a weekly interval in adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D).Materials and MethodsThis open‐label, multiple dose, euglycaemic clamp study was conducted in 56 insulin naïve adults with T2D (mean [±SD] age, 61.9 ± 5.98 years; HbA1c, 7.1 ± 0.64%; BMI, 31.7 ± 3.20 kg/m2). Following a dose‐finding lead‐in period with once‐daily degludec, eligible participants received a one‐time loading dose of 12 U/kg efsitora followed by 9 weekly doses of 4 U/kg. Three 24‐h clamps were conducted on Days 1, 4, and 7 after the last efsitora dose. Model‐based interpolation was used to derive the glucodynamic profile over the weekly dosing interval at steady‐state.ResultsModel‐predicted pharmacokinetic steady‐state was achieved between the 4th and 6th weekly dose of efsitora when using a one‐time loading dose. At steady‐state, the PK profile over the week had a low peak‐to‐trough ratio of 1.16, with a half‐life of 19 days. The model‐predicted glucose infusion rate profile, over the weekly dosing interval, demonstrated a stable glucose‐lowering effect of efsitora. The maximum glucose infusion rate occurred between Days 4 and 5. Efsitora showed an even distribution of the glucose‐lowering effect within a weekly dosing interval, with a peak‐to‐trough ratio of 1.07. Efsitora was safe and well tolerated.ConclusionsEfsitora demonstrated a flat, prolonged PK profile, providing a consistent level of insulin exposure at steady‐state and resulting in a stable glucose‐lowering response across the weekly dosing interval.Keywords: basal insulin, clinical trial, pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics, type 2 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is characterised by insulin resistance and inadequate insulin secretion, resulting in chronic and progressive hyperglycaemia which may require pharmacologic therapy. 1 Despite the emergence of new non‐insulin therapies, many people with T2DM require additional glucose‐lowering agents over time, eventually resulting in the need for exogenous insulin. 2 According to American Diabetes Association standards of care, insulin treatment should be considered if symptoms of hyperglycaemia are present or when HbA1c or blood glucose levels are high (>10% [>86 mmol/mol] or ≥ 300 mg/dL [≥16.7 mmol/L]), regardless of background glucose‐lowering therapy. Insulin therapy is also indicated when glycaemic targets cannot be achieved despite optimal use of non‐insulin glucose‐lowering agents. 2 However, 73% of people with type 2 diabetes treated with basal insulin did not achieve the guideline‐recommended HbA1c target of <7.0% (<53 mmol/mol). 3 Barriers to insulin initiation and optimisation often include treatment complexity or fear of side effects, such as weight gain or hypoglycaemia.4, 5 This clinical and therapeutic inertia leads to suboptimal basal insulin dosing and failure to reach glycaemic targets. 6

Improved basal insulin therapeutic options are needed to address these treatment limitations. Basal insulins designed for once‐weekly administration may reduce barriers to treatment initiation. By lowering the burden of associated with daily basal insulins, once‐weekly basal insulins may improve treatment adherence and persistence, and lead to better real world patient outcomes. 7 Furthermore, weekly basal insulins with nearly flat pharmacokinetic (PK) profiles, providing a consistent amount of insulin, could result in more predictable glycaemic control. 7

Insulin efsitora alfa (efsitora; LY3209590) is an innovative basal insulin designed to have a flat pharmacokinetic profile and long half‐life, allowing for once‐weekly administration. Efsitora is an insulin receptor agonist that combines two novel single‐chain analogues of insulin with a human immunoglobulin G2 Fc domain. This Fc binding creates a molecule with a molecular weight of 64.1 kDa. 8 The prolonged action of efsitora is attributed to its delayed clearance, which is a result of three primary factors: (1) its large molecular size, which diminishes renal clearance; (2) its affinity for the neonatal Fc receptor, which inhibits degradation and facilitates recycling into circulation, thereby establishing a systemic reservoir of basal insulin; and (3) its reduced insulin receptor affinity, leading to decreased receptor‐mediated clearance. 9 This study evaluated the steady‐state pharmacokinetics and glucodynamic effects of efsitora using a euglycaemic clamp procedure in adults with type 2 diabetes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Trial design and participants

This was a Phase 1, single‐centre (Profil, Neuss, Germany), multiple‐dose, open‐label, euglycaemic clamp study (ClinicalTrials.gov registration no. NCT06169982). The protocol was reviewed and approved by the local health authorities and an independent ethics committee (Ethik‐Kommission der Medizinischen Fakultät der Universität Duisburg‐Essen, Essen, Germany) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. All participants provided written informed consent before initiation of any study‐related activities.

Eligible participants included men or women who had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes for 12 months or longer, were insulin naïve, had not experienced more than 1 severe hypoglycaemic event, hypoglycaemic unawareness, or hospitalisation for diabetic ketoacidosis within the past 180 days. Screening HbA1c eligibility criteria were ≥7.0% to ≤9.5%; for participants requiring washout of sulfonylureas and/or sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, the range was ≥6.5% to ≤9.5%. All participants were aged between 18 and 74 years and had a BMI from 27 to 40 kg/m2.

The protocol was subsequently amended to allow participants with type 2 diabetes receiving basal insulin (Neutral Protamine Hagedorn, insulin glargine U100 or U300, insulin degludec, or insulin detemir) for fewer than 5 years prior to screening to be eligible for the study; however, none were included in the study.

Procedures and assessments

The study consisted of a screening visit, a lead‐in period, a treatment period, an inpatient euglycaemic clamp period, and post‐treatment monitoring. The overall trial design is shown in Figure 1.

Study design. CGM, continuous glucose monitoring; FPG, fasting plasma glucose; SC, subcutaneous.

Eligible participants who were receiving sulfonylureas, SGLT2 inhibitors, or both discontinued their medication 2 weeks before the start of the lead‐in period.

The lead‐in period was used to initiate basal insulin therapy and establish the appropriate basal insulin dose for each participant, enabling the identification of eligible individuals for the fixed‐dose efsitora treatment and clamp period. Participants received an initial dose of degludec using a 0.1–0.3 U/kg daily dose, 10 based on investigator discretion in the morning at the clinical research unit. Participants were instructed to self‐administer subsequent degludec doses once a day at approximately the same time in the morning. Insulin dose assessment occurred approximately every 3 days for up to 4 weeks and dose adjustments were based on the median fasting plasma glucose from self‐monitored plasma glucose (SMPG) during the previous 3 days and hypoglycaemia events during the previous week using a dose titration algorithm (Tables S1 and S2). Participants who attained a stable fasting plasma glucose of 80–150 mg/dL (median of 3 consecutive days) within 4 weeks and had a degludec dose requirement between 0.2 and 0.7 U/kg transitioned to the efsitora treatment period. Those who were ineligible were discontinued from the study.

Eligible participants received an initial subcutaneous (SC) loading dose (or a one‐time starting dose) of 12 U/kg (three times the weekly dose) followed by 9 weekly SC efsitora doses of 4 U/kg (Eli Lilly and Company). Participants who had a fasting plasma glucose between 80 and 150 mg/dL during Week 9 received the last dose of efsitora and completed an inpatient period with three 24‐h clamps on Days 1, 4, and 7 post‐dose on Week 10. This permitted characterisation of glucodynamic action at the start of the dosing interval (Day 1), at the time where peak glucose lowering was expected (Day 4), and at the end of the dosing interval (Day 7). The glucodynamic effect was assessed using an automated glucose clamp device (ClampArt®; Profil, Neuss, Germany). Participants were required to be fasted during each clamp procedure. Immediately prior to the last administration of efsitora and the start of the first clamp procedure (Week 10, Day 1), fasting plasma glucose was measured. For each participant, the target clamp level at all three clamp procedures was defined as 20 mg/dL above the individual's fasting plasma glucose on Day 1 of Week 10. The approach of using glucose clamp targets above fasting glycaemia was chosen to ensure a glucose infusion rate (GIR) greater than zero in all participants. 11 At the following two clamp procedures on Day 4 and Day 7, participants were connected to the clamp device for continuous plasma glucose monitoring a few hours before the clamp period. If needed, participants received a variable IV infusion of glucose to obtain a steady plasma glucose at the individual target level (defined as 20 mg/dL above the individual's fasting plasma glucose on Day 1 of Week 10) prior to the start of the clamp procedure. The variable IV glucose infusion was continuously adjusted during the clamp procedure to maintain the individual glucose target clamp level throughout the 24 h clamp period.

Pharmacokinetic samples for efsitora concentration were collected pre‐dose weekly from Weeks 1–11, and at Week 16. Additionally, samples were collected daily during the weekly dosing interval on Week 10 to characterise the steady‐state profile. Samples were assayed using a validated enzyme linked immunosorbent assay method. The lower limit of quantification was 300 pmol/L, and interassay accuracy (% relative error) and interassay precision (% relative standard deviation) were ≤10%. Blood samples were collected to determine fasting plasma glucose levels every week from Week 1–16 using an enzymatic assay (LabCorp, CLS Geneva).

Safety assessments included adverse events (AEs), hypoglycaemic events defined as level 1 (plasma glucose <3.9 mmol/L [<70 mg/dL] and ≥3.0 mmol/L [≥54 mg/dL]), level 2 (plasma glucose <3.0 mmol/L [<54 mg/dL]), and level 3 (altered mental/physical status requiring assistance for recovery). Level 1 and 2 hypoglycaemic events were analysed but not recorded as AEs or serious adverse events (SAEs). Any episodes of level 3 hypoglycaemia were reported as SAEs. Vital signs, electrocardiogram, physical examination, and clinical laboratory assessments were also performed.

Unblinded continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) (Dexcom G6 CGM system, Dexcom Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was initiated at the beginning of the efsitora treatment to alert participants to hypoglycaemia and manage any episodes appropriately. CGM plasma glucose levels below 70 mg/dL were verified with a glucometer.

Samples were collected (pre‐dose) on Days 1, 15, 29, 64, and 106 to assess antibodies against efsitora. An affinity capture elution (ACE) bridge assay was used to screen, confirm, titre, and detect antibodies against efsitora and cross‐reactive antibodies to native insulin. An ACE competitive binding assay detected neutralising antibodies. In silico classification was based on cross‐reactive and neutralising antibody status, as previously described.12, 13

Statistical analysis

The study was not powered on the basis of statistical hypothesis testing and was designed to provide adequate data to support the modelling of the steady‐state efsitora PK and glucodynamic profile.

A sample size of 20 participants would provide at least 90% power to target a 95% confidence interval within 60% and 140% of the geometric mean estimates of clearance and volume of distribution for efsitora. Given the greater variability in glucodynamics, a sample size of 35 participants would provide adequate data for characterising the glucose response of efsitora in this population.

Safety assessments were summarised using descriptive statistics.

Pharmacokinetic‐pharmacodynamic modelling

A PK/PD model was developed using the observed serum efsitora concentrations during the duration of the study and the GIR data obtained in the three 24‐h euglycaemic clamps. The PK/PD model interpolated each participant's full‐week steady‐state GIR profile using their individual model parameter estimates. The model‐based interpolation was used to derive the insulin time‐action profile over the full‐week dosing interval at steady‐state. Further PK/PD modelling details are in the Supporting Information.

RESULTS

Participants

Of the 58 participants enrolled in the study, all received degludec in the lead‐in period, 56 were eligible and received at least one dose of efsitora, and 53 completed the study (Figure S1). Five participants discontinued the study. Two participants failed to meet the criteria to continue into the efsitora treatment period after the degludec lead‐in period. During the efsitora treatment period, three participants discontinued the study: two participants due to concerns regarding the study procedures, and one participant withdrew due to a serious adverse event. Fasting glucose

Upon initiation of degludec treatment (approximately Day −28 or less) during the lead‐in period, fasting glucose levels reduced and achieved the glucose target level for eligibility (80–150 mg/dL) required to transition to efsitora treatment. Fasting glucose levels were further reduced by approximately 10 mg/dL on efsitora treatment and were maintained throughout the treatment period (Figure 2).

Fasting glucose profile following weekly efsitora administration. Data presented are arithmetic mean ± standard error of fasting glucose concentration over time during weekly subcutaneous administration of efsitora. SC, subcutaneous; SE, standard error.

At the conclusion of the efsitora treatment, four out of 53 participants resumed previous glucose‐lowering therapy 5 or 6 weeks after last dose (on Week 15 or 16 of the study) due to fasting glucose levels exceeding 200 mg/dL. Data collected after the reinitiation of glucose‐lowering therapy were omitted from the analysis.

Fasting glucose levels slowly increased over the 6 weeks post‐treatment period (Weeks 11–16 of the study) but did not reach pre‐treatment glucose levels (Figure 2). Following discontinuation of efsitora treatment, the fasting glucose concentration increased by approximately 10% at 2 weeks, 16% at 3 weeks, 29% at 4 weeks, 33% at 5 weeks, and 36% at 6 weeks.

Pharmacokinetics

Following the one‐time loading dose, model‐predicted clinical steady‐state (defined as a trough concentration above 90% of the steady‐state trough level 14 was achieved between the fourth and sixth weekly dose of efsitora (Figure 3A).

The dashed line indicates the threshold for clinical steady‐state and the shaded area indicates efsitora concentrations at clinical steady‐state. Circles indicate individual values (n = 53). (B) Model‐predicted mean steady‐state efsitora concentration versus time profile following SC administration of a 4 U/kg once‐weekly dose. At steady‐state, median time to maximum efsitora concentration was achieved at 2.5 days post dose. Efsitora had a peak‐to‐trough ratio of 1.16 (Figure 3B) with week‐to‐week variability (CV%) of 5% and a half‐life of 19 days.

The model accurately captured the GIR profile during the three‐clamp periods (Figure 4A) and supported the interpolation over the weekly dosing interval. The sinusoidal pattern was observed in each 24‐h GIR profile.

The model‐predicted GIR profile over the weekly dosing interval at steady‐state demonstrated a stable glucose‐lowering effect of efsitora (Figure 4A). At steady‐state, the maximum glucose‐lowering effect occurred between Day 4 and 5 (2.01 mg/kg/min) and the lowest glucose‐lowering effect occurred on Day 7 (1.88 mg/kg/min), resulting in a peak‐to‐trough ratio of 1.07.

The model‐predicted daily proportions of glucose lowering over the weekly dosing interval at steady‐state are shown in Figure 4B. The daily proportions of glucose‐lowering effect on Days 1 to 7 were 13.8%, 13.9%, 14.3%, 14.6%, 14.7%, 14.5%, and 14.2%, respectively. An evenly distributed glucose‐lowering effect over a week was calculated as 14.3% per day and efsitora approached this consistent daily glucose‐lowering effect with only minimal variations on some weekdays. The day‐to‐day variability (within‐subject coefficient of variation) in glucose‐lowering was 2.2%.

Figure S2 shows the normalised mean and individual blood glucose profiles during the three euglycaemic clamp periods. No significant differences were observed in the daily mean glucose targets among the three clamp procedures, and the normalised individual profile values were evenly distributed above and below the clamp target, indicating the appropriate performance of the clamp procedure. Additionally, the precision in the clamp, as measured by the coefficient of variation of the blood glucose measurement, was less than 5%, indicating the high quality of the euglycaemic clamps procedure (Table S3).

Safety

Efsitora was well tolerated in this study population. Overall, 115 treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were reported in 43 participants (76.8%) who received at least one dose of efsitora. The most commonly reported TEAEs were headache (26.8%), nasopharyngitis (21.4%), and injection site hematoma (17.9%). All reported TEAEs were mild (108) or moderate (7) in severity, and none of the TEAEs were deemed related to efsitora. One participant discontinued the study due to a reported serious adverse event (SAE) of intervertebral disc protrusion, which was not considered to be related to the study drug. No injection site reactions (predefined as erythema, induration, pain, pruritus, or oedema) were reported. There were no clinically significant changes in laboratory values or vital signs.

No severe hypoglycaemia events were reported. Overall, 36 participants (64.3%) had at least one hypoglycaemic event during the efsitora 10‐week dosing period, as assessed by CGM and confirmed by glucometer. There was a total of 346 events; 307 of these were Level 1 hypoglycaemia and 39 were Level 2 hypoglycaemia.

No participants tested positive for pre‐existing anti‐drug antibodies at baseline. One participant developed treatment‐emergent anti‐drug antibodies on Day 64, which resolved by the follow‐up visit (Day 106).

DISCUSSION

This is the first clinical study to characterise the steady‐state pharmacokinetics and glucodynamic effects of efsitora in participants with T2D. This study demonstrates the following findings: (i) clinical steady‐state of efsitora was achieved between 4 to 6 weeks using a loading dose; (ii) the PK time‐profile over a weekly interval was flat, characterised by a peak‐to‐trough ratio at steady‐state of 1.16; (iii) the daily proportions of glucose lowering effect during the 1‐week dosing interval at steady state ranged from 13.8% to 14.7% of the total weekly effect; (iv) efsitora demonstrated an even distribution of glucodynamic action with a peak‐to‐trough ratio of 1.07; and (v) following termination of efsitora, glucose levels increased by 36% at the end of the 6‐week washout period.

Pharmacokinetic samples collected throughout the study supported the analysis of the steady‐state timing, the steady‐state concentration‐time profile, and the elimination half‐life. The half‐life of efsitora in this study was approximately 19 days, consistent with a meta‐analysis indicating a half‐life of 17 days across the studies conducted. The use of a one‐time loading dose (one‐time starting dose) enabled clinical steady‐state to be achieved between the fourth and sixth weekly doses of efsitora. A one‐time loading dose of approximately 3× the calculated weekly dose of efsitora was utilised in several of the Phase 3 Once Weekly Insulin Therapy (QWINT) programs and was found to be safe and effective for achieving glycaemic control.15, 16, 17

When at steady state, the peak‐to‐trough (P/T) ratio describes the highest and lowest drug concentration and provides insight into the fluctuation of drug exposure over the dosing interval. 9 A peak‐to‐trough ratio of 1.00 reflects a PK profile without any fluctuations in exposure over the dosing interval. At steady state, the PK profile of efsitora was nearly flat, having a P/T ratio of 1.16 over the weekly dosing interval. This flat weekly insulin profile provides a consistent amount of insulin exposure throughout the day, as well as between days. In contrast, daily basal insulins exhibit variable exposure throughout the day; the P/T ratios of insulin glargine U100 and insulin degludec are approximately 2 and 1.3 over a 24 h dosing interval, respectively. 18 These fluctuations will therefore be repeated seven times over a week (the dosing interval of efsitora).

The glucose‐lowering effect was captured by the changes in magnitude of the glucose infusion rate during the euglycaemic clamp procedure. Within each 24‐h clamp period, the glucose infusion rate demonstrates a sinusoidal pattern, reflective of the diurnal fluctuations in insulin sensitivity. The glucose infusion rate is lowest in the early morning, when insulin sensitivity is reduced, and highest throughout the day as sensitivity increases. 19 A similar sinusoidal pattern in the glucose infusion rate has been observed in euglycaemic clamp procedures with other long‐acting insulins.11, 20 These fluctuations reflect physiological processes and occur regardless of exogenous insulin administration. 19

The three 24‐h euglycaemic clamps covering the start, middle, and end of the dosing interval enabled the model‐based interpolation of efsitora's weekly glucose‐lowering profile. At steady‐state, the maximal glucose‐lowering effect of efsitora was observed between Days 4 and 5, while the minimal effect occurred on Day 7, resulting in a glucose lowering peak‐to‐trough ratio of 1.07. This corresponds to a daily proportion of glucose‐lowering effect during the weekly dosing interval that ranged from 13.8% to 14.7% of the total weekly effect. A completely even distribution across the week was 14.3% per day, and efsitora approached this consistent daily glucose lowering effect with only minimal variations on some weekdays. This data demonstrates that efsitora provided consistently stable glucose‐lowering throughout the week. Importantly, this consistency in glucose‐lowering effect reduced the day‐to‐day within‐subject variability, measured at 2.2% for efsitora.

Glucose levels were monitored following the termination of treatment. Fasting glucose rose gradually over 6 weeks but remained below pre‐treatment values. By Week 6, mean glucose rose by 36%, a modest change considering the tight glucose control in these participants. Only 4 of the 53 participants needed to resume their prestudy glucose‐lowering medication. These findings demonstrate that, given the long half‐life, efsitora may continue to provide a glucose‐lowering effect for weeks after treatment discontinuation.

Efsitora was generally well tolerated among the study population, and none of the reported hypoglycaemia events were classified as severe. The interpretability of these results may be limited as this relatively small clinical pharmacology study used a fixed dose without individual dose adjustments or a contemporary comparator group. By comparison, in the four Phase 3 studies from the QWINT program in adults with type 2 diabetes, efsitora demonstrated glycaemic control and rates of clinically significant hypoglycaemia similar to daily basal insulin comparators.15, 16, 17, 21

The clinical relevance of these findings becomes evident when comparing efsitora to other once‐weekly basal insulins, such as icodec. Icodec's half‐life is approximately 1 week. 22 While its P/T ratio hasn't been formally reported, it's estimated to be approximately 1.8 from its half‐life and published steady‐state PK data. 7 Efsitora's longer half‐life contributes to a lower peak‐to‐trough ratio and a more even distribution of insulin exposure at steady state, which may help reduce the risk of hypoglycaemia; however, direct head‐to‐head comparisons between efsitora and icodec have not been conducted. The longer half‐life of efsitora means that it takes more time to achieve steady‐state, but this can be substantially accelerated with a loading dose. Frequent dose adjustment is difficult with long‐acting once‐daily basal insulins and could be challenging with once‐weekly insulins. Nevertheless, the low peak‐to‐trough ratio of efsitora enables people with type 2 diabetes to achieve good glycaemic control with low hypoglycaemia rates using a simple dosing algorithm, potentially overcoming several practical barriers to insulin therapy. This is supported by results from the QWINT Phase 3 programme, in adults with T2D where efsitora demonstrated glycaemic control and rates of clinically significant hypoglycaemia comparable to daily basal insulin comparators.

The study's strengths included a fixed dose which enabled participants to achieve steady‐state at the same time, facilitating the timing of the steady‐state clamp procedure. Also, the study used individualised glucose clamp targets, which ensured that clamp data was captured from all participants and eliminated the need for exogenous insulin to achieve the glucose clamp targets, which would have confounded the characterisation of the steady‐state glucodynamics of efsitora. Limitations included the use of intermittent clamp procedures to cover the weekly profile due to fasting requirements and need to provide adequate recovery time between clamp procedures; however, this method has been effectively used for other weekly insulin studies.11, 20, 23, 24 The fixed‐dose approach prevented individual titration, potentially resulting in overdosing some participants during efsitora treatment. Lastly, these findings are specific to individuals with T2D and should not be generalised to those with T1D. Although the majority of participants were male, the PK/PD model did not identify any sex‐related influence on efsitora PK or GIR profiles.

In conclusion, efsitora demonstrated a flat and prolonged PK profile enabling a constant level of insulin over the weekly dosing interval. As a result of this PK profile, efsitora showed a consistently stable glucose‐lowering response across the weekly dosing interval with low day‐to‐day variability.
