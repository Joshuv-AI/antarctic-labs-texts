Eye check-up practice and its associated factors among adult diabetic patients attending primary hospitals of Central Gondar Zone, Northwest Ethiopia, 2024: a cross-sectional multicentre study
Authors: Getachew Kassahun Desalegn, Getenet Shumet Birhan, Zinachew Mulat Bogale, Akililu Libasie Assefa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to assess eye check-up practice and associated factors among patients with diabetes attending primary hospitals in the Central Gondar Zone, Northwest Ethiopia.DesignA multicentre hospital-based cross-sectional study.SettingThis study was conducted at primary hospitals in Central Gondar Zone, Northwest Ethiopia, from 10 June 2024 to 10 July 2024.ParticipantsThe study included 689 consecutive adult patients with diabetes who met the inclusion criteria.Primary and secondary outcome measuresParticipants were diagnosed with diabetes mellitus by a general practitioner. Optometrists conducted interviews, reviewed medical records and administered a pretested, structured questionnaire. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify associated factors, with significance considered at a p<0.05 and 95% CI.ResultsA total of 689 participants were involved in this study, with a response rate of 98.4%. The proportion of good eye check-up practice was 20.6% (95% CI: 17.6% to 23.8%). Factors associated with good eye check-up practice included urban residence (adjusted OR, AOR=4.10, 95% CI: 1.72 to 9.82), diabetes duration of 12+ years (AOR=4.98, 95% CI: 1.43 to 17.38), previous eye disease (AOR=3.78, 95% CI: 1.79 to 7.95), good knowledge of diabetic retinopathy (AOR=3.22, 95% CI: 1.42 to 7.29), higher family income (AOR=3.80, 95% CI: 1.27 to 11.36), referral from a diabetic clinic (AOR=3.48, 95% CI: 1.58 to 7.67) and a favourable attitude (AOR=3.75, 95% CI: 1.46 to 9.65).ConclusionsThis study revealed a low proportion of good eye check-up practices among patients with diabetes. Urban residency, longer duration of diabetes, higher income, clinic referrals, history of eye disease, knowledge of diabetic retinopathy and a favourable attitude were significantly associated with good eye check-up practices. Therefore, targeted health education and strengthened referral systems are recommended to improve regular eye check-up practices among individuals with diabetes.Keywords: General diabetes, Cardiovascular Disease, Chronic Disease

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a growing global epidemic with far-reaching health and economic consequences for both developed and developing countries.1 2 As of 2024–2025, approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years are living with diabetes worldwide, which corresponds to about one in nine adults globally.3 4 This number is projected to rise to 853 million by 2050 and even over 1.3 billion by 2050 according to some projections.3 5 Diabetic eye disease, in particular, has become a major concern. Diabetes can affect nearly every structure of the eye, with diabetic retinopathy (DR), refractive changes, dry eye, diplopia, cataracts, glaucoma and corneal complications being the most commonly reported ocular manifestations.2 6 An estimated 60% individuals with DM experience one or more ocular complications,7 and in sub-Saharan Africa alone, 4.51 million people with diabetes are affected by some form of diabetic-related eye disease.8 The prevalence of DR is notably high, with Africa reporting the highest rate globally at 35.90%,1 while in Ethiopia, the prevalence is 19.48%.9 Ocular complications of DM can be largely prevented through proper management of blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol levels. However, the most effective and cost-efficient strategy for prevention is regular eye check-ups. Routine screenings not only aid in early detection but also prompt timely interventions and treatments when necessary, significantly reducing the risk of vision loss and visual impairment.4 In fact, regular eye examinations can prevent up to 95% of vision loss caused by diabetes.10

Without early screening and regular eye check-ups, DR develops in almost all persons with type 1 diabetes and 60% of those with type 2 diabetes after 20 years, largely due to the asymptomatic nature of DR.11 Failure to engage in routine eye examinations among individuals with diabetes significantly increases the risk of vision loss, as the control of hyperglycaemia and other risk factors alone is generally insufficient to fully prevent DR.12

The WHO recommends regular screening for DR as an effective intervention to prevent vision impairment and blindness in individuals with diabetes.13 Similarly, the American Diabetes Association10 and the International Council of Ophthalmology14 have issued guidelines recommending that adults with diabetes undergo comprehensive eye examinations at least once a year.

Several developed countries, including Iceland,15 Denmark16 and the UK,17 have implemented national DR screening programmes to improve access to eye care and reduce the burden of visual impairment and blindness caused by DR. In England, all individuals with diabetes aged 12 and older are invited to attend annual eye screening appointments, resulting in an 80% increase in eye check-up uptake among individuals with diabetes.18

Despite the recommendation for annual eye check-ups, nearly half of individuals with diabetes do not undergo the recommended exams.19 Non-adherence to vision care guidelines among diabetic individuals remains a persistent and complex health issue in developed countries.20 21 This challenge is likely more pronounced in developing countries, where screening programmes are lacking, and most of the individuals diagnosed with diabetes do not follow the recommended ocular examinations to prevent visual impairment and blindness from DR.22,24 Various factors, including economic, social and health system-related challenges, as well as issues related to awareness and knowledge, influence the practice of regular eye check-ups.19 25 26

Evidence on eye check-up practices among persons with DM is essential for promoting the prevention of visual impairment and blindness. However, there is limited information on this topic in Ethiopia, particularly in the study area. Although previous studies have assessed eye check-up practices in Ethiopia, the literature review indicates a lack of research specifically targeting primary hospitals, particularly within the Central Gondar Zone. Additionally, this study employed a multicentre approach, which is important for enhancing the generalisability of the findings and incorporated additional variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing eye check-up practices. Therefore, this study aimed to assess eye check-up practices and associated factors among patients with diabetes attending primary hospitals in the Central Gondar Zone, Northwest Ethiopia. The findings of this study will play a crucial role in reducing the future burden of blindness and visual impairment due to DM.

Materials and methods

Study design, area and period

This multicentre, hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted in selected public primary hospitals in the Central Gondar Zone, Northwest Ethiopia, from 10 June 2024 to 10 July 2024. The Central Gondar Zone, located in the Amhara Regional State, is 737 km from Addis Ababa and 168 km from Bahir Dar. It consists of 15 districts and Gondar City, with a population of 2 669 319 as of July 2023, according to the Ethiopian Statistics Service.27 The zone has nine primary hospitals serving approximately 1.6 million people, along with one tertiary eye care centre and three private specialty eye clinics. Only two of the primary hospitals have ophthalmic clinics, each staffed by one ophthalmic nurse. On average, 2500 patients with diabetes are treated monthly at these hospitals. For this study, Sanja, Dembiya and Wogera Primary Hospitals were selected, with monthly diabetic patient services averaging 230, 346 and 314, respectively.

Source and study population

The source population for this study consisted of all adult patients with diabetes attending outpatient diabetic clinics in primary hospitals across the Central Gondar Zone. The study population specifically included all adult patients with diabetes attending the diabetic clinics in the three selected public primary hospitals of the Central Gondar Zone during the data collection period.

Eligibility criteria

The study includes all adults aged 18 years and older with DM who were attending the diabetic clinics at the three selected public primary hospitals. Patients who were newly diagnosed with diabetes, had less than 1 year since diagnosis or had gestational diabetes were excluded from the study.

For the first objective, a sample size of 318 was selected, as it was larger than that for the second objective. Considering a design effect of 2 for multistage sampling and a 10% non-response rate, the final required sample size was determined to be 700.

Sampling technique and procedure

In this study, a multistage sampling technique was used. The research area consisted of nine public primary hospitals that offer chronic follow-up services. Initially, three hospitals in the Central Gondar Zone: Dembiya, Wogera and Sanja were chosen using a simple random sampling method. The average number of patients with DM treated per month was calculated based on the average monthly follow-up records from the previous three Junes. The numbers were 346 at Dembiya, 314 at Wogera and 230 at Sanja Primary Hospital. The sample size was then distributed proportionally among the selected hospitals. All adults with DM who attended the outpatient diabetic clinics at these hospitals during the data collection period were included in the study.

Variables of the study

The dependent variable in this study is good eye check-up practice. Independent variables include sociodemographic factors such as age, sex, residence, marital status, educational level, occupational status, average monthly family income and the presence of health insurance. Health-related variables comprise the type and duration of DM, referrals for eye check-ups from diabetic clinics, history of eye disease, regular follow-up for DM, glycaemic control status and the presence of hypertension (HTN). Additionally, the study assesses awareness of diabetic eye disease, knowledge of DR and attitudes towards eye examinations.

Operational definition

Adult patient with diabetes

Patient with diabetes aged 18 years and older.28 29

Attitude towards eye examination

Six questions were assessed using a Likert scale with responses ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. For positive attitude questions, scores were assigned as follows: strongly agree=5, agree=4, neutral=3, disagree=2, strongly disagree=1. For negative questions, scores were reversed: strongly agree=1, agree=2, neutral=3, disagree=4, strongly disagree=5.

Favourable attitude

Respondents who answered greater than or equal to the mean attitude questions had a favourable attitude.30

Unfavourable attitude

Respondents who answered below the mean on attitude questions had an unfavourable attitude.30

Eye check-up practice

Was categorised based on patients’ experiences with dilated ocular examinations for eye disease. Patients who had undergone such examinations at least once within the past year were classified as having ‘good practice’, while those who had not received any dilated eye examination in the last year were categorised as having ‘poor practice’.31 32

Eye disease history

A participant who experienced any type of eye disease in the past year.23

Glycaemic control

Glycaemic control was classified as good if the recorded current fasting blood glucose level was less than 152 mg/dL and poor if 152 mg/dL and above.33

Knowledge

The questionnaire included 12 closed-ended questions regarding definitions, risk factors and treatments of DR. Each correct response earned one point, resulting in a total score out of 12. Participants scoring ≥6 were classified as having ‘good knowledge’, while those scoring less than 6 were categorised as having ‘poor knowledge’.23

Regular diabetic check-up

All patients who are undertaking investigations as per their primary health worker recommendations. Those who are undertaking a check-up as recommended were given a score of 1 and otherwise 0.34

Urban

Areas characterised by infrastructures such as paved streets, electric lighting and sewage systems, where the majority of the population is not primarily engaged in agriculture or where surplus employment exists.35 Areas lacking such infrastructure are classified as rural.

Data collection tool and personnel

Data were collected using a pretested, structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from previous research23 30 36 37 and chart reviews for health-related variables. The questionnaire was translated from English to Amharic for ease of communication and then back to English for consistency. It included questions assessing sociodemographic factors (age, sex, residence, marital status, education, family monthly income and occupation), health profile variables (type and duration of DM, regular follow-up, presence of HTN and history of eye disease), as well as awareness and knowledge regarding diabetic eye disease and check-up practices. Specifically, the eye check-up practice section evaluated referrals from diabetic clinics, eye check-up behaviours and reasons for not attending eye check-ups. Data collection was conducted by five trained BSc nurses and one ophthalmic nurse, supervised by three senior clinical optometrists.

Data collection procedures

All consecutive patients with diabetes attending the outpatient clinics of the study hospitals were included, as the sampling fraction (k) was 1 for the three selected hospitals (k=346/272 for Dembiya, 314/247 for Wogera and 230/181 for Sanja Primary Hospital). Participants were informed that their involvement in the study was voluntary, and the information collected would be used exclusively for academic purposes. They were also assured that they could withdraw from the study at any time. Those who agreed to participate provided oral consent. Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews with individuals with DM and a review of their medical charts.

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and then translated into Amharic, the local language used for data collection, with subsequent retranslation to ensure consistency in meaning. Prior to the actual data collection, the questionnaire was pretested at Addis Zemen Primary Hospital with 5% of the total sample size (35 individuals with DM) to assess clarity and understanding. Feedback from the pretest led to necessary modifications for improved clarity and completeness. Data collectors received training on data collection techniques before starting, and the principal investigator and supervisors checked the completeness of the collected data daily. The reliability of the questionnaire was assessed for internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha in SPSS software, yielding values of 0.814 for attitude questions, 0.825 for knowledge about DR and 0.841 for eye check-up practice questions.

Data processing and analysis

After ensuring the completeness and consistency of the data, it was entered into EPI INFO V.7 and subsequently exported to SPSS V.27 for analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all variables demonstrating a VIF of less than 2.5. Descriptive statistics, including proportions and median values, were calculated. Regarding missing data, we conducted a thorough review during data cleaning. The level of missing data was minimal. Therefore, we used complete case analysis, excluding records with missing values for any variable included in the regression model. To identify factors associated with eye check-up practices, binary logistic regression was performed. Variables with a p≤0.2 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable binary logistic regression. In this analysis, factors with a p value of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The Hosmer and Lemeshow test (for model fit) yielded a p value of 0.217, indicating good model fit. The strength of associations was expressed as adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% CIs.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Awareness about diabetic eye disease, knowledge about DR and attitude towards eye examination of study participants

About 53.6% (369) of participants had awareness about diabetic eye disease. The proportion of good knowledge about DR was 32.5%. In addition, the proportion of favourable attitude towards eye examination was approximately 48.5% (334).

Eye check-up practice of study participants

About 39% participants responded that they were referred for eye examination by their primary health provider, in which 30% had some history of eye examination in their lifetime. However, only 20.6% (95% CI: 17.6% to 23.8%) of participants had good eye check-up practice. About 70% study participants had never had any eye examination since their diagnosis of DM.

Factors associated with good eye check-up practice of patients with diabetes

After each variable was entered to bivariable binary logistic regression; variables with p≤0.2 were considered significant. Age, residence, marital status, occupational status, educational level, average family monthly income, duration of DM, presence of HTN, eye disease history, referral from diabetic clinic for eye check-up, knowledge about DR and attitude towards eye examination were significant on bivariable binary logistic regression model. In the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, residence, duration of DM, average family monthly income, eye disease history, referral from diabetic clinic for eye check-up, knowledge of DR and attitude towards eye examination were significantly associated with good eye check-up practice.

Urban residents were about four times (AOR=4.10, 95% CI: 1.72 to 9.82) more likely to practise eye check-ups than their counterparts, rural residents.

Participants with DM duration of 12 and more years were almost five times (AOR=4.98, 95% CI:1.43 to 17.38) more likely to have annual eye check-up practice than their counterparts, those having less than 5 years of DM duration.

This study showed that participants having average family monthly income of greater than ETB4000 were nearly four times (AOR=3.80, 95% CI 1.27 to 11.36) more likely to have annual eye check-up practice than those having average family monthly income of ETB1000 or less. Similarly, having average family monthly income between 2501 and 4000 ETB was nearly three times (AOR=3.27, 95% CI 1.07 to 10.01) more likely to practise eye check-up.

The likelihood of having eye check-up practice among participants with eye disease history was nearly fourfold (AOR=3.78, 95% CI: 1.79 to 7.95) than those who had no eye disease history.

Participants who were referred for ocular examination by primary health provider at diabetic clinic were about 3.5 times (AOR=3.48, 95% CI: 1.58 to 7.67) more likely to get dilated eye examinations than those who were not referred.

The odds of annual eye check-up practice among participants who had adequate knowledge about DR were 3.22 times (AOR=3.22, 95% CI: 1.42 to 7.29) more as compared with those who had poor knowledge about DR.

Participants with a favourable attitude towards eye examinations were approximately four times (AOR=3.75, 95% CI; 1.46 to 9.65) more likely to have annual dilated eye check-up practice than those with an unfavourable attitude (table 4).

Discussion

The proportion of good eye check-up practice in this study was 20.6% (95% CI: 17.6% to 23.8%). This result was in line with previous studies conducted in Ghana,38 which were reported as 21.7%, India 20.8%39 and Bangladesh 22%.40 Similarities in study design, methodology and population with the current study could be the reason for similarity of results.

In contrast, this result was higher than that of the studies done in Kenya 13.3%,22 Benin 12.67%,24 Iraq 3%41 and China 10.2%.42 Difference in socioeconomic status, sample size and study setting might have caused the variation of results; for instance, the study done in Benin24 was conducted on a smaller sample size than the current study, and this might have underestimated the proportion of annual eye check-up practice in the former study. The study conducted in Iraq had excluded patients with known HTN, despite the current study including those individuals, where having comorbidity of HTN could increase the proportion of effective eye check-up practice.41 In addition, the Chinese study42 was conducted at a community-based primary health system, while the current study was done at primary hospitals.

However, the result of this study was lower than that of previous studies from Ethiopia where proportions of annual eye check-up practice were reported as 39.6%, 37% and 44.3%,23 26 30 56.8% at Dongola, Northern State, Sudan,43 41.1% in Togo,44 51.5% Mozambique,45 95% Saudi Arabia,31 India 90%,46 33%47 44.1%,48 56.8% Pakistan,49 87.2% Oman,50 Switzerland 70.5%,51 France 47.9%52 and USA 69%.19 Possible reasons for variation in the proportion of results might be differences in socioeconomic status, educational level of participants and level of health setting. For instance, in the study done at Debark, Ethiopia,23 good eye check-up practice was measured by considering any eye examination in the past 1 year, despite this study considering dilated eye examination. Considering ‘any eye examination’ instead of ‘dilated eye examination’ might have caused overestimation of eye check-up practice in the Debark study.

The Saudi Arabian study31 reported that about 80% of its study participants belonged to middle to high economic status. Higher economic status increases eye health service uptake, evidenced by the current study, which showed that higher eye check-up practice among individuals with higher monthly income. Additionally, the majority (55.25%) of their study participants had secondary school and above educational status while the current study was dominated by participants without formal education. The higher the education level, the higher the chance of having annual dilated eye examinations.25 38 The Indian study47 was conducted among an urban setting; this might have caused variation of results from the current study since the current study comprised both urban and rural participants. This is because urban residents tend to get better access to healthcare facilities, greater health awareness and more frequent exposure to health education in urban areas. In addition to the above, urban environments often offer more specialised eye care services and routine check-up opportunities, which are less accessible in rural areas.

Previous studies done in Ethiopia, Sudan, Togo, Mozambique, Saudi Arabia, India, Pakistan, Oman, Switzerland, France and the USA were conducted at tertiary level health centres. Tertiary healthcare usually comprises specialty eye care services including DR screening and treatment. This might enable diabetic persons in previous studies to have eye check-up practice. Despite the current study being conducted at a primary hospital, where there is no dilated eye examination service. Hence, patients need to go to the next level of eye care in order to get dilated eye check-up services.

The Indian study46 was conducted among diabetic persons attending the ophthalmic outpatient department. This might overestimate the proportion of eye check-up practice as patients at eye care centre tend to get continuous counselling from eye care professionals and are highly likely to have routine practice of eye check-ups.

In this study, longer duration of diabetes was significantly associated with annual eye check-up practice, in which participants with a diabetes duration of greater than or equal to 12 years were nearly four times more likely to have their eyes checked when compared with participants with a DM duration of ≤5 years. This finding is supported by the studies done in Ethiopia,23 South Africa,37 Saudi Arabia,53 Bangladesh25 and Turkey.54 The possible reason for this may be a longer duration of DM increasing their exposure and knowledge to eye screening due to continued counselling and health education programmes.

This study revealed that urban residents were almost five times more likely to have annual dilated eye check-ups than their rural counterparts. This is in agreement with studies done in Southeast Ethiopia,30 Saudi Arabia53 and Singapore.55 This may be due to better access to healthcare facilities, greater health awareness and more frequent exposure to health education in urban areas. Additionally, urban environments often offer more specialised eye care services and routine check-up opportunities, which are less accessible in rural areas. The higher literacy rates and socioeconomic status often found in urban populations may also contribute to a greater understanding of the importance of regular eye check-up practice in managing diabetes and preventing complications.25 38

The likelihood of good eye check-up practice among participants having average family monthly income of greater than ETB4000 was nearly four times more than those having ETB1000 or less average family monthly income. This could be justified as the unavailability of nearby adequate eye care centre needs extra expenses for transport and other services,26 though most of the participants have community-based health insurance. Hence, having higher family monthly income enabled participants to have effective eye check-ups more than those having lower average family monthly income.

Participants with a history of eye disease in the last year were about four times more likely to have routine eye check-up practice when compared with those without a history of eye disease. A similar finding was reported in previous research conducted at Debark, Ethiopia.23 This could be explained as individuals get motivated to have eye examinations due to their illnesses.

Participants with adequate knowledge about DR were about 4.5 times more likely to have annual eye check-up practice as compared with those who had poor knowledge. This was supported by previous studies done in Ethiopia,23 Sudan,43 Ghana38 and Bangladesh.40 This might be due to knowledge about DR helping individuals to understand the need for eye screening and eye check-up after knowing that DR can have treatment options and having knowledge about DR might have initiated participants to know the status of their eye for ocular complications of diabetes.

Participants who were referred for ocular examination by primary health provider at diabetic clinic were about 3.5 times more likely to have effective annual eye check-up practice than those who were not. A similar finding was reported in a study conducted in Kenya.22 This could be explained as a referral by primary healthcare workers from the diabetic clinic being a potential reason for getting an annual eye check-up practice due to patients’ high trust in the information provided by their primary health providers. Different studies supported this, in which the majority of participants are said to be willing to get their eyes checked if recommended by their primary physicians.39 49

Furthermore, participants with a favourable attitude towards eye examinations were about four times more likely to get routine eye check-up practice when compared with their counterparts, those having an unfavourable attitude. This finding is supported by studies from Southeast Ethiopia30 and Oman.50 A possible reason for this could be the worry of losing vision, which might affect participants to develop a positive attitude towards eye examinations, and this, in turn, might obligate them to have routine eye check-up practice.

Mostly cited reasons for not getting eye check-ups were misconceptions about the need for eye check-ups due to lack of visual symptoms in 67.8% participants, which is supported by previous studies conducted in Ethiopia,23 Sudan43 and Switzerland,51 followed by financial restrictions in 17.3% and those who felt they needed to be referred by their physician in 10.2%.

Conclusions

About one-fifth of our study participants had good eye check-up practice. This result is low compared with previous studies. The finding underscores the need for targeted public health interventions to promote regular eye examinations and improve overall eye care practices. Good eye check-up practice was positively associated with urban residence, higher average family monthly income, longer duration of DM, eye disease history, good knowledge about DR and favourable attitude towards eye examination. This reflects the importance of tailored interventions that enhance awareness, promote positive health-seeking behaviours and address socioeconomic and geographic disparities to improve diabetic eye care outcomes.
