Urban land use of national economic sectors in Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area from 2015–2022
Authors: Shiting Li, Qianyuan Huang, Meirong Su, Chao Xu, Yanmin Teng, Yuan Zhang, Qionghong Chen, Yuanchao Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Land underpins nearly all human activities, with different economic sectors exhibiting varying degrees of dependence on land resources. Sector-specific land use analysis is essential for optimizing resource allocation, improving land-use efficiency, and promoting sustainable urban development. However, existing land-use classification data are often coarse-grained and lack the detail needed to capture land use at the level of national economic sectors. To address this limitation, this study constructs a sector-specific land use dataset covering 97 and 42 national economic sectors across 11 cities in the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area for the period 2015–2022. This dataset enables systematic examination of sectoral land use patterns, identification of high land-consuming and low-efficiency sectors, and provides empirical evidence to guide industrial restructuring, spatial planning, and resource–environment policy design. Moreover, the dataset can be integrated with complementary indicators (e.g., carbon emissions, water footprints) to support ecological management and low-carbon development strategies.

Background & Summary

Land is a fundamental resource underpinning production, living, and ecological functions, serving as the foundation for both societal development and ecosystem stability1–3. However, the supply of construction land in China has continued to decline. In 2024, the supply of state-owned construction land was 6.061 million hectares, marking a year-on-year decrease of 19.1%4. Meanwhile, rapid urbanization and industrialization have exerted increasing pressures on land resources in terms of spatial occupation, functional allocation, and ecological conservation3,5,6. This challenge is particularly pronounced in resource-intensive regions, where efficient land utilization has become a critical constraint for sustainable development7,8. According to the China Natural Resources Bulletin, the country has sought to alleviate this situation by actively developing marine resources4,9. At the same time, China’s urban development model is shifting from incremental expansion to stock renewal10. Against this backdrop, systematically analysing land use across economic sectors is essential for optimizing resource allocation, improving spatial planning, and safeguarding ecological functions11.

The Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area (GBA), one of the most economically dynamic urban agglomerations in China, characterized by concentrated industries and high population density, is undergoing crucial urbanization and industrial transformation12,13. The region exhibits three distinctive features: (1) intensive land development, (2) pronounced industrial heterogeneity, and (3) complex land-use patterns. Together, these create dual constraints stemming from rigid resource availability and limited development space14–16. The GBA’s land system is in a highly dynamic process of restructuring17, with economic sectors displaying significant disparities in both dependence on and intensity of land use18. For example, certain land-intensive but low-output industries—such as furniture manufacturing, hardware processing, glass processing, and packaging and printing in Foshan city, —continue to occupy large tracts of land, intensifying ecological stress and spatial tension18–20. Moreover, urban villages and old industrial areas often suffer from scattered layouts and inefficient utilization of existing construction land. To address this, nine cities in Guangdong within the GBA have been designated as pilot areas for the redevelopment of inefficient land21. Therefore, a systematic accounting of land use across for GBA is imperative to identify structural patterns of land resource utilization and to provide scientific support for regional land governance and industrial policy formulation22.

Substantial progress has been made in areas such as land-use efficiency8,23, land-use change2,24–26, land expansion1,27, land-use and land-cover datasets28–30, ecosystem services31,32, and cropland–resource nexuses33–35, but the existing studies or public datasets either mainly focused on agricultural systems or limited to broad land categories. For instance, the FAOSTAT database of the Food and Agricultural Organization of the United Nations36 and the Eurostat by the European Commission37 primarily emphasize natural or agricultural land. As for category, the existing land use classification studies can be broadly categorized into physically based land-cover mapping and functionally oriented land-use identification38. The former focus on the high-resolution, temporally continuous land-cover datasets that characterize physical surface properties, such as the Global Land Cover Characterization (GLCC) datasets39, the Resource and Environment Science and Data Center datasets40 and the specific study41. The latter focuses on the urban functional-zone identification to represent socioeconomic attributes42–45. Efforts have long been made to refined land use classification. For example, the CORINE Land Cover dataset further subdivides artificial areas into (1) urban fabric, (2) industrial, commercial and transport, (3) mine, dump and construction sites, and (4) artificial non-agricultural vegetated area46. The Chinese national classification systems (GB/T 21010–2017), distinguishes 12 primary land-use categories, with more detailed secondary classes, such as separating industrial, mining, and warehouse land into corresponding land-use categories47. Nevertheless, with the continuous advancement of urban development and research demands, land products are increasingly required to provide more detailed land-use information that can explicitly capture intra-industrial heterogeneity48,49. However, in these classification systems, the industrial land is still treated as a single terminal category, which constrains their ability to reflect structural differences among industrial sectors. Similar limitations also apply to other land-use categories. The products of land use classification with fine-grained classification schemes are still limite50. As a result, the existing products are often unable to adequately capture the land use associated with specific economic activities51, thereby limiting analyses of industrial structure, spatial distribution, and land-use efficiency, hindering the practical applications in land optimization and refined urban land management.

Land use based on national economic sectors is well suited to meet the above requirements. First, this classification scheme provides more fine-grained sectoral land-use information that can be integrated with different types of economic activities, thereby helping to reveal how diverse economic activities occupy land. Second, the industry attributes of economic entities are defined based on the national economic industry classification, enabling integration with multi-source big data and aligning with the research needs in the era of big data. Third, the sectoral-based classification can be merged and made compatible with existing national land-use classification system and the input-output table categories, facilitates the integration of land and economic systems. Some scholars have successfully extracted land-use data across 42 sectors by applying either term-frequency methods or deep learning models such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, combined with the Voronoi Diagram or proportional allocation techniques18,51–55. Given that some of these approaches demand extensive original data and the demand of sector-specific land-use data in GBA, this study constructs a spatially analysable dataset of land use across national economic sectors (Industrial Classification for National Economic Activities (GB/T 4754-2017) issued by the National Bureau of Statistics of China)56, derived from the integration of Points of Interest (POIs) data, road network data and impervious surface data.

This database offers sector-specific land-use data along with spatial information, which can be applied in a diverse range of socioeconomic and interdisciplinary issues. Its most distinctive feature lies in its ability to be directly integrated with the economic system (e.g., in combination with input-output table), enabling the characterization of how economic activities occupy land resources at the sectoral level and thereby compensating for the limited representation of socioeconomic attributes in traditional land-use products. On this basis, the database supports land-use optimization and refined management, nexus analyses among land and others elements, assessments of environmental and ecological impacts and so on. Overall, it could offer scientific evidence for land management and decision-making in GBA.

Stage 1: Economic sector classification using the BERT model

Enterprise names are required to included terms reflecting the industry or business characteristics. These terms should correspond to the enterprise’s primary operations and the national economic industry classification standards. Accordingly, a mapping relationship can be established between enterprise names and their respective economic sectors. The mapping relationship was performed using a BERT model tailored to categorize enterprises according to national economic sector standards. The process involved three key steps: data preprocessing, model fine-tuning, and classification of POIs. Enterprise names in the training dataset were first converted into structurally consistent input vectors. After being transformed into word embeddings, these names were processed through multiple Transformer layers of the BERT model, which captured semantic features and generated a special classification token57,58. This token was then used to predict the corresponding economic sector. Model parameters were optimized by minimizing the discrepancy between predicted categories and actual labels. Through iterative fine-tuning, the best-performing model was selected and applied to classify previously unlabeled POIs. This was treated as a single-label classification task, with each enterprise assigned to one economic sector category.Selection of the BERT pre-trained model. Stage 2: Delineation of Urban Street Block

The fundamental data sources were as follows: road network data were obtained from OpenStreetMap (OSM) via GeoFabrik60 (https://download.geofabrik.de); administrative boundary data were provided by the Resource and Environmental Science Data Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences40 (https://www.resdc.cn/DOI/DOI.aspx?DOIID=121); and impervious surface data were drawn from the annual 30 m resolution land cover dataset for China (1985–2023) released by a Chinese research group61,62. The impervious surface data, provided in raster format (.tif), were processed using ArcGIS clipping and extraction tools to generate impervious area layers for the 11 GBA cities from 2015 to 2022, forming the basis for subsequent urban street block delineation.Road network data processing. This section involved four main steps: road classification filtering, centerline extraction, topology checking, and buffer generation. Since the selection of road hierarchy will affect the block delineation, with lower-class roads leads to smaller and more fragmented blocks. Therefore, in the first step, based on the existing studies and the hierarchical of the OSM data (the “fclass” field in the OSM data)18,52, six road categories were retained: motorway, trunk, primary, secondary, tertiary, and unclassified. Roads within the same category were then merged. Since most road types (except tertiary and unclassified) are represented as dual lines, centerlines were extracted for these dual-line segments. Next, topological checks were conducted to correct errors such as dangling nodes, pseudo-connections, and other inconsistencies in the road network. Subsequently, for the road buffer widths designing, since the road buffer width may affect the land-use estimation, with wider buffers reducing and narrower buffers increasing block area. Therefore, the buffer widths were determined based on the following two considerations. According to the Guangdong Province General Trunk Highway Design Standardization Guide (Trial) issued by the Guangdong Provincial Department of Transportation63, the Urban Road Design Standards published by the Shenzhen government64, both standards specify that the minimum design width of a mixed-traffic lane is 3.75 m (speeds above 60 km/h) or 3.5 m (speeds below 60 km/h). In addition, by integrating the cross-section design schemes provided in the Guangdong guidelines (e.g., dual-direction eight-lane and six-lane configurations), investigation of the roads of GBA and the existing studies18,52, the road buffer widths ranging from 4 to 20 meters were assigned to different road types in this study (Table 3). Using 2019 as a temporal reference point associated with the COVID-19 pandemic, the study period is divided into two stages, 2015–2018 and 2019–2022. Then the representative years are selected from the midpoint of each stage to characterize stage-level spatial structural features. Stage 3: Land use accounting by sector

The Voronoi Diagram (VD), also known as Thiessen polygons, is a widely used spatial model suitable for high-density urban built-up areas, especially in areas where a single dominant function prevails65. It partitions a set of points into regions based on equidistant divisions between neighboring points66. There are existing studies that have adopted this approach to estimate the sectoral land use at different scale such as city, urban agglomeration, and country, which has been successfully applied in land use metabolism analyses and biodiversity impacts studies18,52,55. The resulting POI boundaries can be regarded as the minimum functional attribution spaces associated with each POI, representing their relative spatial occupation within the urban environment. Furthermore, these studies also suggest that such simplifications do not significantly compromise the characterization of spatial patterns and structural features from city to national scales. Therefore, in this study, VD was applied to delineate land-use boundaries for each POI within the urban street block parcels. The procedure was as follows: first, the Voronoi polygon tool was used to generate polygons representing the land parcel associated with each POI, forming the POI boundary layer. Second, this boundary layer was clipped using the urban street block layer, and multipart features were split to derive the actual impervious surface area occupied by each POI. Finally, the resulting parcels were labelled and aggregated by economic sector, producing land-use statistics for specific sectors. During the process, as urban roads closely correspond to actual parcel boundaries and functional separations, constraining Voronoi cells by urban street blocks effectively reduces unrealistic spatial over extension and substantially improves consistency with real urban spatial structures, thereby narrowing the gap between theoretical Voronoi allocation and realistic urban land units.

In summary, the annual land-use series by sector for 2015–2022 were constructed by integrating POIs, road network data, and impervious surface data. For 2015–2018, annual POIs and impervious surface data were used, with the 2017 road network data serving as a representative of road conditions during this period. Similarly, for 2019–2022, annual POIs and impervious data were combined with the 2021 road network data, which were assumed to represent the road network conditions for this period.

Data Records

The economic sectoral land use dataset67 is publicly available at figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.29941397), which provides land use data for 97 and 42 national economic sectors across the 11 cities of the GBA from 2015 to 2022. It is stored in a file named “GBA_Land_Use”. The file contains eight subfolders named by year (2015–2022). Each year-based subfolder further contains 11 city-named folders, and each city folder stores the corresponding economic sector land-use data. The dataset is stored in shapefile (.shp) format and includes fields for FID, geometry (Shape), name of 97 economic sectors and 42 economic sectors (both in Chinese and English), name of city, and area, with the area measured in square kilometers.

Technical Validation

Model performance evaluation

During each training iteration, the development (dev) loss was recorded, and the model exhibiting the lowest dev loss was selected to predict unlabeled enterprise names. The fine-tuned model encoded the enterprise names and assigned each enterprise to the economic sector with the highest predicted probability. Model performance was assessed on the testing set using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. True positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives were calculated to evaluate classification quality.

Sensitivity analysis of road-network data from different years

To examine the sensitivity of the block delineation and land-use area estimation to the choice of road-network data, this study compares the road-network of 2017 and 2021. According to the Fig. 5, relative to the road network of 2017, the 2021 one leads to a general increase in impervious surface area across cities (Table S2), accompanied by a widespread increase in the number of blocks and an overall decrease in the average parcel area (Details can be found in Table S2). These results suggest that, with the continuous improvement and densification of road networks, the spatial fragmentation effect of roads is intensified, resulting in a larger number of smaller-sized block units. Although the magnitude of change varies among cities, the overall direction of change remains consistent, indicating that block delineation results exhibit good directional stability with respect to the choice of road-network year. The inter-city differences in change magnitude are mainly attributable to variations in road expansion intensity, the spatial distribution of newly added roads, and patterns of impervious surface growth. Land use allocation verification

Due to the lack of actual boundary and area data for each POI and economic sector in the GBA, indirect validation is performed using existing research data. In this study, we adopt the essential urban land use categories data of EULUC-China_201868 (https://data-starcloud.pcl.ac.cn/iearthdata/7) and EULUC-China_202269 (10.5281/zenodo.16794007) for indirect validation28,70. These two datasets are Chinese urban land-use functional zone classification datasets constructed by integrating multi-source information, including satellite images, OpenStreetMap, nighttime lights, POIs and Tencent social big data. The classification scheme was developed through the adaptation from the Chinese Standard of Land Use Classification (GB/T 21010-2017)47. The difference is that the 2022 version of the dataset is an enhanced version developed based on the 2018 version. Among them, the classification accuracy for certain city reaches up to 82.9%, and the classification accuracy for cities not involved in model training remains above 60%. After further refinement, the overall accuracy of the enhanced 2022 version increased to 72.34%. Although uncertainties remain in the classification of mixed-use areas, it exhibits a high degree of comparability with this study in terms of spatial scale and hierarchy, making it suitable for city-level cross-validation. Moreover, the economic sectors used in this study can be aggregated based on the Chinese Standard of Land Use Classification to match the categories in these two datasets for comparative analysis. Therefore, we consider this dataset to be appropriate reference for validating the results of this study.

The validation procedure involves several steps. Since the reference dataset’s classification is primarily based on categories such as residential, business office, commercial service, industrial, road, transportation stations, airport facilities, administrative, educational, medical, sport and cultural, and park and greenspace28, and given the boundary differences between our dataset and the reference datasets, a spatial intersection between our final results and the reference datasets was performed, and validation was conducted on the overlapping areas.
