Design and early evaluation of a social care network's impact on health care costs
Authors: Kathleen Galper, Cynthia Miguel, Keith LeJeune, Jillian M Rung, Brad Eddy, Amit Kale, Maggie Schano, Emily Brignone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Social Care Network (SCN) is a payor–provider-system integrated social care referral model designed to address historical challenges of scalability and sustainability through structured partnerships with community-based organizations (CBOs) and incentives for referral tracking and service delivery.MethodsTo evaluate the effect of the SCN on completion of referral updates (ie, loop-closure), receipt of services, and medical costs and utilization, we conducted a quasi-experimental difference-in-differences analysis among insured adults referred to the SCN (8/2022-10/2024). Medical costs and utilization were compared between individuals who did and did not receive services. Program outcomes were compared between SCN and non-SCN CBOs.ResultsOf 2305 SCN CBO referrals, 85% were closed-loop versus 24% of 8288 non-SCN CBO referrals (P < .001); 27% of SCN referrals resulted in services received versus 17% of non-SCN referrals (P < .001). Receiving social care was associated with a $617 reduction in per-member-per-month medical costs (P < .001). Inpatient admissions declined by 26 per 1000 members per month (P < .001) and unplanned admissions by 9 (P = .002).ConclusionsThese results suggest that a scalable, sustainable social care network can be implemented within a health system. Embedding community-based social care in value-based contracts aligns accountability across sectors, increases successful referrals, and reduces downstream medical costs.Keywords: real-world evidence, social determinants, community based organization, social health integration, alternative payment models, medical costs, healthcare utilization

Introduction

There is broad recognition that social determinants of health (SDOH)—conditions such as food insecurity, housing instability, transportation barriers, and social isolation—substantially shape morbidity, mortality, and health care utilization.1-3 These mutually reinforcing negative health and social factors may compound, leading to chronic disease exacerbation, missed preventive care, and overuse of emergent services.4,5 This recognition, coupled with the continued rise of expenditures despite advances in medical technology and care delivery, has accelerated interest and investment in social care interventions.6,7

Despite growing investment, few social care models have demonstrated scalable, financially sustainable integration within health systems.8 While many health care systems screen for SDOH and have implemented pilot programs to address social needs, these programs often lack sustainable financing or rigorous evaluation.9-11 As a result, the question of whether addressing social needs meaningfully affects downstream health care utilization remains largely unanswered. This lack of evidence and sustainability is particularly consequential for payor–provider organizations, which must deliver value while mitigating rising costs. For these organizations, the central question is not whether social care is important, but whether social care can be delivered in a way that improves health outcomes and equity, reduces expenditures, and is financially sustainable over the long term.12 Truly sustainable social care may necessitate innovative approaches that strategically integrate the resources and accountability of health care systems with those of community-based organizations (CBOs).

CBOs have long played a role in supporting vulnerable populations, but often operate independently of the health care ecosystem—leading to fragmented care and missed opportunities for demonstrating their positive impacts on health.13-15 Referral-makers rarely know whether patients ultimately received help. In addition to a lack of follow-up and data linkage, increasing referrals from the health care system in the absence of compensation mechanisms strains CBO resources, forcing them to turn away help-seekers when funding is depleted.16,17

The Accountable Health Communities (AHC) Model, launched in 2017 by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, represented a landmark effort to test structured mechanisms for linking beneficiaries with community resources to meet social needs.18 Findings from this initiative are promising but mixed. While they demonstrate the potential benefits of providing navigation services for social needs, including reductions in emergency department visits and inpatient admissions among participating Medicaid and Medicare beneficiaries,19 they also underscore persistent challenges. Evaluations identified barriers such as limited availability of community resources, slow progress in developing effective performance measures and formal accountability structures, and difficulty fostering trust and coordination between health care organizations and community-based partners.17,20-22

Building on lessons from the AHC and previous referral initiatives, Highmark Health, a large integrated healthcare delivery and financing organization, launched a social care network (SCN) in 2022 with philanthropic support from the Richard King Mellon Foundation. This model created an infrastructure designed not just to screen and refer, but to “close the loop” on referrals, incentivize CBOs for their services, and evaluate program impacts on health care outcomes. The goal was to develop lasting and financially sustainable partnerships with community organizations, ultimately moving beyond the pilot phase and generating evidence to guide policy.

There are 2 objectives to this study. First, we examined program implementation outcomes, including loop-closure rates and service delivery, to determine whether the network improved accountability and communication between health care and community organizations. Second, we analyzed whether referred individuals who received services saw a reduction in health care costs and utilization compared with those who did not. Our central hypothesis is that addressing social needs reduces costly utilization, particularly emergency department visits and inpatient admissions, thereby lowering health care spending.

Methods

Program design and implementation

Before the SCN, clinicians and community partners lacked reliable communication channels. Referral-makers seldom received outcome feedback, preventing accountability and evaluation. To address this infrastructure gap, the team adopted a resource and referral platform.23 Factors driving platform selection included secure data exchange, extensive community resource directory, and user familiarity.

Single sign-on integration with the health system's electronic medical record and payer care management systems enabled clinical staff to seamlessly generate referrals during encounters without disrupting their normal workflow. Needs were generally identified through routine social needs screening, which is implemented across multiple payer and clinical settings. For a comprehensive description of the social needs screening process, including the standardized questionnaire utilized and its implementation, please refer to our previous work.24 CBOs were indexed in the resource and referral platform by the services they provide and their geographic location. Search results included both participating and nonparticipating CBOs that met search criteria, and listings for SCN CBOs were marked with a badge indicating their participation. Staff members had varying levels of familiarity with both participating and nonparticipating CBOs at the time of program launch.

CBOs received structured referral information and could input status updates, which automatically flowed into a database, enabling real-time tracking. Referral outcome categories were created for all outcome options to ensure clarity on what constituted a “closed-loop” for the CBOs (diagram available in Appendix A).

The team recruited 37 CBOs to participate in the network. Several factors were considered in CBO selection, including engagement with the resource and referral platform, proximity to areas with large numbers of members, area-level social vulnerability, and alignment between services provided and social needs reported by the local member population. While these factors informed selection, the process was flexible; for instance, some CBOs not initially engaged with the platform were still recruited and subsequently onboarded due to other strategic alignments. CBOs provided support for diverse needs, including food insecurity, energy assistance, housing, transportation, and social isolation (full list in Appendix B). The geographic focus area was strategic, as it included dense concentrations of health plan members and housed several anchor hospitals within the affiliated provider system, maximizing opportunities for referrals and providing a tight feedback cycle for the initial network implementation.

The onboarding process for CBOs included technical training in the referral platform, program profile creation, and support in adapting workflows to ensure prompt referral updates. Onboarding for clinical staff included tutorials on referral-making through single sign-on workflows to minimize data entry errors and reduce administrative burden.

A key component of the program was its incentive structure. Recognizing the time and resources required to complete more referrals and report referral statuses on the platform, the team developed incentive models that paid CBOs for their services. Importantly, payments were not only tied to referrals that resulted in “got help” status, but also to “closed-loop” referrals—those that were updated with any final disposition. This design acknowledged the complexity of service delivery, particularly for vulnerable or difficult-to-reach populations, while ensuring that the health system had visibility into referral outcomes. Dashboards were created to provide the program team and participating CBOs with near real-time data on referral volume, loop-closure rates, outcomes by service domain, and performance incentives. Community engagement specialists provided ongoing support, offering CBOs performance reports and soliciting feedback.

Quantitative study design

To determine whether the SCN improved accountability and communication, we descriptively compared rates of loop-closure and receipt of services between referrals to SCN and non-SCN CBOs. To evaluate the impact of the SCN on health care costs, we designed a quasi-experimental difference-in-differences study comparing individuals who were referred to the SCN and received services with those who were referred but did not receive services. Receipt of social care services was defined as a referral closed as “got help” by the CBO. The comparison group comprised individuals whose referrals were closed with a status other than “got help” or left unresolved. This distinction allowed us to measure the effect of receiving assistance independent from the effect of being referred.

Cohort inclusion criteria

The study included referrals from the network launch in July 2022 through October 2024. Program implementation outcomes (ie, loop-closure and receipt of services) were computed at the referral-level and included all referrals. As individual-level cost analysis requires administrative claims data and the program was not limited to health-plan members, the quasi-experimental cohort included only referred individuals enrolled in the health plan—those for whom administrative data were available during the study period. This cohort included a mix of insurance types, including commercial, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, Dual-Special Needs Plans, and Affordable Care Act plans. Multiple referrals were possible; thus, the index date was defined as the first referral date, or the first “got help” date among those who received services.

Outcomes

Implementation outcomes included referral-level loop-closure and receipt of services. The primary utilization outcome was per-member-per-month (PMPM) medical costs, chosen because it provides a comprehensive measure of financial impact and is directly relevant to program sustainability. Secondary outcomes included inpatient, outpatient/professional, and emergency department (ED) costs and utilization. Costs were defined as the summed paid amount for non-pharmaceutical claims. Utilization outcomes were defined as the total unique admission or service dates. Emergency department visits were characterized as “avoidable” per the Johns Hopkins' expanded Emergency Department Algorithm.25 Inpatient admissions not associated with planned procedures (eg, transplants, hip replacements, etc.) were characterized as “unplanned” per the Johns Hopkins' Adjusted Clinical Groups.26

Notably, behavioral health care for Pennsylvania Medicaid members is covered separately through Behavioral Health Managed Care Organizations.27 While psychiatric prescription fills and basic utilization records are available, treatment costs are unavailable. Given the high rate of behavioral health conditions in this sample, all behavioral health-related costs—claims with a primary International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis code in the “F” family—were removed from cost totals for consistency.

Covariates

Covariates were derived from the baseline period and included metrics of medical complexity, demographics, geographic measures of access and vulnerability, health care utilization, health behaviors, and programmatic details, including referral source, referral count, and estimated referral need intensity. Except for programmatic and geographic measures, all variables were sourced from administrative claims. Member demographics included gender and age at index date. Health behaviors and medical history included drug use, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI),28 frailty status, and mental health status. All factors except for CCI were dichotomized based on the presence of at least one relevant ICD-10 or Current Procedural Terminology (CPT)code in any position. Geographic measures were extracted from the CDC's 2020 Social Vulnerability Index and the Federal Office of Rural Health Policy's rurality indicator29,30 and appended using geographic joining keys per member residential address during baseline. Mental health conditions were identified using prescription fills for psychiatric medication. Utilization counts were based on the total unique service or admission dates. Though mental health care costs were unavailable, we identified encounters with a primary diagnosis code in the “F” family to quantify use of behavioral health and substance use disorder treatment. Case management engagement was defined as enrollment in any payor-sponsored case management program. The referral need intensity measure was developed as a proxy for the complexity and resources required to address different social needs. The measure leveraged a service fee schedule, developed in partnership with CBOs, which assigned referral payment amounts based on the need specified in the referral. Referral need intensity was defined as the sum of these referral service fees.

Analysis

Differences in rates of loop-closure and receipt of services between in-SCN CBOs and non-SCN CBOs were descriptively compared using chi-square tests. To estimate causal effects for individual-level utilization outcomes, we applied a difference-in-differences framework, comparing changes in costs and utilization 6 months prior to and up to 12 months after referral for those who received services relative to those who did not. To further strengthen validity, we used 1:1 propensity score matching to ensure that groups were balanced on baseline characteristics and had similar chances of loop-closure and receiving services. Characteristics representing baseline levels of outcome variables were not included in matching, with 1 exception to address a violation of parallel trends (discussed in Results). To identify departures from parallel trends, we regressed a group-by-time (months from index) interaction on baseline values of the outcome within the matched sample (full details in Appendix C). For primary outcome estimation, we fit Bayesian regression models with a group (got help vs referred) by time (month from index) by period (baseline vs follow-up) interaction and further adjusted with matching variables to address any residual confounding. Inclusion of the three-way interaction and its lower-order terms captures the difference in difference estimates for the SCN program overall and by time in follow-up (ie, deterioration or increase in effect). To capture the nuances of Bayesian estimates while aligning with conventional reporting standards, we refer to both the posterior distributions and the probability of direction, a metric highly correlated with frequentist P-value.31 Planned sensitivity analyses re-estimated models after removing costs incurred in the months immediately before and after the referral to assess robustness against regression to the mean. For additional information on effect estimation, see Appendix D. All analyses were completed in RStudio (see Appendix E for version and package information).

Results

Program implementation outcomes

From July 2022 through October 2024, 2305 referrals were made to the 37 CBOs participating in the SCN, and 8288 were made to nonparticipating CBOs. Among referrals to participating CBOs, 85% were closed-loop, compared to 24% among nonparticipating CBO referrals (P < 0.001). Among referrals to participating CBOs, 27% were marked as “got help” compared with 17% among nonparticipating CBOs (P < 0.001).

Health care cost and utilization outcomes

Among the 2305 SCN referrals, 62% (n = 1439) were made for 751 individuals who were health plan members at some point during the study period. Following exclusions for discontinuous medical coverage and missing covariate data, 464 individuals were available for 1:1 propensity score matching. Most exclusions (83%) came from a lack of medical coverage, with just 5% (n = 15) from missing covariate data (see Appendix F for full breakdown).

To achieve the best sample balance, per standardized difference and variance ratios of imbalanced covariates, we matched on need domain, insurance type, and program-specific variables. No treated individuals were unmatched. The final matched sample included 194 individuals in each group for a total of 388. We found no significant departures from the parallel trends assumption for planned outcomes, except for emergency department encounters. To address this, for the ED outcome alone, we rematched with an additional variable accounting for the change in ED visits over the baseline period at the member-level; this matched sample exhibited parallel trends. Discussion

The SCN experience demonstrates that structured partnerships with CBOs can make social care integration feasible and financially sustainable within large payer–provider ecosystems. Within this model, which combined compensation for referral updates and services rendered with shared data infrastructure and formalized communication channels, participating CBOs achieved a loop-closure rate of 85%, compared with 24% among nonparticipating CBOs. Participating CBOs also reported higher service delivery rates (27% compared to 17%). These findings suggest that the SCN substantially improved documentation of referral outcomes, particularly unsuccessful referrals, thereby generating critical information for evaluating referral pathways and program performance. Cross-sector infrastructure, including automated data sharing and standardized reporting processes, enabled the development of a comprehensive dataset that enabled analysis of health outcomes related not only to referral initiation but also to the receipt of services.

Although payment was an important catalyst for participation, monetary incentives were just one component of a broader collaborative framework. The model formalized expectations for routine communication and improved organizational alignment between CBOs and health system teams. Dedicated community engagement specialists provided ongoing support and encouraged bi-directional communication, fostering a collaborative environment. By aligning financial support with shared infrastructure and communication norms, the model established a durable framework for engagement that supports sustainable community-based service delivery amid ongoing public funding uncertainty.

The integration of the SCN within a blended payor–provider organization provided a unique opportunity to examine the impact of social care integration on health care costs. Notably, while outside of the scope of the present study, follow-up analysis of overall cost benefit found a positive net ROI even after accounting for fixed network costs and incentive payments.

Inferential analyses demonstrated substantive reductions in unplanned admissions and corresponding reductions in inpatient-related costs, along with reductions in outpatient/professional costs. To better understand the nature of these shifts, we conducted exploratory descriptive analyses of costs across more granular service categories (eg, preventive exams, inpatient care, ED, and observation care). These analyses generally showed reductions in higher-acuity care with little to no substantive changes in preventive and management-related care. Sensitivity analysis indicated that cost reductions were not limited to the period immediately surrounding the referral. Even after removing costs incurred in the months immediately before and after referral, meaningful cost reductions persisted throughout follow-up. Paired with reductions in emergency service use (ie, unplanned inpatient stays), this may reflect an improvement in self-management among those who received social care services. While these exploratory findings are promising, a full investigation into the nature of specific utilization changes and their long-term clinical implications remains a crucial area for future research.

Collectively, these findings provide real-world evidence that addressing social needs can curtail health care expenditures and justify integrating social care into financing structures.

Theoretically, these findings can be understood through the stress-buffering hypothesis,32 which posits that meeting basic needs reduces chronic stress. Lower stress translates into lower allostatic load, meaning fewer physiological wear-and-tear effects that can lead to acute health crises. Findings also align with the socio-ecological model,33 which emphasizes the interplay between individual behavior and structural supports. By strengthening available structural supports and reducing material hardship, the SCN may address pathways at multiple levels through which social needs drive costly medical utilization.

Policy implications

These findings offer several lessons for policy-makers and health care leaders working to address unsustainable spending and persistent disparities. Value-based payment models offer a natural pathway for scaling and sustaining social care integration into standard delivery reform. Demonstrated cost savings can help payer–provider systems justify embedding social care reimbursement into contracts. Findings also highlight opportunities for federal and state policy. Medicaid managed care organizations are well-positioned to adopt closed-loop reimbursement structures, given their flexibility and the high prevalence of social needs among Medicaid populations, and Medicare Advantage plans could adapt similar models as supplemental. Policy-makers could encourage adoption by recognizing social care interventions as reimbursable services, effectively moving social care to standard practice.

The observed savings across a mix of commercial, Medicaid, and Medicare Advantage health plan members highlight the broad value proposition of the SCN model, suggesting that its economic benefits are not confined to a single insurance type. This broad applicability underscores the potential for widespread financial benefits and shared investments, leading to more comprehensive and sustainable funding models for community-based social care infrastructure. Future research investigating payer-specific effects can inform even more targeted and efficient multipayer investment strategies as these programs evolve.

The program also demonstrates the importance of interoperable infrastructure in social care. Much like clinical data exchange across providers through electronic health records, referral platforms can enable cross-sector data exchange for social care, reducing fragmentation, preventing duplication, and enabling coordination among health, government, and nonprofit systems. Notably, high-need communities often face the greatest barriers to health and social care, underscoring the importance of designing networks that prioritize equity. Going forward, analyses could also inform how health systems curate CBO networks—identifying the most effective matches between organizations, interventions, and member needs—ensuring that limited social care resources are deployed where they can have the greatest impact.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. Although matching and regression adjustment balanced measurable characteristics, unmeasured confounding may remain. For example, participants who could not be reached by CBOs following referral may experience additional barriers that also influence health care outcomes, including practical challenges such as interrupted phone service or inflexible work hours or psychosocial factors such as perceived stigma surrounding using CBO services or distrust of CBOs or health care. Additionally, certain measures were unavailable, including proximity to services and behavioral health costs, which may have biased the effect. The comparison between participating and nonparticipating CBOs may be biased by selection effects, and the cohort's single-region insured population may limit generalizability. To maintain sample size and accurately reflect the population of interest, we did not require a long follow-up period. Although the mean follow-up was approximately 8 months, and the core group by period by time model interaction accounted for variable follow-up length, the results could be biased by individuals with shorter follow-up. Finally, the intersectionality of social need makes analyzing individual domains difficult, particularly with a smaller sample. Though our sample size prohibited perfect balance on referral domain or treatment effect heterogeneity analysis, we included referral volume, referral need intensity, and geographic indicators to address the matrixed barriers of access, stigma, and dynamic social need to construct a more holistic comparison.

Conclusion

The SCN provides a promising blueprint for integrating social and medical care. Findings demonstrate improvements in loop-closure and service delivery and lower total medical costs following referral among those who received services compared to those who did not. These results suggest that a scalable, sustainable SCN can be implemented in a health system and support making social care reimbursement a standard element of value-based contracts, ensuring that community infrastructure is recognized as part of the health care delivery system.
