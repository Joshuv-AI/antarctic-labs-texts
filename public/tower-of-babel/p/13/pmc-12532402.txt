Association between lipid profiles and early clinical outcomes in acute ischemic stroke: a single-center cohort study in the Chinese population
Authors: Duanlu Hou, Yuanyuan Wang, Shuang Zhai, Xiaoli Yang, Yuping Tang, Danhong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical significance and contribution of the lipid profile in atherosclerosis are well established. However, further investigation is needed in stroke patients, particularly regarding apolipoprotein B100 (ApoB100), a novel non-traditional lipid component in the lipid profile.ObjectivesTo explore lipid parameters and their impact on stroke outcomes in patients with and without thrombolysis.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) at a single center, including those who did and did not receive thrombolysis. Participants were stratified into improvement (favorable outcome at 2 weeks) and non-improvement groups. Demographic, laboratory, imaging, and clinical scale data were compared between groups. Random forest analyses were used to evaluate the predictive value and importance of individual lipid measures: triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), ApoB100, and lipoprotein(a), which better describe the internal characteristics of the profile.ResultsComplete data were available for 262 AIS patients, 165 of whom received thrombolysis. Plasma ApoB100 levels were significantly lower in the thrombolysis group (p < 0.001) and decreased ApoB100 levels were independently associated with 2-week stroke improvement (p = 0.009, OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.93). Random-forest feature-importance plots revealed that HDL and ApoB100 (each contributing > 15%) were the strongest lipid predictors of a favorable outcome, outperforming the other lipid variables.ConclusionsWe found that thrombolysis is associated with ApoB100 decrease and a decrease in ApoB100 can predict the 2-week functional improvement in stroke. HDL and ApoB100 emerge as more important determinants of favorable AIS outcomes in this machine-learning analysis. These findings warrant external validation in multi-center trials.Trial registrationChiCTR1800018315, 11/09/2018.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04444-6.

Introduction

Dyslipidemia is a prevalent risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), i.e., ischemic stroke [1, 2]. The Global Burden of Disease data showed that the annual number of strokes and deaths due to stroke increased substantially from 1990 to 2021 [3], which showed the necessity of stroke-related studies. A lipid profile, including low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides (TG), lipoprotein (a) [Lp(a)], total cholesterol (TC), etc., is widely utilized in both clinical research and practice [4, 5]. These lipid parameters are closely associated with the risk, diagnosis, and prognosis of ASCVD [6–8], as well as with cognitive decline in the elderly population [9]. When compared to a single biomarker within the lipid profile, a combined model demonstrates good efficacy in disease monitoring and prediction [10].

Clinical and genetic evidence suggested that low-density lipoprotein (LDL) can cause ASCVD [11]. The effect of LDL on ASCVD depends on its levels, magnitude and exposure duration [12]. A study in China revealed a J-shaped relationship between LDL-C and mortality, which can be affected by diabetes [13]. Guidelines have emphasized the significance of LDL levels in managing blood cholesterol [14]. Targeting LDL-C lowering by statins or proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin (PCSK) type 9 inhibitors [15] in patients with stroke or transient ischemic attack alone can decrease the overall incidence of stroke recurrence and ASCVD, although there may be a slight increase in the risk of hemorrhagic stroke [16]. Lowering LDL-C levels to 70 mg/dL has been found to reduce the risk of recurrent ischemic events by 50% [17]. There are controversial debates about the risk of hemorrhage or dementia after aggressive LDL-C lowering [18, 19].

Triglycerides (TG), though accounting for merely 7% of LDL content, are highly atherogenic. TG-rich lipoproteins and remnants are four times more atherogenic than LDL and are proven to cause coronary heart disease [20, 21]. The TG/glucose ratio is linked to stroke recurrence and mortality, indicating its potential in risk stratification [22, 23]. Remnant cholesterol (RC) levels are independently associated with ASCVD, even after adjusting for traditional risk factors, LDL-C, and ApoB, underscoring the importance of RC-lowering in primary prevention [24]. A recent study has found that oxidized phospholipid (oxPL) from apolipoprotein B (ApoB) containing lipoprotein predicts cardiovascular events more accurately than Lp(a) [25]. Lp(a), a glycoprotein attached to an LDL-like particle, is associated with coronary heart disease but not ischemic stroke [26], though it can independently promote atrial fibrillation [27]. Clinical and laboratory studies on the oxPL-ApoB have linked the components of lipids to thrombosis/thrombogenesis in ASCVD [28]. ApoB, a key component of LDL, exists in two forms: ApoB48 and ApoB100. Among these, ApoB100 is the primary structural and functional component of LDL and serves as a ligand for the LDL receptor [29, 30]. Due to these properties, it is considered to play a central role in the development of atherosclerosis [31, 32].

Mendelian randomization studies have shown associations between clinical lipid parameters (LDL, HDL, TC, TG, and Lp(a)) and stroke subtypes [33], small vessel disease [34], and even Alzheimer’s disease [28]. Lipid-lowering therapies targeting LDL and stabilizing plaques can reduce stroke incidence. Novel therapies targeting Lp(a) and TG show promise outcomes [35].

While targeted therapies have demonstrated significant value in disease prevention and treatment, many patients still require precision personalized therapy, especially when facing side effects of traditional treatments such as statin resistance and an increased risk of diabetes [36]. Traditional and non-traditional lipid parameters should be considered together to understand their roles in disease, as their contributions to the same population need to be addressed. Our goal is to investigate the significance of the clinical lipid profile in stroke characteristics, including disease progression and improvement.

Methods

Patient enrollment and study design

This is a prospective, clinical and single-centered study in Shanghai, China. We screened patients with acute ischemic stroke admitted to the Stroke Center of Shanghai Fifth People’s Hospital (The Fifth People’s Hospital of Shanghai, Fudan University) between Jan 1, 2018 and Sep 1, 2023, with the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale scores (NIHSS) [37]. The stroke patient selection followed the Declaration of Helsinki, diagnostic and thrombolytic inclusion and exclusion criteria (see Supplementary Materials). Figure 1 shows the selected patients’ flowchart, the study design, and the sample size was calculated to be 254 based on previous studies [38, 39], with a power of 80% and a significance level of 0.05 (see Supplemental Materials).

Fig. 1Flowchart of patients’ enrollment

Flowchart of patients’ enrollment

Written informed consent (file No. 2017LUNSHEN032BEI) was obtained from all patients and their families. The Ethical Review Board of the Shanghai Fifth People’s Hospital approved the study protocol before patient enrollment (approval No. 2017LUNSHEN032BEI and 20220907HDL).

Baseline data collection

Baseline data were gathered from the medical records, covering age, sex, NIHSS score at admission, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and medical history of hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, dyslipidemia, smoking, and alcohol use. Blood samples were obtained upon admission, stored in a refrigerator at −80℃, or analyzed by clinical laboratory technicians within two hours at 4℃. On the second day of admission, the concentrations of fasting plasma glucose, homocysteine, HDL, LDL, TC, TG, lipoprotein a , fasting insulin, hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C), alanine transaminase (ALT), fibrinogen (FIB), international normalized ratio (INR), homocysteine (HCY), and C-reactive protein (CRP) were measured. Remnant cholesterol (RC) was calculated by subtracting HDL-C and LDL-C from TC. ApoB100 levels were determined using an ELISA kit following its manufacture manual. Based upon the Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) criterias, stroke subtypes were defined as large artery atherosclerosis (LAA), cardioembolic (CE), small artery occlusion (SAO), stroke of undetermined etiology (SUE), and stroke of other determined etiology (SOE) [40].

Study outcomes

In our study, variables were meticulously defined and measured to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data. The primary independent variables were lipid profiles, including HDL, LDL, TG, TC, and Lp(a). These lipid parameters were measured using standardized methods. The dependent variable or outcome was the change in NIHSS scores from baseline to the 2-week follow-up, reflecting the symptomatic changes in patients with or without thrombolysis. The NIHSS is a widely used scale for quantifying neurological deficits in stroke patients, and its change over time is a meaningful indicator of patient outcome [37]. Other covariates included age, sex, and comorbid conditions, which were extracted from electronic medical records. All measurements were conducted by trained healthcare professionals following standardized protocols to minimize measurement bias and ensure consistency across assessments.

Clinical assessments

Certified attending physicians evaluated each patient’s NIHSS on the day of admission to the general ward and again on day 14. The NIHSS quantifies the severity of neurological impairment:

< 4 = mild deficit.

5–9 = moderate deficit.

≥ 10 = severe deficit.

Neurological outcome at 24 h post-thrombolysis was defined relative to the pre-thrombolysis NIHSS: an NIHSS of 0 or a reduction of ≥ 4 points indicated improvement [41], whereas an increase of ≥ 2 points signified deterioration [42]. In our stroke center, symptomatic improvement cases (the opposite of the early deterioration) were identified using the following criteria: a decrease of ≥ 4 points in the total NIHSS score at 2 weeks, or ≥ 2 point in the consciousness score (1a–1c), or ≥ 2 point in the motor score (5a–6b), or no new neurological deficit (even if not measurable via NIHSS scores) evaluated by a neurologist [43].

Imaging evaluation

All enrolled patients underwent 3-T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head, magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), or 64-layer CT (Siemens, Forchheim, Germany) and CT angiography (CTA) (Canon, Tokyo, Japan) within 48 h of stroke onset. Two clinical neurologists (D.H. and Y.W.) evaluated the MRI images to confirm the diagnosis of ischemic stroke, as well as to identify white matter hyperintensities and microbleeds. The severity of intracranial arterial stenosis or atherosclerosis on MRA was assessed by a radiologist from the Radiology department and a neurologist (D.H.), who calculated the ratio of the long diameter of the plaque to the artery diameter, with a threshold of < 50% stenosis considered significant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, R27.0.0.0), Python 3.13.1 (Python Software Foundation, https://www.python.org, 3.13.1) and GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software for macOS, Boston, Massachusetts, USA, https://www.graphpad.com, 10.3.1 (464)) (see Supplemental Material). Patients were divided into two groups based on the presence of thrombolysis (recombinant tissue plasminogen activator, or rtPA usage), and of improvement (NIHSS score changes). The Mann-Whitney U test or t-test was used to compare these groups. Logistic analysis was conducted to identify independent factors for early stroke progression. The random forest model was applied to assess the importance of each lipid profile in stroke improvement (see Supplemental Material for more detailed metrics). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

ApoB100 levels decreased after thrombolysis

Of 700 consecutive patients with acute ischemic stroke, 262 fulfilled the inclusion criteria and were analyzed (Table 1). They were stratified into a thrombolysis group (n = 165) and a non-thrombolysis group (n = 97). The mean onset-to-needle time was 2.52 ± 0.87 h. Inter-group comparisons showed significant differences in male sex (p = 0.018), atrial fibrillation (p = 0.017), cardioembolic stroke subtype (p = 0.017), baseline NIHSS score (p < 0.001), and serum ApoB100 levels (p < 0.001). As the collection and test of ApoB100 conducted after the thrombolysis, the ApoB100 levels were decreased significantly after thrombolysis.

Confounders of ApoB100 levels identified by comparisons

Patients were then categorized into two groups based on their ApoB100 levels: higher and lower, using the mean values as the cutoff (Table 2). Specifically, diabetes status (p = 0.02), thrombolysis status (p < 0.001), HDL levels (p = 0.02), and HbA1C levels (p = 0.02) were all significantly different between the higher and lower ApoB100 groups.

Comparisons for the good clinical outcome

Additionally, patients were divided into improvement and non-improvement groups following thrombolysis (Supplementary Table 1). Analysis of these groups showed significant differences in the proportion of females (p = 0.004), cases of atrial fibrillation (p = 0.045), and admission NIHSS scores (p < 0.001). However, among patients who did not undergo thrombolysis (Supplementary Table 2), only admission NIHSS scores were significantly different between the improvement and non-improvement groups.

Negative association between ApoB100 and the early improvement

Furthermore, the improvement rate was compared between patients with higher and lower ApoB100 levels. A significant increase in the improvement rate was observed in the lower ApoB100 group (Fig. 2; p < 0.001), with an improvement rate of 84% in the lower group compared to 69% in the higher group. This finding indicates a negative correlation between ApoB100 levels and improvement rate, which is consistent with the data presented in Tables 1 and 2.

In terms of neuroimaging changes, our analysis found no significant differences between patients who received thrombolysis and those who did not. This included the presence of white matter lesions, microbleeds, and stenosis in the middle, anterior, or posterior cerebral arteries. However, it is worth noting that middle cerebral artery stenosis was more frequently observed in patients who received thrombolysis (Table 1). Additionally, no significant differences in neuroimaging markers were detected between the improvement and non-improvement groups, regardless of whether patients had undergone thrombolysis (Supplementary Tables 1–2).

Logistic analysis of lipids, especially ApoB100, for the early symptom improvement

In a word, thrombolysis is independently associated with ApoB100 decrease, and the lower levels of ApoB100 independently predict the 2-week outcome in patients with stroke.

Importance of clinical factors in the discrimination between symptomatic improvement and non-improvement

To further assess the predictive value of clinical lipid parameters for symptomatic improvement, a random forest model was employed (Fig. 3). After incorporating baseline risk factors, admission NIHSS scores emerged as the most important feature in distinguishing improvement in thrombolysis patients, followed by ApoB100 levels (Fig. 3A), with a model accuracy of 70%. In non-thrombolysis patients, ApoB100 levels were not identified as an important feature, with the model accuracy being 75% (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

In our prospective study in Shanghai, we explored the links between lipid profiles and stroke outcomes. We found that thrombolysis independently reduces ApoB100 levels, with lower ApoB100 associated with better stroke improvement. Using random forest models, we assessed the importance of various lipids (HDL, LDL, TC, TG, Lp(a), ApoB100) in stroke improvement, highlighting their clinical relevance in a population-based context.

We showed that ApoB100 levels decreased post-rtPA administration and were independently linked to good outcomes in AIS patients, unlike other lipid parameters. Zhang W. et al. also found that ApoB100 levels were associated with a good outcome at 90 days and identified a cutoff value of 0.85 g/L in stroke patients receiving thrombolysis in a single center in China [44]. And LDL-C was not associated with a 3-month good outcome in stroke, but pre-stroke statin usage could modify the influence of LDL-C [45]. However, some studies also found that HDL [46], TG, Lp(a) [47], and TG/glucose ratio [48] could predict the post-thrombolysis poor outcome, hemorrhagic transformation [49], or mortality in stroke patients [50, 51]. In addition, regarding ratios, ApoB100/apoAI outperforms TG/HDL-C, TC/HDL-C, and LDL-C/HDL-C as a simple and powerful marker of coronary stenosis extent in Han Chinese undergoing coronary angiography [52]. These results differ from ours, possibly due to differences in study populations, lipid measurement methods, or follow-up durations.

Recent studies collectively highlight the complex relationship between LDL-C levels and outcomes in ischemic stroke patients. For instance, one study reveals a U-shaped relationship between LDL-C and early vascular outcomes [53]. Additionally, another study indicates that non-traditional lipid profiles, particularly the LDL/HDL ratio, can predict residual vascular risk even when LDL-C is well controlled [54]. Moreover, the differential impacts of LDL-C on outcomes were based on stroke subtypes [55]. A meta-analysis of 21 studies shows that traditional lipid markers like TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C aren’t good at predicting short-term outcomes in acute ischemic stroke. Only low TG levels link to higher death rates and early worsening [56]. In our study, the lack of difference in CRP levels between the two groups (p = 0.75) also suggests that low ApoB is not a reflection of systemic inflammation.

Different kind of lipids has different effects on fibrinolysis: HDL can increase tPA synthesis and secretion, LDL can increase tPA-plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI-1) interaction, and Lp(a) decreases tPA activity and plasmin activity [57–59]. ApoB100 affects the fibrinolytic system mainly through its role in forming Lp(a) [3]. Lp(a) resembles plasminogen and interferes with PAI-1, inhibiting plasminogen-to-plasmin conversion. This impairs fibrinolysis and raises thrombotic risk. The size of apo(a) polymorphs in Lp(a) affects its antifibrinolytic impact. Elevated ApoB100 and Lp(a) levels boost cardiovascular risk by disrupting the fibrinolytic system [3, 31].

In individuals with residual cardiovascular risk, such as those with metabolic syndrome or who do not achieve therapeutic goals despite lipid-lowering treatment, ApoB100 levels can provide additional insights beyond traditional markers [31]. The European Atherosclerosis Society (EAS), the American Heart Association (AHA), and the Japanese Atherosclerosis Society (JAS) support the use of ApoB measurement for cardiovascular risk assessment, especially in patients with metabolic syndrome [60–62]. The Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) also recommends using non-HDL-C or ApoB as alternatives to LDL-C when serum triglycerides exceed 130 mg/dL [63].

ApoB100 levels are measured using standardized, accurate, and cost-effective immunoturbidimetric or immunonephelometric assays on automated platforms. However, inconsistent clinical guidelines for interpreting and applying ApoB100 results have limited its routine clinical use [64]. Simpler methods for estimating ApoB levels, such as the formulas ApoB = 20.67944 + (0.551614 × non-HDL-C) and ApoB = (0.60 × non-HDL-C) + 12.0, have been proposed. These methods are particularly useful in low-resource settings due to their ease of calculation, but exhibit lower concordance [65].

Facing the problems that the residual risk of ASCVD exists when LDL-C remains low or under control, ApoB seems to be another good risk factor in these situations [64]. ApoB100’s feasibility for assessing ASCVD risk must consider LDL, HDL, etc. In non-statin users, elevated LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and apoB each signal heightened ASCVD risk. In statin users, elevated non-HDL-C and apoB despite low LDL-C best indicate residual risk. Thus, all three metrics are vital for ASCVD risk assessment and guiding lipid-lowering therapy [66]. Plus, ApoB100 binds to oxidized phospholipids, thereby promoting atherosclerosis and influencing thrombolysis [67]. Metabolomics revealed that intravenous thrombolysis significantly impacts ApoB100 levels, closely linked to early and late adverse outcomes in ischemic stroke patients. Elevated ApoB100 in LDL particles correlates with higher risks of intracranial hemorrhage, non-response to treatment, mortality, and poor outcomes. This highlights its potential as a biomarker for predicting prognosis in patients with stroke with thrombolysis [68].

Vaccines based on peptides from ApoB100 have demonstrated promising results, and novel therapies modulating immune responses are emerging in the treatment of atherosclerosis [69, 70]. It has been found that PCSK7 binding to ApoB100 can increase its secretion, whereas the loss of PCSK7 leads to ApoB100 degradation and reduces lipid accumulation in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [71]. And PCSK9 inhibitors can decrease their levels indirectly by regulating LDL-C levels [72]. However, limited research has focused on the association between ApoB100 and stroke outcomes. Wu et al. discovered that ApoB100 from extracellular vesicles in patients with spinal cord injury promotes the occurrence of coronary heart disease [73].

Random forest algorithm is a nonparametric approach accommodating categorical, quantitative outcomes, and survival times, and is also suitable for analysing complex data such as omics data [74, 75]. Random forest modeling identified ApoB100 and HDL levels as superior predictors of favorable outcomes in AIS patients, with distinct contributions based on thrombolysis status. Zhang et al. demonstrated robust associations between specific lipid fractions (TG, HDL, LDL) and neurological deficit severity—indexed by NIHSS scores—especially in stroke patients with concomitant chronic kidney disease [76]. Pikija et al. further showed that LDL-C independently predicted favorable 3-month functional outcomes (mRS ≤ 2) after endovascular thrombectomy for anterior-circulation occlusions [77]. Since Quehenberger et al. first mapped the human plasma lipidome by quantifying over 500 lipid species [78], lipidomics has advanced to systematically identify and validate lipid biomarkers with unparalleled precision. This progress is particularly evident in stroke research [79], and has also extended to other fields, such as Alzheimer’s disease [80]. These advancements highlight the potential of lipidomics to uncover novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Throughout ischemic stroke progression, lipids dynamically contribute to both injury and recovery, highlighting their complex roles in stroke pathophysiology [81, 82]. In the initial minutes to hours of an ischemic stroke, the brain rapidly releases free fatty acids [83] from cell membranes via phospholipase A2 (PLA2), breaking down membrane phospholipids and releasing arachidonic acid (AA) and other polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) [84]. These free fatty acids serve as substrates for lipid mediators. During the subsequent hours to days, eicosanoids derived from AA become active. Prostaglandins (PG) like PGE2 and PGI2 can either promote inflammation or exert neuroprotective effects, depending on context [85, 86]. Lipoxins (LX), such as LXA4, begin to resolve inflammation and promote tissue repair [84]. The balance between these mediators has a significant influence on brain damage and recovery. In the chronic phase (days to weeks), neuroprotective lipid mediators like DHA-derived neuroprotectin D1 enhance neuronal survival and promote neurogenesis [84]. The brain’s lipid composition normalizes as damaged cells are cleared and new connections form. Lipids also support myelin sheath regeneration, crucial for restoring neural communication.

The cardioprotective roles of HDL cholesterol and its particles in atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, and stroke are well established [87–89]. HDL-C alone is imperfect for cardiovascular risk. HDL subspecies with apoC3 link to higher ASCVD, diabetes, stroke, and dementia risk, while those with apoE show lower risk [90]. The critical influence of apolipoprotein composition on HDL function is underscored by findings showing that ratios like HbA1C/HDL and TG/HDL are promising clinical markers for stroke risk [91, 92]. ApoB100 is a more accurate predictor of CVD risk than traditional LDL-C, especially in individuals with residual risk, such as those with metabolic syndrome, diabetes, or obesity [31]. ApoB100, a key protein in atherogenic lipoproteins like VLDL, IDL, LDL, and Lp(a), drives lipid transport and LDL receptor binding. It fuels atherosclerosis via oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune activation [31].

This study has limitations. First, the single-center design and exclusion criteria may introduce selection bias, skewing findings toward milder strokes or certain subtypes. Factors like statin use, inflammatory markers, and genetic predispositions, which can influence lipid profiles and stroke outcomes, were not fully accounted for. Second, the study lacked long-term follow-up; outcomes such as NIHSS, Barthel Index, and cognitive function at 12 months or beyond were not assessed. Third, the lipid panel was limited; future work should include apoA, ox-LDL, and other lipidomic markers. Fourth, no interventions were performed on serum apolipoproteins for comparison. Fifth, blood was drawn immediately after rt-PA administration, which could have been affected by hemodilution. Lastly, although we recorded statin use prior to admission, we did not differentiate between doses or treatment durations, which may have underestimated the residual effects of LDL-C.
