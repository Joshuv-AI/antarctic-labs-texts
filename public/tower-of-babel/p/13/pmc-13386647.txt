Trends, determinants and inequality of utilization of maternal healthcare services in Bangladesh: an analysis of demographic and health surveys from 2004 to 2022
Authors: Abdul Baten, Raaj Kishore Biswas, Evie Kendal, Jahar Bhowmik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBangladesh, like many low- and middle-income countries, is lagging behind the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target related to reducing maternal mortality by 2030. Maternal healthcare services (MHS) utilization remains low and inequitable, contributing to poor maternal outcomes. This study analyzed trends, determinants, and inequalities in MHS utilization in Bangladesh from 2004 to 2022.MethodsWe analyzed six rounds of the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (2004–2022) with sample sizes of 5,206, 4,773, 6,176, 4,390, 4,928, and 4,921, focusing on women aged 15–49 with the most recent birth who had given birth within five years preceding the surveys. We considered four important maternal health service indicators, including attending at least four antenatal care (ANC) visits with medically trained providers, skilled birth attendance (SBA), institutional delivery (ID), and postnatal care (PNC) for the mother by medically trained providers within two days of childbirth. Survey-adjusted logistic regression and dominance analysis identified key MHS utilization predictors, while concentration curves and absolute and relative measures assessed inequality across equity strata (categories/stratifiers used to compare differences in MHS utilization across population subgroups, such as urban vs. rural, poorest to richest, no education to higher education, etc.).ResultsBetween 2004 and 2022, access to ANC, SBA, and ID increased by 21.6%, 53.1%, and 52.2%, respectively, whereas for PNC, the increase was 32.4% between 2007 and 2022. Key predictors of MHS utilization included residence, education, wealth, media exposure, parity, and pregnancy termination. For instance, in 2022, rural women were 29% less likely to use ANC (AOR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.59–0.86) compared to 54% in 2004 (AOR = 0.46; 95% CI: 0.35–0.60). Wealthier women were significantly more likely to utilize SBA, with the richest quintile being 4.3 times more likely in 2004 and 3.8 times more likely in 2022. Wealth, and the respondent’s and their husband’s education levels were the strongest predictors of MHS utilization. While inequalities across difference places of residence, wealth, and education levels have declined, disparities persist. Wealth-related inequality in ANC, SBA, and ID was lowest in 2022 but highest in 2004, with substantial reductions in relative and absolute disparities over time.ConclusionsDespite increased maternal healthcare utilization in Bangladesh from 2004 to 2022, disparities persist across wealth, respondent and husbands’ education, and place of residence. Targeted interventions are needed to reduce inequality and align with SDG targets by 2030.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09314-8.Keywords: Maternal healthcare services, Bangladesh, Health service utilization, Sociodemographic factors, Inequality

Background

Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG 3), target 3.1, is to reduce the global maternal mortality rate (MMR) to below 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030 [1]. Achieving this goal requires strong government commitment, especially in countries with suboptimal maternal health outcomes, to effectively reduce preventable maternal mortality [2, 3]. Although maternal mortality has significantly decreased in many developing countries, there are still 800 maternal deaths occurring daily worldwide. Notably, 95% of these maternal deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and South Asia (SA) [4]. The MMR of a nation serves as a vital indicator reflecting the overall health status of its mothers [5]. Similar to other LMICs, in Bangladesh, a high MMR symbolizes the culmination of a lifetime experience in which women face societal neglect and deprivation [5].

Despite being one of the most populous LMICs and having an 18.7% poverty rate with high health inequality [6–8], Bangladesh has made significant public health advancements over the past two decades [9, 10]. The country has established an extensive health infrastructure to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [11]. Notwithstanding this progress, the MMR in Bangladesh was recorded at 123 per 100,000 live births in 2020 [4]. Reports indicate that in Bangladesh, this MMR represents a decrease from 441 per 100,000 live births in 2000 [4]. However, Bangladesh lags behind the global SDG target of reducing the MMR to less than 70 per 100,000 by 2030 [1].

Effective utilization of maternal healthcare services (MHS), including antenatal care (ANC) visits by medically trained provider during pregnancy, skilled birth attendance (SBA) at the childbirth, institutional delivery (ID) with health facilities, and postnatal care (PNC) after the childbirth, are steps to reduce both maternal mortality and morbidity rates and improve pregnancy outcomes [10, 12]. Most maternal deaths occur during pregnancy and childbirth, caused by obstetric conditions, including obstructed labour, haemorrhage, unsafe abortion, and hypertensive disorders [13, 14]. Facilities referring to ANC, SBA, ID and PNC services can reduce the risk of pregnancy and childbirth-related complications for pregnant women [13]. Since the antenatal period is crucial for both mother and newborn antenatal care provided by medically trained professionals—such as doctors, nurses, paramedics, midwives, family welfare visitors, community skilled birth assistants, and sub-assistant community medical officers—plays a vital role in detecting early signs of pregnancy-related risks or complications, enabling timely interventions [15, 16]. Skilled care during childbirth and in the immediate postnatal period can save many mothers’ lives. Postnatal care is essential for detecting and managing delivery complications, as well as guiding mothers on caring for themselves and their newborns [10]. Therefore, having skilled health personnel within an institutional facility is essential from pregnancy to childbirth and after childbirth to ensure early detection and timely management of complications [17]. So, this study focuses on these four key MHS indicators, including ANC, SBA, ID and PNC.

Despite the MHS being widely available in healthcare systems in most developed countries, these essential services remain limited for women in many developing countries, particularly in SSA and SA [18]. Like many other LMICs, Bangladesh has a low utilization rate of MHS [10, 19]. For instance, according to the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) 2022 [17], the prevalence of at least four ANC visits from a medically trained provider is below 40%, PNC within two days after delivery has just crossed 50%, and facility delivery care is below 70%. So, Bangladesh lags behind the universal coverage set by SDG 3 [20].

Moreover, inequality exists in the utilization of MHS across different subgroups [21, 22]. Ensuring equitable access to quality MHS can play a crucial role in enhancing maternal and reproductive health outcomes [23]. Disparities in the utilization of MHS hinder the overall progress of reducing maternal mortality [24, 25]. Women from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds often face limited access to MHS and receive lower-quality services, contributing to deaths related to childbirth [26–28]. So, inequality in the utilization of MHS in Bangladesh remains a concern and hampers overall progress.

Additionally, monitoring trends and inequities in maternal healthcare use is essential for tracking progress in reducing health disparities and identifying high-performing indicators as well as those requiring further attention [29]. Identifying the factors driving these inequalities helps guide effective interventions and inform policy. Regular assessment of such trends is also necessary to assess progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), especially SDG 3. To achieve universal health coverage, the WHO recommends at least 80% coverage across all health indicators, including maternal healthcare services, by 2030. Achieving SDG 3 is not possible without ensuring equitable access to reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child health services for all [30].

Several studies have focused on one MHS in a single survey year for Bangladesh, and their inequality and determinants [31–35]. However, there is a lack of studies examining temporal trends in the utilization of MHS among ever-married women in Bangladesh over extended periods. A comprehensive analysis spanning across multiple survey waves can explain the maternal healthcare services at different stages of childbirth journey from antepartum to postpartum with four key indicators of MHS over time. Further assessment of inequality will facilitate the identification of socioeconomic disparities in MHS utilization across socioeconomic groups. This will provide stronger evidence for targeted, policy-relevant interventions to improve overall maternal health outcomes. Therefore, we examined trends, determinants, and disparities in the utilization of ANC, SBA, ID, and PNC services using Bangladesh Demographic and Health Surveys (BDHSs) data from 2004 to 2022 to gain insights into the overall state of MHS, which could inform policy development aimed at achieving SDG 3 by 2030.

Methods

Conceptual framework for maternal healthcare utilization based on Andersen’s behavioural model (adapted)

The study adapted Andersen’s Behavioural (AB) model [36], a widely applied framework for analyzing factors associated with MHS utilization (Fig. 1). The AB model offers a comprehensive analytical framework to understand the determinants of MHS use and guide policy improvements [37]. It comprises external environmental factors alongside three key dimensions of population characteristics, including predisposing, enabling, and need factors [36]. Predisposing factors include socio-demographic characteristics such as age, education, and employment that influence healthcare service use; enabling factors cover resources and conditions like wealth index, decision-making autonomy, and media exposure that facilitate or hinder service use; and need factors reflect the direct reasons for healthcare, including pregnancy intention, history of pregnancy terminations, and parity and external environmental factors include place of residence and region, also shape access to care [38, 39]. Guided by this conceptual model, the study hypothesized that external, predisposing, enabling, and need factors significantly influence the outcome variables.

Data source and study design

This study used data from six rounds of the standard Bangladesh Demographic and Health Surveys (BDHSs) conducted in 2004, 2007, 2011, 2014, 2017-18 and 2022. The BDHS utilized standard Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) questionnaires and employed established data collection techniques [40]. The BDHS is an integral part of the global DHS program, serving as a nationally representative cross-sectional survey. The survey was designed to represent the whole country for the rural and urban areas separately and for each administrative division. Detailed information about sampling methodologies and data collection procedures can be found in the BDHS reports [17]. The survey primarily targets ever-married women aged 15–49 years with children within 5 years preceding the survey. The study included data from the responses of women in the available standard BDHSs concerning maternal healthcare services along with associated selected socio-demographic factors, focusing on their most recent birth who had given birth within 5 years preceding the survey. The study considered cases with complete information for the selected outcome and covariates. The study did not include missing/don’t know data and surveys before 2004 due to the unavailability of some considered outcomes. The details of the study sample selection procedure are provided with a flow diagram in Fig. 1 of Supplementary File 1. The datasets for the study were obtained from DHS authorities after registration and obtaining access permission.

Study variables

Outcome variables

This study considered four outcome variables: ANC visits, SBA, ID, and PNC for mothers. These variables were coded as binary following the guidelines provided by the DHS manual [41]. These variables were coded as 1 if women attended at least four ANC visits with medically trained providers during pregnancy, SBA during childbirth, ID at the childbirth, or PNC for mothers (within two days of childbirth) from medically trained providers, respectively, and 0 otherwise. A detailed description and coding of these outcome variables are provided in Table 1 of Supplementary File 1.

Explanatory variables/ equity strata

For this study, we examined the association of the predictors with MHS (ANC, SBA, ID and PNC) by adapting the AB model, in line with prior studies that applied the AB model to MHS utilization [37, 38, 42, 43]. The selection and coding of explanatory variables were considered by prior research [37, 42–45], as well as availability in BDHSs.

These variables include respondent’s age (≤ 25, > 25), respondent’s age at first birth (< 18, 18–24, ≥ 25), husband’s age (15–34, 35–49, ≥ 50), sex of household head (male, female), level of education (No education, primary, secondary, higher), husband’s education level (no education, primary, secondary, and higher), respondent’s working status (yes, no), wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest), respondent’s decision-making power (recoded as a binary variable; yes, if able to make decisions about own health care, a large household purchase, and visits to family or relatives by herself and/or with a partner, otherwise, no), exposure to media (categorized as ‘Yes’ if the respondent engages in any of newspaper/magazine/television/radio at least once a week; otherwise, no), place of residence (urban, rural), division/region (Barisal, Chattogram, Dhaka, Khulna, Rajshahi, Sylhet), ever had a pregnancy termination (categorized as ‘yes’ if the respondent’s ever had a pregnancy termination, otherwise, no), wanted pregnancy (categorized as ‘yes’ if the respondent’s wanted pregnancy for last child, otherwise, no) and parity (1, 2, ≥ 3). The wealth index, derived from household possessions, was compiled using principal component analysis and was predefined in each DHS [46]. These explanatory variables also considered as common equity strata (categories/stratifiers used to compare differences in MHS utilization across population subgroups like urban vs. rural, poorest to richest, no education to higher education, etc.) to examine inequality in the utilization of MHS [45]. A detailed description and categorization of the variables are provided in Table 1 of Supplementary File 1.

Statistical analysis

First, bivariate analysis (unweighted) using t-tests for continuous and Chi-square tests for categorical variables was conducted for the predictors and outcome variables to assess a brief understanding of the association between them without controlling for any other effects, such as survey weights, clusters, or strata. Second, MHS utilization trends (2004–2022) were analyzed with 95% confidence interval (CI). Third, survey-adjusted logistic regression (quasi-binomial) was used to assess equity factors. Models were adjusted with survey weights, clusters, and strata [47]. Quasi-binomial logistic regression was used to address potential overdispersion, as the unequal proportions of binary outcomes suggested variance greater than that assumed under the standard binomial model [48, 49]. Fourth, dominance analysis (DA) was conducted to rank the explanatory variables based on their relative importance. Finally, rate difference (RD) and rate ratio (RR) were employed to measure MHS utilization inequality for binary equity strata (e.g., residence, distance to a health facility, and employment status). For ordered strata with multiple categories (e.g., women’s education, wealth quintile, husband’s education, and parity), the concentration curve (CC), relative concentration index (RCI), and relative index of inequality (RII) were used. Details of DA, RD, RR, RCI and RII with mathematical formulas are reported in Supplementary File 2. All analyses were carried out in R (version 4.2.3), and the models were fitted using the ‘survey’ package (version 4.2.1).

Results

The trend of maternal healthcare services (ANC, SBA, ID and PNC) in Bangladesh

A total of six rounds of BDHSs between 2004-2022, and four indicators of MHS were included in the analysis. The analyzed sample size for 2022, 2017-18, 2014, 2011, 2007 and 2004 BDHSs was 4921, 4928, 4390, 6176, 4773 and 5206, respectively, with complete information for selected outcomes and predictors (Table 2 of Supplementary File 1, Fig. 1 of Supplementary File 1). Table 2 of Supplementary File 1 and Fig. 2 present the prevalence of MHS with 95% CI for 2004 to 2022 (PNC from 2007). MHS prevalence generally increased over time with some exceptions. SBA rose steadily from 16.6% (95% CI; 15.6–17.7) in 2004 to 69.7% (95% CI; 68.4, 71.0) in 2022, while ID increased from 12.3% to 64.5%. Overall, PNC service use increased from 2007 to 2022, though the growth was minimal between 2017 and 18 (52.9%) and 2022 (54.1%). ANC (≥ 4 visits by medically trained providers) peaked at 46.2% in 2017–18 before declining to 39.5% in 2022 (Fig. 2).

Trend of maternal healthcare services prevalence with 95% confidence interval

Bivariate association (unweighted) between maternal healthcare services and sociodemographic characteristics of women from 2004 to 2022

Table 3 of Supplementary File 1 shows unweighted bivariate associations between MHS utilization (ANC, SBA, ID, PNC) and socio-demographic factors (2004–2022) (PNC from 2007). MHS was significantly associated (p < 0.001) with residence, division/region, education, wealth, and age at first birth across all indicators. Media exposure, parity, and pregnancy intention also correlated (p < 0.001) with MHS. Other factors, such as respondent age, household head’s sex, decision-making power, husband’s age, and pregnancy termination history, were linked to specific indicators and years (Table 3 of Supplementary File 1).

MHS utilization varied across socio-demographic groups from 2004 to 2022, with no consistent pattern by indicator. For instance, ANC use among rural women was 51.6% in 2014, 57.0% in 2017–18, and 55.8% in 2022, while SBA use was 56.3%, 57.1%, and 62.1%, respectively. ID utilization declined from 28.7% in 2004 to 26.1% in 2022 among the highly educated women, whereas PNC use rose from 13.1% in 2007 to 25.0% in 2022. Detailed results are in Table 3 of Supplementary File 1.

Factors associated with maternal healthcare services

Figure 3 shows adjusted logistic regression results (2004–2022) (PNC from 2007), identifying key predictors of MHS utilization, including residence, education, wealth, media exposure, parity, and pregnancy termination (for details, see also Table 4 of Supplementary File 1).

Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% confidence interval for important significant predictors of maternal healthcare services (ANC, SBA, ID and PNC) in Bangladesh from 2004–2022 (PNC from 2007)

Women who resided in rural areas were less likely to utilize MHS over time, with some exceptions. For instance, in 2022, women who resided in rural areas were 29.0% (AOR = 0.71; 95% CI = 0.59, 0.86) less likely to utilize ANC compared to those in the urban areas, whereas it was 54.0% (AOR = 0.46; 95% CI = 0.35, 0.60) in 2004. Similarly, in 2022, women who resided in rural areas were 23.0% (AOR = 0.77; 95% CI = 0.62, 0.69) less likely to utilize SBA compared to the women who resided in the urban area, whereas it was 59.0% (AOR = 0.41; 95% CI = 0.33, 0.52) in 2004. Similar trends were found for ID (Fig. 3; Table 4 of Supplementary File 1). For PNC, rural women were 18% (AOR = 0.82; 95% CI = 0.67, 0.99) less likely to use the service in 2022 but 4.0% (AOR = 0.96; 95% CI = 0.75, 1.22) less likely in 2007. Though in 2007, the place of residence had no significant impact.

Higher education among women and their husbands has consistently been significantly associated with increased MHS utilization over time. For example, women with higher education were 4.8 (AOR = 4.79; 95% CI = 2.94, 7.79), 3.5 (AOR = 3.45, 95% CI = 2.12, 5.61), 3.9 (AOR = 3.93; 95% CI = 2.56, 6.02), 2.8 (AOR = 2.79; 95% CI = 1.53, 5.10), 3.1 (AOR = 3.12; 95% CI = 2.10, 4.64), and 2.5 (AOR = 2.54; 95% CI = 1.55, 4.18) times, respectively, more likely to utilize ANC compared to the women with no education from 2004 to 2022. Similarly, women with higher education were 3.6 (AOR = 3.60; 95% CI = 1.88, 6.89), 4.5 (AOR = 4.46, 95% CI = 2.61, 7.60), 3.7 (AOR = 3.65; 95% CI = 2.35, 5.66), 2.9 (AOR = 2.93; 95% CI = 1.85, 4.63), 2.7 (AOR = 2.66; 95% CI = 1.79, 3.95) and 2.0 (AOR = 2.02; 95% CI = 1.37, 3.19) times respectively more likely to ID compared to those with no education from 2004 to 2022. Similar trends were observed for SBA and PNC. In 2022, women with highly educated husbands were 1.6 (AOR = 1.63; 95% CI = 1.21, 2.19) times more likely to use ANC compared to those whose husbands had no education, down from 1.9 times in 2004 (AOR = 1.92; 95% CI = 1.29, 2.86). For SBA, the likelihood was 2.0 (AOR = 2.03; 95% CI = 1.46, 2.83) times in 2022 and 2.2 (AOR = 1.92; 95% CI = 1.39, 3.33) times in 2004. Similar patterns were found for ID and PNC (Fig. 3; Table 4 of Supplementary File 1). Throughout the study period, women from the middle to the richest wealth index groups were consistently more likely to utilize MHS than those from the poorest group. For instance, women from the richest wealth index group were 2.6 (AOR = 2.59; 95% CI = 1.93, 3.49) times more likely to utilize ANC compared to those from the poorest wealth index group in 2022, whereas it was 4.6 (AOR = 4.63; 95% CI = 2.98, 7.17) times in 2004. It was also found that women from the richest wealth index group were 3.7 (AOR = 3.70; 95% CI = 2.66, 5.14) times more likely to ID compared to those from the poorest wealth index group in 2022, and it was 4.8 (AOR = 4.76; 95% CI = 2.68, 8.44) times in 2004. Similar results were observed for SBA and PNC (Fig. 3, and Table 4 of Supplementary File 1).

ANC was significantly associated with media exposure from 2004 to 2022, except in 2007. Women with media access were 50% (AOR = 1.50; 95% CI = 1.29, 1.75) more likely to use ANC in 2022 and 48% (AOR = 1.48; 95% CI = 1.10, 1.98) in 2004. SBA showed a similar trend, except in 2007 and 2014, with media-exposed women 39% (AOR = 1.39; 95% CI = 1.17, 1.67) more likely to use SBA in 2022 and 46% (AOR = 1.46; 95% CI = 1.11, 1.92) in 2004. Comparable results were found for ID and PNC (Fig. 3; Table 4 of Supplementary File 1).

Higher parity reduced ANC utilization from 2004 to 2022. In 2022, women with two or more children were 33% (AOR = 0.67; 95% CI = 0.51, 0.89) less likely to use ANC and 38% (AOR = 0.62; 95% CI = 0.42, 0.89) less likely in 2004. ID use was 55% (AOR = 0.45; 95% CI = 0.33, 0.60) lower in 2022 and 71% (AOR = 0.29; 95% CI = 0.18, 0.46) in 2004. For PNC, lower utilization among women with two or more children was observed from 2011 to 2022, with a 40% (AOR = 0.60; 95% CI = 0.46, 0.79) reduction in 2022 and 60% (AOR = 0.40; 95% CI = 0.30, 0.54) in 2011, though no significant association was found in 2007.

Women with a history of pregnancy termination were 52% (AOR = 1.52; 95% CI = 1.26, 1.84) more likely to utilize ANC in 2022, compared to 42% (AOR = 1.42; 95% CI = 1.11, 1.81) in 2004. Pregnancy termination was significantly associated with SBA and ID from 2004 to 2022, except in 2007 and 2014. In 2022, women with a termination history were 65% (AOR = 1.65; 95% CI = 1.36, 2.01) more likely to use SBA and 57% (AOR = 1.57; 95% CI = 1.29, 1.90) more likely to ID compared to those without such pregnancy termination history. PNC was also linked to pregnancy termination in 2022 and 2017-18, with women 36% (AOR = 1.36; 95% CI = 1.13, 1.62) and 37% (AOR = 1.37; 95% CI = 1.14, 1.64) more likely to utilize it, respectively. Other factors, such as the respondent’s and husband’s age, region, decision-making power, and the household head’s sex, were also associated with MHS use in specific years. Detailed results are presented in Table 4 of Supplementary File 1.

The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (AUC) for all models (ANC, SBA, ID, PNC) from 2004 to 2022 (PNC from 2007) indicate a good fit (Fig. 2 of Supplementary File 1). Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) measure confirms no multicollinearity issues (Table 5 of Supplementary File 1), except for the wealth index for MHS (ANC, SBA, ID and PNC) in 2014 (VIF: 5.0–11.0) and wanted pregnancy for PNC (VIF: ~6.0).

Inequality measures for the maternal healthcare utilization indicators by common equity strata for 2004–2022

Table 2 presents RD and RR inequality measures for MHS utilization in Bangladesh (2004–2022) (PNC from 2007) across key equity strata with two categories. Positive RR and RD values indicate higher utilization among women in urban areas, with media exposure, decision-making power, pregnancy termination history, and a wanted last child. Urban-rural inequality decreased over time. In 2022, RR values for ANC, SBA, and ID by residence were 0.199, 0.154, and 0.160, with RD values of 1.606, 1.238, and 1.270, compared to 2004 values of 0.249, 0.235, and 0.191 (RD: 3.464, 3.539, 4.001). Media-related inequality varied. In 2022, RR values for ANC, SBA, ID, and PNC were 0.187, 0.182, 0.208, and 0.137, with RD values of 1.613, 1.298, 1.380, and 1.289, compared to 2007 values of 0.220, 0.239, 0.188, and 0.084 (RD: 3.267, 3.527, 3.654, 1.495). Similar trends were observed for decision-making, pregnancy termination, and the most recent birth being a wanted child. Detailed results are in Table 2.

Discussion

This study investigated the trends, determinants and inequality of utilization of MHS in Bangladesh from 2004 to 2022 (PNC from 2007). The findings indicate a continuous increase in MHS utilization during 2004–2022 across all outcomes, with variations among the indicators. The wealth index was the most influential factor of MHS utilization in Bangladesh, followed by husband’s education and respondents’ education as the second and third important factors, respectively. Despite a consistent reduction in place of residence, wealth, and education-related inequalities in MHS utilization over the past 20 years, disparities persist across different sociodemographic subgroups.

The utilization of MHS in Bangladesh has steadily increased from 2004 to 2022. A major driver of increased MHS utilization in Bangladesh has been the growing role of for-profit private healthcare facilities, influenced by factors such as demand, convenience, and revenue generation [29]. Over the past two decades, the private sector has become increasingly prominent in Bangladesh’s healthcare landscape, including maternal health services [29, 50, 51]. Private healthcare services are often preferred due to perceived higher quality, shorter waiting times, and greater confidentiality compared to public facilities [29]. For instance, in 2022 who deliveries in health facilities, 45.0% of deliveries took place in the private medical sector [17]. Additionally, Bangladesh’s pluralistic health system, community-based interventions, establishment of community clinics, and implementation of sector-wide approaches have contributed to improved reproductive, maternal, and child health coverage [52, 53]. Moreover, from 2007, with support from donor agencies, the Government of Bangladesh has implemented a demand-side financing scheme providing free ANC, delivery services, postnatal checkups, treatment for pregnancy complications, and financial support for laboratory tests and transportation to healthcare facilities [54]. Despite improvements in female literacy, socioeconomic conditions, and public health infrastructure, barriers such as lack of awareness, service unavailability, and high out-of-pocket healthcare costs limit access to maternal health services, particularly among disadvantaged populations [20, 55]. The coverage of MHS is impressive at the national level, but current projections indicate that the country may still fall short of the 80% universal coverage for all people by 2030 [20].

Moreover, variations in utilization among indicators of MHS were observed throughout the study period. Overall, access to delivery care (SBA and ID) were higher than other MHS indicators, and PNC utilization was higher than ANC. This trend may be attributed to the critical nature of childbirth, which often compels families to prioritize facility-based delivery over other maternal healthcare services [56]. Institutional delivery with SBA and immediate healthcare after childbirth is vital for reducing maternal and newborn health risks and plays a key role in achieving SDG targets related to health and inequality reduction [57]. In patriarchal societies, there is often a stronger emphasis on newborn care, prompting governments to allocate greater resources toward ensuring SBA due to its immediate life-saving impact [58, 59]. Additionally, the multiple visits required for ANC can pose challenges for women in remote areas, whereas a single-event delivery at a health facility may be more feasible, even if it necessitates long-distance travel [60]. As a result, life-saving SBA and PNC services in healthcare facilities are often prioritized over other maternal healthcare interventions. But overall, MHS coverage with all important indicators needs to be improved to achieve SDG 3 by 2030.

Bangladesh has made significant progress in reducing maternal mortality in recent years, but social inequalities in maternal healthcare utilization remain a challenge in Bangladesh [26]. MHS utilization remains influenced by sociodemographic factors and is unevenly distributed across social subgroups. This study observed significant progress in maternal health, with increased MHS use and reduced disparities in the population subgroups over time. However, inequalities persist in the population subgroups due to the influence of socio-economic factors such as household wealth, women and their husbands’ education, particularly between the poorest and the richest wealth groups, between no education to higher education levels. Other key factors linked to MHS utilization include place of residence, media access, parity, and pregnancy termination. These findings align with studies from various LMICs, including Ethiopia, Zambia, Mali, Nigeria, Myanmar, India, Pakistan, and Nepal [61–68].

Wealth index is the highest dominating factor for MHS utilization and is liable for differences in utilization in wealth subgroups since women in higher-income households are better able to cover direct, indirect, and opportunity costs associated with MHS utilization [69]. Additionally, greater socioeconomic status often leads to increased empowerment and awareness, influencing individual behavior through social and community networks [70]. Economic status significantly impacts healthcare access, with wealthier women affording maternal health services while poorer groups struggle with out-of-pocket costs [71, 72]. Financial constraints limit essential care access and widen the rich-poor gap in MHS utilization [45].

Two other key predictors of MHS utilization, and contributors to inequality are the education levels of respondents and their husbands. Education plays a crucial role in improving literacy, economic opportunities, and access to healthcare, allowing women to seek and receive comprehensive maternal care [73]. Moreover, women with higher education levels tend to have a better understanding of modern medical treatments and health services, enabling them to navigate the healthcare system effectively [69]. Furthermore, having an educated partner enhances MHS utilization, as educated husbands are more likely to recognize the importance of maternal healthcare and actively support their spouses in accessing services before, during, and after childbirth [74]. Highly educated women and their husbands are generally more aware of modern medical treatments, skilled birth attendants, and potential pregnancy complications, leading to a greater inclination toward utilizing MHS [75]. Consequently, differences in healthcare perceptions between highly educated and non-educated individuals contribute to inequalities in maternal healthcare utilization [45].

Other factors, such as place of residence, and media access also significantly influenced MHS utilization and contributed to disparities. In urban areas, higher education, decision-making power, media access, better healthcare systems, private facilities, transportation, and self-worth likely boost MHS use [73, 74]. On the other hand, long distances to health center and poor road conditions often hinder rural women from accessing maternal healthcare services, exacerbating inequality [76]. Media exposure is key to improving health education and boosting maternal health service use. Governments, non-government organizations (NGOs), and healthcare providers use mass media to highlight the importance of MHS and raise awareness of the risks of neglecting maternal care [73].

Parity and pregnancy termination history were also found to be significant, and parity-based inequality in MHS utilization exists in Bangladesh. Women are more likely to seek maternal healthcare for their first pregnancy, as they perceive it to be riskier than subsequent pregnancies [34, 77]. Women with multiple prior births may rely on past childbirth experiences, experienced time constraints related to childcare, and financial limitations, leading to greater self-confidence and a reduced perceived need to seek professional maternal healthcare services [73, 78]. Additionally, women who have not experienced complications in previous pregnancies may also be less likely to seek MHS for subsequent pregnancies [79]. As a result, maternal healthcare utilization declines with subsequent births, reinforcing limited access. Pregnancy termination history significantly predicts MHS utilization, as affected women tend to seek more care. Integrating family planning workers into maternal healthcare education and monitoring could enhance MHS use through government-healthcare collaboration [80, 81].

To achieve progress for all important MHS indicators and improve maternal healthcare equity in low-income countries like Bangladesh, both supply- and demand-side strategies have been proposed [82]. These include community-based healthcare, strengthened supervision, user fees, health information campaigns, free or subsidized care, cash transfers, vouchers, and infrastructure investment [82–84]. However, poorer populations often benefit less from public health spending than wealthier groups, highlighting the need for equity-focused program design.

Strengths and Limitations

This study’s strength lies in its comprehensive analysis of MHS indicators, considering four important indicators with advanced statistical techniques to assess progress, determinants, and inequalities in MHS utilization in Bangladesh from 2004 to 2022. Inequalities of MHS utilization in subgroups are presented both numerically and graphically. Additionally, DA was conducted to rank key factors influencing MHS use. However, the study has limitations. First, though the BDHSs are available from 1993 to 94 to 2022, the analysis is limited to six BDHS survey rounds, as data on all selected indicators (ANC, SBA, ID and PNC) were unavailable in the surveys conducted before 2004. Though, in case of 2004 BDHS, indicator PNC for mothers are available but it is not consistent with other selected BDHSs and rely on some assumptions, as questions on postnatal checkups for mothers were not asked for births delivered in a health facility, and it is assumed that deliveries in any health facility received a postnatal checkup for the mother and the child within the first two days of delivery, as a part of routine institutional delivery care. So, to keep the comparison consistent with other BDHs PNC, we excluded the results, interpretation and comparison for the 2004 BDHS PNC in our main analysis. Second, as this study used data from the cross-sectional nature designs, it does not allow for causal inferences or the establishment of cause-and-effect relationships among variables. In the multivariable analysis, the term ‘influence’ refers to a strong association between variables, but it does not imply causality. Third, the secondary data, collected through self-reported retrospective interviews, may be subject to recall bias, inaccuracies, and omissions of important details. However, given the standardized methodology and trained data collectors of BDHS, and considering cases with complete information, any resulting bias is expected to be minimal [85].

Conclusion

While maternal healthcare utilization in Bangladesh has increased from 2004 to 2022, it still falls short of the universal standard set by SDG, with inequality in utilization remaining across key equity strata. These inequalities continue to exist, between the poorest to the richest, less educated to highly educated, and rural to urban women. Therefore, merely increasing utilization will not be enough to achieve the overall maternal healthcare objectives.

Enhancing service availability and implementing targeted interventions are crucial to increasing maternal healthcare utilization among those most in need. Addressing inequalities requires both demand- and supply-side strategies, including financial support, improved service delivery, and health education. Monitoring disparities at the subnational level considering key socioeconomic indicators is essential for effective policymaking. Integrating maternal healthcare inequality factors into national frameworks can help bridge gaps between privileged and underprivileged groups. Achieving universal health coverage and related SDGs by 2030 demands context-specific policies focusing on vulnerable populations, including the poorest and poorer women, those with no or lower education, and women in rural communities. Beyond healthcare, policies should address broader developmental factors such as female education, economic empowerment, and infrastructure. Mixed-methods research is essential for understanding experience and barriers to access in maternal healthcare and ensuring evidence-based interventions.
