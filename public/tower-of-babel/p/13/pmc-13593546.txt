Preprocedural virtual reality simulation predicts residual conduction in pulsed field ablation: A case report

Published: 2026

Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) technology enables the reconstruction and augmentation of real-world environments using computer-generated 3-dimensional spaces. In the field of education, VR has been shown to enhance learning outcomes by providing highly immersive simulation experiences.1 Beyond its initial applications in entertainment, VR has increasingly been adopted in industrial and medical domains, with accumulating evidence supporting its utility in anatomy education and procedural training.1

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as a novel non-thermal ablation modality for the treatment of atrial fibrillation, offering tissue-selective ablation with improved safety profiles compared to conventional thermal techniques.2 Although high acute success rates have been reported, lesion durability remains a concern due to potential lesion regression and reconnection during the chronic phase.3 Therefore, achieving consistent lesion formation requires optimal catheter–tissue contact and appropriate energy delivery strategies. However, decisions regarding device sizing and the need for additional applications in PFA are often operator-dependent, leading to variability in procedural approaches across institutions.3, 4, 5

In light of these challenges, approaches to optimize catheter positioning and energy delivery are warranted.2 VR technology enables intuitive 3-dimensional visualization of anatomical structures and device–tissue interactions, making it a promising tool for both education and procedural planning.

We report a case in which preprocedural VR simulation was used to optimize catheter positioning and energy delivery during PFA, with the potential to improve device–tissue contact and lesion formation.

Case report

A 77-year-old woman with a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and chronic kidney disease presented with persistent atrial fibrillation of 6 months’ duration. As the arrhythmia was refractory to antiarrhythmic drugs, catheter ablation, including pulmonary vein (PV) and posterior wall isolations, was planned after obtaining informed consent. Preprocedural cardiac CT demonstrated left atrial enlargement with a volume of 225 mL, and no evidence of intracardiac thrombus.

A patient-specific 3-dimensional cardiac model was reconstructed from computed tomography (CT) data using GUIDEYE Research and Education (Anreal Twin, Gifu, Japan) and visualized in a VR environment using Gravity Sketch (Gravity Sketch, London, UK) with a Meta Quest 3 headset (Meta Platforms, Menlo Park, CA). This model enabled comprehensive assessment of the spatial relationships among the inferior vena cava (IVC), right atrium, fossa ovalis (FO), left atrium, PVs, posterior wall, and left atrial appendage and the anticipated transseptal access route.

Device geometry was additionally modeled using Fusion 360 (Autodesk, San Rafael, CA, USA), incorporating a Faradrive sheath and a 31-mm Farawave catheter (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA). The Faradrive sheath, a steerable sheath, was modeled with its geometry reconstructed at 10-degree increments of deflection. Both basket and flower configurations were reproduced and integrated into the VR environment, enabling simulation of catheter positioning based on actual device size and deployment characteristics (Figure 1A, Supplemental Video 1). The procedure was performed under conscious sedation with systemic anticoagulation maintained at an activated clotting time of 350–400 seconds. A 3-dimensional mapping system (EnSite; Abbott Medical, Abbott Park, IL) was used, and the procedure was conducted during atrial fibrillation. Following femoral venous access, a 20-pole catheter (BeeAT; Japan Lifeline, Tokyo, Japan) was positioned in the coronary sinus. A single transseptal puncture was performed under intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) using a ViewFlex Xtra catheter (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) and fluoroscopic guidance, followed by exchange to a Faradrive sheath. PFA was subsequently performed using the Farapulse system (Boston Scientific). PV isolation was performed with 8 applications per vein (2 pairs in both basket and flower configurations, with a 45° rotation between pairs). Posterior wall isolation was performed with 10 applications each at the roof and bottom regions. The sheath and catheter positions were consistent with those predicted by the preprocedural VR simulation (Figure 1B–1E).

During ablation, atrial fibrillation organized into atrial tachycardia. High-density mapping with an HD Grid catheter (Abbott Medical) demonstrated a perimitral atrial flutter and confirmed electrical isolation of the PVs and posterior wall (Figure 2A). Although no apparent gaps were identified between the superior PVs on the 3-dimensional mapping system, residual potentials were identified at the left PV carina (Figure 2B). Notably, preprocedural VR simulation had suggested a risk of incomplete lesion formation at this site based on the distance between the PVs and the carina anatomy (Figure 2C). In accordance with this prediction, additional applications were delivered to the left PV carina. Catheter positioning and sheath manipulation were adjusted to reproduce the alignment defined in the VR simulation (Figure 3A–3E) (Supplemental Video 2). 4 additional applications were delivered, resulting in the elimination of residual potentials (Figure 3F). As this was the initial ablation procedure for persistent atrial fibrillation and the planned lesion set had been completed, additional ablation targeting the perimitral flutter circuit was not performed. Discussion

In this study, preprocedural VR simulation enabled detailed visualization of the 3-dimensional relationships among the PVs, left atrial posterior wall, and left atrial appendage, facilitating appropriate device selection and ablation planning.

Based on this simulation, a relatively wide separation between the left superior and inferior PVs was recognized preprocedurally, suggesting a potential risk of incomplete lesion formation at the left PV carina. However, because routine empirical ablation of the left PV carina is not part of our standard strategy, this finding did not lead to a predefined modification of the initial ablation plan. Instead, it prompted careful intra-procedural reassessment of this region. Consistent with the VR-based prediction, residual potentials were subsequently identified at the left PV carina on high-density mapping and were successfully eliminated with additional applications. These findings suggest that VR-based preprocedural planning can enhance lesion predictability and procedural precision during PFA.

PFA is a non-thermal ablation modality that induces electroporation through the application of microsecond-duration electric fields, enabling preferential myocardial ablation while minimizing collateral injury.2, 3, 4, 5 Owing to its favorable safety profile and procedural efficiency, its clinical use has rapidly expanded in recent years.4 However, lesion formation with PFA is highly dependent on the degree of electric field exposure and catheter–tissue contact. In particular, pentaspline catheters may produce heterogeneous lesion depth across individual splines.6,7 Consequently, even when acute electrical isolation is achieved, this does not necessarily reflect durable and continuous lesion formation, and lesion integrity remains difficult to assess.3,4

Indeed, previous studies have shown that acute electrical isolation can often be achieved with relatively limited energy delivery; however, lesion regression and reconnection may occur during follow-up.3 This discrepancy highlights the potential gap between apparent procedural success and true lesion durability, emphasizing the importance of optimal catheter positioning and ablation strategy.

At present, catheter position and tissue contact are assessed using fluoroscopy, ICE, and 3-dimensional mapping systems; however, each modality has inherent limitations.2 Fluoroscopy and ICE are highly operator-dependent, and in the context of PFA—where reliable contact indices are lacking—accurate evaluation of optimal catheter positioning can be challenging. 3-dimensional mapping systems offer valuable spatial information; however, their accuracy may be affected by patient movement, vagal responses, or impedance changes.6 In addition, PFA-specific complications—including unintended left atrial appendage isolation and atrial tachycardia arising from excessive or incomplete lesion formation—have been reported and may be associated with suboptimal catheter positioning and energy delivery.7,8

The VR-based simulation proposed in this study may help overcome several of these challenges. VR enables intuitive 3-dimensional visualization of cardiac structures. Although the anatomical information used for VR simulation was derived from conventional CT data and could potentially be appreciated using conventional 3-dimensional reconstruction, the immersive VR environment facilitated a more intuitive understanding of the spatial relationships between the PVs and surrounding structures. In the present case, this enhanced anatomical perception increased awareness of the relatively wide separation between the left superior and inferior PVs and the potential for incomplete lesion formation at the left PV carina. Using high-resolution electrocardiogram-gated CT data, preprocedural simulation facilitates assessment of sheath trajectory, catheter deployment, and transseptal puncture site. Furthermore, device sizing can be individualized based on PV distance and patient-specific anatomy.

Notably, in cases with a large distance between the PVs, VR simulation may assist in identifying regions at risk for incomplete lesion formation, such as the carina, and may facilitate heightened awareness and targeted intra-procedural reassessment of these regions. This capability facilitates the formulation of a proactive ablation strategy aimed at ensuring contiguous lesion formation, particularly given the known heterogeneity and potential regression of PFA lesions. The clinical application of VR in catheter-based interventions has been increasingly reported, including in percutaneous coronary intervention and left atrial appendage closure, where preprocedural simulation and device reshaping strategies have demonstrated potential benefits.9, 10, 11 Our findings extend these concepts to PFA, indicating that VR-guided planning may enhance procedural precision and strategy. Beyond procedural planning, VR-based simulation may also have educational value by enabling operators to better understand complex 3-dimensional anatomy and device–tissue interactions, potentially improving technical proficiency and reducing operator dependency.

Several limitations of this approach should be acknowledged. First, the residual potential observed at the left PV carina in this case may have been influenced by suboptimal catheter contact. However, no apparent gaps were identified around the PVs on the mapping system, and multiple applications from different orientations had been delivered, suggesting that insufficient contact alone is unlikely to account for the finding. Importantly, this region corresponded to an area predicted by VR simulation to be at risk for incomplete lesion formation, suggesting an anatomical contribution. Second, this approach necessitates preprocedural cardiac CT, which involves radiation exposure, contrast use, and potential variability in institutional workflows. In addition, atrial size and geometry may vary depending on volume status and hemodynamic conditions at the time of imaging, which may not fully reflect the intraprocedural anatomy. Third, the VR environment does not support collision detection or physics-based simulation; therefore, catheter deformation upon contact with the cardiac wall cannot be reproduced. Fourth, additional ablation based on simulation findings may increase the number of applications and procedural duration, potentially increasing complication risk. Nonetheless, these limitations may be mitigated through careful patient selection and procedural planning, and the potential benefits of improved lesion durability and reduced recurrence may outweigh these concerns. The VR simulation process required approximately 10 minutes, suggesting its feasibility in routine clinical practice, although generalizability across institutions remains to be established. Although optimization of the transseptal puncture angle was beyond the scope of this case report, the VR environment enabled visualization of the anticipated access route. With further technological development, VR-based simulation may facilitate patient-specific planning of transseptal puncture sites and sheath trajectories. Advances in low-dose CT, non-contrast imaging techniques, and broader accessibility of VR technology may further facilitate its clinical adoption.

Conclusion

In this case, preprocedural planning using VR simulation contributed to the optimization of catheter positioning and energy delivery strategies during PFA. The ability to anticipate anatomically driven heterogeneity in lesion formation and the concordance of these predictions with intraprocedural findings support the clinical utility of this approach. Although PFA offers a favorable safety profile, visualization of lesion formation remains limited, highlighting the importance of strategic preprocedural assessment. VR-based simulation may improve procedural reproducibility and clinical outcomes from a personalized medicine perspective. Further studies in larger cohorts are warranted to confirm its clinical impact.
