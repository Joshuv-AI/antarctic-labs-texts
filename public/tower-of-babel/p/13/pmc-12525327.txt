Sources and aging of individual atmospheric particles in New York City: Integrating novel functional group data from optical photothermal spectroscopy with elemental and mass spectrometry data
Authors: Yao Xiao, Emily J. Costa, Xu He, Mitchell J. Rogers, Jessica A. Mirrielees, Tori N. Hass-Mitchell, Taekyu Joo, Benjamin A. Nault, Drew R. Gentner, Rachel E. O’Brien, Andrew P. Ault
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Elevated concentrations of fine particulate matter (PM2.5, particles < 2.5 μm) lead to negative health outcomes in urban areas, such as New York City (NYC). The sources of particles contributing to PM2.5 in NYC are variable and complex due to the range of primary anthropogenic and biogenic emissions, as well as secondary aerosol formation (i.e., aging) from gaseous precursors. To improve understanding of the contributors to PM2.5, single particle microspectroscopy uses chemical fingerprints to identify sources and the extent of aging, but few studies have integrated multiple microspectroscopy methods to understand PM2.5 in NYC. Herein, we focus on a recently-developed form of microspectroscopy that can measure atmospherically-sized particles (>~0.8 μm), optical photothermal infrared (O-PTIR). We compare O-PTIR to existing microspectroscopy methods [Raman, fluorescence, and energy dispersive X-ray (EDX)] to study sources and aging of the complex NYC aerosol based on functional group and elemental information, which we also relate to bulk mass spectrometry methods. Single particle data shows submicron aerosol composition dominated by carbonaceous particles that fluoresce mixed with ammonium and sulfate, with a range of oxidized organic functional groups observed. At larger sizes, more primary sources (salts, dust, and biological) were observed, with nitrate being the dominant secondary anion. Collectively, the results from OPTIR and other instruments across case-study days reveal variations in sources and aging, with greater variability at larger diameters. Demonstrating the potential of O-PTIR when combined with the other methods to provide data that is important for improving air quality in urban megacities.

Introduction

Ambient particulate matter (PM) is associated with negative impacts on human health, including cardiovascular and respiratory diseases (Pope, Ezzati, and Dockery 2009; Pope et al. 2002) and consists of particles emitted from a range of sources (Prather, Hatch, and Grassian 2008). In particular, fine mode PM (PM <2.5 μm in diameter) is strongly associated with negative health effects (Pope and Dockery 2006), and remains a concern for public health, particularly in densely populated megacities (Dedoussi et al. 2020; Dockery et al. 1993). New York City (NYC) has a population of >8 million people with >20 million residents in the larger metropolitan area. However, fewer air quality studies have been conducted in NYC in comparison to some other US cities, such as Los Angeles, Atlanta, Pittsburgh, and Houston (Ditto et al. 2018; Joo et al. 2021; Schroder et al. 2018; Tan et al. 2014; Yoon et al. 2021). Given the changing landscape of aerosol precursor emissions (McDonald et al. 2018), it is critical to understand the contributions of both biogenic and anthropogenic sources and precursors to the ambient aerosol in NYC.

Most prior field studies in NYC and surrounding areas have focused on bulk aerosol composition, including inorganic (e.g., sulfate, nitrate, and ammonium) and carbonaceous species (e.g., organic carbon and elemental carbon) (Bae, Demerjian, and Schwab 2006; Hass-Mitchell et al. 2024; Schwab, Felton, and Demerjian 2004; Sun et al. 2011). Secondary inorganic species, such as sulfate, nitrate, and ammonium have historically been large contributors to PM2.5 (54–67%) in NYC (Nault et al. 2021; Qin, Kim, and Hopke 2006). In 2009, Sun et al. (2011) showed that organic aerosol (OA) and sulfate were the two dominant species in NYC, contributing over 70% of non-refractory submicron particle mass. With improving air quality due to regulations and technology, OA comprised around 80% of the aerosol mass, with secondary OA (SOA) compriseing 73–76% of OA mass (Hass-Mitchell et al. 2024). Primary OA (POA) remains important with contributions from hydrocarbon-like OA (HOA) including traffic, cooking OA, and also biomass burning (Hass-Mitchell et al. 2024; Joo et al. 2024). The high fractions of SOA and sulfate are roughly similar to bulk, non-refractory PM in other urban areas, but differences, such as greater amounts of more oxidized–oxidized organic aerosol (MO-OOA) in Atlanta and Houston vs. NYC reveal differences with more detailed analyses (Budisulistiorini et al. 2013; Dai et al. 2019). On top of the different source factors for OA observed in urban environments, including NYC, diverse organic functional groups have also been detected, including hydroxyls, carboxylic acids, esters, ethers, organonitrates, and organosulfates (Ditto et al. 2020). These functional groups can provide further information on sources, chemical processes, and particle aging, which vary between urban areas (e.g., Atlanta).

Individual particle analysis provides insights into sources and aging of aerosol particles that is complementary to mass-based (i.e., bulk) analyses (Ault and Axson 2017; Bauer, Ault, and Prather 2013; Bondy et al. 2018; Pratt and Prather 2012; Shen et al. 2016). The diversity of aerosol sources in a complex environment like NYC, including primary (e.g., soot, sea spray aerosol (SSA), biological, and dust) and secondary (e.g., SOA, sulfate, nitrate, ammonium), makes understanding their relative contributions to the overall aerosol population and its mixing state as a function of air mass challenging. To better characterize such aerosol populations, tools that can probe the chemical composition of all particles are needed, such as single particle analysis, which has been used to understand particle sources and aging with single particle microspectroscopy and mass spectrometry techniques (Ault and Axson 2017; Pratt and Prather 2012; Riemer et al. 2019). For example, computer-controlled scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (CCSEM-EDX) has been used to probe the contributions of broad sources [e.g., SSA] by using individual particle elemental composition and clustering algorithms (Bondy et al. 2017). CCSEM-EDX has also been used to characterize the overall aerosol population during field work and combined with other electron microscopies, specifically scanning transmission X-ray microscopy–near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy (STXM-NEXAFS), to understand the mixing state of particles from multiple field campaigns. Some of these campaigns include the Carbonaceous Aerosol and Radiative Effects Study (CARES) in California, Observations and Modeling of the Green Ocean Amazon (GoAmazon) in Brazil, and the Southern Oxidant and Aerosol Study (SOAS) in the southern U.S. (Bondy et al. 2018; Fraund et al. 2017; O’Brien et al. 2015), which often found less internally mixed particle populations than bulk analyses predicted at those sites.

Vibrational microspectroscopic techniques like Raman microspectroscopy and the recently-developed optical photothermal infrared spectroscopy (O-PTIR) have been used less often to characterize the chemical composition of individual ambient atmospheric particles (Cooke et al. 2024; Craig et al. 2017; Olson et al. 2020), but have potential to provide complementary information to CCSEM-EDX. For example, Cooke et al. (2024) explored the distribution of sulfur, sulfate, and organosulfates within individual particles in Mexico City for a few samples with a combination of CCSEM-EDX, O-PTIR, and Raman microspectroscopies. The study demonstrated that 25–41% of particles contained sulfur by CCSEM-EDX while OPTIR + Raman confirmed that sulfur-containing components within particles were mixtures of sulfate, bisulfate, and organosulfates, showing the capability of these microspectroscopy methods to both provide information on the form of sulfur present and that the aerosol was not a homogeneous internal mixture. Therefore, in this study, to improve understanding of aerosol sources and aging as a function of size, the recently developed O-PTIR method was used in combination with Raman and CCSEM-EDX to analyze individual atmospheric particles collected during a range of atmospheric conditions during the New York City metropolitan Measurements of Emissions and Transformation (NYC-METS) campaign from July to August 2022 in NYC. This individual particle data was combined with complementary mass spectrometry data to gain a greater understanding of the sources and aging of aerosols in NYC.

Experimental

Aerosol sample collection and wind measurement

Atmospheric aerosol particles were collected at the City University of New York (CUNY) Advanced Science Research Center (ASRC) (40.8157° N, 73.9497° W; 38 m a.m.s.l.) between 11th July and 4th August 2022 (Figure 1a). Size-resolved particles were collected using a 10-stage micro-orifice uniform deposit impactor (MOUDI, TSI Corp. Model 110NR) and a 10-stage mini-MOUDI (TSI Corp. Model 135–10B) sampling at 28 and 2 L min−1, respectively, with a PM10 cyclone (URG Model 2000–30EA) to exclude particles larger than 10 μm in front of the MOUDI. The 50% aerodynamic diameter (da) size cut points for both the MOUDI and mini-MOUDI used in this analysis are 0.56, 0.18, and 0.056 μm for stages 6, 8, and 10, respectively. For individual particle analysis, particles were impacted onto transmission electron microscopy (TEM) grids (Carbon Type-B film copper grids, Ted Pella Inc.) and silicon substrates (Ted Pella Inc.) for SEM-EDX, O-PTIR, fluorescence, and Raman analysis after collection with the mini-MOUDI. Substrates were inspected before the field campaign and unused ones served as blanks. Particles were also impacted onto Teflon (SKC Inc.) filters or aluminum foil substrates (TSI) in the MOUDI for offline aerosol mass spectrometry (AMS) analysis. The analysis herein focuses on samples collected on 17, 24, 25, 29, and 31 July, which represent a range of ambient conditions (Table 1). Samples were stored under ambient indoor conditions in sealed Petri dishes (Pell Corporation) before microspectroscopy analysis to avoid modification by freeze-thaw cycling or condensation after refrigeration or freezing (Laskina et al. 2015). Samples for offline AMS analysis were stored in a freezer (−20 °C) before analysis.

Figure 1b shows wind speed, direction, and wind rose plots during the selected dates. The Hybrid Single-Particle Lagrangian Integrated Trajectory (HYSPLIT) model from NOAA was used to calculate air mass backward trajectories to determine the air mass source region (Rolph et al. 2017; Stein et al. 2015). Trajectories in Figure 1c were calculated at a starting height of 500 m above sea level for 2 days (48 h) with 3 h intervals. Trajectories were run using 1° Global Data Assimilation System (GDAS) meteorology. Heights of 200 and 1000 m above sea level were also calculated using the same conditions and are shown with 500 m in the Supporting Information for each period in Figure 1b (Figures S1–S7). The backward trajectories were grouped by source region and came from the northwest on 17th and 29th July, west on 25th July, southwest on 24th July, and southeast on 31st July, matching wind direction measured at the site. This correlated with previous studies showing air masses coming from the southwest during the summer (Hass-Mitchell et al. 2024; Rogers et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2016).

Single particle analysis

Microspectroscopy approaches used for single particle analysis are shown in Table 2.

Computer controlled scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (CCSEM-EDX)

Particles collected on stages 6, 8, and 10 of the mini-MOUDI were analyzed using a FEI Quanta environmental SEM coupled with a field emission gun operating at 20 keV with a transmission detector (high-angle annular dark field, HAADF) (Bondy et al. 2018; Laskin et al. 2012). An EDX detector (EDAX, Inc.) was used to quantify X-rays of elements with atomic numbers (Z) ≥ 6. Initial manual analysis provided insight into loading and particle morphology samples. A total of 13,806 individual particles were analyzed by CCSEM-EDX (details in Table S1), as described previously (Ault et al. 2012; Bondy et al. 2018). Relative abundances of the following elements were determined from EDX spectra: C, N, O, Na, Mg, Al, P, S, Cl, K, Ca, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, and Zn. Cu was collected (due to presence in the TEM grids), but subsequently removed before quantification, while Si was excluded from analysis of silicon substrates. CCSEM-EDX data was analyzed by K-means clustering algorithm in MATLAB R2022a (MathWorks, Inc.) as described in Ault et al. (2012). The dataset was categorized into 20 and 15 clusters for particles collected on TEM grids and silicon substrates, respectively, which then were grouped into particle classes based on established EDX spectral signatures from prior ambient aerosol studies: organics (organic + sulfate, organic + nitrate, organic + sulfate + nitrate) and soot (Bondy et al. 2018; Cooke et al. 2024; Laskin, Cowin, and Iedema 2006; Li et al. 2003, 2016; Moffet et al. 2010), biomass burning (Hopkins et al. 2007; Li et al. 2003), mineral dust and concrete dust (Ca-S) (Ault et al. 2012; Axson et al. 2016; Krueger et al. 2004), sea spray aerosol (Ault et al. 2013b; Bondy et al. 2017; Kirpes et al. 2018, 2020), and biological particles (Bondy et al. 2018).

Optical photothermal infrared (PTIR) microspectroscopy

A mIRage instrument (Photothermal Spectroscopy Corp.) was used to collect simultaneous optical photothermal infrared and Raman spectra of individual particles (O-PTIR + Raman) collected on stage 6 of the mini-MOUDI (da: 0.56–1.0 μm). The mIRage consisted of an Olympus microscope with a visible objective (4 × 0.13 numerical aperture, 17.3 mm working distance, Nikon Plan Fluor) for visualization and a Cassegrain reflective objective used for simultaneous IR + Raman collection (40 × 0.78 numerical aperture, 8.3 mm working distance). A continuous wave laser was used as the probe (532 nm, 200 mW) and two pulsed, tunable IR lasers to generate photothermal enhancement. For the fingerprint region, a quantum cascade laser (QCL) source covered 880–1950 cm−1 with an average spectral resolution of 2 cm−1. An optical parametric oscillator (OPO) laser source covered 2700–3600 cm−1 with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1. The instrument has previously been applied to submicron aerosol samples to measure the chemical composition while avoiding contact with the sample for laboratory samples (Olson et al. 2020), field experiments (Mirrielees et al. 2022), and field campaigns (Cooke et al. 2024). The spectra were collected at a scan rate of 100 cm−1/s for 15 s acquisition and averaged over three accumulations. The IR laser repetition rate was set at 100 kHz and 300 ns per pulse. IR power and visible laser were set at 4–10 mW depending on the particle response. At least 30 particles were measured for each sample to determine representative particle types. Background spectra were also collected to identify any interference.

Raman (and fluorescence) microspectroscopy

A Raman microspectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba Inc.) consisting of an Olympus microscope with a 100× optical objective (0.9 numerical aperture, Olympus), a 532 nm Nd:YAG laser (30 mW), and a CCD detector was used to collect Raman spectra and fluorescence maps for particles collected on mini-MOUDI stage 6. Spectrum were acquired from 500 to 4000 cm−1 with a 600 groove/mm grating, giving 2 cm−1 spectral resolution. Samples were photobleached with a 30 s delay, after which each spectrum was collected with 30 s acquisitions and 3 accumulations. A 20° polynomial curve baseline correction was used in the Horiba LabRAM software to assist with deconvolution of the Raman peaks from the fluorescence background. Fluorescence maps were acquired from 545 to 605 nm over an area of 20 × 20 μm with a step size of 0.5 μm for 0.1 s acquisitions and 5 accumulations as in May et al. (2018).

Bulk chemical analysis

Aerosol mass spectrometry (AMS)

Offline AMS analysis was carried out similar to previous work using a small volume nebulizer (SVN) (O’Brien et al. 2019). All AMS mass spectra were processed using SQUIRREL (v1.65C) and Pika (v1.25C). Elemental ratios were calculated using the improved ambient method (Canagaratna et al. 2015). Ammonium iodide (AI) was used as the internal standard and two different methods were used for the two sample sets. The sample collected on 17 July 2022 was collected for 24 h as a baseline for day and night on Teflon filters on MOUDI stages 3–10 and a micro-extraction method was used to prepare extracts for the nebulization. For this extraction, an 8 μL droplet of MilliQ water containing 0.5 g/L AI was placed on the surface, and the solution was drawn up into the syringe and then placed back down in the same spot 10 times to facilitate mixing/extraction. After this, the droplet was left on the surface for 2 min and then drawn back up into the syringe and injected into the SVN. The higher surface tension of water created a bead on the surface that was easily extracted back up into the syringe. The relative mass fractions of sulfate, nitrate, and chloride were calculated using the signal for each ion family relative to iodide (the internal standard) and the standard relative ionization efficiencies of each salt.

The samples collected on 31 July 2022 were collected for 12 h (day and night) on aluminum foil substrates for diel cycle baseline analysis at normal ambient conditions. These samples were extracted with ~2 mL of methanol in a baked glass vial with shaking for 1 h. After extraction, the liquid was moved into a new clean baked glass vial and then it was concentrated to dryness under ultra-pure nitrogen for 30 min. To reconstitute, ~100 μL of methanol was added and gently swirled for 1–2 min. Solutions to inject onto the SVN were made using an internal standard solution in methanol with 0.1 g/L AI and a solution combination of 10 μL of the AI solution combined with 25 μL of the reconstituted sample. Approximately 8 μL of this mixed solution was injected into the SVN for each injection and at least three replicates were run for all experiments. Blanks were field blanks collected at the same time as the field samples and prepared in the same manner as the samples. Additionally, due to challenges during sampling, additional offline-AMS samples were not available from this study.

Aerosol chemical speciation monitor analysis (ACSM)

An Aerodyne Time-of-Flight Aerosol Chemical Speciation Monitor (ACSM) with a standard vaporizer and PM1 lens was collocated with filter measurements for online, time-resolved measurements of the mass concentration and chemical composition of non-refractory aerosols (i.e., organic, nitrate, sulfate, ammonium, and chloride) (Fröhlich et al. 2013). Upstream of the ACSM, the sample flow passed through a PM2.5 cyclone at 3 lpm and was dried to relative humidities below 35% by a Nafion dryer. Instrument data was collected in 40 s packets (20 s ambient, 20 s particle filter) and saved at 80 s before being averaged to 5 min resolution during analysis. Additional discussion on sampling configuration, instrument calibrations, and data processing as well as positive matrix factor analysis is available in Hass-Mitchell et al. (2024).

Results and discussion

Description of particle classes

Based on elemental composition from SEM-EDX, eight particle categories were identified: organic, organic + sulfate, soot, biomass burning, Ca-S, dust, aged sea spray aerosol (SSA), and biological particles. Figure 2 shows a representative SEM image and EDX spectrum for each class of particles. Organic/soot particles were identified based on high intensity of C and O, with no other elements observed. Organic particles were generally circular with a circularity range from 0.95 to 1, with 1 being a perfect circle (Ault et al. 2012). Fresh soot particles have previously been identified by SEM images based on their unique morphology from aggregated spheres leading to lower circularity (Bondy et al. 2018; Li et al. 2003). However, K-means clustering is based purely on elemental composition and could not distinguish between organic particles and soot, particularly aged soot which collapses into a more spherical morphology and were combined in CCSEM-EDX analysis (China et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2017). Sulfate is one of the major components of PM in the atmosphere and was 10% of total PM1 mass in NYC during a recent study (Hass-Mitchell et al. 2024). The presence or lack of sulfur (atomic < 1%) in the EDX spectra was used to distinguish sulfate-containing particles from pure soot or organic particles. Although organic + sulfur particles exhibited a similar circularity to organic particles, some were observed to have morphology indicative of liquid-liquid phase separation (LLSP) (i.e., core-shell morphology), as seen in Figure 2c. The S measured by EDX is likely SO42−, HSO4−, or S incorporated into organosulfate species (Chen et al. 2021; Cooke et al. 2024; Hettiyadura et al. 2019; Liao et al. 2015; Shakya and Peltier 2015). Biomass burning particles were typically circular with K, S, and N, in addition to C and O (Adachi and Buseck 2008; Hopkins et al. 2007; Li et al. 2003; Pósfai et al. 2003).

Dust particles were identified by metal oxides, which were indicated by the strong signal of O combined other metal elements like Mg, Al, Si, Ca, Ti, and Fe in the form of metal oxides (Coz et al. 2009; Laskin et al. 2005; Sobanska et al. 2003). Ca-S has been separated from the dust category as it is most likely gypsum (CaSO4) from cement and construction sites, as observed previously in Cleveland, Ohio (Ault et al. 2012). SSA was identified by the characteristic 10:1 ratio of Na to Mg with small contributions from K and Ca, which corresponded to the ion ratios in seawater (Pilson 2012). Cl to Na ratios that match seawater ratios indicate fresh SSA. Cl is depleted in aged SSA due to heterogeneous reactions with HNO3 from NOx oxidation and H2SO4 from SO2 oxidation (Bondy et al. 2017; Gard et al. 1998; Kirpes et al. 2018), such that aged SSA is identified by depleted Cl (or no Cl) present with other characteristic SSA ions (Na, Mg, K, and Ca) (Ault et al. 2013a; Kaluarachchi et al. 2022). The final particle class was primary biological aerosol particles (PBAPs), which are primarily composed of C from organic macromolecules, small amount of N from amines/amino acids, O and P from phosphates, S, and K (Bondy et al. 2018; Huffman et al. 2012; Li et al. 2020). While these sources and types of particles have been observed in different urban areas previously, the combination was unique within NYC, with a greater number of primary sources leading to a more heterogeneous and externally mixed aerosol distribution at the larger particle sizes (~da = 1 μm) analyzed by CCSEM-EDX in comparison to other cities (e.g., Houston, Barcelona, and Shanghai) where submicron aerosol were more internally mixed and dominated by secondary species (Brean et al. 2020; Tiszenkel et al. 2025; Yao et al. 2018).

Chemical composition measured via O-PTIR and Raman

Vibrational spectroscopies (O-PTIR + Raman) were used to explore covalently-bonded inorganic species and organic functional groups. Figure 3 shows a representative O-PTIR spectrum stitched together from individual submicron particles for the fingerprint region (880–1800 cm−1) and for the C–H, N–H, O–H stretching region (2700–3600 cm−1). In the fingerprint region, the peak at 1085 cm−1 corresponds to the anti-symmetric stretch of sulfate, vas(SO42−), while the peak at 1411 cm−1 is the ammonium bending mode, δ(NH4+) (Olson et al. 2020). The peak at 1735 cm−1 corresponds to v(C ═ O), which can result from the presence of carbonyls, ethers, carboxylic acids, ketones or aldehydes (Molina et al. 2025). Those functional groups have previously been observed by both bulk Fourier-transform IR (FTIR) analysis in Los Angeles (Maria et al. 2002) and near Long Island Sound by bulk liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC MS) (Ditto et al. 2020). Esters can contribute 7–39% of total OA mass in NYC (Ditto et al. 2020).

In the C–H, N–H, O–H stretching region, three major peaks were observed. The peak at 2861 cm−1 in the C–H stretching region is assigned to symmetric vibrations of methylene groups, vs(CH2), while the peak at 3055 cm−1 is more sp2 hybridized, indicative of aromatic or alkene-associated vibrations, v(═C–H). Alkanes, alkenes, and aromatics have been observed to contribute ≥50% of OA mass from bulk FTIR spectra among 21 field measurement campaigns from 2000 to 2009 (Russell, Bahadur, and Ziemann 2011). The broad band peaking at 3205 cm−1 is likely from N–H stretching in ammonium (NH4+), matching the δ(NH4+) signal observed at 1411 cm−1, while the lower intensity shoulder at higher frequencies is from O–H stretching of OH-containing organics or water.

There was considerable variation in the PTIR spectra observed across the particles examined on the same stage (Figure 4). The most common modes observed were for δ(NH4+) near 1415 cm−1 and vas(SO42−) at 1100 cm−1, along with vs(HSO4−) at 1050 cm−1, indicating the co-location of ammonium and sulfate/bisulfate in most particles, but not all particles had both modes, and there was a range of intensities and peak ratios. The carbonyl mode, v(C ═ O), shifted position considerably (e.g., peaks observed at 1711, 1727, 1733, and 1747 cm−1), likely indicating that a range of carbonyl-containing functional groups were present. NH4+ and SO42− were also observed without detectable organics [e.g., v(C ═ O)] in several particles, indicating that the aerosol was not fully internally mixed. Low frequency peaks from 1610 to 1640 cm−1 could be lower intensity carbonyls, such as the Amide I for proteins (Shi et al. 2025), or higher frequency alkenes, while peaks closer to 1550 cm−1 are more confidently assigned to carbon-carbon double bonds. In the higher frequency region, the O–H stretching mode (O–H) was observed at 3427 cm−1 in some spectra (Olson et al. 2020). There was less variation in the higher frequency region, such that the 3 spectra shown cover the range of spectra observed (Figure 4a). Overall, Figure 4 highlights that what might appear as relatively homogeneous or internally mixed particles from bulk elemental composition (C, S, N, and O) was actually quite varied with a range of contributions from NH4+, SO42−, and a number of organic moieties.

Raman spectra provide complementary functional group information to the PTIR, as their intensity is driven by bond polarizability and not dipole moment, though the highly fluorescent nature of most particles measured in NYC limited its efficacy, highlighting a benefit of O-PTIR. Figure 5a shows three example spectra: organic, organic + sulfate, and soot, along with the silicon background. The majority of the organic particles autofluoresce, which results in an undulating baseline. Although a baseline correction was applied, minor fluorescence fluctuations can still be observed. In the C–H stretching region, Raman peaks associated with more sp3 hybridized carbon at 2865 and 2939 cm−1 are assigned to symmetric stretching of methyl and methylene groups, vs(CH2/CH3), and 2942 and 2992 cm−1 assigned to asymmetric stretching, vas(CH2/CH3) (Ebben et al. 2013; Fankhauser et al. 2025). The peak intensity above 3000 cm−1, including the peak at 3129 cm−1 is associated with more sp2 hybridized organic species, including aromatic and alkene-containing functional groups (Larkin 2011; Lee and Chan 2007), which is consistent with the associated fluorescence that accompanies conjugated, absorbing species. For example, multiple classes of organic aerosol components can contribute to the fluorescence, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), other aromatics, brown carbon, humic-like substances, and cellulose (Liu et al. 2023). However, comparing the optical image in Figure 5b with the fluorescence map shown in Figure 5c shows that the fluorescence is uniformly distributed across all particles in the image, indicating the source of the fluorescence is likely SOA. This is true across all substrate samples tested during NYC-METS in 2022, indicating the fluorescent components could be from SOA formed from aromatic precursors. It is worth noting that the particle samples still autofluoresced months to years after collection, indicating that the fluorescing species are very stable under ambient conditions after long storage times.

Another notable feature in Figure 5a was the peak at 974 cm−1, corresponding to symmetric stretching of SO42−, vs(SO42−), which is a Raman active (IR inactive band) corresponding to either (NH4)2SO4 or aqueous SO42− (Olson et al. 2020). Detailed analysis of this region was complicated by the silicon phonon mode from 930 to 980 cm−1. With the complementary PTIR and Raman peaks, sulfate could be confidently identified despite the fluorescing particles.

Particles containing soot were easily separated from organic particles without soot based on the intensity of the peaks at 1326 and 1596 cm−1, which corresponded to the D and G bands of graphitic carbon, respectively (Cooke et al. 2024; Doughty and Hill 2020; Herdman et al. 2011; Le et al. 2019; Sadezky et al. 2005). After collecting spectra, the particles did not exhibit signs of burning and the D and G bands did not increase relative to the fluorescent signal, indicating the bands were from soot, which was also identified by SEM imaging in Figure 2c. Soot samples were often present with organic peaks from ν(CH2/CH3) and ν(═C–H), which pure soot particles do not contain (Le, Lefumeux, and Pino 2022). This demonstrates greater particle-to-particle compositional variability than elemental information from EDX could provide in isolation.

Variation in size dependent particle classification and mass concentration

To quantify the fraction of particles containing organic functional groups and inorganic species, particle fractions are shown for particles with da = 0.56–1 μm (Figure 6a). Over 95% of the analyzed particles contained NH4+, SO42−, and C–H stretching modes based on PTIR spectra, while the carbonyl mode, v(C ═ O), was identifiable in only ~50% of particles. 11% of particles contained soot, based on the Raman spectra (green bar, right). All spectra used in these calculations are shown in Figure S8. To roughly quantify the different contributions, peak areas for each individual functional group were calculated for 31st July (Figure 6a inset). The areas between δ(NH4+) for the QCL and v(NH4+) for the OPO were set to equal each other, due to the different intensities from the two excitation lasers, while all other PTIR areas were normalized to δ(NH4+). Alkane, alkene, and carbonyl functional groups accounted for 27, 16, and 9% of the total peak area, respectively. The sum of all organic functional groups comprised 47% of the total peak area, while the inorganic functional groups sulfate and ammonium were 27 and 25%, respectively. Further efforts to quantify mass contributions from different functional groups will be incorporated in the future. To compare to the ensemble average, mass concentrations of bulk aerosol components in the submicron range were measured by ACSM for 31st July (Figure 6c), including organic (4.31 μg/m3), sulfate (0.49 μg/m3), nitrate (0.26 μg/m3), ammonium (0.22 μg/m3), and chloride (0.01 μg/m3), showing that >60% of total PM1 mass is organic and inorganics (e.g., sulfate and ammonium) are <20%. Combining with O-PTIR/Raman, the inorganic components were a broadly distributed across most particles, indicating a largely internally mixed population in the accumulation mode. Similar mixing state has been observed in California where CCSEM-EDX demonstrated a mixture of internal and external mixing states with a higher mixing state index for aged sea spray aerosols and a lower mixing state index for carbonaceous particles (O’Brien et al. 2015). On the other hand, while SOA material is a large mass fraction, carbonyls were only observed in ~50% of particles, suggesting that organic composition is not homogeneous across accumulation mode particles. While similar, diel day vs. night variations for 7/31 are shown in Figures S9 and S10. The mass concentration of each individual component was similar to the average measurements through the entire NYC-METS 2022 campaign (Hass-Mitchell et al. 2024). Further information on PMF analysis and mass spectra are included in the Supporting Information (and Figure S11).

To look more deeply into the inorganic components, size-dependent mass fractions of sulfate, nitrate, and chloride measured by offline AMS are shown in Figure 7 for 17th July 2022. Nitrate was dominant at larger sizes (1–3.2 μm), while a sharp transition from nitrate to sulfate was observed for PM smaller than 1 μm. Sulfate contributed over 90% of total inorganic mass from 1 to 0.1 μm, aligning with ACSM data with higher sulfate than nitrate concentrations and the O-PTIR observing sulfate in nearly all particles from 0.56 to 1.0 μm. Little to no chloride was observed in the submicron range from offline-AMS, agreeing with minimal Cl observed by EDX. However, there is a lack of intense antisymmetric vibration for nitrate ~1380 cm−1 in the particles analyzed by O-PTIR in Figure 6a compared to the nitrate observed in the ACSM data (Figure 6c). This is possibly due to the variations in the salt composition across the aerosol size distribution shown in Figure 7. The size cutoff for the ACSM is 2.5 μm, so it will sample larger particle sizes with a relatively high nitrate fraction (e.g., Stages 4 and 5). However, the microspectroscopy in Figure 6 shows data from stage 6, which has a much lower nitrate fraction (Figure 7). Note, the data set for Figure 7 does not match the date for Figure 6 because the 7/31 sample was analyzed with methanol extracts. However, the trends in the nitrate fraction shown here are representative of the trends observed for multiple sets of samples during this campaign. Moreover, ammonia gas uptake to acidic sulfate particles can be slowed by increasingly viscous organic coatings, which may result in the decrease of aerosol ammonium concentration observed in NYC (Liggio et al. 2011; Silvern et al. 2017). However, further studies of aerosol coatings and acidity in NYC are needed. Additional offline AMS analysis of organic components, including O:C and N:C ratios, as shown in Figures S12 and S13.

To break down the organic mass composition further from the ACSM, source apportionment via positive matrix factorization (PMF) analysis was applied to OA spectra and four source factors were identified: MO-OOA, less oxidized OOA (LO-OOA), cooking-related OA (COA), and HOA (Ulbrich et al. 2009). The online mass spectrometry data shows that only a portion of the organic aerosol is quite oxidized (MO-OOA + LO-OOA = 69%) and from the offline microspectroscopy it was shown that OOA is not even distributed across particles by the fact only 50% contain carbonyl peaks in the PTIR spectra. Due to the spatial resolution limitation for O-PTIR of ~800 nm, which limits analysis of particles smaller than da ~500 nm before impaction and spreading, CCSEM-EDX was utilized to characterize the size-resolved particle variations covering from 0.056 to 0.1, 0.18 to 0.32, and 0.56 to 1 μm (Figure 8a). For particles 0.56–1 μm the fraction of carbonaceous (organic and/or soot) particles (C and O) containing S on 31 July 2022 (day and night) was consistent with O-PTIR measurements, composing 83% of total particles counts while carbonaceous particles without sulfur made up the remainder. In contrast, for particles with diameters of 0.056–0.1 and 0.18–0.32 μm, carbonaceous particles (with no detectable S) accounted for 81 and 73%, respectively, while carbonaceous with sulfur particles only contributed to 12 and 23%, respectively. Carbonaceous particles with nitrogen were also observed, but were only a small fraction in this size range (3%). As expected, dust, biomass burning, and SSA have limited contributions in the submicron size range with <1, 2, and <1% observed, respectively (Bondy et al. 2018). The same trend is observed through all time periods shown except the 25 July 2022 rush hour. Over 22% of the total particles during 25 July 2022 rush hour were from dust likely caused by increased mechanical road dust generation due to more cars on the road. Compared with ACSM data in Figure 8b, organic masses dominate the aerosol mass across all meteorological conditions, similar to the CCSEM-EDX result. A notable observation is that from the EDX data sulfur (likely sulfate) is predominantly observed from 0.56 to 1.0 μm, where most of the ACSM observed organic mass would be seen, and observed less in the smaller size ranges analyzed (0.056–0.01 and 0.18–0.32 μm). This aligns with the findings in Rogers et al. (2025) that elevated secondary inorganic components (e.g., sulfate) facilitate aqueous SOA formation under humid conditions and points to this being most prevalent in larger accumulation mode particles where surface area typically peaks.

Conclusions

This study investigated the chemical composition of individual particles in NYC through multiple microspectroscopy techniques in comparison with bulk mass spectrometry data from the ACSM and offline AMS. Eight main categories of particles were observed in the submicron range: organic, organic + sulfate, soot, biomass burning, Ca-S, dust, aged SSA, and biological. Based on the chemical composition measured with O-PTIR spectroscopy (0.56–1.0 μm) for the first time for ambient conditions, NH4+ and SO42− were found in over 95% of particles from 0.56 to 1 μm. Alkane and aromatic/alkene functional groups were also present in nearly all particles, while only 50% contained carbonyls, which was surprising given that oxidized SOA was such a large mass fraction and merits further investigation. Soot was identified with Raman microspectroscopy (11% of particles), while nearly all particles analyzed by Raman exhibited fluorescence, likely due to absorbing species (likely SOA from aromatic precursors). Bulk particle mass concentrations (ACSM) on 31 July 2022 showed primarily organics (81%), sulfate (9%), and ammonium (4%). Size-dependent chemical composition from single particle CCSEM-EDX showed primarily C, O, and S in submicron particles, with less particles containing S at smaller sizes. Bulk offline AMS data showed a sharp transition between sulfate (submicron) and nitrate (supermicron). Overall, this combination of single particle composition with microspectroscopy and bulk mass concentrations from mass spectrometry can assist with improving apportionment of sources and aging, with the goal of informing efforts to reduce PM concentrations and health impacts in NYC and other urban areas.
