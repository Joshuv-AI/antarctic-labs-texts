Tensin-2 interactomics reveals interaction with GAPDH and a phosphorylation-mediated regulatory role in glycolysis
Authors: Paula Turkki, Iftekhar Chowdhury, Tiina Öhman, Latifeh Azizi, Markku Varjosalo, Vesa P. Hytönen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Integrin adaptor proteins, like tensin-2, are crucial for cell adhesion and signaling. However, the function of tensin-2 beyond localizing to focal adhesions remain poorly understood. We utilized proximity-dependent biotinylation and Strep-tag affinity proteomics to identify interaction partners of tensin-2 in Flp-In 293 T-REx cells. Interactomics linked tensin-2 to known focal adhesion proteins and the dystrophin glycoprotein complex, while also uncovering novel interaction with the glycolytic enzyme GAPDH. We demonstrated that Y483-phosphorylation of tensin-2 regulates the glycolytic rate in Flp-In 293 T-REx and MEF cells and found that pY483 tensin-2 is enriched in adhesions in MEF cells. Our study unveils novel interaction partners for tensin-2 and further solidifies its speculated role in cell energy metabolism. These findings shed fresh insight on the functions of tensin-2, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for diseases associated with impaired cell adhesion and metabolism.

Introduction

Integrin adaptors, such as tensins, regulate the activation and signaling of integrins, affecting crucial cellular functions such as cell growth, survival and development. Also, integrin regulation often plays a role in disease outcome. For that, understanding the functions of integrin adaptors is of high importance.

Tensins are a family of integrin adaptor proteins that are generally considered to interact with integrin cytoplasmic domains in the cell-ECM adhesions1. Tensin-2, also known as C1 domain containing protein with homology to tensin (C1-TEN) or tensin-like C1 domain-containing phosphatase (TENC1), is less studied member of the tensin protein family comprising of four proteins, with tensins 1–3 sharing high homology. Tensins are often collectively categorized as focal adhesion proteins1. However, individual reports state that tensin-1 and tensin-3 are fibrillar adhesion proteins (tensin-1 located also in focal adhesions), whereas tensin-2 has been considered mostly as focal adhesion protein2,3.

In focal adhesions, tensins are believed to connect the integrin tails and actin cytoskeleton via binding to both partners. Tensin-2 PTB domain binds to integrin cytoplasmic domains of β3, β5, β7 and β1A, whereas β2 does not bind tensin-2 PTB4. The SH and PTB domains of tensin-2 are shown to bind receptor tyrosine kinase Axl5 , tumor suppressor proteins DLC16 and DLC27, DISC18, c-MpI9 and tyrosine kinase Syk10 (Fig. 1A). Since phosphorylation is a common regulator of protein function, we took a look at the known phosphorylation sites catalogued in Phosphosite plus for tensin-211 and observed that Y483 is the most often phosphorylated residue of tensin-2 (Fig. 1B). The biological role of tensin-2 is currently poorly understood. The protein–protein interaction networks of integrin-mediated adhesions have been studied in detail recently with talin-114, paxillin15,16 and kindlin as baits16. Whereas tensin-1 and -3 were commonly found in these screens, tensin-2 was not a prominent interaction partner to any of these central adhesion proteins. Also multiplex protein–protein interaction study with 16 different integrin-adhesion complex proteins as baits suggested that among the tensin family members, only tensins 1 and 3 belong to the central group of adhesion proteins17. In line with those, tensin-2 was listed within the meta-adhesome (2000 proteins) but was not present in the consensus adhesome (60 proteins)18. Furthermore, overexpression of tensin-2 does not influence beta-integrin 1 activation, as does the overexpression of tensin-1 and tensin-319, suggesting slightly different role in adhesion biology for tensin-2 as compared to tensins 1 and 3. Tensins 1 and 3 are not able to activate the integrin themself but are able to maintain the activated integrin state once integrin has been activated by another adaptor protein such as talin-119.

Tensin-2 has been associated with nephrotic diseases and a truncated form of tensin-2 is believed to cause the phenotype of the ICGN mouse which is used as a model for nephrotic syndrome20. Tensin-2 has been associated with insulin signaling via regulation of insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1)21,22 and cell metabolism via AMP-activated kinase (AMPK)23. Indeed, a small inhibitory molecule targeting tensin-2 has been indicated as potential therapeutic agent in metabolic syndrome through dual targeting of IRS1 and AMPK23. The connection to IRS and AMPK pathways and potential role as metabolic regulator requires further understanding of the biological functions of tensin-2.

Here we used proximity-based biotinylation technique called BioID to examine the proximal interactome (10–15 nm radius) of tensin-2 and affinity proteomics utilizing Strep-tagged tensin-2 to study its direct interactome in Flp-In 293 T-REx cells. In addition we studied the location of endogenous tensin-2 in MEF cells and the glycolytic rate in Flp-In 293 T-REx and MEF cells expressing tensin-2 constructs.

Results

Tensin-2 is a unique member within the tensin family with predicted localization to nucleus

In order to understand tensin-2 biological role we first compared it to other members of the tensin family. The conserved domains at the N- and C-terminal ends of tensin-1 and tensin-2 are highly similar with roughly 60% and 67% similarity, respectively24. However, in addition to conserved domains in the terminus, the central part of tensin-2 contains long proline-rich unstructured region without known functions and that has only low similarity to other tensins24. Altogether basic local alignment (BLAST)25 showed 49%, 52% and 47% sequence similarity when tensin-2 was compared to tensin-1, tensin-3 and tensin-4, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

We used bioinformatics analyses and reviewed databases to build a comprehensive view of tensin-2 location and function. We first used several bioinformatics tools to predict the subcellular locations of tensin family members of human and mouse origins. WoLF PSORT26 and CELLO27 ranked nucleus as the most prominent location while DeepLoc28 predicted cytoplasmic and plasma membrane localizations to be the highest for tensins 1–3 (Table 1). However, nuclear localizations also had relatively high scores in DeepLoc (tensin-1 hu/mo: 0.3466/0.3424, tensin-2 hu/mo: 0.3274/0.3192, tensin-3 hu/mo: 0.3534/0.3708 and tensin-4 hu/mo:0.3791/0.3636) (Table 1, Supplementary File 1), suggesting that overall tensins mostly reside in the nucleus, cytoplasm and plasma membrane. Tensin-2 shows high-to-medium expression levels in specialized tissues, with the highest level of protein expression found in kidneys (Human protein atlas29) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Gene-disease-association (GDA) score of tensin-2 is highest in nephrotic diseases according to DisGeNET30 (Supplementary Fig. 1C) and tensin-2 gene expression often decreases heavily in tumorous tissues as visible from GEPIA31 (Supplementary Fig. 1D). In line with this, downregulation of tensin-2 has been shown to enhance the tumorigenicity of A549 and HeLa cells32.

One phosphorylation site (Y483) was highly enriched and reported in almost 1600 previous studies (Fig. 1B). As these references were investigated more closely, 1576 associations were detected with MS analysis and only one using phospho-specific antibodies and Western blotting33. Closer inspection of the 1576 MS studies revealed that majority of the studies were associated with cancers (Supplementary Fig. 1E), mostly with lung and liver cancer studies (Supplementary Fig. 1F).

Tensin-2 proximal interactome reveals associations with proteins from the cytoplasm, plasma membrane, nucleoplasm and focal adhesions in Flp-In 293 T-REx cells

In order to gain understanding of the location and interaction partners of tensin-2 and the function of Y483 phosphorylation site, we performed a BioID proximal interactions analysis for Flp-In 293 T-REx cells stably expressing tensin-2 WT, phospho-mimicking (Y483E) and phosphorylation-negative (Y483F, Y483A) forms of the tensin-2. BioID reveals the proximal interactions of the bait protein and therefore enables defining the molecular environment near the bait-protein (Fig. 2A). To get insight into the molecular environment found in focal adhesions we used talin-1 as a reference protein, which is well-known focal adhesion-specific integrin adaptor. Interactome from membrane-targeted myristoylated eEGFP (myr-eEGFP) was used to define the “non-specific” proteome proximal to cell membrane. After statistical SAINT34 and CRAPome47 filtering, the high-confidence interacting proteins (HCIPs) were identified and the interactions clustered and visualized as dot plot (Fig. 2B and Supplementary File 2).Figure 2BioID proximity interactomics of tensin-2. Tensin-2 proximal interactome was only partially overlapping with the interactome of talin-1 and there was only minor interactions seen between tensin-2 and talin-1 and only few mutual adhesion-related interaction partners such as paxillin and kank-2. The three most often found proximal partners for tensin-2 were utrophin, peak1 and dystonin. The proximal interactomes between the mutant versions did not differ much. High confidence interactions enriched with the phosphonegative mutants (Y483F, Y483A) but not found with the phosphomimicking form (Y483E) were Rho GTPase activating protein ARHGAP22, RNA helicase DDX50, BRISC and BRCA1-A complex member BABAM2, Na+/K+-ATPases subunit AT1A1, RNA-dependent ATPase DX39B, adhesion protein paxillin, Spermatogenesis-associated protein 2 and ATP- and NAD(P)H-dependent mitochondrial enzyme P5CS. Enriched proximal interactions with the phosphomimicking form (Y483E) were ribosomal protein RL28, ribosome biogenesis factor NOP53 and prohibitin 1 (PHB1).

To obtain the molecular context and cellular functions of the high-confidence interacting proteins, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis. The enriched GO: Cellular Components and GO: Molecular Functions are shown in the Fig. 2C (for full data, please see Supplementary File 3). Tensin-2 WT was found mostly associated with proteins from the cytoplasm, plasma membrane and focal adhesions. Also, proteins present in nucleoplasm were found, suggesting, similarly to our predictions (Table 1), that tensin-2 could also be found in the nucleus. Interestingly, recently several members of the adhesome have been located to the nucleus and are proposed to control transcription, although their role there is not yet fully understood35.

Several interactions were detected to be modulated by these mutations (Fig. 3A, Supplementary File 4). Focal adhesion protein testin (TES) was the only interaction that was increased in Y483E and not in Y483F or Y483A as compared to WT tensin-2. Tensin-2 is associated with glycolytic enzyme GAPDH in Flp-In 293 T-REx cells

In order to detect the direct interaction partners of tensin-2 forms we performed affinity purification and mass spectrometry analysis (AP-MS). The data was similarly analysed with SAINT analysis and Crapome filtering as with the BioID data. We observed close correlation of interactomes of tensin-2 WT and phosphomimicking mutant Y483E, whereas the phosphodeficient mutants showed similar interactomes with each other (Fig. 4, Supplementary File 2). Similarly to BioID, utrophin (UTRN) was one of the high confidence hits also in this approach. Interestingly, glycolytic enzyme GAPDH showed high confidence interaction with WT form in AP-MS. Since GAPDH was not observed in the BioID analysis and did not seem to be associated with the phosphorylated forms with high confidence, we performed a pull-down analysis to verify their association. Here all tensin-2 forms were found to co-immunoprecipitate together with GAPDH (Supplementary Fig. 2A) while LacZ used as a negative control showed no binding. It should be noted here that the co-immunoprecipitation experiments were performed with overexpressed proteins. We also verified the tensin-2 expression levels for the stably transfected cells using SDS-PAGE and Western blotting and observed that tensin-2 antibody only recognized the recombinantly expressed tensin-2, suggesting that Flp-In 293 T-REx cells express very little to none of endogenous tensin-2 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). The expression levels of the tensin-2 constructs were equal between samples (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Y483-phosphorylated Tensin-2 is enriched in cell-ECM adhesions in MEF cells

In order to study the role of endogenous tensin-2 in adhesion biology in more detail, we moved on to analysing mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells, which as mesenchymal cells have prominent focal adhesions, and their large size is ideal for confocal microscopy analysis. We first labelled the cells with tensin-2 specific antibody (ab25182, Abcam) and found that tensin-2 was mainly located within the nucleus with faint adhesion-like staining on the cell edges (Fig. 5A). Nuclear localization was verified with another tensin-2 specific antibody (11990, Cell signaling technology) and visualized with orthogonal slicing of the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 3A,B). With careful inspection with the second antibody (11990, Cell signaling technology) adhesion-located signal was seen close to the basal membrane of the cells (Supplementary Fig. 3C), confirming the findings made with the first antibody. Labeling with the phosphotyrosine-specific pY483 antibody revealed clear adhesion-located pattern, with no nuclear staining (Fig. 5A), suggesting that the phosphorylated tensin-2 form is strongly associated with adhesions. To verify the location into the focal adhesions, we co-immunolabelled MEF cells with tensin-2 and paxillin antibodies (Fig. 5B) and found strong colocalization especially with the Y483 labeling. Localization to adhesion sites was further verified with co-immunolabeling of pY483 and active β1-integrin (Supplementary Fig. 3D). We also were curious to see whether we can detect colocalization between tensin-2 and GAPDH and performed co-immunolabeling targeting those antigens. GAPDH was found in same areas aspY483 phosphorylated tensin-2 (Fig. 5C). However, since GAPDH was found diffusely all around the cells with no specific compartmentalization, it is unreliable to quantify their colocalization (Fig. 5C).Figure 5Tensin-2 is found in the nucleus and focal adhesions in MEF cells. We then observed how the exogeneous tensin-2 localize in Flp-In 293 T-REx and MEF cells. We first inspected the Flp-In 293 T-REx cells stably expressing tensin-2 or talin-1 with the aid of antibody targeting the HA-tag included into expression constructs (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Flp-In 293 T-REx cells showed intense and diffuse tensin-2 localization widely in the cytoplasm with no changes in the location between the mutants and WT. Cytoplasmic localization of overexpressed tensin-2 in Flp-In 293 T-REx cells have been shown also previously5. We next overexpressed the MAC-tag-tensin-2-HA constructs in MEF cells and found that tensin-2 WT, Y483E and Y483F all showed localization to adhesions, nucleus and cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. 3F). However, in addition to adhesion-like staining (Supplementary Fig. 3G, zoom 1) vesicle-like large puncta were found (Supplementary Fig. 3G, zoom 2) implicating expression artifacts.

Altogether these results show that endogenous tensin-2 is mostly located in nucleus in MEF cells with the Y483 phosphorylated form enriched in adhesion plaques near the cell boundary, colocalizing with paxillin and active integrin.

Tensin-2 Y483 plays a role in the regulation of glycolysis and affects gene expression

We then assessed the effects of tensin-2 expression and Y483-phosphorylation on cell migration and phenotype. Tensin-2 expressing Flp-In 293 T-REx cells were migrating slower (total distance traveled 202.4 µm, SEM 13.59) than myr-EGFP (279 µm, SEM 20.69) expressing cells in eight hours. Expression of Y483F (270.6 µm, SEM 15.54) increased cell migration velocity a bit as compared to tensin-2 WT while Y483E (233.2 µm, SEM 13.52) had no significant effect. Tensin-2 Y483E (1671 µm2, SEM 93.59) and Y483F (1380 µm2, SEM 64.18) expressing cells were larger than WT (1040 µm2, SEM 48.24) and myr-EGFP (1100 µm2, SEM 71.91) expressing cells. We detected GAPDH among the tensin-2 -interacting proteins in the Strep-tag affinity proteomics (Fig. 4). We were thus curious to see if overexpression of tensin-2 WT, Y483E or Y483F would have an effect on Flp-In 293 T-REx glycolysis rate. With the aid of seahorse XF analyser we found that tensin-2 Y483E expressing cells had 50% lower rate of extracellular acidification (ECAR; 32.33 mpH/min), suggesting lower rate of glycolysis when compared to cells expressing Y483F (64.71 mpH/min). WT tensin-2 (42.76 mpH/min) or myr-EGFP (48.74 mpH/min) had no significant change as compared to Y483E (32.33 mpH/min) but were both showing higher mean glycolysis rate (Fig. 6B). As Flp-In 293 T-REx cells do not normally express tensin-2 (Supplementary Fig. 2B), we decided to verify the results by measuring the glycolysis rate of MEF cells overexpressing tensin-2 constructs. In line with the findings made with Flp-In 293 T-REx cells, a 25% decrease in glycolysis rate was detected in MEF cells transiently transfected with tensin-2 Y483E when compared to non-transfected (ctrl) or Y483F transfected cells (Fig. 6C).

Previous studies have found glycolytic enzymes PFK-1, aldolase or GAPDH to be regulated via their associations with actin cytoskeleton38. Since tensin-2 is actin-binding protein, it is possible that the regulation of glycolysis is actin-dependent process. Indeed, drug treatments suggested that the changes in the glycolysis rate of Flp-In 293 T-REx cells by 483 phosphorylation was regulated mostly via actin dynamics: Rock inhibitor Y-27632 treatment significantly increased the glycolysis rate of Y483E cells (from 24.05 to 39.82 mpH/min) whereas Y483F (from 45.09 to 49.52 mpH/min) was not significantly affected. Other drug treatments affected both cell lines similarly suggesting that these were not involved in glycolysis regulation: AMPK inhibitor compound C was bringing all glycolysis down with both cell lines whereas FAK inhibitor FAK14, actin-stabilizing jasplakinolide and PI3K inhibitor Wortmanin had no significant effect to either of the cell lines (Fig. 6D).

In order to confirm that the increase in glycolysis was not due to changes in gene expression status of glycolytic enzymes between Y483E and Y483F mutations, we performed RNA-seq analysis on the Flp-In 293 T-REx cells stably expressing either Y483E or Y483F version of tensin-2. There were no major changes in the gene expression in general (Supplementary Fig. 4A) and no significant changes in the mRNA levels of the glycolytic enzyme GAPDH. Four genes were found to be significantly differentially expressed with the most significant difference in the expression of ABCA12 with log2 fold change of -4.4 with Y483E as compared to Y483F (Fig. 6E,F). ABCA12 belongs to the family of lipid transporters known as ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters39 and has been associated with glucose-regulated insulin secretion of beta-cells40. The other genes showing significantly different expression between tensin-2 Y483E and Y483F were B3GALNT (log2 fold change 1.14), a membrane-bound glycoprotein, a transcription factor SALL3 (log2 fold change 1.03) and metalloprotease LMLN2 (log2 fold change -1.2). While mRNA sequencing outruled expression level changes within glycolytic enzymes as an explanation for changes in glycolysis rate, the mechanism of how tensin-2 Y483 influences the expression of these genes requires further studies.

To determine if the changes in glycolysis were due to changes in GAPDH activity, we monitored GAPDH activity from stably expressing Flp-In 293 T-REx cell lysates (Supplementary Fig. 4B). We found identical GAPDH activity in the lysates, suggesting that tensin-2 is not directly affecting the activity of GAPDH. Whether tensin-2 affects to GAPDH availability or its location in relation to the glycolytic enzymes requires further studies.

Altogether these results show that cells expressing Y483F migrate faster than the WT expressing cells, while Y483E expressing cells are slightly larger than WT or Y483F expressing cells, suggesting that actomyosin dynamics could be influenced by tensin-2 phosphorylation. Affinity proteomics and extracellular acidification analysis suggest that tensin-2 phosphorylation enhances its association with glycolytic enzymes and tensin-2 appears to regulate glycolysis via Rho-dependent mechanism. While direct interaction with GAPDH was observed, tensin-2 phosphorylation seems not to influence the enzymatic activity of GAPDH directly.

Discussion

In focal adhesions tensins and talins share a similar role connecting integrin tail and actin cytoskeleton. Tensins and talins are known to bind to the same NPxY-motif in the integrin tail. However, phosphorylation of the integrin NPxY domain inhibits talin binding but does not affect tensin binding and could thus provide the mechanism how to change the adaptor protein within cell–matrix adhesions41. Overall, the interplay between talin-1 and tensin-2 within focal adhesions is poorly understood. Here we showed that the proximal interactomes of tensin-2 and talin-1 differ substantially in Flp-In 293 T-REx cells (Fig. 2), suggesting that they could be involved in subsequent steps during adhesion maturation. Our first encounter with tensin-2 was during the study of talin-1 mechanotransduction14 where we found tensin-2 Y483 phosphorylation highly enriched in MEF cells displaying a phenotype where adhesion dynamics were stalled and cells were unable to polarize due to truncation of talin-1 (talin ΔR1-R12). In these previous studies we did not observe tensin-2 Y483 phosphorylation enriched with full-length talin, suggesting that the Y483 phosphorylation could come to play during the late adhesion maturation phase and become enriched when the adhesion turnover is inhibited. Also, inhibition of microtubule-mediated disassembly of focal adhesions by nocodazole treatment has been shown to enrich tensin-2 into the focal adhesion sites10, indicating that tensin-2 could be involved in the late maturation phase or even in the recycling step of cell–matrix adhesion.

Two out of three bioinformatics tools used here predicted tensins 1–4 to localize predominantly into nucleus, and our immunolabeling studies with two different antibodies showed that in addition to adhesion sites, endogenous tensin-2 resides in the nucleus in MEF cells. Prediction tools also revealed tensin-2 to have both NLS and NES and BioID experiment showed tensin-2 to be localized in the proximity of proteins from the nucleoplasm. Overexpression in MEF cells revealed tensin-2 residing substantially outside nucleus and in large vesicle-like puncta, which might be due to artifacts caused by overexpression model. Previously the C1 domain of tensin-2 has been shown to locate to the nucleus in DU145 cells5. Tensin-2 localization in the nucleus is not so surprising as recently several adhesion proteins have been found from the nucleus in SCC cells and were termed as a nucleo-adhesome35. The role of the adhesion proteins in the nucleus and the functions of the nucleo-adhesome still requires more studies.

Our immunolabeling studies further showed that Y483 phosphorylated tensin-2 localizes into the focal adhesions in MEF cells and the location was verified by paxillin and active integrin labeling. As the phospho-negative Y483F/A forms were also found in the adhesions in overexpression model in MEF cells, we propose that the phosphorylation may not direct tensin-2 into adhesion sites but rather adhesion-localized tensin-2 may become phosphorylated. Previously both Src12 and Axl-kinase13 have been shown to phosphorylate Y483 of tensin-2. Axl kinase is involved in several steps of cancer progression such as tumor cell growth, metastasis, invasion and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. More specifically, it has been shown to regulate actin-driven cytoskeletal rearrangements that can contribute to cancer cell invasion42. Tensin-2 has been shown to control rho-mediated actomyosin contractility in DLC-1 dependent fashion and therefore tensin-2 might serve as a link between Axl and regulation of the actin network. We were not able to find either Axl or Src kinase within tensin-2 interaction partners in Flp-In 293 T-REx cells, probably due to their low abundance in Flp-In 293 T-REx cells or due to the highly transient nature of their possible interaction. Also, several other previously identified binding partners were not found, suggesting low sensitivity for BioID to detect transient interactions. Novel interactions partners found in this study were GAPDH and utrophin. Utrophin was found as the most prominent hit in both, the BioID and AP-MS screen. GAPDH was found only for the AP-MS screen and its association with tensin-2 was further verified with co-IP (Supplementary Fig. 2A). The role of utrophin and tensin-2 association warrants further research while in this study we concentrated on the link between tensin-2 and glycolysis.

Tensin-2 has been previously linked to energy metabolism and shown to play a dual role in IRS1 regulation; the catalytic cysteine 231 has been shown to act as a tyrosine phosphatase of IRS1, decreasing IRS1 stability22 whereas Y483 phosphorylation has been shown to stabilize IRS113. As Y483 phosphorylation site was studied further, Cheng et al. noticed that Axl-knockdown mediated downregulation of tensin-2 expression and Y483 phosphorylation and led to decreased glucose import13. On the contrary, overexpression of tensin-2 Y483F led to increased expression of glucose transporter GLUT4, IRS1 and PDK1, which all regulate insulin-mediated glucose metabolism13, suggesting that tensin-2 has a somewhat complex role in glucose-metabolism. Our RNA-seq data did not reveal those changes in gene expression but we observed that expression of tensin-2 Y483E decreases the glycolytic rate via ROCK-mediated mechanism.

Cheng et al.13 showed that tensin-2 Y483 phosphorylation status controls glucose uptake via Axl-dependent mechanism in U2OS cells. Whether the lower glucose uptake was due to lower levels of glycolysis as shown here, or whether the lower levels of glycolysis in cells expressing Y483E are due to lower glucose uptake, requires further studies. Nevertheless, both findings support the model that tensin-2 Y483 phosphorylation contributes for the control of cell glucose metabolism. In addition to glucose uptake, also changes in enzyme activity could explain the changes seen in glycolysis rate. Our data revealed interaction between GAPDH and tensin-2, but we observed no changes in GAPDH activity levels in lysates from cells expressing tensin-2 Y483E and Y483F. Therefore, other mechanisms, such as accessibility of GADPH or its localization in relation to other glycolytic enzymes could explain the observed decrease in glycolysis rate associated with phosphomimicking tensin-2.

Link between actin and glycolytic enzymes is well known; changes in actin cytoskeleton and actomyosin contractility has been shown to regulate the activity of the glycolytic enzymes38 and the abundance of proteases acting on glycolytic enzymes43. Tensin-2 has been shown to promote cell contractility via Rho-mediated mechanism3, and actomyosin contractility has been indicated to regulate the activity of GAPDH, as it is inactive while bound to actin fibers38. The link between tensin-2 and GAPDH has not been shown previously. Our data here shows that tensin-2 is associated with GAPDH. Furthermore, Y483- phosphorylated tensin-2 is enriched at cell adhesions, where cell contractility is regulated, and Y483 phosphorylation was shown to control glycolysis rate (Fig. 7). Last, we showed here that Y483-phosphorylated tensin-2 regulated glycolysis can be overturn via Rho-Associated kinase (ROCK) inhibitor, providing support for the hypothesis that adhesion dynamics play a role in the regulation of energy metabolism.

Our study provides important insights into the cellular localization of tensin-2 and molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of energy metabolism by the adhesion protein tensin-2. The comprehensive analysis of tensin-2 interactors identified novel interaction partners, shedding new light on the diverse functions of this important protein. Both bioinformatic prediction and immunofluorescence analysis suggests that tensin-2, in addition to cell-ECM adhesions, also localizes into cell nucleus and Y483- phosphorylated form is specifically enriched in focal adhesions in MEF cells. The identification of novel interaction partners for tensin-2 provides new avenues for future research, as we work to unravel the complex regulatory networks that govern cellular processes.

While the exact biological role of tensin-2 remains to be fully elucidated, our results opens possibility that the suggested tumor suppressor role of tensin-2 in human cancers32 could be linked to adhesion biology and cell energy metabolism. Overall, our study represents a significant advance in our understanding of the link between adhesion biology and energy metabolism, as summarized in Fig. 7.

Summary of the main findings. Tensin-2 has been shown to promote cell contractility via Rho-mediated mechanism3 and is an actin-binding protein. Actin has been further shown to associate with GAPDH and to regulate glycolysis via different mechanisms38. Here we have shown that tensin-2 is associated with GAPDH and controls glycolysis via Y483 phosphorylation and actin dynamics.

Materials and methods

Plasmids

For protein expression plasmid backbone we used the MAC-tag-C44 (Addgene: #108077) where the tags have been embedded in the C-terminus of the bait protein.

For tensin-2 constructs we used the NCBI sequence NP_705761.2 where we introduced Y → A, Y → E and Y → F mutations on the position 483. For talin-1 we used the NCBI sequence NP_035732.2. For myristoylated-bait we used the signal sequence of MGNKCCSKRQ. The expression plasmids are deposited to AddGene (plasmid ID's 201794 - 201799).

Generation of stable cell lines

For generation of the stable cell lines inducibly expressing the MAC-tagged versions of the baits, Flp-In™ T-Rex™ 293 cell lines (Invitrogen, Life Technologies, R78007, cultured in manufacture’s recommended conditions) were co-transfected with the expression vector and the pOG44 vector (Invitrogen) using the Fugene 6 transfection reagent (Roche Applied Science). Two days after transfection, cells were selected in 50 μg/ml streptomycin and hygromycin (100 μg/ml) for 2 weeks, and then the positive clones were pooled and amplified in 150 mm plates. Ten plates were used for the affinity proteomics with Strep-tagged bait protein (AP-MS) approach, in which 1 µg/ml tetracycline was added for 24 h induction, and 10 plates for BioID approach, in which in addition to tetracycline, 50 µM biotin was added for 24 h before harvesting. Cells from five plates were pellets as one biological samples; thus, each bait protein has two biological replicates. Cells were snap-frozen and stored at − 80 °C.

Cell culture and transfections

MEF cell line was a kind gift from Dr. Wolfgang Ziegler and has been previously described by Xu and Baribault45 and stably tensin-2 expressing Flp-In 293 T-REx cells were maintained in a humidified 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. High glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) was in all experiments, unless otherwise stated. The cell lines were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination. Tensin-2 expression in Flp-In 293 T-REx cells was induced with tetracycline 24 h prior each experiment. MEF cells were transfected with 20–40 µg plasmid DNA per 106 cells using Neon transfection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the following parameters: 1300 V, 30 ms, one pulse.

Affinity purification and mass spectrometry

Affinity purification and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), analyses were performed as previously described44. Briefly, for AP-MS approach, samples were lysed in ice-cold mild lysis buffer (0.5% IGEPAL, 50 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaF, 1.5 mM NaVo3, 5 mM EDTA, with 0.5 mM PMSF, and protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich)). For BioID approach, cell pellets were lysed in lysis buffer described above supplemented with 0.1% SDS. In addition, BioID lysates were sonicated and treated with benzonase (Bio-Rad).

Affinity purification was done using Strep-Tactin beads (IBA GmbH). Bound proteins were eluted with freshly prepared D-biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The sample proteins were reduced with 5 mM TCEP (Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine), alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide, and digested with sequencing grade modified trypsin (Promega). Before LC–MS analysis, the samples were desalted with C18 reversed-phase microspin columns (The Nest Group).

LC–MS analysis was done with a Q Exactive ESI-quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer coupled to an EASY-nLC 1000 nanoflow LC using the Xcalibur version 3.1.66.10 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each sample, two technical replicates were loaded into a C18-packed precolumn (Acclaim PepMap™100, 75 μm × 2 cm, 3 μm, 100 Å, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated using a C18-packed analytical column (Acclaim PepMap™RSLC, 75 μm × 15 cm, 2 μm, 100 Å, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a 60-min linear gradient from 5 to 35% of buffer B [98% acetonitrile (ACN) and 0.1% formic acid] at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The mass spectrometry analysis was performed in data-dependent acquisition in positive-ion mode. MS spectra were acquired from m/z 200 to m/z 2000 with a resolution of 70,000, with a full automatic gain control (AGC) target value of 1,000,000 ions and a maximal injection time of 100 ms, in profile mode. The ten most abundant ions of which charge states were 2+ to 7+ were selected for subsequent fragmentation [higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD)] and MS/MS spectra were acquired with a resolution of 17,500 with AGC target value of 5000, a maximal injection time of 100 ms, and the lowest mass fixed at m/z 120, in centroid mode. Dynamic exclusion duration was 30 s.

Database analysis

SEQUEST search algorithm in Proteome Discoverer software version 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for peak extraction and protein identification with the human reference proteome of UniProtKB database (www.uniprot.org, Uniprot human 05_2020). Allowed error tolerances were 15 ppm and 0.05 Da for the precursor and fragment ions, respectively. Database searches were limited to fully tryptic peptides allowing two missed cleavage and carbamidomethylation of cysteine residue was set as fixed, and oxidation of methionine and biotinylation of lysine and N-termini as variable modifications.

Identification of the high-confidence interactions (HCIs) and interactors (HCIPs)

In order to identify specific high-confidence interactions from our mass-spectrometry data, we utilized the Significance Analysis of INTeractome (SAINT)-express version 3.6.034,46 and the Contaminant Repository for Affinity Purification (CRAPome, http://www.crapome.org/)47 as statistical tools. GFP control runs were used as a control count for each hit, and the final results were based on proteins with a SAINT score of 0.73 or higher. This corresponds to a protein-level Bayesian FDR of less than 0.05. Additionally, we employed the CRAPome database, setting a cutoff frequency of 30% or higher and requiring an average spectral count fold change of at least 3 for assigning high-confidence interactions (HCIs).

Network visualisation and GO analysis

Protein–protein interaction data was visualized using Dotplot and generated via ProHits-viz online tool (https://prohits-viz.org/). GO analysis were carried out using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov) using MF (molecular function)_DIRECT and CC (cellular component)_DIRECT annotation in the Functional annotation tool and p-values < 0.05 for enriched GO terms by a modified Fisher exact test.

Antibodies and dyes

Anti HA tag (#26183, ThermoFisher), anti tensin-2 (#11990, Cell signaling technology), anti tensin-2 (#ab25182, Abcam), anti tensin-2 pY483 (OASG07109, Aviva systems biology), anti active beta-1 integrin (clone 9EG7, BD Pharmingen), anti GAPDH (#60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti paxillin (610051, BD Biosciences). Alexa Fluor phalloidin-568 (Life technologies). Alexa fluor secondary antibodies against mouse, rat and rabbit were used (Life technologies).

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

To validate the interaction pairs by Co‐IP, HEK293 cells (5 × 105 per well) in 6‐well plate were co‐transfected with MAC‐tagged (500 ng) bait and V5‐tagged (500 ng) GAPDH constructs using Fugene 6 transfection reagent (Promega). After 24 h of transfection, cells were rinsed with ice‐cold PBS and lysed with 1 ml HENN lysis buffer per well (50 mM HEPES pH8.0, 5 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaF, 0.5% IGEPAL, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 1.5 mM Na3VO4, 1 × Protease inhibitor cocktail) on ice. The cell lysate was collected, and a clear supernatant was obtained by centrifugation (16,000g, 20 min, 4 °C). 50 µl of lysate was collected for the use as input. 30 µl of Strep‐Tactin® Sepharose® resin (50% suspension, IBA Lifesciences GmbH) was washed in a microcentrifuge tube twice with 200 µl HENN lysis buffer (4000g, 1 min, 4 °C). The clear lysate was added to the pre‐washed Strep‐Tactin beads and incubated for 1 h on a rotation wheel at 4 °C. After incubation, the beads were collected by centrifugation and washed three times with 1 ml HENN lysis buffer (4000g, 30 s, 4 °C). After the last wash, 60 µl of 2 × Laemmli sample buffer (Bio‐Rad, 1610737) was added directly to the beads and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were later used for immunodetection via dot‐blot.

Dot‐Blot

The Bio‐Dot® Microfiltration System (Bio‐Rad, 1703938) was assembled according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The nitrocellulose membrane was pre‐washed with TBS to hydrate the membrane. 10 µl of sample was spotted onto the nitrocellulose membrane in the center of the well and drained under vacuum pressure. Nonspecific sites were blocked with 5% fat‐free milk in TBS‐T (0.05% Tween‐20 in TBS) for 60 min at RT with gentle shaking. The membrane was then incubated with primary antibody (mouse anti-V5, Thermo fisher #R960-25) in TBS‐T with a1:5,000 dilution overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was washed three times for 10 min with TBS‐T followed by incubation with secondary antibody conjugated with HRP (goat anti‐mouse/HRP, Agilent Dako #P0447) with a 1:2000 dilution for 60 min at RT with gentle shaking. The membrane was washed three times for 10 min with TBS‐T followed by one additional wash with TBS on a shaker. Amersham™ ECL™ Prime (Cytiva) solution was added to the membrane and incubated for 5 min prior to imaging the blot using iBright Imaging Systems (Thermo Fisher). The same membrane was then stripped by incubating with Restore Plus Stripping buffer (Thermo Fisher) for 15 min and was re‐blocked with 5% fat‐free milk in TBS‐T for 60 min at RT with gentle shaking. The membrane was then incubated with the another primary antibody (rabbit anti‐HA, Cell Signaling Technology #5017) in TBS‐T with a 1:2000 dilution overnight at 4 °C for different detections followed by respective secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit/HRP, Agilent Dako #P0448) with a 1:2000 dilution for 60 min at RT with gentle shaking followed by above mention chemiluminescent detection.

Immunofluorescence and confocal imaging

Flp-In 293 T-REx or MEF cells cells were plated on coverslips coated with 10 µg/ml human fibronectin and incubated overnight. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilised and immunostained using standard protocol. Cells were mounted with prolong diamond (w/wo DAPI) (ThermoFisher). Samples were imaged with Zeiss Axio Observer.Z1 inverted microscope (Zeiss LSM800, Oberkochen, Germany) using Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.40, WD 0.19 mm oil immersion objective.

Time-lapse imaging

Cells were plated on well-plates coated with 10 µg/ml fibronectin and incubated overnight in the cell culture incubator. Next day media was changed prior to imaging. The time-lapse images were captured with EVOS FL auto microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in + 37 and 5% CO2. Cells were kept in the microscope incubator for 90 min prior to imaging. Images were analysed manually using ImageJ (Fiji) and MTrackJ plugin49. Cell shape indicators were assessed with the cell shape descriptors in ImageJ (Fiji).

Seahorse assays

Cells were plated on Fn-coated (15 µg/ml) Seahorse Bioanalyzer XFe24 culture plates (30,000 or 100,000 cells/well for Flp-In 293 T-REx or MEF cells, respectively) in culture media and cultured overnight. Next day the assay was executed as instructed in the manual of Glycolysis stress test (Agilent, kit 103020). Briefly, one hour prior the measurements cells were changed into glucose-free media (Seahorse XF base medium with 2 mM glutamine) and incubated in non-CO2 incubator with normal oxygen and humidity. Basal rate was measured for 30 min after which glucose (10 mM in final volume), oligomycin (Abcam, ab141829) (1 µm in final volume) and 2-deoxyglucose (Abcam ab142242) (50 mM in final volume) were subsequently added. Glycolysis was determined by the maximum ECAR rate before oligomycin injection minus last measurement before glucose injection. When inhibitors were used, these were added to the cells either 1 h prior with jasplakinolide 3 µM (Abcam, ab141409) and FAK14 5 µM (Abcam ab144503) or 24 h prior as with compound C 5 µM (Abcam ab120843), Y27632 5 µM (Abcam, ab120129) and wortmannin 3 µM (Abcam, ab120148).

mRNA sequencing

mRNA was isolated using RNeasy kit (Qiagen). Three independent replicate samples were prepared and the sequencing and the analysis was performed by Novogene (Novogene Co Ltd, Cambridge, UK). Raw data (raw reads) of fastq format were firstly processed through in-house perl scripts. In this step, clean data (clean reads) were obtained by removing reads containing adapter, reads containing ploy-N and low quality reads from raw data. At the same time, Q20, Q30 and GC content the clean data were calculated. All the downstream analyses were based on the clean data with high quality. Reference genome and gene model annotation files were downloaded from genome website directly. Index of the reference genome was built using Hisat2 v2.0.5 and paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 v2.0.5. We selected Hisat2 as the mapping tool for that Hisat2 can generate a database of splice junctions based on the gene model annotation file and thus a better mapping result than other non-splice mapping tools. The mapped reads of each sample were assembled by StringTie (v1.3.3b)48 in a reference-based approach. StringTie uses a novel network flow algorithm as well as an optional de novo assembly step to assemble and quantitate full-length transcripts representing multiple splice variants for each gene locus.

Quantification of gene expression level

FeatureCounts v1.5.0-p3 was used to count the reads numbers mapped to each gene. And then FPKM of each gene was calculated based on the length of the gene and reads count mapped to this gene. FPKM, expected number of Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript sequence per Millions base pairs sequenced, considers the effect of sequencing depth and gene length for the reads count at the same time, and is currently the most commonly used method for estimating gene expression levels.

Differential expression analysis of two conditions/groups (two biological replicates per condition) was performed using the DESeq2R package (1.20.0). DESeq2 provide statistical routines for determining differential expression in digital gene expression data using a model based on the negative binomial distribution. The resulting P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg's approach for controlling the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05 found by DESeq2 were assigned as differentially expressed. Significantly differential gene expression threshold was log2-FoldChange ≥ 1 and padj ≤ 0.05.

Western blotting

Equal amounts of cells were plated on well-plates and incubated overnight. Next day, cells were collected to 2xLaemmli SDS sample buffer. Replicate gels were made from the same sample with 400,000 cells /lane loaded into Any kD precast protein gel (BIO-RAD). After SDS–PAGE and blotting onto polyvinylidene fluoride membrane, membranes were immunostained with appropriate antibodies. Appropriate secondary antibodies (LI-COR) and a LI-COR imaging system were used to detect bound primary antibodies.

GAPDH activity assay

To determine if overexpression of tensin-2 variants influence GAPDH activity, lysates of Flp-In 293 T-REx cells stably expressing tensin-2 variants were assayed to measure the GAPDH activity. The Glyceraldehyde 3 Phosphate Dehydrogenase Activity Assay Kit (Colorimetric) (ab204732) was utilized according to manufacturers instructions.
