‘A kiss that took my legs away’: a rare presentation of Epstein–Barr virus in the older population
Authors: Hussain Mahmood, Marwah Kiani, Yasser Madani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present the case of a 70-year-old woman presenting with nausea, diarrhoea and a generalised rash. Initial blood tests revealed obstructive deranged liver function tests and low haemoglobin. A haemolysis screen revealed raised reticulocytes, low haptoglobin and a positive direct antiglobulin test. 6 days into her admission, she developed lower limb weakness and loss of sensation. MRI spine showed no significant findings. Cerebrospinal fluid showed raised white blood cell count and raised protein. Nerve conduction studies were normal. The clinical picture was in keeping with transverse myelitis. Autoimmune and viral screens were negative except for a single result which provided the unifying diagnosis: Epstein–Barr virus (EBV). She responded to high dose intravenous corticosteroids and her rehabilitation is ongoing. EBV should be considered even in the older population.KEYWORDS: infectious diseases, Epstein–Barr virus, transverse myelitis, infectious mononucleosis, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia

Introduction

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is a double-stranded DNA virus that belongs to the herpes virus family. EBV often results in infectious mononucleosis (IM). It largely infects patients during childhood and adolescence, presenting with non-specific symptoms. The classic triad of symptoms includes fever, pharyngitis and cervical lymphadenopathy.1 EBV is found in saliva, and it is transmitted through coughing, sharing of food and kissing, and thus it is often termed the ‘kissing disease’.2

Additional clinical features may include splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, fatigue, headache and rash. EBV causes a benign, self-limiting illness in the majority of healthy patients. Rarer manifestations of EBV may involve the central nervous system, such as demyelinating disease, encephalitis, meningitis or myelitis.3

Case presentation

A 70-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with nausea, diarrhoea and an itchy rash on her thighs and lower abdomen. The rash started 5 days after her initial abdominal symptoms, and subsequently resolved on admission. Prior to admission she reported no sore throat, fever or lymphadenopathy.

Her past medical history included hypercholesterolaemia and a previous cholecystectomy. She reported no unusual food intake, no contact with animals, no foreign travel and no rural travel. She was independent in her activities of daily living and was able to walk up to 5 miles unaided prior to admission. She had never smoked, denied illicit drug use and had minimal alcohol intake.

Clinical examination revealed intermittent low-grade pyrexia, jaundice and no abdominal tenderness; in particular, she had no right upper quadrant tenderness.

Blood tests on admission showed significantly raised bilirubin, ALP and ALT, suggesting an obstructive process. C-reactive protein was mildly elevated and full blood count was normal. Subsequent blood tests revealed a new normocytic anaemia with a drop in haemoglobin from 115 g/L to 93 g/L between days 1 and 2 of admission, with no evidence of bleeding. In addition, the blood tests showed a declining serum sodium, with a drop in serum sodium from 132 mmol/L to 128 mmol/L between days 1 and 4 of admission.

A haemolysis screen revealed a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) screen by IgG, raised reticulocytes, and low haptoglobin. There was no evidence of a lymphoproliferative process or any structural cause of jaundice on CT abdomen and pelvis and a subsequent magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography scan. The haematology team advised that this is likely to be an autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA).

Seven days into her admission, the patient developed lower limb weakness, paraesthesia and loss of sensation of the lower limbs up to the mid to upper abdomen. She reported preceding urinary urgency and frequency and bilateral lower limb paraesthesia for 2 days prior to this, which was progressively limiting her mobility. She later developed frank urinary and faecal incontinence.

Clinical examination revealed normal power and sensation in the upper limbs, except for reduced grip strength bilaterally. In the lower limbs, hip flexion was reduced bilaterally, knee flexion was reduced only on the right side and plantarflexion and dorsiflexion were preserved. There was reduction of pinprick sensation in the lower limbs, up to the level of T7-8, and in the hands bilaterally. There was hyperreflexia of the upper limbs, absent reflexes in the lower limbs, including absent flexor plantar reflexes bilaterally. Cranial nerves were intact. On balance, and given the rest of the clinical features and history, the neurology team felt that the examination findings were consistent with spinal cord involvement.

Thus, there was a high clinical suspicion of transverse myelitis. Other differential diagnoses considered were post-infective polyradicular neuropathy (such as Guillain–Barré syndrome) and cauda equina syndrome. A CT head was normal and MRI whole spine showed cervical spondylosis and mild cervical canal stenosis. Nerve conduction studies and electromyography of the upper and lower limbs were normal. A lumbar puncture was performed following the MRI, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) results revealed raised protein and white blood cell count. The CSF findings demonstrated mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, which can be observed in myelitis. Viral CSF polymerase chain reaction was negative, including for EBV.

Extensive autoimmune and infection screens were requested, including neuronal antibodies, HIV, CMV, Borrelia, VZV, HSV 1 and 2, enterovirus, parechovirus, leptospira and hepatitis screen. Among these investigations, the only positive result was a positive EBV IgM antibody consistent with acute EBV infection. The EBV viral load was reported as 39,871 copies/ml.

The neurology team felt that her presentation and investigations were in keeping with transverse myelitis. She was treated with five doses of intravenous (IV) methylprednisolone from day 11 of admission onwards. She was treated with one dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in view of a reduction in forced vital capacity (FVC) <2.0 L, but this was subsequently stopped due to low clinical suspicion of upper cord involvement. Subsequent FVC recordings improved and then remained stable. The patient's neurological symptoms significantly improved following treatment with IV methylprednisolone. A repeat MRI whole spine revealed no new findings.

The case was discussed with the infectious diseases consultant, who referred the case to the local virologist; they both felt that the patient's presentation was due to EBV. They did not believe antiviral therapy was indicated, especially given her clinical improvement.

Liver function tests, sodium and haemoglobin normalised prior to discharge. A repeat haemolysis screen was negative, suggesting that the acute haemolytic process had halted. A repeat EBV IgM remained positive, but the EBV viral load had reduced significantly to 4,273 copies/ml. The patient began to safely mobilise with input from physiotherapy and at the time of writing, she was still undergoing further rehabilitation.

Discussion

EBV is a common infection affecting 90% of the global population. Primary infection usually occurs in childhood; those affected at this age are usually asymptomatic. When a primary EBV infection affects individuals in adolescence or adulthood it causes IM; its incidence peaks at age 17 and by age 40, cases are very rare.4 The patient in this case report was 70 years of age and had evidence of a primary EBV infection which, in itself, is a rare presentation.

The colloquial name of IM is ‘kissing disease’ and the moniker was first coined due to the viral transmission of EBV through salivary contact in students in the 1920s.5 This is the most common mode of transmission, but there is also evidence of sexual transmission; however, levels of EBV are much higher in saliva than in genital secretions.6 Other rarer forms of transmission include organ transplantation and blood transfusion.6 The patient in this case report had no recent history of organ transplantation or blood transfusions. This suggests that she may have been exposed to EBV through salivary contact.

The patient did not have the typical features of sore throat, fever or lymphadenopathy associated with IM. She was jaundiced, which only 5% of all patients with EBV present with.6 In adults, the incidence is slightly higher, with 9% of patients presenting with jaundice.6 Beyond the positive serology for EBV, the patient showed evidence of haemolytic anaemia in her blood tests. The haematology team felt that this could represent a cold type of AIHA, which is a rare manifestation of IM.7

AIHA is a rare complication of EBV, occurring in only 0.1–3% of patients with EBV.8 The pathogenesis of EBV-related AIHA is not known, but is suspected to be related to either failure of erythropoiesis due to EBV suppression of bone marrow, pure red blood cell aplasia or antibodies against EBV cross-reacting with antigens on the red blood cell membrane.8

This case report suggests that an initial EBV infection caused transverse myelitis. This has been confirmed by existing literature as a rare presentation of EBV.4 The incidence of neurological complications of EBV have been reported as 0.37–7.3%.9 In this case the initial symptoms of EBV resolved spontaneously. But when the patient developed neurological sequelae, treatment that was administered included corticosteroids and IVIG, but no antiviral medication.

Regarding treatment for EBV itself, there are some data showing that antiviral therapy has not been found to be efficacious,10 but treatment with agents such as corticosteroids have been shown to improve symptoms and treat hemophagocytic complications.11 In vitro studies on patients who are immunocompromised have shown that treatment with anti-viral medication for IM caused by EBV did not result in clinical improvement.12

The data on treatment for central nervous system (CNS) manifestations of EBV, however, have shown some evidence in favour of ganciclovir,13 particularly in immunocompromised patients, with some reports also suggesting evidence for using IVIG alongside this.14 Unfortunately, ganciclovir is associated with severe toxicity and there is reluctance to use it for this reason.15

Overwhelmingly, treatment for transverse myelitis regardless of whether it is secondary to EBV, or unknown causes, often involves the use of pulsed methylprednisolone.16, 17 There is also some research that supports the use of acyclovir alongside methylprednisolone for severe manifestations of EBV including CNS involvement.18

In a study on the effect of immunosuppressive agents and IVIG on acute transverse myelitis, there was no beneficial outcome in 12/16 patients. However, in the four patients who showed clinical improvement, all of them received IV corticosteroids initially. Half of these patients then received IVIG, and only then were improvements in symptoms noted.19 The author suggests there may be an immune-mediated pathogenic mechanism for transverse myelitis. This conclusion has also been supported by a paper which suggests that the neurological manifestations of EBV are a result of an immune reaction following primary infection from EBV.20

In this case report, the relationship between EBV and transverse myelitis has been explored; however, this is not the only neurological complication of EBV.

There are case reports linking primary EBV with GBS of varying severity. Although most patients had mild symptoms not necessitating any treatment, some patients developed respiratory failure and quadriplegia, which was treated with IVIG and plasma exchange.21, 22 A recent case report where a patient died of EBV encephalitis suggested that the pathogenesis of this disease was inflammation secondary to an immune response, rather than the virus itself.23 Indeed, patients with EBV encephalitis have always been treated with IVIG and corticosteroids.24, 25

Conclusion

This case report not only highlights a rare presentation of EBV in terms of age, but also various rare complications of the condition itself, including AIHA, jaundice and transverse myelitis. In elderly patients who present with deranged liver function tests and anaemia with no clear precipitating illness, we suggest testing for EBV. If an elderly patient presents with muscle weakness, altered sensation in limbs, bowel or bladder disturbances or any neurological symptoms, it is important to consider EBV in the differential diagnosis. It is also important to arrange spinal and cranial imaging to ensure neurological manifestations of EBV are not missed.

The role of antiviral medication in treating EBV has not been well supported in the literature, however, with severe manifestations of the disease or in patients who are immunocompromised, the role of acyclovir and ganciclovir has received some support and needs to be explored further.

In patients with EBV who develop neurological symptoms, it is likely due to an immune reaction following the primary infection. The role of IV methylprednisolone has been well established for transverse myelitis and encephalitis, whereas IVIG has been used as treatment for GBS. The pathogenesis of GBS, transverse myelitis and encephalitis may have some overlap in patients with EBV, which could affect treatment choices. The role of IVIG and IV methylprednisolone need to be researched further as a potential treatment for neurological manifestations of EBV.

Although in our case we cannot comment with complete certainty on what exactly led to the resolution of neurological symptoms, given that corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment in such cases, her clinical improvement may have been due to this.

This case represents several rare manifestations of EBV as the patient presented with atypical symptoms and was outside of the typical demographic of patients with primary EBV infection. This case serves as an illustration that EBV should not be forgotten as a differential diagnosis in the older population presenting with complicated primary infection in the form of neurological symptoms.
