Lower motor neuron facial palsy unmasking a rare brainstem stroke: a case report of medial Pontomedullary junction infarction
Authors: Aws Murad Frhood, Ahmed Abdulhussain Shahatta, Sajjad Ghanim Al-Badri, Abdulrahman Khaldoon Hamid, Muntadher Yousif Hasan Al Gehadi, Nabeel Al-Fatlawi, Riyam mudheher nabbat AL-Ajrash, Ali Saad Al-Shammari, Zaryab Bacha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedial Pontomedullary Junction (MPMJ) infarction is an uncommon cerebrovascular event with distinctive clinical presentations that often mimic benign conditions, such as Bell’s palsy, complicating early diagnosis. This case report describes a 43-year-old female presenting with acute facial droop, dysarthria, dysphagia, and sensory deficits suggestive of Bell’s palsy. Despite an unremarkable initial CT scan, advanced imaging with MRI revealed a left MPMJ infarction, highlighting the critical role of diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) in detecting brainstem lesions. The clinical features of lower motor neuron facial palsy, contralateral sensory deficits, and dysphagia distinguish MPMJ infarction from peripheral facial nerve disorders. Risk factors such as poorly controlled hypertension and diabetes likely contributed to arterial compromise in this patient. This report highlights the need for detailed neurological assessments and advanced imaging to accurately diagnose and manage atypical brainstem strokes, reducing severe morbidity risks.

Introduction

Medial pontomedullary junction (MPMJ) infarction is a rare cerebrovascular event characterized by its unique clinical presentations, including peripheral facial palsy, sensory deficits, and dysphagia. The condition arises from ischemia at the pontomedullary junction, a critical area of the brainstem that houses pathways and nuclei responsible for vital motor and sensory functions. While strokes in the posterior circulation, including the brainstem and cerebellum, are less common than those in the anterior circulation, their clinical impact is profound, often involving complex syndromes such as Foville and Millard-Gubler syndromes [1]. MPMJ infarction differs in its distinct constellation of symptoms, such as ipsilateral facial weakness and contralateral sensory impairments, which often mimic other conditions, including Bell’s palsy and peripheral nerve dysfunctions [2].

The rarity of MPMJ infarction complicates its diagnosis, as initial imaging can sometimes miss brainstem lesions, and symptoms may overlap with more benign conditions like Bell’s palsy [3]. Despite these challenges, timely recognition is essential to prevent misdiagnosis and enable appropriate management strategies. The condition often results from arterial occlusions, such as those of the anterior inferior cerebellar artery or paramedian perforating branches of the basilar artery, and can lead to significant morbidity if left untreated [4].

This report underscores the diagnostic complexity of MPMJ infarction by presenting a case where the infarction masqueraded as severe Bell’s palsy. Through this case, we aim to highlight the importance of clinical vigilance, the role of advanced imaging techniques, and the need for a thorough neurological examination in differentiating brainstem strokes from other conditions.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old female with a history of poorly controlled hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), and migraine headaches presented with a chief complaint of facial droop. Her current medications included Valsartan 160 mg daily for hypertension, insulin and sitagliptin for diabetes management, and metoprolol tartrate 50 mg twice daily for migraine prophylaxis, with NSAIDs used as needed for acute migraine episodes. The patient was a non-smoker with no history of alcohol consumption and had a BMI of 27 kg/m2. Three hours prior to the presentation, she noticed her face ‘drooping to the right,’ accompanied by a left-sided headache, right-sided facial and body numbness, and mild dysarthria. On initial evaluation, her vital signs revealed a blood pressure of 185/100 mmHg and her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was 15/15.

General examination demonstrated that the patient was alert and oriented. Cranial nerve examination showed normal function of cranial nerves I-VI and VIII-XII, while cranial nerve VII (facial nerve) showed complete paralysis of the left upper and lower face, resembling severe Bell’s palsy. The patient demonstrated a positive Bell’s phenomenon (upward deviation of the eye when attempting to close the eyelid) but was unable to achieve complete eye closure on the affected side. Also, no nystagmus was observed.

In motor examination, upper limbs demonstrated normal power (5/5), tone, and reflexes bilaterally, also lower limbs demonstrated normal power (5/5), tone, and reflexes bilaterally.

Additionally, sensory examination observed a decreased sensation of pinprick and cold temperature over the right side of the face and right arm. Cerebellar examination observed negative cerebellar signs with no evidence of dysdiadochokinesia, dysmetria, or ataxia.

The patient also exhibited dysarthria and significant dysphagia, with a failed swallow response characterized by severe weakness, uncoordinated movement, delayed initiation, and profoundly reduced laryngeal elevation.

In the emergency department, her random blood sugar (RBS) was elevated at 250 mg/dL.

A non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain was unremarkable. Subsequent laboratory investigations revealed an HbA1c of 8%, indicating poor glycemic control, while the lipid profile, complete blood count (CBC), liver function tests (LFT), and renal function tests (RFT) were all within normal limits. Further serological tests, including a vasculitic screen, were negative. Echocardiography revealed no abnormalities, and computed tomography angiography (CTA) showed normal vasculature. The patient was admitted to the stroke unit with a presumptive diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke despite the presence of lower motor facial palsy.

Axial diffusion-weighted MRI (DWI) showing a hyperintense lesion at the left medial pontomedullary junction, indicative of ischemic infarction. This lesion correlates with the clinical presentation of lower motor neuron facial palsy, contralateral sensory deficits, dysarthria, and dysphagia, characteristic of medial pontomedullary junction infarction.

The patient was classified as having a minor stroke and received dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin 300 mg and clopidogrel 300 mg for the initial 2 days. Atorvastatin 80 mg daily was initiated for secondary stroke prevention. Antihypertensive medications were temporarily withheld for 7 days as per acute stroke protocol. Diabetes management continued with insulin therapy under close monitoring. Physiotherapy was commenced early to address swallowing difficulties and facial weakness. The patient showed significant improvement over the following month. Her swallowing ability markedly improved, and the paresthesia resolved. Most notably, she demonstrated excellent recovery from her facial paralysis. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was planned to further evaluate the cerebrovascular anatomy, but the patient refused this investigation. The patient was followed up in the stroke clinic for ongoing secondary prevention monitoring. Given her HbA1c of 8%, she was referred to the diabetology team for optimization of her diabetes management, with a target HbA1c of less than 7%. Her antidiabetic medications were adjusted accordingly during this consultation.

Discussion

Medial pontomedullary junction (MPMJ) infarction is a rare cerebrovascular stroke marked by atypical clinical manifestations. It can mimic other conditions, such as Bell palsy. The patient’s presentation with lower motor neuron facial palsy, sensory deficits, dysarthria, and dysphagia is characteristic of brainstem involvement, particularly the pontomedullary junction [1].

The initial diagnosis for isolated facial weakness is Bell palsy; however, the presence of contralateral sensory deficits, dysarthria, and dysphagia pointed towards the MPMJ infarction. These additional features distinguish MPMJ infarction from Bell palsy, which only involves ipsilateral facial weakness [2].

MRI diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) has been superior to non-contrast CT imaging [5]. Non-contrast CT was used initially and it failed to detect brainstem ischemic lesions, which shows the limitation of this technique. However, the DWI revealed the precise location of this infarction and showed hyperintensity at the left lower medial pontomedullary junction, but it sometimes shows a false negative within 24 hours after the presentation. This was demonstrated in 139 stroke patients by Oppenheim et al. [4], which showed that 5.8% of patients had negative MRI results within the first 24 hours after presentation. Repeating MRI may help detect infarction that was negative initially.

The pathophysiology of this syndrome results from occlusion of the anterior inferior cerebellar artery or paramedian branches of the basilar artery. The patient’s medical condition with diabetes mellitus and hypertension increased the risk of stroke and vasculature compromise [6].

Other diseases that come with similar symptoms include:

1- Brainstem tumors: They present with similar symptoms including facial palsy, sensory deficits, and cranial nerve paralysis. However, the acute onset of symptoms of MPMJ infarction makes the tumor less likely.

2- Multiple sclerosis: It presents with similar symptoms but is differentiated by the presence of other neurological symptoms and less acute onset of symptoms.

3- Bell palsy: It presents with fewer neurological symptoms.

Management of MPMJ is supportive and focuses on the management of risk factors such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension. Also, acute patients require intensive care management for complications like respiratory failure, and long-term management may involve rehabilitation for facial motor and sensory deficits. However, patients are prohibited from taking thrombolysis due to delayed presentation of symptoms [8].

This case demonstrates the complexity of brainstem strokes and shows the importance of keeping in mind the atypical symptoms associated with them. It also shows the importance of advanced imaging like MRI and emphasizes the need for comprehensive clinical assessments [9].

Future research should focus on a larger cohort of MPMJ infarctions. It should also enhance the diagnostic criteria and algorithm for rare diseases like MPMJ infarction to differentiate the peripheral from central facial nerve disorders and include a detailed investigation of vascular pathways.

Conclusion

This case report highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by medial pontomedullary junction (MPMJ) infarction, a rare cerebrovascular event that often mimics benign conditions like Bell’s palsy. The patient’s presentation, characterized by lower motor neuron facial palsy, contralateral sensory deficits, dysarthria, and dysphagia, underscores the importance of recognizing atypical stroke syndromes and the need for advanced imaging techniques like diffusion-weighted MRI (DWI) for accurate diagnosis. Early identification is critical to guide appropriate management and mitigate the risk of significant morbidity associated with brainstem strokes. This case emphasizes the necessity of thorough clinical assessment, vigilant consideration of stroke mimics, and tailored diagnostic approaches to enhance outcomes in rare cerebrovascular conditions. Future research should focus on refining diagnostic criteria, understanding the pathophysiology of rare infarction sites, and developing targeted management strategies to address their unique challenges.
