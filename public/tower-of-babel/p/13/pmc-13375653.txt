Spatial mapping and senolytic targeting of senescent and disease-associated microglia in aged mouse brain white matter
Authors: Chase M Carver, Paul T Gomez, Sonia L Rodriguez, James T Redden, Amanda S Latham, Yooree Ha, Jennifer M Kachergus, Yi Liu, Ji Shi, Tommy Tran, Liguo Wang, Simon Melov, E A Thompson, Marissa J Schafer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain white matter undergoes structural and functional alterations linked to late-life cognitive decline, but the cellular and molecular basis of its selective vulnerability remains incompletely defined. Here, in naturally aged mice, we demonstrate that senescent and disease-associated microglia (DAM) phenotypes converge in hippocampal-adjacent white matter, particularly in the fimbria. Using regional gene expression profiling, immunolabeling, GeoMx digital spatial profiling and CosMx spatial molecular imaging, we identify an aged brain-exclusive microglial population concentrated in white matter that expresses DAM genes together with a ‘SenBrain’ senescence gene signature, including galectin-3 (GAL3/Lgals3). Single-cell spatial trajectory analyses suggest that multiple cell fate transitions may give rise to this aged, proinflammatory, senescent- and DAM-linked state. Pharmacogenetic or pharmacological senotherapeutic interventions reduced white matter GAL3+ DAM abundance and restored a more youthful microglial organization in aged fimbria. These findings identify a senescence- and DAM-enriched microglial state as a prominent and partially reversible feature of aged brain white matter.Subject terms: Microglia, Ageing, Cellular neuroscience

Main

Aging increases the risk of cognitive decline and neurodegenerative conditions, yet the cascades of cellular and molecular events that confer region-specific brain dysfunction in aging remain unclear. The hippocampal formation is an age-vulnerable mediator of learning and memory, comprising a cortical gray matter infolding directly abutted by dense white matter tracts, the corpus callosum and fimbria-fornix. As core components of the Papez circuit, the hippocampus and fimbria-fornix transmit signals to the hypothalamus, thalamus and cingulate gyrus, supporting memory- and emotion-related functions1. Imaging studies reveal that white matter dysfunction and, particularly, changes in the fimbria-fornix may precede cortical gray matter dysfunction, representing an early corticolimbic impairment underlying memory dysfunction in the progression of cognitive decline2–5.

Cell-specific molecular mediators of aging are potential intervention opportunities for preserving brain health and preventing neuropathogenesis in aging. Inflammatory and senescent fates have emerged as maladaptive cellular trajectories in aging tissues. In the aged and neurodegenerative brain, DAM accumulate and exhibit downregulation of homeostatic genes alongside upregulation of neuroinflammatory, lysosomal stress and neurodegeneration-related genes6–9. The APOE-TREM2 pathway represents a critical regulator of DAM-like microglia, which are thought to initially confer protective functions before shifting to a deleterious fate, including inflammatory disruption of brain microenvironments8,10–14. White matter microglia fulfill overlapping and distinct roles relative to microglia in gray matter, as defined by interactions with densely myelinated fiber tracts and neurons, respectively. Single-cell RNA sequencing identified white matter-associated microglia (WAM) as a TREM2-dependent population that increases with aging15. This study and others collectively demonstrated that WAM are enriched for molecular signatures related to lysosomal function, phagocytosis, iron regulation and lipid metabolism that do not clearly separate from DAM15–18. Thus, we ascribe the white matter designation as an anatomical characterization and use the broader DAM designation to refer to the continuous DAM/WAM molecular profile.

Characterizations of DAM from aged and Alzheimer’s disease brains have revealed overlapping phenotypes with senescence19–22. Senescent DAM exhibit gene expression profiles that include cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (Cdkns; Cdkn2a/p16ink4a, Cdkn1a/p21Cip1), chemoattractant factors (Ccl2-5 and Spp1), pro-survival factors (Bcl2), canonical DAM factors (Apoe and Itgax) and lysosomal stress factors (Lgals3). GAL3, encoded by Lgals3, binds to β-galactosides and β-galactosidase on stressed lysosomes23–25 and is an established microglial factor that interacts with neuroinflammatory receptors, contributes to phagocytic remodeling and is linked to maladaptation and disease states26–31. Senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity is a widely used lysosomal stress senescence biomarker32, including in senescent DAM that express CLEC7A21. We propose GAL3/Lgals3 is a senescent DAM biomarker that requires further characterization by spatial mapping15,20.

Emerging spatial -omic technologies permit high-dimensional mapping of cellular and molecular states with preserved anatomical tissue context. These platforms overcome isolation artifacts and population biases inherent to dissociative single-cell methods and facilitate classification of rare cell populations, unique phenotypes and cell–cell communication. Importantly, two recent studies of aged brain cell signatures analyzed by spatial transcriptomics identified white matter as distinctly altered in aging33,34.

Building on our previous work typifying aged microglia in single-cell workflows20, we sought to investigate senescent and DAM microglial identities in age-vulnerable corticolimbic gray and white matter in naturally aged mice. Through use of three spatial mapping platforms (imaging mass cytometry (IMC), GeoMx digital spatial profiling (DSP) and CosMx spatial molecular imaging (SMI)) together with gold-standard gene expression and imaging techniques, we mapped senescence and DAM cell features, which exhibited pronounced accumulation in aged limbic white matter. We delineate a white matter-enriched microglial population that accumulates with age and is characterized by the highest expression of the SenBrain gene signature, with particular enrichment of senescence-associated-secretory phenotype (SASP), organelle stress and cell surface domains. Bioinformatic trajectory inference demonstrates multiple possible transition states involving age-increased microglial populations also harboring molecular senescence and DAM biomarkers that terminate in the old age-exclusive inflammatory SASP-enriched population. We applied systemic pharmacogenetic and pharmacological strategies to target senescent cells, which reduced aged white matter senescence and DAM markers and restored microglial morphological and organizational properties in the aged fimbria to a more youthful state.

Results

Expression of DAM and senescence genes, including p16ink4a and Lgals3, increase in aged brain white matter

We previously demonstrated that the aged female hippocampal region harbors increased DAM and senescence markers20. We investigated senescence and microglial gene expression patterns in microdissected fimbria-fornix and corpus callosum white matter relative to hippocampal gray matter of aged female and male mice (Fig. 1). We distinguish between detection of Cdkn2a (referring to the broader p19arf and p16ink4a locus) and p16ink4a alone. p16ink4a (Fig. 1a), Cdkn2a (Fig. 1b), Lgals3 (Fig. 1c), Cdkn1a/p21 (Fig. 1d) and Ccl2 (Fig. 1e) expression were significantly higher in fimbria-fornix and corpus callosum compared with hippocampus. In aged female mice, Ccl5 (Fig. 1f), Apoe (Fig. 1g) and Gpr34 (Fig. 1h) expression were significantly higher in fimbria-fornix and corpus callosum compared with hippocampus. Relative to young counterparts, p16ink4a, Cdkn2a, Csf1r, Gpr34 and Trem2 expression increased in both gray and white matter microdissections of old male and female mice (Fig. 1i,j). Apoe, Bcl2a1a, Cd11b, Klk8, Lgals3, Cdkn1a/p21 and Tmem173 expression increased in old female and male fimbria-fornix and corpus callosum. Ccl2, Ccl5, Il1b and Tyrobp increased in old male fimbria-fornix and corpus callosum (Fig. 1j). In aged female and male mice, senescence and DAM gene expression was more pronounced in white matter relative to hippocampal gray matter. At the regional level, we observed age-dependent increases in genes such as p16ink4a, Cdkn2a, Lgals3, Apoe, Bcl2a1a, Cd11b, Csf1r, Klk8, Tmem173, Trem2 and Tyrobp more consistently in old fimbria-fornix and corpus callosum (Fig. 1k) than in the hippocampus or cerebellum, emphasizing that white matter is susceptible to late-life changes in senescence and DAM transcriptional signatures.

GAL3+ myeloid cells accumulate in the hippocampal-adjacent white matter of old mice

Based on the observation that senescent DAM cells express Lgals3/GAL320,35, we investigated the abundance of GAL3+ immunoreactive cells across the hippocampus and adjacent white matter in young and old brain sections (Extended Data Fig. 1). GAL3+ cells were rare in young mouse brains. In aged brains, we observed GAL3+ cells in multiple limbic regions (Extended Data Fig. 1a) with dense localization in white matter tracts, especially the fimbria (Extended Data Fig. 1a). In aged fimbria, GAL3 signal colocalized with the myeloid/microglial marker IBA1 but not with the astrocyte marker GFAP (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). GAL3 immunoreactivity comprised a larger area of old versus young female fimbria; however, GAL3 area was not significantly different in fimbria between old and young male mice (Extended Data Fig. 1d). In the old female fimbria, IBA1+GAL3+ cells were larger relative to IBA1+GAL3− cells, (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f). PCA of IBA1+GAL3+ and IBA1+GAL3− populations from old mouse fimbria revealed size-specific principal components based on cellular morphology and distinguished by GAL3+ immunoreactivity (Extended Data Fig. 1g). Similar to the fimbria, anterior commissure and corpus callosum white matter tracts exhibited strong GAL3+ immunoreactivity (Extended Data Fig. 1h,i). IBA1 and GAL3 also colocalized in deep cerebellar nuclei (Extended Data Fig. 1j). Thus, immunofluorescent imaging and gene expression demonstrate increased abundance of the senescent and DAM marker GAL3/Lgals3 in aged white matter, including the fimbria, which was more pronounced in aged female mice.

IMC demonstrates microglia positive for senescent and DAM markers are abundant in the aged fimbria

To investigate the molecular phenotypes of aged microglia across microenvironments, we implemented IMC, which combines immunolabeling and time-of-flight mass spectrometry to map and quantify ten protein markers with 1-µm spatial resolution in the aged female mouse brain (Fig. 2). We measured the abundance and colocalization of senescent and DAM markers (GAL3, TMEM173, MHCII, uPAR, dPP4/CD26, phospho-p38MAPK and CD38) with IBA1 and CX3CR1 as myeloid markers and CD45 as a pan-immune marker (Fig. 2a). Fimbria myeloid cells exhibited greater intensity of IBA1, GAL3, CD38, uPAR, dPP4 and CD45, relative to hippocampal myeloid cells, which exhibited greater CX3CR1 protein levels (Fig. 2b). Stratification of GAL3+ and GAL3− cells in the fimbria showed that GAL3+ cells harbored trending or significantly higher levels of senescence-related markers uPAR and CD38 (Fig. 2c). These protein-based results validate and extend our observations from regional gene expression and immunofluorescent analyses, demonstrating that the aged fimbria accumulates senescent and DAM markers, including GAL3 and uPAR.

GeoMx DSP confirms enrichment of conserved DAM and expanded gene signatures in aged fimbria white matter

We next examined microglial transcriptional signatures specific to the hippocampus and fimbria of old female mice using GeoMx DSP. We segmented the hippocampus and fimbria regions with positive selection based on GAL3, IBA1 and SYTO83 labels and negative selection by GFAP (Fig. 3a). First, we compared IBA1+ populations in the hippocampus versus fimbria (Fig. 3b). Established senescence and DAM genes Fth1, Ctss and Apoe and candidate senescence-related genes, Ccno and Sesn3, were enriched in IBA1+ cells of the fimbria. Adam3, Cdc20b, Ciao3, Efcab8, Enkd1 and Ttc21b were enriched in hippocampal IBA1+ cells. We next compared transcriptional profiles of IBA1+GAL3+ and IBA1+GAL3− microglia specifically in aged fimbria (Fig. 3c, d). Apoe was the most abundantly expressed gene in IBA1+GAL3+ cells, relative to IBA1+GAL3− cells.

CosMx SMI reveals diverse aged microglial identities, including white matter-concentrated DAM- and senescence-linked states

We used CosMx SMI to define microglial transcriptional signatures at single-cell resolution in the hippocampus, corpus callosum, fimbria, cortex, dorsal thalamus and choroid plexus of aged female mice (Fig. 4). We developed a 50-gene custom panel of aging- and senescence-associated genes based on a two-step process of (1) selection of genes from our and others’ published senescence profiles20,36–38 and (2) confirmation of expression in aged brain single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptome datasets20,33,39,40. We used the custom panel combined with the 950-plex CosMx mouse neuroscience panel, enabling investigation of 102 senescence-related genes that we term the ‘SenBrain’ panel, which we analyzed alongside brain function, cell-typing and control probes (see the Methods for the SenBrain description; Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

We initially assayed CosMx on five brain sections from four aged female mice (for one aged mouse, two technical replicates were included) and identified 2,620 total microglial cells, which clustered into nine populations (Fig. 4a,b and Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Microglia were identified based on expression of canonical markers, including Csf1r, Ctss, Cx3cr1, Hexb, Selplg, Itgam, P2ry12, Tmem119 and Trem241,42. We excluded nonmicroglial immune cells (185 cells), which separated clearly from microglia based on uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction, gene expression profiles and spatial location (Supplementary Fig. 1a–d). We detected immune cells in the choroid plexus and brain parenchyma with shared molecular signatures (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Although rare in tissue sections compared to microglia, immune cells expressed Bsg, Crip1, Cd74, Cd63, H2-Aa and H2-Ab1, with low or no expression of microglia-specific markers (Supplementary Fig. 1c–e).

All microglial populations comprised cells from both white and gray matter anatomical regions; however, clusters 1, 2, 4, 7 and 9 were proportionally enriched in microglia localized to fimbria and corpus callosum white matter regions (Fig. 4c,d and Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). An inherent artifact of current single-cell spatial platforms and segmentation algorithms is low-level detection of transcripts from adjacent or interacting cells. By exploiting this signal as a variant of molecular ‘contact-tracing’43, we observed that white matter-localized microglia were enriched for oligodendrocyte transcripts Mbp, Mobp and Plp1, whereas gray matter-localized microglia were distinctly enriched for neuronal transcripts Camk2a, Adcy1, Sncb and Snap25, indicating close proximity to, or potentially prior engulfment of these cells (Fig. 4e). Distinct astrocytic markers were enriched in gray (for example, Aldoc) and white (for example, Gfap) matter-localized microglia (Fig. 4e).

Analysis of top differentially expressed genes revealed distinct and shared signatures across the nine microglia clusters (Fig.4f). Clusters 3, 5, 6 and 8 were characterized by low expression of Apoe and Fth1 and moderate-to-high expression of Cx3cr1, Hexb, P2ry12, Selplg and Tmem119, suggestive of homeostatic populations (Fig. 4f and Extended Data Fig. 2e). Clusters 2 and 6 demonstrated elevated immune regulatory genes (for example, B2m, C1qa, Cst3 and Tmem173), while retaining high expression of homeostatic genes. However, DAM-related transcripts Apoe, B2m, Cd63, Clec7a, Csf1, Cst7, Ctsb, Ctsd, Fth1, Ftl1, Lyz1/2, Spp1, Trem2 and Tyrobp were higher in clusters 1, 2 and 7 (Fig. 4g and Extended Data Fig. 2e).

Across pooled Lgals3+ microglia and among Lgals3+ microglia from each individual brain, DAM- and senescence-associated genes Apoe, B2m, Clec7a, Ctsb, Ctsd, Cd9, Cd63, Csf1, Fth1, Ftl1, Gpnmb, Itgax, Lilrb4a/b, Lyz1/2, Lpl, Mmp12 and Spp1 were co-expressed (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 2f–i). Microglial cluster 7 harbored the greatest frequency of Lgals3+ cells (Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 2j) and demonstrated greater frequency and/or higher intensity expression of Apoe, Axl, Ccl3–4, Cd63, Clec7a, Csf1, Ctsb, Ctsd, Fth1, Ftl1, Gpnmb, Itgax, Lgals3, Lilrb4a/b, Lpl, Mmp12, Plaur, Spp1 and Tyrobp (Extended Data Fig. 2e,k). Lgals3+ cells were also present in clusters that expressed Mbp and Mobp (Extended Data Fig. 2l). Among all aged microglia, we observed that the expression profiles across distinct and total Cdkn+ microglia were heterogenous (Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, we detected low-level expression of multiple Cdkns across most individual microglial populations; cluster 8 harbored the highest expression (Extended Data Fig. 2j).

To profile transcripts from neighboring and/or interacting cells, we devised canonical gene set modules of distinct brain cell types (Supplementary Table 2) using previously published data39,42 and interrogated the ‘contact-tracing modules’ and enrichment for SenBrain and DAM signatures in clusters (Fig. 4j–k). We detected enrichment for immune cell and choroid plexus related gene modules in cluster 1, 7 and 9, oligodendrocyte module in clusters 1 and 4, neuronal module in cluster 3 and astrocyte and vascular modules in cluster 9 (Fig. 4k). We identified the highest enrichment of the SenBrain gene module in aged microglial cluster 7 and lowest enrichment of the SenBrain module in cluster 3 (Fig. 4j–l), suggesting that senescence genes were least prevalent in neuron-adjacent microglia.

We parsed the SenBrain panel into senescence domains (Supplementary Table 2) and investigated the scores and percentage of cells enriched for individual modules44. The white matter-enriched cluster 7 demonstrated pronounced frequency and expression enrichment across all investigated senescence domains, including cell cycle regulation, nuclear stress, SASP, senescent cell anti-apoptotic pathway (SCAP), organelle stress and cell surface markers (Fig. 4m). This corresponded to comparatively elevated expression of specific senescence-related genes, including Cdkn2d/p19ink4d (cell cycle regulation); Cenpb, Hmgb1, Pak2 and Smad2 (nuclear stress); Ccl3–4, Csf1, Mmp12 and Spp1 (SASP); Akt1 and Bcl2l1 (SCAP); Cyba, Lgals3, Lgals3bp and Lpl (organelle stress); and Axl, Cd9, Gpnmb and Plaur (cell surface markers) (Fig. 4g). Cluster 2, also proportionally concentrated in white matter, was characterized by the second highest SenBrain score and demonstrated enrichment for cell cycle, nuclear stress, SASP, organelle stress and cell surface domains (Fig. 4m). Collectively, CosMx delineated diverse microglial populations defined by molecular and anatomical features, including a distinct white matter-enriched population characterized by DAM and senescence gene expression.

Young versus old CosMx spatial maps reveal age-altered, white matter-enriched microglia

We next leveraged CosMx SMI to explore age-dependent microglial states in an independent dataset of corticolimbic sections from four old 24-month-old versus four young 4-month-old female mice (Figs. 5 and 6). We first separated microglia from nonmicroglial immune cells (Supplementary Fig. 1f). We generated a UMAP of 316 nonmicroglial immune cells and observed that young and old samples heterogeneously contributed to three distinct clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Gradient expression of Ttr, a choroid plexus-enriched marker, broadly defined cells more likely to be choroid plexus localized (clusters 1 and 2) versus infiltrated parenchymal cells (cluster 3) (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Parenchymal immune cells expressed Aldoc, Apod, Cpe, C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, Csf1r, Fth1, Gfap, Hmgb1, Malat1, Mbp, Mobp, Ptn, Slc1a2, Sparcl1 and Tmsb4x, whereas Ttr-high immune cells were enriched for markers expressed in choroid plexus epithelium, including Atp1b1, Bsg, Cab39l, Chchd10, Cox4i1, Htr2c, Ndufa4 and Psap45,46 (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Relative to young immune cells, higher levels of astrocyte (Gfap), oligodendrocyte (Plp1, Mbp and Mobp), and microglia (C1qa, C1qb and Ctss) markers were detected in old immune cells, suggesting proximity to brain-resident glia (Supplementary Fig. 3d).

We next interrogated anatomical composition by annotating microglia as either gray matter (cortex, hippocampus or thalamus) or white matter (corpus callosum or fimbria) localized (Fig. 5g,h). In the aged brains, clusters 2, 3, 9, 10 and 15 were white matter-enriched. Clusters 1, 4, 7 and 11–14 had 70% or greater gray matter resident microglia in both young and old mice. Consistent with these anatomical characterizations, Mbp and Mobp oligodendrocyte markers were detected in white matter-majority clusters 2, 3, 9, 10 and 15, and Snap25 and Syt1 were detected in clusters 4, 7 and 14 (Extended Data Fig. 4g,h). We further annotated microglial anatomical locations within the caudate putamen, cingulate cortex, corpus callosum, cortex, dentate gyrus, fimbria, hippocampus and dorsal thalamus (Fig. 5i and Extended Data Fig. 4i). Cluster 7 and cluster 12 were primarily localized in the dorsal thalamus. Cluster 2, 3, 10 and 15 were identified in similar proportions in the corpus callosum and fimbria. Clusters 5, 6 and 8 had a greater proportion of corpus callosum-localized microglia, whereas cluster 9 had a greater proportion of fimbria-localized microglia (Extended Data Fig. 4i). Regional siloing confirmed an expansion of age-related microglial clusters 3, 10 and 15 in white matter, with aged brain-exclusive cluster 10 displaying the most pronounced, reproducible DAM gene expression profile.

To further investigate microenvironment characteristics, we implemented the contact-tracing gene set modules initially described in Fig. 4 (Fig. 5j and Supplementary Fig. 5). Microglial clusters 2, 3, 9, 10 and 15 exhibited higher oligodendrocyte module scores, whereas clusters 4, 7 and 14 exhibited higher neuron module scores, corroborating the white and gray matter-majority anatomical localizations (Fig. 5j, Extended Data Fig. 4c–i and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Clusters 1 and 13 were enriched for the microglia cell-type module, suggestive of homeostatic populations, whereas clusters 3, 10 and 13 were enriched for the DAM module (Fig. 5j). Cluster 10 was characterized by the highest score and percentage of cells for the DAM module and was positive for the immune cell module, which likely reflects an overlapping inflammatory profile and/or proximity to peripheral immune cells (Fig. 5j). Based on spatial proximity, aged microglia were closer to the nearest-neighbor immune cell than young microglia (Supplementary Fig. 3e,f), potentially indicating changes in microglial and immune cell abundance in the aged brain and increased interactions, as suggested by GSEA analysis of aged-increased cluster 10 (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Cluster 13 was proportionally greater in the aged brain and demonstrated both DAM and homeostatic characteristics. Cluster 3 was more abundant in the aged brain and demonstrated DAM and immune enrichment. Cluster 15 was enriched for an ependymocyte module, suggesting proximity to ventricular borders (Fig. 5j and Supplementary Fig. 5d).

We next modeled fate trajectories that may give rise to age-increased microglial populations within the aged (n = 4) versus young (n = 4) dataset (Figs. 5 and 6). We examined SenBrain gene expression gradients that may indicate a trajectory from homeostatic microglia (cluster 1) toward the age-exclusive cluster 10 (Fig. 6e). Across clusters 1, 13, 3 and 10, we observed gradient increases in the percentage of cells and average expression for several senescence and DAM-associated markers, including Apoe, Axl, C4bi, Cd9, Clec7a, Ctsb, Lgals3 and Tyrobp. We applied Monocle3 trajectory inference48, excluding peripheral clusters (2, 6, 11 and 12, as defined by the UMAP) that were unreachable from the main core partition (Fig. 6f). Our initial model included homeostatic cluster 1 as the pseudotime root and proceeded to cluster 13, following to 3, which then branched into 9, 10 or 15 → 8 terminal populations (Fig. 6g(i)). This 1 → 13 → 3 → 10 trajectory tracked with the age-increased transcript gradients described above (Fig. 6e) and was characterized by proportional decreases in young cell abundance (Fig. 5e(ii)). To explore alternative trajectories resulting in the age-increased populations, we interrogated a retrograde model with terminal cluster 10 as the pseudotime root (Fig. 6g(ii)). Inferred retrograde trajectories branched from cluster 10 to 3, bearing both DAM and SenBrain-enriched genes, then branched toward three trajectories: (1) ependymocyte-, astrocyte- and oligodendrocyte-associated cluster 15 and astrocyte-associated cluster 8, (2) choroid plexus and oligodendrocyte-associated cluster 9 and (3) DAM- and homeostatic-microglia-associated cluster 13 with continued branching toward neuron-associated populations, including homeostatic cluster 1 (Fig. 6g(ii)). Oriented from cluster 10 as the root, pseudotime values were inversely correlated with SenBrain and DAM module scores, and the greatest pseudotime distance from cluster 10 was represented in terminal gray matter populations (Supplementary Fig. 6). Based on trajectory branching of subpopulations, we determined that the retrograde-inferred astrocyte-, ependymocyte- and oligodendrocyte-associated microglial trajectory involving population 15 (8 → 15 → 3 → 10) was the most direct route to cluster 10 and involved the fewest branch points, whereas the gray matter-enriched homeostatic microglial trajectory (1 → 13 → 3 → 10) contained more branch points (Fig. 6g(ii)). Critically, gene expression gradients and the pseudotime trajectory modeling suggest that microglia harboring senescent and DAM molecular characteristics (including Lgals3 expression) are not a single static population. Age-increased populations 3 and 15 are possible precursors to the age-exclusive senescent DAM population 10 (Fig. 6e–g). The models suggest that senescent DAM cells may emerge along multiple possible molecular trajectories, probably resultant of anatomically defined microenvironment priming that ultimately confers common age-selective transcriptional signatures.

Senotherapeutics revert age-altered GAL3+ and APOE+ cell abundance and organization in the fimbria

We next assessed how senotherapeutics influence microglia positive for DAM and senescence markers in the aged fimbria. The average size of aged IBA1+GAL3− cells (46.8 ± 2.0 μm2) was similar to IBA1+ cells from young mice (51.1 ± 2.7 μm2), whereas aged IBA1+GAL3+ cell size was 109.6 ± 15.4 μm2 (Extended Data Fig. 1f), suggesting that the age-dependent increase in IBA1+ cell size in fimbria partially corresponds to IBA1+GAL3+ microglia. In old control female mice, GAL3+ cells occupied greater fimbria area compared with young (Fig. 8f). Accordingly, we tested whether senotherapeutics alter age-increased IBA1+GAL3+ signals (Fig. 8a,f,h). AP20187 or venetoclax reduced the area and frequency of IBA1+GAL3+ cells in aged female fimbria (Fig. 8f,g). AP20187 did not significantly alter GAL3 area in old male fimbria compared to control (Extended Data Fig. 8c). GAL3 fluorescence intensity of remaining IBA1+GAL3+ cells from AP20187-treated female mice was lower compared with old controls (Fig. 8h). Although venetoclax decreased Lgals3 transcript (Fig. 7c) and reduced the total number of IBA1+GAL3+ cells in white matter (Fig. 8g), we did not detect differences in GAL3 fluorescence intensity of remaining IBA1+GAL3+ cells (Extended Data Fig. 8d,e). This collectively suggests distinct effector populations in venetoclax-mediated BCL2 versus AP20187-mediated p16 targeting converge on GAL3+ microglia.

Based on the aged fimbria GeoMx and CosMx profiles demonstrating pronounced Apoe expression in GAL3/Lgals3+ microglia, we performed immunostaining for APOE, GAL3 and IBA1 (Fig. 8i). We observed high APOE+ cell density in the fimbria and corpus callosum (Extended Data Fig. 9a), as well as colocalization of APOE and GAL3 (Fig. 8i). APOE+ cells were seldom found in the hippocampus (Extended Data Fig. 9a). Old control mice had significantly greater APOE immunofluorescence intensity than young mice, and AP20187 and venetoclax both reduced APOE signal (Fig. 8j and Extended Data Fig. 9b). AP20187 also reduced the percentage of APOE cells colocalized with IBA1 (Fig. 8k). Both senotherapeutics reduced the percentage of APOE+ cells colocalized with GAL3 (Fig. 8l). Interestingly, we discovered a distinct architectural pattern in white matter, characterized by localization of APOE+ cells to the lateral ventricle in old mice (Extended Data Fig. 9a). We quantified the relative distance of APOE+ cells from the fimbria midline in two dimensions (Fig. 8m). AP20187- or venetoclax-treated mice exhibited more dispersed APOE+ cell organization characterized by trends of decreased distance from the midline, relative to old control mice (Fig. 8i,m and Extended Data Fig. 9c–f). Collectively, the senotherapeutics reverted microglial morphological and molecular properties in the aged fimbria to more youthful states.

Discussion

White matter integrity is a key determinant of neuronal activity and cognitive function54,55. In mice and humans, white matter tracts are selectively vulnerable in aging5,34,56,57, and maladaptive, microglia-mediated neuroinflammation is emerging as an underlying driver13–15,33–35,58. We applied imaging and gene expression analyses with spatial molecular profiling to map cell-type specific morphological, anatomical and molecular features of microglia in the aged mouse brain, with a specific focus on senescent signatures. We generated an atlas of young and aged microglia, revealing an old-age-exclusive population enriched for SenBrain and DAM signatures. This DAM senotype (senescent phenotype) is concentrated in white matter but may originate through distinct fate trajectories in white or gray matter regions. Our results also implicate GAL3/Lgals3 as a key senescence- and DAM-linked biomarker of aged white matter and show that systemic senescent cell targeting partially restores aged white matter microglial identity and organization toward more youthful states.

Building on our earlier work20, we implemented three spatial mapping platforms enabling characterization of distinct and conserved microglial features in aged hippocampal gray and white matter, while delineating strengths and constraints of each technology. Each platform informs on anatomical region-defined molecular changes and cell-type interactions, while avoiding information loss or artifacts associated with single cell or nucleus dissociation protocols and retaining all cell types in their endogenous molecular states.

IMC enabled validation of the immunofluorescent imaging observation that GAL3+ microglia are more abundant in fimbria white matter, relative to hippocampal gray matter. We also showed that the fimbria harbored higher levels of senescence-related proteins, including uPAR, dPP4 and CD38, which were enriched in GAL3+ microglia. Although signal overlap is minimal for IMC, the limited resolution and reliance on customized antibody panels can confer variable performance across experiments. Nevertheless, IMC enabled multiplexed quantitation of senescence-related GAL3+ microglia in aged fimbria. This includes discovery that uPAR, a cell surface senescence marker under immunotherapeutic development59, is highly expressed in aged white matter GAL3+ cells, highlighting a potential alternative targeting strategy.

We used a whole-transcriptome mapping strategy, GeoMx DSP, to interrogate IBA1+ microglial gene signatures in the fimbria and hippocampus. We discovered conserved senescence- and DAM-associated genes (Fth1, Apoe and Ctss) and emergent genes (Ccno and Sesn3) were enriched in aged fimbria microglia. Apoe strongly distinguished GAL3+ versus GAL3− microglia in the fimbria. These experiments underscore the utility of GeoMx for expression signature discovery; however, GeoMx DSP remains limited for molecularly phenotyping rare or heterogenous cell populations.

Using CosMx SMI, we mapped single-cell microglial spatial-transcriptional profiles in two separate datasets corresponding to four 24-month-old brains in our initial testing, followed by an independent set of four 24-month-old versus four 4-month-old brains, revealing that integration of single-cell molecular and anatomical information enables clear demarcation of distinct populations, including those that are concentrated in white versus gray matter. Current spatial mapping molecular markers and segmentation algorithms are imperfect, resulting in detection of ‘molecular shadows’, corresponding to low-level transcript localizations of cell identity markers that probably originate from adjacent cells rather than the segmented cell. We exploited this methodological artifact as a way of classifying spatial neighborhood characteristics of cell populations, which were confirmed with anatomical registration. Senescence and DAM biomarkers were identifiable across aged microglia subclusters and were most pronounced in a subpopulation that was distinctly present in old brains. Similar to the previously described continuous nature of DAM and WAM states15, this population (cluster 10) was identifiable in white and gray matter but was concentrated in white matter. Age-exclusive microglial cluster 10 expressed canonical senescence and DAM markers. In our analysis of 24- and 4-month-old female mice, senescence-associated transcriptional programs appear integrated with the broader DAM signature. At older ages or in neurodegenerative models, senescent programs may be further amplified, revealing specific senescent DAM subsets, such as the Ccl4+ senescent population identified within DAM microglia in a mouse model of tauopathy22. Defining distinct age- and pathology-specific senotypes is an important direction for future work, as well as validation of signatures with larger sample sizes for the spatial platforms.

Our expression profiling and immunofluorescent imaging demonstrate that systemic p16 targeting reduces senescence and DAM markers in aged white matter. Additional validation of p16-InkAttac targeting with larger sample sizes are needed to identify the full efficacy of aged phenotype reversal. Cdkn2a was lowly detected across microglial CosMx populations; however, Cdkn2a CosMx probes include p16ink4a and p19arf exons, and this probe-based method does not involve signal amplification to improve detection of lowly abundant transcripts. Thus, additional approaches are needed to conclusively map p16-postitive microglial identities and impacts in the aging brain. Of note, Cdkn2c/p18ink4c, which can coordinate cell cycle arrest through interactions with CDK4 or CDK660, was lowly detected in the cluster 10 senescent DAM population. BCL2 targeting also reduced DAM markers but not Cdkn2a/p16ink4a expression, and Bcl2 was detected in microglial cluster 13, which was proportionally enriched in the aged brain. Contextualized with trajectory analyses, senolytic strategies may target cells tracking toward the terminal age-increased dominant SenBrain-high senotype. It is also likely that senotherapeutics target other senescent brain and peripheral cell types, which could impact microglia through local or circulatory interactions. Related to this notion, the fimbria is spatially adjoined by choroid plexus and the lateral ventricle, heightening its exposure to systemic circulating factors and infiltrating immune cells45,61,62. We posit that the anatomical location of the fimbria may underlie both its vulnerability in aging and its responsiveness to senotherapeutic intervention.

Structural and pharmacokinetic parameters of the drugs tested here suggest that venetoclax may more directly affect the brain, relative to AP2018751, and yet, we discovered that both senotherapies influenced aged fimbria molecular and cellular fates through distinct and overlapping profiles. Young fimbria microglia were mildly responsive to both drugs, which corresponded to low detection of Cdkn2a and Bcl2 in the young brain and emphasizing that senolytics can have effects that limit their application in young organisms. Furthermore, it is unclear how senotherapeutics variably penetrate aged brain regions to act on parenchymal cells versus how the drugs modulate circulating and/or barrier cells to indirectly influence parenchymal brain cells. Future approaches to consider are cell-specific interventions coupled with microglia-depletion studies to determine if directly targeting senescent microglia alters white matter integrity in aging52.

Microglial trajectory inference indicated multiple possible scenarios may give rise to the age-exclusive, Lgals3/GAL3-expressing, white matter-enriched, SASP-high DAM senotype: (1) homeostatic microglia may migrate to white or gray matter niches and obtain a stress-induced DAM phenotype, followed by activation of additional senescence pathways with continued stress exposure, (2) persistent stress in anatomical regions may contribute to progressive changes in resident microglia or (3) white matter resident and ventricular-bordering microglia, in communication with infiltrating immune and barrier cells, may transduce peripherally-derived signals from blood and CSF to activate DAM and senescence pathways. The prominence of each scenario may correspond to subregion characteristics. For example, fimbria microglia may be proportionally more impacted by scenario three, relative to gray matter regions. Additional spatially resolved lineage mapping and cell–cell communication analyses are needed to validate lineage inferences and the context-dependent signaling networks that drive cell fate trajectories in brain aging and disease, as well as the adaptive versus maladaptive functions of distinct age-increased microglial populations.

Ongoing research is beginning to clarify important functional consequences of intersecting DAM and senescent microglial states. Studies in demyelination and lysosomal storage models have shown that microglial phagocytosis of myelin and other lipid-rich debris can overload the endosomal–lysosomal system, triggering lysosomal membrane permeabilization and GAL3 accumulation63, supporting a model in which an overburdened lysosomal system may shift DAM from an adaptive program into a progressively senescent and maladaptive state. Senescent cells characteristically display increased lysosomal mass, altered lysosomal pH and lysosomal damage, marked by galectin-puncta32,64. Aged-increased white matter microglial Lgals3/GAL3 signal may reflect chronic phagocytic load and cumulative lysosomal stress associated with persistent myelin and debris clearance in the aging brain. Considering other functional aspects, a recent transgenic lineage tracing study examined DAM-expressing Spp165, a SASP factor in our DAM senotype. Using stroke injury, they showed that Spp1-expressing DAM in juvenile brains are irreversible, whereas neonatal DAM are comparatively plastic. A separate study demonstrated that microglia from 24-month-old mice harboring DAM/WAM and senescent markers, including Lgals3 and Spp1, are resistant to microglial depletion and are associated with recruitment of CD8+ T cells, myelin perturbation and axon degeneration14. Consistent with these findings, we previously showed that the SASP of p16+ senescent microglia chemoattracts peripheral immune cells in vitro, and systemic senescence targeting reduces DAM-like microglia and infiltrating adaptive immune cells in the aged brain while preserving cognitive function20. Furthermore, a recent study investigating senescent microglia in focal demyelination demonstrated that senescent-like myeloid cells within spinal cord lesions upregulate Lgals3, Spp1 and other senescence- and DAM-associated markers, which are pronounced in aged mice, and senotherapy improved remyelination in adult but not old mice58. Thus, further defining the temporal windows, regional niches, molecular trajectories and signaling pathways through which senescent DAM states arise and either resolve or persist and influence neighboring cells are important next steps.

Through use of established and emerging imaging and molecular methods, we demonstrate senescence and DAM signatures are pronounced in aged white matter microglia, which may underlie neuropathological cascades eventuating in cognitive decline. Our results demonstrate that systemic BCL2 or p16 targeting is an effective strategy to counter DAM- and senescence-linked profiles in aged white matter and revert microglial molecular and cellular organization to a more youthful state.

Methods

This research complies with all ethical regulations, research practices and experimental protocols by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and guidelines for ethical animal research were followed from the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Animals

Mouse experiments were performed under protocols approved by Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Heterozygous p16-InkAttac male and female mice (C57BL/6J background) were used in this study, and sex-specific features of age were analyzed independently. Mice were group-housed in ventilated cages with a constant temperature of 25 °C, 30–70% humidity and 12-hour light–dark cycle. Water and the 5053 PicoLab Rodent Diet 20 were provided ad libitum. For gene expression and treatment studies, young mice were 3–4 months of age and old mice were 16–20 months; a subset of mice analyzed in this study were included in systemic analysis of senolytic targeting in Carver et al.51. Alternatively, C57BL/6 were acquired from the National Institute of Aging and brains collected at 24–28 months of age for spatial profiling experiments. Mice were euthanized with a lethal dose of pentobarbital and transcardially perfused with ice-cold phosphate-buffered serum (PBS). Mice with pronounced splenomegaly were excluded from study. Left hemisphere brain cortex, cerebellum, hippocampus and hippocampus-adjacent white matter were immediately removed and placed in TRIzol for gene expression analyses. Right hemisphere brain was fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h. Brains used in immunofluorescent imaging were transferred to 10% w/v sucrose in PBS for 24 h, then 20% sucrose for 24 h, then 30% sucrose for 48 h, then cryopreserved in 30% glycerol, 30% ethylene glycol and 40% PBS w/v at −80 °C. Brains used for IMC and spatial-omics profiling were removed from 4% PFA to cold 70% ethanol and embedded within paraffin wax within 24 h for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) processing, cut at 5–6-μm slices with sliding microtome.

Senotherapeutic interventions

For AP20187 drug dilution, a 12.5 mg ml−1 stock solution in 100% ethanol was created and stored at −20 °C. A total of 1 ml AP20187 working solution (2 mg kg−1) was prepared by mixing 40-µl stock solution with 100 µl PEG-400 and 860 µl of 2% Tween-20 in ddH2O. The solution was vortexed and administered to mice by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection within 30 min of diluting the stock. The corresponding vehicle was composed of ethanol, PEG-400 and Tween-20 and was administered to control animals via i.p. injection. For venetoclax drug dilution, a stock oral gavage vehicle was created as a solution of 60% phosphatidylcholine in propylene glycol (Phosal 50 PG, Lipoid GmbH), 30% PEG-400 and 10% ethanol. Venetoclax was vortexed into vehicle solution at room temperature to reach a working solution of 50 mg kg−1 and stored at 4 °C the week of injections. Old p16-InkAttac mice were randomized (based on body weight and age, 16–20 months old) to receive vehicle, AP20187 (2 mg kg−1, i.p. injection) or venetoclax (50 mg kg−1) by oral gavage. All mice received both gavage and i.p. treatments, with drug treatment groups also receiving vehicle by i.p. or by gavage. Young and old p16-InkAttac control mice received the same vehicle dosing strategy. Venetoclax (ABT-199, #A8194) was acquired from APExBio Technology. AP20187 was acquired from WuXi AppTec. All other reagents, unless otherwise denoted, were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich/Millipore. Mice received five consecutive daily administrations for 1 week, 2 weeks off and then another 5-day treatment. Treatments were discontinued 1 week before necropsy. Samples sizes of each experimental group, YNG CON: n = 6 female/5 male; OLD CON: n = 6 female/8 male; OLD AP: n = 5 female/8 male; OLD VEN: n = 5 female/6 male; YNG AP: n = 6 female; YNG VEN: n = 6 female.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) gene expression profiling

Tissue samples collected at necropsy were immediately stored in TRIzol reagent. RNA was isolated using TRIzol-based chloroform-isopropanol precipitation, followed by NanoDrop concentration and purity analysis. For all tissues, 2 µg of total RNA was used for cDNA synthesis through M-MLV reverse transcription (Invitrogen, cat. no. 18091200). Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed on a QuantStudio5 RT-PCR system (Thermo Fisher) with PerfeCTa FastMix II Low ROX (Quantabio) and Taqman PrimeTime qPCR assays from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) (Supplementary Table 3). Genes were normalized to Hprt housekeeper gene expression. Relative gene expression compared with the young control group was derived from the 2−ΔΔCT value for each tissue sample.

Immunofluorescence imaging

Right hemisphere brains were embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT compound and sagittally sliced at 30-μm-section thickness on a Leica CM3050 S cryostat (Leica Biosystems). Free-floating sections were stored in wells containing ice-cold PBS + 0.01% sodium azide. Sections were blocked and permeabilized in 8% donkey serum, 0.1% Triton-X, 0.1% Tween-20 for 2 h at 25 °C. After 5 × 5 min thorough washes in cold PBS, sections were quenched for 10 min with 1× True Black Plus lipofuscin autofluorescence quencher (Biotium, cat. no. 23014), diluted from a 40× stock in PBS. After 5 × 5 min thorough washes in cold PBS, sections were stained with primary antibodies diluted in 8% donkey serum, overnight at 4 °C. We used IBA1 immunoreactivity as a microglia-specific label and spatially restricted selection to brain parenchyma. IBA1 can be expressed by monocytes but only when the cells reach brain compartments and mature66. Antibody combinations consisted of: rabbit polyclonal anti-IBA1 (Fujifilm Wako, #019-19741, RRID:AB_839504) or goat polyclonal anti-IBA1 (Abcam, #ab5076, RRID:AB_2224402), rat IgG2A monoclonal anti-galectin-3 clone eBioM3/38 (Invitrogen, #14-5301-85, RRID:AB_837133), goat polyclonal anti-GFAP (Abcam, #ab53554, RRID:AB_880202) or rabbit polyclonal anti-apolipoprotein E clone EPR19392 (Abcam, #ab183597, RRID:AB_2832971) (Supplementary Table 4.1). The next day, after 3 × 5 min washes with PBS, sections were stained with donkey host secondary antibodies conjugated to fluorophores for 2 h at 25 °C (Jackson Immunoresearch). After 3 × 5 min washes with PBS to remove excess fluorophore, sections were mounted onto Superfrost Plus microscope slides with Vectashield with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, Vector Laboratories, #H-1200) with a 1.5 glass coverslip. Sections were imaged with a Nikon Ti2 Eclipse Inverted microscope with 10×, 20× and 40× Plan Apo objectives, eight-channel Spectra III light engine and using the Orca Fusion BT sCMOS camera with Nikon Elements AR software; 405-, 488-, 594- and 647-nm laser lines were used with an image exposure time of 200 ms per channel in 16-bit readout mode. For each mouse, three to six sections were analyzed for each immunolabel, and three to five mouse brains were collected for each sex and group. ImageJ was used to process fluorescence intensity and colocalization image data. Total corrected cellular fluorescence intensity was calculated from the mean integrated density—(mean local background X cell area). Fluorescence was normalized to controls, scaled per channel and represented by arbitrary units. IBA1+ cell size was calculated as the area of the soma and any connected processes that registered higher than local background. Alignment of microglia to fiber tracts was performed by calculating the feret length, the longest axis of each IBA1+ cell and calculating the most acute angle to the direct X–Y line of the nearest fiber tract of linear DAPI+ nuclei within the same plane, to a maximum of 90°. A microglial phenotyping composite index that captures neuroinflammatory morphologies53 was applied to quantify morphological properties of IBA1+ immunoreactive microglia from young, old and senotherapeutic treated mouse fimbria (Supplementary Table 1). Cell morphology parameters included cell area, convex hull area, density (convex hull area/cell area), cell perimeter, convex hull perimeter, convex hull span ratio, Feret length, oligodendrocyte fiber tract-angle offset and circularity. The DiAna ImageJ plugin tool was used for spatial analyses of cell–cell objects and cell-edge proximities67.

Imaging mass cytometry (IMC)

IMC (Standard BioTools) uses metal isotope-conjugated antibody labeling, laser ablation and time-of-flight mass spectrometry to quantify spatial protein distributions across tissue sections. The right hemispheres of aged female mouse brains (23–24 months) were collected and placed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h, then transferred to 70% ethanol. FFPE sagittal sections (5 µm) were prepared by the Mayo Clinic Pathology Research Core. FFPE slides were baked at 60 °C for 45 min. The slides were deparaffinized by submerging in xylene (three times for 5 min), 100% ethanol (two times for 1 min), 95% ethanol (two times for 1 min each) and 70% ethanol (one time for 1 min). For antigen retrieval, the slides were incubated in 1× Tris/EDTA Epitope Retrieval Solution (Leica Biosystems, #RE7119) at 97 °C for 20 min. This was followed by a 5 min wash with ultrapure water for rehydration. Sections were outlined with a hydrophobic barrier on the slide. The tissues were then incubated in Superblock Blocking Buffer (Thermo Fisher) at 25 °C for 30 min, followed by a wash step with PBS-TB (3 × 5 min). The mass-tagged antibody cocktail (Supplementary Table 4.2) was applied to the sections and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a hydration chamber with slight agitation. Immunofluorescent imaging of free-floating sections was performed to validate the binding of antibodies included in the panel for IMC. After overnight staining, slides were washed with PBS-TB (three times for 5 min each) and stained with an iridium nucleic acid intercalator solution (1:400 in PBS-TB) at 25 °C for 30 min. A wash step with PBS-TB (5 min) was performed, followed by air drying at 25 °C for 20 min. The Helios (Standard BioTools) time-of-flight mass cytometer within the Mayo Clinic Immune Monitoring Core was used to laser-ablate the tissue within pre-selected regions of interest at 1-µm resolution, followed by a plasma-based ionization step. The resulting signals from each metal-conjugated antibody, distinguished by time-of-flight mass cytometer, were acquired by the Hyperion Imaging System (Standard BioTools), reconstructed into an image. Representative images were converted to OME.TIFF files using MCD Viewer software (Standard BioTools). Single microglia cells were isolated and segmented on CX3CR1 and IBA1 combined channels. Values were collected for each channel for each segmentation selection. Average local background was subtracted from the intensity value of each cell (cell intensity – (background X cell area)) to derive corrected total cellular protein levels per antibody label (shown as arbitrary units), and therefore, each cell quantification reflected normalized protein density relative to other labels among the cells.

GeoMx digital spatial profiling (DSP)

GeoMx DSP (Nanostring/Bruker) uses protein-labeled antibody imaging to define and select areas of interest (AOI) for in situ hybridization and probe-based collection for downstream sequencing. The 5-μm sagittal sections were sliced from 28-month female mouse brain FFPE blocks and mounted on Superfrost Plus slides. Slides were baked at 60 °C for 30 min, followed by deparaffinization with xylene (3 × 5 min washes), 100% EtOH (2 × 5 min washes), 95% EtOH (1 × 5 min wash) and PBS (1 × 1 min wash). Slides were steamed for 15 min. at 99 °C in 1× Tris EDTA Epitope Retrieval Solution, pH 9 (Leica, #RE7119-CE) followed by a 5 min wash in PBS. Slides were incubated in 1 μg ml−1 proteinase K for 15 min at 37 °C and washed in PBS for 5 min. A 200-μl mouse whole-transcriptome hybridization solution containing GeoMx RNA probe mix (Nanostring) was applied to each slide and incubated 37 °C overnight in a hybridization chamber. Slides were then incubated in Stringent Wash Solution at 37 °C for 2 × 25 min washes, followed by 2 × 2 min washes in SSC-T solution. Slides were blocked with GeoMx Buffer W for 30 min, quenched with True Black as above and immunostained with the morphology markers anti-IBA1, anti-GAL3 and anti-GFAP, and SYTO83 (Invitrogen, #S11364) as nuclear stain. Secondary antibodies were subsequently added as anti-rabbit AF488, anti-rat AF594 and anti-goat AF640. Slides were washed 2 × 5 min with SSC before processing in GeoMx profiler (n = 5 mice, two to three sections per mouse).

Areas of interest were selected from cells in the hippocampus and fimbria for IBA1+SYTO83+, GAL3+SYTO83+ alone and IBA1+GAL3+SYTO83+ cells, with GFAP as a negative-selection marker for each AOI. Segmentation order was (1) IBA+/GFAP−/GAL3−, (2) GAL3+/IBA1−/GFAPØ, (3) IBA1+/GAL3+/GFAPØ, with (+) indicating include, (−) indicating exclude and (Ø) indicating ignore. Bound oligonucleotides were collected in a 96-well plate with annotation for mouse and section identities, brain structure of origin (hippocampus or fimbria) and AOI group. Primer libraries were created for mouse whole-transcriptome according to Nanostring GeoMx guidelines, and sequencing was performed on the Illumina System 2 × 150 bp, ~350 million PE reads (Azenta Life Technologies). The GeoMx NGS Pipeline was utilized to process FASTQ files and transform them into the digital count conversion (DCC) file format, which can be read by the DSP instrument for subsequent analysis. The pipeline followed several key steps: (1) selection of raw FASTQ files for a specific pipeline run; (2) quality assessment of the selected reads; (3) removal (trimming) of adapters and merging of paired-end reads to obtain high-quality reads; (4) alignment of reads to the readout tag sequence-ID barcodes; (5) removal of PCR duplicates by matching based on the unique molecular index; (6) generation of the DCC file for further analysis, in which each AOI possesses a count value for each gene in the mouse Whole Transcriptome Atlas. Genes of interest for differential comparison were selected with a priori threshold of two standard deviations greater than the geometric mean of the negative probe counts per AOI. A gene was filtered out if the percentage of AOIs surpassing the negative probe threshold was less than 15%. The combined gene list from each labeling condition group was then processed using thrid quartile normalization for differential expression. Differential expression analyses were conducted using the R package DESeq2 (version 1.36.0)68. As an exploratory method, genes were considered statistically significant in differential expression with a P value <0.05. The first variable analyzed for IBA1+ microglia segments was brain region of origin, and comparisons were made between microglia collected from either hippocampus or fimbria. For hippocampus (n = 8 AOIs) versus fimbria (n = 15 AOIs) comparison of IBA1+ segments, each section was individually counted. Genes were tabulated according to fold change differences relative to microglia in fimbria (positive fold change denotes increased expression in fimbria). Second, for fimbria region comparison of IBA1+GAL3+ and IBA1+GAL3− segments, AOIs were pooled per mouse to achieve enough area from the sequencing. Genes were tabulated according to fold change differences relative to IBA1+GAL3+ segments (positive fold change denotes increased expression in IBA1+GAL3+ segment).

CosMx spatial molecular imaging (SMI)

CosMx SMI (Nanostring/Bruker) uses probe-based in situ hybridization, cyclic fluorescent imaging and barcode-based signal decoding to spatially quantify highly multiplexed RNA transcripts in tissue sections. CosMx was performed as previously described69. We developed a 50-gene custom panel of aging- and senescence-associated genes, identified from SenMayo, SenSig and other published senescence gene profiles20,36–38, which we applied in tandem with the 950-plex mouse neuroscience panel for CosMx transcriptional mapping (Supplementary Table 1). In situ hybridization and four-marker antibody labeling (histone, rRNA, DAPI and GFAP) were performed on 5-μm FFPE sagittal sections from 4-month (Figs. 7 and 8) and 24-month (Figs. 4, 7 and 8) female mouse brains. Slides were baked at 60 C for 30 min, and the CosMx Mouse Neuroscience Panel for RNA reagent application protocol was followed as specified in the user manual within the Mayo Clinic Spatial Biology Core. For data acquisition, 500 μm × 500 μm fields of view were collected in a gridded manner across a section of the brain including cortex, hippocampus, thalamus and surrounding white matter. In situ transcript counts were derived from cell segments rendered from the antibody marker labeling. Nanostring AtoMx and the R packages Seurat (4.3.0)70 and Giotto Suite (3.3.0)71 were used for CosMx data analyses (Supplementary Information). Cells with fewer than 20 total transcripts and cell segmentation polygons with area greater than 750 μm2 were excluded from further analyses (Supplementary Fig. 7). Transcript counts per gene were normalized to the total transcripts per cell and scaled. To visualize and delineate subpopulations of cells, highly variable features were derived from high coefficient of Pearson variance and PCA was generated. UMAP plots were then created from the PCA, followed by Leiden clustering across 1,000 iterations. For each brain processed through CosMx, microglia were selected as a dataset based on previously validated markers Csf1r, Ctss, Cx3cr1, Hexb, Selplg, Itgam, P2ry12, Tmem119 and Trem2 41,42. Immune cells including macrophages were delineated out of the microglia cluster by detection of a specific gene matrix including concerted upregulation of genes Cd74, H2-Aa and H2-Ab1 (Supplementary Fig. 1). All microglia were then processed for clustering via Leiden algorithm with resolution set to 0.1 and run through 1,000 iterations. The Gini and Scran methods were used to detect differently expressed gene markers among the subclusters, utilizing two-sided Wilcoxon ranked-sum test72,73. Differentially expressed gene markers were detected for each cluster relative to other microglia clusters. Single-brain normalized expression and cluster metadata were then converted into a Seurat object for integration into a merged object of all samples in that experiment. Scaling, detection of variable features, PCA, nearest-neighbor detection and subclustering with Leiden algorithm were then processed for individual and pooled microglia single-cell datasets. Significantly expressed genes were represented as log2-transformed fold change at an adjusted P value <0.05. Experiments for Fig. 4 were run with one technical replicate section each from four mice, no. 231, no. 232, no. 233 and no. 234 and a second technical replicate section for no. 231. Experiments for Figs. 5 and 6 were run together on the same machine with four biological replicates for young mice no. YF01, no. YF02, no. YF03 and no. YF04 and four biological replicates for old mice no. 236, no. 237, no. 239 and no. 243. Additional data analysis methods are supplied in the Supplementary Information.

SenBrain panel

We developed the SenBrain panel to detect senescence-associated biomarkers and identify senescent brain cells using CosMx SMI. Genes included in SenBrain were hypothesized to be expressed in senescent cells, based on a strong literature foundation. Secondary senescence or genes shown to be involved in the induction or regulation of senescent cells were also considered. As a disclaimer, we encourage analysts using SenBrain or any other senescence gene list to consider a minimum of two to three distinct biomarkers and/or senescence domains as recommended by Suryadevara et al. criteria44. There is no single biomarker for senescence, and any biomarkers in the SenBrain list may in fact be altered in distinct brain contexts not related to senescence. We compiled senescence-associated genes from experimental results and published senescence profiles and confirmed brain-specific expression of each gene in published brain single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptome aging datasets20,33,36–40. For custom probe target design, sequences were confirmed with the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) for Mus musculus. The 950-plex CosMx Mouse Neuroscience panel was previously annotated by the company to include markers of senescence and a subset of these genes were combined with the custom 50-gene probeset to generate the 102 gene SenBrain panel. We partitioned the SenBrain panel into categorical senotype domains: cell cycle regulation, nuclear stress, SASP, SCAP, organelle stress and cell surface markers (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Genes lacking a dominant domain-specific classification based on current senescence literature were not assigned to a domain (that is, Apoe, Lmna, Malat1 and Pten) but are recognized for a role in senescence and maintained in the broader panel, as annotated in Supplementary Table 2. The Seurat package AddModuleScore average expression for SenBrain and/or senotype domains was calculated for each microglial population. Aggregate scores were used to guide examination of the functional senescence domains and key gene biomarkers expressed within each domain. Populations exhibiting high expression of multiple senotype domains and biomarkers, together with enrichment in the aged brain, were considered consistent with SenNet-defined criteria for senescence, enabling senotype classification using molecular and anatomical features.

Harmonized cross-experiment validation of CosMx microglia

To demonstrate consistency and validation of gene signatures across CosMx experiments, we used the R package Harmony v 1.2.347 to combine microglia from the experiment in Fig. 4, dataset 1, and experiment in Figs. 5 and 6, dataset 2 (Extended Data Fig. 7). UMAP dimension reduction analysis and cell clustering resulted in 10 clusters that were similarly distributed between the two batches of datasets. Differential expression analysis identified top genes in each cluster, similar to the previous analyses on each dataset in relationship to previous cluster definitions. SenBrain module scoring identified cluster c6 as highest enrichment and cluster c4 as lowest enrichment. The highly and lowly expressed markers of harmonized datasets from both cluster c6 and Lgals3-enriched microglia were very consistent with single dataset Lgals3+ enriched microglia, senescent/DAM microglia from cluster 7 in dataset 1, and senescent/DAM microglia from cluster 10 in dataset 2 (Figs. 5 and 6). Harmonized cluster c6 also demonstrated relative greatest SASP, organelle stress and cell surface senescence domains.

Statistics and reproducibility

For RT-PCR gene expression comparisons in old mouse brains in Fig. 1a–h, each region was normalized to the relative expression of hippocampus samples. The data were first tested for normality by Shapiro–Wilk test; outliers were pre-excluded from statistical analysis by Grubb’s test and then the data were compared for each sex by unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction and significance denoted at P < 0.05. In comparisons of young and old RT-PCR data in Fig. 1i–k, values were normalized as relative expression to young and compared by unpaired t-tests with FDR multiple comparisons correction, *q < 0.05 versus young tissue. For IMC data, background-subtracted, total cellular protein values were compared between the myeloid cells located in hippocampus and fimbria or between fimbria-localized GAL3− and GAL3+ myeloid cells by unpaired t-tests with FDR multiple comparisons correction. For GeoMx data, differentially expressed genes were detected on P value <0.05 using the DESeq2 Wald test68. For CosMx data, individual brain datasets were normalized and profiled by detection of differentially expressed genes by Gini and Scran methodologies. For pooled brain datasets in the Seurat pipeline, differentially expressed genes were detected by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with significance set at an adjusted P value <0.05. For RT-PCR and immunohistochemistry and morphometric analyses across experimental groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were performed with Dunnet’s test for multiple comparisons. Drug-treated groups were determined to be significantly different than the old vehicle-treated control group at adjusted P value <0.05, with one-way ANOVA test and multiple comparisons correction. Mouse treatment sample sizes were five to eight mice per group. No statistical methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publication20,51. The cell fluorescence intensities for cumulative distribution populations of IBA1+ cells were compared by Kolmogorov–Smirnov test at P < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 9 and R 4.3.0 were used for statistical analyses. For treatment studies, animals were randomized using simple randomization based on body weight and age. Collection order was randomized using simple randomization for age-specific transcriptomic analyses. Data collection and analysis were performed with the experimenter blinded to the condition of the experiment.
