A network analysis study of anxiety, depression and loneliness among middle-aged and elderly people in Xining area
Authors: Bixuan Dong, Bin Li, Xiaowei Fan, Hongru Chen, Zhancui Dang, Ze Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow oxygen levels, low atmospheric pressure, and intense sunlight characterize high-altitude regions. These environmental factors can disrupt the body's biochemical mechanisms and neural functions, which may increase the risk of mental health issues in affected individuals. Depression, anxiety, and loneliness are common mental health conditions. Our study conducted network analysis methods to investigate the relationships among anxiety, depression, and loneliness in middle-aged and older individuals in Xining City, Qinghai Province, China (average altitude: 2,261 m). Additionally, the study conducted a comparative analysis of the network structures to explore gender differences.The objective of this study was to provide theoretical intervention strategies and practical guidelines for the mental health of middle-aged and older individuals in high-altitude regions.MethodThis study utilized convenience sampling to select 673 middle-aged and older individuals from Xining City, Qinghai Province, China (average altitude 2,261 m). The following instruments were utilized in the study: a basic demographic survey questionnaire, the Nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), the Seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7), and the UCLA Loneliness Scale Chinese Simplified Version (ULS-8). R version 4.4.1 was used for the statistical description and network analysis.Descriptive analysis was presented using mean (SD) or number(percentage).We explored the network structure comprising anxiety, depression, and loneliness. It also compared the network structures of male and female samples to explore gender differences.ResultsIn the network, the strongest association was found between GAD2 "Cannot stop/control worrying" and GAD3 "Worrying too much". The core symptoms identified were GAD4 "Trouble relaxing"; PHQ2 "Depressed mood"; and ULS3 "Feeling left out". Bridge symptoms included GAD5 "Being restless"; PHQ8 "Psychomotor retardation or agitation"; and ULS6 "Feeling lonely". A comparison of male and female networks indicated that there was no difference in the global network; however, a significant difference in the global strength was observed. The top three pairs with the strongest associations were: ULS7 "I would like to make friends" —ULS8 "I can find someone to be with me when I want to be"; GAD3 "Worrying too much" —GAD2 "Cannot stop/control worrying"; and PHQ1 "Anhedonia" —PHQ2 "Depressed mood". However, the weight of PHQ1 "Anhedonia" — PHQ2 "Depressed mood" was significantly higher in the female network than in the male network, while the weights of the other two pairs were similar. The core symptoms of the male network were GAD4 "Trouble relaxing"; GAD5 "Being restless" and PHQ2 "Depressed mood". The core symptoms of the female network were identified as: PHQ2 "Depressed mood"; ULS3 "Feeling left out"; and GAD3 "Worrying too much".The PHQ2 "Depressed mood" was identified as a core symptom in both the male and female networks. However, the expected influence index of the PHQ2 "Depressed mood" for the female network was found to be higher than that for the male network.Conclusion"Trouble relaxing", "Depressed mood", "Feeling left out", "Being restless", "Psychomotor retardation or agitation", and "Feeling lonely" could be key targets for the prevention of anxiety, depression, and loneliness in the middle-aged and older population in high-altitude areas.Additionally, there were significant differences in overall intensity between male and female networks, with gender-specific strategies recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03248-0.Keywords: High altitude, Loneliness, Depression, Anxiety, Network analysis

Introduction

The United Nations《World Social Report 2023》found that the global population was aging at a rapid and irreversible rate. Within the next 30 years, the number of people aged 65 and above worldwide will more than double [1]. By 2050, the number of people aged 60 and above will account for more than 40% of the total population, indicating that China will enter a "super-aged" society [2].

Gordon [3] found that the aging trend made individuals more susceptible to mental illness, with depression and anxiety becoming common psychological disorders among older adults worldwide. Additionally, loneliness has been increasingly prevalent in aging societies [4].In 2019, China had the highest incidence of depression and anxiety among the elderly population in aging countries [5]. Anxiety, depression, and loneliness in older adults were associated with an increased risk of suicide [6], dementia [7], cardiovascular diseases [8], and a greater social burden [5]. The comorbidity rate of depression and anxiety ranged from 44 to 60% [9]. Moreover, loneliness and depression were closely linked, with each condition exacerbating the other [10]. Japanese scholar Niimura found [11] that older adults were more susceptible to anxiety due to various forms of loss. Factors such as bereavement, health conditions, and loneliness contribute to a higher risk of depression in this age group. The study identified a clear correlation between social isolation, anxiety, depression, and loneliness among older adults in Japan. This relationship typically follows a pattern: "social isolation → loneliness → anxiety/depression" as well as "anxiety/depression → loneliness."

Anxiety, depression, and loneliness were closely related, and it was difficult to treat one mental illness alone [12]. Previous studies have tackled this issue by identifying the main symptoms of comorbidity for intervention. In recent years, network analysis has been widely applied in the field of psychopathology [13]. Network analysis used mathematical graph theory to measure the significance of each symptom and its connection to other symptoms [14].In network analysis, nodes represented observable variables, while edges illustrated the relationships between these nodes. By calculating centrality indicators, network analysis can identify the most critical symptoms in a network, enabling more accurate and effective treatment [13].Bridge symptoms connected different groups within a network, promoting the spread or interaction of symptoms. Once bridge symptoms of mental disorders were identified, targeted measures can be taken to prevent comorbidity [15]. Accurately describing these interactions was crucial for understanding the psychopathological mechanisms of these disorders and developing targeted intervention strategies [16].Previous studies have highlighted the centrality of various symptoms in mental health networks.Mi found [17] that in the loneliness, anxiety, and depression networks of students, central symptoms such as GAD2 (excessive worrying), PHQ4 (fatigue), and PHQ2 (feeling down) were the most influential symptoms. Additionally, some studies further analyzed and compared the differences in these networks based on gender. In a study of the network structure comprising life satisfaction, loneliness, depression, and anxiety among Japanese individuals, Noda [18] found that the strength values for restlessness and tiredness were significantly higher in the network for males than in the network for females. In the network of depression and anxiety among adolescents [19], the female network exhibited greater connectivity, particularly in GAD6 “irritability” and GAD2 “uncontrollable worry.” Additionally, in the network structure comprising depression, anxiety, and self-efficacy among diabetic patients [20], “PHQ8” (exercise) was a unique bridge symptom in the female group.

Previous research has rarely explored anxiety, depression, or loneliness among individuals living in high-altitude areas. These regions were characterized by low oxygen levels, lower atmospheric pressure, and intense sunlight. Living at high altitudes can impact the body's biochemical mechanisms and neurological functions, making people more prone to mental health issues [21]. Gamboa [22] found that individuals residing at an elevation of 4,300 m have a higher prevalence of depression than those living at sea level; Bian [23] found that people exposed to high altitudes of 3,700 m exhibited significantly higher anxiety scores.

In summary, this study used network analysis to explore the network relationships between symptoms of anxiety, depression, and loneliness among middle-aged and older adults in plateau regions.It also conducted a comparative analysis of the network structures to explore gender differences. This study provided theoretical intervention strategies and practical guidelines for improving mental health among middle-aged and older adults in plateau regions.

Methods

Participants

This study employed a cross-sectional design for quantitative analysis. A convenience sampling method was used to select 673 middle-aged and elderly study participants in Xining City, Qinghai Province, China (Average altitude 2,261 m). The survey was conducted from July to December 2024. Inclusion criteria: (1) age greater than or equal to 45 years old; (2) clear consciousness and able to communicate and read normally; (3) provide patients and their families with informed consent and participate in this study voluntarily. Exclusion criteria: those who had cognitive impairment and could not complete the self-assessment scale.

Procedure

Standardized investigators followed the principle of voluntary participation, ensuring that each participant obtained informed consent before the survey and signed an informed consent form. Subsequently, investigators administered face-to-face interviews, using paper-based questionnaires to collect data. After the survey's conclusion, the researchers conducted a data review on the same day. The questionnaires were checked for logical consistency and completeness. Consequently, A database was created by Epidata. A total of 807 questionnaires were distributed in this study, of which 5 were discarded due to insufficient completeness, 129 were distributed by participants under the age of 45, and 673 valid questionnaires were ultimately collected. The survey was conducted from July 2024 to December 2024. Participants'names were not required to be anonymized.

Ethics statement

In this study, all researchers obtained informed consent from participants before completing the questionnaire survey, and the participants subsequently signed written informed consent forms. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Medical Research Ethics Committee of Qinghai University Medical College approved the ethical review of this study (2023–027).

Measures

Basic demographic survey questionnaire

This questionnaire included name, gender, age, ethnicity, educational background, and marital status.

PHQ-9

The Nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [24] consisted of 9 questions, and the answer to each entry consisted of 4 options: "never," "several days," "more than half the days," and "close to every day." These responses were scored as follows: 0 for "never," 1 for "several days," 2 for "more than half the days," and 3 for "close to every day." The total possible score on the questionnaire was 27. A total score of 0–4 was classified as no depression or no clinically significant anxiety; 5–9 may have mild depression; 10–14 may have moderate depression; and 15–27 may have severe depression. A total score of 5 or higher was considered positive and indicated possible depression. Cronbach's coefficient was 0.84.

GAD-7

The Seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7) [25] consisted of seven questions, each with four possible responses: "never," "for a few days," "for more than a week," and "close to every day." The corresponding scores were 0, 1, 2, and 3, with a total score of 21. A total score of 0–4 was categorized as no anxiety or no clinically significant anxiety; 5–9 as mild anxiety; 10–14 as moderate anxiety; and ≥ 15 as severe anxiety. A total score of 5 or above on this assessment was considered positive, indicating the possibility of anxiety. Cronbach's coefficient of 0.87 was used.

ULS-8

The UCLA Loneliness Scale Chinese Simplified Version (ULS-8) [26] was a simplified and adapted version of the UCLA-20, which was one of the most widely used self-reported loneliness measurement scales in China. The ULS-8 consisted of 8 items, each with four response options: Never, Seldom, Sometimes, and Always. The corresponding scores for these options were 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively, leading to a maximum possible score of 32 points. Two items in the scale were reverse-scored, including questions 7 and 8. For these questions, the scoring was inverted: "Never" receives 4 points, while "Always" receives 1 point. Higher total scores indicated higher levels of loneliness, with a Cronbach's coefficient of 0.81.

Statistical analyses

R version 4.4.1 was used for the statistical description and network analysis [27]. Descriptive analysis was presented using mean(SD) or number(percentage). Additionally, A t-test was used to compare statistically significant differences in depression, anxiety, and loneliness scores between the female and male groups [28]. The network analysis included four components: network estimation [29], network centrality estimation [30], network accuracy and stability estimation [31], and network comparison [32].

Network analysis

The network analysis in this study was conducted using R version 4.4.1. The R packages used in this study include bootnet [33], glasso [34], and mgm [35]. In the network, each node represents a symptom, and the edges between nodes indicate the relationships between these symptoms. The Spearman correlation coefficient was used to estimate the weights of the edges, and a penalty parameter was adjusted to control the complexity of the network and remove false edges. To meet grouping requirements, nodes were categorized into three classes and distinguished by color. Each variable was assigned a name, and the network was visualized using plotting functions. In the network diagram, the thickness of edge lines indicates the strength of the relationship between nodes [29]. Edge line colors represent positive or negative correlations. Blue indicates a positive correlation, and red indicates a negative correlation. The thicker the edge, the stronger the correlation between the two symptoms; conversely, the thinner the edge, the weaker the correlation.

Network centrality

The expected influence (EI) indicator measures the centrality of nodes and represents the severity of symptoms [30]. This analysis was performed using the qgraph package in R. T was performed using the qgraph package in R [36]. The EI of a given node is defined as the sum of all edges extending from that node, rather than the sum of their absolute values. This approach makes it more suitable for networks that contain both positive and negative edges. In this study, the network model included three communities: the depression symptoms community, the anxiety symptoms community, and the loneliness symptoms community. To examine the bridge symptoms connecting these communities, we calculated the Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) using the networktools package [37]. Nodes with higher expected influence values demonstrate a stronger association with other groups, which is crucial for understanding the relationships between different symptoms across groups. In addition, the mgm package in R was used to calculate the predictability of a node [38]. Predictability reflects the extent to which a node can be explained by its neighboring nodes. It also represents the degree of self-maintenance within the entire network structure [39].

Network accuracy and stability

Use the R package bootnet to assess the accuracy and stability of regularized partial correlation networks [31]. Initially, the bootstrap method was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals to assess the accuracy of edge weights. The narrower the confidence interval, the more accurate the estimates of both edge weights and centrality metrics. Then, the correlation stability coefficient (CS) was calculated using the case-decent bootstrap method to estimate the stability of centrality metrics. Generally, a CS value exceeding 0.25 is considered acceptable, while a value greater than 0.5 indicates better stability [40].

Network comparison

The Network Comparison Test package in R software was used to conduct network comparison tests (NCT) on middle-aged and older adults of different genders in the Xining area, analyzing the differences in network structure between the two groups [32]. Three invariance tests were conducted: network structure invariance test, global network strength invariance test, and local invariance test. The local invariance test included differences in edge weights and node centrality indices [41]. Initially, 1,000 permutations were used to compare the absolute values of each edge weight in the network. We used the Holm-Bonferroni algorithm to correct P-values for each edge weight. Subsequently, differences in edge values were compared by subtracting edge weights from the network model in pairs. Finally, independent t-tests were conducted to determine whether there were statistically significant differences between male and female participants.

Network accuracy and stability

By estimating the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the edge weights (Figure S1), it was found that the 95% CIs of the bootstrapped sample set overlapped with those of the original data set to a high degree, indicating that the network model had high accuracy. Stability was assessed using the correlation stability coefficient (CS). The stability coefficient results for EI and BEI were CS = 0.75, indicating that the results were sufficiently stable.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the psychological network structure comprising anxiety, depression, and loneliness among 673 middle-aged and elderly individuals in Xining City, Qinghai Province, China (average altitude 2,261 m). The analysis identified both core symptoms and bridge symptoms within these networks. We also compared the differences in the network structures comprising anxiety, depression, and loneliness between different genders in this sample, identifying the core symptoms for both male and female networks. Importantly, these networks showed good stability, which enhances the confidence in the conclusions drawn from this study. This research provided reliable intervention strategies for the mental health of middle-aged and elderly individuals living in high-altitude regions.

Fan [42] found in a network analysis comprising anxiety, depression, and loneliness related to high-altitude exposure. The study found that the detection rates for moderate to severe anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 10) and depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) were 23.1% and 38.9%, respectively. The average scores for the anxiety and depression scales were (6.18 ± 4.17) and (8.03 ± 5.06), respectively. These rates and average scores were higher than those observed in this study, which may be attributed to differences in ethnicity. In Fan's study, the population was entirely Han Chinese, while over half of the participants in this study were Hui Chinese. Previous research [43] has indicated that the prevalence of depression and anxiety was higher among Han Chinese individuals compared to Hui Chinese individuals with Parkinson's disease (P < 0.05).Yu's network analysis [44] comprising anxiety, loneliness, and depression during the COVID-19 pandemic, revealed that the strongest positive connection in the anxiety community was between GAD5"Being restless"and GAD7"Feeling afraid as if something awful might happen". In the depression group, the most significant connection was found between PHQ7"Concentration problems"and PHQ8"Psychomotor retardation or agitation". However, these findings were inconsistent with the results of this study, which may be attributed to the unique circumstances created by the COVID-19 pandemic. Research has indicated that the progression of the pandemic has led to severe mental health issues for many individuals [45]. Epidemiological studies have shown that people experienced various mental health symptoms during the pandemic, particularly related to anxiety [46] and depression [47].

The core symptoms identified in this study include GAD4 ("Trouble relaxing"), PHQ2 ("Depressed mood"), and ULS3 ("Feeling left out"). These symptoms served as effective targets for the prevention and treatment of anxiety, depression, and loneliness in middle-aged and older adults. Huang [48] conducted a network study comprising depression, anxiety, and loneliness among older adults in the community and found that the core symptoms of depression included PHQ4 "Fatigue", PHQ3 "Sleep problems", PHQ2 "Depressed mood" and ULS3 "Feeling left out". This finding differed from the results of this study, which could be attributed to differences in the research questions and the study subjects. In particular, since the subjects in our study have lived in high-altitude areas for a long time, this environment has affected their physiological metabolism, which in turn has affected their mental health [21]. Hypoxia as a factor influencing behavior was difficult to detect. Physiological changes driven by the sympathetic nervous system, such as increased heart rate and ventilation, were only perceived when oxygen consumption reached significant levels [49]. Therefore, the implementation of coping strategies to improve mental health was hindered mainly. In this study, the items with the highest bridge effect index (BEI) scores in the loneliness, depression, and anxiety groups were ULS6 "Feeling lonely", PHQ8 "Psychomotor retardation or agitation" and GAD5 "Being restless". Identifying these bridge symptoms can help unravel the complexity of comorbidity and provide potential targets for intervention in co-occurring disorders [50]. Cao [51] found in his network analysis of loneliness, depression, and anxiety among older adults that ULS6 "Feeling lonely" was a bridge symptom connecting the loneliness community with the anxiety and depression communities. This study concluded that loneliness increased the probability of anxiety and depression by 65.8%, which was consistent with the results of our study [52].Loneliness was an independent risk factor for depression and a sensitive indicator for predicting suicide [53]. Wu [54] discovered that loneliness among the elderly was positively correlated with anxiety. When individuals felt lonely, they were prone to low moods and social avoidance behaviors. This led to decreased social participation and impaired social skills, ultimately resulting in increased anxiety. PHQ8 "Psychomotor retardation or agitation" was a bridge symptom connecting the depression community with the anxiety and loneliness community. Alzahrani [55] found a moderate positive correlation between depression, anxiety, and loneliness scores, suggesting that individuals with higher levels of depression often experience more anxiety and loneliness. "Psychomotor retardation or agitation"was associated with certain medical conditions, such as Parkinson [56] and Alzheimer [57]. Mi [17] found that GAD5 "Being restless" was a bridge symptom connecting the anxiety community with the loneliness and depression community.

In the gender comparison network, the strongest positive correlations were the same for both male and female networks. However, within the female network, PHQ1 "Anhedonia" was more strongly associated with PHQ2 "Depressed mood". Furthermore, PHQ2 "Depressed mood" was a common core symptom in both male and female anxiety, depression, and loneliness symptom networks. However, PHQ2 "Depressed mood" has a more significant influence on the female network. Liu [58] found gender differences in adolescent mental health, in that girls exhibited stronger interconnections between depressive symptoms than boys. These findings were consistent with the results of this study. Women were more susceptible to depression. Compared to men, women were almost twice as likely to suffer from depression, and this difference persisted from adolescence to the age of 50 [59]. Women were more prone to depression than men due to factors such as health, work, and social relationships [60]. On the one hand, these biological processes were believed to be related to women's susceptibility to depression. Sassarini found that genetic factors determine emotional vulnerability in women [59]. Additionally, women's hormone levels fluctuate more cyclically, affecting areas of the brain related to mood and behavior, causing them to feel inexplicably depressed and discouraged.On the other hand, social roles and cultural norms played an important role in gender differences in depression [61]. Economic inequality (such as economic discrimination and workplace inequality) and the primary responsibility for both housework and childcare can lead to role conflict and role overload, which can have harmful effects on mental health. The division of traditional social roles may lead women to place greater emphasis on factors such as emotional support and intimacy in interpersonal relationships in certain situations [62]. Therefore, emotional issues such as "feeling neglected" and "excessive worry" were core symptoms unique to women, rather than core symptoms of men. Research indicated [63] that men who adhere to traditional masculine gender roles (such as being strong, independent, and emotionally restrained) may experience higher levels of psychological stress and anxiety. This stress was referred to as "Masculine Gender Role Stress"(MGRS). High MGRS was associated with anger, anxiety, and unhealthy behaviors (such as alcohol abuse). Therefore, this may explain why the core symptoms identified in this study (GAD4 and GAD5) were unique to men and not core symptoms for women. Based on the differences in gender network results, it was important to adopt more personalized and differentiated strategies. Due to the different ways in which men and women cope with psychological stress, women tended to adopt emotional coping strategies (such as reflection and seeking social support). In contrast, men were more likely to adopt problem-solving strategies [64]. Therefore, for men, listening to soothing music and traveling can be beneficial. Traveling was an activity that could stimulate men's psychological sense of security and sense of accomplishment, thereby alleviating anxiety [65]. For women, it may be necessary to pay more attention to their emotional expression. This approach can more effectively address the psychological issues of different gender groups and thereby promote their mental and physical well-being.

Limitations and future directions

This study also had certain limitations. First, this study was a cross-sectional design, which can only explore the interrelationships between network symptoms and cannot determine causal relationships. Therefore, future studies may consider using longitudinal data to explore the temporal changes between psychological symptoms. Second, the majority of participants in this study had an education level of elementary school or below, and more than half of the participants were from the Hui ethnic group. The results may be influenced by education level and ethnicity. Future studies could consider incorporating these covariates into the network to construct a mixed graphical model to determine the influence of these factors within the network.

Conclusions

This study examined the relationship between anxiety, depression, and loneliness among middle-aged and older adults in Xining City, Qinghai Province, China (elevation over 2,000 m) from a network analysis perspective. "Trouble relaxing", "Depressed mood", "Feeling left out", "Being restless", "Psychomotor retardation or agitation", and "Feeling lonely" were the core symptoms of anxiety, depression, and loneliness in the network. These may be key targets for preventing anxiety, depression, and loneliness in the middle-aged and older population in high-altitude regions. Additionally, there were significant differences in overall intensity between male and female networks, with gender-specific strategies recommended.
