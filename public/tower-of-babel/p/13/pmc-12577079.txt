Impact of national reimbursement policy supporting test strips on glycemic control in people with insulin-treated diabetes: retrospective cohort study
Authors: Seohyun Kim, Mira Kang, Dohyun Park, Gyuri Kim, Jae Hyeon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-monitoring blood glucose (SMBG) is an important factor for glycemic control in people with diabetes. Many countries have implemented policies to support SMBG for better glycemic control. The long-term impact of supporting the cost of SMBG strips for glycemic control in type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and insulin-treated type 2 DM (T2DM) have been determined in only a few studies.MethodsPeople with T1DM and insulin-treated T2DM for whom the cost of blood glucose strips was and was not supported were included in the present study. Longitudinal changes in HbA1c were repeatedly measured in people with T1DM and insulin-treated T2DM at baseline, and 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. The inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) linear mixed effect model (LMM) analysis was used.ResultsAmong people with T1DM and insulin-treated T2DM, subjects who received support for strip prescriptions showed significantly decreased hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels (0.78% and 0.92% at 12 months, respectively), with significant between-group differences compared with subjects who did not receive support (p < 0.001). When using the IPTW-LMM after adjusting for covariates, the mean reduction in HbA1c every 3 months in T1DM and insulin-treated T2DM subjects with strip prescriptions was − 0.105% (95% CI: −0.197 to − 0.014) and − 0.112% (95% CI: −0.143 to − 0.082), respectively, compared with their respective control groups without strip prescriptions.ConclusionsIn conclusion, the reimbursement policy supporting the cost of blood glucose strip prescriptions was associated with significant reduction in HbA1c levels in people with T1DM or insulin-treated T2DM.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24617-9.Keywords: Type 1 diabetes, Insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, Blood glucose test strips, Inverse probability of treatment weighting, Linear mixed effect model

Introduction

The estimated global prevalence of diabetes is expected to increase from 10.5% in 2021 (536.6 million people) to 12.2% in 2045 (783.2 million people). In addition, the estimated cost for treating, managing, and preventing diabetes and its associated morbidities, such as microvascular and macrovascular complications, is expected to rise from $966 billion in 2021 to $1,054 billion in 2045 across the countries [1]. According to previous studies based on Korea’s national registry, the estimated prevalence of diabetes mellitus was 16.7% in 2020, with approximately 4.9% of these patients’ using insulin [2]. Glycemic control for individual glycemic targets is regarded crucial for preventing diabetes-related microvascular and macrovascular complications such as myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, or death from cardiovascular disease (CVD) [3] as well as poor cognitive function [4, 5]. Self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) is recommended for people with diabetes, especially for individuals on insulin therapy, to detect hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia, and changes in glycemic patterns to appropriately adjust insulin doses and help recognize the influence of diet, physical activity, stress and sleep quality on glycemic control [6–8]. In Korea’s standard of care, individuals with T1DM or insulin-treated adults with T2DM are advised to perform SMBG regularly, including before and after meals, at bedtime, early morning, around exercise, and during hypoglycemic events, with timing and frequency personalized to each patient’s condition [9]. Although the effects of SMBG on lowering hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) were identified in people with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and insulin-treated type 2 DM (T2DM) in previous studies, the evidence remains controversial [10–12]. While some meta-analyses found SMBG to be effective in decreasing HbA1c levels, others reported no significant long-term differences between SMBG and control groups, with the observed reductions in HbA1c (around 0.3%) often falling below the clinically meaningful threshold of 0.4% [13–15].

In a cohort study in Germany and Austria in which the association between the frequency of SMBG and glycemic control was investigated, with an additional daily SMBG, HbA1c was reduced 0.32% in T1DM people with intensified insulin therapy and 0.16% in people with insulin-treated T2DM [16]. In a previous study, people with DM who paid higher out-of-pocket expenses than their insurance coverage for SMBG test strips had a lower frequency of SMBG and people without insurance coverage for SMBG test strips had worse glycemic control than those with insurance coverage [17]. Therefore, the overall insurance coverage for the cost of strips for SMBG is associated with increased adherence to SMBG for improved glycemic control [17, 18]. The Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS) introduced a policy to support the cost of self-monitoring devices for diabetes self-care in people with T1DM in July 2011 and other people with DM who are treated with insulin therapy in November 2015. KNHIS provides coverage for recurrent use of lancets, insulin syringes, and insulin pen needles.

In a previous study, an association was identified between increased frequency of SMBG and reduced HbA1c in the Korean population when the patients were supported for SMBG strips by a national reimbursement policy [19]. However, only People with and subjects who were registered for the reimbursement program for blood glucose strips were included without a comparable study group. Although this policy has incurred a large expenditure, the long-term effects of supporting the cost of SMBG strips on lowering glucose in people with T1DM or insulin-treated T2DM in Asian and Korean populations have been identified in only a few studies. Therefore, in the present longitudinal study, the effectiveness of the national reimbursement policy supporting blood glucose test strips on glycemic outcomes in people with T1DM or insulin-treated T2DM was evaluated.

Methods

Study design and population

This hospital-based retrospective cohort study included people with T1DM or T2DM treated with insulin therapy at Samsung Medical Center (SMC), Seoul, Republic of Korea. The study population consisted of people who were supported for the cost of blood glucose strips, aged ≥ 18 years, visited SMC in Seoul, Republic of Korea, had an E10 ICD-10 diagnostic code from July 2011 to May 2022, and received an insulin prescription at least once on the same date when the E10 code was recorded (N = 1,626). Among these individuals, 907 had HbA1c measurements at both baseline and 12 months, and 676 had complete data for covariates including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides (TG). Finally, 554 individuals received blood glucose strip prescriptions and were classified as the treatment group (interest arm), while 112 individuals did not receive prescriptions and were classified as the control group. The T2DM group included outpatients who visited SMC between January 2015 and May 2022, had E11–E14 diagnostic codes, were aged ≥ 18 years, and received an insulin prescription at least once on the same date the E11–14 code was recorded (N = 10,782). Among these individuals, 5,865 had HbA1c measurements at both baseline and 12 months, 4,214 maintained a continuous insulin prescription at 12 months, and 3,486 had the aforementioned covariates, with 2,918 not changing their regimen of oral glucose-lowering agents during the follow-up period. Based on strip prescription status, 2,078 individuals were classified in the treatment group (interest arm) who were supported for the cost of blood glucose strips through the reimbursement policy, while 840 individuals comprised the control group, who did not receive strip prescriptions from baseline to 12 months. All subjects were followed up for 3, 6, 9, and 12 months from baseline. Finally, a total of 666 T1DM and 2,918 insulin-treated T2DM subjects were analyzed (Figs. 1 and 2).

Variables

Data were retrospectively extracted from the Clinical Data Warehouse (CDW) DARWIN-C of SMC for this study. BMI was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by height squared (). Laboratory data including HbA1c, eGFR, ALT, AST, total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, and TG were collected after an overnight fast. HbA1c levels were collected at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. eGFR was calculated using the modification of diet in renal disease equation [20]. The chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [21, 22].

The index date was defined as the date of input of both E10 or E11–14 diagnostic codes and insulin prescription in the control group and the date of the first strip prescription in the treatment groups. For people with type 2 diabetes who were receiving insulin treatment, we investigated the medication history within a year of α-glucosidase inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), metformin, meglitinides, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i), sulfonylureas (SU), and thiazolidinediones (TZD).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical data are presented as numbers with percentages. A comparison of baseline characteristics was performed using a two-sample t-test for continuous variables and a chi-squared test for categorical variables. The mean and standard deviation of changes in the HbA1c value at each follow-up from baseline were obtained and whether the changes were significant was evaluated using a paired t-test. In addition, a two-sample t-test was performed to determine whether the difference in the changes in HbA1c values between the control groups and treatment groups was significant. Due to reduced imbalance in covariates between control and treatment groups in people with T1DM or T2DM, the inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) linear mixed effect model (LMM) analysis was performed [23–25]. First, the propensity score was calculated as the probability of benefit from the policy of cost for blood glucose test strips using logistic regression with all covariates, such as age, sex, baseline HbA1c, ALT, AST, total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and presence of CKD. For people with type 2 diabetes who are receiving insulin treatment, the usage history of α-glucosidase inhibitors, DPP-4i, GLP-1RA, metformin, meglitinides, SGLT-2i, SU, and TZD was also included in the propensity score calculation. Second, the weights were estimated using inverse of the probability and inverse of (1-probability) based on propensity score assigned to control and treatment groups, respectively. Next, LMM was used for longitudinal data with random intercepts and random slopes to assess the glycemic effect of the support for the cost of blood glucose test strips on repeatedly measured HbA1c levels [26]. The four models were fitted to assess the glycemic effect when subjects were supported for the cost of blood glucose strips from the reimbursement policy. Model 1 was a crude model. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and baseline HbA1c. Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, ALT, AST, total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and presence of CKD. History of using α glucosidase, DPP4i, GLP1-RA, metformin, meglitinide, SGLT2i, SU, or TZD was further adjusted in people with insulin-treated T2DM in Model 3. Model 4 was weighted LMM based on IPTW and adjusted for all covariates of Model 3. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.3 for Windows.

Results

The baseline characteristics of 3,594 subjects are summarized Table 1. Among people with T1DM, 122 (18.0%) did not receive support for the cost of blood glucose strips from the reimbursement policy and 554 (82.0%) did. Among subjects with T2DM, 840 (28.8%) did not benefit from the policy and 2,078 (71.2%) did. Although people with T1DM or T2DM who benefited from the policy tended to be younger, have higher HbA1c, and less CKD at baseline, after the IPTW, discrepancies in demographic and metabolic factors between target and control groups decreased (Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

HbA1c changes from baseline to each follow-up in people with insulin-treated T2DM

Table 2 shows the changes in HbA1c from baseline to each follow-up in people with insulin-treated T2DM. Significant reductions in the HbA1c levels from baseline at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months were observed in both groups (all p < 0.001). People with strip prescriptions showed greater changes in HbA1c values from baseline to 12 months (− 1.01%) compared with subjects without strip prescriptions (− 0.26%), with significant differences between the groups (p < 0.001). The difference in HbA1c reduction between the reimbursement and non-reimbursement groups was 0.70 (95% CI: 0.57–0.83), 0.80 (95% CI: 0.65–0.94), 0.78 (95% CI: 0.63–0.92), and 0.75 (95% CI: 0.63–0.88) at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, respectively, in patients with insulin-treated type 2 diabetes (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 3).

Average difference in decreased HbA1c every 3 months from baseline in people with and without strip prescriptions

To balance the covariates and reduce bias in each treatment duration and analyze the relationship between longitudinal data of HbA1c levels, covariates, and duration of strip prescription, IPTW-LMM analysis was performed (Table 3). After adjustment for covariates including age, sex, baseline HbA1c value, BMI, ALT, AST, total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and the presence of CKD (Model 4) with IPTW, the mean reduction in HbA1c every 3 months in people with and without strip prescriptions was − 0.151% (95% CI: −0.195 to 0.107) and − 0.046% (95% CI: −0.125 to 0.034) in people with T1DM, respectively. Compared with people without strip prescriptions, the mean reduction in HbA1c every 3 months in T1DM people with strip prescriptions was − 0.105% (95% CI: −0.197 to − 0.014) (Model 4). In people with insulin-treated T2DM, mean changes of − 0.2% (95% CI: −0.22 to 0.18) and − 0.079% (95% CI: −0.106 to 0.052) in HbA1c levels every 3 months were observed in subjects with and without strip prescriptions, respectively (Model 4). In Model 4, a significant mean reduction of − 0.121% was observed in HbA1c every 3 months (95% CI: −0.155 to − 0.088) in insulin-treated T2DM people with strip prescriptions compared with subjects without strip prescriptions.

Discussion

In this longitudinal study, the impact of a reimbursement policy supporting the cost of blood glucose strip prescriptions on longitudinal glycemic control in people with T1DM or insulin-treated T2DM were assessed. Subjects who benefited from the policy showed significant reduction in HbA1c levels after 3, 6, 9, and 12 months of follow-up compared with baseline in people with T1DM or insulin-treated T2DM, resulting in significant between-group differences. Considering a reduction of ≥ 0.5% in HbA1c as a clinically meaningful improvement, the mean differences in HbA1c reduction between two groups were consistently ≥ 0.5 at all-time points in both patients with type 1 diabetes and those with insulin-treated type 2 diabetes [27]. Among patients who received support for strip prescriptions, people with T1DM showed a significantly decreased HbA1c of 0.11% every 3 months compared with subjects who did not receive support. Similarly, people with insulin-treated T2DM demonstrated a significant group difference with a decreased HbA1c of 0.12% every 3 months when using the IPTW-LMM model after controlling for demographic and metabolic covariates.

The reimbursement policy supporting the cost of self-monitoring devices for diabetes self-care in people with T1DM and other insulin-treated DM was initiated in 2011 and 2015, respectively. However, the longitudinal effects of this policy on glycemic control have not been evaluated. In a previous cross-sectional study, a higher daily frequency of SMBG was shown associated with 0.32% and 0.16% HbA1c reduction in people with T1DM and insulin-treated T2DM, respectively [16]. In the present study, patients who benefitted from the policy showed marked reduction in HbA1c by 0.78% and 0.92% after 12 months from baseline in people with T1DM and insulin-treated T2DM, respectively. A significant change was observed in HbA1c levels 3 months after the strip prescription and was maintained for 12 months of the study period in people with T1DM or insulin-treated T2DM.

In a systematic review of randomized control trials in people with non-insulin-treated T2DM, at 12 weeks, the HbA1c reduction in the SMBG group was − 0.31% (95% CI: −0.57 to − 0.05) compared with the no SMBG group [15]. In a cohort study in Germany and Austria, an additional daily SMBG reduced HbA1c 0.32% in T1DM people with intensified insulin therapy and 0.16% in T2DM subjects treated with insulin, showing an association between the frequency of SMBG and glycemic control [16]. In the present study that included insulin-treated people with diabetes in real-world settings, the mean difference in HbA1c changes was the largest 12 months after the strip prescription (− 0.92%) in people with insulin-treated T2DM. Furthermore, for more efficient glycemic management, patients should be educated to measure blood glucose frequently and control their glucose levels with appropriate insulin adjustments. The present study had several strengths. The people with T1DM and insulin-treated T2DM were defined strictly based on the EMR database using their medical records regarding diagnostic code and insulin prescription. Due to the repeatedly measured HbA1c levels at four time points (3, 6, 9, and 12 months), imbalanced baseline HbA1c levels, and covariates between groups, the effects of supporting the cost of strip prescriptions were estimated using IPTW-LMM analysis that considers repeated measurement correlation longitudinally and multivariate adjustment for covariates. However, the present study had several limitations. First, this study was conducted in a single center that may limit the ability to generalize the results to other settings. Second, because the prescription information for glucose test strips was used in the study, discrepancies may exist between the prescription and patients’ actual compliance with SMBG. The lack of direct measurement of SMBG frequency or adherence further limits the assessment of actual testing behavior. Because access to the actual number of measurements in the dataset was limited, it was not possible to analyze the relationship between the number of measurements and the reduction in glycated hemoglobin. Third, the data source in this study does not include variables related to socioeconomic status, limiting our ability to account for its potential impact on SMBG adherence and glycemic control. Finally, while physicians recommend that people with diabetes receive strip prescriptions, there are instances where most patients were not inclined to frequently self-monitor their blood glucose and may choose not to do so, or believe they are managing their blood glucose adequately. To clarify the reasons for not receiving strip prescriptions, it would be necessary to measure this through self-reported questionnaires; however, this variable was not recorded in this retrospective study, which is a major limitation. The frequency of SMBG was not measured in our study, which is a significant limitation. The assumption that financial support for SMBG implies increased frequency and improved glycemic control may not hold true, as several factors can influence SMBG frequency, including patient education and the titration of insulin therapies. Therefore, the association between reimbursement policies, SMBG frequency, and glycemic control requires further investigation.

In conclusion, although the SMBG frequency was not considered, the reimbursement policy supporting the cost of blood glucose strip prescriptions was associated with significant reduction in HbA1c levels in people with T1DM or insulin-treated T2DM. However, further studies are needed to confirm the causal relationship between policy and glycemic control, as well as to explore potential secondary outcomes.
