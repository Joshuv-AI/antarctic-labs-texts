Proton therapy for breast cancer: Reducing toxicity
Authors: Kailin Qiao, Yuchun Wei, Cheng Tao, Jian Zhu, Shuanghu Yuan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiotherapy is a crucial component in the holistic management of breast cancer, with approximately 60% of individuals diagnosed with breast cancer requiring this treatment. As the survival rate of individuals with breast cancer has significantly increased, there is a growing focus on the long‐term well‐being of patients. Proton therapy (PT) is a new and rapidly developing radiotherapy method. In comparison with conventional photon therapy, PT offers the benefits of decreased radiation toxicity and increased dosage in the designated region. This can extend patients' lifespan and enhance their overall well‐being. The present analysis examines the function of PT in diminishing the harmful effects of radiation in cases of breast cancer, while also providing a brief overview of the future potential and obstacles associated with PT for breast cancer.Keywords: breast cancer, cardiotoxicity, organs at risk, proton therapy, pulmonary toxicity

INTRODUCTION

Among female cancers, breast cancer is the most prevalent type and has the highest incidence rate. 1 The use of radiotherapy is crucial in the comprehensive treatment of breast cancer, and postoperative radiotherapy is a vital component in the care of individuals with breast cancer. 2 In cases where surgery is challenging or has an impact on function or appearance, radiotherapy is frequently employed either as a substitute for mastectomy or as an additional measure to eliminate suspected residual subclinical lesions. Clarke and colleagues showed a decrease of 5.4% in the mortality rate caused by breast cancer 15 years after radiotherapy. 3 Multiple pieces of evidence indicated that the utilization of radiotherapy plays a crucial role in diminishing the likelihood of local reoccurrence of breast cancer and enhancing overall survival. 4 , 5

Technological advancements have been crucial in enhancing precision, managing diseases, and minimizing toxicity in the course of radiation therapy's evolution. One of the current obstacles is to decrease the amount of radiation that normal tissues, especially the heart and lungs, are exposed to, while also enhancing the treatment of important breast cancer targets like regional nodules. 6 Enhanced radiation methods have the potential to enhance treatment success rates by expanding the range of targeted areas and minimizing harm to vital organs. However, traditional three‐dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3D‐CRT), intensity‐modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), and volumetric arc therapy (VMAT) all have some disadvantages of high exposure of organs at risk (OAR). 7

Not only does proton therapy (PT) enhance target coverage but it also greatly decreases the total dose and OAR dose. Given the favorable outlook and extended lifespan of individuals with breast cancer, it is crucial to pay heed to potential complications like cardiac toxicity or the development of secondary tumors. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 As a treatment choice for patients with breast cancer, although the application of PT is limited by capital and facilities, it has been the subject of discussion. 13 , 14 The use of intensity‐modulated proton therapy (IMPT) for breast cancer treatment has garnered significant interest due to its potential to provide better coverage of the clinical target volume and reduce exposure to OAR. 15 , 16 IMPT decreases OAR dose, potentially lowering the likelihood of long‐term negative effects. 16 , 17

RATIONALE AND CLASSIFICATION OF PT

Unlike megavoltage photon radiation, proton beams are composed of particles that have mass and electrical charge, leading to distinct interactions with tissues. PT is characterized by its unique dose distribution, including the Bragg peak effect. It delivers a high dose to a limited area and then decreases rapidly (Figure 1). 18 , 19 Because of the sudden halt at the end of the proton range, very little amount of radiation is administered past a specific depth. Hence, photon radiation inevitably generates an “exit dose,” whereas protons do not cause any extra irradiation beyond the intended target. 19 The significance of this characteristic is especially crucial for individuals with breast cancer and provides PT with a significant benefit in minimizing contact with surrounding healthy tissue, thereby potentially decreasing the overall risk of toxicity 20 (Figure 2).

Protons can be delivered in two primary manners. The oldest technique is the passive one. To treat big tumors, protons are dispersed through a dual scattering mechanism involving two successive scatterers. To treat tumor targets at various depths, the protons' range and energy are adjusted to create a spread‐out Bragg peak (SOBP) that is sufficiently broad. 21 Pencil beam scanning (PBS) or the active technique is a recently developed method. Proton energy modulation is achieved through various methods depending on the equipment used, such as cyclotron, synchrotron, or synchrotron. PBS employs a compact beam that is magnetically scanned across the entire tumor target, starting from the far end and moving toward its near end. Afterward, the scanning magnet disperses the protons toward the sides and outer edges of the tumor. The use of this method is highly responsive to movement and may necessitate the reconfiguration of the layers. Compared to passive methods, PBS offers greater flexibility and has the ability to provide improved protection for OAR, including the skin. PBS is the primary equipment found in modern PT facilities. Optimizing the dosage can be achieved through the utilization of either single field uniform dose or IMPT. Nevertheless, PBS is responsive to setup uncertainties, while persisting with technical and physical challenges. 22

CARDIAC TOXICITY

Reducing long‐term cardiovascular events is one of the objectives when it comes to irradiation for breast cancer. Thoracic radiation therapy is known to cause cardiotoxicity, which is considered a significant negative outcome. The exact causes of radiation‐induced heart disease are not completely understood, but it is believed that radiation triggers several pathological processes, which include direct harm to cells, oxidative stress, impaired functioning of endothelial cells, inflammation, and fibrosis. 23 , 24 The manifestation of this can occur in various forms, such as restrictive cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, pericardial disease, and heart failure with either preserved or reduced ejection fraction. While it is typically observed that evident illness manifests itself after a period of 5 years or longer following exposure to radiation, research indicates that there is a possibility of subclinical irregularities in both structure and function emerging shortly after exposure to radiation. 25 , 26 Multiple research studies have indicated a higher likelihood of cardiovascular incidents linked to unintended exposure of the heart to radiation. 27 , 28 , 29 Taylor et al. 11 approximated that for the overall relative risk of cardiac death within a decade following breast cancer radiation, rate ratio was 1.30 (95% CI, 1.15–1.46; p < 0.001) on the basis of 1253 cardiac deaths. Cardiac death after radiotherapy is primarily caused by coronary artery lesions. 30 In Darby's 2 significant study, it was demonstrated that there is a direct correlation between the dose received by the heart and major coronary events. Despite the utilization of intricate photon radiotherapy techniques like IMRT, sufficient coverage of the breast and internal mammary node (IMN) targets can expose a significant amount of the heart to radiation. According to studies, the cardiac dose from different IMRT methods for breast treatment varies between 2.6 and 8.7 Gy. 31 , 32 , 33 Modalities and techniques to mitigate cardiac side effects include prone position, deep inspiratory breath‐hold, and PT. 34 , 35 , 36 Compared to conventional radiation therapy, PT decreases the mean cardiac dosage during breast cancer irradiation 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 and potentially decreases the occurrence of delayed cardiovascular toxicity. Several studies have indicated that the typical average cardiac dose for PT is less than 1 Gy.

It has been verified that PT can decrease the dosage of the heart and left anterior descending (LAD) in individuals diagnosed with left breast cancer. 42 , 43 , 44 Verma and his team provided evidence that PT could potentially reduce coronary events by administering mean heart doses of ¾ 1 Gy. 45 Luo et al. 46 reported 42 consecutive patients who underwent mastectomy for non‐metastatic breast cancer were treated with adjuvant chest wall and regional nodal PT; the median dose of radiation administered was 50 Gy (relative biological effectiveness [RBE]) (range 45–61.2 Gy [RBE]). Median planning target volume V95 was 95.9% (range 79.4%–99.6%). The median mean heart dose for all patients was 0.7 Gy (RBE) (range 0–3.2 Gy [RBE]). For left‐sided breast cancer treatment, median mean heart dose was 0.84 Gy (RBE) (range 0–3.2 Gy) and for right‐sided breast cancer treatment, it was 0.07 Gy (RBE) (range 0–0.82 Gy). Stick et al. 47 found that the additional risk of heart problems from photon therapy at the age of 80 was minimal, with median values of 1.0% (range, 0.2%–2.9%) and 0.5% (range, 0.03%–1.0%) for individuals with and without cardiac risk factors, respectively. However, the risk was even lower with PT, at 0.13% (range, 0.02%–0.5%) and 0.06% (range, 0.004%–0.3%), respectively. With photons, the median estimated excess absolute risk of breast cancer recurrence after 10 years was 0.10% (range, 0.0%–0.9%), whereas with protons, it was 0.02% (range, 0.0%–0.07%). Jimenez et al. 48 confirmed that the average doses to the heart and LAD during PT are 0.50 Gy (RBE) and 1.16 Gy (RBE), respectively. These doses are considered to be very low, even though the nearby IMNs were thoroughly treated, and cannot be achieved with alternative radiotherapy methods. Typically, in the present time, the average doses of the heart for individuals undergoing traditional radiotherapy on the left side, including the IMNs, range from 2.5 to 6 Gy. Additionally, the average doses delivered to the LAD can range from 10 to 30 Gy. 49 , 50 The studies successfully demonstrated the possibility of administering uniform, substantial amounts of radiation to optimize cancer management while protecting the heart. A previous study comparing the doses to the heart in breast cancer patients using PT and IMRT indicated that PT resulted in a lower average mean heart dose (2.6 Gy) compared to IMRT (5.6 Gy). 37 Hassan et al. 51 conducted a prospective, observational study of 70 women undergoing PT for breast cancer. Before PT, serial assessments were conducted for global longitudinal strain (GLS), high‐sensitivity troponin I, and N‐terminal pro hormone B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP). These assessments were repeated 2 and 4 weeks after PT completion. 32% of patients had hypertension. The average radiation doses to the heart and left ventricle (LV) were 0.44 and 0.12 Gy, respectively. There were no significant changes observed in left ventricular ejection fraction (65 ± 5 vs. 66 ± 5 vs. 64 ± 4%; p = 0.15), overall GLS (−21.7 ± 2.7 vs. −22.7 ± 2.3 vs. −22.8 ± 2.1%; p = 0.24), segmental GLS, high‐sensitivity troponin, or NT‐proBNP in PT from pre‐PT to post‐PT. In contrast, females with high blood pressure experienced a more significant reduction in GLS following PT in comparison with females without high blood pressure (−21.3 ± 3.5 vs. −24.0 ± 2.4%; p = 0.006). The LV function remained unaffected by PT, and there was no correlation with an elevation in biomarkers. In recent years, the application of hypofractionated regimens in radiotherapy has gradually increased. In a randomized phase 2 trial of PT for breast cancer, the median mean heart dose was 0.54 Gy in the conventional fractionation group and 0.49 Gy in the hypofractionation group, so hypofractionation may result in less cumulative exposure to OAR than conventional fractionation. 52 These data support the potential benefits of PT compared to conventional radiotherapy for the heart (Table 1).

SECONDARY MALIGNANCY

Various factors, including smoking or chemotherapy, can impact the likelihood of developing secondary cancers. 11 , 53 , 54 However, approximately 9% of secondary cancers in individuals with breast cancer are caused by exposure to radiation. 55 , 56 Grantzau and Overgaard 57 calculated that the standardized incidence of secondary malignancies after radiotherapy in breast cancer patients was 1.23, compared with 1.08 in patients who did not receive radiation therapy. In a meta‐analysis involving more than 40 000 women who were randomly allocated to receive radiation therapy for breast cancer or no radiation therapy, it was found that patients in the radiation therapy group had a considerably higher likelihood of developing contralateral breast cancer, lung cancer, esophageal cancer, and leukemia. 11 Compared to untreated patients, patients with breast cancer face a slightly higher risk of developing secondary malignancies following radiotherapy. 58 , 59 The adverse effects of radiation therapy in the later stages and the possibility of developing additional cancers have a detrimental effect on the well‐being of individuals with breast cancer, potentially outweighing the advantages of radiotherapy.

Because protons have a particulate characteristic, the total dose attained through PT was reduced, resulting in a considerably lower occurrence of secondary cancer compared to photon radiotherapy. A comparative analysis by Chung et al. 60 showed that the occurrence of secondary cancers among patients in the PT group was 5.2%, whereas it was 7.5% in the photon therapy group. A study by Raptis et al. 61 showed that the overall risks linked to PT were roughly four times less than the corresponding risks of photon therapy in the competition model (around 0.15% vs. 0.60%).

Lung cancer

The lungs pose the greatest danger for developing secondary malignancies following radiation treatment for breast cancer, and the likelihood of this risk is directly linked to the amount of radiation received by the lungs. Furthermore, the average lung dose was linked to mortality caused by secondary lung carcinoma, with the overall risk being especially significant among individuals who have a smoking background. 11 In PT, the lung dose volume histogram showed a decrease in both the low and high dose components compared to photon radiotherapy techniques. 39 Additionally, dose modeling studies indicated a decreased risk of second lung cancer after PT. 62

Raptis 61 and his colleagues reported that the lung received an average radiation dose of 1.45 Gy in photon therapy and 0.58 Gy in PT. They found that the incidence of secondary lung cancer decreased from 0.31% with photon therapy to 0.12% with PT. Bradley et al. 39 compared PT with conventional radiotherapy and found that the median V5 and V20 of the lung on the same side were improved after PT (median V5, 35.3% vs. 60.5%, p < 0.0001; median V20, 21.6% vs. 35.5%, p < 0.0001). PT resulted in a noteworthy decrease in low‐dose radiation (V5, V10) and a decrease or comparable effect on medium to high‐dose radiation (V20, V40) in comparison with IMRT. 63 Luo et al. 46 found a median ipsilateral lung V20 of 16.1% (range, 2.1%–30.3%) and an ipsilateral lung V5 of 34.0% (16.4%–53.8%) with PT. De Rose et al. 64 compared the risk of breast and lung cancer when treating breast cancer patients with VMAT and PBS, and the excess absolute risk per 100‐PY was significantly greater for VMAT compared to PBS for contralateral structures (breast [0.1 vs. 0.01]; lung [0.14 vs. 0.02]) and approximately two times higher for ipsilateral lung (0.35 vs. 0.2). Paganetti et al. 62 estimated the excess absolute risk, excess relative risk, and lifetime attributable risk for secondary malignancies using dosimetric data from 34 breast cancer patients (10 photon‐VMAT, 10 photon 3D‐CRT, 14 PBS). Patients with intact breasts were administered a total dose of 45.0 Gy to the entire breast, followed by a boost of 14.4 Gy in the cavity where the tumor was removed, at a rate of 1.8 Gy per day. The risk of PBS was the lowest (up to 5.56%) for the same‐side lung, with 3D‐CRT (up to 6.54%) and VMAT (up to 7.7%) following closely behind. The 3D‐CRT and PBS methods (risk < 0.86%) demonstrated a notable superiority over VMAT (up to 4.4%) in terms of the opposite lung.

Contralateral breast cancer

Due to the absence of dependence on tangential fields for avoiding normal tissue, proton beams deliver the least amount of radiation to non‐target tissue, and the contralateral breast is not significantly exposed to radiation. 65 , 66 , 67 Reducing the dosage could potentially decrease the occurrence of secondary breast cancer in the opposite breast. According to modeling studies, it has been demonstrated that PT can decrease the lifetime probability of developing a second primary breast cancer on the opposite side in comparison with 3D‐CRT and IMRT. 68 , 69

Raptis et al. 61 discovered a 0.1% chance of developing a secondary malignancy in the opposite breast when exposed to photons, whereas the risk was almost nonexistent when exposed to protons. Ko et al. 70 reported that the VMAT method led to an average dose of more than 2.1 Gy to the opposite breast, which was greater than that accomplished with alternative methods. On the other hand, the PT method achieved an average dose of less than 0.005 Gy to the opposite breast, which was lower compared to the doses acquired through alternative methods. The contralateral breast tissue was effectively shielded by solely employing the PT method, administering doses in the range of 0 Gy (0.003–0.005 Gy). It is important to restrict the average dose to all sections of the opposite breast to less than 1 Gy, particularly in females under the age of 40. Research has indicated that women under the age of 40 who are exposed to a radiation dose higher than 1 Gy in the breast quadrant have a risk of contralateral breast cancer that is 2.5 times higher than women who have not been exposed. 71 Those who are at a high risk of harmful genetic changes or have complex anatomy may find it especially valuable to take into account the possibility of developing secondary cancer in the opposite breast.

RADIATION PNEUMONITIS

Pneumonitis occurring as a result of radiation therapy for breast cancer using proton and photon is an infrequent occurrence. 48 , 72 Radiation‐induced lung inflammation is observed in approximately 5% of individuals undergoing breast external beam radiation therapy, and this likelihood can rise to as much as 14.6% when concurrent chemotherapy is administered. 73 , 74 PT decreases lung radiation exposure in early‐stage and locally advanced breast cancer patients compared to 3D‐CRT and IMRT, 39 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 resulting in reductions of several Gy across all studies. These reductions effectively lower the occurrence of radiation pneumonia.

In the study by Sun et al., 79 IMPT strategies resulted in a decrease in the average lung dose and all dose‐volume parameters. The data indicated a strong association between the average lung dose, as well as the dosimetric factors V10, V20, V30, V40, and V50 of the lung, and advanced radiation fibrosis. 80 Therefore, IMPT decreases the occurrence of severe respiratory complications in contrast to photonic methods. Additional research has also indicated that lung V5 and V20 play a role in the likelihood of developing radiation pneumonia. 81 , 82 , 83 Huang et al. 84 found that there was a 29% occurrence of grade 1 late pulmonary fibrosis after postmastectomy radiation therapy with electrons and regional nodal irradiation (RNI), while no instances of grade 2 late pulmonary fibrosis were observed. Lind et al. 74 reported that 4.1% of individuals who underwent RNI experienced radiation pneumonitis. In the study by Bradley et al., 39 only one patient experienced radiation pneumonitis, and the patient underwent a treatment that combined proton and photon while also receiving chemotherapy simultaneously. Pneumonitis occurred specifically in the breast tangent field, where photons were used for treatment. A prospective study 48 of proton beam radiation in patients undergoing RNI showed encouragingly low levels of lung damage, with only one patient experiencing grade 2 pneumonitis and no cases of grade 3.

SKIN TOXICITY

PT has been found to have drawbacks such as higher skin dose and varying levels of dermatitis, in contrast to photon therapy. 85 , 86 Sun et al.’ s 79 research showed that IMPT plans resulted in a notable rise in the skin dose when compared to the techniques of IMRT and VMAT. DeCesaris et al.’ s 15 study analyzed the occurrence of acute radiation dermatitis (RD) and skin hyperpigmentation (SH) was examined in 86 individuals with breast cancer who underwent PBS (n = 39) or photon (n = 47) radiotherapy. Among patients who received PBS and photon therapy, the occurrence of RD was 69.2% and 29.8% (p = 0.002), with grade 3 RD occurring in 5.1% and 4.3% (p = 0.848), and no notable disparity in the incidence of SH. In comparison with patients who received photon radiation, patients who received PT experienced a greater occurrence of grade 2 RD. The incidence of toxicity at grade 3 was minimal in both groups, and there were no instances of grade 4–5 toxicity. A prospective clinical trial 77 enrolled 98 patients with stage I breast cancer who underwent accelerated partial breast irradiation (APBI) treatment (32 Gy, divided into 8 doses twice a day). Out of the total patients, 19 received PT while the remaining 79 patients were treated with photon or a combination of photon and electron therapy. At 7 years, 62% of patients receiving PT had satisfactory or excellent overall cosmesis, while 94% of patients receiving photon therapy had the same level of satisfaction (p = 0.03). The occurrence of long‐term telangiectasia, alteration in skin color, and skin toxicity resulting from PT was found to be higher in this research.

In a study 46 involving 42 patients with breast cancer who received postoperative auxiliary PT, it was observed that all participants experienced mild to moderate acute skin toxicity. However, no severe acute skin toxicity was reported. Regarding advanced skin toxicity, approximately ⅓ to ½ of patients experienced grade 1 advanced skin toxicity characterized by SH, telangiectasia, or fibrosis, while there were no instances of grade 2 or higher advanced skin toxicity. MacDonald et al. 35 conducted a prospective clinical trial; 12 patients received 50.4 Gy PT for chest wall. The scientists assessed the skin toxicity throughout the therapy and at 4 and 8 weeks following the conclusion of the treatment. They discovered that the highest level of skin toxicity experienced during the radiation phase was grade 2. Also, the cosmesis at 4 and 8 weeks was favorable, since most patients had only mild erythema or pigmentation. Cuaron et al. 40 conducted a study involving 30 patients who underwent proton postmastectomy radiation therapy (PMRT), and they reported the occurrence of grade 2 dermatitis was 71.4%. Hence, it was crucial to carefully evaluate the dosage of PT in order to reduce both immediate and prolonged toxicity. Implementing low segmentation as a tactic could potentially mitigate the chances of skin toxicity, as evidenced by a phase 3 randomized study which revealed that low segmentation was linked to a noteworthy decrease in the occurrence of severe acute skin toxicity (3% vs. 8%, p < 0.0001). 87 Pasalic et al. 88 made an interim analysis of acute and delayed side effects and cosmetic outcomes following proton APBI. No acute or late toxicities of grade ≥3 was observed, with telangiectasia (17%) being the most frequent late cosmetic effect. After 12 and 24 months, the patients' treatment/results achieved a satisfaction rate of 96% and 100%, respectively. The findings of this research validated that proton APBI is an advancing choice for treatment, exhibiting excellent local control and satisfactory mid‐term cosmetic outcomes. Tommasino et al. 89 conducted a model‐based technique that showed that PBS with skin dose optimization could potentially reduce the likelihood of acute skin toxicity than IMRT.

According to the above research, despite the drawback of elevated skin dose, PT predominantly exhibits skin toxicity of grade 1–2, with minimal or no toxicity exceeding grade 2. Patients experienced high levels of satisfaction with the long‐term follow‐up in terms of cosmetic outcomes, without any impact on their survival.

BREAST RECONSTRUCTION

Recently, the utilization of immediate breast reconstruction (IBR) in breast cancer has been on the rise, offering numerous benefits to patients. 90 , 91 Nevertheless, IBR might present a distinct obstacle for administering PMRT to patients. 92 The risk of complications in reconstruction, such as infection, wound opening, skin flap death after mastectomy, capsular contracture, or poor aesthetic outcome, significantly increases after IBR with PMRT. 93 , 94 , 95 , 96 PMRT may also heighten the likelihood of reconstruction failure, typically characterized by the extraction of tissue expander or implant, leading to the absence of ultimate reconstruction or self‐reconstruction. 93 , 94 , 97

It is evident that the reconstructed breast irradiated by IMPT has a higher number of reconstruction complications compared to the unirradiated contralateral reconstruction, as observed following photon therapy. 76 However, the general occurrence of complications following IMPT treatment is superior to the findings reported for photon therapy. 97 The advantages in terms of radiation dosage may be more apparent in individuals who have undergone IBR surgery, particularly those who have undergone bilateral reconstruction. 43 In comparison with passive scattering PT, IMPT administered through PBS has the ability to modify the proton dose on the skin surface in order to minimize the occurrence of hot spots on the reconstructed breast mound. Simultaneously, it ensures the desired dose is delivered to the cutaneous lymphatic vessels for effective control of microscopic diseases. However, in patients receiving IMPT treatment, reconstruction complications are still a problem of clinical significance. Additional investigation is required to identify approaches for minimizing complications and the loss of implants following photon and PT.

PERSPECTIVES

PT is a relatively new radiotherapy method, which seems to have a bright future in the comprehensive treatment of breast cancer. Reducing toxicity seems to be the most attractive place to use PT, such as protecting the heart, reducing the risk of secondary cancer, and reducing the risk of radiation pneumonia. Despite the well‐established dosimetric benefits of PT, it will take several years to several decades to observe the clinical effects of long‐term adverse reactions like significant cardiovascular incidents and secondary malignant tumors. 98 Therefore, it is necessary to conduct long‐term follow‐up studies to determine the extended survival and enhanced quality of life resulting from PT in the treatment of breast cancer. Furthermore, there remains an issue of skin toxicity, but recent studies indicate that the majority of skin toxicity induced by PT is of a mild nature, thereby not impacting patient survival. Moreover, innovative PT techniques like PBS have the potential to mitigate PT‐related skin toxicity.

The limited accessibility of breast cancer PT is a prominent issue, and the expenses associated with setting up and running a PT center can impede the utilization of this technology. Considering the substantial capital and facility needs, PT remains a scarce resource. 99 However, the initial findings of economic studies indicate that it could potentially be a more economical option compared to photons for individuals with a high risk of cardiovascular illness. 100 , 101 , 102 Despite the huge cost, PT is becoming more and more available in academia and private sector. In the coming years, ongoing enhancements and competitive technological advancements will enhance the efficiency and affordability of PT.

CONCLUSION

In general, breast cancer treatment with PT offers several benefits such as decreased toxicity, increased patient survival time, and enhanced quality of life when compared to photon therapy due to its distinct dosimetry characteristics. At the same time, additional investigation is required to ascertain whether it is genuinely advantageous for patients with high risk of advanced morbidity and is cost‐effective.
