Integrating the physical environment into Alzheimer's disease and Alzheimer's disease related dementias research: A Gateway Exposome Coordinating Center (GECC) agenda on gaps and priorities
Authors: Dany Doiron, Zorana Jovanovic Andersen, Matthew Bozigar, Rima Habre, Perry Hystad, Ingrid Jarvis, David Knapp, Tom J. Luben, Priya Patel, Jayant M. Pinto, Beate Ritz, John Volckens, Gregory A. Wellenius, Jinkook Lee, Michael Brauer, Jeffrey Brook, Sara D. Adar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe physical environment is an important yet underexplored domain in Alzheimer's disease and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD), shaping exposures and health‐related behaviors across the life course. We identified key gaps and priorities for incorporating physical environment measures into population‐based research, with an emphasis on geospatial data that can be applied in large cohorts and administrative datasets. The Gateway Exposome Coordinating Center (GECC) synthesized input from six town halls, a 4‐day expert workshop, and the literature to build consensus on priority physical environment exposures and a roadmap for advancing exposure data. We also identified four methodological imperatives: developing pathway‐relevant metrics, addressing temporality, incorporating behavior into exposure estimation, and integrating co‐occurring exposures. Advancing guidance and exposure metrics in these areas can support prevention‐oriented, policy‐relevant research and improve understanding of how physical environments influence brain health.

INTRODUCTION

The 2024 Lancet Commission on Dementia Prevention, Intervention and Care estimates that up to 45% of dementia cases could be prevented or delayed by addressing 14 modifiable risk factors, including less education, head injury, physical inactivity, smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, hypertension, high cholesterol, obesity, diabetes, hearing loss, vision loss, depression, infrequent social contact, and air pollution.1 Although air pollution is explicitly recognized as an environmental contributor to dementia risk(2), many other established risk factors, such as physical inactivity, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, hearing loss, and limited social engagement, may also be influenced—directly or indirectly—by the physical environment. We define the physical environment here as the outdoor natural and built surroundings in which individuals live, work, recreate, and age and which shape exposures, behaviors, and physiological processes. Given the ubiquity of environmental risk factors and the potential for improved quantification of modifiable Alzheimer's disease and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) risk factors, additional attention on the impact of the physical environment on dementia risk is warranted. With further research, additional modifiable risk factors related to the physical environment are likely to be identified.

While there is increasing interest in understanding how environmental factors contribute to the development and progression of AD/ADRD, data on these exposures may not be readily available or easily integrated into the health databases used in AD/ADRD research. Relatedly, multiple datasets exist for some exposures, so consensus guidance is needed to help researchers select the data that is most appropriate for specific studies and research questions. The Gateway Exposome Coordinating Center (GECC) is addressing these gaps through a concerted effort to develop guidance for incorporating environmental and social exposures into AD/ADRD research. The GECC is organized around six interconnected domains (social environment, policy environment, community services environment, life experiences, extreme weather, and physical environment), each focused on different aspects of the exposome—the sum of all non‐genetic exposures an individual experiences over their life (3).

In this paper, we focus on one of these domains, the physical environment, and the role of geospatial methods and data in enabling the assessment of the external exposome in space and time to help characterize large, population‐based cohorts and administrative health datasets spanning decades. These approaches, which are largely centered on “where you live,” complement biomarkers or internal exposome measures (i.e., biological responses and molecular changes) that are typically limited to smaller samples and are difficult to assemble in long‐term longitudinal designs. Relatedly, we acknowledge the close overlap of the physical environment with several other domains of the GECC, most notably the extreme weather, community services, and social environment domains. This reflects the inherent complexity of real‐world environments and underscores the importance of the multidisciplinary and collaborative approach that is central to the GECC. This paper outlines the key gaps in our understanding of “place‐based” exposures encountered in the physical environment that influence AD/ADRD, other than extreme weather, which is being covered elsewhere, and provides guidance as to how to best estimate and integrate physical environment variables in AD/ADRD research.

METHODS

The GECC's work is grounded in a broad consultative process designed to identify key gaps, establish research priorities, and support the development of standardized, policy‐relevant exposure metrics to gain a better understanding of how environmental factors may shape brain health across the life course. In Fall 2024, the GECC hosted a series of six virtual town hall meetings to gather insights from the international scientific and public health communities. These sessions engaged nearly 300 participants from 128 organizations across 21 countries, reflecting a wide range of disciplines relevant to environmental and social influences on AD/ADRD. In small breakout groups, participants responded to the central question: “What are the major environmental influences (both positive and negative) on brain health as people age?” Responses were captured and thematically coded using physical environment–focused keywords and frequency of mentions were summarized to inform preliminary prioritization. Town hall participants underscored the need for standardized, scalable data and tools to support integration of physical environment exposures into AD/ADRD research, and highlighted specific exposures for which knowledge gaps remain. The input gathered during these sessions played a critical role in shaping the agenda for subsequent consensus‐building activities.

In March 2025, the GECC convened > 80 leading experts in Los Angeles, California for an in‐person workshop focused on identifying and refining research priorities at the intersection of exposure science and AD/ADRD(4), building on feedback gathered during the town hall meetings. Workshop participants included researchers and practitioners from diverse fields including exposure science, geography, neuroscience, medicine, epidemiology, gerontology, demography, biostatistics, sociology, economics, engineering, and public policy. Over 4 days of structured discussion and domain‐specific breakout sessions, participants collaboratively identified and prioritized key gaps in understanding how various aspects of the external environment are related to AD/ADRD; refined short‐ and long‐term priorities for exposure‐related data generation and curation; and outlined strategies for disseminating new methods, tools, and guidance across the research and policy communities. This workshop helped develop a roadmap for advancing data and guidance in support of AD/ADRD research.

RESULTS

This synthesis identifies four exposure priorities in the physical environment for near‐term guidance and metric development: (1) air pollution, (2) greenspace, (3) noise and soundscapes, and (4) community amenities and urban form. Extreme weather (i.e., events such as heat waves) and neighborhood‐level socioeconomic factors relevant to ADRD that were mentioned during the consultations are covered by other GECC domains. Exposure to neurotoxic chemicals such as lead and pesticides were highlighted in consultations and are considered a complementary focus area. While chemical exposures may come from a variety of sources including dietary intake and drinking water, they are included here because the physical environment influences such exposures either directly (e.g., lead‐contaminated soil) or indirectly via proximity to sources (e.g., agricultural pesticide application). Building on these priorities, we also highlight four cross‐cutting methodological gaps that consistently arose from community consultations when discussing how to improve integration of physical environment risk factors into AD/ADRD research. First, there is a need for pathway‐informed conceptual models and data that move beyond associations to identify drivers of place‐based risk and targets for intervention. Second, explicit consideration of temporality (i.e., addressing timing of exposure, duration of exposure, cumulative dose, and sensitive life‐course windows) is needed. A third gap to be filled is the incorporation of behavioral patterns into health‐based analyses to reflect how people interact with their physical environments because these characteristics may mediate or modify exposure–outcome relationships. Finally, there is a need to understand exposures within the larger context of the complex, interconnected environments in which people live rather than as isolated stressors. Air pollution

Air pollution was the most frequently discussed exposure in research community consultations, likely because it is already a widely recognized and accepted modifiable risk factor for AD/ADRD 1 . Ambient air pollution comprises mixtures of particulate matter and gaseous pollutants originating from traffic, industry, wildfires, residential combustion, and other anthropogenic and biogenic sources. Air pollution is estimated to account for ≈ 3% of global dementia cases 1 and reductions in air pollution have been associated with lower dementia risk 5 , supporting a plausible causal relationship. Putative mechanisms of action include air pollution's ability to promote systemic oxidative stress and inflammation, cerebrovascular injury, and central neuroinflammation leading to neurodegeneration 6 , 7 . Beyond potential direct neurotoxic effects, air pollutants may also increase dementia risk via cardiovascular and cardiometabolic pathways; long‐term exposure to air pollutants is linked to hypertension, stroke, ischemic heart disease, and diabetes mellitus 8 , which are established risk factors for ADRD 9 , 10 . Evidence for mediation, however, remains inconsistent 11 , 12 .

While much is already known about the air pollution–AD/ADRD relationship, and association between air pollution from particulate matter with diameter < 2.5 µg/m3 (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and dementia is now considered well established 2 , experts consulted by the GECC agreed on the need to refine future research to better characterize pollutant sources, evaluate exposure mixtures of different air pollutants, and to differentiate between particulate matter size fractions and components (e.g., ultrafine particles, wildfire smoke, exhaust‐ and non‐exhaust emissions, metals) most relevant to neurocognitive outcomes and/or specific populations of interest. Gaps also persist in historical exposure estimations that have to date prevented a proper understanding of the contributions of exposures earlier in the life course, which are likely important given the long preclinical stage of AD/ADRD. Finally, although PM2.5 and NO2 have been the most studied air pollutants and several geospatial model prediction datasets are available, workshop participants noted a lack of clear guidance on dataset selection for specific research questions, underscoring the need for consensus on which models are most appropriate across research contexts.

Several high‐quality air pollution datasets are already widely used in epidemiologic studies of AD and other chronic outcomes, including gridded or address‐linked estimates of PM2.5, NO2, and ozone generated using satellite remote sensing, land‐use regression, and chemical transport models 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 . In addition to making high‐quality air pollution data available, a key priority should be the curation of source‐resolved exposure metrics that distinguish contributions of traffic, including both non‐exhaust and exhaust traffic contribution, wildfire smoke, and other major air pollutant sources. Efforts should also prioritize the development of multipollutant approaches and accompanying guidance to better reflect real‐world air pollutant mixtures and move beyond reductionist approaches that focus on a single air pollutant at a time. Such work could help clarify source‐specific contributions and the relative toxicity of pollutants and pollutant mixtures in dementia etiology and better inform protective policies. Given its increasing contribution to ambient PM2.5 in many regions 19 and the uncertain long‐term implications of repeated smoke episodes for dementia and cognitive decline, wildfire smoke is an emerging exposure for which metrics capturing frequency, duration, intensity, and composition should be considered 20 . Finally, guidance on dataset selection, when multiple exposure surfaces exist for common pollutants (PM2.5 and NO2), should be developed to promote consistent use of the high‐quality measures to facilitate comparisons of findings across studies.

Noise and soundscapes

Noise exposure also emerged as a priority for the physical environment domain in the expert workshops, albeit less frequently during the town halls. Environmental noise encompasses chronic and acute exposure to noise from transportation (road, rail, aircraft) and other community sources such as construction sites, industrial operations, and recreational venues (e.g., concert and sporting venues), as well as occupational noise exposures. Pathways of interest include sleep disturbance, stress response, and annoyance, that can in turn adversely affect psychosocial well‐being and increase risk of cardiovascular disease, hearing loss, and direct neurophysiological responses, all relevant for AD/ADRD pathology. In fact, the link between environmental noise and cardiometabolic disease is well established 21 , and emerging evidence suggests associations of transportation noise with dementia, even after controlling for other physical environment exposures such as greenspace and air pollution 22 , 23 . Experimental models also suggest that noise may promote AD/ADRD‐relevant neuropathology 24 , 25 .

Most of the research on health effects of noise comes from Europe, due to availability of good noise exposure models that have been linked to cohort data. This research demonstrates that it is a highly prevalent exposure in urban areas, with a recent review by the European Environment Agency (EEA) summarizing the evidence on health effects of environmental noise concluding that ≈ 17 million Europeans experience long‐term annoyance due to noise from transport and ≈ 4.6 million suffer from severe sleep disturbances 26 . Despite this evidence, noise exposure data remain sparse in other regions including North America, where there is limited temporal and geographic coverage, simplistic or inconsistent characterization of daytime/nighttime noise, and weak cross‐regional harmonization. Existing health studies predominantly use commonly available metrics—such as the continuous equivalent sound level (LAeq) averaged over a 24‐hour time period—that simplify complex acoustic profiles. There is also a lack of characterization of soundscapes that may be beneficial for health, and there is minimal information on indoor noise exposure that may be most important for health.

To address these gaps, there are several key priorities, including the development of broader, harmonized noise exposure data. Guidance for the field should help researchers align noise exposure metrics and study design with hypothesized pathways linking noise to AD/ADRD and emphasize fit‐for‐purpose measures. To avoid over‐reliance on any single indicator, epidemiologic analyses should leverage multiple complementary noise metrics that capture distinct pathways of exposure. In addition, because chronic nocturnal noise may be particularly consequential for brain health 27 , studies should routinely differentiate between daytime and nighttime conditions when investigating noise–AD/ADRD outcomes. Finally, it may be valuable to expand metrics beyond harmful exposures to also consider potentially beneficial auditory environments (e.g., natural or restorative sounds) or soundscapes. Emerging evidence suggests that exposure to natural sounds reduces physiological stress and perceived annoyance and may improve mood, cognitive performance, and other health outcomes 28 , but the strength and generalizability of these associations remain uncertain.

Greenspace

Greenspace encompasses vegetated environments such as parks, street trees, forests, gardens, and natural areas in and around places where people live, work, and socialize. Regular interaction with or visibility of greenspaces has been associated with improved cognitive function, slower cognitive decline, and lower risk of cognitive impairment and dementia 29 , 30 , 31 . Despite these observed associations based upon simple greenspace exposure proxies, a major gap is the limited evidence identifying the pathways by which greenspace influences dementia outcomes and which exposure proxies best represent such pathways. Plausible means by which greenspace influences AD/ADRD include stress reduction, improved mental health, promotion of physical activity, increased social engagement, and mitigation or reduction of environmental hazards (e.g., cooling during heat, absence of pollution sources) 32 . Some pathways are “active,”; that is, for the hypothesized beneficial exposure to occur the individual must interact with the greenspace (e.g., visit a park and be physically or socially active). Others are “passive,” in which case the presence of greenspace within some proximity of an individual's living environment (i.e., their residence) confers the benefit (e.g., reduction of heat). As such, benefits likely depend on geographic context (e.g., cities, suburbs, rural communities) and accessibility. Similarly, sustained, routine contact with green environments is likely beneficial for health, but exposure to greenspace during sensitive periods (e.g., mid‐life and older age) may be particularly relevant for cognitive health 30 , 33 , 34 .

The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), the most commonly used proxy or metric to estimate green space exposure in epidemiologic studies, provides wide geographic coverage and comparability across studies by estimating vegetation density values from satellite image pixels. In health studies, this type of greenspace metric is typically assigned to a subject's residential location and aggregated within a defined buffer (e.g., percentage of tree canopy within 300 m, parks within 600 m). However, NDVI and buffered greenspace estimates are not pathway aligned; they do not capture type, quality, accessibility, or actual use of vegetated spaces, and may therefore inadequately represent proximal processes (e.g., stress reduction, social interaction, physical activity, hazard mitigation) that link greenspace to dementia outcomes.

A key priority should be the development of guidance on pathway‐relevant greenspace indicators that capture different functions of vegetated areas in cognitive health, including opportunities for physical activity and social interaction, stress reduction, and absence of co‐exposures/emissions (heat, noise and air pollution) 35 . This effort should emphasize metrics with appropriate spatial and temporal resolution appropriate for both rural and urban areas and leverage freely available datasets on land cover and park locations to derive greenspace accessibility, tree canopy cover (and where available, canopy height/quality) to derive greenspace availability, and street‐level imagery to derive greenspace visibility. Spatial specification should be tailored to the exposure construct and hypothesized functions and processes; for instance, simple radial (straight‐line) buffers may better reflect availability and passive pathways (e.g., heat mitigation), whereas network‐based buffers that follow roads and walkways more closely represent accessibility and behaviorally active pathways (e.g., physical activity). Finally, priority should be placed on indicators that capture the quality and use of greenspace as well as the frequency, duration, timing, and context of exposure, while acknowledging that long‐term (multi‐decade) exposure is likely most relevant for AD/ADRD outcomes.

Community amenities and urban form

The community amenities and urban form theme captures the presence, accessibility, and usability of neighborhood destinations and the design of the public realm (street network, land‐use mix, and mobility infrastructure). Examples of this include housing, public transit, bike lanes, public toilets, sidewalks/benches, or public gathering and brain health‐promoting spaces such as libraries and educational centers. These features shape opportunities for physical activity, social engagement, cognitive stimulation, stress relief, and access to essential services, and may also modify co‐exposures such as ambient air pollution and noise. Thus, they are increasingly considered by studies focused on physical environment risk factors for AD/ADRD.

In aging populations, age‐friendly physical environments, as outlined in the World Health Organization Age‐friendly Cities framework, emphasize neighborhood design and retrofits (e.g., barrier‐free access, safe crossings, lighting, shade, benches, toilets, sheltered transit) that make exercising and socializing accessible and safe for older adults 36 . The concept of “cognability” provides a complementary lens for assessing how well a neighborhood in which a person resides supports cognitive health and for identifying specific neighborhood features that influence cognitive function 37 . Applications in cohort settings have shown that community features such as civic and social organizations, recreation centers, coffee shops, restaurants, arts centers, and museums are linked to better cognitive outcomes in older adults 37 . In this context, modifiable neighborhood design and amenities provide the critical context that enables AD/ADRD‐relevant behaviors that confer benefits. Despite its relevance, measures of community amenities are rarely harmonized across large geographies, often lack information on accessibility and quality (i.e., beyond simple presence), and rarely account for activity spaces, cultural variation, or life‐course timing.

To address these gaps, key priorities should include the identification and development of community amenities and urban form metrics that (1) characterize the presence, accessibility/affordability, and quality of amenities within relevant activity spaces; (2) capture behavior‐mediated potential for use of such amenities; and (3) where possible, ensure compatibility of measures over large geographic areas, including for both urban and rural locales. Future measures of these exposures should aim to characterize neighborhoods that provide cognitive stimulation via destinations, opportunities for social connection, and enable supportive, walkable mobility. Practically, large open‐source geospatial points‐of‐interest databases can be leveraged to derive composite indices of community destinations coupled with accessibility measures (e.g., travel time by foot or transit and service frequency) to these destinations. These types of data are available over large geographic areas and can therefore allow for the production of metrics that are harmonized across many different locations within North America and internationally. Evaluating approaches to derive public‐space quality indicators (e.g., shade, seating, perceived safety, visual clutter) consistently across different geographic locations should also be explored. Methodologically, research should develop activity‐space metrics that leverage mobility data from mobile devices, and context‐sensitive constructs to test the generalizability of early evidence linking community amenities and urban form to AD/ADRD across populations and settings. This should include examining how these features vary with climate and geographical or cultural norms (e.g., urban vs. rural, differences between broader regions or countries). As with other physical environment exposures, explicit consideration of temporality aligning exposure windows with AD/ADRD‐relevant pathways should be prioritized.

Neurotoxic chemicals

Although discussed less frequently in consultations, neurotoxic chemicals remain important and under‐investigated environmental risk factors for AD/ADRD. Living near agriculture, industrial or legacy sites, and high‐traffic infrastructure can lead to higher neurotoxic chemical exposure burdens. Multiple exposure routes—air, drinking water, household dust/soil, and food—often operate simultaneously, making exposure attribution a complex endeavor. Internal biomarkers are often the most direct indicators of neurotoxic chemical dose, but they are typically available only in smaller samples and over limited time windows. Complementary geospatial approaches can map source‐related gradients for toxic emissions 38 , 39 , identify potential hotspots, and inform interventions and policy. Biomarker‐only designs, by contrast, have limited ability to implicate specific sources of public concern.

Studies have shown that exposure to chemicals—especially certain pesticides and metals—is associated with AD 40 . A meta‐analysis of seven studies reported a positive association between pesticide exposure and incidence of AD/ADRD 41 . A case–control study also showed that higher levels of serum dichlorodiphenyldichloroethylene (DDE), a persistent metabolite of the organochlorine pesticide dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT), were associated with increased AD risk, and experimental work showed that both DDE and DDT increased levels of amyloid precursor protein, lending mechanistic plausibility to these associations 42 . Metals show more mixed findings overall 40 , though a recent cohort study shows associations between levels of different urinary metals (arsenic, cadmium, cobalt, copper, tungsten, uranium, and zinc) with poorer cognitive performance and higher dementia risk 43 . Lead is a well‐established neurotoxin 44 , and results from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) and US Department of Veterans Affairs Normative Aging Study show bone lead levels are negatively associated with cognitive scores 45 , 46 . An important limitation across the chemicals literature is the relatively small size and short follow‐up of studies, which contribute to inconsistent results and limits causal inference. A further challenge is that the proliferation and persistence of potentially neurotoxic organic chemicals has outpaced toxicological and epidemiologic evaluation.

To complement biomarker‐based studies in smaller epidemiologic studies, geospatial exposure estimation for neurotoxic chemicals linked to large population cohorts and longer follow‐up is recommended. For example, publicly available datasets can be leveraged to construct longitudinal indicators of pesticide exposure 47 (e.g., crop/pesticide/land use–based models, application registries, drinking water contaminant data) and area‐level proxies for metals exposure (e.g., groundwater arsenic maps, private wells for drinking water, emissions inventories) at regional and national scales. Recent studies have combined land‐use data with pesticide sales and application information to show spatio‐temporal trends of pesticide use and identify populations at risk for non‐occupational pesticide exposure 48 , 49 . In the United States, a study developed a pesticides exposures map using pesticide application rates and the US Department of Agriculture cropland data layer 50 . In California and Arizona, state‐mandated pesticide use reporting by applicators has been integrated with agricultural land use data to generate pesticide‐specific and temporarily well‐defined exposure maps that cover many decades 51 , 52 . At the global scale, the PEST‐CHEMGRIDS dataset provides gridded pesticide application rates for major crops and pesticide groups at a global scale 47 . When more local grids are required, a local pesticides exposures map can be developed using locally relevant data layers. However, in practice, data inputs to build such datasets (e.g., agricultural land use information, pesticide use/sales reporting) remain sparse in some regions and coverage is uneven over time; therefore, uncertainty should be clearly described and estimates refined as new data become available. Complementary evidence from smaller biomarker‐based studies and larger studies relying on geospatial chemical exposure modeling based on detailed emissions or pesticide application data can help clarify which chemicals and pathways are most relevant to AD/ADRD etiology and assist in identifying exposure sources, thereby supporting policy‐relevant risk assessment and prevention strategies.

Methodological imperatives for physical environment–ADRD research

A list of priority physical environment metrics for AD/ADRD research begets a need to clearly map the pathways linking exposures, biological and pathophysiological responses, and ensuing neurocognitive outcomes. With the exception of air pollution, many of the physical environment exposures linked to dementia have been operationalized with broad proxy measures rather than with metrics that explicitly capture the ways an exposure exerts its effect; that is, the proximal processes through which the physical environment influences cognitive health. For example, while there is growing evidence that “greenspace” is associated with lower risk of developing AD/ADRD, it is still unclear what forms of exposure or aspects of an individual's interaction with greenspace are most critical for cognitive health. Without a better understanding of proximal processes linking the physical environment exposures to dementia, both causal interpretation of observed associations and the design and implementation of effective interventions to reduce risk are constrained.

The GECC consultations with the scientific community also highlighted the need to explicitly address temporality (e.g., timing, duration, frequency) of exposures when examining the impact of the physical environment on AD/ADRD risk. Pathological changes associated with dementia have been shown to occur more than a decade prior to the emergence of clinical disease symptoms 53 , 54 . However, substantial work is still needed to pinpoint sensitive life‐course windows during which environmental exposures may trigger or accelerate cognitive decline. Adequate temporal resolution and extended historical coverage of physical environment measures—paired with temporally resolved participant residential location information over the life course—would help provide a more complete picture of how the physical environment influences AD/ADRD risk and progression. In addition, there may be even more granular time periods of added importance for certain exposures. For instance, to assess noise‐related sleep disruption and its role in cognitive health, studies should prioritize night time–focused noise metrics that can capture intensity, frequency, and intermittency of noise events 55 .

To move the field forward toward actionable science, there is also a need for greater focus on how behavioral patterns and indoor environments influence an individual's exposure to environmental stressors in the physical environment. This can relate not only to how people engage with local greenspace or benefit from community amenities, but also whether they are exposed within their work, home, and recreational environments to air pollution, noise, chemicals, and/or heat. However, accurately assessing individual exposures as a result of behavior and indoor environments can be challenging from a practical standpoint. In terms of epidemiologic measurements, such integration may require well‐resolved mobility data (e.g., individual‐level activity spaces, time–activity patterns) coupled with the added explanatory value of context‐specific behavior, yet dependence on behavior varies by exposure. For greenspace, some effects can be passive—for example, visual exposure leading to stress reduction—whereas others are active and depend on engagement, such as physical activity or social interaction in parks. Relatedly, the indoor environment can be challenging to estimate with predictors varying by exposure type as well as the presence or absence of indoor sources. Where feasible, more pathway‐relevant metrics can complement existing proxy measures by refining exposure assessment and informing clinical and policy strategies to reduce AD/ADRD risk.

A further methodologic goal will be to enable analyses which treat exposures as co‐occurring systems that collectively represent a person's exposome, rather than isolated factors. Physical environment features (e.g., traffic‐related air pollution, community amenities, greenspaces and noise) frequently cluster and also interact with broader external exposome domains such as education, socioeconomic conditions, social connectedness, and community services. This co‐occurrence raises issues of confounding, mediation, and interaction that single‐exposure analyses cannot resolve. Importantly, these co‐exposures also create trade‐offs (e.g., higher density of amenities may coincide with less greenspace and more traffic‐related air pollution/noise) that are challenging to optimize toward reduced AD/ADRD burden. Progress therefore requires harmonized, geospatially linkable, and time‐resolved measures across domains, aligned to common spatial units and life‐course windows, so that multiple exposures can be modeled jointly to allow assessment of their cumulative direct, indirect, and modifying effects. Further, development of multi‐exposure analysis tools such as cluster analysis or environmental scoring systems that combine different elements of the environment that act on brain health will enable more holistic understanding of physical environment impacts on ADRD. Linking multiple exposure datasets to longitudinal cohorts and administrative health records will allow better disentangling of causal factors and quantification of cumulative burdens, and can help inform interventions that improve brain health.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

Understanding how the physical environment where a person lives shapes AD/ADRD risk across the life course is a critical frontier for aging research. Building on the GECC's consensus process, our goal is to enable rigorous, pathway‐informed studies by delivering guidance and data resources to support the use of key geospatial physical environment measures relevant to AD/ADRD incidence and progression.

Figure 1 situates the physical environment within GECC's external exposome frame and summarizes the priority exposure areas and methodological imperatives that structure our roadmap for integrating place‐based measures into AD/ADRD research. This roadmap prioritizes physical environment metrics that focus on proximal pathways, address temporality and behavioral factors and emphasizes the importance of integrating multiple co‐exposures in analyses. The roadmap emphasizes the need for source‐resolved air quality data; harmonized noise models that distinguish day–night conditions and that capture intensity, frequency, and intermittency of noise; greenspace indicators that capture a range of characteristics relevant to AD/ADRD research; and community amenity and urban form indicators that characterize how living environments can promote cognitive stimulation, social connections, and active mobility.

In a first phase, the GECC will seek to provide practical guidance for using existing (secondary) linkable datasets. In addition, decision aids on dataset selection, fit‐for‐purpose temporal and spatial resolutions for specific AD/ADRD research questions, and the suitability of different datasets for pathway‐informed hypotheses will be developed. Science‐ready data will also be shared. Over the longer term, the GECC is working toward developing novel datasets and guidance to help fill key knowledge gaps. As appropriate, the GECC will leverage the expertise across domains to advance measures that are at the intersection of multiple domains such as greenspace for heat reduction (interfacing with extreme weather experts), occupational experiences and exposures (interfacing with life experience and social environment experts), and community amenities and urban form (interfacing with the community services, policy, and social environment experts). This integrative approach ensures that the exposures defined by the GECC are informed by multiple disciplinary perspectives.

All GECC materials will align with open science and FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) principles 56 : wherever feasible, data will be publicly available; measures will use standardized definitions and transparent processing pipelines; and will include sufficiently detailed metadata and provenance to enable reproducibility and cross‐study comparability. Interoperability and reusability—within the physical environment domain and across all GECC domains—will be supported through harmonized spatial and temporal specifications, clear uncertainty documentation, and versioning. This cross‐domain alignment will facilitate linkage and joint analysis of multiple exposures with health databases. Finally, we will emphasize the generation of and guidance around measures that have clear policy relevance; prioritizing indicators that are modifiable, interpretable by decision makers, actionable for evaluation of interventions, and equitable in their application across diverse populations and settings.

Together, these steps, which foster consistent, well documented data and guidelines available to all researchers, are intended to accelerate discovery, promote high‐quality science, and facilitate comparability across studies on how physical environments influence cognitive aging. In doing so, we aim to support the scientific community to generate evidence that informs prevention strategies and interventions. Ultimately, GECC aims to provide a clear framework to reduce dementia risk and to improve brain health across the life course, aligning with the 2024 Lancet Commission's call to address modifiable factors and strengthen prevention in the face of limited treatment efficacy and the rising global burden of dementia.
