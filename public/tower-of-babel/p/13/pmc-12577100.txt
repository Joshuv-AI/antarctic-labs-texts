Core needs screening items for informal caregivers of older adults: an online modified Delphi study
Authors: Vivian W. Q. Lou, Dara Kiu Yi Leung, Shan Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdentifying the needs and risks of family caregivers for older adults can enable timely recognition and support for informal caregivers and older adults. However, the literature highlights substantial variation in how caregiver needs and risks are assessed. This study aimed to identify a set of core items to be included in a needs screening for informal caregivers of older adults through a modified Delphi approach.MethodsThe Delphi study engaged an expert panel in Hong Kong comprising three stakeholder groups: service providers, policymakers/think tanks, and academics. We started by identifying items reflecting caregiver needs through literature review and item generation meeting. The consensus-building process included two rounds of surveys where panel members rated the importance of items, followed by a feedback meeting. Panel members could suggest additional items in the first survey round, which were reviewed by an external panel of social workers before being added to the second survey round. Consensus was reached with ≥ 70% agreement. Informal caregivers were invited to review the items derived from the Delphi study to ensure comprehensibility.ResultsForty panel members joined the item generationmeeting, 40 completed the round 1 survey, 43 completed the round 2 survey, and 20 joined the feedback meeting. Ten informal Caregivers evaluated the face and content validity of the items. The total number of items in the final round dropped from 62 to 37. There were 27 caregiver-related items (e.g., care burden, caregiving self-efficacy, family relationship, social support, financial and job conflicts, physical health, mental health, and service utilization), six care recipient-related items (e.g., care recipient’s characteristics), and four care dyad-related items (e.g., care dyad relationship and physical environment). Consensus was achieved for 35 items in the surveys and two items in the feedback meeting.ConclusionsThis set of core needs screening items can potentially reduce the heterogeneity of needs and risk assessments across studies and clinical practices and inform the development of a standardized need screening tool.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06443-1.

Introduction

Family Caregivers are crucial in caring for the rapidly growing aging population. Approximately 27% of older adults require assistance with their daily living around the globe [1]. In Hong Kong, the number increases to over 50% among people aged 80 years or above [2]. These caregiving tasks range from assisting with daily living activities, emotional support and companionship, medication management, and complex medical device operations [3–5]. While caregiving can bring positive outcomes, such as a positive life orientation and a sense of capability [6], it can also pose risks (e.g., caregiving burdens) to adverse health outcomes (e.g., depressive symptoms) [7–12]. The Informal Caregiving Integrative Model (ICIM; Fig. 1) [13] has illustrated that the caregiving experience is shaped by the caregivers’ psychosocial characteristics, caregiving context (e.g., primary and secondary stressors and care recipient’s specific needs), and social environment, with caregivers’ appraisal of their situation and their relationship quality with the care recipients playing key mediating roles.

Despite the growing interest in supporting informal caregivers in healthcare settings, there is a lack of practical interventions for evaluating informal caregivers’ needs and risks [14]. In research, scholars have emphasized different outcomes using different standardized measures. For example, the Neuropsychiatric Inventory measures caregivers’ distress caused by care recipients’ neuropsychiatric symptoms [15], whereas the Caregiver Burden Inventory Korean version measures the negative changes in the relationship between the caregiver and care recipient [16]. The Zarit Burden Inventory, while being widely used, focuses heavily on emotional strain and may not adequately capture physical (e.g. caregiver’s health status), relational (e.g., care dyad relationship), or practical challenges (e.g., time commitment) [17]. The current landscape lacks a single comprehensive caregiver assessment tool that covers all items outlined in the ICIM. While consolidating existing assessments could potentially address this gap, it may lead to a lengthy assessment that impedes practitioners from evaluating informal caregivers’ needs and risks promptly and efficiently. Furthermore, some items may be more suitable for initial screening, whereas others may be better suited for follow-up assessment. It is imperative to seek input from stakeholders on which items are most effective for timely screening purposes.

Healthcare professionals, including doctors, nurses, social workers, and family caregivers have advocated for a brief, standardized needs assessment to recognize informal caregivers’ efforts, facilitate clinician-caregiver communication, and improve patient care [14]. Caregiver-centered care and interprofessional collaboration are widely accepted as essential components for providing efficient, safe, and quality care to patients and informal caregivers with complex needs [18]. However, challenges arise due to professionals’ differing knowledge, identities, and routines [19]. Therefore, the assessment should be designed to include multidimensional items that are most effective in identifying potential risks that may impact informal caregivers’ well-being and resilience and patient’s care plan, and guiding referrals across disciplines and healthcare settings [14, 18]. Furthermore, a self-administered approach could ensure accuracy and honesty in the assessment and resolve the time constraints of healthcare professionals [14].

This study aimed to identify a set of core needs and risks that should be included in a screening tool for family caregivers of older adults through a modified Delphi approach. The Delphi method is a widely used approach in healthcare research to identify concepts, essential outcomes, and future directions [20–22]. It involves a series of structured rounds of anonymous feedback from experts in the field, with the aim of reaching a consensus on the best practices and guidelines for a particular issue [23]. The Delphi method is ideal for our study because it allows us to gather input from a diverse group of experts and stakeholders in the field of caregiving. Additionally, the study focused on ensuring the screening tools’ user-friendliness for self-administration by discussing with informal caregivers of older adults. The ultimate goal is to create a content-valid, evidence-based needs screening tool for family caregivers of older adults, applicable at any stage of their caregiving journey and across various healthcare disciplines and settings. Its quality would align with current consensus guidelines on caregiver assessment [24].

Methods

Study design

This study is part of the Jockey Club (JC) Carer Space Project [25]. The project aims to pilot-test a caregiver-centric service model to provide and connect informal caregivers to timely and appropriate services in the community. We used a modified Delphi method [26] consisting of three stages to identify the essential screening items, followed by a face and content validity evaluation. First, we identified items related to caregiving impact and modifiable intervention outcomes through a literature review and an item generation meeting with the Delphi expert panel. Second, the panel members completed a two-round Delphi study. We anticipated additional items from the panel after the first round of Delphi and invited a group of social workers (external panel), who were not involved in the survey, to review and streamline the number of additional items involved in the second round. Third, we held a feedback meeting with the Delphi expert panel to achieve consensus on the dimensions and items to be included in the core screening set for needs screening. Finally, we evaluated the face and content validity of the screening items with family Caregivers of older adults. The study took place in Hong Kong between March and April 2023.

Participant recruitment

We recruited the Delphi expert panel from three stakeholder groups: service providers (e.g., physicians, nurses, and social workers), policymakers/think tanks, and academics. Eligibility included being 18 years or above, with at least five years of work experience in caregiver-related fields, and being able to read and communicate in Chinese. These experts were included to ensure the relevance of identified items to healthcare practices and policies, and to provide a comprehensive perspective that integrates caregiver needs identified in research. An invitation letter was sent to the researchers’ existing network through email or WhatsApp, which included the schedule for the item generation meeting, two rounds of the Delphi survey, the feedback meeting, and the registration links for the meetings. The experts confirmed their participation through email or WhatsApp. Informed consent was obtained at either the item generation meeting or Round 1 Delphi survey, depending on the stage at which the expert first joined. We recruited social workers from the JC Carer Space Project, who had experience working with informal Caregivers, to review the additional items proposed from the Round 1 Delphi survey. Family caregivers of older adults were recruited from community centers involved in the JC Carer Space Project. We informed five social workers that the project was recruiting informal Caregivers of older adults to provide feedback on a caregiver needs screening tool, and they referred eligible and interested informal caregivers to us. Eligibility included being over 18 years of age, a family caregiver providing over six hours of care to an older person per week, and able to communicate in Chinese.

Stage 1: generation of screening items

The objective of this stage was to generate a comprehensive list of screening items that can address the diverse needs and risks faced by informal caregivers. We first assessed a Locally developed 11-item caregiver risk screening tool [27] against the ICIM [13]. Despite its brevity being an advantage, it lacked validation and did not capture risks related to care recipients’ conditions and relationships between informal caregivers and care recipients, which are critical factors contributing to caregiver burnout. To address this, we expanded the list with additional informal caregivers’ risks and needs identified from systematic reviews [7–11] and an item generation meeting with the Delphi expert panel.

The item generation meeting was held online on March 29, 2023 and focused on three questions: (1) Who are the “needy” informal caregivers (2)?, Which are the three types of informal caregivers whose health would be negatively affected without timely social and health intervention? and (3) To what extent do informal caregivers with different levels of risk and preparedness need support? Participants’ answers were captured using Mentimeter, an interactive presentation software that allows the audience to provide instant feedback to questions displayed during a presentation. The item generation meeting was recorded and distributed to absent panel members to ensure their understanding and consistent progress among all panel members.

Following the literature review and item generation meeting, DKYL and a research assistant listed all outcomes in an Excel spreadsheet. Similar outcomes, such as sleep disturbances and insomnia, were consolidated into a single outcome, such as sleep problems. This process led to a refined list of distinct caregiver needs and risk items.

Stage 2: Delphi survey

Round 1

The list of informal caregivers’ needs and risk items generated from Stage 1 was presented to the expert panel using Qualtrics on April 3, 2023. Following the Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation working group for assessing the level of importance of research evidence [28], panel members were asked to rate the importance of each item on a 9-point Likert scale. Ratings were grouped into three categories: “of limited importance” (ratings of 1 to 3), “important but not critical for inclusion” (ratings of 4 to 6), and “critical for inclusion” (ratings of 7 to 9). Panel members were invited to suggest any other items deemed important but not captured. They were sent three email reminders to complete Round 1. Consensus was reached when at least 70% of participants rated the items as critical for inclusion.

There is no definite way to handle additional items; some studies included all additional items proposed by the expert panel [29] and others included items that were proposed by more than two experts [30]. However, the former may result in a large number of additional items in the next round, whereas the latter may exclude some important items that are not raised by multiple experts as participants may not always provide free text responses. To address this, we invited social workers from the JC Carer Space project, who were experienced in caregiver service, to form an external panel to review the additional items proposed by the Delphi expert panel. They were briefed on the study’s objective during a regular operational meeting and instructed to vote to either ‘include’ or ‘exclude’ each item in an anonymous online polling on Qualtrics. The additional items proceeded to Round 2 if they received 70% or higher agreement for inclusion. The cut-off value of 70% was selected based on existing recommendations [28]. This approach of handling additional items significantly reduced the number of items to be rated in the next round, saving the experts’ time and preventing selection bias regarding the items.

Round 2

Regardless of whether they completed Round 1, all Delphi expert panel members were invited to complete Round 2. Involving panel members who missed a previous round in a later round can better represent the opinions of the originally invited panel and decrease the risk of reaching a false consensus while not affecting the final outcome of the Delphi process [31]. An anonymized summary of Round 1 results was sent to panel members on April 14, 2023. The results included the percentage of selections for scores between 1 and 3, 4 to 6, and 7 to 9, the median, and 25th and 75th centile scores for each item. They were invited to rate the additional items and change their ratings on the initial items as they found appropriate. Since this round of survey was to achieve consensus, no additional items were allowed. The criteria for items considered most important to measure after Round 2 was defined as a priori, 70% and above of all panel members rating an outcome ‘critical’.

Stage 3: feedback meeting

Delphi expert panel members who completed any stage of the study were invited to attend a recorded online feedback meeting on April 26, 2023. An anonymized summary of Round 2 results was sent to participants one day before the meeting. The meeting focused on the items reaching the marginal inclusion threshold (ratings of 7 to 9 by 65–69% of all participants). Participants voted to either ‘include’ or ‘exclude’ each additional item. The items with 70% or greater agreement on inclusion were included in the critical screening items. The meeting was recorded and shared with absent panel members. They were invited to express any reservations they may have had regarding the consensus reached on the item under discussion.

Stage 4: face and content validation with informal caregivers

Informal Caregivers were invited to review the needs screening items derived from the Delphi study on April 27, 2023 . They were presented with the screening items in a tool format. Various rating scales were employed to assess informal caregivers’ responses and determine the most effective and user-friendly scale. These included binary scale (0 = No, 1 = Yes), 4-point Likert scale (0 = Did not apply to me at all to 3 = Applied to me very much, or most of the time), and 5-point Likert scale (0 = Totally disagree/Not at all to 4 = Totally agree/Always). They were asked to complete the screening tool independently and were offered support or clarification if they encountered difficulties understanding any statement. Additionally, they were asked to assess whether these items corresponded to the challenges they encountered in caregiving and the moments when they desired additional support. Considering the caregivers’ busy schedule, priority was given to completing questionnaires and addressing immediate concerns rather than conducting cognitive interviews. Nonetheless, the procedure was designed to promote participant involvement and feedback, contributing to the evaluation of the face and content validity of the screening items.

Results

A total of 65 stakeholders were invited to the Delphi study and 50 of them agreed to join the expert panel. They were from three stakeholder groups: service providers (n = 35), policymakers/think tanks (n = 6), and academics (n = 9). Forty stakeholders (80%) joined the item generation meeting, 40 (80%) completed survey Round 1, 43 (86%) completed survey Round 2, and 20 (40%) joined the feedback meeting. Thirty-nine panel members (78%) completed both rounds of survey. The acceptance and retention rates varied within each group, with no clear pattern indicating that one group consistently had higher or lower acceptance or retention rates compared to the others (Table 1). Stage 1: identification of items

A total of 51 items were identified from the literature review, the Locally developed 11-item caregiver risk screening tool, and the item generation meeting (Supplementary Table 1). Building on the 11-item screening tool, we incorporated additional items related to secondary stressors (e.g., impact on employment), social support, care recipients’ physical functioning (e.g., need of ADL support, presence of dementia), and relationship quality between informal caregivers and care recipients (e.g., conflicts). VWQL and DKYL discussed and grouped these items into three dimensions: caregiver-related concerns, care recipient-related concerns, and care dyad-related concerns, inspired by the ICIM [13]. This categorization approach, instead of detailed components from the ICIM, aimed to simplify the structure and improve comprehension for non-academic audiences.

Stage 2: Delphi study

In Round 1, 26 items achieved consensus (Supplementary Table 1). A further 39 items were suggested by the stakeholders and 15 of them achieved consensus among the external panel (Supplementary Table 2). Four items duplicated with existing items in Round 1 (e.g., caregiver’s age, employment status, and relationship with care recipient) and were therefore removed. One item about mental disorders was split into two separate items to specify whether the caregiver or the care recipient was affected. Two additional items related to relationships with domestic helpers were combined and added to the item pool despite not reaching consensus, as it reflected a unique caregiving culture in Hong Kong. The decision was discussed between VWQL and DKYL. A total of 11 items proceeded to Round 2. In Round 2, 35 out of 62 items achieved consensus to be included in the core needs screening items. Responses from different stakeholder groups are presented in Supplementary Table 3. All items included were rated critical by at least two stakeholder groups. Items related to caregivers that were deemed less important primarily focused on caregiver characteristics (e.g., old age, low education, and being a spousal caregiver), knowledge about caregiving support, and impact of caregiving on employment. Items concerning care recipients that were excluded involved medical and long-term care services arrangement.

Stage 4: face and content validation with informal caregivers

Three participants with Lower education levels required the research team to read aloud the statements to them to aid comprehension. However, all participants were able to independently complete the screening tool by providing their responses without further assistance, implying a high level of comprehension of the statements. Feedback from the participants indicated that the items effectively captured caregiving needs, with no additional items suggested for inclusion in the tool. These findings supported the face and content validity of the screening items in assessing caregiving experiences that could contribute to increased caregiving burnout. Participants expressed a preference for a 4-point Likert scale to rate the applicability of statements over the past week (0 = Did not apply to me at all to 3 = Applied to me very much, or most of the time). This format was deemed suitable for assessing both frequencies of events and intensity of emotions.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify core needs and risks related to Caregivers, care recipients, and the caregiver-recipient dyads for inclusion in a screening tool for informal caregivers of older adults, using a Delphi process. The use of a combination of synchronous and asynchronous online platforms was found to be feasible to include policymakers, think tanks, service providers, and academics in the consensus process. Through the Delphi approach, stakeholders identified 37 core needs items with strong consensus. These items were further validated by informal caregivers of older adults, who found them comprehensible for self-administration. Taken together, these established the content validity of the needs screening tool.

Our Delphi study identified critical items in all three domains: caregivers, care recipients, and their relationships, underscoring the importance of considering both parties’ circumstances when supporting informal caregivers [32]. Although the study was conducted in the Chinese context, the identified screening items demonstrated alignment with international evidence, indicating its potential broader adaptation in other context in future. For example, a systematic review of determinants of caregiving burden in Western countries found that the most important predictors are the duration of caregiving and the care recipient’s dependency level, which often stemmed from care recipient’s cognitive capacity or behavioral problems [33]. In our study, items related to care demand (e.g., providing 24-hour care), care recipients’ functioning abilities and behavioural problems were consistently rated as critical issues by all stakeholder groups. Moreover, caregiver’s loneliness was considered critical, aligning with the growing literature suggesting that loneliness, alongside social isolation or weak social support network, is associated with increased caregiver burden and warrants target intervention [34].

Following the ICIM [13], the tool recognizes caregiving as a dynamic process that involves a complex interplay of factors between the caregiver and care recipient. It addresses caregiver characteristics (e.g., having chronic diseases), primary stressors (e.g., providing 24-hour care), secondary stressors (e.g., balancing work, own family, and caregiving), social environment (e.g., lacking care support from other family members), appraisal (e.g., inability to support care recipient’s activities of daily living or instrumental activities of daily living), care dyad relationship quality (e.g., poor relationship with care recipient), coping (e.g., accepting formal and informal support), and burnout (e.g., feeling depressed). Compared to standardized tools focusing on a specific dimension, this scale consists of simple but comprehensive screening items, allowing for higher time efficiency in practical application. Demographic characteristics of caregivers, such as age, education level, and relation to care recipients, and care arrangement of the care recipients were not recognized as critical screening items by the stakeholders. This may reflect an assumption that support services are accessible to all caregivers regardless of these factors, emphasizing the importance of equitable access to caregiver support for everyone in need.

The ICIM conceptualizes burnout as a tridimensional syndrome in response to caregiving demand, including emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment, However, given the primary goal of identifying unmet needs and risks in informal caregivers, items related to positive impact of caregiving, such as reward and meaning were excluded [7, 10]. Depersonalization, which involves a detached response to the care recipient, did not emerge as a critical caregiving risk in this study. An item, “Caregiver starts to procrastinate and avoid the needs of the care recipient”, was proposed by stakeholders in Round 1 but was rated for inclusion by only 53% of external panel members. Few studies have examined depersonalization in informal caregivers, and among those, depersonalization appears to be only slightly more common than in non-caregivers [35]. This limited and modest evidence may affect the perceived relevance or importance of this construct in screening efforts. Further research is needed to investigate the prevalence, impact, and potential intervention for this underexplored phenomenon in the informal caregiving context [13]. Furthermore, although the significance of a weak social network as a caregiving risk is well-documented, conflicts between informal caregivers and their family members emerged as a contentious issue. This hesitancy may be due to service providers’ reluctance to get involved in complex family matters, which can be time-consuming and may divert attention from their other responsibilities [36]. The inclusion of this item in the feedback meeting, following initial hesitance in the Delphi survey, highlights the necessity for further research to better understand the intricate relationship between family dynamics and social support, both within and outside the family unit.

The strong consensus on mental health problems and care demand in the Delphi study underscored the pressing need for mental health and respite support for informal caregivers. The findings also emphasized the pivotal role of informal caregivers’ willingness to accept support. Merely increasing the availability of support services may not be effective if informal caregivers are not open to receiving help. Therefore, beyond taking a break from caregiving tasks, informal caregivers should learn to prioritize psychological respite by learning effective coping strategies, knowing when and how to ask for help, and processing difficult experiences [37]. Future studies and interventions can consider focusing on modifiable factors, such as self-criticism and self-compassion, that are related to mental health problems, self-care, and help-seeking behaviors [38].

We anticipate further testing of the reliability and validity of the screening tool. While the 37 items were generated through a Delphi process with different stakeholders, the potential impact of a lengthy tool on usability and feasibility in clinical settings is duly noted. Future research could employ item reduction techniques to eliminate redundant items and use predictive modelling to retain only those items that most accurately predict caregiver risk, such as burnout or other negative health consequences. It is essential to engage in discussion with interdisciplinary healthcare professionals to determine how to implement more detailed assessments to develop effective care plans when specific items or combinations of items are identified. This could prevent ineffective collaboration resulting from inadequate awareness about the practice of different disciplines [39]. It incorporates items that can effectively direct referrals, aligning with the recommendation from a prior study [14]. For instance, when a caregiver reports frequent depression symptoms, practitioners may recommend social or psychological services, with further evaluation by social care or mental health professionals to assess symptom severity and the need for mental health services. In cases where informal caregivers experience falls or chronic diseases, practitioners may arrange medical monitoring and instrumental support if their condition substantially impacts their ability to provide care. The screening tool also covers items related to risks that could affect the care plan for the older person [14], such as falls and fecal or urinary incontinence.

Screening tools can help identify at-risk populations to provide timely treatment or support schemes to prevent or lessen symptoms and potential consequences, thereby enhancing their health outcomes in a cost-effective manner [40]. The term “needs screening” was chosen over “risks screening” to reduce negative fearful emotions and stigma associated with identifying potential risks among informal caregivers. It also implies that the focus is on identifying informal caregivers’ needs and providing support, which aligns with how services cater to their needs. By framing the screening in a positive light, informal caregivers may feel more positive about seeking support, ultimately leading to better outcomes for both informal caregivers and care recipients. From a public health perspective, early identification allows timely and appropriate interventions, which can support informal caregivers in continuing to provide care in the community and delay institutionalization [32]. Therefore, the potential benefits of the screening items extend beyond individual informal caregivers to the broader healthcare system.

This study also offers methodological insights for future Delphi studies. We created an effective synchronous and asynchronous communication platform with the expert panel. Item generation meeting and feedback meeting allowed for real-time feedback, whereas meeting recordings allowed experts to revisit the discussion at their convenience and provide additional feedback when necessary. This allows the engagement with geographically, time zone-diverse experts in the Delphi process. Our study diverged from the traditional Delphi method by involving extra experts to assess proposed additional items to be included in the subsequent round. This contrasts with the usual practice of relying solely on the core panel for item selection. This alternative way of handling additional items could mitigate selection bias concerning the items and reduce the number of items to be rated, saving experts’ time.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. We invited fewer stakeholders from policymakers/think tanks, medical professionals, and academics in the Delphi process, potentially steering the findings towards a social care perspective. This reflects the caregiving landscape in Hong Kong, where support is primarily delivered through social welfare services, such as carer allowances, respite care, and training provided by community centres, and policy and healthcare responses to informal caregiving are still in early development [41]. As such, the generalisability of the findings may be limited to the local context. However, the study’s findings are supported by cross-sector validation. Items identified as critical by social service providers were also endorsed by at least one other stakeholder group, suggesting a broader consensus beyond a single-sector viewpoint. This aligns with the global trend toward interprofessional collaboration in caregiver support [18]. Cross-cultural validation studies could be undertaken to assess the tool’s feasibility and psychometric properties across diverse cultural and healthcare system contexts. Engaging local professionals in each setting will be critical not only to ensure cultural relevance and practical implementation, but also to guide the development of timely, context-specific interventions.

Another limitation was recruiting stakeholders through the researcher’s network, which could introduce bias in the study outcomes, despite improving the response rate. There was also attrition bias across the Delphi round. We mitigated the bias by including stakeholders who did not join Round 1 survey in Round 2 survey, but there was a 68% attrition rate in the feedback meeting. Attrition is, however, common in Delphi study and our final sample size is generally considered to be sufficient in consensus building [29]. Furthermore, although anonymity is a crucial aspect of the Delphi method, participants in the study were not anonymous to each other during the online meetings. However, they remained unaware of each other’s individual responses in the Delphi rounds to mitigate any potential biases or influences that may have emerged. Finally, there is a limited presentation of informal Caregivers in our study. While 10 informal caregivers were given the opportunity to review the items derived from the Delphi study, their absence as experts during the Delphi process represents a missed opportunity in developing a screening tool specific to this population.

Conclusion

This modified Delphi study identified 37 items that should be included in a needs screening in informal caregivers of older adults. This needs screening tool is designed for informal caregivers at any stage of their caregiving journey and across healthcare disciplines and settings. The items identified by policymakers/think tanks, healthcare professionals, and informal caregivers were grouped into three dimensions: caregiver-related, care recipient-related, and care dyad-related. With the content validity established, future work can examine the psychometric properties of the needs screening tool. A combination of the synchronous and asynchronous online format of stakeholder engagement also provides methodological insights for future Delphi studies.
