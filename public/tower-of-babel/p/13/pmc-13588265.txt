Epigenome-wide association analysis of prenatal organophosphate ester flame retardant exposure in maternal blood implicates maternal immune and neuronal epigenetic alterations
Authors: Wanyi Tang, Helen Foley, Xiaoran Yang, Sahra Mohazzab-Hosseinian, Tingyu Yang, Ixel Hernandez-Castro, Kurunthachalam Kannan, Yijin Xiang, Huaiyu Mi, Joseph Wiemels, Juan Pablo Lewinger, Kimberly Siegmund, Theresa Bastain, Carrie Breton, Chang Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Organophosphate esters (OPEs) are widely used flame retardants and plasticizers prevalent in indoor air, dust, and water. Studies suggest prenatal OPE exposure is linked to adverse maternal and infant outcomes, but the biological mechanisms remain unclear.Methods:We examined associations between nine urinary OPE metabolites measured in the third trimester and DNA methylation in maternal blood from 175 pregnant women in the MADRES cohort. 837,856 CpGs were profiled using the Illumina EPIC array. We assessed the effect of each OPE (in tertile or detection status) and the joint effect of OPE mixtures using quantile g-computation models on DNA methylation sites, regions, and epigenetic age acceleration (EAA). Functional enrichment analyses were performed for suggestive CpGs and DMRs.Results:We identified cg09294299 (P = 2.33 × 10−8) and cg13756168 (P = 3.94 × 10−8; ALS2) as epigenome-wide significant CpGs associated with BCEP and BDCIPP, respectively. Mixtures of OPEs also had joint effects on these two CpGs and three additional CpGs near PPP1R12A, AZI1 and ZDHHC11 (P < 0.05). We identified six differentially methylated regions (DMRs) associated with OPEs: BBOEP and BMPP were both associated with a DMR near RP11–661A12.5, BBOEP with a DMR near NNAT/BLCAP (P = 4.80 × 10−28), BDCIPP with DMRs near SLFN12 (P = 4.63 × 10−14) and ZDHHC11 (P = 1.42 × 10−12), and BEHP with DMRs near POU5F1 (P = 2.74 × 10−20) and PLA2G1B (P = 4.26 × 10−14). OPE mixtures showed a suggestive association with one DMR near POU5F1 (Padj = 0.1193). Functional enrichment analyses implicated neurogenesis, immune signaling, and stress response pathways at the CpG site level, with shared transcription factor enrichment at DMRs across multiple OPEs, including EZH2, EHMT2, and POLR2A. Higher BCEP levels were associated with accelerated aging by DunedinPACE, whereas higher BMPP and BEHP were associated with epigenetic age deceleration by Horvath and Levine clocks, respectively; no significant association with epigenetic age acceleration was found for the OPE mixture.Conclusions:Prenatal OPE exposure is associated with altered maternal DNA methylation and epigenetic aging, highlighting immune, inflammatory, and neurodevelopmental pathways.

Introduction

Organophosphate esters (OPEs) have become the dominant class of flame retardants, with global production surging from 186,000 tons in 2001 to approximately one million tons per year by 2018 (Fu et al., 2023). OPEs are pervasive in daily life and have been detected in indoor air, household dust, water, sediment and soil (Wang et al., 2019). Due to the absence of covalent bonding within the products they incorporated, OPEs are prone to leaching into the surrounding environment. Therefore, humans are continuously exposed through multiple pathways including ingestion, inhalation, and absorption through the skin (Chen et al., 2024; Mäkinen et al., 2009). After exposure, OPEs are metabolized into their corresponding monoesters or diesters, which are primarily excreted in the urine (Hou et al., 2016; Cooper et al., 2011; Van den Eede et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2016). Multiple studies have detected OPEs in human biospecimens in the United States, Canada, Sweden, China, Japan, the Philippines and Vietnam (Zhang et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2014; Sundkvist et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2019; Ospina et al., 2018; Hoffman et al., 2017; Foster et al., 2024). Over 80% of individuals in the United States have measurable levels of at least four OPE metabolites in urine, reported by the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) (Ospina et al., 2018).

OPEs pose potential risks to maternal health, with mounting evidence implicating immune, inflammatory, and neurodevelopment-related adverse outcomes. Mechanistically, OPE exposure induces oxidative stress and DNA damage in human systems, which may activate pregnancy-relevant immune dysregulation and inflammatory signalling (Chen et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2019). Consistent with these mechanisms, mid-gestation cohort data have reported associations between flame retardant exposures and maternal inflammatory biomarkers (Nguyen et al., 2025). A cohort study of pregnant women in Puerto Rico reported positive associations between urinary OPE metabolites and oxidative-stress biomarkers (Ingle et al., 2020). Neurotoxic pathways have also been implicated. Several OPEs can access and disrupt the central nervous system in animal models: triphenyl phosphate (TPP/TPHP) triggers neuronal injury, including apoptosis, whereas tris(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (TDCIPP/TDCPP) alters hippocampal synaptic and oxidative-stress–related protein networks, leading to measurable cognitive deficits in adult rodents (Liu et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2024). These findings in animal models were consistent with epidemiological studies in humans. Higher urinary OPE metabolites were associated with greater depressive symptoms during the prenatal period, and a similar study found that higher OPE concentrations in household dust were associated with elevated maternal depressive symptoms, collectively suggesting maternal neurobehavioral sensitivity to OPEs (Foster et al., 2024; Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024). Moreover, prenatal OPE exposure has been associated with adverse offspring neurodevelopmental outcomes. Emerging human evidence suggests that prenatal OPE exposure may impair early motor development, with greater vulnerability observed among female infants (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024). A recent birth cohort reported that higher prenatal exposure to OPEs was associated with increased autistic traits and greater odds of autism diagnosis in children (Ames et al., 2025). Other studies in children have similarly linked prenatal OPE exposure to poorer cognitive performance and attention deficits (Yao et al., 2021; Vuong et al., 2024; Hall et al., 2023). Consistent associations between OPE exposure and cognitive impairment in elderly populations further support the neurotoxicity of these compounds (Zhao et al., 2022).

Prenatal exposure to OPEs has also been associated with multiple maternal physiological systems, including endocrine, reproductive, vascular and metabolic functions (Yao et al., 2021; Vuong et al., 2024; Hall et al., 2023; Glazer et al., 2018; Hawkey et al., 2023; Chupeau et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024). Evidence from human studies indicates that higher concentrations of OPE metabolites are related to thyroid hormone disruption, with positive associations between specific metabolites and maternal thyroid-stimulating hormone levels (Percy et al., 2021; Tao et al., 2021). An analysis of women undergoing assisted reproductive treatments like in vitro fertilization (IVF) observed an inverse relationship between maternal OPE levels and assisted pregnancy success (Li et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025). These findings aligned with evidence in animal studies that OPEs caused reproductive and endocrine toxicity in zebrafish and rats, including hormone imbalance and reduced fertility (Xu et al., 2017; Li et al., 2025). OPEs may also impact vascularization processes in pregnancy, with some studies examining associations with preeclampsia and blood pressure changes, though results remain mixed (Lueth et al., 2024). OPE exposure has further been linked to metabolic disorders, with epidemiologic studies reporting higher risks of diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, metabolic syndrome, and hyperglycemia (Doherty et al., 2019; Aimuzi et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2020; Lin, et al., 2024; Nan et al., 2024), which was consistently supported by an in vitro study showing that several OPEs impair liver cell function and may disrupt lipid homeostasis in HepG2 cell lines (Yu et al., 2024).

Although the adverse effects of OPE exposure on health have been observed in human and animal studies, the underlying biological mechanisms, particularly maternal epigenetic effects during pregnancy, remain underexplored (Genomic and Epigenomic Biomarkers of Toxicology and Disease, 2022). An animal study showed increased DNA methylation levels at promoter regions of selected key genes associated with prenatal OPE exposure in zebrafish embryos (Li et al., 2020), while a human study suggested that prenatal OPE exposure may alter placental DNA methylation in the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) signaling pathway (Chen et al., 2024). Evidence from two recent birth cohorts indicates that prenatal OPE exposure is associated with altered DNA methylation in cord blood, suggesting possible maternal epigenetic involvement (Schrot et al.t, 2025; Mendy et al., 2025). To address the knowledge gap about the effect of OPE exposure on maternal health, our study conducted an epigenome-wide study (EWAS) examining the relationship between prenatal OPE exposure and DNA methylation (DNAm) in maternal blood among 175 pregnant women during the third trimester. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine OPE-related DNA methylation changes across the maternal epigenome. Here, we aimed to identify differentially methylated CpG sites, regions and biological pathways associated with individual urinary OPE metabolites and their combined mixture.

Methods

Study population

The study population is a subset of the Maternal And Developmental Risks from Environmental and Social Stressors (MADRES) cohort, a prospective pregnancy cohort study in Los Angeles, California as previously described in Bastain et al. (Bastain et al., 2019). Between 2015 and 2023, pregnant participants were recruited from three partner community clinics, an obstetrics and gynecology practice, and self-referrals via local outreach. Participants enrolled at either <20 weeks or 20–30 weeks of gestation, with detailed demographic and clinical data, along with biospecimens, collected up to three times across pregnancy. Postpartum annual follow-up assessments collected longitudinal data on maternal and child health outcomes. Eligible participants were at least 18 years old and fluent in English or Spanish. Exclusion criteria included current incarceration, inability to consent due to mental or physical conditions, multiple gestation or HIV-positive status. All participants provided written informed consent at study recruitment. The study protocol was approved by the University of Southern California’s Institutional Review Board.

Measurement of OPEs in maternal urine samples

As previously described (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024), third-trimester urine and blood samples were collected during study visits at an average gestational age of 31.5 weeks (SD = 2.0). Urine samples were obtained using 90 mL sterile containers, and urine specific gravity was assessed on-site with a handheld refractometer (ATAGO PAL-10S). Specimens were then stored at −80 °C until batch shipment to the Human Health Exposure Analysis Resource at the Wadsworth Center for laboratory analysis.

Nine urinary OPE metabolites were quantified in 426 third-trimester urine samples, one per participant, using established protocols. Methylation data were available for 175 of these participants, who comprised our study sample. OPE metabolites include bis(butoxyethyl) phosphate (BBOEP), bis(2-chloroethyl) phosphate (BCEP), bis(1-chloro-2-propyl) phosphate (BCIPP), bis(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (BDCIPP), bis(2-ethylhexyl) phosphate (BEHP), bis(2-methylphenyl) phosphate (BMPP), di-n-butyl phosphate (DNBP), di-isobutyl phosphate (DIBP), diphenyl phosphate (DPHP), and dipropyl phosphate (DPRP). Measurements were conducted via solid-phase extraction and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; ExionLC™ system, SCIEX, Redwood City, CA) with tandem mass spectrometry (QTRAP 5500+, AB SCIEX, Foster City, CA). Due to co-elution, DNBP and DIBP were reported as combined values. Values below the limit of detection (LOD) were imputed as LOD/√2, and urinary OPE metabolite concentrations were then standardized for specific gravity to account for urine dilution using a previously described formula (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024; Romano et al., 2017).

Given that most OPE urine metabolites were not normally distributed (Fig. S1), we categorized them into tertiles or a binary indicator for detected vs. non-detected using a previously established approach that accounts for detection rates (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024). To enhance comparability with prior studies, exposure categories were defined using reference-based cut-points derived from the full cohort sample (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024). Metabolites detected in more than 80% of samples (DPHP, DNBP + DIBP, BDCIPP) were classified into tertiles (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024). For those with detection rates between 50% and 80% (BCEP, BBOEP, BCIPP), concentrations were grouped as ≤ limit of detection (LOD), below the median, and ≥median (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024). Metabolites detected in fewer than 50% of participants (BMPP, BEHP, DPRP) were dichotomized as non-detected and detected (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024).

DNA methylation measurement and quality control pipeline

Peripheral blood samples (10 mL) were collected in EDTA tubes (BD# 366643) during the third trimester as previously described (Mohazzab-Hosseinian et al., 2024; Mohazzab-Hosseinian et al., 2023). Samples were transported on ice to MADRES laboratory within the Norris Cancer Center, processed within one hour of collection, and centrifuged to separate plasma. DNA was extracted from peripheral blood cells using the AllPrep DNA kit (Qiagen), and plasma aliquots were stored at −20 °C. Bisulfite conversions were performed using the EZ DNAm Kit (Zymo Research) in batches, and DNAm was profiled using the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylationEPIC (850 K) assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol (N = 175).

We followed a standard DNAm preprocessing and quality control protocol as illustrated in Fig. S2 using minfi (version 3.2.1) (Aryee et al., 2014). Initial sample and probe quality were evaluated using detection P-values calculated by the detectionP function (Aryee et al., 2014). A total of 1596 probes with detection P-values >0.01 were removed from the dataset (Fig. S2). Additionally, 26,407 probes identified with cross-reactive mapping issues were excluded based on the list published by Zhou et al while other probes near SNPs were flagged (Zhou et al., 2017). At the sample level, we checked and flagged samples with low median methylated signal intensity, and conducted sex discrepancy checks (Aryee et al., 2014). A total of 837,856 probes and 175 samples were included in the following quality control and downstream analysis (Fig. S2). Background correction and dye-bias normalization were accomplished using preprocessNoob (Triche et al., 2013). To account for population structure and cell heterogeneity, genetic ancestry principal components (PCs) were inferred from DNAm data using EPISTRUCTURE (Rahmani et al., 2017) and cell composition was estimated via ReFACTor (Rahmani et al., 2016) using the GLINT (version 1.03) (Rahmani et al., 2017). Surrogate variable analysis (SVA), a statistical method used to account for hidden and unmeasured sources of variation in high-dimensional data, was applied to identify and estimate batch effect surrogate variables and other unknown sources of variation (Leek et al., 2012) using the same model as EWAS. All downstream analyses were conducted using DNAm M–values, adjusting for ancestry principal components (PC1–6), cell composition principal components (PC1–2), and surrogate variables (SV1–6). The selected ancestry and cell composition PCs were chosen to capture the major sources of variation in population structure and estimated cell composition, respectively, with PC1–6 explaining approximately 76% of the variance in ancestry and PC1–2 explaining approximately 81% of the variance in cell composition (Fig. S3). Surrogate variables were estimated using the sva method with the ancestry PCs, cell composition PCs, and other covariates included as adjustment covariates to capture residual unmeasured variation. The number of SVs was estimated in a data-driven manner using the sva method (Leek et al., 2012).

Sociodemographic and clinical variables

Sociodemographic and clinical data were obtained from interviewer-administered questionnaires throughout pregnancy and prenatal medical records abstracted after childbirth. Confounders were selected based on previous literature and a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024). The DAG used in the present study is provided in the Supplementary Materials (Fig. S4). Maternal age (continuous years), household income (<$30,000 vs. ≥$30,000), and smoking during pregnancy were collected from prenatal questionnaires. Season of sample collection and gestational age at sample collection (weeks) were derived from sampling dates. Pre-pregnancy BMI (kg/m2) was calculated using self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and measured height at a study visit. Pregnancy complications such as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were extracted from medical records.

Statistical analyses

The analytical pipeline included single-site EWAS, differentially methylated region (DMR) analysis, functional enrichment, methylation age acceleration analysis and mixture analyses. Primary association analyses were conducted using linear regression models. Models were adjusted for maternal age, gestational age, study site, smoking during pregnancy, household income, season of sample collection, pre-pregnancy BMI, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, ancestry principal components (PC1–6) (see Methods 2.3), cell composition principal components (PC1–2) (see Methods 2.3), and surrogate variables (SV1–6) (see Methods 2.3), where applicable, as informed by a DAG (Fig. S4). Multiple testing was controlled using Bonferroni correction. Suggestive EWAS hits were defined as CpG sites with P-values < 1 × 10−6. Suggestive DMRs were defined as regions meeting an FDR < 0.05 threshold and were used exclusively for pathway enrichment analyses.

Epigenome-wide association studies (EWASes) for each of the nine OPE metabolites

EWASes were independently conducted to identify significant CpG sites associated with each of the nine OPE metabolites. Models were adjusted for the covariates described above (see Methods 2.5). Genomic inflation was evaluated and corrected using the Bayesian Adjustment for Confounding and Overdispersion (BACON) method (van Iterson et al., 2017), which estimates the empirical null distribution to correct for both bias and inflation. To correct for multiple testing, we applied the Bonferroni threshold (0.05/837,856 = 5.97 × 10−8) for significance and sites with P-values below 1 × 10−6 were discussed as suggestive. Gene annotations and genomic region annotations (CpG island, shore, shelf, and open sea) were obtained using IlluminaHumanMethylationEPICanno.ilm10b2.hg19 (Hansen, 2016) in the minfi package (Aryee et al., 2014). Gene annotations were based on the Illumina EPIC array manifest, which maps CpG sites to genes within 1500 bp (TSS1500) or 200 bp (TSS200) upstream of the transcription start site, the 5′UTR, first exon, gene body, or 3′UTR. CpG islands were defined as regions with high CpG density, with shores defined as ±2 kb flanking CpG islands and shelves as 2–4 kb from islands. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted by excluding hypertensive disorders of pregnancy from all primary EWAS models to assess the potential for overadjustment. Trend across OPE tertiles was assessed for significant CpGs using linear regression with the exposure tertile modeled as an ordered factor, and the p-value for the linear term was used to evaluate linear trends.

Gene ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using ebGSEA (Dong et al., 2019), which integrates EWAS summary statistics across genomic regions to identify enriched biological pathways while accounting for the correlation structure of CpG sites. Gene sets were obtained from the MSigDB GO Biological Process collections (Liberzon, 2015), and pathways with at least five genes were retained. Statistical significance was determined using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (P < 6.59 × 10−6).

Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) for each of the nine OPE metabolites

DMRs associated with each of the nine OPE metabolites were identified using DMRcate (Peters, 2015) and validated with dmrff (Suderman, 2018). Significant DMRs were first identified by DMRcate as regions containing at least two CpGs within 1,000 base pairs and meeting the Bonferroni threshold (P < 5.97 × 10−8). To ensure robustness, these DMRs were then cross validated against dmrff results using the same significance threshold, and only regions significant in both methods were retained for further interpretation. Suggestive DMRs were defined as regions identified by DMRcate using FDR < 0.05, and were similarly compared with dmrff outputs with FDR < 0.05. Suggestive DMRs are presented in Table S2 and were only used for pathway enrichment analyses.

To explore biological relevance of suggestive DMRs, transcription factor binding site (TFBS) enrichment analysis (Lee et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2015) was performed using ENCODE ChIP-seq binding site annotations (ENCODE Project Consortium, 2012; ENCODE Project Consortium, 2011). Transcription factor (TF) binding sites were defined based on UCSC encRegTfbsClustered data (ENCODE Project Consortium, 2011) and restricted to high-confidence regions (score = 1000 and source count ≥2). Enrichment was evaluated by assessing whether DMRs overlapped TF binding sites and by comparing their frequencies to a genomic background defined by all EPIC array loci using Fisher’s exact test (Lee et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2015). Statistical significance was determined using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (P < 2.48 × 10−4).

Methylation age acceleration associated with prenatal OPE exposures

To explore the potential epigenetic aging effects of OPE exposure, we examined associations between urinary concentrations of OPE metabolites and four DNA methylation clocks: Horvath (Horvath, 2013), Hannum (Hannum et al., 2013), Levine PhenoAge (Levine et al., 2018), and DunedinPACE (Belsky, 2022). Epigenetic age acceleration was defined as the difference between DNAm age and chronological age, capturing age acceleration or deceleration, while the DunedinPACE score directly quantifies rate of aging using machine learning models (Belsky et al., 2022). Models were adjusted for the covariates described above (see Methods 2.5).

The effect of OPE mixtures on differentially methylated CpG sites, regions and epigenetic age using quantile g-computation

To estimate the effect of combined mixtures of nine OPEs on epigenome and epigenetic age, we used the quantile g-computation (qgcomp) (Keil et al., 2020). This method estimates the expected change in the epigenome associated with a one-quantile increase in all OPE exposures, and the proportional contribution of each OPE to the overall effect (Keil et al., 2020). Continuous OPE concentrations were used as inputs, as qgcomp internally assigns exposures into quantiles (q = 4 in our analysis). Due to computational limitations, the OPE mixture analyses were restricted to significant and suggestive CpG sites (P < 1 × 10−6) and significant DMRs (P < 5.97 × 10−8) identified in the previous section.

For each selected CpG site, we fit qgcomp models (q = 4) adjusting for the same covariates as in the EWAS (see Methods 2.5). The scaled positive or negative partial effect sizes of each OPE exposure on the epigenome were estimated, which sum to the overall mixture effect (Keil et al., 2020). Confidence intervals of the scaled effect size were estimated by 1,000 bootstrap samples. To estimate the OPE mixture effects on DMRs, qgcomp (Keil et al., 2020) was applied to individual CpG sites for significant DMRs (P < 5.97 × 10−8). Then, we used dmrff (Suderman et al., 2018) to obtain region-level OPE mixture effect estimates by combining CpG-specific mixture effects from qgcomp within each DMR (see Table S3), while accounting for the correlation structure among CpGs. Lastly, we also estimated the OPE mixture effect on four different epigenetic aging biomarkers by qgcomp (Keil et al., 2020), adjusting for the same covariates as the epigenetic age analysis.

Nearly half of participants reported an annual household income of less than $30,000 (47.4%), while 23.4% earned $30,000 or more. Most participants did not smoke during pregnancy (98.3%) and 5.7% had a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy. The distribution of quantiles for each OPE is shown in Table 1. Epigenome-wide associations of each OPE

Given the low correlations among OPE metabolites (r = ~0.2, Fig. S5) in the MADRES cohort reported by Hernandez-Castro I et al. (2024) (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024), we first conducted separate analyses for each OPE and further evaluated their joint effects (Methods 2.5.4). The Q-Q plots for each OPE EWAS showed that lambda values were consistently below 1.08 both before and after BACON adjustment, indicating no inflation for all OPE EWASes (Fig. S6). We also conducted sensitivity analyses excluding hypertensive disorders of pregnancy from all primary EWAS models to address the possibility of overadjustment. Results were highly consistent with the main analyses, and CpG sites meeting our significance threshold remained significant (Tables S4–12).

We identified two epigenome-wide significant and four suggestive CpG sites associated with several OPE metabolites during pregnancy after adjusting for confounders including demographics, pregnancy complications, cell types, genetic ancestry and batch effects (see Methods 2.3) (Table 2, top 100 hits for each OPE are reported in Tables S13–S21). cg09294299 was significantly associated with BCEP exposure (coef = 0.08, P = 2.33 × 10−8; BACON-adjusted P = 1.00 × 10−9, Fig. 1). Another CpG site, cg13756168 (ALS2, which is involved in motor neuron function), showed an epigenome-wide significant association with BDCIPP (coef = 0.14, P = 3.94 × 10−8; BACON-adjusted P = 2.44 × 10−8, Fig. 1). The methylation intensity at cg13756168 (ALS2) increased with higher urinary BDCIPP levels (Fig. 2A; trend-test P = 4.2 × 10−6), and became more pronounced after adjusting for confounders (Fig. 2B; trend-test P = 3.3 × 10−7). Although unadjusted methylation intensity at cg09294299 showed no clear association with BCEP, the adjusted methylation levels at cg09294299 revealed a significant positive association with increasing BCEP levels (Fig. 2C–D; trend-test P = 6.1 × 10−8).

To explore the biological relevance of OPE-associated CpG sites, we performed gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) on EWAS results for each OPE metabolite (Table S22). Neurogenesis was the most consistently enriched pathway, reaching significance for five of the nine OPE metabolites (BBOEP, BCEP, BEHP, DNBP + DIBP, and DPHP; all P < 5.50 × 10−6). Related neurodevelopmental terms, including generation of neurons (BCEP, BEHP, DNBP + DIBP, DPHP; all P < 3.24 × 10−6), central nervous system development (BCEP, DNBP + DIBP; both P < 3.16 × 10−6), neuron migration (BCEP; P = 4.84 × 10−6), and neuron development (DPHP; P = 1.29 × 10−7), were also significantly enriched. Beyond neurodevelopment, GSEA results implicated pathways related to cell survival and stress response. Apoptotic process was enriched across BCEP, DNBP + DIBP, and DPRP (all P < 5.28 × 10−7), and regulation of programmed cell death was shared between DNBP + DIBP and DPRP (both P < 1.75 × 10−7). Cellular response to stress was enriched for both BDCIPP and DNBP + DIBP (both P < 3.27 × 10−7), while response to oxygen-containing compound (P = 6.77 × 10−7) and response to hormone (P = 3.64 × 10−7) were also enriched for DNBP + DIBP, pointing to potential oxidative stress and endocrine-related mechanisms. Transcriptional regulation was another shared theme: negative regulation of nucleobase-containing compound metabolic process was enriched for BCEP, BDCIPP, DNBP + DIBP, and DPRP (all P < 4.68 × 10−6).

Joint effect of OPE mixtures on top CpG sites

We further evaluated the joint effect of OPE mixtures (Table 2, Fig. 3) on these six CpG sites (P < 1.0 × 10−6) by using quantile g-computation (qgcomp) (Keil, 2020). Notably, five CpG sites were statistically significant in the mixture analysis. A one-quantile increase in all OPE metabolites was associated with a 0.07 unit increase in methylation M–value (ψ = 0.07, P < 0.01) at cg09294299, which was also significant in the BCEP single-exposure model. Similarly, increases of 0.12 (ψ = 0.12, P < 0.01) and 0.06 (ψ = 0.06, P = 0.0381) in methylation M–values were observed at cg13756168 (ALS2) and cg19012384 (PPP1R12A), respectively, both associated with BDCIPP. In contrast, a one-quantile increase in all OPE metabolites was associated with a 0.38 decrease in methylation M–value (ψ = −0.38, P < 0.01) at cg04166638 (AZI1), which was also borderline significant in the DNBP + DIBP model, and a 0.36 decrease was found (ψ = −0.36, P = 0.0237) at cg16999677 (ZDHHC11, associated with BDCIPP), corresponding to hypomethylation. The directions of these effects were consistent with those observed in the corresponding single-exposure models (Table 2).

We estimated the contribution of each OPE on the joint effect of all OPEs to these CpG sites by qgcomp (Keil, 2020). As expected, BCEP contributed most (scaled effect size = 0.05, P < 0.01) among all OPEs to the hypermethylation of cg09294299, while BCIPP also showed borderline-significant contribution (scaled effect size = 0.02, P = 0.0634, Fig. 3B). A similar pattern was observed at cg16999677 (ZDHHC11), mostly driven by BDCIPP (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, BDCIPP (scaled effect size = 0.05, P < 0.01) and BMPP (scaled effect size = − 0.03, P = 0.0465) exhibited opposing contributions to the hypermethylation of cg19012384 (PPP1R12A), while both DNBP + DIBP (scaled effect size = − 0.22, P < 0.01) and BCEP (scaled effect size = − 0.13, P = 0.0274) were significant contributors to the hypomethylation of cg04166638 (AZI1) (Fig. 3B). While these results are mostly aligned with single-exposure EWAS analyses, the OPE mixture analysis suggests that some OPEs synergistically work together on the epigenome.

Six DMRs were identified by both DMRcate and dmrff at the Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold. One of the six overlapping DMRs was associated with both BBOEP and BMPP exposures corresponding to a shared region on chromosome 8 annotated to RP11–661A12.5 (a non-coding RNA) (Table 3, Fig. 4A). For BBOEP, an overlapping DMR was annotated to NNAT/BLCAP (neural development) on chromosome 20 with 42 CpGs, showing hypermethylation (P = 4.80 × 10−28, Table 3, Fig. 4B). Joint effect of OPE mixtures on top DMRs

We further investigated the joint effect of OPE mixtures on the six significant DMRs identified in the previous section (P < 5.97 × 10−8, Fig. 6). We first evaluated the OPE mixture effect on each CpG site within the significant DMRs, and then applied dmrff (Suderman, 2018) to obtain region-level mixture effect estimates (see Methods 2.5). As shown in Fig. 6, most CpG sites across the six DMRs did not show statistically significant mixture effects at the individual-site level, and consequently, no significant region-level mixture effects were detected for these DMRs. However, within DMR5 (POU5F1; chr6:31148332–31148748, 16 CpGs), dmrff identified a sub-region of 9 contiguous CpGs (chr6:31148463–31148657) with a suggestive region-weighted mixture effect estimate of − 2.33 (P = 0.016, adjusted P = 0.119, Fig. 6), indicating that a one-quantile increase in overall OPE exposure was associated with coordinated hypomethylation across this sub-region. Consistent negative mixture effect estimates were also observed at most individual CpG sites within DMR5 (Fig. 6). This sub-region was located near the downstream end of POU5F1 and overlapped the 3′ end of PSORS1C3.

Comparison of OPE EWASes between maternal blood and offspring’s cord blood

Two recent studies also examined prenatal OPE exposure in relation to cord-blood DNA methylation (Schrott et al., 2025; Mendy et al., 2025). Mendy et al. conducted an OPE EWAS on cord blood using the EPIC array among 252 pregnant women in the Health Outcomes and Measures of Environment (HOME) Study. They quantified four OPEs in house dust and three urinary OPE metabolites (BCEP, BDCIPP, and DPHP) during the second trimester. They identified a DMR (chr6: 31148332–31148748) near POU5F1 associated with prenatal BDCIPP exposure (Mendy, 2025). Notably, we also found this exact DMR near POU5F1 in maternal blood associated with BEHP and suggestively associated with the overall OPE mixture (Table 3; Fig. 4), suggesting a potentially inherited methylated region in response to prenatal OPE exposure. Mendy et al. identified a differentially methylated site at cg12796841 associated with tris(1,3-dichloroisopropyl) phosphate (TDCIPP) measured in dust, but not with its main metabolite in urine, BDCIPP (Mendy, 2025). cg12796841 was not epigenome-wide significant in our analysis of OPE metabolites in urine (MADRES P = 0.1127, ECHO FDR P = 0.01). The full list of CpG sites of the OPE EWAS in cord blood is not available (Mendy, 2025). Schrott et al. conducted the study within the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Cohort, including 736 samples from seven sites. Their study measured five OPE biomarkers in maternal urine during the second and third trimester and assessed DNA methylation in cord blood, primarily using 450 K array (Schrott, 2025). No epigenome-wide significant CpGs were identified in their study. We compared the overlapping CpG sites in Tables S24–S28. Since the CpG coverage differed substantially between EPIC and 450 K arrays, we could not find some of our top CpG sites in their results. Comparing the BCEP EWAS results in our maternal blood samples in MADRES and cord blood samples in ECHO, the highest-ranked overlapping CpG (cg17239923) showed a P-value of 1.32 × 10−5 in our analysis, but a P-value of 0.4596 in ECHO; for BDCIPP EWAS, the top overlapping significant CpG (cg05711842) appeared at P = 1.52 × 10−6 in our analysis and 0.7833 in ECHO. Only one suggestive CpG for BBOEP EWAS in MADRES (cg07965110) was presented in ECHO’s dataset and it was not significant (ECHO P = 0.8562). Furthermore, no DMRs were shared between the two cohorts.

Discussion

In this study, we examined associations between prenatal exposure to OPE flame retardants and maternal DNA methylation during pregnancy using both single-metabolite and mixture-based approaches. We identified CpG sites and DMRs associated with multiple urinary OPE metabolites and their mixtures. Annotated genes were involved in neuronal maintenance, immune regulation, and cellular signaling, and these associations were further supported by pathway enrichment evidence.

Our mixture analyses revealed synergistic effects of multiple OPEs on CpG sites and DMRs identified in single-exposure analyses. As expected, results from single OPE analyses were largely consistent with those from the OPE mixtures. The epigenome-wide significant signals in single metabolite analyses, cg09294299 (associated with BCEP exposure) and cg13756168 (ALS2, associated with BDCIPP exposure), also remained significant in the mixture models, with the mixture effects at these two sites primarily driven by BCEP and BDCIPP, respectively. The exposure to BDCIPP was also reported to be associated with global hypomethylation in cord blood, although no specific genes were identified (Schrott et al., 2025). ALS2 plays a key role in motor neuron maintenance and is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases. The CpG site cg04166638 exhibited hypomethylation in response to combined OPE exposures, primarily driven by both DNBP + DIBP and BCEP, while the association between cg04166638 and DNBP + DIBP approached epigenome-wide significance (AZI1, P = 8.71 × 10−8). AZI1 encodes a centrosomal protein involved in ciliogenesis and innate immune signaling through the STING–TBK1 pathway. Its role in ciliary and Hedgehog signaling also connects AZI1 to neurodevelopmental and neuroinflammatory regulation (Renaud et al., 2023; Silva et al., 2016).

Additional suggestively associated CpG sites further support immune and inflammatory biological pathways, mapping to genes such as ZDHHC11 (BDCIPP), PPP1R12A (BDCIPP), RCAN3 (BBOEP). Among them, CpG annotated to ZDHHC11 and PPP1R12A also show significant association with OPE mixture. Notably, ZDHHC11, which is also annotated to significant DMRs, encodes a palmitoyltransferase essential for immune signaling. Given that related family members like ZDHHC13 regulate mitochondrial function and liver metabolism, altered expression or activity of ZDHHC11 may also contribute to hepatic responses to toxicant exposure (Shen et al., 2017). Supporting this, OPE exposure has been shown to alter liver cell phenotype and function in human HepG2 (Yu et al., 2024). PPP1R12A, which encodes a myosin phosphatase subunit involved in cytoskeletal regulation, has also been linked to neurodevelopmental processes and brain malformations (Hughes et al., 2020; Alkhunaizi and Chitayat, 1993). RCAN3 (BBOEP) modulates calcineurin-dependent inflammatory pathways and may respond to oxidative stress regulation, a key mechanism underlying OPE toxicity (Chen et al., 2019; Yao et al., 2021).

GSEA of single-site EWAS results provided pathway-level evidence that complements the above findings. Neurogenesis pathway was enriched for five of the nine OPE metabolites in GSEA, making it the most recurrent signal. This finding is consistent with our EWAS results showing hits at neurodevelopment-related genes such as ALS2, AZI1, and PPP1R12A. It also aligns with experimental evidence that multiple OPEs can disrupt neural cell proliferation, differentiation, and neurotransmitter signaling in rodent and zebrafish models (Hogberg et al., 2021; Dishaw et al., 2014). Enrichment of apoptotic process, cellular response to stress, and response to oxygen-containing compound across multiple OPEs is consistent with in vitro findings that OPEs induce oxidative stress and apoptosis via mitochondrial dysfunction and p53-dependent cascades, and with epidemiological evidence linking prenatal OPE exposure to oxidative damage and endocrine disruption (Yao et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2022; An et al., 2016). These stress and apoptotic pathways are closely tied to inflammatory and immune signaling, aligning with the immune-modulatory genes identified in EWAS, including ZDHHC11, RCAN3, and AZI1.

DMR analyses provided further support for the involvement of neurodegeneration and immune-related pathways associated with prenatal OPE exposure, consistent with the known endocrine-disrupting and neurotoxic potential of OPEs (Meeker and Stapleton, 2010; Vuong et al., 2020; Rojas et al., 2025; Dishaw et al., 2014). Identified regions included BEHP-associated hypomethylation at POU5F1, a pluripotency regulator critical for stem-cell maintenance and neural differentiation, and previously implicated in pollutant-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) processes (Martín-Leyva et al., 2024). Evidence for this region was also shown in our OPE mixture analysis. The HOME study also reported a DMR near POU5F1 in association with BDCIPP (Mendy et al., 2025). As a key regulator of early development, this locus may be sensitive to prenatal OPE exposure. Hypermethylation at PLA2G1B (BEHP) may reflect alterations in lipid metabolism and immune response. Regions annotated to NNAT/BLCAP (BBOEP), which are involved in neuronal growth and apoptosis have shown sensitivity to chemical-induced developmental toxicity (Joshi et al., 2013) and could plausibly contribute to the association between OPE exposure and neurodegeneration (Rojas et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2022). RP11–661A12.5 (associated with BMPP and BBOEP), is a non-coding RNA locus potentially linked to the rate of decline in a cognitive function test called CDR-SB (_,Li et al., 2021). SLFN12 and ZDHHC11 (BDCIPP) are both connected to immune modulation. SLFN12 has been identified as a potential immunoepigenetic responder, with exon 1 hypermethylation observed in T cells from multiple sclerosis patients (Rhead et al., 2018). Additional evidence from allergen exposure studies also highlights its sensitivity to external immunologic stress (Tilmisani et al., 2024). Given OPE-induced oxidative and immune stress, similar epigenetic modulation of SLFN12 may plausibly occur following prenatal exposure. ZDHHC11, also identified in single-site EWAS analysis, was independently validated through regional DMR approaches, reinforcing its biological importance.

TF enrichment analysis further emphasizes potential neurological and immune consequences. Among the shared TFs, EZH2 binding sites were enriched in DMRs from four OPEs. EZH2 is the catalytic component of Polycomb repressive complex 2 and a critical regulator of neurogenesis, and studies have shown its loss in neural progenitor cells disrupts the balance between self-renewal and differentiation in the cerebral cortex, impairs neuronal migration and morphogenesis, and reduces adult neurogenesis (Pereira et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2023; Hwang et al., 2014). EHMT2, which showed the highest fold enrichment of all TFs, mediates H3K9 methylation to maintain transcriptional silencing during neural development (Park et al., 2022). The shared enrichment of stress-responsive ATF7 and ATF3 points to a connection between OPE-associated DMRs and inflammatory signaling. ATF7 suppresses innate immunity genes in macrophages, and its disruption establishes long-term epigenetic changes in innate immunological memory (Yoshida et al., 2015). ATF3 functions as a hub of the cellular stress-response network, linking oxidative stress, Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, and NF-κB-mediated inflammatory cytokine production, and has been implicated in neuroinflammatory responses following acute brain injury (Hai et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023).

We found that individual OPE metabolites exhibited heterogeneous associations with DNA methylation age acceleration across the four clocks examined. Higher BCEP levels were significantly associated with increased DunedinPACE acceleration, whereas detectable BMPP levels were linked to lower Horvath age acceleration and detectable BEHP levels were also significantly associated with lower Levine PhenoAge acceleration, indicating epigenetic age deceleration. Similar inverse associations have been reported for prenatal DEHP exposure, including decreased Horvath age acceleration in children (Khodasevich et al., 2023) and in infant girls (Merrill et al., 2024). While accelerated epigenetic age is typically linked to higher morbidity and mortality (Faul et al., 2023; Yusupov et al., 2023), lower epigenetic age acceleration in early life has been interpreted as either developmental deceleration or a younger epigenetic profile (Merrill et al., 2024; Roubinov et al., 2021), and our findings extend this pattern to pregnant women, raising the possibility of transplacental implications for offspring. The null results in the OPE mixture analysis for methylation age acceleration likely reflect the offsetting influence of individual metabolites with opposing directions of association, like the conflicts between BCEP, BMPP and BEHP shown in Table 4, a phenomenon also observed for other chemical classes such as heavy metals (Dutta et al., 2023).

To contextualize our findings, we compared them with OPE EWASes in cord blood (Schrott et al., 2025; Mendy et al., 2025). The lack of consistent signals across these studies is not unexpected, given substantive methodological and biological differences between maternal whole blood and newborn cord blood. These tissues differ markedly in cellular composition and methylation architecture, and cross-tissue concordance for environmentally responsive CpG sites is generally low (Bakulski et al., 2016; Jones et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2017). Second, CpG coverage varied greatly across studies, as ECHO conducted a meta-analysis across 27 K, 450 K and EPIC arrays, whereas our analyses used the EPIC platform exclusively. Many of our leading CpG sites were not included in the ECHO testing set (Supplemental File 1). In addition, biological factors also likely play a role. Recent evidence indicates that only a small proportion of CpG sites show parent-offspring resemblance independent of genetic variants, and most intergenerational methylation patterns do not follow classical Mendelian transmission (Zhang and Sirard, 2021; Díez-Villanueva et al., 2023). These non-Mendelian inheritance features, together with the difference in the exposure to the outcome window (pregnancy versus birth), provide a biological basis for the discrepancies observed across cohorts.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first comprehensive epigenetic studies to investigate the association between prenatal OPE exposure and maternal DNA methylation, extending prior work that focused mainly on fetal epigenetic changes (Chen et al., 2024; Schrott et al., 2025). We comprehensively assessed the associations of nine OPE metabolites and their overall mixture effects at both the CpG-site and region levels across the epigenome, and further assessed the underlying biological pathways and their effect on the DNA methylation age acceleration. We adopted several methods to enhance reliability of our findings, including the use of BACON to adjust for inflation in EWAS, and the use of both DMRcate and dmrff to validate DMR results. We also used quantile-based g-computation for OPE mixture effects to enhance the robustness and interpretation of our findings. The direct measurement of OPE metabolites in urine samples instead of house dust also makes the results more directly reflect human exposure and health (Foster et al., 2024). Importantly, our study involved a predominantly Hispanic, low-income population – a highly vulnerable group that faces elevated environmental hazards but is underrepresented in research.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. Our study sample size is relatively small (N = 175), which limits the statistical power to detect differences in methylation or methylation age acceleration (Mansell et al., 2019). The measurement of DNA methylation in blood and OPE metabolites in urine only during the third trimester may not represent the full trajectory of epigenetic changes across the prenatal period. We also acknowledge that categorizing OPE concentrations instead of continuous measures may result in some loss of information, though this approach was particularly important for metabolites with low detection frequencies to reduce measurement error (Hernandez-Castro et al., 2024). Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these associations, and longitudinal designs with repeated measurements across pregnancy would help identify critical windows of epigenetic vulnerability.

In summary, these findings suggest that prenatal OPE exposure is associated with epigenomic changes in maternal blood. Differential DNA methylation can lead to differences in gene expression and downstream biological pathways related to neuronal degeneration, inflammation, immune response and cell signaling. Larger studies need to be conducted to replicate our findings, to disentangle the biological mechanisms of OPEs and to investigate whether these will lead to adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes.
