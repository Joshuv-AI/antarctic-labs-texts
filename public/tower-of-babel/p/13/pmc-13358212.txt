Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become a standard treatment for advanced renal cell carcinoma (RCC). The programmed death‐1 (PD‐1) inhibitor pembrolizumab is commonly used with lenvatinib, with favorable clinical outcomes; however, immune‐related encephalopathy is an extremely rare neurological adverse event associated with this class of agents. The diagnosis of immune‐related encephalopathy is often challenging because the symptoms are nonspecific, and neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid findings are frequently unremarkable [1].

Here, we report a case of suspected early‐onset immune‐related encephalopathy following pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib therapy for RCC.

Case Presentation

A 70‐year‐old independent man with no significant medical history or medications experienced general fatigue and loss of appetite for 3 months before presentation, and developed fever and right abdominal fullness 1 month before admission. His symptoms worsened 3 weeks before presentation, and he visited a local hospital. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a large right renal mass (Figure 1a), and he was referred to our institution. Laboratory findings on admission revealed anemia, thrombocytosis, elevated inflammatory markers, and hypoalbuminemia (Table 1a). Contrast‐enhanced CT on admission suggested right RCC (Figure 1b,c). According to International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium criteria, the patient was classified as intermediate risk based on anemia, thrombocytosis, and a short interval from diagnosis to treatment initiation. Renal tumor biopsy confirmed right clear cell RCC. Imaging studies suggested invasion into adjacent retroperitoneal structures and possible colonic involvement, consistent with locally advanced cT4N0M0 disease. The tumor was therefore considered unresectable, and pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib therapy was initiated on hospital Day 13.

Six days after treatment initiation, however, he developed fatigue and impaired consciousness. Two days later, he became unable to stand and had difficulty communicating, and was urgently readmitted. On admission, his blood pressure was 156/82 mmHg, indicating that it was not significantly elevated at symptom onset. Blood tests and whole‐body CT were performed but showed no significant abnormalities. Serum cortisol levels were normal (17.3 μg/dL). Although thyroid‐stimulating hormone was mildly elevated (6.49 IU/mL), free T4 levels were within the normal range (1.04 ng/dL), and there were no clinical signs of hypothyroidism. Possible intracranial disease was investigated by contrast‐enhanced brain CT, MRI, and electroencephalography, but no abnormalities were detected. Nerve conduction studies and electromyography were not performed because impaired consciousness was the predominant clinical feature, and the neurological manifestations were considered to be primarily central in origin. Cerebrospinal fluid examination was unremarkable, and meningitis, encephalitis, and leptomeningeal carcinomatosis were excluded (Table 1b).

Brain metastasis, Trousseau syndrome, infectious encephalitis, endocrine disorders, and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome were considered as differential diagnoses but were ruled out based on the above examinations. In the absence of any other identified causes, immune‐related encephalopathy was suspected. Considering grade 3 disease, pembrolizumab and lenvatinib were discontinued and corticosteroid therapy was initiated.

Intravenous prednisolone was initiated at 2 mg/kg/day (125 mg/day). On Day 3 after steroid initiation, his consciousness level had improved but remained insufficient, and intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (1000 mg/day for three consecutive days) was initiated. His neurological symptoms improved by Day 7 after steroid initiation, and steroid tapering was started with oral prednisolone resumed at 1 mg/kg/day (55 mg/day). Corticosteroids were discontinued 46 days later.

The patient's higher brain function was assessed after completion of steroid pulse therapy. Selective attention impairment was detected, but other cognitive functions were within the normal ranges. His cognitive function gradually improved, and he eventually recovered enough to resume driving (Figure 2).

Clinical course of steroid therapy and neurocognitive assessments. Corticosteroid therapy resulted in gradual neurological improvement. Neuropsychological testing initially showed mild selective attention impairment (HDS‐R 24, MMSE 25; TMT‐J Part A abnormal), followed by progressive cognitive recovery (HDS‐R 30 at discharge and 29, MMSE 30 on follow‐up). Steroids were tapered and discontinued on Day 46.

Although his neurological symptoms resolved completely, his general condition deteriorated due to tumor progression with colonic perforation, and he died 4 months after the initial hospitalization.

Discussion

ICIs are widely used for advanced RCC. Neurological immune‐related adverse events are uncommon, occurring in approximately 1%–5% of patients, with central nervous system involvement being particularly rare [2]. Most immune‐related adverse events occur within the first few months after the initiation of ICIs, although delayed onset has also been reported [3]. Combination immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, particularly nivolumab plus ipilimumab, is associated with a higher incidence of immune‐related adverse events than monotherapy [4]. These events include central and peripheral neurological manifestations, such as encephalitis, aseptic meningitis, and neuropathies [2].

Immune‐related encephalopathy represents a diffuse disturbance of brain function that may occur with no overt inflammatory findings. In contrast to encephalitis, encephalopathy may present with nonspecific symptoms such as cognitive decline and altered consciousness, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis and neuroimaging results may appear normal. The diagnosis is thus often made clinically, based on the response to immunosuppressive therapy, after excluding other possible causes such as infection, metastasis, or metabolic disorders [5].

Immune‐related encephalopathy should be managed according to established guidelines for neurological immune‐related adverse events. For Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade ≥ 2, ICIs should be discontinued and high‐dose corticosteroids initiated promptly [6]. Early treatment is associated with favorable neurological recovery, whereas delayed intervention may lead to persistent deficits or fatal outcomes [2]. In refractory cases, intravenous immunoglobulin, plasmapheresis, or additional immunosuppressive therapy may be required [7, 8].

Cases involving pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib therapy for RCC were extremely limited; therefore, we also included reports of neurological immune‐related adverse events associated with the same therapy in other malignancies for comparison (Table 2) [9]. In contrast to previously reported cases, which developed months after therapy initiation and often required additional treatment such as intravenous immunoglobulin, the present patient manifested neurological symptoms as early as 6 days after starting treatment. However, unlike previously reported cases that required intravenous immunoglobulin therapy, early therapeutic intervention on Day 3 after symptom onset led to gradual neurological recovery in the present case, and the encephalopathy improved to CTCAE grade 0 with corticosteroid therapy alone. This favorable outcome may be attributable to early therapeutic intervention, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and prompt treatment for favorable neurological outcomes.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of immune‐related encephalopathy following pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib therapy for RCC. Early recognition and prompt corticosteroid treatment are essential when unexplained neurological symptoms occur during ICI therapy.
