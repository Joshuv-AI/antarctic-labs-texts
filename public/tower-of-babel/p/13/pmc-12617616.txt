Renal oncocytomatosis: A rare case of bilateral multifocal oncocytomas 15 Years after solitary lesion: a case report
Authors: Marco Rosario, Darshan Sitharthan, Paul Sved
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal oncocytomas are benign epithelial tumors accounting for 3–7 % of renal neoplasms. While typically solitary and indolent, oncocytomatosis, characterized by multiple bilateral oncocytomas, is exceedingly rare and poses diagnostic and management challenges. We present the unusual case of a 55-year-old man who initially underwent a left partial nephrectomy in 2008 for a solitary renal oncocytoma confirmed histologically and, after 15 years of surveillance with no recurrence, presented in 2023 with bilateral renal lesions.

Introduction

Renal oncocytomas are benign neoplasms originating from the intercalated cells of the distal renal tubules. They represent approximately 3–7 % of all renal masses and are typically solitary, well-circumscribed lesions with an indolent clinical course.1 Histologically, oncocytomas are characterized by large eosinophilic cells with abundant granular cytoplasm rich in mitochondria, often requiring electron microscopy and immunohistochemical staining to differentiate from chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (RCC), which shares overlapping features.2

Renal oncocytosis, as proposed by Tickoo et al. refers to kidneys with innumerable oncocytic nodules, with or without hybrid oncocytic/chromophobe lesions, whereas Katz et al. recommended reserving the term “oncocytomatosis” for diffuse infiltration by oncocytomas.3

This case presents a very long interval (15 years) between a solitary renal oncocytoma and the later development of bilateral multifocal oncocytic lesions, with biopsy-proven benign histology and a stable clinical course under active surveillance.

The natural history and optimal management of multifocal oncocytic disease remain poorly defined due to its rarity. While surgical resection is curative for solitary oncocytomas, bilateral multifocal involvement often necessitates a conservative approach to preserve renal function.4

Case presentation

A 55-year-old man initially presented in 2008 with a solitary solid lesion in the left kidney detected on MRI during evaluation for flank discomfort. He underwent a left partial nephrectomy, and frozen section analysis at surgery confirmed clear margins. Final histopathology demonstrated a benign oncocytoma characterized by well circumscribed nests of eosinophilic cells with abundant granular cytoplasm and no evidence of malignancy or vascular invasion. The patient had an uneventful postoperative course and was recommended to undergo surveillance with ultrasound in 12 months.

He remained well and asymptomatic for 15 years with no evidence of recurrence or new lesions on intermittent imaging. In 2023, he presented with right flank discomfort. Contrast-enhanced CT imaging revealed two solid, well-circumscribed enhancing lesions in the right kidney measuring up to 40 mm, and a smaller heterogeneous lesion in the lower pole of the left kidney measuring 17 mm. A prior left partial nephrectomy scar was evident. Differential diagnosis included renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and multifocal oncocytomas.

The patient underwent percutaneous biopsy of the right kidney lesions with electron microscopy performed at a specialist center. Histology confirmed the diagnosis of benign oncocytomas in the sampled lesions. No features suggestive of chromophobe RCC or malignancy were identified.

Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for CD117 and patchy CD10, with focal CK7 expression, and negative staining for AMACR and CA-IX. BAP1 expression was retained, and colloidal iron stain was negative, supporting the diagnosis of oncocytoma.

Surveillance imaging performed in 2024 demonstrated progression of disease with four heterogeneous enhancing lesions up to 42 mm in the right kidney and approximately seven similar lesions up to 25 mm in the left kidney (Fig. 1). No regional lymphadenopathy or metastatic disease was identified.

Multiphasic CT scans (non-contrast, corticomedullary, nephrographic, and excretory phases) were used to monitor enhancement patterns. Lesions showed persistent, heterogeneous enhancement. Over 12 months, the dominant right renal lesion increased from 39 mm to 45 mm (6 mm/year), while the largest left renal lesion grew from 25 mm to 27 mm (2 mm/year).

Baseline renal function was normal (creatinine 85 μmol/L, eGFR 81 mL/min/1.73m2), and remained stable (latest creatinine 89 μmol/L, eGFR 78). He had no episodes of hematuria, was not on anticoagulants or antiplatelet therapy, and had no history of bleeding events. Blood pressure readings were consistently within normal limits (120–150 mmHg systolic).

The case was discussed at a multidisciplinary meeting, and in the context of biopsy-proven benign disease, stable renal function, and absence of symptoms, a decision was made to pursue active surveillance. The patient is currently monitored with 6-monthly multiphasic CT imaging. Intervention is reserved for rapid lesion growth, development of symptoms, or decline in renal function.

The patient has not been evaluated for Birt-Hogg-Dubé (BHD) syndrome at this time, as there is no family history of renal tumors, pneumothorax, or fibrofolliculomas, and the patient's age and clinical presentation are not strongly suggestive of a hereditary syndrome.

Discussion

Renal oncocytomas are benign tumors derived from intercalated cells of the collecting duct and generally have an excellent prognosis following surgical excision.1 While usually presenting as solitary lesions, the occurrence of multiple oncocytomas, particularly bilateral multifocal disease, is termed renal oncocytosis or oncocytomatosis — rare and poorly understood entities.3

Oncocytomatosis can be difficult to distinguish radiologically from multifocal renal cell carcinoma, particularly chromophobe RCC, which shares overlapping imaging and histologic features. Classic radiological signs such as central stellate scar, homogenous enhancement, or absence of necrosis are unreliable and cannot definitively exclude RCC5. Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological evaluation supplemented by electron microscopy and immunohistochemistry to differentiate oncocytomas from chromophobe RCC and other eosinophilic renal neoplasms.2,6

Our case illustrates several key points. Firstly, the 15-year interval between the initial solitary oncocytoma and development of bilateral multifocal lesions highlights the indolent natural history of oncocytic tumors. Secondly, the progression of lesions in both kidneys over one year emphasizes the need for structured surveillance. Thirdly, the biopsy-confirmed benign nature of the lesions supports a conservative, nephron-sparing management approach, especially given the risk of renal impairment with repeated surgical interventions.4

There are few reports of oncocytomatosis in the literature, and optimal management remains controversial. Some authors advocate for nephron-sparing surgery or ablation when lesions cause symptoms or compromise renal function, while others recommend active surveillance in asymptomatic patients due to the low risk of malignancy and metastasis.3,7 Our multidisciplinary approach aligns with the latter, reserving intervention for complications such as bleeding or significant loss of renal function.

Multifocal or bilateral oncocytic tumors should prompt consideration of hereditary conditions such as Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome (BHD), which is associated with hybrid oncocytic/chromophobe tumors. Genetic counseling and testing may be warranted in patients with younger age of onset, cutaneous findings, pulmonary cysts or family history of renal tumors.8

Conclusion

Renal oncocytomatosis is a rare and often under-recognized condition characterized by multifocal, bilateral renal oncocytomas. This case highlights the potential for long-term recurrence even after curative partial nephrectomy, emphasizing the importance of continued surveillance. Accurate diagnosis using histopathology and electron microscopy is essential to avoid misclassification as renal cell carcinoma, which could lead to unnecessary intervention. Given the benign nature of oncocytomas, conservative management with close monitoring is often appropriate, especially in the setting of multifocal bilateral disease where nephron preservation is critical. This case contributes to the limited literature on oncocytomatosis and supports an individualized, surveillance-based approach to management.
