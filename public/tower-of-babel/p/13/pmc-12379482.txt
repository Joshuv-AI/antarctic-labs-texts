Reducing radiomics errors in nasopharyngeal cancer via deep learning-based synthetic CT generation from CBCT
Authors: Ying Xiao, Weixiang Lin, Fangping Xie, Lipeng Liu, Gaoyin Zheng, Chengjian Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study investigates the impact of cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) image quality on radiomic analysis and evaluates the potential of deep learning-based enhancement to improve radiomic feature accuracy in nasopharyngeal cancer (NPC).MethodsThe CBAMRegGAN model was trained on 114 paired CT and CBCT datasets from 114 nasopharyngeal cancer patients to enhance CBCT images, with CT images as ground truth. The dataset was split into 82 patients for training, 12 for validation, and 20 for testing. The radiomic features in 6 different categories, including first-order, gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM), gray-level size-zone matrix(GLSZM), neighbouring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM), and gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM), were extracted from the gross tumor volume (GTV) of original CBCT, enhanced CBCT, and CT. Comparing feature errors between original and enhanced CBCT showed that deep learning-based enhancement improves radiomic feature accuracy.ResultsThe CBAMRegGAN model achieved improved image quality with a peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) of 29.52 ± 2.28 dB, normalized mean absolute error (NMAE) of 0.0129 ± 0.004, and structural similarity index (SSIM) of 0.910 ± 0.025 for enhanced CBCT images. This led to reduced errors in most radiomic features, with average reductions across 20 patients of 19.0%, 24.0%, 3.0%, 19%, 15.0%, and 5.0% for first-order, GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, NGTDM, and GLDM features.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that CBCT image quality significantly influences radiomic analysis, and deep learning-based enhancement techniques can effectively improve both image quality and the accuracy of radiomic features in NPC.

Introduction

Radiomics refers to a methodology that leverages advanced computational techniques to extract and quantify a wide range of features from medical imaging data, enabling the transformation of visual information into mineable data for clinical and biological interpretation. These extracted features—ranging from morphological properties like shape and volume, to texture-based metrics, histogram-derived parameters, and responses to various filters—are obtained through complex algorithms [1–4]. Several studies have highlighted the potential of radiomics as a non-invasive imaging tool [5, 6], which can enhance diagnostic accuracy and prognostic capabilities, thus providing better support for clinical decision-making in oncology [7]. Radiomic features from CT and CBCT have shown promise in predicting outcomes such as locoregional recurrence, distant metastasis, and overall survival [8, 9]. In head and neck radiomics research, CBCT-based radiomics has emerged as a valuable approach, supporting adaptive radiotherapy planning and offering predictive value for treatment response and long-term outcomes [10–12].

While diagnostic CT images are commonly used for treatment planning, CBCT scans are primarily employed for patient setup verification in radiation therapy. Due to their lower acquisition dose and prolonged scanning duration—sometimes up to a minute—CBCT images are more prone to noise, scatter artifacts from flat-panel detectors, and motion distortions caused by respiration or patient movement. Nevertheless, because of their frequent acquisition (either daily or weekly during the course of treatment), CBCT images offer a unique opportunity to monitor intra-treatment anatomical changes over time. These longitudinal variations may serve as early imaging biomarkers reflecting tumor response to therapy. However, differences in imaging parameters between CBCT and CT-including spatial resolution, noise levels, scatter effects, and motion artifacts-can significantly affect the stability and repeatability of radiomic features. To address these challenges, various image enhancement techniques have been proposed to improve the quality of CBCT or CT images and enhance the consistency of derived radiomic data. For instance, super-resolution models have been developed to modify slice thickness and improve spatial resolution [13], while generative adversarial networks (GANs) have been applied to harmonize CT images acquired under different conditions, such as varying reconstruction kernels [14]. Previous research has mostly concentrated on upgrading CT image quality. In radiation therapy, CBCT scans are routinely acquired before each treatment session to ensure accurate patient alignment. Research by Benjamin et al. [15] demonstrated that longitudinal changes observed in CBCT images throughout head and neck radiotherapy can serve as predictive indicators for chronic xerostomia. Although daily CBCT imaging holds considerable promise for monitoring treatment progression, its application in radiomic analysis has received limited attention. These routinely collected CBCT images play a critical role in evaluating treatment response, guiding adaptive planning, and predicting clinical outcomes [16–19]. However, due to constraints on scanning time and radiation exposure in clinical settings, CBCT reconstruction often relies on a limited number of projection views. This under-sampling leads to increased image noise and streaking artifacts, which compromise overall image quality. Therefore, improving CBCT image fidelity is essential for enhancing the reliability of radiomic features. Such improvements can ultimately lead to more accurate outcome predictions through radiomic modeling.

Various conventional and deep learning-based approaches have been proposed to enhance CBCT image quality [20]. Early studies primarily relied on traditional techniques, such as prior-knowledge-driven estimation [21], and compressed sensing with iterative optimization algorithms [22]. Nevertheless, these approaches have certain limitations. For example, prior-knowledge-driven methods require deformable registration, which is time-consuming. Compressed sensing methods, on the other hand, may overly smooth images, potentially removing important fine anatomical details and edges when eliminating artifacts. Moreover, iterative frameworks generally involve complex computations and lengthy processing times, which can limit their practicality in clinical settings. In recent years, deep learning has emerged as a powerful and efficient alternative for medical image enhancement. One representative example is the symmetric residual convolutional neural network proposed by Jiang et al. [23], which demonstrated superior performance in improving CBCT image quality. Their experimental results showed a significant improvement in image quality, surpassing traditional methods in both visual clarity and quantitative metrics. Building on this work, a study explored the relationship between 4D-CBCT image quality and the accuracy of radiomics features in lung cancer applications [24]. The findings indicated that the quality of 4D-CBCT images can substantially impact the accuracy of derived radiomics features. By improving 4D-CBCT images, the deep learning model was able to enhance the accuracy of radiomics analysis.

This study evaluates the accuracy and robustness of radiomic features in CBCT images affected by under-sampling artifacts. A deep learning framework trained on nasopharyngeal cancer (NPC) patient data was utilized to enhance CBCT image quality for these patients. Radiomic features were extracted from both original and improved CBCT images, with CT features as the reference standard. The study found that the CBAMRegGAN model substantially boosts CBCT image quality, resulting in more precise radiomic features. This improvement is vital for clinical uses like outcome prediction and treatment evaluation.

Materials and methods

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the CBCT and CT datasets used in this study. The dataset comprises a total of 114 patients diagnosed with NPC who were treated at our institution between January 2023 and May 2025. In our study, the median interval from CT acquisition to the first radiotherapy fraction (during which CBCT was performed) was 5 days (range 4–6 days). Among these, 82 cases were allocated for model training, 12 for validation, and 20 for testing. Each patient’s dataset includes paired CT and CBCT images. The CBCT scans were acquired during the initial radiation therapy session using a Varian VitalBeam linear accelerator. To align the CT images with CBCT geometry, deformable image registration was performed using SimpleITK (Python 3.8). Subsequently, the registered CT images were rigidly aligned to the corresponding CBCT images and resampled to match the CBCT voxel dimensions. All images were cropped to a uniform size of 512 × 512 pixels. Original CT voxel spacing was 0.58(± 0.1) × 0.58(± 0.1) ×3(± 0.1) mm³; CBCT voxel spacing was 0.51 × 0.51 × 1.99 mm³. The reconstructed CT and CBCT images were resampled to 0.51 × 0.51 × 1.99 mm³ voxel size. The original CT HU ranges were [–1000, 3071], and [–1000, Variable] for CBCT. To reduce intensity inconsistencies across modalities, histogram matching was applied for intensity normalization. Both the original CBCT and the deformably registered CT (dCT) images were scaled to a normalized intensity range of [− 1, 1].

For model training, several deep learning architectures—namely CycleGAN, Pix2Pix, RegGAN, and CBAMRegGAN—were implemented using PyTorch (version 1.12.0) on an NVIDIA RTX 2080 GPU with 12 GB of memory. A mini-batch size of 1 was used throughout the training process. The initial learning rate was set to 0.0001 for both generator and discriminator networks. Optimization was carried out using the Adam optimizer. The models were trained for a maximum of 40 epochs. Training the models takes about 66 h, 27 h, 34 h, and 40 h for CycleGAN, Pix2Pix, RegGAN, and CBAMRegGAN. Training was stopped when the loss no longer decreased significantly. The final model was selected based on the best performance on the validation set.

Radiomic feature extraction

The original CBCT images were acquired using a linear accelerator, while enhanced versions of these images were generated through a deep learning-based enhancement model. Tumor delineation was performed manually by experienced radiation oncologists, following established contouring guidelines to ensure consistency across observers. Following segmentation, the tumor volumes were resampled to a uniform voxel size in order to standardize the feature extraction process. Radiomic features were subsequently extracted using the open-source pyradiomics toolkit [25].

A total of 93 radiomic features were computed from the tumor regions of interest. These features were categorized into two main groups: first-order statistics and texture-based features. Specifically, the dataset included 18 first-order features such as mean intensity, median value, maximum pixel value, and energy. The remaining 75 features were texture-related, comprising 24 gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features, 16 gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM) features, 16 gray-level size-zone matrix (GLSZM) features, 14 gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM) features, and 5 neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM) features. Shape-related features were excluded from the analysis because the same tumor contour was applied consistently across all CT and CBCT images for each individual patient.

Radiomic features selection

Due to limitations in computational efficiency and model complexity, it is not feasible to incorporate all 93 extracted radiomic features into the prediction model. Additionally, some of these features may exhibit high redundancy or random variability, which could negatively impact the performance and generalizability of the outcome prediction model. Therefore, feature selection plays a crucial role in improving both the efficiency and reliability of predictive modeling. In this study, we investigated how image quality influences a subset of radiomic features that were previously identified in a highly cited CT-based lung cancer outcome prediction study [9], Huynh et al. employed a feature selection strategy based on intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC), retaining only those features with ICC values exceeding 0.8 to ensure reproducibility. Using the concordance index as an indicator of each feature’s predictive relevance, six representative features were selected: one from each category—first-order, GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, NGTDM, and GLDM. Our analysis applied these externally validated features to assess how image quality variations affect radiomic feature stability and accuracy.

Models

GANs show great potential in medical image-to-image translation and domain adaptation. But their clinical use is restricted by challenges like training instability, poor cross-modality generalization, and reliance on high-quality paired data. For example, Pix2Pix [26] needs exactly matched image pairs, which are often unavailable. CycleGAN can handle unpaired image pairs but may produce inconsistent results—such as residual reconstruction artifacts [27], deterministic collapse that erases output diversity [28], and multimodal deficiency under hard cycle-consistency constraints [29], which reduce its reliability in high-precision tasks. RegGAN [30] solves these problems by treating misalignment artifacts as label noise and adding a registration subnetwork to model and adapt to noise from misalignment. Through joint optimization, this dual-framework approach improves image translation quality and registration accuracy, effectively tackling multi-task learning challenges in deformable medical image analysis.

We modified the RegGAN network in PyTorch by adding a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) [31] into the generator of the conventional to enhance the local details. The overall architecture is illustrated in Fig. 1 CBAM, a lightweight attention module, combines channel and spatial attention to adaptively recalibrate feature maps, helping the model focus on key features. While the original RegGAN uses residual blocks for context and multi-scale feature extraction, it struggles with complex scenes and fine details. By incorporating CBAM, our CBAMRegGAN model achieves better performance in generating synthetic CT images from CBCT data. Figure 2 presents the architecture of the modified generator utilized in this study. In the CBAMRegGAN generator, a CBAM module is added before the residual blocks to improve feature extraction. After initial convolution, the feature map enters the CBAM module, where channel attention highlights important channels using pooling and a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), followed by spatial attention that emphasizes key spatial regions. This dual attention mechanism enhances relevant features, suppresses noise, and improves model expressiveness. The refined feature map then proceeds to up-sampling, enabling the generator to produce higher-quality, more detailed images with greater realism and accuracy.

Results

Image evaluation

The proposed CBAMRegGAN model’s results were quantitatively compared with three baseline models: Pix2Pix, CycleGAN, and RegGAN. Image quality was assessed using NMAE, PSNR, and SSIM, comparing generated CBCT images with real CT scans. CBAMRegGAN showed a significantly higher PSNR than conventional CycleGAN (29.52 ± 2.28 dB vs. 28.19 ± 1.18 dB) (Table 2 and Fig. 3). CBAMRegGAN also exhibited marginal improvements in NMAE (0.0129 ± 0.004 vs. 0.0157 ± 0.004) and SSIM (0.910 ± 0.025 vs. 0.889 ± 0.024) compared to CycleGAN. Pix2Pix outperformed CycleGAN, especially in PSNR (28.91 ± 2.09 dB vs. 28.19 ± 1.18), demonstrating better structural and visual quality preservation through unpaired image-to-image translation. RegGAN achieved a PSNR of 29.13 ± 2.25 dB, an SSIM of 0.905 ± 0.026, and an NMAE of 0.0135 ± 0.004, surpassing CycleGAN in all metrics.

Discussion

Radiomic features are increasingly used in clinical research to characterize tumor heterogeneity and detect subtle pathological changes that are not visually apparent. However, their reproducibility remains a concern due to sensitivity to image quality variations. For instance, feature values can vary significantly across different reconstruction kernels, acquisition settings, or slice thicknesses, even for the same patient [11, 13]. This study demonstrates that deep learning-enhanced CBCT images of NPC exhibit reduced noise and artifacts compared to conventional CBCT, resulting in more stable radiomic features and improved outcome prediction reliability. Image quality is influenced by multiple factors—including scanner hardware, reconstruction algorithms, and anatomical variability—highlighting the need for standardized protocols. Importantly, the impact of image degradation on radiomic features is non-linear and cannot be corrected using simple scaling methods. Instead, deep learning plays a key role in restoring image quality before feature extraction. Our model effectively corrected CBCT-derived radiomic features: as shown in Zhang et al. [24], the linear correlation (R² = 0.0143) between uncorrected 4D-CBCT and reference 4D-CT features was minimal, but deep learning significantly reduced the mean squared error (MSE = 0.4314), demonstrating its efficacy. These findings emphasize the importance of deep learning-based enhancement in improving CBCT radiomic accuracy. Without such correction, CBCT features may introduce systematic biases, leading to less reliable outcome predictions compared to CT-based models.

This study aims to enhance the accuracy of radiomic features through deep learning-based CBCT image improvement. As noted earlier, CBCT images are more susceptible to noise artifacts, spatial distortions, and HU inconsistencies compared to CT. As shown in Fig. 5, the original CBCT HU histogram deviates notably from reference CT scans, primarily due to under-sampled acquisition data. Such under-sampling violates the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, resulting in incomplete projection data and an ill-posed reconstruction process. This leads to inaccuracies in pixel intensity values, which can compromise radiomic feature extraction. This study has several limitations. First, the effectiveness of CBCT enhancement varies with tumor size. As the model was trained on whole images, larger tumors show greater feature accuracy improvement. Future work will explore region-specific training focused on tumor regions. Second, deformation errors may arise during CT–CBCT alignment due to patient positioning or respiratory motion. Residual misalignment after deformable registration can affect radiomic consistency and introduce uncertainty. Third, the improved HU accuracy of the synthetic CT supports potential application in dose calculation for adaptive radiotherapy. Future work could integrate this framework with high-fidelity dose engines such as the GoldMC Monte Carlo model, which has demonstrated accurate dose computation across multiple linacs for flattened (FF) and flattening filter free (FFF) 6 MV beam energies [34], thereby enabling end-to-end validation that directly links image-quality gains with dosimetric reliability using CBAMRegGAN-enhanced synthetic CT. Finally, changes in tumor texture over time further contribute to observed discrepancies. Future efforts will target improved registration accuracy to enhance CT reconstruction and radiomic reliability.

Our study explores how CBCT image quality affects NPC radiological analysis. We compared our new model with CycleGAN, Pix2Pix, and RegGAN—models not widely explored in prior studies. This focus is vital for building robust radiological analysis and adds a novel dimension to our research. To expand on these findings, we intend to collect more data from other institutions. Additionally, while we used the CBAMRegGAN model for image enhancement, we haven’t compared it with emerging models like diffusion models. Future research could incorporate these comparisons to further refine our results.

Conclusion

This study investigated how CBCT image quality affects radiomic features in NPC and assessed the potential of deep learning-based enhancement to improve radiomic analysis. We found that factors such as under-sampling and reconstruction algorithms influence radiomic features to varying degrees. Our results indicate that deep learning can enhance the accuracy and consistency of radiomic feature extraction from CBCT images in NPC.
