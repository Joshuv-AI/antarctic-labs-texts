The relationships between tensile strength, mode I and mode II fracture toughness of rock: An empirical and microcracking perspective
Authors: Wei Sun, Jinsheng Zhao, Shunchuan Wu, Wenbing Guo, Zhiqiang Hou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The empirical relationships between tensile strength, mode I and mode II fracture toughness obtained by previous studies have the problem of a small amount of data for fitting, and more data are needed for further study. There is a discrepancy between the theoretical prediction and the experimental results of the relationship between mode II and mode I fracture toughness. Because of this, a review is conducted to determine the relationships among tensile strength, mode I fracture toughness, and mode II fracture toughness from the data gathered from the available literature. The results indicate that the mode I and mode II fracture toughness have linear relationships with tensile strength with the linear coefficient of 0.141 and 0.219, respectively. The linear coefficient between mode II and mode I fracture toughness is 2.389, indicating that the classical mixed mode criteria are mistaken the empirical relationship between mode II and mode I fracture toughness. The microcracking mechanism and failure model are quantified and compared in detail among tensile test, mode I and mode II fracture toughness tests based on moment tensor. It found that the nature of the source can be related to each other, which gives some light on the reason for the existence of certain relationships between these macroscopic parameters from a microcracking perspective.

Introduction

The tensile strength and fracture toughness of rocks are important parameters for studying failure theory and determining engineering stability. To date, the International Society for Rock Mechanics (ISRM) has developed two, four and one core-based standard methods for measuring the tensile strength (σt), mode I fracture toughness (KIc) and mode II fracture toughness (KIIc), namely, direct tensile test (DT), Brazilian test (BT) [1], the chevron bend (CB) test [2], short rod (SR) test [2], cracked chevron notched Brazilian disc (CCNBD) test [2], semicircular bend (SCB) test [3]and punch-through shear test (PTS) [4]. Compared with the mode I and mode II fracture toughness test methods, the suggested methods for determining the tensile strength are easy to prepare and simple to load. Therefore, establishing an empirical relationship between tensile strength and fracture toughness may be useful for evaluating mechanical parameters and even easier to obtain mode I and mode II fracture toughness. The relationships can be the reference for preliminary design, and the micro-analysis is meaningful for understanding fracture mechanics of rock.

Several studies have attempted to establish general empirical relationships between σt, KIc and KIIc to gain insight into rock fracture mechanics behaviour and facilitate engineering applications. Some studies summaries typical values of KIIc/KIc for several rock types. Rao et al. [10] and Al-Shayea et al. [11] found that the coefficient of 2–3 is usually assumed for ambient conditions, while Lockner [12] found that the coefficient of 15. However, these studies have a small amount of data for fitting. In the past 20 years, the research of fracture toughness has been a hot field, and a large number of laboratory test data have been generated. It is necessary to collect these data to obtain a more representative empirical relationship.

The phenomena observed in the tests for hard and brittle rocks indicate that the stress-induced microcracks are axial tensile cracks rather than shearing, which is also responsible for the expansion and hysteric effects of the rock. The relationship between the compressive strength and tensile strength exists because they have similar mesoscopic fracture mechanisms. The mode I fractures in rocks are separated by tensile stress, and the fracture mode is related to tensile strength [6]. Three-dimensional brittle shear fracturing by tensile crack interaction [13]. The relationship between fracture parameters and macroscopic parameters of rocks can be used to investigate the fracture behaviour of rocks. At present, the research on the relationships between σt, KIc and KIIc are mostly focusing on macroscopic phenomenological research, and lacking the explanation of mesoscopic mechanisms.

The purpose of this work is to count a large number of laboratory experimental data obtaining more representative empirical relationships and analyze their associations from a mesoscopic perspective. The new empirical relationships are suggested. The classical fracture criterions are reviewed and analyses under tensile, mode I and II loading are presented based on bonded particle model and moment tensor to quantitatively describe the event mechanisms of different tests.

The reason why the mode I fracture toughness and tensile strength are related

Theoretically, for a small range of yield condition, the shape and size of the fracture process zone are approximately estimated using linear elasticity theory. There is a formula: KIc=σt2πr (plane stress), showing that the KIc is linearly related to the tensile strength. In terms of experiments, some studies have shown that there is an empirical relationship between some strength parameters of rock. Jaeger and Cook [14] showed that the ratio of compression (UCS)-tensile strength (TS) is about 8∼15, and found the relationship between UCS and TS through the Griffith criterion: σ1 = 8σt or σ1 = 12σt. Subsequently, the studies on empirical formulas between parameters was extended to point load strength (PLS), crack initiation stress (CI), mode I fracture toughness (KIc) [6,15,16].

Zhang [6] summed up the reasons for the relationship between KIc and σt from the perspectives of fracture surface and fracture characteristics: (1) the macroscopic fracture characteristics of similar fracture surfaces, namely the propagation of a single crack or a few microcracks in the same plane, forming only two fracture surfaces (under quasi-static or low-speed loads); (2) the similar meso-fracture characteristics of the fractured surfaces. The meso-cracks induced by loading; (3) the KIc is dominated by the maximum principal stress (i.e., σt) instead of strain energy in experiments, implying that there is an inherent relationship between KIc and σt.

In terms of mesoscopic simulation, Potyondy and Cundall [17] studied the force distribution near the crack tip in a cracked cubically contact-bonded disk assembly, and concluded that the relationship between the KIc and the mesoscopic bond tensile strength is as follows: KIc=σt′παR, where σt′ is the tensile strength of the bonded particle material, α is a non-dimensional factor related to the packing irregularity, strength heterogeneity and bond ductility,αR represents the apparent internal length scale of the bonded particle material.

In this study, particle flow code (PFC) and moment tensor were used to study the meso-fracture mechanism of both tests. Refer to Appendix 2 Methods and our team's literature [[18], [19], [20]] for a more detailed description of the PFC and moment tensor. Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 show the source mechanics and T-k plot of both tests. The result indicates that the deformation characteristic of the notch tip region of the mode I fracture is the tensile effect perpendicular to the surface of the cracked notch, which is similar characteristics to the meso-fracture of the Brazilian test as the fracture mechanism dominated by horizontal tensile sources. The statistical data in this study included various test methods such as CCBD, SC3PB, and PTS. The results of KIIc determination with and without confining pressure and determination with low and high pressure are very different, which is the main reason for the relatively low correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.66). It should be noted that up to now, among the test methods, the PTS test is the only ISRM suggested method. Backers and Stephansson [21] reported a shear stress increases nonlinearly with confining pressure, and KIIc also exhibited similar behaviour. The increase in shear stress can be explained by a bilinear relationship: with the increase of the confining pressure, the average shear stress between the notches increases sharply at low confining pressure; while with the increase of the confining pressure, the shear stress required for crack propagation increases moderately at high confining pressure. Constant fracture toughness can be obtained by testing at very high confining pressures and without the friction effect. The mode II fracture toughness is susceptible to test method and confining pressure, therefore, the general empirical relationship between σt and KIIc still needs a lot of experiments and a variety of test methods to verify.

The reason why the mode II fracture toughness and tensile strength are related

The maximum circumferential stress criterion is a commonly accepted hypothesis, showing that crack extension starts in the plane perpendicular to the direction of maximum tension [22]. Consequently, mode II fracture toughness were controlled by tensile strength. This implies an inherent relationship between mode II fracture toughness and tensile strength, i.e., σt ∼ KIIc. Backers [22] reported that mode II fracture toughness has a strong linear correlation with tensile strength at low confining pressure, and a weak correlation with tensile strength at high confining pressure in PTS-test.

In terms of micro-mechanisms (Fig. 7 and Fig. 8), the author's team [23]used PFC3D and moment tensor to simulate the meso-fracture mechanism of the PTS-test, showing that the tensile cracks are the dominant, or at least effective, tensile micro-fracturing at low confining pressure. The tensile cracks were suspended and arrested with increasing the confinement and the several tensile micro-cracks can generate a large shear source mechanics. Some sources are not pure shear cracks but contain an isotropic component (tensile mechanics), and tensile sources also play an important role. The reason why the mode I and mode II fracture toughness are related

Three classical mixed mode fracture criteria have been established on the assumption that the crack extension are governed by stress, strain or energy, i.e., maximum circumferential stress criterion (σθ-criterion) [25], maximum strain energy release rate criterion (G-criterion) [26] and minimum strain energy density criterion (S-criterion) [27]. The σθ-criterion states that, the crack extension begins when σθmax reaches a critical value, i.e., KIIc = 0.886KIc. The G-criterion also obtain a similar result that KIIc is smaller than KIc (KIIc = 0.627KIc). The S-criterion obtains a result that KIIc is close to or slightly larger than KIc for the case of Poisson's ratio, v = 0.2–0.3 (KIIc = 0.9–1.7KIc).

Backers [22] and Healy et al. [13]reported that tensile, uniaxial failure and three dimensional brittle shear fracturing suggested to be generated by the tensile crack interaction, hence they should depend on KIc. A commonly accepted hypothesis is that crack extension starts in the plane perpendicular to the direction of maximum tension. Consequently, fracture toughness, irrespective of mode, were controlled by tensile strength. This implies an inherent relationship between fracture toughness and tensile strength, i.e., σt ∼ KIc, σt ∼ KIIc and, hence, KIIc ∼ KIc. For PTS-test suggested by ISRM, Backers [22] reported that a linear relationship between KIc and K0IIc versus σt, where K0IIc represents the mode II fracture toughness with low confining pressure. Furthermore, they concluded that K0IIc ∼ KIc. It is concluded from these considerations that mode I is the dominant, or at least effective, mode of microfracturing at low confining pressure in the PTS-test. The comparison of theoretical and experimental results shows that all mixed mode fracture criteria are impossible to predict mode II fracture toughness. There is a clear contradiction between the theoretical and the statistical results of experimental data. The new mixed mode fracture criterion is needed for determining mode I and II fracture of rocks.

For brittle material, this is obviously impractical, since the shear strength of brittle rock is known to be greater than the tensile strength. The S-criterion obtained the KIIc is close to or slightly larger than KIc for the case of Poisson's ratio less than 0.33 (KIIc = 0.9–1.7KIc). In general, KIIc is larger than KIc in experiments. Bahrami et al. [28] demonstrated that KIIc is about 4, 2.5 and 4.5 times greater than KIc in samples of limestone, marble and granite, respectively. Rao et al. The experimental results are higher than the theoretical predictions by the three classical criteria, which may be the enhanced fracture resistance in mode II caused by friction on the fracture faces, or may be difficult to adjust an accurate of crack direction (i.e., KI = 0).

Discussions

Detailed assessment of data quality

We elaborate a larger dataset of experimental data with the purpose, among others, of making a sounder assessment of the empirical relationships connecting these mechanical properties. This is some sort of “force brute” approach by which the validity of the result is linked to the acritical use of a large dataset and acceptably high correlation coefficients. To date, four methods are suggested by ISRM to measure the KIc, namely CB (ISRM 2007) [2], SR (ISRM 2007) [2], CCNBD test (ISRM 2007) [2], and SCB test (Kuruppu et al., 2014) [3]. The previous findings (Funatsu et al., 2015, Aliha et al., 2017, Wong et al., 2019) [29,30,31] that the KIc obtained by SCB test are generally lower than that obtained by CB test. Wong et al. (2019) [30] reported that the mean KIc values measured by the SCB test is 25.8 % and 56.5 % lower than those using CB test and the CCNSCB test, respectively. It is easy to see from Appendix 1 tables that the values measured by non ISRM recommended methods differ significantly from those measured by ISRM recommended methods, and the data reliability of its measured values may be questionable. Therefore, non ISRM measurement data with annotations ∗ is excluded from the mode I fracture toughness values in Appendix 1 data Tables 2–4 Even the fracture toughness determined by the ISRM recommended method still varies among different methods. This study did not grant the acceptability of the results coming from the different mode I fracture toughness testing methods due to the lack of a definite multiple relationships. In future research, quantitative conversion should be conducted between test experimental data from different methods. To avoid this issue, we use one test method (SCB, CB, SR or CCNBD) for either KIc and σt in this study. Fig. 13 shows the relationships between KIc and σt for different ISRM-suggested methods. SCB, CB, SR or CCNBD methods contain 50, 25, 37, 30 sets of points, resulting that the linear coefficients are 0.114, 0.178, 0.142 and 0.128. The correlation coefficient R2 presents a higher value than that obtained from all data. The results obtained by different mode I fracture toughness measurement methods have the following statistical relationship: CB > SR > CCNBD > SCB. It is a limitation that this study did not consider the impact of rock geological factors (size effect, intergranular cement, grain interlocking, porosity, foliations, fluids, temperature, etc.) on fracture toughness, which changes the internal structure of the rock and the forces acting on it.Fig. 13Relationships between KIc and σt for different ISRM-suggested methods.Fig. 13

The influence of rock type on the results

The data is further grouped into sedimentary, igneous and metamorphic rock according to lithology. Sedimentary rock contains 88 sets of points, including sandstone, limestone, tuff, shale, coal, and dolostone. Igneous rock contains 123 sets of points, including andesite, granite, diorite, lherzolite, gabbro, basalt, and syenite. Metamorphic rock contains 83 sets of points, including marble, amphibolite and rust stone. Fig. 14 shows the relationships between KIc and σt for sedimentary, igneous and metamorphic rock with 95 % confidence band. The linear coefficients for the three lithological rocks are 0.133, 0.147 and 0.126, respectively. The reason for large dispersion for igneous and metamorphic rocks may be due to the complexity of rock composition and the different measuring methods. In the future, fitting research should be conducted focusing on one lithology and one measurement method. In addition, a weakness of this study is that there is relatively little data on mode II fracture toughness, and more data are needed for further grouping discussion.Fig. 14Relationships between KIc and σt for (a) sedimentary, (b) igneous and (c) metamorphic rock.Fig. 14

Conclusions

In this paper, first, a large number of laboratory test data were collected for statistical analysis, and general empirical relationships between tensile strength, mode I and mode II fracture toughness were obtained. Then, the source natures of tensile, mode I and mode II loading are analyzed and compared using PFC model and moment tensor to quantitatively describe the internal connections between macro and micro scales. The main concluding remarks are as follows:1.The tensile strength, mode I and mode II fracture toughness can be empirically related by the equations, KIc = 0.141σt, KIIc = 0.219σt. In experiments, the tensile strength can be determined more easier and lower cost than that of mode I and mode II fracture toughness of rock, which gives a helpful method for predicting the mode I and mode II fracture toughness from the tensile strength.2.The mode I and mode II fracture toughness can be empirically related by the equation, KIIc = 2.389KIc. A further theoretical study is presented based on three classical mixed mode fracture criteria, showing that the KIIc/KIc of the empirical relation is larger than the results of theoretical prediction.3.With the use of PFC model and moment tensor, the tensile strength, mode I and mode II fracture toughness tests are quantitatively analyzed in detail from a microcracking perspective, shedding some light on the interlinkages between those parameters.
