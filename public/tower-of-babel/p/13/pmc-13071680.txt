Mitochondrial DNA (MtDNA) 3243 A>G mutation associated with recurrent cholangitis post-Kasai procedure in biliary atresia
Authors: Jie Sun, Yanan Zhang, Dayan Sun, Jinshi Huang
Published: 2026

We sincerely thank Prof. Josef Finsterer for his interest in our case report (1) and for the insightful and constructive comments (2). We appreciate this opportunity to clarify the clinical details and address the points raised in his letter.

Association between m.3243A>G and recurrent cholangitis

We fully agree that recurrent cholangitis is a well-recognized postoperative complication of the Kasai procedure and cannot be ascribed solely to the m.3243A>G mitochondrial variant. Our objective was not to imply a direct causative link but to highlight a potential association between mitochondrial dysfunction and persistent inflammatory activity in a patient with biliary atresia (BA).

As supported by Koh et al. [2018] (3), mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations may aggravate cholestatic liver injury and fibrosis in BA. Our case contributes to this evidence by describing a patient with a confirmed m.3243A>G variant who exhibited recurrent, treatment-resistant cholangitis and rapid progression of liver fibrosis despite standard therapy. Larger functional and genetic cohort studies are needed to establish whether mtDNA mutations predispose certain BA patients to worse inflammatory or fibrotic outcomes.

Clinical summary and multisystem assessment

The patient was a 7-month-old female infant who presented at 2 months of age with progressive jaundice, acholic stools, and dark urine. Initial laboratory results revealed markedly elevated cholestatic enzymes (gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase: 578.8 U/L), with a total bilirubin of 127.47 µmol/L and a direct bilirubin of 92.35 µmol/L. Imaging confirmed the absence of bile duct patency, leading to a Kasai hepatoportoenterostomy. Histopathology of the liver demonstrated early biliary fibrosis and inflammation consistent with extrahepatic biliary obstruction (Ishak stage 4 fibrosis). Despite initial postoperative improvement, the patient experienced three readmissions within 3 months for recurrent cholangitis, characterized by fever (>39 ℃) and elevated CRP (up to 257 mg/L), without identifiable bacterial pathogens. Ultrasound elastography revealed progressive liver stiffness, increasing from 12.1 to 42.0 kPa, suggesting accelerated fibrosis. Subsequent whole-exome sequencing identified the pathogenic mtDNA 3243A>G variant with a blood heteroplasmy rate of 79.08%. Her mother was also identified as an asymptomatic carrier with 15.72% heteroplasmy, indicating maternal transmission. The patient was monitored for possible multisystem involvement. No findings consistent with myopathy, encephalopathy, cardiomyopathy, diabetes, renal dysfunction, or hearing loss were detected. Serum lactate levels were intermittently elevated during febrile episodes (peak: 3.8 mmol/L); however, cerebrospinal fluid testing was not performed due to the absence of neurological symptoms. This clinical profile supports a predominantly hepatic phenotype of mitochondrial dysfunction in this case, without the systemic manifestations typically seen in classical mitochondrial syndromes.

Heteroplasmy, mtDNA copy number, and haplotype

The heteroplasmy rate of 79.08% was calculated as 9,195 mutant reads from 11,627 total reads in peripheral blood. mtDNA copy number and haplotype were not analyzed, which we acknowledge as limitations. These parameters may influence disease penetrance and severity and should be systematically assessed in future investigations involving BA patients with mitochondrial mutations.

Liver tissue evaluation

Because the liver transplantation was performed at another center, post-transplant tissue analysis (including respiratory chain enzyme assays and liver-specific heteroplasmy testing) was not feasible. We agree that such analyses—especially of the explanted liver—would provide valuable insights into tissue-level mitochondrial impairment and should be incorporated into future studies exploring the relationship between mitochondrial variants and BA pathogenesis.

Prognosis and post‑transplant outcome

After liver transplantation (performed 5 months after Kasai portoenterostomy), the patient made an uneventful recovery. At the most recent follow-up (20 months of age), she remained clinically stable, with normal liver graft function, improved bile flow, and no recurrence of cholangitis. Her current immunosuppressive regimen includes tacrolimus and corticosteroids. No clinical or biochemical evidence of mitochondrial toxicity or secondary organ involvement has been observed to date. We continue close multidisciplinary follow-up to monitor graft function and potential long-term mitochondrial effects.

Pedigree and maternal inheritance

We appreciate the observation regarding Fig.3. The patient’s mother was confirmed to be an asymptomatic carrier (heteroplasmy 15.72%), demonstrating maternal inheritance of the m.3243A>G variant. We agree that the figure caption should specify the heteroplasmy ratios for both mother and proband to improve clarity and avoid misinterpretation of phenotypic transmission.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we agree with Prof. Finsterer that a causal link between the m.3243A>G variant and BA cannot be established from a single case report. However, our patient’s rapidly progressive hepatic fibrosis, persistent inflammation, and high-level heteroplasmy raise the possibility that mitochondrial dysfunction may exacerbate the disease process or modulate postoperative recovery in certain BA patients. We hope this report stimulates further research into the pathogenic and prognostic roles of mitochondrial genetics in BA and related cholestatic liver diseases.

We are deeply grateful to Prof. Finsterer for his valuable feedback, which has strengthened our interpretation and highlighted important directions for future studies.
