COVID vaccination and economic game decisions: The role of analytical thinking
Authors: Z-W Zher-Wen, Rongjun Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNon-vaccination is often described as non-cooperation, particularly as free-riding (i.e. the exploitation of others who cooperated), but empirical studies linking non-vaccination with non-cooperation/free-riding are scarce. The current study recruited 758 participants from the Prolific platform when the most severe COVID variant (Delta) was circulating. We examined whether the vaccinated and non-vaccinated differed in their decisions in various economic games (including social dilemmas and resource-allocation games) and whether such differences were explained by relevant dispositional traits (dark traits, generalized trust, and analytical thinking). We particularly aim to see whether traits that explained the link between vaccination and game decisions, also predicted vaccination itself. Results show no differences between vaccination groups in social-dilemma decisions involving the potential to free-ride. Instead, vaccination was only associated with resource-allocation decisions without elements of cooperation or exploitation. Specifically, supportive vaccination (getting vaccinated coupled with having a positive attitude toward vaccines) was associated with outcome-maximization in resource-allocation games, and this was largely explained by higher analytical thinking ability. Higher analytical thinking ability also predicted vaccination itself, and this link was partially mediated by generalized trust. Regardless of whether the act of non-vaccination itself is free-riding or not, the current study suggests that the motivations behind non-vaccination may relate more to weaknesses in analytical thinking. Our findings may inform about how to improve the strategies used to promote vaccination.KEYWORDS: Cooperation, free-riding, trust, dark trait, social dilemma, utilitarianism, vaccine hesitancy, public good, intelligence, resource allocation

Introduction

Combating a pandemic requires large-scale cooperation1–4 in complying with health and safety measures, including getting vaccinated to prevent the spread of infections. COVID-19 vaccines are estimated to save up to 20 million lives in the year 2021.5 They significantly reduce infection rates and prevent severe symptoms (e.g., hospitalization/ICU) and deaths,6,7 and may also protect against Long-COVID.8

Non-vaccination: free-riding?

A high vaccination rate results from the cooperation of the majority of the population, and some individuals may opt to refuse vaccination and instead “free-ride” on herd immunity9,10 to avoid any potential risks or side effects from the vaccines. Free-riding has been specifically defined as the exploitation of others who cooperated.3,4 Free-riders “take advantage of the benefits of other people’s cooperative behavior without themselves engaging in cooperative behavior,”11 and this also extends to other disease-preventing behaviors such as mask-wearing12,13 and social distancing.3,14

Free-riding in vaccination can be gauged by examining how participant’s perceived vaccination coverage rate influences their own vaccination decision/intention. An early study by Hershey et al.15 suggests the bandwagon effect (i.e., conformity behavior16,17): higher perceived coverage rate led to higher vaccination intention. This effect only disappeared when participants were cued on the possibility to free-ride on herd immunity, a finding that was also corroborated by more recent studies.18,19 Parker et al.20 found that only 9.5% of respondents claimed that a high coverage rate would make them less likely to get the flu vaccine, and only 35% of this group cited free-riding motivations. Their follow-up study corroborated the bandwagon effect in self-reported flu vaccine uptake.21 Two separate studies on flu vaccination22 and COVID vaccination23 intentions suggest that the bandwagon effect only dominates up to a certain coverage rate: at higher coverage rates where herd immunity starts to become possible, both free-riding and bandwagon effects were observed (although participants were not cued on the possibility of free-riding). In contrast to the studies above, various incentivized vaccination games showed that free-riding is the dominant strategy throughout.24,25

Several COVID vaccination studies directly asked participants for free-riding intentions. Caserotti et al.26 found among 5006 participants that both COVID vaccine hesitancy and the intention to free-ride on a high coverage rate (“When everyone else is vaccinated against COVID-19, then I don’t have to get vaccinated”), were low on average. Meanwhile, Su et al.27 attempted to gauge free-riding intention by asking if respondents engage in “wait-and-see behavior and waiting for others to be vaccinated.” However, this measure is problematic as it did not gauge what participants would eventually do if they saw many others getting vaccinated, which could either be free-riding or the opposite bandwagon effect.

Bradley and Navin11 argued that the non-vaccinated rarely possess attitudes and beliefs related to free-riding, i.e., vaccine refusal is not associated with the intention to free-ride. However, Parker et al.20 suggest that although individuals did not report consciously considering free-riding in their decisions to not vaccinate, some may nevertheless “subconsciously take advantage of herd immunity.”20 Galizzi et al.22 also suggest that free-riding effects found at high coverage rates occurred at least partially without participants’ awareness. Therefore, when attempting to examine the psychological basis of this link, directly asking participants their free-riding intentions may not be suitable.

Non-vaccination and free-riding behaviors/tendencies

Instead of directly asking about free-riding intentions or making inferences from coverage rate-dependent vaccination intentions, two studies28,29 linked non-vaccination with other forms of free-riding behaviors/tendencies: specifically, non-contribution toward public goods outside of COVID or disease-preventing context. This suggests that the non-vaccinated may in fact have a general tendency to free-ride.

Paseyro Mayol and Razzolini29 compared 1,506 municipalities within the Italian region of Lombardy, which was the region with the highest number of COVID cases and deaths in the country. At the municipality level, the payment rate of an inexpensive TV tax (compulsory but not enforced, which therefore reflects willingness to contribute toward public goods) was found to be the dominant predictor of COVID vaccination rate. In contrast, organ donation sign-up rate (proposed to reflect altruism) was a very weak predictor, while voting rate (proposed to reflect civic engagement) was not a significant predictor.

Meanwhile, at the individual level, Basili et al.28 compared the COVID vaccinated and non-vaccinated in an incentivized public good game (PGG) (with three slight variations between-subject), while also taking risk-taking tendency (measured through a risk escalation task) and trust (measured by asking participants to guess how many others in the group contributed in the PGG) into account. A borderline significant effect was only found when all three PGG variants were combined: non-vaccinated individuals were less likely to contribute compared to the vaccinated. Regression analysis also showed that non-vaccination was associated with reduced trust, although no group difference in trust was found between the vaccinated and non-vaccinated. The authors concluded that although the non-vaccinated were less likely to contribute in PGG, this was due to a reduced trust that others would contribute, rather than differences in selfish motives or risk aversion.

Dispositional traits implicated in cooperation and vaccination

While there is some preliminary evidence suggesting that non-vaccination and free-riding behaviors/tendencies are related,28,29 the potential underlying mechanisms are still unclear, with (dis)trust being one potential explanation.28 If non-vaccination reflects free-riding, then we should see some shared predictors for vaccination and other cooperation-related behaviors. For example, Fischer et al.30 proposed that stockpiling during a pandemic is a type of social dilemma,31 where there exist “a non-cooperative course of action that is (at times) tempting for each individual, and if all pursue this non-cooperative course of action, all end up worse off than if all had cooperated.”32 This argument is supported by the finding that Honesty–Humility, a dispositional trait that consistently predicts cooperative and prosocial behaviors in economic games,33 was also negatively related to stockpiling intentions and justifiability of stockpiling during the COVID pandemic.30 Free-riding in vaccination may also be described as a form of social dilemma: although individuals can avoid getting infected (in addition to avoiding any risks of the vaccines) by free-riding on others’ vaccination, mass infections would occur if all decides to free-ride. Motivated by Fischer et al.,30 we reviewed several major traits that were previously implicated in vaccination and cooperative behaviors.

Dark traits

Thielmann et al.’s33 meta-analysis of 770 studies found that dark traits associated with “ethically, morally, and socially questionable behaviors,”34 are the biggest predictors of non-cooperation in economic games. The most widely studied dark traits34 are the Dark Triad consisting of Machiavellianism, Narcissism, and Psychopathy.35 Machiavellianism involves “a cynical disregard for morality, and a focus on self-interest and personal gain,”36 Narcissism involves “a grandiose view of the self, a strong sense of entitlement and superiority, a lack of empathy, and a need for social admiration,”37 whereas Psychopathy involves “enduring antisocial behavior, diminished empathy and remorse, and disinhibited or bold behavior.”36

Scigała et al.’s38 meta-analysis showed that dark traits are negatively associated with both COVID-related compliance and the perception that COVID-preventing guidelines are protective. Several studies specifically showed (mostly) negative links between the Dark Triad and COVID vaccination, although findings were highly inconsistent on how they relate to each other. Hughes and Machan39 found that the negative relationship between all three Dark Triad and COVID vaccination intention is mediated by conspiracy-mindedness. Meanwhile, Konc et al.40 found that Machiavellianism (specifically deviousness) is the only Dark Triad that (negatively) predicts COVID vaccination willingness, controlling for risk-taking tendencies. In contrast, Howard41 found that Machiavellianism positively (and directly) predicted both COVID and flu vaccine uptake. Narcissism and Psychopathy were both negatively associated with COVID and flu vaccination, which the author attributed to a lack of trust in vaccine manufacturers (although trust was not measured). The discrepancy across studies may be partly explained by using different scales to measure the Dark Triad, and/or the inclusion of different covariates.

Trust propensity

Trust propensity (also called “generalized trust” or “social trust”) is consistently shown to predict cooperative/prosocial behavior in economic games,33 and is also implicated in vaccination decisions. As discussed above, Basili et al.28 suggest that the non-contribution in PGG seen among the non-vaccinated is due to the lack of trust that others will contribute. Indeed, institutional distrust (e.g., distrust in doctors, scientists, or government) is consistently linked with non-vaccination.42,43 Murphy et al.44 found that three types of institutional trust significantly predict vaccine acceptance even after controlling for dozens of other psychological variables.

Generalized trust per se, however, tends to be overlooked by vaccination studies.45,46 Generalized trust, trust in the flu vaccine, and trust in the vaccine production process were shown to be distinct (albeit highly related) concepts.47 Generalized trust and trust in the healthcare system were found to independently predict the intention to vaccinate against H1N1.48 In terms of COVID, the relationship between generalized trust and vaccination intention shows mixed findings. Several studies suggest a positive relationship,49,50 with Ahorsu et al.51 suggesting that trust in healthcare mediated the relationship between generalized trust and vaccination intention. However, two other studies show that while various types of institutional trust predict vaccination intention, generalized trust does not.52,53 Therefore, the relationship between generalized trust and vaccination requires further assessment.

Analytical thinking

The associations between analytical thinking and cooperative behavior are less clear,33 although it is crucially implicated in vaccination. Analytical thinking ability (versus intuitive thinking) is typically measured with the Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT),54,55 and a higher CRT score is positively associated with COVID vaccination intention/behavior.44,56–59 Importantly, Murphy et al.44 showed that a higher CRT score remained a significant predictor of COVID vaccine acceptance after controlling for dozens of other psychological variables. Pro-vaccination messages normally explain the effectiveness, safety, and importance of vaccines using statistics, and hence those with higher analytical thinking ability are more able to accurately judge that vaccination is the safer option.57,60

Non-vaccination is also widely linked with conspiracy-mindedness44 or beliefs in COVID conspiracy.42 Correspondingly, a higher CRT score is also negatively associated with beliefs in COVID conspiracies.59,61,62 Therefore, higher analytical thinking ability may also promote vaccination through reduced susceptibility to false information or COVID conspiracy.59

Although CRT was designed to measure analytical thinking, a recent meta-analysis suggests that CRT is in fact a measure of general intelligence.63 Studies examining the link between intelligence and vaccination are scarce, with some evidence suggesting that increased intelligence (measured through established tests such as Raven’s Progressive Matrices) is related to decreased vaccine hesitancy64 and increased vaccine uptake.65

The relationship between CRT/intelligence and cooperative behavior is more complex. In repeated games especially, those with higher intelligence often show cooperative behavior inconsistent with the Nash equilibrium, which has been attributed to social considerations.66 Such cooperation, however, appears to be context-dependent.67–69

The current study

Vaccine refusal is frequently described as non-cooperation and free-riding,2–4,70–74 but empirical studies linking non-vaccination with non-cooperation are scarce. Directly asking participants about their free-riding intentions26,27 may not be suitable, as it is argued that free-riding may occur without explicit intention/awareness.20,22 Importantly, two recent studies managed to relate non-vaccination behaviorally with other forms of non-contribution toward the public good,28,29 suggesting that the non-vaccinated may in fact have a general tendency to free-ride.

The current study expands on the approach by Basili et al.28 and Paseyro Mayol and Razzolini.29 In addition to public good contribution, free-riding behavior also occurs in other cooperation scenarios such as the Prisoner’s Dilemma.11 Additionally, other scenarios outside of cooperation, such as resource allocation, also contain affordances and motives (e.g., exploitation) relevant to vaccination.31 We therefore examine whether the vaccinated and non-vaccinated differ in their decisions in various social dilemmas and resource allocation scenarios unrelated to vaccination, as measured through economic games. Importantly, the shared predictors for vaccination and other cooperation-related behaviors30 are still unclear. We therefore examine whether differences in game decisions are explained by relevant dispositional traits, and whether these traits also predict vaccination itself.

Understanding the predictors of non-vaccination is crucial in informing us about how to formulate more effective pro-vaccination strategies. For example, if non-vaccination is indeed related to free-riding, then increasing the payoffs for vaccination versus non-vaccination should promote vaccine uptake,11,70 such as through providing economic incentives for vaccination and/or restricting access to goods and services for non-vaccination.

Methods

Due to word count constraints, additional details of the methodologies are presented in the supplementary materials.

Participants and procedure

Owing to the unique design of this study, when it was first conducted, there was only one previous study with a slightly similar research question: Fischer et al.30 who examined whether traits that predict decisions in economic games also predict COVID-related stockpiling intention (another social dilemma besides vaccination). They had 797 participants in the final sample, and the current study used this as a general sample size guideline and recruited 758 participants. After excluding 15 participants who failed the attention checks and 18 participants belonging to a low sample group (see below), the final sample had 725 participants.

All tasks and surveys were presented with Qualtrics, and data was collected through recruiting participants from the Prolific platform. Participants were told that we aim to examine “how data on decision-making can be used in predicting decision-making styles and preferences of individuals and groups.” There was no indication that the study was related to vaccination, and vaccination data were collected directly from the Prolific platform (i.e., they were not part of the Qualtrics surveys).

All data were collected between November 14 to 24, 2021, when the Delta COVID variant was circulating. The Omicron variant was only detected at the very end of November 2021, and became the dominant variant from mid-December 2021 or January 2022 onwards.75 In contrast with the subsequent Omicron variants, vaccines were highly effective at preventing Delta’s transmission.76 Moreover, Delta’s symptoms and mortality rate were also considerably more severe than the Omicron variants,77 and Delta is indeed the most severe COVID variant to date.78 Therefore, vaccination for the Delta variant was highly justifiable.

Although vaccine uptake is normally assumed to also entail support for vaccines, the vaccinated can actually still display vaccine hesitancy.80–83 Individuals who oppose vaccines may ultimately get vaccinated for multiple reasons, and they would likely have different psychological dispositions compared with vaccine supporters. Moore et al.81 interviewed 1475 participants during the 15-min waiting period after they received the COVID vaccine, and found that 867 of the respondents reported some level of vaccine hesitancy. These 867 respondents provided a wide range of reasons for getting the vaccine, including prosocial motivations (22% of all reasons given), desire to protect loved ones (14%), desire to protect self (34%) and reasons related to vaccine mandates (16%). Following Zher-Wen et al.,83 we assessed vaccine uptake while controlling for vaccine attitudes, with three different groups: supportive vaccination (S-V, getting vaccinated with a positive attitude, n = 486), non-supportive vaccination (NS-V, getting vaccinated without positive attitude, n = 119), and non-supportive non-vaccination (NS-NV, neither getting vaccinated nor have a positive attitude toward vaccine, n = 120). An additional 18 participants were non-vaccinated supporters (S-NV), which were excluded from analyses due to the extremely low sample size. Most participants in NS-V had a neutral (n = 105) rather than a negative (n = 14) vaccine attitude, while NS-NV had a slightly more balanced split of neutral (n = 49) and negative (n = 71) attitudes.

Measures

Economic games

Eight types of decisions in economic games were measured (see supplementary materials for more details): four decisions involved social dilemmas, while another four involved resource allocation.

For the four social dilemma decisions,31 one concerns how much to contribute in a Public Good Game: “PGG: contributed amount,” and three concerns whether to cooperate or defect in a sequential Prisoner’s Dilemma (SPD): “First mover: Partner choice unknown,” “Second mover: Partner cooperated,” and “Second mover: Partner defected.” These social dilemmas allow measuring cooperation and free-riding behaviors.

Meanwhile, different variants of the Ultimatum Game (UG) were used to measure four resource allocation decisions: “Proposer: proposed offer,” “Responder: MAO,” “Responder: MAO for random offer,” and “Proposer: generosity game.” Resource-allocation games31 do not involve any cooperation elements, but just like social dilemmas, they involve affordances (e.g., potential for exploitation) and motives (e.g., altruism, fairness, maximizing social welfare) relevant to vaccination decisions.84 Out of all four decisions here, only “Proposer: proposed offer” involves the potential to exploit, which is defined as increasing participant’s own outcome at others’ costs.31

Dispositional traits

Key dispositional traits previously implicated in vaccination and cooperation were measured. The Dark Triad was measured through (a) the Short Dark Triad (SD3) scale consisting of three subscales85 each containing nine items: Machiavellianism, Narcissism, and Psychopathy; and (b) the Machiavellianism Personality Scale (MPS) consisting of four subscales86: amorality (5 items), distrust of others (5 items), desire for control (3 items), and desire for status (3 items). A separate Machiavellianism scale (i.e., MPS) was included in order to take a closer look at Machiavellianism since previous studies found conflicting results on whether it is positively41 or negatively39,40 related to COVID vaccination. Crucially, the MPS also contains the highly relevant “distrust of others” subscale, measuring generalized trust. Lastly, analytical thinking ability was measured through the seven-question Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT).54,55 A higher score indicates a higher level of analytical thinking (or higher intelligence63).

Analysis

The first set of analyses examined whether the vaccinated and non-vaccinated differ in each game decision and each dispositional trait. In all analyses, vaccination group (S-V, NS-V, NS-NV) is the independent/predictor variable. Dependent/outcome variables are categorized into four categories according to their nature: social dilemmas, resource-allocation games, dark traits, and CRT. The list of statistical tests performed is described below. Firstly, CRT score was subjected to a between-group ANOVA. For resource-allocation games, all decisions in the UG-based games were together subjected to a between-group MANOVA: “Proposer: proposed offer,” “Responder: MAO,” “Responder: MAO for random offer,” and “Proposer: generosity game” (each scored out of 30). For dark traits, the SD3 subscales and the MPS subscales were together subjected to a between-group MANOVA. Lastly, for social dilemmas (PGG and SPD), “PGG: contributed amount” (scored out of 20) was subjected to a between-group ANOVA, while each of the three decisions under the SPD (where 0 = defect, 1 = cooperate) was respectively subjected to separate binomial logistic regressions. Since there were four separate tests of social dilemmas, a Bonferroni-corrected alpha value of 0.0125 was also used to determine the statistical significance of these analyses.87,88

If any significant group differences in game decisions were found, the second set of analyses examined whether such differences are explained by any of the dispositional traits. Here, hierarchical logistic (for SPD decisions) or linear (for all other game decisions) regressions would be conducted. In each regression, vaccination group (S-V, NS-V, NS-NV) would be entered as the predictor in block 1 (dummy coded for linear regressions, with S-V as the reference group), while dispositional traits would be additionally entered as the predictors in Block 2.

The final analyses were multinomial logistic regressions identifying the dispositional traits that predict COVID vaccination. We particularly aim to see whether traits that explain the link between vaccination and game decisions also predict vaccination itself. Vaccination group (S-V, NS-V, NS-NV) is the outcome variable. In Regression 1, the SD3 subscales (Machiavellianism, Narcissism, and Psychopathy) and CRT scores were entered simultaneously as predictors. Regression 2 is the same as Regression 1, except that the MPS subscales (Amorality, Control, Status, and Distrust) were entered as predictors instead of the SD3 subscales. MPS and SD3 were not entered together in the same regression, due to the high overlap (correlations) between their subscales.

Results

Due to word count constraints, additional details of the results are presented in the supplementary materials.

Dispositional traits accounting for group differences in game decisions

The first set of analyses examines whether the vaccinated and non-vaccinated differ in each game decision and each dispositional trait (see supplementary materials). For traits, significant group differences were found in Machiavellianism, Narcissism, “desire for status,” “distrust of others,” and CRT. For game decisions, group differences were only found in three types of resource-allocation games: “Responder: MAO,” “Responder: MAO for random offer,” and “Proposer: generosity game.”

The second set of analyses therefore used hierarchical linear regressions (see Table 3) to examine whether any of the dispositional traits can account for differences in game decisions. Step 2 (vaccination and traits) is significant and explained 3.3% of the variance; R2 Change = .023, F(6, 714) = 2.772, p = .011. Higher analytical thinking ability is another major predictor of COVID vaccination (see Figure 1, right panel). In both regressions (R1 and R2), a higher CRT score significantly predicted S-V relative to NS-V (AOR: R1 = 1.178, R2 = 1.124) and NS-NV (AOR: R1 = 1.324, R2 = 1.298). Higher CRT score also significantly (R2 AOR = 1.154) or marginally (R1 AOR = 1.124) predicted NS-V relative to NS-NV. In other words, higher analytical thinking explains vaccine uptake itself, in addition to vaccine support.

We did an exploratory analysis to examine the potential mediating role of distrust in the link between CRT score and vaccine uptake. Here, the vaccine uptake variable was binary (0: non-vaccinated, 1: vaccinated). Increased CRT score was found to predict decreased distrust, b = −0.482, p < .001; and decreased distrust in turn predicts vaccine uptake, b = −0.048, p = .005. The indirect effect of CRT on vaccine uptake through distrust was significant, b = 0.023, SE = 0.010, 95% CI = 0.005–0.046. The direct effect of CRT on vaccine uptake while controlling for distrust, was also significant, b = 0.238, p < .001. Therefore, distrust propensity partially mediated the relationship between CRT score and vaccine uptake.

Discussions

Analytical thinking explains the link between vaccination and outcome-maximization in games

Vaccine refusal is often conceptualized as non-cooperation, particularly as free-riding,2–4,71–74 but there is a lack of empirical evidence linking them. Two recent studies provide preliminary evidence suggesting that non-vaccination and free-riding behaviors/tendencies are related.28,29 The current study, however, found no differences between vaccination groups in social-dilemma decisions involving the potential to free-ride. Instead, vaccination is associated with resource-allocation decisions without elements of cooperation or exploitation. Specifically, supportive vaccination (getting vaccinated coupled with having a positive attitude toward vaccines) is associated with maximizing own’s outcome as a Responder in two variants of the Ultimatum Game, and maximizing other’s outcome as the Proposer in the Generosity Game. In all three cases, maximizing own’s/other’s outcome also maximizes the collective outcome.31 This outcome-maximizing tendency is consistent with the findings of a separate study we conducted,83 which found that supportive vaccination is associated with utilitarianism, a moral-related principle to always choose the option with the best collective outcome (i.e., the best outcome aggregated across all persons involved).89

Crucially, vaccination is no longer associated with resource-allocation decisions after accounting for relevant dispositional trait variables. The only variable that consistently predicts outcome-maximization across all three decisions is a higher CRT score. As discussed below, a higher CRT score, which reflects analytical thinking ability (or general intelligence63), also predicts vaccination itself.

Analytical thinking also predicts vaccination itself

Higher analytical thinking ability was found to explain both vaccine uptake and vaccine support. In this specific case, it may reflect the ability to more accurately weigh the risks and benefits of getting the COVID vaccine,57,60 instead of believing in false information or COVID conspiracies.59

Our exploratory mediation analysis suggests that the effect of analytical thinking on vaccine uptake is partially mediated by distrust propensity: a higher CRT score predicts decreased distrust of others, and decreased distrust in turn predicts COVID vaccine uptake. In the current study, a higher score on the “distrust of others” subscale reflects “a cynical outlook on the motivations and intentions of others, with a concern for the negative implications that those intentions have for the self.”86 Previous studies have widely shown that increased intelligence (measured through various scales including the CRT) is associated with an increased propensity to trust others.90–96 The authors of these studies suggest that those with higher intelligence can more accurately judge others’ motivations and trustworthiness, and hence their trust is less often betrayed (leading to higher generalized trust). However, this claim is speculative and not currently supported by empirical evidence.92

The link between trust propensity and vaccination may be explained by two potential factors. First, studies conducted before the COVID pandemic have already shown that generalized trust is highly related to institutional trust concerning vaccines and the healthcare system.47,48 One study specifically showed that trust in healthcare system mediated the relationship between generalized trust and COVID vaccination intention,51 and similar mechanisms may explain the findings of the current study. Secondly, trust propensity may lead to vaccination through conformity behavior (bandwagon effect). Previous studies suggest that both flu vaccination15,21,22 and COVID vaccination23 are largely driven by the bandwagon effect, where individuals follow the judgment of the majority. It is speculated that individuals scoring high on “distrust of others” may be less likely to confirm to majority judgment and majority behavior (i.e., vaccine support and uptake).

Importantly, no links between dark traits and (non-)vaccination were found. Although the “distrust of others” subscale predicts non-vaccination, distrust merely reflects a negative/cynical outlook toward others,86 which is not considered a dark trait/characteristic per se.34 The three vaccination groups showed no differences in either psychopathy, amorality, or desire for control; which are dark traits involving malicious characteristics (e.g., “I am willing to be unethical if I believe it will help me succeed”). Therefore, the lack of vaccine uptake or support is not associated with any malicious disposition. This finding is again consistent with our other study83 which shows that non-vaccination is not associated with deficits in moral striving, and in fact is actually associated with higher sensitivities toward avoiding causing harm.

Implications

The current study suggests that pro-vaccination strategies should take into account weaknesses in analytical thinking. Lower analytical thinking ability is consistently linked with COVID conspiracy beliefs.59,61,62,97 Pisl et al.59 suggest that short-term strategies to promote vaccination may focus on debunking COVID conspiracies, but the more ideal solution is improving analytical thinking and health literacy to reduce susceptibility to conspiracies.

Although there are some studies claiming that CRT performance (which reflects cognitive ability in terms of analytical thinking and/or intelligence) may be slightly malleable with brief interventions or specific instructions, the effects and benefits appear to be very limited with poor generalizability.98–100 Indeed, a recent review has questioned the reliability of studies claiming that cognitive abilities/performances can be improved through brief interventions (typically spanning a few weeks/months).101 In light of this, effective pro-vaccination strategies may benefit from targeting the intuitive thinking system rather than the analytical thinking system. Okuhara et al.60 highlighted that anti-vaccination messages (such as conspiracy theories) normally target the intuitive system by “directly stimulating and threatening the drive for survival and reproduction” and “appealing to emotions.” Meanwhile, pro-vaccination messages instead make use of statistical data on vaccine efficacy and safety, which necessitates processing by the analytical system. Statistical processing has only emerged more recently in the evolutionary history of humans102 and may not easily override vaccine hesitancy induced by the intuitive system, particularly for those with lower analytical thinking ability.103 Therefore, besides didactic messages based on statistics, it is proposed that pro-vaccination messages should also target the intuitive system, such as by using narratives focusing on the stories and experiences of patients suffering from the disease.60,104–106 Another strategy targeting the intuitive system is using heuristic rules based on social norms60 (e.g., four out of five people are vaccinated. If the majority others are doing it, it must be good). However, as discussed above, the current study suggests that invoking social norms may not be effective due to the non-vaccinated having a higher propensity to distrust others.

Limitations and future directions

We presented hypothetical (vs. incentivized) economic games, and one potential concern is that since hypothetical games are costless, social desirability bias (SDB) may cause participants to “behave more prosocially to appear nice and/or to protect their positive self-image.”33 Our findings are unlikely to be contaminated by SDB. First, Thielmann et al.107 showed that SDB in economic games is prevented mainly by providing anonymity, rather than by providing incentives. In the current study, all data were collected anonymously online on the Prolific platform, with no way to link the data with the names/identities of the participants. Indeed, in an online study conducted through the MTurk platform (the main alternative to Prolific, where responses are also anonymized), Amir et al.108 showed that decisions in UG, PGG, and Trust Games were identical regardless of whether incentives were provided or not. This suggests that online anonymity is sufficient to eliminate SDB in these games. Secondly, our results themselves also demonstrate the lack of SDB. For example, if SDB is present, the cooperation rate for “Second mover: Partner cooperated” should be higher than for “First mover: Partner choice unknown” in the Sequential Prisoner’s Dilemma. However, in all three groups (including the supportive vaccination group), a decrease in cooperation rate (indicating increased exploitation and free-riding) was seen instead (see Table S1 in supplementary materials).

Our study suggests that analytical thinking may promote vaccination through higher trust in others, but the exact mechanisms underlying this link remain unclear. A recent study suggests that there are four different types of analytical thinking styles, with “Actively Open-minded Thinking” and “Preference for Intuitive Thinking” relevant to COVID vaccine attitudes and intentions.109 Further studies may explore how (dis)trust is implicated in these links.

Conclusions

Regardless of whether the act of non-vaccination itself is considered as non-cooperation/free-riding or not,71,73 the current study suggests that the motivations behind non-vaccination may relate more to weaknesses in analytical thinking. Our research extends previous findings that higher CRT scores predict COVID vaccination intention/behavior and provides preliminary evidence that this relationship may be partially mediated by (dis)trust propensity. Our findings may inform about how to improve the strategies used to promote vaccination.
