Patterns of penetrating trauma in New York City before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Elias Youssef, Bonny J. Baron, Andrew Sweeny, Hossein Kalantari, Roshanak Benabbas, Valery Roudnitsky, Donald Doukas, Hope A. Taitt, Nathanaens O. Estervil, Shahriar Zehtabchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe COVID-19 pandemic profoundly impacted multiple sectors of society, including healthcare. Major cities such as New York City (NYC) saw shifts in injury numbers and patterns during this period, underscoring the need to track these fluctuations to optimize resource allocation, improve trauma protocols, and inform public health policy. We examined trends in penetrating trauma in NYC before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from the NYC Health + Hospitals, which includes 11 acute care hospitals and trauma centers citywide. We analyzed electronic medical records from five level I trauma centers and one level II trauma center (pediatric level I) from August 1, 2019, to November 30, 2024. The study included patients with penetrating injuries and excluded minor-mechanism penetrating injuries or those incompatible with stab or gunshot mechanisms. Patients presenting for follow-up of prior injuries were excluded. The study timeframe was divided into three periods: pre–COVID-19 (August 1, 2019–February 29, 2020), COVID-19 (March 1, 2020–December 31, 2021), and post–COVID-19 (January 1, 2022–November 30, 2024). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and injury characteristics. Proportions are reported as percentages and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs).ResultsData for 9,118 patients were analyzed. Most were male (87.5%), with a median age of 30 years (IQR, 23–39 years). The incidence of penetrating trauma increased significantly during the COVID-19 period (164 cases per month) compared with pre–COVID-19 (110 cases per month) and post–COVID-19 (136 cases per month). Stab wounds were the most common injury type across all periods (53.7%).ConclusionsPenetrating trauma cases increased notably during the COVID-19 pandemic in NYC, consistent with nationwide trends. Although not directly measured, socioeconomic stressors such as economic instability, social isolation, and substance abuse are plausible contributors to this increase.

INTRODUCTION

The COVID-19 pandemic struck New York City (NYC) with unprecedented severity. The first confirmed case emerged on March 1, 2020, in a healthcare worker who had returned from overseas. However, genomic analyses suggest the virus had been circulating in the city since January 2020, predominantly linked to European strains. By March 9, there were 16 confirmed cases, but the numbers escalated rapidly; by March 25, over 17,800 cases had been reported, with 199 deaths. The city’s infection rate at that time was five times the national average and accounted for one-third of all US cases [1,2].

In response to the escalating crisis, the New York State implemented the “New York State on PAUSE” executive order on March 20, mandating the closure of all nonessential businesses and urging residents to stay home. The usually vibrant streets of NYC became eerily quiet, with landmarks like Times Square nearly deserted. Public transportation services were significantly reduced, and schools transitioned to remote learning [3,4].

The lockdown profoundly affected residents’ lives and livelihoods. Hundreds of thousands of New Yorkers faced job losses, with unemployment reaching 21% in May 2020 [5]. This was associated with projections of at least $7.4 billion in lost tax revenue for the year. Broadway theaters, restaurants, hotels, and the transit system were among the hardest hit. By April 2020, between 475,000 and 1.2 million jobs, primarily low-wage positions in the retail, transportation, and restaurant sectors, were expected to be lost [6,7].

NYC’s confirmed COVID-19 deaths exceeded 45,000 by mid-2023, making the pandemic one of the deadliest disasters in the city’s history [8]. The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic was felt across all aspects of society, including healthcare systems and community dynamics. Notably, many major cities, including NYC, experienced a historic rise in penetrating trauma cases during this period [9]. Understanding fluctuations in the prevalence of penetrating trauma is crucial for several reasons. Tracking such changes allows healthcare systems to anticipate resource needs, optimize trauma care protocols, and inform public health policy. It also helps policymakers develop targeted interventions to address the root causes of fluctuations in violence and prepare accordingly to lessen their impact on communities. This study aims to investigate the patterns of violence-related penetrating trauma in NYC before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was deemed exempt from ethical review and informed consent by the Institutional Review Board of Biomedical Research Alliance of New York (BRANY IRB) because patients were anonymized and could not be identified.

Study setting

This retrospective cohort study used data from the New York City Health + Hospitals (NYC H+H) database. NYC H+H is the largest public healthcare system in the United States. The system includes 11 acute care hospitals (of which five are level I trauma centers) and numerous community health clinics across the five boroughs of NYC. Data from NYC H+H facilities provide a representative snapshot of trauma patterns and capture the city’s diverse patient population. We obtained data from the five level I NYC H+H trauma centers and one additional level II adult trauma center (level I pediatric trauma center). All centers currently use Epic electronic medical records (EMR; Epic Systems); however, centers transitioned to the Epic EMR on different dates. Notably, not all study centers were using Epic before August 2019. As a result, for the pre–COVID-19 period, we were only able to collect data starting on August 1, 2019.

We included patients with penetrating injuries due to stab wounds or gunshot wounds (including shotgun injuries) during the study period. We excluded penetrating injuries from minor mechanisms or injuries not compatible with a stab or gunshot mechanism. We also excluded individuals who did not present with acute injuries but were instead seeking follow-up care for previously sustained injuries.

Study protocol

We searched for all patients who arrived in the emergency department (ED) from 12:00 ᴀᴍ on August 1, 2019, to 11:59 ᴘᴍ on November 30, 2024. In the EMR, the first step in creating an ED encounter is “arriving” the patient, which generates a unique chart for treatment and documentation. We chose to filter based on an “arrived” status, rather than “registered” or “disposition,” because some patients were not registered due to a lack of identification and others left during treatment. We then included charts that met any of the following five criteria: (1) the chief concern recorded by the triage nurse was “stab wound” or “gunshot wound (GSW)”; (2) the primary ED diagnosis was recorded as “GSW,” “assault with GSW,” “knife wound,” or “assault by knife”; (3) the diagnosis description contained any of the following words: “GSW,” “stabbed,” “shot,” “stab,” “penetrating,” “gunshot,” “gun shot,” “knife,” “stabbing,” or “trauma”; (4) the free-text commentary associated with the chief concern entered by the triage nurse contained any of the following words: “GSW,” “stabbed,” “shot,” “stab,” “penetrating,” “gunshot,” “gun shot,” “knife,” “stabbing,” or “trauma”; or (5) the free-text commentary associated with arrival details entered by the triage nurse contained any of the following words: “GSW,” “stabbed,” “shot,” “stab,” “penetrating,” “gunshot,” “gun shot,” “knife,” “stabbing,” or “trauma.”

Relying solely on diagnosis codes to capture visits for penetrating trauma was insufficient due to inconsistencies in coding and the large number of codes that could indicate such trauma (e.g., a splenic laceration might be coded simply as “trauma,” while a tibial fracture from a gunshot wound may carry only a fracture code). Therefore, to avoid misclassification or missed ED visits for penetrating trauma, we used a combination of search strategies. The triage nurse free-text commentaries were the most reliable source, supplemented by specific ED diagnoses.

A study protocol was created and endorsed by all investigators before data collection. An electronic data collection sheet was developed to capture relevant variables from patient records. All eligible cases of penetrating trauma presenting to NYC H+H hospitals during the study period were transferred to this database. One trained data abstractor reviewed each case individually to ensure it met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. To ensure data quality, 10% of abstracted entries were independently crosschecked by one of the investigators (SZ). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

For comparison, we divided the study participants into three distinct cohorts: pre–COVID-19 (August 1, 2019–February 29, 2020), COVID-19 (March 1, 2020–December 31, 2021) and post–COVID-19 (January 1, 2022–November 30, 2024; end of data collection).

Rationale for selection of COVID-19 periods

March 2020, considered the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic for this study, was when the situation reached widespread global awareness and began severely impacting public health systems, economies, and daily life. The World Health Organization declared COVID-19 a pandemic in March 2020. Soon after, widespread disruptions and government interventions (e.g., lockdowns, travel bans, and healthcare system strain) affected society at scale. The period beginning in March 2020 represents the height of the pandemic, with hospitals overwhelmed, normal healthcare services disrupted, and social behavior shifting due to increased isolation and economic strain. The end of 2021 serves as a natural endpoint for the COVID-19 period, as vaccination campaigns were initiated around the world, natural immunity rates increased, and many regions saw declines in the most severe pandemic-related disruptions. While COVID-19 continued into 2022 and beyond, late 2021 was a turning point in terms of widespread vaccination efforts and recovery in many areas. Accordingly, we designated the period before March 2020 as the pre–COVID-19 phase and the period from March 2020 through December 31, 2021, as the COVID-19 phase [10].

The beginning of 2022 marked a new era in which certain societal structures and a degree of normalcy began to return. With the advent of widespread vaccination and the easing of restrictions, societies started to recover, although the pandemic’s effects on healthcare, public health, and other areas were still evident. This date reflects a point at which many countries, including the United States, saw the worst of the pandemic subside and began shifting focus toward long-term recovery, managing COVID-19 as an endemic virus, and addressing broader social and economic impacts. By this time, many areas had already begun lifting emergency measures, and the global push for post-pandemic recovery was well underway.

Given the different durations of each period (7 months for pre–COVID-19, 23 months for COVID-19, and 35 months for post–COVID-19), we used the number of cases per month to compare the three periods. To account for seasonality, we calculated monthly case counts consecutively from 2019 through 2024.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics, injury characteristics, and outcomes. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are presented as means or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs).

RESULTS

Our search identified a total of 9,473 patients. After excluding minor or unintentional penetrating injuries (e.g., lacerations caused by kitchen knives, puncture wounds from stepping on sharp objects) and subacute follow-up visits related to previous penetrating trauma (if the initial encounter occurred within the study period, only the index visit was counted), the analysis included 9,118 patients. Most participants were male (87.5%), with a median age of 30 years (IQR, 23–39 years).

Of the 9,118 participants, 3,628 (39.8%) sustained a gunshot wound, 4,898 (53.7%) had a stab wound, and 592 (6.5%) had penetrating trauma without a clear mechanism. Patients categorized as having “other penetrating trauma” or “penetrating trauma with unclear mechanism” were those with a documented diagnosis of “penetrating trauma” or “assault” and injuries supporting those diagnoses (e.g., ulnar artery laceration, hemothorax), but for whom the mechanism of injury (gunshot wound or stab wound) was not documented.

In total, 771 patients were identified during the pre–COVID-19 period, 3,601 during the COVID-19 period, and 4,746 during the post–COVID-19 period. DISCUSSION

This study investigated patterns of penetrating trauma in NYC before, during, and after the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic. Consistent with findings from other studies, we observed a notable increase in the rate of penetrating trauma in NYC during the pandemic.

Several studies have examined the incidence of penetrating trauma in the United States during the COVID-19 pandemic. Sheets et al. [11] observed significant changes in trauma volumes during this period. That study collected data from 88 hospitals across 18 states and enrolled 169,892 patients. Mechanisms of injury differed significantly between 2019 and 2020, with fewer blunt injuries (89.6% in 2019 vs. 88.4% in 2020; P<0.001), more burn injuries (1.8% vs. 2.0%; P=0.21), and more penetrating injuries (8.6% vs. 9.8%; P<0.001). Compared with 2019, patients in 2020 had significantly higher mortality (2.6% vs. 2.9%; P=0.01), and longer hospital length of stay (3.9±6.9 days vs. 4.1±6.6 days; P<0.001). They highlighted fluctuations in trauma mechanisms and outcomes throughout the pandemic.

McNair et al. [12] reported an increase in penetrating trauma in South Carolina during the COVID-19 lockdown, which was associated with higher alcohol consumption. The study suggests that the psychosocial impacts of government-mandated lockdowns may have contributed to increased injury severity and morbidity in the trauma population. This work demonstrated an increase in penetrating trauma during the lockdown that coincided with significantly higher alcohol consumption. The authors further suggested that excess alcohol use, particularly during the pandemic and beyond, may drive a higher incidence of severe trauma, including penetrating injuries requiring massive transfusion.

Pino et al. [13] examined trends in violent penetrating injuries during the first year of the pandemic compared with previous years, providing insights into how the pandemic influenced such injuries. This retrospective cross-sectional study compared the prevalence of violent penetrating injuries from March 2020 to February 2021 with the preceding 5 years (March 2015–February 2020) among all patients with penetrating injuries presenting to Boston Medical Center, an urban level I trauma center. A total of 2,383 patients were evaluated. Injuries increased during the first pandemic year compared with the prior 5 years, with a rise in shootings (0.61±0.89 injuries per day vs. 0.46±0.76 injuries per day) but not stabbings (0.60±0.79 injuries per day vs. 0.60±0.82 injuries per day). This surge in firearm violence began while Massachusetts was still under a stay-at-home advisory and before large-scale racial justice protests began. Patients presenting with penetrating injuries during the pandemic surge months (April–October 2020) compared with the same period in previous years were disproportionately male (93.3% vs. 87.6%), unemployed (57.4% vs. 46.6%), and Hispanic (26.0% vs. 17.9%) and were more likely to have no previous history of penetrating injury (89.0% vs. 80.9%).

Chodos et al. [14] found a significant increase in the proportion of penetrating trauma evaluations during the COVID-19 period (22.6% vs. 15.1% pre–COVID-19). Both firearm-related and stabbing injuries increased significantly during this time. This retrospective multicenter study included all adult trauma centers in the Washington, DC, region and used trauma registry data from January 1, 2019, to May 31, 2020. The COVID-19 period was defined as March 1 through May 31, 2020, and the pre–COVID-19 period as January 1, 2019, through February 28, 2020. There was a 22.4% decrease in the overall incidence of trauma during the COVID-19 phase, compared with a 3.4% increase that had been noted over the pre–COVID-19 period. Blunt mechanisms decreased significantly during the COVID-19 period (77.4% vs. 84.9%). Firearm-related and stabbing mechanisms both increased significantly during the COVID-19 era (11.8% vs. 6.8% and 9.2% vs. 6.9%, respectively). The authors concluded that, despite an overall reduction in trauma volume with the arrival of COVID-19, penetrating trauma rose significantly.

A retrospective cohort study by Mokhtari et al. [15] was conducted at four level I trauma centers across three geographic regions in the United States. The study period spanned from April 1 to July 31, 2020, with a month-matched 2019 cohort. Patients were categorized into “pre–COVID-19” and “first COVID-19 surge” cohorts. The investigators reported an increase in penetrating injuries during the initial months of the pandemic, along with a significant rise in gunshot wound cases. The study also discussed the potential influence of stay-at-home orders on these trends.

These studies collectively indicate that, while overall trauma incidents may have decreased during the pandemic, the proportion of penetrating injuries, particularly those related to firearms and stabbings, rose notably across various regions of the United States.

The increase in penetrating injuries during pandemic lockdowns can be attributed to several socioeconomic factors involving shifts in social dynamics, economic stressors, and psychological impacts on individuals and communities. Key contributors include the following:

(1) Economic stress and uncertainty: The pandemic led to widespread job losses, furloughs, and economic instability. Many individuals faced financial difficulties, potentially heightening tensions within households. In some cases, stress triggered by economic pressure contributed to interpersonal and domestic violence [16].

(2) Social isolation and increased domestic violence: During lockdowns, individuals were often isolated at home, intensifying domestic tensions. Social distancing measures separated people from support networks (family, friends, or community organizations), leaving individuals more vulnerable to violence. Sheltering in place with abusive partners also led to an increase in violent injuries, especially among women and children [17].

(3) Mental health strain and substance abuse: Uncertainty, fear, and isolation during the pandemic created significant mental health challenges for many individuals. Increased rates of anxiety, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder were reported and may have contributed to aggressive behavior and violent outbursts [18]. Lockdowns were also associated with increased alcohol and drug use as individuals turned to substances to cope with the stress of isolation, unemployment, and fear. Alcohol use has been linked to higher rates of violent behavior [19].

(4) Disruption of social services and support networks: Many social services (including shelters, mental health services, and domestic violence support systems) were closed or operated at limited capacity due to lockdown measures, making it more difficult for individuals to seek help or escape violent situations [20].

(5) Increased exposure to stressful environments: For people in lower socioeconomic groups, living conditions were often especially strained. Crowding, inadequate housing, and lack of private space in economically disadvantaged areas increased the likelihood that tensions would escalate into violence [21].

(6) Access to firearms and weapons: In some regions, firearm sales rose during lockdowns, potentially contributing to violent injuries. The combination of economic stress, isolation, and increased access to firearms created an environment in which violence was more likely to occur [22].

(7) Loss of routine and increased aggression: The abrupt changes in daily routines caused by lockdowns, including the closure of schools, workplaces, gyms, and other public spaces, contributed to frustration and exacerbated violent behavior. The loss of social structures that previously provided behavioral outlets enabled more aggression to manifest in private spaces [23].

In summary, the abrupt shift in NYC’s social climate, combined with socioeconomic, psychological, and environmental factors, led to a higher crime rate and subsequent violence. The disruption of daily life heightened frustration and aggression across the population. Widespread economic instability, coupled with social unrest, increased stress among quarantined individuals, which can escalate crime rates. Lockdown and social distancing regulations, although necessary at times, have been shown to lead to social isolation, a greater risk of mental health challenges, and more frequent behaviors resulting in injury. These socioeconomic factors collectively contributed to an increase in trauma during the pandemic lockdowns, highlighting the complex intersection of economic, psychological, and social stressors exacerbated by the global health crisis [24].

Mechanistically, during the COVID-19 pandemic period of the study, rates of gunshot wounds nearly equaled those of stab wounds, a pattern distinct from the pre– and post–COVID-19 periods. Several factors may have contributed to this shift, including pandemic-related social and economic stressors, increased firearm purchases, and changes in policing and community dynamics. Although our dataset precluded direct investigation of these underlying drivers, this area merits further exploration in future studies.

Limitations

This study is subject to the inherent limitations of a retrospective chart review, including the potential for missing or incomplete data, variability in documentation practices across hospitals and providers, and the possibility of selection bias. Furthermore, reliance on hospital-based data may not capture all cases of penetrating trauma, particularly among patients who did not seek medical care.

Although we have provided a rationale for selecting the cutoffs for defining the periods before, during, and after COVID-19, these choices are somewhat arbitrary and may not fully capture the nuanced socioeconomic and health-related changes that occurred. We recognize that defining these periods is complex and that categories can vary by context, region, and criteria (e.g., infection surges, vaccination rollout). Our approach was to use broad timeframes aligned with definitions commonly employed in similar trauma studies to allow meaningful comparison. Nonetheless, factors such as the Omicron surge in 2022 and the timing of vaccination campaigns complicate the definition of a uniform “COVID-19 period.”

The variability in Epic implementation across centers introduces the potential for data inconsistencies. Data collection was limited to the timeframe after all NYC H+H centers implemented the Epic electronic medical record system. It is unclear how data entry practices may have changed during the course of the study. In addition, the shorter pre–COVID-19 period (August 2019–February 2020) may not account for seasonal variability (e.g., summer peaks in trauma) as reflected in the COVID-19 and post–COVID-19 groups. Therefore, the numbers in the pre–COVID-19 group might not truly represent the pre–COVID-19 prevalence of penetrating trauma.

The peak increase in penetrating injuries was most notable between June and August 2020, essentially a 3-month window. These months may represent outliers rather than a pandemic-wide trend. This pattern might also reflect social and environmental factors specific to that period, such as early reopening after lockdown, seasonal patterns in trauma incidence, and broader societal unrest, rather than a uniform pandemic effect. Additionally, the COVID-19 period in this study overlapped with the racial justice protests of summer 2020 and likely included many patients served by NYC H+H. Although the level of activism and occasional violence associated with these protests may have been influenced by the pandemic, this remains a confounding factor in our study.

Our search criteria for identifying cases of penetrating trauma were constrained by the completeness of injury documentation in participants’ medical records. Insufficient documentation hindered accurate categorization of some cases. For participants categorized as “other,” we relied on our judgment, using text entries from triage notes or specific diagnostic codes (e.g., a combination of “assault” and “laceration”) to include them. However, this approach is susceptible to bias and error.

The data for this study were collected from hospitals within one large network of public hospitals in NYC. These hospitals serve patient populations that may differ substantially from the general city population. Therefore, our results may not be fully generalizable to all NYC hospitals or patient populations.

Lastly, NYC was the first large urban center in the United States to experience a high-volume surge of COVID-19 infections and was subsequently referred to as the US epicenter of COVID-19. The findings of this study may not be generalizable to other cities or regions with different demographic and socioeconomic characteristics or different lockdown or stay-at-home mandates. However, similar trends in penetrating trauma during the COVID-19 period have been reported across the nation, supporting the broader relevance of our findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated a notable surge in penetrating trauma cases in NYC during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although not directly measured here, socioeconomic stressors, such as economic instability, social isolation, and substance abuse, are plausible contributors to this increase. Understanding these trends is crucial for improving trauma care and shaping effective public health responses in future crises.
