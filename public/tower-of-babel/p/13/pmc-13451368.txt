Biopolymeric templates for green synthesis of nanoporous silica adsorbents for wastewater remediation
Authors: Erfan Khomand, Maryam Afsharpour, Minoo Shariati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Porous silica nanostructures were synthesized using various polysaccharides including Tragacanth gum, Guar gum, Arabic gum, Xanthan gum, and Chitosan as environmentally friendly soft templates. The role of biopolymer chemical structure and topology of supramolecular network on the formation of porous silica structures and its effect on the heavy metal adsorption was investigated. The adsorption capacity of the synthesized materials was evaluated for the removal of Cu²⁺, Pb²⁺, Cd²⁺, and Ni²⁺ ions. Among the ions investigated, Cu²⁺ showed the highest adsorption capacity (60.55 mg g-1). The adsorption potential is strongly influenced by adsorbent structure and metal behavior. Guar Gum showed the enhanced adsorption capacity due to the highest specific surface area and the largest total pore volume among all synthesized materials. Adsorption equilibrium were analyzed using nonlinear Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Sips isotherm models. The Freundlich and Sips models showed the best fitting performance, suggesting heterogeneous adsorption behavior associated with structurally diverse mesoporous silica surfaces. Thermodynamic analysis shows the spontaneous endothermic adsorption process which is predominantly governed by chemisorption in Guar gum and physisorption interactions in other absorbances. Two-Way Analysis of Variance shows the selective affinities of specific adsorbents toward particular metal ions. The synthesized adsorbents also exhibited good regeneration capability, maintaining stable adsorption performance during multiple adsorption–desorption cycles. The obtained results indicate that the supramolecular organization and network topology of natural biopolymer templates play a decisive role in directing silica condensation and controlling the final porous architecture. The environmentally friendly synthesis strategy developed in this work provides an effective route for preparing high-performance mesoporous silica adsorbents for heavy metal removal from wastewater.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59635-z.

Introduction

Heavy metal contamination in aquatic environments has become one of the most serious environmental and public health concerns due to the non-biodegradable nature, toxicity, and bioaccumulation tendency of metal ions and its adverse impact on ecological balance.1–3 This urgent issue has prompted extensive research into removing these contaminants from aqueous environments. The continuous discharge of industrial effluents from mining, electroplating, battery manufacturing, metallurgy, pigments, and electronic industries has significantly increased the concentration of hazardous metals such as Pb(II), Cd(II), Cu(II), and Ni(II) in water resources.4 These toxic ions can accumulate in biological systems and induce severe physiological disorders even at low concentrations.5 Therefore, the development of efficient, economical, and environmentally approaches for the removal of heavy metals from wastewater remains an urgent scientific and technological challenge.

Various methods, including chemical precipitation, ion exchange, membrane filtration, electrochemical reduction, adsorption, and solvent extraction have been employed to remove heavy metals from wastewaters. Despite their effectiveness in specific applications, many of these methods suffer from several limitations such as high operational cost, sludge generation, membrane fouling, energy consumption, and reduced efficiency at low metal ion concentrations.5,6 Among these various treatment methods, adsorption has been widely recognized as one of the most effective techniques due to its simplicity, high efficiency, low operational cost, and ease of regeneration.7–9 However, using nanostructures is a new and critical method for removing metal pollutants from water.9–12 It has received extensive attention due to its low cost, effectiveness, and simplicity of operation.12,13 In recent years, a wide variety of nanostructured adsorbents, including carbonaceous materials, metal oxides, zeolites, polymeric adsorbents, and silica-based nanomaterials, have been investigated for heavy metal removal.7–9 Among these materials, porous silica has emerged as promising adsorbents for wastewater treatment because of their high surface area, tunable pore structure, chemical stability, and surface accessibility.14–21 The adsorption performance of silica materials is strongly influenced by their pore architecture, morphology, surface functionality, and internal network structure. Therefore, controlling the formation of porous silica structures is critically important for optimizing adsorption efficiency.14–21

Conventional synthesis routes for mesoporous silica often rely on synthetic surfactants and hard-template methods, which may involve complicated preparation procedures, high costs, and environmental concerns.22,23 Therefore, increasing attention has been directed toward the development of environmentally friendly and sustainable soft bio-template approaches. This approach offers several advantages, such as improved reproducibility, scalability, and reduced environmental impact.24,25 Natural biopolymers have been employed as sacrificial templates for preparing porous materials with tailored morphologies and hierarchical pore structures.24–28.

These biomaterials possess abundant hydroxyl and amino functional groups capable of forming extensive hydrogen-bonding networks, which can guide the organization of different porous silica frameworks.29,30 In soft-template systems, the supramolecular organization and network topology of the biopolymer template play a critical role during silica formation. The spatial arrangement, chain entanglement, intermolecular hydrogen bonding, and interaction of hydrolyzed silica species with organized polymeric domains can significantly influence silica packing density, pore connectivity, and final morphology. After calcination and removal of the organic phase, these differences are preserved as distinct porous silica architectures.

Previous studies have demonstrated the successful synthesis of bio-inspired silica materials for catalysis, drug delivery, gas separation, and adsorption applications31–33. However, most reported investigations have focused on the use of a biomaterial containing Si as a silica precursor or a single biomaterial template have relied on post-synthesis surface functionalization to enhance adsorption performance.28–33 In many cases, the relationship between the intrinsic molecular structure of natural biopolymers, the resulting silica morphology, and the adsorption behavior has not been systematically explored. Furthermore, several functionalization strategies require complicated synthesis procedures, toxic reagents, or additional chemical modifications, which reduce the environmental and economic advantages of silica-based adsorbents.

Although bio-templated silica synthesis has been widely reported, a clear understanding of how different naturally derived polysaccharide structures direct the formation of porous silica architectures and subsequently influence heavy metal adsorption performance remains limited. In particular, comparative studies investigating multiple natural soft templates under similar synthesis conditions are still scarce. Moreover, the direct structure-property relationship between hydrogen-bond-driven biopolymer networks, silica pore formation, and adsorption efficiency without additional functionalization has not been comprehensively addressed. This unresolved issue represents an important research gap in the rational design of sustainable silica adsorbents.

In the present study, porous silica nanostructures were synthesized through a green and cost-effective sol-gel approach using tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) as silica precursor and five natural biopolymers including Tragacanth gum, Guar gum, Arabic gum, Xanthan gum, and Chitosan as environmentally friendly soft templates. Unlike conventional hard-template or surfactant-assisted methods, the porous structures in this work were generated through the intrinsic hydrogen-bonding interactions between the hydroxyl and amino groups of the biopolymer templates and hydrolyzed silica species. These interactions promoted the formation of interconnected organic–inorganic networks that governed the final morphology and pore architecture of the silica materials. After removal of the carbonaceous template during calcination, porous silica structures with distinct textural and morphological characteristics were obtained while largely preserving the structural organization induced by the original biopolymer network.

The novelty of this work lies in the systematic comparative investigation of multiple natural soft templates and the establishment of a direct correlation between biopolymer structure, silica morphology, pore characteristics, and adsorption performance toward Pb (II), Cd (II), Cu (II), and Ni (II) ions. In contrast to many previous studies, the synthesized silica adsorbents exhibited efficient heavy metal removal without requiring additional surface functionalization or complex chemical modification. The findings of this study provide new insights into hydrogen-bond-directed bio-templating mechanisms and offer a sustainable pathway for designing environmentally benign porous silica adsorbents with tunable structural properties for water purification applications.

Results and discussion

Characterization of nanoporous silica

Figure 1 presents the FT-IR spectra of the five porous silica samples derived from different polysaccharide templates. In all samples, the broad band around 3400 cm⁻¹ and the band near 1630 cm⁻¹ are attributed to the O–H stretching and bending vibrations of adsorbed water and surface hydroxyl groups, respectively.34,35 The intense band at 1132 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the asymmetric stretching of Si–O–Si, confirming the formation of the silica network, while the band near 800 cm⁻¹ is associated with Si–O–Si symmetric stretching and silanol-related surface vibrations. Although the overall functional-group profiles are similar, the relative intensity and breadth of the hydroxyl-related bands may reflect subtle differences in surface hydroxyl density and condensation degree introduced by the polysaccharide templates. These surface hydroxyl and silanol groups are particularly important because they provide the active sites for hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interaction, and metal-ion complexation during adsorption. Therefore, the FT-IR results not only confirm successful silica formation but also support the role of surface chemistry in governing the samples’ adsorption behavior.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the synthesized nanoporous silica samples show the semi-crystalline naturewith both amorphous and crystalline phases (Fig. 2). The sharp diffraction peak observed at 21.8° is the characteristic diffraction of the crystalline tetragonal α-cristobalite phase (JCPDS No. 39-1425). Additionally, a broad amorphous region is observed in the baseline between 15°–30°, confirming an amorphous silica matrix.36–38 Minor secondary peaks observed at approximately 27.8°, 28.4°, 31.4°, and 36.1° are consistent with crystalline silica polymorphs (α-cristobalite and traces of quartz), which indicates the thermally phase transformation from amorphous silica to a more stable crystalline network.36–38.

To evaluate the influence of the biopolymeric templates on the crystallization of the silica network, the average crystallite size was calculated using the Scherrer equation. The crystallite sizes of 22.48 nm, 22.48 nm, 16.86 nm, 13.49 nm, and 12.26 nm were calculated for the Tragacanth, Arabic gum, Chitosan, Guar gum, and Xanthan templates, respectively. This reduction in crystallite size confirms the successful formation of nanostructured silica controlled by the specific specific structure of soft template.

The changes in peak intensities, peak width, and noise levels indicate that the structural morphology of the natural gums plays an important role in directing the nucleation and subsequent phase transformation of silica during calcination:

Tragacanth and Arabic Gum show the highest peak intensities and well-defined secondary reflections (the doublet at 2Ɵ ≈27.8°and 28.4°). The highly branched carbohydrate networks and polar functional groups (-OH and -COOH) in Tragacanth and Arabic gum facilitate a highly ordered, homogenous arrangement of the silicate precursors. This optimal template-precursor interaction promotes crystalline growth and minimizes lattice strain during thermal treatment, resulting in larger crystallite sizes (22.48 nm) with enhanced crystallinity.

The XRD pattern of the silica obtained from Chitosanshows a noticeable peak broadening and a minor shift to lower 2Ɵ angles. Chitosan as a cationic polysaccharide with amine (-NH2) groups alters the pH and the kinetics of sol-gel hydrolysis/condensation process. The restricted mobility and unique electrostatic binding of silicate species onto the Chitosan chains induce structural strain and retard grain growth to form a lower crystallite size of 16.86 nm.

The silica prepared with Guar gum shows the higher noise levels and a suppressed primary peak. Guar gum is a linear galactomannan with extremely high viscosity in aqueous media. This high viscosity restricts the molecular diffusion and self-assembly of silicate species around the polymeric chains. Consequently, condensation occurs randomly to form a highly disordered amorphous silica matrix with small crystalline domains (13.49 nm).

The silica obtained from Xanthan shows an amorphous halo combined with a sharp primary peak with absent of high-angle secondary reflections . Xanthan gum possesses a rigid, rod-like cellulosic backbone. This structural rigidity forces the bulk silica to form a disordered amorphous matrixby nucleation at the dense junction zones of the polymer. This unique template behavior yields the smallest crystallite size among all samples (12.26 nm).

It can be concluded that the choice of the natural gum soft templateacts as a effective tool to tunning the crystallinity and nanostructural dimensions of porous silica. Flexible and highly branched biopolymers facilitate the evolution of highly crystalline cristobalite networks, while rigid or highly viscous linear polysaccharides effectively restrict grain growth to produce nanostructured domains below 15 nm.

Figure 3A illustrates the proposed formation mechanism of the porous silica framework templated by the natural biopolymer. The backbone of these biopolymer chains is highly enriched with active functional groups, specifically hydroxyl and amino groups, which undergo a spontaneous self-assembly process. Strong hydrogen bonds are established between the neighboring hydroxyl and amino groups, cross-linking the chains into a well-defined, interconnected 3D biopolymer network that dictates the final structural morphology.29,30 After adding silica precursor, silanol (Si-OH) groups readily form extensive hydrogen bond networks with the -OH and -NH2 groups of the polymer, thereby yielding a highly integrated biopolymer–silica hybrid matrix.29,30 During the subsequent aging stage, a continuous polycondensation reaction takes place among the adjacent silanol species. This condensation leads to the formation of rigid siloxane networks, which wrap around and lock the biopolymer template into a robust, dense hybrid network architecture. In the final step, the thermal treatment completely burns off and removes the sacrificial organic biopolymer template. The elimination of the internal polymer skeleton leaves behind a highly interconnected 3D porous silica framework.

Figure 3B shows the SEM micrographs of the prepared silica samples. The differences observed in the final morphology (Fig. 3) and also pore structure (Fig. 4) can be attributed to the supramolecular organization and network topology of the biopolymer templates during the silica condensation process. Although the organic templates were completely removed after calcination, the initial organization of the polymeric chains played a decisive role in directing the formation of the final porous silica framework.

Unlike conventional hard-template systems, the present synthesis route is governed by soft-template self-assembly phenomena. In this process, the spatial arrangement, flexibility, branching, and intermolecular organization of the biopolymers determine the local condensation environment of hydrolyzed silica species derived from TEOS.

The employed biopolymers possess different molecular architectures and distributions of hydroxyl (–OH) and amino (–NH2) groups. These functional groups participate in intermolecular hydrogen bonding within the polymer network itself and simultaneously interact with hydrolyzed silanol groups (Si–OH) generated during TEOS hydrolysis. However, the primary factor influencing the final porous structure is not merely the interaction strength, but rather the way in which these interactions organize the polymer chains into distinct supramolecular assemblies.

Depending on the molecular topology of the biopolymer template, different chain conformations such as linear alignment, coiled structures, interconnected domains, or compact aggregated assemblies may form during silica condensation. These distinct polymer organizations create different local environments for silica nucleation and growth. Consequently, after calcination and removal of the organic phase, different porous silica morphologies and pore-connectivity patterns remain.

Heavy metal removal

Removal mechanism

Silica is a common and versatile material that can carry a surface charge that may be either negative or positive. This charge is influenced by the pH of the surrounding solution as well as the presence of various functional groups on the silica’s surface. In typical aqueous solutions, silica predominantly showcases a negative surface charge, which allows it to effectively adsorb positively charged heavy metal ions. The process of adsorption occurs through electrostatic interactions, which enhance the efficiency of silica as an adsorbent in water purification and environmental remediation applications.39,40 Silica functional groups enhance its ability to exchange ions with the surrounding solution, making it particularly useful in processes such as water purification. The selective adsorption can be effectively utilized for extracting valuable metal ions from solutions or removing contaminants, highlighting the importance of understanding both the chemistry of silica and the behavior of metal ions in various environmental and industrial contexts.41

On the other hand, as a physical mechanism, silica, particularly in its mesoporous forms, interacts with heavy metal ions through the physical and chemical processes, including adsorption, electrostatic attraction, and complexation. Physical adsorption, driven by weak forces such as van der Waals forces, strengthens the overall connection. These weak, short-range attractive forces between molecules play a role in the initial attraction and binding of heavy metal ions to the silica surface. The negatively charged surface of silica in water opens the door for electrostatic attraction with positively charged metal ions, leading to their successful adsorption. Mesoporous silica structures can facilitate the entrapment of metal ions within their pores, contributing to the overall adsorption process.42,43

The effectiveness of the adsorption process is greatly influenced by specific operating conditions, such as the initial adsorbent dose, contact time, and initial pollutant concentration, as demonstrated in various scientific investigations.44 Therefore, paying close attention to determining the most favorable operational parameters is crucial. Due to this, the impact of various parameters on five distinct types of synthesized nanoporous silica was examined. This research aimed to provide insights into the effectiveness of nanoporous silica in removing heavy metal pollutants from water and to understand the influence of different bio-templates on its adsorption capabilities.

Effect of templating

Silica can be precisely shaped using a carbon template, which dictates the final form of the resulting silica material. This carbon template is constructed from carbonaceous substances, serving as a mold during the silica deposition or synthesis process. The formation of silica occurs either within the intricate pores or on the surface of the carbon template, closely adhering to its size and shape. Various techniques can be employed for this silica synthesis, including sol-gel synthesis, hydrothermal synthesis, and vapor deposition, with each method being selected based on the desired characteristics of the silica material and the specific structure of the carbon template. For instance, sol-gel synthesis allows for the formation of highly uniform silica nanoparticles, while hydrothermal synthesis can produce larger crystalline structures under elevated temperatures and pressures. Once the silica has sufficiently formed, the carbon template is removed, resulting in a detailed replica of the original template made entirely of silica.45 This removal can be achieved through different methods: one common approach involves burning the carbon template in an oxidizing atmosphere, which leaves behind a silica skeleton. Alternatively, chemical etching can be employed, where specific solvents dissolve the carbon, leaving the silica structure intact. The resulting silica material replicates the dimensions and architecture of the carbon template, enabling the creation of silica structures with carefully controlled morphologies.46 These can include a wide range of porous structures tailored with specific pore sizes and geometries, as well as hollow silica formations designed with customized cavities. Due to their high porosity, these silica materials typically exhibit remarkably high surface areas, which enhances their utility for various applications. In applications such as adsorption, these silica materials demonstrate excellent capabilities in capturing and immobilizing diverse substances.47

Figure 5 demonstrates that the highest adsorption capacity is observed in the silica obtained from Guar gum, which has the largest surface area and pore sizes predominantly in the 5 nm range. Chitosan is ranked next, possessing the second-largest surface area, but with smaller pore sizes, mostly ranging from 2.5 to 4 nm. The lowest adsorption capacity belongs to Arabic gum and Tragacanth. Specifically, Arabic gum, which has very large pores, exhibits the lowest adsorption (Fig. 5).

Effect of pore size and surface area

Larger pore sizes generally enhance the adsorption of heavy metals by silica materials. This improvement occurs because larger pores allow for the easier diffusion of heavy metal ions into the pore structure, which increases the surface available for adsorption. However, it is essential to note that while larger pores can improve accessibility, they may also limit the overall adsorption capacity. Conversely, a low surface area can reduce the number of available adsorption sites, potentially restricting the total amount of heavy metals that can be absorbed. The ideal pore size, however, depends on the specific heavy metal being targeted and the intended application. Silica with larger pore volumes can increase the overall surface area available for adsorption, which may lead to enhanced adsorption capacity. There is a balance between pore size and surface area. Larger pores might facilitate diffusion, but a high surface area is still necessary for a high overall adsorption capacity. Although Arabic gum has a larger pore size than other compounds, it is not successful in absorbing heavy metals, which can be attributed to its low surface area. As can be seen, silica prepared from Chitosan and Guar, despite having a smaller pore size, exhibits acceptable metal adsorption due to its high surface area.48 Guar gum with higher pore size than chitosan shows higher adsorption capacity.

Correlation analysis (Fig. 6)showed a strong positive relationship between adsorption capacity and BET surface area (r = 0.8055). Pore volume also demonstrated a positive correlation with adsorption capacity (r = 0.6393)which indicates that interconnected porous channels facilitated ion diffusion. Conversely, pore size showed a negative correlation with adsorption behavior (r = -0.6736). Overall, the results establish a direct structure–property relationship in which the molecular architecture of natural gum templates controls the morphology, porosity, crystallinity, and silica adsorption behavior through hydrogen-bond-directed self-assembly.

Morphological effect

Silica’s morphology significantly impacts its ability to adsorb metal ions. The surface area, pore structure, and overall shape of silica materials influence the efficiency of metal ion adsorption. Porous silica materials generally exhibit superior metal ion adsorption capabilities compared to non-porous or layered silica structures. This is due to the increased surface area and pore volume of porous silica, which facilitates greater interaction with metal ions. The pore size and surface charge density also play a crucial role in determining adsorption efficiency. Pores that are the right size and shape can promote efficient diffusion of metal ions into the silica structure. Regularly shaped silica, especially with larger sizes, generally exhibits higher imprinting factors and adsorption capacities compared to irregularly shaped silica with a broad particle size distribution.

As shown in Fig. 5, the highest adsorption rates correspond to the layered structures (Guar gum and Chitosan). These two structures possess the largest surface area (Fig. 4). The silica derived from Guar gum, which has larger inter-layer spacing, showed a higher adsorption capability. The lowest adsorption rate is related to Arabic gum, which exhibits a spherical nanoparticle morphology (Fig. 3).

Effect of contact time

In the subsequent phase of the experiment, we systematically investigated the optimal time for achieving the highest percentage of metal removal through the interaction of silica nanopores with metals. To comprehensively assess this, we compared the amount of metal removed within a 15-minute duration with that of three additional periods. This allowed us to gain a better understanding of the temporal dynamics of metal removal by silica nanopores.

The interaction between the adsorbent and adsorbate plays a crucial role in enhancing the likelihood of adsorption, which intensifies with extended contact duration. In this study, the stirring time was carefully varied from 15 to 60 min to investigate the effect on adsorption capacity. The results, meticulously illustrated in Fig. 5, offer valuable insights into the relationship between contact time and adsorption efficiency. As the contact time increased, the adsorption capacity of the adsorbent exhibited a notable upsurge. The initial rapid rise in adsorption capacity within the first 15 min can be attributed to the swift sorption of ions into the surface pores of nanoporous silica. This intriguing phenomenon persisted for 30 min, demonstrating consistent behavior across all metals and absorbents. Beyond the initial 30 min, a gradual increase in adsorption capacity was observed, potentially indicating the migration of ions from the upper adsorbent surface to the inner pores. However, after 45 min, minimal change in adsorption capacity was noted, signifying that all active sites had been occupied. This detailed observation concludes that 45 min represents the optimal contact time for maximum adsorption efficiency.

Effect of absorbent dosage

The quantity of adsorbent utilized plays a critical role in adsorption, significantly influencing the adsorbent’s capacity at a consistent initial pollutant concentration. The removal of metal ions escalates as the adsorbent dosage increases. Figure 7 shows the effects of five concentrations of an active substance on the removal of heavy metals (20 ppm) over 45 min. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the elimination of Ni (II), Cd (II), Cu (II), and Pb (II) intensifies with an increase in the amount of adsorbent. We are augmenting the adsorbent quantity, which results in an expanded adsorption surface, thereby enhancing the probability of removal. The increasing trend in removal capacity can be attributed to the increased availability of adsorption sites for metal ions. Increasing the amount of silica has increased the removal of heavy metals in all bio-templated silica compounds. As can be seen, with the increase in the amount of porous silica, the amount of adsorption has increased in proportion. Indeed, the increase in silica content can ultimately enhance the removal rate of metals. Beyond the optimum dosages, further increases in SiO₂ concentration resulted in improvements in removal percentages. This suggests that while more active sites become available with higher dosages, there may also be a saturation point where additional adsorbent does not significantly enhance performance.

Metals effect

The size and type of metal ions play a crucial role in determining the effectiveness of silica-based materials in absorbing heavy metals, which is essential for applications such as wastewater treatment and heavy metal removal. Smaller metal ions, due to their reduced atomic radii and higher charge density, tend to form stronger interactions with the silica surface. This results in enhanced adsorption capacities, allowing for more efficient removal of contaminants. Smaller cationic heavy metals, due to increased affinity, facilitates greater adsorption compared to larger or neutral ions, enhancing the overall effectiveness of the silica-based materials in sequestering heavy metals from solution. On the other hand, smaller metal ions, due to their higher charge density and reduced size, effectively permeate the micropores and surface sites of silica. This characteristic enables stronger binding and enhances the material’s adsorption capacity. In contrast, larger metal ions may find it challenging to access these surface sites, which could limit their interaction with silica and reduce their overall adsorption potential. Cationic heavy metals demonstrate a strong affinity for the negatively charged silanol groups and siloxane oxygen atoms on the silica surface. This interaction occurs through electrostatic attraction and chemical bonding, promoting effective binding. Conversely, anionic heavy metals face more difficulties in establishing similar interactions with the silica surface, primarily due to their lack of positive charge. Understanding these interactions can lead to improved design and optimization of materials for environmental remediation efforts, ultimately contributing to cleaner water sources.

This suggests that the silica derived from Guar gum was particularly effective in removing up to 83% of copper metal and equally effective in reducing the concentration of other metals. Silica prepared from Chitosan has a relatively similar function, and Xanthan likely exhibits superior performance. Silica prepared from Arabic gum exhibited the lowest performance in removing all metals due to its lower surface area and porosity. Nevertheless, this type of silica is still compatible.

Comparing the removal rates of different metals reveals that all silica structures effectively removed Cu (II) ion (low cation size) and exhibited the lowest metal removal rate for Pb (II) (highest cation size) (Fig. 9).

Cu (II) has a unique electronic configuration that leads to a significant Jahn-Teller distortion in its complex geometry. This distortion greatly enhances its ability to form stronger and more stable complexes with surface ligands (such as Si-OH) compared to the other divalent ions. The strong affinity of Cu (II) for surface complexation dominates the process, leading to the highest adsorption among the studied metals.49,50

In general, Pb (II) has a more challenging adsorption and its relative removal is less than that of the other metals. Lead ions can form strong chelation complexes with specific ligands, especially those that contain functional groups such as thiols. In contrast, lead ions generally do not form strong bonds with the silicon-oxygen (Si-O) network present in silica. The Si-O bonds in silica are stable and do not favorably interact with Pb.51 Furthermore, the largest cation size and the lowest charge density cause the relatively weak electrostatic interactions between Pb ions and the negatively charged surface of silica. This limited interaction can influence the mobility and availability of lead in environments such as soil and water, where silica is prevalent.51

As can be seen, both cadmium and nickel are removed effectively by all species, following the same trend as copper. Nickel and cadmium ions possess a remarkable ability to form robust complexes with ligands, particularly with the oxygen atoms found on the silica surface. This coordination significantly enhances the binding strength, leading to a more effective and efficient adsorption mechanism.52

The ability of a metal ion to form strong, inner-sphere complexes with the surface functional groups can override the inhibitory effect of hydration energy. Ni (II) ions with the smallest cation size have the highest charge density, resulting in the strongest binding to water (highest hydration energy). High hydration energy makes it energetically less favorable for the ion to desorb from its water shell and bind to the surface, which generally limits the adsorption capacity.

Cadmium demonstrates a moderate affinity for forming inner-sphere surface complexes with the silanol groups Si-OH on the silica surface. This tendency for complexation places it higher in adsorption performance than simple electrostatics would predict. In general, the good adsorption of Cd (II) stems from a favorable balance between its relatively low hydration energy (due to its intermediate cationic size) and its moderate tendency for surface complexation, positioning it above Lead and competitive with Nickel. Cd (II) generally shows higher adsorption than the largest ion, Pb (II), due to its smaller size and better balance between hydration and complexation tendency. While Ni (II) is smaller and should have higher electrostatic attraction, its very high hydration energy significantly limits its effective adsorption. Cd (II), with its lower hydration energy, can often exhibit comparable or higher adsorption depending on the specific surface chemistry.

Competitive selectivity

Under competitive adsorption conditions, Cu2+ showed the highest adsorption capacity compared with Pb2+, Cd2+ and Ni2+ indicating the stronger affinity of the adsorbent toward Cu2+ ions. The selectivity coefficients confirmed the preferential adsorption behavior, which may be attributed to differences in hydration energy, ionic radius, and affinity toward surface silanol groups (Fig. 10).

Reusability

The reusability performance of the synthesized mesoporous silica adsorbents was evaluated through consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles in order to investigate the stability for repeated heavy metal removal. After each adsorption cycle, the adsorbents were separated, immersed in 0.5 M HCl for 24 h, washed with deionized water, dried, and subsequently reused under identical experimental conditions.

The obtained results demonstrated that the adsorption capacity remained nearly unchanged during the first four consecutive cycles, indicating the high structural stability of the porous silica framework. A slight decrease in adsorption performance was observed during the 5 cycles. This reduction may be attributed to the partial blockage of pores by residual metal ions. Nevertheless, the adsorption efficiency remained relatively high even after multiple cycles, confirming the promising regeneration capability of the synthesized adsorbents for practical wastewater treatment applications (Fig. 12).

Isotherms fitting

The results of adsorption isotherm analysis was reported for Cu²⁺ ions, whichshowed the highest adsorption performance among the ions investigated . The equilibrium adsorption behavior of synthesized mesoporous silica adsorbents were analyzed using Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Sips isotherm models (Table S3). The good fitting of the adsorption isotherm models was evaluated by correlation coefficient (R²), root mean square error (RMSE), and chi-square (χ²) analysis. Lower RMSE and χ² values and higher R² values indicate better agreement between experimental and calculated adsorption data. The data of isotherms fittings are summarized in Table 2.

The Langmuir model showed the high correlation coefficients (R² = 0.902–0.983) which indicates the monolayer adsorption . Tragacanth exhibited the highest Langmuir maximum adsorption capacity (qmax = 126.91 mg g–1), indicating the presence of highly accessible adsorption sites in porous structure. However, the differnce of some experimental data from the ideal Langmuir model indicates that the adsorbent surfaces are not completely homogeneous.

The result of Freundlich model showed the good fitting compared with the Langmuir model due to the higher R² values (0.967–0.996) and lower RMSE values. This result confirms the heterogeneous nature of the adsorption surfaces and the existence of adsorption sites with different energies. The Freundlich constant n ranged between 1.42 and 2.51 for all adsorbents, indicating favorable adsorption behavior of Cu²⁺ ions on the porous silica structures.

The Temkin model also reasonably described the adsorption process, suggesting the presence of adsorbate–adsorbent interactions and gradual variation in adsorption energy during surface coverage. The relatively high B values observed for Tragacanth and Xanthan imply stronger energetic interactions between Cu²⁺ ions and the adsorption sites generated by these porous structures.

Among all investigated models, the Sips isotherm exhibited the best overall fitting performance, with R² values approaching unity and the lowest RMSE values. The excellent fitting of the Sips model indicates that the adsorption process follows combined Langmuir–Freundlich behavior and occurs on structurally heterogeneous surfaces. This finding is consistent with the mesoporous silica network generated by different polymeric templates.

Thermodynamic parameters showed that the adsorption of Cu²⁺ ions onto the synthesized porous silica adsorbents was generally spontaneous, as confirmed by the negative ΔG° values at all temperatures. For most adsorbents, ΔG° became more negative with increasing temperature, indicating highly spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable adsorption athigh temperatures.

Furthermore, the relatively low ΔH° values (below 17 kJ mol–1) indicate that the adsorption process is mainly governed by physisorption interactions rather than strong chemisorption. The enthalpy change values (ΔH0) of Guar gum obtained 36.77 kJ mol–1 indicates a chemisorption mechanism. This implies that the binding of Cu (II) involves electrostatic attraction coupled with the formation of bond complex with the surface silanol (Si-OH) groups, rather than weak physical van der Waals forces (physisorption). The highest ΔH0 value for the Guar gum-derived silica indicates the formation of extremely strong chemical interactions facilitated by its unique multilayered, high-surface-area architecture.

The positive ΔS° values observed for most adsorbents indicate increased randomness at the solid–solution interface during adsorption. This behavior is likely associated with the release of hydration water molecules surrounding Cu²⁺ ions during adsorption onto silanol-rich porous silica surfaces.

Adsorption kinetic models

Kinetic studies are essential for understanding the rate-controlling step and the mechanism of the adsorption process. The performance of an adsorbent is evaluated using pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and intraparticle diffusion models.

Pseudo-first-order kinetic model assumes that the rate of adsorption is proportional to the number of available active sites on the adsorbent surface. Pseudo-second-order model is based on the assumption that the adsorption rate is controlled by the chemisorption (chemical adsorption) mechanism involving electron sharing or exchange. The pseudo-first-order and the pseudo-second-order models help to identify the adsorption process, but they couldn’t identify the diffusion mechanism. So, the intraparticle diffusion model is used to determine if pore diffusion is the rate-limiting step. The curve typically consists of multiple linear stages, indicating different control mechanisms. It often provides a superior fit for heavy metal removal across the entire adsorption period.

The results of the pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models for Ni, Pb, Cu, and Cd onto synthesized porous silica absorbents are provided in Fig. S1. Results revealed that the pseudo-first-order model provided better agreement with the experimental data compared with the pseudo-second-order model. This behavior suggests that the adsorption process is mainly governed by physical adsorption and diffusion-controlled interactions rather than strong chemisorption mechanisms.

The intraparticle diffusion model is presented in Fig. 14 to identify the diffusion mechanism. As shown in Fig. 14, both film diffusion (boundary layer diffusion) and intraparticle diffusion contribute to the rate control in all of absorbents. The three slopes in each curve with decreasing rate constants (k1 > k2 > k3) indicates that there were three diffusion steps contribute to the adsorption process.

In the first step, the adsorption process occurs rapidly due to external surface adsorption and film diffusion. During this stage, heavy metal ions migrate from the bulk solution toward the external surface of the adsorbent particles through the liquid boundary layer. The relatively steep slope observed in this region indicates a high adsorption rate caused by the large concentration gradient and the abundance of readily accessible adsorption sites on the external surface. Since the porous silica surfaces initially contain a high number of available silanol groups, rapid interaction between metal ions and surface adsorption sites occurs.

The second step corresponds to intraparticle diffusion and gradual penetration of metal ions into the internal mesoporous network of the silica adsorbents. In this region, the diffusion rate decreases because metal ions must travel through interconnected pore channels and overcome increasing diffusion resistance inside the porous framework. The lower slope (k₂) compared with the first stage (k₁) indicates slower diffusion kinetics associated with pore accessibility, tortuous diffusion pathways, and internal mass transfer limitations. This stage is strongly influenced by pore structure, surface area, pore connectivity, and mesoporous organization generated by the different biopolymer templates.

The third step represents the final equilibrium stage, where adsorption gradually approaches saturation and the diffusion rate becomes significantly slower. The nearly flat slope (k₃) indicates that most adsorption sites are occupied and the concentration gradient between solution and adsorbent surface becomes minimal. At this stage, adsorption equilibrium is established and only limited additional adsorption occurs.

The differences observed among the synthesized adsorbents can be directly related to their distinct porous structures and surface characteristics. Silica samples synthesized using Guar gum and Chitosan exhibited steeper slopes and faster diffusion behavior due to their larger surface areas, higher pore accessibility, and more interconnected mesoporous frameworks. These structural properties facilitate rapid transport of metal ions into the porous network and improve adsorption kinetics. In contrast, silica synthesized using Arabic gum and Tragacanth displayed slower diffusion behavior and less pronounced multi-stage diffusion characteristics, which may be attributed to lower surface area, reduced pore connectivity, and limited accessibility of internal adsorption sites.

Methods

Materials and methods

Tragacanth gum (MW ~ 840,000; viscosity = 2000–4000), Arabic gum (MW ~ 250,000–1,000,000; Viscosity = 300), Chitosan (MW ~ 50,000–120,000; Viscosity = 200–800), Guar gum (MW ~ 250,000; viscosity = 2500–3500), and Xanthan gum (MW > 2,000,000; viscosity = 1200–1600) were used as bio-templates and were prepared naturally and used after purification. (The gums were dissolved in water, and the insoluble materials were removed by filtration). Tetraethylorthosilicate, acetic acid, and ethanol were purchased from Merck and used to prepare the silica precursor. The standard heavy metal solutions were meticulously crafted using ultra-pure salts of copper, lead, nickel, and cadmium, combined with the potent properties of hydrochloric acid and nitric acid (HNO3) provided by Merck.

The SEM images were taken using a Tescan VEGA3 Scanning Electron Microscope. A Bruker Vector spectrometer was used to record infrared spectra (FT-IR). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were used to identify the crystalline phases using a Bruker Axs D8 Advance diffractometer with filtered CuKα radiation (λ = 1.54060 Å). N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms and pore volume distributions were obtained on the Belsorp mini-instrument. The adsorption of heavy metals by silica samples was measured using an atomic adsorption spectrophotometer (HACH, Model DR6000).

Precursor preparation

Given that natural precursors such as Tragacanth, Arabic gum, Guar, Chitosan, and Xanthan lack specific molecular weights, it is not feasible to establish a precise molar and molecular relationship between their weight and the quantities of solvent and silicon precursor. Consequently, experimental procedures were conducted based on each material’s water solubility. The initial dissolution involved 25 mL of water per gram of Arabic gum and Tragacanth, 30 mL of water per gram of Guar and Xanthan, and 20 mL of a 1% acetic acid solution per gram of Chitosan.

Water was the designated solvent for all bio-template components in this experimental procedure. It is crucial to note that the introduction of Guar and Xanthan must be precisely timed to coincide with the water’s imminent boiling point, which occurs between 75 and 80 °C. Deviating from this temperature range could result in the undesirable formation of insoluble and heterogeneous masses resembling balls. The bio-templates should be incorporated into the stirrer and heated at a low temperature to facilitate dissolution.

On the contrary, silica sol is prepared by combining tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS), water, and ethanol in a molar ratio of 1:3:4. The acetic acid solution was added until the mixture reached a pH of 4–5, and the mixture was stirred for 1 h. Following this preparation, TEOS is added to each compound at a 1:2 ratio with the bio-template precursor and mixed for an additional hour. During the addition of the silica precursor, ultrasonic treatment is applied for 30 min to enhance mixing, thereby promoting the formation of nanometer-scale pores in the final silica structure.

Synthesis of nanoporous silica

Following mixing of the bio-templates and tetraethylorthosilicate solution, the resulting mixture underwent a 6 h drying period and an 8 h hydrothermal process at 90 °C, facilitating crystal formation as the solvent evaporated. After drying, the samples underwent a subsequent transformation in a furnace, where they were heated at 5 °C per minute until reaching 750 °C. After six hours at this temperature, the bio-templates were removed, and the samples were converted into porous silica nanostructures. Subsequent phases involved characterizing and evaluating the samples.

Study of heavy metal removal

To evaluate the efficacy of nanoporous silica synthesized from 5 specific templates in removing copper, lead, cadmium, and chromium from water samples, an aqueous solution with precisely measured metal salt concentrations was carefully prepared in batches. The batch adsorption process is a practical and efficient method for eliminating pollutants from liquid solutions during water purification. The investigation considered various parameters to assess their influence on the successful removal of heavy metals.

We conducted batch tests by combining 0.75 g of nanoporous silica with 50 mL solutions containing 20 mg L− 1 of Ni (II), Pb (II), Cu (II), and Cd (II) in separate containers at room temperature at pH solution of 5. The ability of the synthesized silica samples to remove this metal pollutant was evaluated using different parameters such as the type and amount of adsorbent (0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 g), the required time, pH (2–6), and temperature (298, 313, and 323 K) in the removal efficiencies for different metals in 5, 10, 20, and 30 mg L− 1. Conclusion

Porous silica nanostructures with special porous architectures were successfully synthesized using various natural biopolymers as environmentally friendly soft templates through a sol–gel-assisted self-assembly process. The results showed that the functional groups and supramolecular topology of the biopolymer templates played an important role in directing silica condensation and controlling the final morphology, pore structure, and adsorption behavior of the synthesized materials. SEM and BET analyses confirmed the formation of porous silica frameworks with different structures depending on the type of biopolymer template. These differences were attributed to changes in organization of polymer chain , intermolecular interactions, and silica packing during the self-assembly process. The synthesized mesoporous silica materials showed efficient adsorption performance toward heavy metal ions including Cu²⁺, Pb²⁺, Cd²⁺, and Ni²⁺. Among the ions investigated , Cu²⁺ showed the highest adsorption capacity, which was strongly adsorbed by silica obtained from Guar gum. Adsorption equilibrium studies showed that the Freundlich and Sips models provided the best fitting performance, indicating heterogeneous adsorption behavior associated with structurally diverse mesoporous surfaces. The adsorption process was governed by surface heterogeneity, pore accessibility, and differences in silica network organization generated by the biopolymer templates. Thermodynamic analysis exhibits the spontaneous endothermic adsorption process which is predominantly governed by chemisorption in Guar gum and physisorption interactions in other absorbances. This study demonstrates that natural biopolymers can serve as effective sustainable soft templates for the controlled synthesis of mesoporous silica adsorbents with tunable pore structures and promising heavy metal removal performance. The proposed environmentally friendly synthesis strategy provides a simple and efficient approach for developing high-performance porous adsorbents for wastewater treatment applications.
