Hybrid Slow Sand Filtration for Emerging Applications in Water Treatment
Authors: Makhabbat Kunarbekova, Ulan Zhantikeyev, Kainaubek Toshtay, Sapargali Inabat, Ronny Berndtsson, Shynggyskhan Sultakhan, Jechan Lee, Seitkhan Azat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The current review summarizes recent advances in slow sand filtration (SSF) for water treatment, with a focus on addressing key gaps identified in recent studies. SSF has progressed beyond traditional biophysical processes through the integration of hybrid systems and advanced materials. The addition of zerovalent iron, graphene oxide, activated carbon, and biocomposites has improved the removal of turbidity, pathogens, heavy metals, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and emerging contaminants, including antibiotic resistance genes. Advancements in molecular biology tools, flux modeling, and system analysis have enhanced understanding of the mechanisms underlying SSF performance. Implementation across rural, urban, and industrial contexts demonstrates SSF’s versatility, though operational challenges persist. However, significant knowledge gaps remain, particularly regarding nutrient removal efficiency, long-term performance, scalability, and the absence of standardized operational protocols. In any case, SSF is emerging as a flexible and multifunctional water treatment platform. Future research should prioritize long-term field validation, economic assessment, and process optimization to enable sustainable large-scale implementation.

Introduction

Slow sand filtration (SSF) is known as a low-cost, sustainable water and wastewater treatment method, particularly for smaller communities and developing countries due to its simplicity, low energy requirements, and robustness against fluctuating water quality. − SSFs rely on the schmutzdecke, the biofilm that develops on the sand bed’s surface, to remove pollutants. This method is effective at reducing turbidity (e.g., suspended solids) and removing pathogens (e.g., bacteria, protozoa, and viruses) and organic matter in the form of Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) and Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD). However, SSFs may not be as effective at removing certain dissolved pollutants, such as nitrates and phosphates, requiring additional treatment steps for nutrient removal. Numerous studies have shown that the performance of SSF is governed by a combination of physical, chemical, and biological mechanisms. − Adsorption, adhesion, and straining are examples of physicochemical processes that are supported by biofiltration, which is fueled by the activity of fauna that live inside or on top of the filter. These organisms, which include viruses, phages, meiofauna, macrofauna, bacteria, protozoa, and archaea, are essential to the elimination of contaminants. − Microorganisms in the upper layers of the sand bed secrete Extracellular Polymeric Substances (EPS), which alter the characteristics of the sand surface, improve pollutant adhesion, and decrease permeability. − This is one of the biological processes in SSF. Predation and viral inactivation aid in lowering pathogen burdens, whereas microbial uptake and mineralization of organic matter assist in removing both labile and refractory dissolved organic carbon. − The primary biological zone of SSF has historically been thought to be the topmost bioactive layer of the schmutzdecke, which needs time to develop before it can function effectively. Recent data, however, indicate that deeper sand layers also play a substantial role in biofiltration, indicating that processes taking place outside of the surface biofilm must be considered to fully comprehend SSF performance.

Quantitative insights are still scarce, despite the established connection between biological activity and SSF purification efficiency. The degree to which mineralization and respiration drive the removal of organic matter, the relative contributions of various biological compartments (such as bacteria vs micro- to macrofauna), and the vertical distribution of biological processes within the filter bed are some of the major knowledge gaps. , This is consistent with current studies that aim to optimize SSF by carefully regulating design factors, including temperature, media grain size, and filtration rate, as well as by incorporating modified filter media (e.g., anthracite and biochar) and hybrid treatment systems. SSFs can be modified to overcome their historical drawbacks while still being appropriate for decentralized, small-community, and low-resource water-treatment applications by combining these tactics.

The paper aims to outline key design parameters for SSFs, such as filtration rate, media grain size, and temperature, that influence performance and emphasize the suitability of SSFs for decentralized and small-community water treatment systems. In addition, new materials (e.g., modified sand, biochar, and anthracite) and hybrid systems combining SSFs with pretreatment or disinfection steps have been developed to improve performance. These methods to enhance SSF performance for addressing conventional SSF’s limitations are reviewed and discussed.

Applications of SSF Systems

A representative example of SSF application is the use of pilot-scale SSFs targeting improved agricultural reuse for post-treating digestate from a low-tech anaerobic digester in rural Colombia, which has been tested. Three sand grain sizes (D10 = 0.14, 0.33, and 1.06 mm) were assessed to assess the impacts on removal efficiencies for solids, organic matter, nutrients, and pathogens. The finest sand (0.14 mm) yielded the highest removal ratesup to 50% total solids, 55% dissolved organic carbon, 90% phosphate, and 99.5% fecal coliformsindicating potential for compliance with fertigation standards. However, even under optimal conditions, ammonium-N removal was limited, with initial increases in effluent NH4 + due to inhibited nitrification, only partially recovering after COD load declined. This suggests that SSFs alone are insufficient for robust nitrogen management without process enhancements.

Although the system demonstrated considerable potential as a decentralized treatment for small-scale farms, there were clear trade-offs. Fine sand improved performance but increased the risk of clogging and reduced the filtration velocity. Operational sustainability under varying feedstock quality, particularly with digestates rich in solids or salinity (e.g., from cheese whey), remains uncertain. Moreover, while pathogens were markedly reduced after biological layer maturation, fluctuations persisted due to feed variability, and some residual phytotoxicity was noted, driven by high electrical conductivity and ammonium levels. Although diluting effluents decreased phytotoxicity in bioassays, this strategy is not always practical in water-limited rural contexts.

The authors projected modest economic benefits, estimating savings of ∼ €200/year from reduced fertilizer purchases. Yet, this value may be optimistic, given limited nitrogen removal and potential dilution requirements. Comparisons with vermifiltration systems, which show greater nutrient recovery, suggest SSF may offer environmental gains but fall short in cost-efficiency unless nutrient reclamation is fully optimized.

Importantly, the study fills a gap in pilot-scale SSF research for digestate treatment, but broader applicability is still constrained by the lack of long-term field trials and variable feedstock composition. Future work should address process scaling, maintenance protocols for clogging prevention, and integration with other low-tech systems (e.g., vermifiltration and aerated biofilters) to improve nitrogen removal and operational robustness. Overall, SSFs show promise for low-cost pathogen and phosphate control but require further refinement to serve as a comprehensive digestate treatment solution in rural circular bioeconomy frameworks.

The effectiveness of upflow slow sand filters (USSFs) in removing the cyanobacterium Cylindrospermopsis raciborskii a known producer of toxins such as saxitoxin (STX), from highly contaminated raw water (≈9.1 × 105 cells/ml) sourced from a tropical Brazilian lake, was evaluated. Two USSF systems achieved impressive cell removal rates of 98–99%, reducing concentrations to 1.1–2.1 × 104 cells/ml. Additionally, both filters significantly reduced turbidity (>80%), apparent color, total coliforms (85–93%), and particulate organic carbon. Crucially, these filters operated continuously for over 80 days without clogging or requiring prefiltration, suggesting a potential advantage over traditional downflow systems, which typically require roughing filters to prevent rapid head loss.

Despite operational success, the study leaves critical gaps. The most notable limitation is the absence of any toxin quantification (e.g., STX or cylindrospermopsin) in either raw or treated water. This undermines the conclusions regarding safety, especially as residual cell densities (>104 cells/ml) could enable toxin release through cell lysis or regrowth, posing potential risks postdisinfection. Furthermore, true color and Dissolved Organic Carbon (DOC) were poorly removed, and UV254 absorbance increased slightly after treatmentboth indicators of persistent dissolved organic matter that could lead to disinfection byproduct (DBP) formation. While the support gravel layer in the USSF may compensate somewhat for the lack of a schmutzdecke (as in downflow filters), its role in microbial or toxin removal remains insufficiently characterized.

Pollutant Removal by SSF

The removal of organic pollutants (three isomers of monochlorophenols: 2-CP, 3-CP, and 4-CP) from water using hybrid treatment methods (Figure ), specifically SSF and ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, was studied. 4-CP could be effectively removed by SSF even without microbial acclimation, while 2-CP and 3-CP required a week of biofilm development to achieve significant removal. In contrast, UV irradiation removed all CPs rapidly, with complete mineralization observed within 5 min and an associated stoichiometric release of chloride ions. The study also performed molecular orbital calculations, revealing that the degradability of CPs under UV correlated with electrophilic frontier density and HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) energy, with 4-CP showing the highest susceptibility to degradation. The study stands out by combining biological and advanced oxidation processes and further strengthening the findings through molecular modeling. It effectively demonstrates the complementary role of UV treatment in degrading persistent chlorinated organics that are less amenable to biodegradation.

Removal Efficiency of SSF of Various Organic Pollutants

The effectiveness of a granular activated carbon (GAC) sandwich in SSF for removing four representative pharmaceutical and personal care products (PPCPs)DEET (N,N-diethyl-meta-toluamide), paracetamol, caffeine, and triclosanfrom synthetic tertiary wastewater was assessed. Using lab-scale filters (Figure ) with varying GAC layer depths and a coarse sand matrix (0.6 mm), the study demonstrated significantly enhanced PPCP removal (p < 0.05) compared to traditional sand SSFs. The optimal configuration (filter 3:10 cm sand/20 cm GAC/20 cm sand) achieved an average PPCP removal of 98.2% at 10 cm/h. Interestingly, the performance did not significantly differ between filtration rates of 10 and 20 cm/h, suggesting operational flexibility. Pseudo-second-order kinetic modeling confirmed that adsorption onto GAC was the dominant removal mechanism, particularly for recalcitrant compounds like DEET. Filter 5 (GAC only) underperformed compared to the sandwich filters, indicating that the combination of biological and adsorptive processes is synergistic. Overall, GAC-sandwich SSF offers a promising low-energy solution for trace organic removal, but further field validation and lifecycle cost analysis are needed for real-world implementation. Overall, the study supports USSF as a low-cost, operationally resilient option for bulk cyanobacterial cell removal but underscores the need for complementary treatment steps and toxin monitoring to ensure safe potable water production.

From Figure , the GAC sandwich SSF operates through complementary mechanisms: the upper sand layer promotes schmutzdecke formation, enabling biodegradation, biosorption, and pathogen removal; the GAC layer provides effective adsorption of recalcitrant organic micropollutants; and the lower sand layer retains biomass and GAC fines, ensuring stable effluent quality. The gravel layer supports hydraulic stability and uniform flow, allowing biological and physicochemical processes to act synergistically within the SSF system.

The effectiveness of SSF for removing lindane (a persistent and toxic organochlorine pesticide) from drinking water was evaluated. The study is methodically structured, examining the impact of lindane concentration, turbidity, bed depth, and types of Schmutzdecke (biofilm layer) on removal efficiency. It shows that SSF can achieve up to 100% removal efficiency at low lindane concentrations (20 μg/L), but its capacity diminishes at higher concentrations (e.g., 85.4% at 200 μg/L). The use of an acclimated schmutzdecke significantly enhanced removal by approximately 15%, indicating a potential for bioaugmentation in SSF systems. The use of both natural and adapted schmutzdecke layers adds depth to the analysis and suggests pathways for enhancing biodegradation in SSF systems. Additionally, while the study asserts that turbidity had little effect on lindane removal, it does not consider longer-term clogging or operational stability. Finally, results are limited to batch-scale pilots and do not include long-term or seasonal performance assessments.

The performance of SSF in reducing antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and mobile genetic elements (MGEs) in wastewater effluent used as irrigation water was investigated. The SSFs achieved significant log reductions in several ARGs (up to 3-log for sul1 and intI1genetic markers commonly used to study antibiotic resistance and horizontal gene transfer in environmental microbiology), with greater removal efficiency observed for extracellular DNA than intracellular DNA. However, complete removal was not attained, indicating potential environmental risks from residual genetic contaminants. Seasonal variations and filter media type (quartz sand vs anthracite) also influenced removal efficiency, with quartz performing better. This study is pioneering in its focus on genetic pollution in water reuse, addressing a pressing global issue related to the spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). It effectively combines molecular biology techniques (qPCR) with environmental engineering approaches, presenting a nuanced understanding of SSF limitations and capabilities. The inclusion of both intracellular and extracellular DNA analysis, as well as seasonal monitoring, adds robustness to the conclusions. Nonetheless, the study’s scope is constrained to a pilot-scale setup and does not assess downstream ecological impacts or human health risks directly. Moreover, while differences in filter media were noted, the underlying microbial mechanisms or physicochemical interactions driving these differences were not fully explored. Still, this paper offers critical insights into the role of SSFs in mitigating the dissemination of ARGs in treated wastewater and underscores the need for additional treatment barriers or disinfection steps in water reuse applications.

The removal efficiency of SSF systems for various pollutants is variable (Table ). For metallic trace elements like Cd, Cu, Pb, and Zn, extraction capacities vary, with Pb showing a high removal rate of 94.8%, while Cd has a lower efficiency of 9.2%. Turbidity can be reduced from 30 NTU to ≤0.1 NTU, demonstrating significant clarity improvement. Lead can be effectively removed by 96%, while for turbidity and phosphate, reductions from 67.35 to 2.28 NTU and 6.87 to 0.23 mg/L, respectively, are noted. In terms of microbiological pollutants, coliforms are reduced from 1400 to 43.88 CFU/100 mL. For organic pollutants like monochlorophenols, concentrations drop from 20 to 0 μg/L, and other chemicals such as DEET, paracetamol, and caffeine also see significant reductions. Filtration rates range from 20 mL/min to 3–4 m/day, indicating the adaptability of SSF in handling various pollutants effectively under different operational conditions.

The efficiency of SSF in removing lead (Pb) from wastewater originating near Morocco’s Draa Lasfar mine was investigated by ref . The study addressed contamination of the Tensift River caused by untreated mine wastewater, which has resulted in severe Pb pollution and associated health risks for nearby communities. The study focused on the effect of flow velocity on Pb removal, using three lab-scale SSF columns operated at 0.5, 1.0, and 2.5 cm/min. Lower flow rates yielded superior removal, with 100% Pb removal initially observed at 0.5 cm/min, though performance declined over time as the filter media became saturated. The research highlighted breakthrough and exhaustion points, with faster flows reaching saturation quicker and removing less Pb due to insufficient residence time. To model these dynamics, the authors employed a Generalized Richards Model (GRM), which incorporated water velocity as a key variable and demonstrated significantly better fit with experimental data than the conventional logistic model. While the use of GRM enhances theoretical understanding of SSF behavior, the study lacks discussion of long-term filter maintenance, potential pathogen removal, or real-world implementation barriers. Moreover, only Pb was considered, which limits broader applicability to mixed-contaminant scenarios. Despite these limitations, the research reinforces SSF’s promise as a low-cost, adaptable option for rural water treatment and underscores the need to carefully control hydraulic parameters to optimize pollutant retention. Future work should validate these findings in field-scale systems and integrate biological and operational factors to improve the robustness and scalability of SSF for heavy metal removal.

The efficiency of SSF for removing heavy metals (e.g., Cd, Cu, Pb, and Zn) from water contaminated by effluents of the Draa Lasfar mine in Morocco, with a particular focus on the effect of filter height, was assessed. Laboratory-scale SSF columns (8 cm diameter) with sand bed heights of 5, 10, and 15 cm were evaluated by using real wastewater from the Tensift River. Results demonstrated that metal removal was initially complete (100%) but declined over time due to the progressive saturation of adsorption sites (Figure ), with longer filter beds maintaining removal efficiency for a greater duration.

From Figure , the evolution of the Cu concentration in the effluent shows a typical breakthrough behavior that strongly depends on the sand filter height. The shortest bed (H = 5 cm) reaches saturation rapidly, resulting in an early increase in the Cu concentration, while higher filter depths (H = 10 and 15 cm) delay breakthrough and maintain lower effluent concentrations for longer filtration times. This trend indicates that increasing the sand bed height provides additional active adsorption sites and extends the mass transfer zone, thereby enhancing Cu removal efficiency in slow sand filtration systems.

The reviewed studies collectively show that SSF effectively removes a wide range of contaminants, including turbidity, pathogens, heavy metals, nutrients, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and emerging pollutants, such as antibiotic resistance genes. While conventional SSF excels at filtering out particulate matter and readily biodegradable organic compounds, the reduction of nutrients and trace contaminants depends on more complex and often synergistic mechanisms. These mechanisms include biological degradation mediated by biofilms in the schmutzdecke, adsorption onto mineral and carbonaceous media, redox-driven transformations of contaminants, and extended hydraulic residence times associated with low filtration rates.

For persistent organic micropollutants, such as chlorophenols, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and personal care products, biological degradation alone is often insufficient. Therefore, it is necessary to incorporate modified filter media and additional treatment barriers. Studies that utilize granular activated carbon, biochar, graphene oxide, and iron-based materials consistently demonstrate the enhanced removal of nutrients and trace contaminants. This improvement is attributed to an increased adsorption capacity, catalytic activity, and better microbial colonization.

Enhancement of SSF Performance

Filtration Rate

A study of the influence of filtration rate and filter media on rainwater purification and microbial community dynamics in SSFs, using three media types, quartz sand (QS), activated carbon (AC), and activated alumina (AA), was conducted. The study combined pollutant removal performance with detailed microbial ecology assessments across the biofilm and water phases. It confirms that higher filtration rates (0.3 m/h vs 0.1 m/h) reduce COD, NH4 +-N, and total phosphorus (TP) removal efficiencies, undermining SSF efficacy. Activated carbon was most effective for ammonium removal (up to 64%), while alumina achieved >90% TP removal through sorption. The study innovatively applied molecular ecological network and neutral community modeling to show that bacterial community assembly is governed by stochastic processes, especially in the water phase, where 86% of the operational taxonomic units were consistent with the neutral theory.

While the work offers valuable insights into microbiome–function relationships in SSFs (Figure ), it is limited by the use of synthetic rainwater, which omits real-world variability. Moreover, practical aspects like filter longevity, maintenance, and pathogen control remain underexplored. The reliance on relative abundance for keystone species identification also overlooks potential metabolic contributions of rare taxa. Nevertheless, the findings strengthen the ecological framework underpinning SSF optimization, especially under variable hydraulic loads. Future studies should validate these findings under field conditions, incorporate real rainwater matrices, and assess implications for pathogen removal and long-term system resilience. Overall, this study advances both microbiological theory and applied engineering practice but would benefit from broader validation and integration of operational challenges.

The effect of filtration rate on the performance of SSF in removing total coliforms from river water, with a particular focus on the schmutzdecke layer, has been examined. The study compared two filtration rates (0.2 and 0.4 m/h) using raw water from the Amprong River, pretreated with a horizontal roughing filter (HRF). Both SSF units achieved high coliform removal efficiencies, 99.6% for the slower rate and 99.4% for the faster rate, though neither met the Indonesian standard for potable water (zero coliforms per 100 mL). Schmutzdecke characterization via Scanning Electron Microscopy–Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM–EDX) and Gram staining revealed diverse microbial communities, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacilli, forming a biologically active layer essential for contaminant removal. The filtration rate did not show a statistically significant impact on performance (p > 0.05), but the authors noted that higher rates risk more frequent clogging and schmutzdecke damage.

The study by Matuzahroh (2020) investigated the performance of schmutzdecke (a biologically active layer in slow sand filters) under different filtration rates (0.2 and 0.4 m/h) for the removal of total coliform bacteria from river water. Using water from the Amprong River and a combination of HRF and SSF, the research found that a filtration rate of 0.2 m/h yielded a coliform removal efficiency of 99.6%, slightly higher than the 99.4% achieved at 0.4 m/h. Despite this, the treated water still failed to meet the Indonesian drinking water standard, which requires zero coliforms per 100 mL. This study makes a valuable contribution by focusing on the biological behavior of the schmutzdecke layer in SSFs, specifically under different hydraulic loading rates. It effectively combines biological, microbiological, and morphological analyses through SEM–EDX imaging and bacterial identification, enriching the understanding of microbial activity within the filter. The inclusion of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria with diverse colony morphologies highlights the microbial complexity of schmutzdecke. However, while the findings are promising, the operational duration was short (10 days), and the inability to meet drinking water standards suggests that SSF alone may be insufficient and must be complemented with disinfection. Additionally, despite bacterial identification, the study does not analyze specific functional groups responsible for coliform degradation.

Flux Modeling

Only a limited number of studies have quantitatively addressed flux behavior and transport modeling in SSF systems. Nieć et al. applied a modified Iwasaki deep-bed filtration framework coupled with a logistic biomass growth function to describe solids deposition, biomass accumulation, and clogging dynamics as functions of filter depth and operational time, demonstrating good agreement with experimental data and highlighting the depth-dependent variability of the filter coefficient.

Hydraulic loading and pore-space evolution in governing flux decline and mass transfer limitations during long-term SSF operation, identifying biomass growth and particulate accumulation as dominant drivers of permeability loss, were studied by Melloul et al. In a field-oriented context, a regression-based SSF performance model linking effluent quality to discharge, media depth, and detention time achieved an R2 of 77.2%, confirming that reduced filtration flux and increased residence time significantly enhance microbial and turbidity removal in real surface water treatment scenarios.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate the potential of flux-based and transport models to describe SSF performance and clogging behavior; however, their limited number and site-specific calibration requirements currently constrain the development of generalized predictive flux models for SSF systems.

Hybrid SSF Systems and Modifications

A dual-barrier system combining an intermittent household slow sand filter (HSSF) and a UV–C disinfection reactor for treating harvested rainwater in Brazil has been proposed. The study was conducted over 100 consecutive days under real conditions and is noteworthy for its practical relevance to rural and decentralized water treatment. A major strength of this study is the simulation of real-world contamination by inoculating rainwater with E. coli, which allowed the authors to evaluate disinfection efficacy under worst-case scenarios. The findings revealed that while the HSSF provided only limited E. coli removal (1.10 ± 0.33 log), the UV reactor achieved substantial inactivation (4.60 ± 0.58 log), with 87.5% of samples reaching drinking water standards. Importantly, the authors concluded that for microbiologically compromised but physically clean rainwater, the filtration step may be redundant. Despite its practical contributions, the study has several limitations. The schmutzdecke did not fully develop during the 100-day operation, limiting the HSSF’s biological filtration potential.

An interesting approach which involves the integration of 3D-printed clay and crawfish shell composite bricks into SSF systems for decentralized wastewater treatment has been proposed. Three configurations were tested (Figure ): SSFs with clay bricks, SSFs with composite (clay + 10% crawfish shell) bricks, and a sand-only control. Across a 36-day trial using pre-UV-treated municipal wastewater, all systems showed strong performance in turbidity (up to 96%), COD (22–85%), total organic carbon (TOC) (3–74%), and bacterial removal (E. coli and total coliforms: ∼97–99%). The clay-brick reactor achieved the highest mean reductions for turbidity (87%), COD (55%), and TOC (47%), while the composite-brick reactor excelled in removing a broader range of contaminants of emerging concern (CECs), especially sucralose (p = 0.026), suggesting unique sorptive properties linked to chitin-derived biopolymers.

The data presented in Figure indicate that using 3D-printed clay and clay–crawfish composite bricks enhances pollutant removal in slow sand filtration systems. This enhancement is due to the bricks providing additional porous surfaces that facilitate both adsorption and microbial attachment.

The CEC removal performance, though impressive for select compounds (e.g., fluoxetine: 100%), was variable across analytes, with minimal removal for carbamazepine and sulfamethoxazole, indicating the limited universality of the material. Cost analysis supports economic feasibility (∼100–200 USD per system), but the sustainability of crawfish shell sourcing and scalability remain open questions. While the study effectively introduces a novel waste-valorization approach to SSF using 3D printing, further work is needed to optimize material formulations, assess life cycle impacts, and validate microbial safety and hydraulic performance under real-world rural conditions.

The performance of SSF for the removal of organic and inorganic pollutants has been improved by building on material innovation methodologies (Table ). It has been demonstrated that media functionalization, such as iron oxide-coated sand, greatly improves the removal of dissolved organic carbon from baseline values of approximately 45–55% to 75–90%, while sand layers altered with biochar can raise the removal efficiency of COD from approximately 50–65% to 85–95%. The performance of unmodified SSFs is approximately doubled by dual-media configurations that combine sand and anthracite to boost turbidity removal to near-complete levels (>95%) and zeolite–sand composites that improve ammonium removal to 70–85%. BOD5 elimination is further increased to up to 98% through bioaugmentation with specific pollutant-degrading bacteria, demonstrating the possibility of combining biological enhancements with optimal media.

Summary of Modifications Which Enhances SSF Performance

Performance optimization also heavily relies on operational enhancements. Grain size distribution optimization (0.15–0.35 mm) increases pathogen removal from 1 to 2 log units to 3–5 log units, whereas pretreatment with horizontal roughing filters can increase COD removal from ∼50–60% to 80–90%. In contrast to intermittent operation (resting times), which raises COD removal efficiency to 75–90% by lowering biofilm stress and preserving microbial activity, extended ripening periods encourage higher DOC removal by permitting more mature and active biofilm growth. A further defense against waterborne illness is provided by novel antimicrobial modifications, such as adding silver nanoparticles to the sand layer, which have achieved >99% pathogen clearance. Together, these changes show how integrating operational and material-based approaches can overcome SSF’s drawbacks and greatly increase its suitability for decentralized and small-community water treatment systems.

A comprehensive interdisciplinary study on optimizing SSF for drinking water production by examining how sand type, grain size, and schmutzdecke inoculation influence microbial community development and Escherichia coli removal in mini-scale filters has been conducted. Their findings affirm that fine-grain virgin sand (D50 = 0.16 mm) outperforms coarse sands in supporting schmutzdecke development, evidenced by higher EPS production, protein/carbohydrate ratios, and microbial biomass, and achieves the highest bacterial removal (up to 1.58 log10 units). The addition of mature schmutzdecke inoculum modestly accelerated biofilm development (Figure ) but had a limited impact on long-term community structure or removal performance. Using HYDRUS-1D modeling, the authors show that bacterial removal is governed primarily by attachment kinetics, with biofilm maturity introducing additional interaction sites that are not present in fresh filters. Regression analysis revealed that the protein/carbohydrate ratio of the biofilm, rather than biomass or diversity metrics, is the strongest predictor of removal efficiency.

While the study is methodologically robust, several limitations constrain its broader applicability. It relies on tap water (a low-nutrient matrix) and synthetic E. coli spikes rather than complex natural influents, limiting generalizability to rural or decentralized contexts. Additionally, the inoculum’s limited effect may result from inadequate adhesion or hydraulic washout, highlighting the need for improved inoculation strategies. The study also lacks exploration of viral or protozoan removal and long-term filter operation. Nonetheless, its novel integration of microbial ecology, biochemical profiling, and transport modeling offers valuable design insights for enhancing SSF performance and suggests that the protein/carbohydrate ratio may serve as a practical bioindicator for filter efficacy.

Oliveira et al. (2019) studied the efficacy of two types of SSFs, traditional and modified, in removing Giardia duodenalis cysts and turbidity from surface water in Brazil. The modified filter incorporated a gravel prefilter layer, intended to enhance initial removal performance. Over a 40-day operation period, the modified filter achieved a mean turbidity removal of 97.1% and a Giardia removal efficiency of 90.1%, compared to 88.9% and 80.1%, respectively, in the traditional setup. Despite notable reductions, the filters did not fully meet Brazilian drinking water standards, which demand complete G. lamblia removal.

The effectiveness of upflow roughing filtration (RF) as a pretreatment for slow sand filtration in a water treatment plant serving the Aqbat Jaber refugee camp in Palestine, particularly during the rainy season when canal water turbidity spikes, was evaluated. Their pilot study tested two RF columns under various filtration rates (0.5 and 1.5 m/h) and feedwater conditions, including extremely turbid spiked water (800–1000 NTU), with and without ferric chloride coagulant addition. The study found that RFs without coagulant successfully reduced turbidity from ∼90 NTU to <20 NTU, suitable for SSF input, especially at lower filtration rates. Coagulation significantly enhanced removal under extreme turbidity conditions but led to faster clogging and potential iron residue. This paper is commendable for its practical application, clear experimental design, and relevance to water-stressed regions relying on low-tech solutions. It convincingly demonstrates that RFs, especially when optimized for filtration rate and gravel stratification, can significantly extend SSF filter runs and maintain operation during high-turbidity events. However, while coagulant use proves beneficial for extreme conditions, the associated operational issues, such as shortened filter runs and elevated residual iron, necessitate careful management. The study could benefit from integrating SSF performance data post-RF treatment and further assessing the long-term maintenance needs. Still, it provides a strong case for the use of rough filtration in decentralized and emergency water treatment scenarios.

The performance of a biologically active slow sand filter (BASSF) integrated with a membrane filtration unit to remove contaminants from surface waterfocusing on turbidity, organics, and microbial indicatorswas explored. The study revealed that the BASSF effectively reduced turbidity (up to 98%) and total coliforms, while also lowering DOC and UV254 absorbance, indicating the removal of organic matter. The membrane unit further improved water quality, achieving consistent microbial safety and reduced membrane fouling, attributed to the biofilm’s pretreatment effect. This study is notable for its realistic pilot-scale design (Figure ) and combined treatment strategy that leverages the biological capacity (Figure ) of SSF and the physical barrier of membranes. It demonstrates how biofilms in the SSF not only enhance organic and microbial contaminant removal but also serve as an effective pretreatment stage that extends membrane lifespan and efficiency. Long-term monitoring (over 300 days) strengthens the reliability of the results. However, while the integration improved treatment outcomes, operational complexities such as cleaning protocols, membrane fouling over time, and cost implications were not deeply analyzed. Furthermore, the study primarily evaluates standard water quality parameters without delving into trace contaminants or emerging pollutants. Nevertheless, it provides a compelling case for coupling natural treatment processes with modern membrane technology for decentralized or semiurban water treatment systems.

The resilience and functional recovery of mature SSFs postmaintenance have been investigated. The study is significant in its scope, focusing on microbial biomass recovery, community composition, and removal efficiency of DOC and NH4 + after the mechanical disturbance caused by schmutzdecke scraping. The combination of molecular (16S rRNA gene sequencing), biochemical (adenosine triphosphate), and flow cytometry demonstrates that microbial biomass is initially reduced in the top layers postscraping but quickly recovers due to the compensatory activity of deeper, undisturbed sand layers. Furthermore, the removal of DOC and NH4 + remained stable, underlining the functional redundancy and robustness of the microbial communities in mature SSFs. This challenges the prevailing assumption that the schmutzdecke layer is indispensable for filtration, particularly in well-established systems.

The treatment of culinary wastewater by system dynamics (SD) modeling using STELLA software to evaluate the performance of RF and SSF was calculated. The simulations included the removal of turbidity, total suspended solids, biological oxygen demand, chemical oxygen demand, and phosphate using real experimental data and model predictions. The critical strength of this study lies in its integration of system dynamics modeling, which allows the researchers to explore causal relationships between variables such as dissolved oxygen and microbial growth. The model validation showed error margins below 10%, confirming the model’s accuracy and reliability. The RF showed excellent performance in removing turbidity (80.5–85%) and total suspended solids (TSS) (70.6–85%). Meanwhile, the SSF achieved variable results, with BOD and COD removals depending significantly on dissolved oxygen levels and microbial activity. Notably, the SSF effluent initially exceeded regulatory standards for BOD and COD, but through scenario simulations, by increasing dissolved oxygen and microbial growth rate, the system achieved compliant effluent levels. While the model structure and validation are well-developed, the scope could be enhanced by including other influential factors such as hydraulic loading rates, temperature, or media characteristics, which were acknowledged in the conclusion. Furthermore, the phosphate removal efficiency was relatively low (∼15–27%), indicating the need for further optimization or addition of chemical treatment for nutrient removal.

Comparative analysis of SSF configurations reveals distinct performance trends across various studies. Combining SSF with complementary physical or chemical barriers, such as roughing filters, UV disinfection, or membrane units, consistently improves contaminant removal efficiency and operational stability. Modified media, including iron oxide-coated sand, biochar, zeolite, and waste-derived composites, exhibit enhanced adsorption and biological activity, effectively addressing the limitations of conventional sand beds.

In particular, dual-media systems and biochar amendments enhance the removal of organic and microbial pollutants, while zerovalent iron (ZVI) and graphene oxide layers target the removal of trace metals and micropollutants. These advancements indicate a shift from traditional passive filtration methods to multifunctional hybrid systems that integrate adsorption, biodegradation, and catalytic reactions.

However, the scalability, cost-effectiveness, and long-term stability of these systems require further validation in real-world settings to ensure their practical implementation in decentralized and resource-limited environments.

Enhancement of Schmutzdecke Formation via Organic Additives

The enhancement of SSF with the antagonistic fungus Trichoderma atroviride to suppress Rhizoctonia solani in closed soilless cucumber cultivation systems was investigated. Using a factorial design, the disease severity, incidence, and control efficacy were assessed across five replicated trials. Results showed that inoculating SSF with T. atroviride significantly reduced disease severity and incidence, achieving an average control percentage of 86%, which was 49% higher than that of SSF alone and 86% better than having no control method. Interestingly, even the application of T. atroviride in irrigation water, though less effective (77%), demonstrated substantial pathogen suppression and can be applied for water conservation (Figure ). In contrast, the SSF without T. atroviride achieved only 37% control, highlighting the added benefit of biological enhancement.

While there is strong evidence for biologically enhanced SSF in phytopathogen control, it is effective for the short trial duration (2 weeks), which leaves long-term filter performance, persistence of T. atroviride colonization, and resilience under fluctuating environmental conditions as areas for future work. Additionally, the closed-loop system design, although innovative, does not fully simulate large-scale or commercial horticultural conditions, limiting external validity. Furthermore, the study narrowly focused on R. solani, and broader pathogen spectra or potential interactions were not addressed. Nevertheless, the integration of microbial biocontrol into SSF presents a promising strategy to minimize the use of chemical pesticides and improve drainage reuse in water-scarce regions.

Flux modeling of biofilm growth in slow sand filters presents an innovative and comprehensive mathematical framework to simulate biofilm dynamics. The model integrates a nonlinear convection-diffusion-reaction system with a discontinuous flux, capturing the spatial and temporal behavior of microbial communities within the SSF. A notable strength of the study lies in its multiphase formulation, which distinguishes between the biofilm matrix, enclosed water, and flowing suspension, enabling a realistic representation of mass transfer, attachment–detachment processes, and ecological interactions. One of the main contributions is the inclusion of a Cahn–Hilliard-type equation to represent biofilm expansion, paired with an adaptive time-stepping numerical scheme that ensures stability and physical realism. Unlike previous models, which often oversimplify the biofilm structure or assume uniform behavior, this model accommodates heterogeneity in microbial activity and structural dynamics. However, while the model is theoretically robust and flexible, its practical implementation may be limited by computational intensity and the requirement for numerous parameters, many of which are difficult to measure or estimate in situ. The authors provide valuable insights into the development and performance of the schmutzdecke over a 13-month period in both mini- and midi-scale SSF (Figure ), examining its biochemical properties, microbial composition, and bacterial removal. Both filter scales exhibited comparable trends in schmutzdecke formation and biomass accumulation over time; however, bacterial removal, particularly of E. coli, remained low (<0.7 log10) even after biofilm maturation, and the addition of mature biofilm inoculum only slightly improved performance. Although biochemical maturationreflected in protein, carbohydrate, and biomass accumulationwas consistent across scales, the microbial community composition within the schmutzdecke diverged notably. Dominant taxa such as Comamonadaceae, Nitrosomonadaceae, and Hyphomicrobiaceae were present in both systems, but their relative abundances and active fractions varied with filter size and age. Overall, these findings indicate that while biochemical development followed a similar trajectory across scales, the microbial community structure and activity were scale-dependent and not directly correlated with biochemical indicators of filter maturity.

Modified SSF Systems with Functional Chemical Additives

Zero-valent iron (ZVI) incorporated into sand columns as a permeable layer to enhance bacterial and algal removal under both slow and fast filtration conditions has been investigated. Their results show that amending sand columns with 10 wt % ZVI achieved complete capture and inactivation of both Gram-negative E. coli and Gram-positive B. subtilis across a range of ionic strengths (1–100 mM NaCl), flow velocities (4 and 90 m/day), and even in real river water and humic acid-rich conditions. ZVI improved performance not just through enhanced bacterial adsorptionowing to favorable electrostatic interactionsbut also through inactivation mechanisms involving oxidative stress, extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) disruption, and membrane damage. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by ZVI oxidation played a key role in microbial lethality, as evidenced by increased lactate dehydrogenase release and reduced intracellular adenosine triphosphate. Furthermore, the system showed strong durability during prolonged filtration (100 pore volumes) and maintained efficiency even when ZVI was regenerated and reused. Algal cells (Microcystis aeruginosa) were also completely captured and inactivated, suggesting broader applicability. Despite the filtration performance remaining high, the long-term impact of biofouling and iron oxide buildup on hydraulic conductivity was not fully addressed. Totally, when comparing slow filtration to fast filtration, the latter reduced bacterial capture efficiency due to increased hydrodynamic drag, but ZVI-amended sand columns maintained complete removal under both conditions.

Incorporation of graphene oxide (GO)-coated sand in SSF to enhance the removal of micropollutantsspecifically atrazine (ATZ) and atenolol (ATL)from real groundwater has been tested. The study demonstrated that GO-coated sand significantly outperformed conventional sand, achieving near-complete removal of ATZ and ATL, as well as superior reductions in total organic carbon and turbidity. Through batch adsorption experiments and filtration trials, the pollutant removal has been attributed primarily to GO’s electrostatic, hydrogen bonding, and π–π interactions with target molecules, rather than the biological layer (schmutzdecke). A techno-economic analysis estimated additional costs of 0.34–3.25 USD per m3 of treated water depending on GO price (10–100 USD/kg), suggesting economic feasibility at scale if GO becomes more affordable. The study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions with relatively low influent organic loads (TOC ≈ 2–3 mg/L), which may underestimate the fouling risk in real, variable source waters. The 30-day filtration duration leaves longer-term GO stability, regeneration potential, and leaching risks untested. Moreover, the decline in performance when the schmutzdecke was present suggests operational challenges in real-world applications, where biofilm formation is inevitable.

The article by Fitriani et al. (2023) explores the effectiveness of an intermittently operated slow sand filter (ISSF) using modified filter mediablood clam shells (CC) and a mixture of sand and blood clam shells (SC)under varying acclimatization periods (14 and 28 days). This study is particularly valuable for wastewater treatment in resource-limited settings, where affordable and locally sourced materials are crucial. The research presents compelling evidence that SC media significantly improves turbidity removal (up to ∼46%), while CC media performs better in ammonia and phosphate removal, likely due to its higher calcium content aiding adsorption and chemical precipitation. A major strength of this study is its comprehensive experimental design, integrating SEM-EDX to characterize biofilm development and surface morphology, alongside standard analytical techniques to assess removal efficiencies. The conclusion is that acclimatization time enhances filter stability and performance, correlating with biofilm maturation and schmutzdecke formation. Nevertheless, it lacks detailed microbial community analysis that could explain biological contributions to nutrient reduction. Furthermore, the short duration of filter operation (10 days postacclimatization) might not fully capture long-term stability or clogging behavior. The study would benefit from including pathogen indicators (e.g., coliforms) to better evaluate its suitability for decentralized sanitation.

Performance of SSF and Its Real-World Applications

Recent studies have extended the application of SSF, a long-established and dependable water treatment technique, to a range of challenging water matrices, such as contaminated groundwater, industrially influenced waters, and municipal effluents. SSF is frequently assessed for its capacity to eliminate pathogens, suspended particles, turbidity, heavy metals, nutrients, and a variety of organic pollutants in both real-world and pilot-scale applications, − as summarized in Table . For particular targets like arsenic, chlorophenols, and refractory organics, changes to the filter bed, such as adding biochar, iron-based media, or zerovalent iron, have been extensively explored to improve removal efficiency. − These studies shed important light on the operational adaptability and constraints of SSF in practical settings. SSF has proven to be a successful tertiary polishing step in various municipal wastewater treatment scenarios, lowering turbidity and total suspended particles to satisfy reuse criteria and drastically reducing fecal indicator bacteria (E. coli, Enterococci) by up to three log units. It has been demonstrated in both lab-scale and pilot-scale experiments that adding iron-coated sand or biochar improves turbidity and microbial clearance, with biochar increasing the biodegradation rate within the schmutzdecke layer. SSF can achieve significant BOD reductions (79–92%) and moderate COD reductions (40–60%) for nutrient and organic matter control when applied to secondary effluent, demonstrating its feasibility for effluent polishing in both centralized and decentralized systems.

Results of SSF Testing for Real Contaminated Water

The composition of the medium affects the effectiveness of heavy metal removal. When iron layers or iron-coated sand was added to SSF beds, groundwater that had been spiked with arsenic showed sustained removal, exceeding traditional sand media. Similarly, high and quick arsenic removal efficiencies have been attained using filters altered with zerovalent iron (ZVI). Both drinking water and industrial wastewater applications can benefit from the substantial improvements in copper, zinc, iron, manganese, and phosphate removal, as well as the decreased levels of coliform that biochar-modified biosand filters have demonstrated. − These examples show how media adaptation can preserve the fundamental advantages of SSF while addressing the contamination characteristics.

More complicated organic pollutants have also been evaluated for removal using SSF in addition to inorganic ones. SSF eliminated 4-CP without acclimation in investigations where monochlorophenol isomers (e.g., 2-CP, 3-CP, and 4-CP) were added to surface water; however, biofilm adaptation was necessary for the other isomers. It has also been shown that pretreating wastewater with SSF prior to ultrafiltration reduces organic foulants such as proteins and polysaccharides, lowers membrane fouling rates, and lengthens run times. These results highlight how versatile SSF is for a variety of contaminant classes, especially when paired with specific media modifications or included in hybrid treatment systems. Under practical operating conditions, the examined real-sample case studies validate SSF’s adaptability in eliminating contaminants, turbidity, metals, nutrients, and both biodegradable and resistant organics. Certain removal efficiencies can be greatly increased by filter bed modifications, particularly the use of biochar and iron-based media, without sacrificing SSF’s low-energy, low-maintenance characteristics. SSF is still a potential technique for decentralized water treatment and as a pretreatment step in multibarrier systems because of its demonstrated versatility, particularly in rural or resource-constrained settings.

Conclusions and Future Perspectives

This review offers a comprehensive analysis of recent advancements in slow sand filtration technologies, emphasizing their adaptability for pollutant removal in decentralized and small-community water treatment systems. The evolution of SSF from traditional biophysical systems to hybrid configurations enhanced with functionalized materials, such as graphene oxide, zerovalent iron, activated carbon, biochar, and waste-derived composites, has significantly improved treatment performance and expanded its applicability.

The incorporation of advanced analytical and modeling tools, including molecular biology, flux modeling, and system dynamics, has enhanced our understanding of SSF behavior and allowed for the optimization of both the biological and physicochemical processes.

Several trends have emerged in the studies reviewed. Modified or hybrid SSF systems consistently show superior removal of turbidity, organic pollutants, pathogens, heavy metals, and trace organics compared to conventional sand filters. For instance, dual-media and biochar-amended filters improve adsorption and biodegradation; zerovalent iron (ZVI) and iron-oxide coatings enhance the removal of heavy metals and arsenic; and graphene oxide or composite-based media increase SSF’s capacity for controlling micropollutants and nutrients.

Optimal hydraulic conditionsparticularly lower filtration rates (0.1–0.3 m/h)and extended ripening periods are essential for achieving high microbial stability and removal efficiency. The integration of biological enhancers, such as Trichoderma atroviride, or coupling SSF with UV disinfection, roughing filters, or membrane units creates multifunctional hybrid systems capable of addressing a wider range of contaminants.

Real-world case studies confirm that SSF operates reliably under various water qualities and environmental conditions. In both municipal and rural applications, SSF has achieved up to a 3-log reduction in fecal indicator bacteria, over 90% removal of turbidity and biochemical oxygen demand, and significant reductions of heavy metals (lead, cadmium, copper, zinc) and nutrients from mining, agricultural, and domestic effluents. Field-scale trials using mixed-media or biochar-modified filters demonstrate the robustness of SSF as a tertiary polishing step, producing effluent turbidity below 1 NTU and ensuring compliance with reuse standards. These findings validate SSF’s potential for decentralized water purification, particularly in low-resource settings. Despite promising laboratory and pilot-scale results, a clear gap remains between controlled experimental studies and full-scale SSF implementation, especially concerning long-term operational stability, maintenance needs, hydraulic variability, and cost-effectiveness under real-world conditions.

Challenges remain, including the removal of nutrients and emerging contaminants, long-term sustainability, and standardization of operational parameters. Future research should focus on scaling up hybrid and modified systems, evaluating life cycle costs, and validating long-term field performance. Overall, SSF remains a low-cost, energy-efficient, and environmentally sustainable technology, with hybrid and tailored designs offering strong potential to meet global water treatment and reuse demands.
