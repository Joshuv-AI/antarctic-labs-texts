Built Environment Implementation Strategies to Promote Physical Activity: Expert Consensus for Local Implementation of Physical Activity Policy, Systems, and Environment (ECLIPPSE)
Authors: Stephenie C. Lemon, Karin Valentine Goins, Julien Leider, Christiaan G. Abildso, Mark Fenton, Cynthia K. Perry, Sandy J. Slater, Jamie F. Chriqui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Context:Despite evidence supporting the role of the built environment in promoting physical activity, there is limited guidance on how to implement recommended built environment changes at the local level.Objectives:The objective of this project was to develop a compilation of implementation strategies that hold promise for aiding communities in implementing built environment changes that support active transportation and leisure-time physical activity, titled “Expert Consensus for Local Implementation of Physical Activity Policy, Systems, and Environment (ECLIPPSE).”Design:A modified Delphi Technique using 3 web-based survey rounds was conducted from 2024 to 2025.Setting:A multidisciplinary national expert panel was recruited to participate in this online panel.Participants:The panel consisted of 23 professionals with expertise in the implementation of built environment changes to support physical activity at the local level.Main Outcome Measure:The Round 1 and 2 surveys utilized a rating process to establish a compilation of potentially promising strategies for aiding communities in implementing physical activity-supportive built environment changes. The Round 3 survey involved rating these strategies on potential feasibility and impact and endorsing the final compilation.Results:Fifty-five strategies emerged in 2 broad domains. The first domain, “Get Ready,” included 4 categories: (1) Partnerships and collaboration (5 strategies); (2) staffing (5 strategies); (3) training and education (4 strategies), and (4) interactive assistance (2 strategies). The second domain, “Take Action,” included 6 categories: (1) Data and community informed prioritization (6 strategies); (2) evaluative (8 strategies); (3) community awareness and demand (8 strategies); (4) engagement with decision-making processes (5 strategies); (5) funding (7 strategies); and (6) pilots or demonstration projects (5 strategies). The final compilation was endorsed by 95% of respondents.Conclusions:The ECLIPPSE strategies can be used to aid implementation of built environment changes at the local level to support physical activity.

Physical activity is critical for preventing chronic diseases,1 promoting positive mental health2 and reducing health care costs.1 However, the majority of Americans do not meet recommended levels of physical activity.3 Physical activity can be achieved through both leisure-time physical activity and active transportation4 via walking, biking, and use of mobility devices.3,5

Physical activity can be promoted when the built environment supports safe and accessible opportunities to be active.5 The Community Preventive Services Task Force recommends built environment interventions that combine transportation interventions to improve pedestrian or bicycle transportation systems with land use and environmental design interventions to increase physical activity for all Americans.6 Example transportation interventions include street connectivity, street design/traffic calming/traffic control devices, pedestrian infrastructure, bicycle roadway infrastructure, multi-use pathway/trail infrastructure and public transit infrastructure and access. Example land use and environmental design interventions include mixed-use development, increased residential density, proximity to destinations, parks, and recreational facilities access and green infrastructure.6,7

Implementing built environment changes requires a policy, systems, and environment (PSE) approach. Built environment decision making typically happens under the jurisdiction of the local government, which decides how and where to invest local, state, and federal resources and dis/approve private investments.8 Several evidence-informed policies (eg, complete streets, pedestrian-oriented zoning) and plans (eg, mobility, long-range transportation) can lead to activity-supportive built environment changes.9,10 State- (eg, bicycle-pedestrian plans) and federal-level (eg, funding, policy guidance) initiatives influence local decision making. However, adopting a policy or plan does not automatically mean that it will be implemented as intended.11 Critical knowledge gaps remain regarding how to implement these policies and plans to achieve built environment changes supportive of physical activity.

Implementing built environment changes requires influencing systems responsible for local decision making. Based on an adapted version of the Interactive Systems Framework (ISF),12 we propose 3 interactive systems specific to the built environment at the local level that can influence implementation (Figure 1). (1) The inside system has decision-making authority within local government for transportation and land use and typically includes departments such as planning, public works, and parks. Public health departments, while not the ultimate decision maker, can play a role as the entity with a health focus.13-15 (2) The outside + system consists primarily of non-government entities such as community-based organizations, advocacy groups, Cooperative Extension agents, and citizen advisors. Government representatives may or may not participate. This system focuses on increasing engagement of the community in built environment efforts and ensuring community needs are considered.15-17 (3) The implementation support system focuses on building capacity and providing resources for the inside and outside + systems. Members of this system are typically not part of the day-to-day work (eg, state health departments, national funders, consultants). Their involvement can be time-limited and funding-dependent. Members of each of these systems will differ across communities, but the core responsibilities remain similar.FIGURE 1Interactive Systems Framework for Built Environment Change, adapted from the Interactive Systems Framework.12

Successful implementation of physical activity-supportive built environment changes can entail use of strategies that support the inside and outside + systems to fulfill their implementation roles. Implementation strategies are “specific methods or techniques used to promote the adoption, implementation, and sustained use of an evidence-based intervention or policy within a real-world setting.”18 The Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) project established a compilation of promising implementation strategies, originally for the health care setting, that serves as a standardized taxonomy for research and common reporting and comparison across projects.18-20 Efforts have been made to adapt the ERIC strategies to non-health care settings,16 including the Implementation Strategies Applied in Communities (ISAC) compilation.21,22 A growing body of literature is evaluating and establishing the utility of a range of implementation strategies. However, most of this research has focused on strategies supporting the implementation of interventions aimed at individuals, particularly in health care or allied service settings.23 While the ISAC compilation does include strategies applicable to the PSE interventions broadly, it was not designed specifically for the built environment.17

Identifying effective implementation strategies that support systems can facilitate built environment changes that promote physical activity.6,10,24 However, there has been no systematic attempt to enumerate, define or evaluate implementation strategies specific to this context. The purpose of this study was to develop a compilation of implementation strategies, titled Expert Consensus for Local Implementation of Physical Activity Policy Systems and Environment (ECLIPPSE), that hold promise for aiding communities in implementing built environment changes that support active transportation and leisure-time physical activity.

Methods

Study overview

This project used a modified Delphi method25,26 to capture input from a sample of experts in implementation of built environment changes supportive of physical activity from across the US. This approach, also used in the development of the ERIC compilation,18 was selected because it allows for a group of experts to achieve consensus on a complex topic. To develop the strategies, the research team developed an initial set of possible strategies and then sought iterative input from the expert panel over 3 rounds. Each round was conducted using an online Qualtrics survey. After each round, results were summarized; Round 1 and 2 results informed the development of the subsequent round’s survey. The project was conducted and reported in accordance with the Conducting and Reporting of Delphi Studies checklist (see Supplemental Digital Content Table A, available at http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B546).27

Initial strategy development

The initial set of strategies was developed by the project team, each of whom has relevant expertise in built environment, land use, and/or transportation approaches and physical activity. First, the 73 strategies included in the ERIC compilation,18 were each reviewed to determine relevance for supporting built environment changes. Strategies that were deemed to have conceptual overlap with each other were consolidated into single items (eg, identify early adopters, visit other sites, shadow experts merged into learn from other communities). The retained strategies were then reviewed for needed adaptations. Two types of adaptations were made: major adaptations involved substantive changes (and sometimes title changes) as well as wording changes for relevance to the built environment (eg, use mass media changed to conduct community outreach campaigns to promote awareness and acceptance), while minor adaptations entailed only wording changes for relevance (eg, obtain and use patients/consumers and family feedback changed to obtain and use community feedback). Second, the research team developed additional strategies relevant for supporting built environment changes (eg, support quick builds). These were informed by strategies suggested by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC),10 the American Planning Association28 and the project team’s research and practice-based experience. The initial set of strategies was developed by 3 members of the team (S.C.L., K.V.G., J.F.C.). These strategies were discussed and modified with input from 4 other team members (C.G.A., M.F., C.K.P., S.J.S).

The initial set included 65 strategies. Of these, 40 strategies were adapted from ERIC (30 from individual ERIC strategies, 10 consolidated from 20 different ERIC strategies), and 25 new strategies were developed. Strategies were organized into 2 conceptual domains with 10 total categories. To define categories, we first reviewed a categorization of the ERIC strategies.19 Six of 9 ERIC categories were deemed appropriate and adapted, and 4 new categories were created.

The first domain, titled “Get Ready”, included 4 categories of strategies: (1) Partnerships/collaborations, (2) staffing, (3) training and education, and (4) interactive assistance. The second domain, titled “Take Action”, included 6 categories of strategies: (1) Data and community-informed prioritization strategies, (2) evaluative strategies, (3) community awareness and demand, (4) engagement with decision-making processes, (5) funding strategies and (6) small pilots or demonstration projects.

Participants

A multidisciplinary panel with experience related to implementation of built environment changes at the local level from a range of geographic areas across the US and disciplines was invited. They were identified by the project team and funder (CDC) and included advisors to and recipients of the CDC’s Division of Nutrition, Physical Activity and Obesity’s State Physical Activity and Nutrition, High Obesity Program and Racial and Ethnic Approaches to Community Health initiatives24 and members of the Physical Activity Policy Research and Evaluation Network.29

Recruitment and retention

Email invitations were sent to each selected expert. The invitation described the project goal and participation requirements, which entailed completion of the 3 surveys over a 5 to 6-month period. Participants were offered a $100 incentive for completing all 3 rounds.

The invitation included a link to complete the Round 1 survey. For Rounds 2 and 3, an email invitation, which reminded participants of the project’s purpose and described the goal of the round, was sent with a link to complete that round’s survey. A similar reminder schedule was implemented at each round, with 4 weekly reminders following the initial invitation. Round 1 was completed from June to July 2024. Round 2 was completed from September to October 2024. Round 3 was completed from December 2024 to January 2025.

A sample size of 10 to 12 experts is recommended when the sample is homogenous.30,31 Because sought a sample with experience across a range of settings (eg, urban-rural, region), we aimed to enroll approximately 20 experts. We thus invited 25 individuals to account for non-response. Twenty-three of the 25 invitees agreed and completed the Round 1 survey (92.0%). Twenty-one (91.3% retention rate) and 21 (91.3% retention rate) completed the Round 2 and Round 3 surveys, respectively (not the same unique participants in Rounds 2 and 3)(Figure 2).FIGURE 2Summary of 3 Modified Delphi Rounds, ECLIPPSE.

Modified Delphi method

Round 1

Participants were asked about their professional position and experience. Participants were then asked to rate each of the 65 strategies on the extent to which it is promising to aid communities in implementing built environment changes that support active transportation and leisure-time physical activity. Ratings were done on a 4-point scale ranging from not at all (4) to extremely promising (1). The survey also asked participants whether they believed the strategy was most appropriate for the inside system only, outside + system only, or both systems. Strategies that at least 80% of respondents rated as extremely or quite promising were retained from Round 1, based on a criterion used in another physical activity-focused modified Delphi study.32 In Round 1 participants were also invited to list additional strategies, but none were suggested.

Round 2

Participants were first informed of the criterion for automatically retaining strategies from Round 1 (80% agreement). They were then asked to review the strategies for which less than 80% of Round 1 respondents rated the given strategy as extremely or quite promising. Respondents had the opportunity to indicate if they believed each strategy should be retained, and if so, to describe their reasoning. Strategies were retained if at least 1/3 of Round 2 respondents endorsed retaining the strategy. This criterion was selected because most of these strategies (8 of 11) were in the “Get Ready” domain and respondents’ comments indicated that they were foundational or important components of a larger set of strategies. A confirmatory factor analysis of the Round 1 ratings was conducted for retained strategies according to category using the principal factor method in Stata/MP 18.0.

Round 3

Participants were presented with the final set of strategies by domain and category. Respondents were asked to rate each strategy on 2 dimensions: feasibility and potential impact; each rating was based on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from not at all (4) to extremely (1). Respondents were then asked whether they endorsed the final set. Mean feasibility and impact ratings for each strategy were calculated and then plotted against each other.

Round 2

Twenty-one of the initial 65 strategies did not achieve the 80% threshold in Round 1 and were included in the Round 2 survey. In Round 2, 11 of these 21 strategies (52%) were endorsed by at least 33% of respondents as strategies that should be retained. Thus, a total of 55 strategies (44 from Round 1, 11 added back in after Round 2) were retained. These 55 strategies included 32 strategies adapted from ERIC (24 from individual ERIC strategies, 8 consolidated from 20 different ERIC strategies) and 23 new strategies. Table 2 lists the final 55 strategies with the Round 1 and Round 2 ratings.

As shown in Supplemental Digital Content Table C, available at http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B548, eigenvalues from the confirmatory factor analysis were generally consistent with single-factor models across all 10 categories. Factor loadings were at least 0.28 for all but 2 strategies (“support for applying for infrastructure funding” and “facilitate relay of relevant data”), suggesting consistency in how strategies were rated within categories. One strategy, “data/equity dashboard,” was originally categorized under Data and Community Informed Prioritization Strategies but was recategorized under Evaluative Strategies based on the initial factor analysis results (0.41 versus −0.18).

Discussion and Conclusion

Guided by an adapted version of the ISF,12 ECLIPPSE provides a compilation of promising strategies to support the local systems responsible for implementing built environment changes that promote physical activity. Through this expert panel methodology, 55 strategies emerged within 2 overarching domains and 10 categories.

While ERIC18 provided a reasonable starting point, substantial changes were required to fit the context of this project. Seven of the 10 ECLIPPSE categories were adapted from 6 ERIC categories (ERIC category Use Evaluative and Iterative Strategies was separated into Data and Community Informed Priorities and Evaluative Strategies in ECLIPPSE). Within these ECLIPPSE categories, adaptations were needed. For example, within the funding category, 2 strategies entailed a minor modification from ERIC (access new funding, alter incentive/allowance structures), 1 was a major modification (fund and contract for the clinical innovation changed to fund partners), and 4 strategies were newly developed (eg, change use of state transportation funding). Six ERIC funding-related strategies were not included (eg, make billing easier). Engagement with decision making is a newly developed category that reflects unique aspects of how built environment changes occur at the local level. Pilot and Demonstration Projects are a single strategy in ERIC. These were identified as critical to obtaining buy-in by one of the co-authors who provides technical assistance to communities of built environment change (MF) and thus established as its own category with 5 distinct strategies. Overall, 23 ERIC strategies were deemed irrelevant for ECLIPPSE (eg, remind clinicians, use capitated payments) and thus not included.

Like ECLIPPSE, other efforts adapted the ERIC strategies to specific contexts (eg, schools, community),21,22 underscoring the need for practical guidance to inform implementation research and practice in specific settings. The ISAC adaptation included strategies for community settings that can be applied to multi-level evidence-based interventions targeting different behaviors. For example, ISAC includes 3 strategies around community engagement that relate to obtaining and acting upon community priorities. ECLIPPSE extends these concepts to the built environment change context by including specific strategies for obtaining such information within the Community and Data-driven Priorities category and creating a new category, Increase Community Awareness and Demand, with strategies specific to the built environment and local jurisdictional decision-making.

Context-specific implementation strategies limit comparisons of the utility of specific strategies across different contexts. However, general strategies may be less useful in practice, particularly among those who are not familiar with implementation science or for whom standardization across this scientific discipline is not a primary concern. Relying on standardized strategies (and definitions) also poses challenges for contexts that are newer to implementation science and its established approaches. Of the 16 strategies in the “Get Ready” domain, 13 (81.25%) were adapted from ERIC and 3 (18.75%) were newly developed. In contrast, of the 39 strategies in the “Take Action” domain, 19 (48.7%) were adapted from ERIC and 20 (51.3%) were newly developed. This could imply that while more foundational strategies (eg, training, technical assistance) are common across contexts, there are important strategies that would go undocumented in the absence of context-specific guidance.

Feasibility and impact ratings identified important considerations for practice. In general, impact was rated higher than feasibility. However, there were differences across categories and individual strategies. Within the “Get Ready” domain, a few strategies stood out as having high feasibility and impact ratings, including build a multi-sector collaborative approach, educate and build relationships with decision-makers, and learn from other communities. These may be high-yield approaches, particularly for communities that are just beginning this work.

Within “Take Action,” most strategies were rated as having moderately high (above the midpoint) feasibility and impact. In the Community Context and Prioritization category, all strategies were above the midpoint for feasibility and impact, with little variability. All Evaluative Strategies were rated as moderately impactful, yet all but 1 was just above the midpoint for feasibility and 1 was below it, likely due to the resources required to support implementation. There was variability in the Create Community Awareness and Demand category, with building relationships with community or neighborhood groups rated as most impactful and celebrating and promoting successes most feasible. With respect to Engagement with Decision-Making Processes, community members serving on local boards were rated as most impactful, while creating testimonials and creating talking points for engaged community members were most feasible. Notably, strategies in the Funding category had high impact but low feasibility ratings. These strategies may be more appropriate for communities with greater community and decision-maker buy-in, track records, and resources. Regarding Small Pilots and Demonstration Projects, projects for pedestrian and bicycle facilities and traffic calming stood out as having both high impact and feasibility.

ECLIPPSE is not prescriptive. Rather, it provides communities with a range of options, or combinations of options, to select from based on their local context, readiness, and capacity. It also recognizes that change takes time and incremental actions are often necessary before large-scale built environment improvements are accomplished. Specific strategies will likely have greater utility depending upon where communities are in the maturity of their work. Some strategies may be best implemented with guidance from an external support system. For example, local technical assistance and training could support funding strategies or pilot and demonstration projects.

The ECLIPPSE strategies are supported by different degrees of scientific evidence. Few studies have specifically tested implementation strategies for built environment changes.33,34 Several strategies have evidence to support their use from other contexts,23 while some—especially the newly developed strategies—are supported only by practice-based experience.24,28 There is growing recognition of the value of practice-based evidence, particularly in policy-related areas and in those that require timely action.35 ECLIPPSE provides a basis for future research on how to support implementation of built environment changes.

Practically speaking, however, built environment work is happening in practice and communities are in immediate need of guidance. ECLIPPSE provides reasonable evidence and practice-informed guidance. Users of ECLIPPSE are encouraged to document their use of strategies, incorporate rigorous evaluation, and share products, results, and lessons learned. Proctor’s implementation outcome taxonomy,36 which includes measures such as acceptability, fidelity, penetration, and costs, provides a useful evaluation framework.

Future research and evaluation activities should increase understanding of the utility of the strategies in specific contexts. Examples include utility by community experience, capacity, track record, and decision-maker buy-in, differences across urban, suburban, and rural settings, appropriateness for inside and outside + systems, and whether an external support system is necessary for a given strategy compared to a community doing it on its own.

Study strengths include the expert panel methodology and the systematic adaptation of an existing ISF12 and implementation strategy compilation (ERIC).18 Although consistent with guidance for Delphi methods,25,26 the sample size and general approach did not allow assessment of which strategies are most appropriate for specific community contexts, nor which combinations of strategies may be appropriate. The Midwest region had lower representation on the panel. The quantitative survey did not assess why specific strategies were rated low or high on feasibility and impact.

ECLIPPSE provides a compilation from which communities can select strategies to support the implementation of built environment changes that promote physical activity. Future research and evaluation activities are necessary to build the evidence base for these strategies in practice.
