Continuous generation of cross-linked polymer nanoparticles employing an ultrasonic microreactor
Authors: Aniket Pradip Udepurkar, Wim Dermaut, Christian Clasen, Simon Kuhn
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Nanoparticles, Microreactors, Ultrasound, Miniemulsion Polymerization, Tubular Microreactor

Introduction

Polymeric nanoparticles have extensive applications in adhesives, coating, inks, and paints and are gaining interest for applications in encapsulation, drug delivery, and biosensors [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]. Emulsion polymerization is a conventional technique for the synthesis of polymer nanoparticles and is traditionally performed in batch reactors [7], [8], [9]. However, the synthesis of complex macromolecules is challenging and often impossible with emulsion polymerization [10]. Miniemulsion polymerization is an effective technique to realize the synthesis of complex polymers, hybrid nanoparticles, and functional nanoparticles [10], [11]. To enable the synthesis of complex nanoparticles, the attention shifted to miniemulsion polymerization [11], [12].

The common technique employed for miniemulsion polymerization is free radical polymerization [4], [5], [10]. It involves the addition of a water-soluble thermal initiator to the continuous aqueous phase or oil-soluble thermal initiator to the dispersed monomer phase. In case of free radical polymerization, the polymerization can proceed via droplet nucleation or secondary nucleation [4], [5]. Droplet nucleation is favored in free radical polymerization as it can aid in controlling the final size of the latex particles synthesized with miniemulsion polymerization [4], [13]. Recently, oil-soluble photoinitators have also garnered interest for miniemulsion polymerization for the synthesis of latex particles [14], [15], [16]. In addition, controlled radical polymerization (CRP) has also been employed for the synthesis of well controlled (co)polymers using miniemulsion polymerization [12], [17]. CRP enables the synthesis of polymers or copolymers (star polymers, block polymers, brush polymers) with narrow molecular weight distribution and predetermined molecular weight tailored for specific applications [10], [12], [17], [18], [19].

Miniemulsion polymerization has enabled the synthesis of functional nanoparticles or nanocapsules for the encapsulation of drugs, pigments, dyes, or magnetite [2], [10], [11]. Nauman et al. recently utilized membrane emulsification to generate monomer-in-water miniemulsion in various size ranges for nanoparticle synthesis [20]. They reported that using an initiator with the lowest solubility in water resulted in a final nanoparticle size close to the miniemulsion droplet size due to droplet nucleation dominating the polymerization. They were able to synthesize nanoparticles in a wide size range of 300–1200 nm, albeit for a long polymerization time of up to 24 h in a batch system. Usman et al. demonstrated the synthesis of poly(PMMA-EDGMA) magnetic nanospheres by miniemulsion polymerization with a polymerization time of 18 hr [21]. Harun et al. demonstrated the encapsulation of silicon quantum dots in polymer nanoparticles to increase their stability [22]. Marsden et al. synthesized size tunable crosslinked p(MMA) nanoparticles valuable for biomedical applications [23]. Landfester et al. have covered many examples detailing the application of miniemulsion polymerization for encapsulation [10]. However, the time for polymerization to reach full conversion is usually in the order of a few hours. The long reaction time could result from the poor heat transfer in batch reactors [24]. Flow reactors can offer excellent heat transfer, improved reproducibility, and control over the polymerization to achieve desired results in a shorter time [24].

Yadav et al. investigated the miniemulsion polymerization of styrene utilizing a tubular flow reactor. They observed no clogging of the reactor and obtained high conversion [25]. Lobry et al. demonstrated the photopolymerization of a miniemulsion in a glass microreactor with a channel diameter of 1 mm [16]. High conversion of 83 % was obtained for a low solid loading of 5 % and a miniemulsion droplet diameter of 60 nm respectively for a residence time of 10 min. However, higher solid loadings or droplet sizes resulted in low conversion and fouling for similar residence time. Recently, Nauman et al. utilized a continuous tubular reactor for the photopolymerization of a 6 wt.% methyl methacrylate miniemulsion for the generation of nanoparticles in the size range of 200–950 nm [15].

In this work we focus on free radical polymerization for the generation of cross-linked polymer nanoparticles. Miniemulsion polymerization involves two steps for the generation of polymer nanoparticles. In the first step a monomer-in-water miniemulsion (50 nm < d < 500 nm) is generated by ultrasonication, static mixers, glass membranes, or high-pressure homogenization [4], [5], [20], [26]. In the second step, the miniemulsion undergoes subsequent polymerization with the help of a thermal initiator or a photoinitiator [4], [6], [16], [20], [27], [28], [29], [30]. The polymer nanoparticle size is usually governed by the miniemulsion droplet size, which can be advantageous to synthesize functionalized polymer nanoparticles of a desired size [4], [5], [13], [27].

Ultrasound is the favored technique for the generation of monomer-in-water miniemulsions for miniemulsion polymerization [4], [5], [6], [13], [27], [31]. Recently, ultrasonic microreactors gained more attention for the generation of emulsions due to their smaller footprint, better process control, and rapid formulation [32], [33], [34], [35]. These advantages make them an attractive alternative to batch sonication for emulsification. Such ultrasonic microreactors have been successfully utilized for miniemulsion generation [36]. Nieves et al. utilized an ultrasonic microreactor for the generation of a monomer-in-water miniemulsion and subsequent photopolymerization in a vial [36]. A wide particle size distribution was obtained, which was unattractive for further applications.

As mentioned before, continuous generation of nanoparticles can be beneficial for the synthesis of functionalized nanoparticles or microparticles tailored to desired size and properties. Previous studies on miniemulsion polymerization have shown that continuous reactors are attractive for the generation of polymer nanoparticles [16], [25], [31]. However, the main bottleneck was the choice between either high conversion or high solid content. To address this issue, in this work we demonstrate an ultrasonic microreactor coupled with a coiled tubular reactor for the continuous generation of polymer nanoparticles. In addition, we report a high conversion for continuous miniemulsion polymerization for solid content up to 30 wt.% for the generation of nanoparticles in the size range of 50–250 nm.

First, a glass ultrasonic microreactor with a rough microchannel was successfully utilized for the successful generation of monomer-in-water miniemulsion. The continuous phase consisted of Milli-Q water and the surfactant Lutensol AT 50. The dispersed monomer phase consisted of the monomer butyl methacrylate (BMA), the cross-linker ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA), and the costabilizer hexadecane (HD). Hexadecane was added to the monomer to stabilize the monomer droplets during the polymerization step. To obtain the optimal polymerization parameters, the batch miniemulsion polymerization was studied using potassium persulfate (KPS) as a thermal initiator. A continuous miniemulsion polymerization setup, as shown in Fig. 1, was utilized for continuous miniemulsion polymerization. The p(BMA-co-EGDMA) nanoparticles were successfully synthesized employing this continuous setup. In addition, their size was tuned by altering the miniemulsion droplet size.

Experimental section

Materials

Monomer butyl methacrylate (BMA, ≥ 99.0 %), cross-linker ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA, 98 % with 90–110 ppm monomethyl ether hydroquinone as inhibitor), and thermal initiator potassium persulfate (KPS, ≥ 99.0 %) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Hexadecane (HD, 99 %) was purchased from Acros Organics. The surfactant Lutensol AT 50 (BASF) was kindly provided by Agfa. The chemicals were used as received. Milli-Q water was used for all the experiments.

Ultrasonic microreactor

The borosilicate glass ultrasonic microreactor (Little Things Factory GmbH, Germany) consists of three layers. The top and bottom 1 mm thick layers sandwich a 1.2 mm layer in which square serpentine microchannels of cross-section 1.2x1.2 mm2 and length 700 mm were etched with waterjet cutting. The total reactor volume is 1 ml. The side walls of the etched channels have a surface roughness (Ra) of 4 μm. More details on the reactor can be found elsewhere [37]. The glass microreactor was bonded to a piezoelectric plate transducer (PZ 26, Meggitt Ferroperm, 80x40x1.67 mm3) using epoxy glue (EpoTek 301). The admittance of the reactor was measured using an Impedance Analyzer (16777 k, SinePhase) to determine the resonance frequency (47 kHz). The reactor was operated at the resonance frequency for all experiments. The reactor was connected to an amplifier (RF 1040L, 400 W, E&I) coupled with a signal generator (33500B, Keysight) to actuate ultrasound. The reactor was placed on a Peltier Element (RS Components) connected to a DC power supply (Velleman) to regulate the temperature during sonication. Syringe pumps (Fusion 200 and Fusion 4000-X, KR Analytical) were employed to deliver the aqueous and the monomer phases during the emulsification and continuous polymerization stages.

Miniemulsion generation

The continuous aqueous phase of the miniemulsion was prepared by adding the surfactant Lutensol AT 50 (9.02 g) to Milli-Q water (100 g). The mixture was stirred at 40 °C for several hours to ensure complete dissolution of the surfactant in water. The dispersed monomer phase was prepared by mixing the cross-linker EGDMA (2.92 g, 14.7 mmol), the costabilizer HD (2.11 g, 9.3 mmol), and the monomer BMA (50 g, 351.6 mmol) in a glass bottle. The total flow rate of 0.25 ml min−1 amounting to a reactor residence time of 4 min was chosen for miniemulsion generation. The ultrasound was actuated at a frequency of 47 kHz and ultrasound power of 15 W for the generation of monomer-in-water miniemulsion. The reactor outlet temperature was maintained at 30 °C. Monomer-in-water miniemulsion sample was collected after 3 reactor residence time and the droplet size distribution was measured by dynamic light scattering. The droplet size distribution was performed in triplicate for all the conditions.

Batch miniemulsion polymerization

For batch miniemulsion polymerization, a miniemulsion of a monomer volume fraction of 20 % was generated employing the ultrasonic microreactor. The aqueous and monomer phase flow rates were 0.2 ml min−1 and 0.05 ml min−1 respectively. 8 ml of the miniemulsion was collected in a glass bottle (Duran, 25 ml). The miniemulsion polymerization was performed at the temperature of 75 °C and 85 °C. The glass bottle was placed on a heating plate (IKA, 100 rpm) to heat to the miniemulsion to the desired temperature. The glass bottle was capped with a rubber stopper to prevent evaporation. An aqueous initiator solution was prepared by mixing KPS (2.06 g, 7.6 mmol) in Milli-Q water (100 g). Once the miniemulsion reached the desired temperature, the initiator solution (0.496 ml) was added to initiate the polymerization. At regular intervals, a sample (0.3 ml) was drawn to monitor the progress of the polymerization reaction gravimetrically. The sample was weighed to determine its initial mass and the mass of monomers (15.6 wt.%) was determined. The batch polymerization was replicated thrice for the temperatures of 75 °C and 85 °C. On completion of the polymerization step, the cross-linked polymer nanoparticle size was measured by dynamic light scattering.

Continuous miniemulsion polymerization

A helical coiled tubular reactor was utilized for the continuous miniemulsion polymerization step. A PFA tubing (Inner Diameter (ID) = 2 mm, Outer Diameter (OD) = 3 mm, Length = 1.9 m, Bola) was coiled around a 3D printed support (Figure S2). The coiled tubular reactor was placed in a custom-made glass heating bath filled with water. The bath was closed from the top with a PVC lid and the bath temperature was monitored with a thermocouple. The bath was heated to 85 °C while gently stirred (250 rpm). The temperature was considered even across the bath and the tubing was assumed to reach the same temperature as the bath. The tubular reactor volume inside the bath was 5 ml which amounted to a residence time of 20 min.

The ultrasonic microreactor, employed for continuous miniemulsion generation, was connected in series with the tubular reactor. To start up the continuous synthesis, aqueous phase flow was initiated and the bath was allowed to reach 85 °C. After reaching the desired bath temperature, the miniemulsion generation was started by starting the flow of the dispersed phase and ultrasound actuation. The aqueous initiator solution was added to the miniemulsion close to the inlet of the tubular reactor using a T-junction. Samples were collected after 3 tubular reactor residence times (60 min). The polymerization conversion was determined gravimetrically. The miniemulsion droplet size and the nanoparticle size were measured by dynamic light scattering. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

The continuous polymerization was performed for monomer volume fractions of 10 %-36 % keeping a constant surfactant concentration of 8.27 % and for surfactant concentrations of 2.07 %-8.27 % keeping a constant monomer volume fraction of 36 %. The monomer composition was the same as for the batch experiments. The flow rates and the compositions are shown in Table 1.

Droplet and particle size measurement

The miniemulsion and polymer nanoparticles size was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (LS Instruments). 10–20 μl sample was introduced into a cylindrical glass vial filled with Milli-Q water (approximately 3 ml). The droplet or particle size distribution measurement was performed at a scattering angle of 120° and a temperature of 20 °C. The size measurement for a sample was performed for 60 s and replicated 5 times to obtain an average autocorrelation function. CONTIN method was employed to extract the droplet or particle size distribution from the autocorrelation function.

Results and discussion

Batch miniemulsion polymerization

The monomer-in-water miniemulsion was generated for a monomer volume fraction of 20 % employing the ultrasonic microreactor. For the total flow rate of 0.25 ml min−1, residence time of 4 min, ultrasound frequency of 47 kHz, and load power of 15 W, the monomer underwent complete emulsification, and no monomer slugs or large droplets were seen in the microchannel. A sample was collected after 3 reactor residence times and the droplet size was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS). The monomer-in-water droplet size distribution (see Fig. 4) reveals the droplet size below 1000 nm (miniemulsion) with the majority of the droplets with a diameter below 200 nm. The ultrasound frequency and power of 47 kHz and 15 W respectively were sufficient for miniemulsion generation and thus were chosen for future experiments.

Miniemulsion droplet size distribution (--) and polymer nanoparticle size distribution (–) were synthesized by continuous miniemulsion polymerization for the monomer volume fraction of 10% (a), 20% (b), and 36% (c).

The water-soluble thermal initiator, potassium persulfate (KPS), was utilized for the initiation of polymerization in batch miniemulsion polymerization. Previous studies have shown that water-soluble initiators are better suited for miniemulsion polymerization compared to oil-soluble initiators due to their faster polymerization rates [4], [5]. The polymerization was performed at temperatures of 75 °C and 85 °C to observe the influence of temperature on the conversion, time to reach full conversion, and final nanoparticle size.

The polymerization conversion with time, which was obtained by gravimetric analysis, is shown in Fig. 2. The polymerization reaction was close to completion at 10 min for a temperature of 75 °C. At 85 °C, this time was halved to 5 min. An increase in temperature facilitated the increase in the rate of radical generation for the thermal initiator KPS. The higher radical generation rate in turn facilitated an increase in the rate of polymerization, and thus decreased the time required to achieve full conversion. It is important to note the conversion of 7.7 % at 0 min (no initiator added to the miniemulsion). Previous studies on the utilization of ultrasound for miniemulsion polymerization have shown that radicals generated due to transient cavitation aid the polymerization of nanometer-sized monomer droplets [19], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]. The low conversion at 0 min can be explained by polymerization due to radicals generated in the microreactor in the absence of an initiator. In addition, conversion higher than 100 % was obtained for some samples, which could have resulted from moisture or hexadecane trapped in the crosslinked nanoparticles. The sample vials were heated on a heating plate (75 °C) to evaporate the trapped moisture or hexadecane. The sample weight was unchanged even after heating the vials overnight, which potentially points to the trapped moisture or hexadecane in the cross-linked polymer. Thus, conversion greater than 100 % can be considered as completion of the polymerization (>99 % conversion).

Polymerization conversion with time for the monomer-in-water miniemulsion (batch experiments).

The particle size of the polymer nanoparticles (p(BMA-co-EGDMA)) was measured after the completion of the polymerization step. Fig. 3 shows that the particle size distribution is similar to the monomer droplet size distribution. The difference between the polymer nanoparticles polymerized at 75 °C and 85 °C is insignificant. In the event of polymerization by droplet nucleation, the final particle size would be similar to the monomer droplets [5], [20]. The batch polymerization at 75 °C and 85 °C likely proceeded primarily via droplet nucleation and thus resulted in the final particle size distribution resembling the droplet size distribution. This would also explain why temperature played no influential role in the final nanoparticle size and only affected the polymerization time. The rapid generation of initiator radicals coupled with small miniemulsion droplet size has been shown to promote droplet nucleation during miniemulsion polymerization [4], [5]. Likewise, the small droplet size (<300 nm) and low time to reach full conversion (≤10 min) for the polymerization system demonstrated in this work follow a similar trend of droplet nucleation as the driving mechanism for polymerization of the miniemulsion droplets. The free radical polymerization mechanism and the schematic representation of the polymer structure are discussed in the SI.

Droplet size distribution of the miniemulsion (--) and particle size distribution of the p(BMA-co-EGDMA) nanoparticles (NP) synthesized by batch polymerization at 75 °C (–) and 85 °C (–‧–).

The batch polymerization experiments have exhibited that the water-soluble initiator KPS is an effective initiator for achieving polymerization with high conversion in a short time. In addition, the dominating droplet nucleation mechanism for the miniemulsion polymerization of the BMA-EGDMA-HD system with KPS could be utilized in controlling the final nanoparticle size by tuning the miniemulsion droplet size. In the following section, a continuous synthesis of p(BMA-co-EGDMA) polymer nanoparticle synthesis was attempted taking advantage of the short polymerization time and high conversion. A temperature of 85 °C was selected for the miniemulsion polymerization step in the continuous synthesis.

Continuous miniemulsion polymerization

The continuous polymerization step of the miniemulsion polymerization was performed in a coiled perfluoroalkoxy (PFA) tubular reactor. Previous studies have shown that PFA or polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) tubing is better suited compared to stainless steel for continuous polymerization [14], [43]. The coiled tubular reactor, wrapped around a 3D printed support, was placed in a rectangular glass bath filled with water to heat the coiled reactor to the desired temperature of 85 °C. The residence time in the tubular reactor was 20 min at a flow rate of 0.25 ml min−1. At the low flow rate of 0.25 ml min−1, the flow regime in the tubular reactor is laminar. The laminar flow profile in the tubular reactor would result in a wide residence time distribution. To ensure the completion of the polymerization reaction in the tubular reactor, a residence time of 20 min (4x polymerization time at 85 °C) was chosen. The tubular reactor was connected in series with the ultrasonic microreactor, as seen in Fig. 1. The miniemulsion was generated in the microreactor. Subsequently, the aqueous initiator solution was added to the miniemulsion with a T-junction placed before the tubular reactor.

Influence of monomer volume fraction

The influence of monomer volume fraction on the polymerization conversion and nanoparticle size was investigated. Monomer volume fractions of 10 %, 20 %, and 36 % were obtained by changing the monomer and aqueous phase flow rate ratio for a total flow rate of 0.25 ml min−1 (see Table 1). As seen in Fig. 4, the miniemulsion droplet size increased for higher monomer volume fraction. With an increase in the monomer volume fraction, a higher amount of surfactant would be needed to cover the droplet surface and stabilize the same droplet size to account for an increase in the surface area. The surfactant concentration in the aqueous phase was, however, unchanged for the continuous miniemulsion generation, which would mean a lower number of surfactant molecules per unit volume at a higher volume fraction. Thus, the decrease in the surfactant concentration resulted in an increase in the emulsion droplet size. This trend in agreement with previous results where increase in the nanoparticle size was reported for an decrease in surfactant concentration [41].

For all the monomer volume fractions, the final nanoparticle size distribution was similar to the miniemulsion droplet size distribution. It is likely that droplet nucleation was also the driving mechanism for miniemulsion polymerization in the tubular coiled reactor. For the final nanoparticle size, represented in Fig. 4, an increase in the particle size was observed with an increase in the monomer volume fraction. The polymerization reaction achieved full conversion in the coiled loop reactor for a residence time of 20 min in the reactor.

Influence of surfactant concentration

Next, the influence of the surfactant, Lutensol AT 50, concentration in the aqueous phase of the miniemulsion on the miniemulsion droplet size and the final nanoparticle size was also investigated. The miniemulsion droplet size, shown in Fig. 5, increased with the decrease in the surfactant concentration. As mentioned before, the decrease in the number of surfactant molecules per unit volume of the dispersed phase would increase the droplet size. The nanoparticle size distribution was similar to the miniemulsion droplet size for all the surfactant concentrations. Thus, droplet nucleation was the dominating mechanism during the continuous miniemulsion polymerization. The polymerization reaction was close to completion for the residence time of 20 min for all the cases.

Miniemulsion droplet size distribution (--) and polymer nanoparticle size distribution (–) synthesized by continuous miniemulsion polymerization for Lutensol AT 50 concentrations in the aqueous phase of 8.27 wt% (a), 4.14 wt% (b), and 2.07 wt% (c).

Continuous nanoparticle synthesis

The comparison of the miniemulsion droplet size distribution and nanoparticle size distribution revealed that miniemulsion polymerization proceeded predominantly via droplet nucleation for the continuous polymerization system demonstrated above. By virtue of droplet nucleation, the final nanoparticle size distribution was similar to the miniemulsion droplet size distribution. This enabled tuning the nanoparticle to the desired size by tuning the miniemulsion droplet size. As shown in Fig. 6, nanoparticles of various size ranges could be obtained by changing the process parameters, viz. surfactant concentration and monomer volume fraction in this case. The cross-linked p(BMA-co-EGDMA) were further analyzed with SEM. As seen from Fig. 7, the cross-linked polymer nanoparticles are spherical and have a narrow size distribution. In addition, the current system achieved a miniemulsion polymerization for a solid content of up to 30 wt% without any issues with clogging while maintaining a high conversion.

Conclusion

A microfluidic approach to the continuous generation of cross-linked polymer nanoparticles is presented in the current work. The ultrasonic microreactor reported here is effective for miniemulsion generation. The batch polymerization step for the monomer-in-water miniemulsion was first studied with KPS as the thermal initiator. The polymerization proceeded rapidly to completion in 5 min at a temperature of 85 °C.

The continuous polymerization step was carried out in a tubular coiled reactor connected in series with the ultrasonic microreactor and placed in a water bath of 85 °C. The residence time was 20 min in the tubular reactor. The influence of monomer volume fraction and surfactant concentration on the monomer miniemulsion droplet size and the final polymer nanoparticle size was investigated. The miniemulsion droplet and nanoparticle size increased with an increase in the monomer volume fraction and a decrease in the surfactant concentration. The comparison of the miniemulsion droplet size distribution and the nanoparticle size distribution revealed that droplet nucleation was the dominating mechanism in the polymerization step. A high polymerization conversion was achieved without any issues such as fouling or clogging of the tubular reactor.

Overall, the continuous synthesis of cross-linked p(BMA-co-EGDMA) nanoparticles was achieved by performing the continuous miniemulsion generation and continuous polymerization step in series. The fast polymerization step and droplet nucleation demonstrated in this work can help in the generation of nanoparticles or microparticles of desired sizes by manipulating the process parameters to alter the droplet dimensions. A full conversion of the polymerization reaction could be reached in a few minutes, as opposed to a few hours in the previous reports. This was achieved while not sacrificing the conversion or the solid loading in the continuous tubular reactor. In addition, the ultrasonic microreactor and coiled reactor system, owing to the simple setup and adaptability, can be utilized for the generation of functionalized nanoparticles or microparticles tailored to specific applications in the future.
