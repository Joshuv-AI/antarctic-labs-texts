Long lasting complement neutralisation by RAY121, an engineered anti-C1s antibody with C1q displacement function
Authors: Adrian W.S. ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAntibody drugs blocking the complement classical pathway (CP) often require frequent intravenous administration to overcome the high abundance or rapid turnover of complement proteins. This makes treatment compliance burdensome for patients.MethodsScreening was performed to identify anti-C1s antibodies specific for the CUB domain of C1s with CP neutralising function. Antibody engineering was performed on the anti-C1s antibody to prolong its half-life in circulation. The variable region was mutated to confer pH-dependent binding to C1s, and the antibody Fc was modified to lower its isoelectric point and to have enhanced binding to the neonatal Fc receptor.FindingsA combination of pH-dependent binding to C1s and optimisation of charge characteristics was required for the final engineered antibody, RAY121, to achieve a long half-life in circulation and long-lasting neutralisation of the CP. In male cynomolgus monkeys, a single subcutaneous injection suppressed CP activity for more than a month. RAY121 neutralises the CP by using a unique mechanism of displacing C1q from the C1 complex and blocking its reassembly. Structure analysis by cryo-electron microscopy revealed that the RAY121 epitope on C1s is distinct from the C1q binding site, and that C1q displacement is mediated by the Fab arm sterically obstructing C1q binding to C1s.InterpretationRAY121 is able to neutralise the CP for an extended duration and is effective at doses that can be administered subcutaneously for convenient treatment. These data present RAY121 as a promising agent for the treatment of CP-driven autoimmune disorders.Funding10.13039/100010795Chugai Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Aberrant activation of the complement classical pathway (CP) is associated with several autoimmune diseases. Sutimlimab is an anti-C1s antibody drug approved for treatment of patients with cold agglutinin disease, an autoimmune haemolytic anaemia with CP-driven destruction of red blood cells. Due to high plasma C1s concentration and its rapid turnover, large and frequent intravenous doses of drug are required to sustain CP inhibition. Frequent visits to medical facilities for intravenous drug administration can be burdensome to patients being treated for such chronic autoimmune diseases.

Added value of this study

This report describes the preclinical development of RAY121, an engineered anti-C1s antibody. RAY121 is able to neutralise CP for an extended duration and can be administered subcutaneously for greater patient convenience. We detail how RAY121 was engineered and optimised to achieve a long half-life in cynomolgus monkeys. In addition, RAY121 has a unique mode of action of displacing C1q from the C1 complex to inhibit complement activity. Using cryo-EM, RAY121's unique epitope on C1s was mapped to shed light on its C1q displacement mechanism.

Implications of all the available evidence

RAY121 is presently under phase 1b open-label basket trial to inhibit CP in immunological diseases (trial identifier NCT06371417). These data provide evidence to support RAY121 as a promising antibody candidate for effective and convenient treatment of CP-driven diseases. Apart from its therapeutic potential, RAY121 may also be a useful tool to shed light on how C1q interacts with C1s, enabling a better understanding of how the C1 complex functions.

Introduction

The complement system comprises about 30 complement proteins which function as a cascading series of enzymes. It can be divided into three distinct pathways: the classical pathway (CP), the lectin pathway (LP), and the alternative pathway (AP). The CP cascade is activated when hexameric C1q binds to the Fc region of immobilised antibodies on a target surface. In solution phase, C1q interacts weakly with the Fc portion of IgGs. However, when IgGs are bound at high density on a target surface they can assemble into hexameric structures,1 permitting avid interactions between the six C1q globular heads and hexamerised Fcs. This tight binding of C1q to Fc is thought to trigger zymogen C1r within the C1 complex to undergo conformational change, leading to C1r autoactivation and cleavage of C1s proximal to it. The activated C1s then cleaves complement components C2 and C4 into C2a/C2b, and C4a/C4b fragments respectively. This leads to the assembly of C4b2a, a C3 convertase, on the target surface. The C3 convertase then cleaves C3 to form C3b which in turn cleaves C5 to initiate the formation of the terminal membrane attack complex, C5b-9. In addition, the deposition of C4b and C3b on the target surface also function as eat-me-signals which are recognised by complement receptors on phagocytic cells.2

Apart from its role in IgG-mediated host defence, the CP is also a key player in maintaining homoeostasis by mediating the clearance of apoptotic cells3 and inducing immunological tolerance.4 When the CP is aberrantly activated, it is associated with several complement-driven disorders such as autoimmune haemolytic anaemias, Behcet's disease, bullous pemphigoid, dermatomyositis, immune thrombocytopaenia, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, antiphospholipid syndrome etc. Inhibition of excessive or uncontrolled activation of the CP can provide clinical benefit to these patients. As C1s acts near the top of the CP cascade, anti-C1s antibodies have several advantages over complement inhibitors that act on downstream targets. Firstly, it leaves the LP and AP intact, preserving complement-mediated immunity against pathogens. As C3 and C5 inhibitors suppress all 3 pathways of complement, the FDA requires these drugs to carry a black box warning noting the risk of meningococcal infections. Secondly, anti-C1s antibodies are able to overcome bypass pathways in the complement cascade such as the C2 bypass pathway where Factor B can substitute for C4 to form a hybrid C3 convertase,5 and the C3 bypass pathway where C4b can bind and activate C5.6 Thirdly, by inhibiting the early-complement-pathway, anti-C1s has the potential to address fatigue, which is a common but inadequately treated symptom in complement-driven diseases.7,8

Sutimlimab, an anti-C1s antibody, is an approved drug for treatment of patients with cold agglutinin disease (CAD).9,10 Apart from achieving the primary efficacy endpoint of more than 1.5 g/dL increase of haemoglobin and no blood transfusion required, it also reduced patient's fatigue levels and positively impacted their quality of life.11 Sutimlimab neutralises C1s enzyme function by binding the substrate recognition domain of C1s, preventing activation of C4.12 Although sutimlimab is highly effective in neutralising the CP, an extremely large dose of 7500 mg is required once every 14 days for sustained CP inhibition.13 Similarly, the anti-C1q therapeutic antibody ANX005, which is currently in Phase III, requires extremely high drug amounts. In cynomolgus monkeys, C1q-mediated haemolysis could only be inhibited for approximately 2 weeks when ANX005 was administered at 100 mg/kg.14 Since many autoimmune diseases require long term treatment, frequent visits to medical facilities for intravenous drug administration can be burdensome to patients.

With these issues in mind, we set out to develop an anti-C1s drug that can neutralise the CP for a longer duration and at a dose that can be administered subcutaneously for greater patient convenience. We chose to focus on C1s rather than C1r as sutimlimab's approval validated C1s as a target with good safety profile. A key factor to achieve this aim was to minimise the target-mediated drug disposition (TMDD) of the antibody. C1q has been reported to promiscuously bind a wide range of molecules, including antigens on apoptotic cells such as calreticulin,15 phosphatidylserine16 and nucleoli antigens.17 As large numbers of apoptotic cells are constantly being cleared under homoeostasis, antibodies that bind the C1 complex are expected to show accelerated clearance. In normal rats, radiolabelled 125I–C1q was cleared rapidly with a plasma half-life of 12.4 h.18 Consistent with this report, the half-life of IgG4-based anti-C1q ANX005 antibody was estimated to be 18 h in cynomolgus monkeys when injected at a low dose of 10 mg/kg.14 To minimise TMDD arising from C1q-mediated clearance, we developed a screening strategy to pick out C1s antibodies that can neutralise the CP by displacing C1q from the C1 complex.

Antibody-mediated displacement of another protein bound to the antibody's target have been reported previously. For example, anti-SIRPα antibodies with “kick-off” function were reported to displace CD47 from SIRPα,19 and anti-PCSK9 antibodies with minimally overlapping epitopes were also described to be able to displace one another.20 By selecting for binders specific to C1s CUB1-EGF-CUB2 domains that interact with C1q, we successfully identified antibodies that were able to displace C1q from C1s and neutralise the CP. TMDD was further reduced by engineering the antibody to have strong pH-dependent binding to C1s and increased affinity for the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). This enabled the antibody to rapidly release C1s in acidic endosomes after uptake into cells and recycle back to the blood via FcRn. Finally, we also engineered the charge characteristics of the antibody to improve its pharmacokinetic profile. The final optimised antibody, RAY121 was able to inhibit the CP for more than a month after a single subcutaneous dose in cynomolgus monkeys, significantly longer than a conventional anti-C1s antibody. To uncover the C1q displacement mechanism of RAY121, we mapped its epitope by cryo-electron microscopy. Interestingly, RAY121's epitope does not overlap with the C1q binding site on C1s but instead utilises the Fab to sterically obstruct the interaction between C1s and C1q. In this report, we present the discovery and detailed characterisation of RAY121.

Methods

Expression of recombinant complement proteins and antibodies

The following recombinant proteins were expressed using FreeStyle293-F cell line (Thermo Fisher, RRID:CVCL_D603): full-length human C1s (M1-D688) with 8 × Histidine tag, C-terminal human C1s, CCP1-CCP2-SP domain (M292-D688) with 8 × Histidine tag, and full-length cynomolgus C1s (M1-D688) with Flag tag. All tags were preceded by a GGGGS linker. His-tagged proteins were purified by HisTrap excel column (Cytiva) and eluted with imidazole. Flag-tagged proteins were purified by anti-Flag M2 affinity resin (Sigma) and eluted with Flag peptide (Sigma). Fractions containing protein of interest were pooled and applied to a Superdex 200 gel filtration column (Cytiva), pooled, concentrated, and stored at −80 °C.

Recombinant C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer was obtained by co-transfection of mutant C1r with C-terminus Flag tag and wildtype C1s with C-terminus 8 × Histidine tag into Freestyle 293-F cells (Thermo Fisher, RRID:CVCL_D603). Mutant C1r contained the mutations R463Q and S654A to prevent auto-activation and to silence C1r enzymatic activity respectively.21 Conditioned media expressing recombinant C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer was applied to anti-Flag M2 affinity resin (Sigma) and eluted with Flag peptide (Sigma). Fractions containing recombinant C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer were subjected to an IMAC column (Cytiva) and eluted with imidazole gradient. Eluted fractions containing recombinant C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer were collected, concentrated and subsequently subjected to a Superdex 200 gel filtration column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 1 × TBS, 2 mM CaCl2 buffer. Fractions containing recombinant C1r2s2 Flag/His were then pooled, concentrated, and stored at −80 °C. After purification, reducing Western blot was performed to ensure majority of the recombinant C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer was in proenzyme form.

Tag-free recombinant human and cynomolgus C1r2s2 tetramers were obtained by co-transfection of mutant C1r R463Q S654A and wildtype C1s plasmids in Freestyle 293-F cells (Thermo Fisher, RRID:CVCL_D603). Conditioned media containing the tetramers were applied to HiTrap Q HP anion exchange chromatography column (Cytiva) and eluted with NaCl gradient in 50 mM Tris–HCl, 1 M NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2 pH 8.0 buffer. Eluted fractions containing the recombinant C1r2s2 tetramers were subjected to a Superdex 200 gel filtration column (Cytiva) using similar conditions as the tagged tetramer above. Fractions containing the recombinant tag-free C1r2s2 tetramers were then pooled, concentrated, and stored at −80 °C.

Recombinant human C1s-CUB-G4S-FLAG (C1s-CUB) for cryo-EM was expressed transiently using Expi 293F cells (Thermo Fisher, RRID:CVCL_D615). The sequences encoding human C1s-CUB (M1-M292) was cloned in a mammalian expression vector with C-terminus GGGGS linker and Flag-tag. The culture supernatant was applied to a column packed with anti-Flag M2 affinity resin (Sigma) and eluted with Flag peptide (Sigma) in 1 × TBS, 2 mM CaCl2 buffer. Fractions containing the protein were pooled and applied to a Superdex 200 gel filtration column (Cytiva), pooled, concentrated, and stored at −80 °C.

Recombinant antibodies were expressed transiently using the Expi 293-F cells (Thermo Fisher, RRID:CVCL_D603) and Expifectamine 293 (Thermo Fisher), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Recombinant antibodies were purified with protein A (Cytiva) and eluted in PBS, TBS or Histidine buffer (20 mM Histidine, 150 mM NaCl, pH 6.0). Size exclusion chromatography was further conducted to remove high molecular weight and/or low molecular weight components, if necessary. The polynucleotides of the heavy and light chain variable regions of the anti-C1s antibody, IPN009VH2 and IPN009VK3 were obtained from patent WO2016164358. The heavy and light chain variable regions were cloned into expression vectors containing an IgG4 heavy chain constant region and a kappa light chain constant region respectively.

Animal immunisation and antibody generation

New Zealand White rabbits (Invivos, Singapore) were immunised intradermally with native human C1s proenzyme (CompTech, A103) or in-house generated recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer. Four or five repeated doses were given over a two-month period followed by blood and spleen collection. For B cell selection, recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer, biotinylated native human C1s proenzyme, biotinylated recombinant human C1s-His, biotinylated recombinant human C1s CCP1-CCP2-SP-His and recombinant cyno C1s-Flag were prepared and used. B cells which could bind to native human C1s proenzyme, recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer, recombinant human C1s-His or recombinant cyno C1s-Flag were stained and sorted using a cell sorter (FACSAria III, BD Biosciences) and then plated and co-cultured for 7–12 days in 96-well plates at one cell/well density together with 25,000 cells/well of EL4 cells (European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, RRID:CVCL_0255) in diluted (20-fold) activated rabbit T-cell conditioned medium. EL4 cells were pre-treated with mitomycin C (M4287, Sigma) for 2 h and washed three times before culture. Activated rabbit T-cell conditioned medium was prepared by culturing rabbit thymocytes in RPMI-1640 containing Phytohaemagglutinin-M (Roche, 11082132001), phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Sigma P1585) and 2% FBS (Phoenix Scientific). After cultivation, the B cell culture supernatants were collected for further analysis and the B cell pellets were cryopreserved.

Antibody screening

Recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer and recombinant cyno C1s-Flag binding were evaluated by ELISA using the B cell culture supernatants. B cells which bound to recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer and recombinant cyno C1s-Flag were selected for epitope analysis. Epitope characterisation was conducted by ELISA. Recombinant human C1s CCP1-CCP2-SP-His was used for this characterisation. B cell lines were categorised into C1s CUB1-EGF-CUB2 binders or C1s CCP1-CCP2-SP binders. The neutralising activity for C1s CUB1-EGF-CUB2 binders was screened using the RBC lysis assay described below. B cells with good neutralising activity were selected for gene cloning.

SPR assay

The binding specificities of the six C1s CUB1-EGF-CUB2 binders were determined at 37 °C using BIACORE® T200 instrument (Cytiva). Recombinant Protein A/G (Pierce) was immobilised onto all flow cells of a CM4 sensor chip using an amine coupling kit (Cytiva). Antibodies and analytes were prepared in pH 7.4 buffer containing 20 mM ACES, 150 mM NaCl, 1.2 mM CaCl2, 0.05% Tween 20, 0.005% NaN3. Each antibody was captured onto the sensor surface by protein A/G. Antibody capture levels were aimed at 100 resonance units (RU). Native proenzyme human C1s (Comptech, A103) (concentration calculated as 50 nM monomer) or recombinant human C1s CCP1-CCP2-SP-His (concentration calculated as 100 nM monomer) was injected, followed by dissociation. Sensor surface was regenerated every cycle with 10 mM Glycine-HCl pH 1.5.

The C1q displacement function of the antibodies was demonstrated by a C1r2s2 capture method using BIACORE® T200 instrument (Cytiva) at 37 °C. An anti-His antibody (Cytiva 27-4710-01, RRID:AB_771435) was immobilised onto all flow cells of a CM4 sensor chip using an amine coupling kit (Cytiva). The antibodies, recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer and native human C1q (Comptech, A099) were prepared in pH 7.4 buffer (20 mM ACES, 150 mM NaCl, 1.2 mM CaCl2, 1 mg/mL BSA (IgG-free), 1 mg/mL carboxymethyldextran (CMD), 0.05% Tween 20, 0.005% NaN3, pH7.4). Recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer was first captured onto the sensor surface by the anti-His antibody. The capture levels were aimed at 200 RU. Native human C1q was injected at 100 nM to have a capture of 200 RU, followed by antibody injection at 500 nM at 10 μL/min for 1200 s immediately. To avoid differences in sensors, the binding data for each run was recorded using the same flow channel and not by parallel channels. The sensor surface was regenerated every cycle with 10 mM Glycine-HCl pH 1.5.

To assess the blocking of C1q binding to C1r2s2 by the antibodies, the assay was performed at 37 °C using BIACORE® T200 instrument (Cytiva). An anti-His antibody (Cytiva 27-4710-01, RRID:AB_771435) was immobilised onto all flow cells of a CM4 sensor chip using an amine coupling kit (Cytiva). The antibodies, recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer and native human C1q (Comptech, A099) were prepared in pH 7.4 buffer (20 mM ACES, 150 mM NaCl, 1.2 mM CaCl2, 1 mg/mL BSA (IgG-free), 1 mg/mL CMD, 0.05% Tween 20, 0.005% NaN3). Recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer was first captured onto the sensor surface by the anti-His antibody. The capture levels were aimed at 200 RU. The antibody variants were injected at 500 nM, followed by native human C1q injection at 100 nM. The sensor surface was regenerated every cycle with 10 mM Glycine-HCl pH 1.5.

The binding properties of antibodies at pH 7.4 and pH 5.8 in Fig. 4b and Table 2 were determined 37 °C using BIACORE® T200 instrument (Cytiva). Purified mouse anti-human Ig kappa light chain (Cytiva) was immobilised onto all flow cells of a CM5 sensor chip using an amine coupling kit (Cytiva). 20 mM ACES, 150 mM NaCl, 1.2 mM CaCl2, 1 mg/mL BSA (IgG-free), 1 mg/mL CMD, 0.05% Tween 20, 0.005% NaN3, pH 7.4 or pH 5.8 buffer was used as running buffer. Each antibody was captured onto the sensor surface by anti-human Ig kappa light chain (BD Biosciences, RRID: AB_396124). Antibody capture levels were aimed at 50 RU. To determine the KD value at pH 7.4, human C1r2s2 was diluted with buffer and injected at 0, 25, 40, 100, 200, 400 nM or 0, 12.5, 25, 40, 100, 200 nM or 0, 6.3, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 nM at 30 μL/min. To determine the KD value at pH 5.8, human C1r2s2 was diluted with buffer and injected at, 0, 200, 400, 800, 1600, 3200 nM or 0, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800 nM at 30 μL/min. Sensor surface was regenerated every cycle with Glycine pH 2.0 (Cytiva).

The binding properties of antibodies at pH 7.4 and pH 6.0 in Table 4 were determined at 37 °C using BIACORE® T200 instrument (Cytiva). First, anti-human C1r2s2 antibody IPN009VH2VK3 was immobilised onto all of the flow cells of a CM5 sensor chip using Amine Coupling Kit, type 2 (Cytiva). 20 mM ACES, 150 mM NaCl, 1.2 mM CaCl2, 1 mg/mL BSA (IgG-free), 1 mg/mL CMD, 0.05% Tween 20, 0.005 w/v % NaN3, pH 7.4 or pH 6.0 buffer was used as the running buffer. Next, tag free recombinant human C1r2s2 tetramer (hC1r2s2) and recombinant cynomolgus C1r2s2 tetramer (cyC1r2s2), prepared to have a binding response of 100 RU, were captured on the sensor surface by IPN009VH2VK3. The antibody solution was added thereto at 30 μL/min for 120 s, the running buffer was then added at 30 μL/min for 180 s, and the dissociation constant (KD) of each antibody for hC1r2s2 and cyC1r2s2 was calculated. In order to calculate the KD value at pH 7.4, antibody solutions were diluted with pH 7.4 running buffer and injected at 0, 0.8, 1.6, 3.1, 6.3, 13 nM. To calculate the KD value at pH 6.0, antibody solutions were diluted with pH 6.0 running buffer and injected at 0, 6.3, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 nM. The sensor surface was regenerated every cycle using 3 M MgCl2. The KD value was calculated using BIACORE® T200 evaluation software version 2.0 (Cytiva).

Capillary isoelectric focusing (cIEF)

The pI of antibodies in Table 2 was determined using Maurice cIEF application with Maurice cIEF cartridge (Protein Simple). The anolyte and catholyte solutions were from iCE Electrolyte Kit (Protein Simple, 102506). Antibodies were diluted to 0.25 mg/mL with deionised water. 20 μL of antibody solution was mixed with 80 μL of measurement solution. The measurement solution included 35 μL of 1% methyl cellulose solution, 3 μL of 200 mM IDA (iminodiacetic acid), 2 μL of 500 mM arginine, 0.5 μL of pI marker 5.85 and 10.17, and 2 μL of pharmalyte 8–10.5 and 5–8. Mixed samples were vortexed and centrifuged prior to loading into the autosampler compartment, which was kept at 15 °C. Samples were separated with focusing at 1.5 kV for 1 min and at 3.0 kV for 7 min. The pI value of each sample was detected in fluorescence mode.

The pI of antibodies in Tables 3 and 4 was determined by cIEF using iCE3 whole-capillary imaging system using fluorocarbon-coated capillary cartridges (Protein Simple). The anolyte and catholyte solutions were 0.08 M phosphoric acid in 0.1% m/v methyl cellulose (MC) and 0.1 M sodium hydroxide in 0.1% m/v MC, respectively. All analysed samples contain a working concentration of 0.2 mg/mL antibody, 0.35% m/v MC, 6 mM IDA, 10 mM arginine, 0.5 w/v % pI marker (5.85) and 0.5 w/v % pI marker (9.99), and 2 vol % pharmalyte 8–10.5, and 2 vol % pharmalyte 5–8. All samples were vortexed and centrifuged briefly prior to loading into the autosampler compartment. Samples were incubated in the autosampler for 2 h before starting the measurement. Focusing conditions was 1.5 kV for 1 min followed by 3.0 kV for 7 min. The autosampler compartment was kept at 10 °C. Measurements were repeated two times for each sample, and pI values of samples were obtained by calculating the average of the two measurements.

Cryo-EM structure determination

To determine the Cryo-EM structure of C1s-CUB-G4S-FLAG (C1s-CUB) in complex with RAY121 Fab, the Fab fragment of RAY121 was obtained by limited digestion with the endoproteinase Lys-C (Roche), followed by loading onto a protein A column (MabSelect SuRe, Cytiva) to remove Fc fragment, followed by a cation exchange column (HiTrap SP HP, Cytiva), and finally a gel filtration column (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75 pg, Cytiva). C1s-CUB and RAY121 Fab complexes were prepared by mixing at a molar ratio of 1: 1.2 (C1s-CUB: RAY121 Fab) at a total concentration of 5.0 mg/mL in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM CaCl2, 0.03% DDM. 3 μL of the sample solution was applied to a glow discharged Quantifoil R 0.6/1 on Cu 300 mesh grid. The grid was blotted for 2 s with a blot force of 2 at 4 °C, 100% humidity, and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher).

Cryo-EM data was collected on a Krios G4 microscope (Thermo Fisher) installed at RIKEN Yokohama Campus operating at 300 kV and equipped with a K3 BioQuantum (Gatan) with a pixel size of 0.83 Å. Movies were collected with a total dose of 60e−/Å2 and 48 frames per micrograph with a defocus ranging from −1.5 to −2.0 μm. The detailed condition that was used for obtaining the cryo-EM data are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Image stacks were subjected to beam-induced motion correction using the patch motion correction, and the contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were determined using patch CTF estimation. Data processing was performed using cryoSPARC v4.5.3.22 8272 movies were used for data processing. Particles were initially selected using a blob picker. Particles were selected by two-dimensional (2D) classification and selected particles were used to reconstruct the three-dimensional (3D) volume. Reconstructed 3D volume was used for template picker and selected particles were used for 2D classification and ab-initio reconstruction again. Heterogeneous refinement and non-uniform refinement were followed by reference-based motion correction. Subsequently, a second round of heterogeneous refinement and non-uniform refinement was performed. C2 symmetry was applied using the volume alignment tool to the 3D volume obtained from non-uniform refinement. A mask covering Fv domain of RAY121 Fab fragment and CUB1 and EGF domain of C1s was generated using UCSF ChimeraX built-in SegMap tool23 and local refinement with focused mask produced a map with a resolution of 2.62 Å. Initial model of the Fv domain of RAY121 was built using ModelAngelo (RELION 5.0-beta-1).24 Crystal structure of CUB1-EGF-CUB2 (PDB ID: 4LMF)25 was also used for initial model. All models were built and refined by iterating between manual rebuilding and refinement in Coot,26 and automated global real-space refinement in Phenix.27 Model geometry and map–model validations were evaluated by phenix Molprobity28 and are given in Supplementary Table S2. The structure of the complex has been deposited in the RCSB Protein Data Bank with PDB code 9X1H and Electron Microscopy Data Bank with EMDB code EMD-66460. All graphical representations were prepared with PyMOL version 2.5.7 (Schrödinger, LLC) and UCSF ChimeraX version 1.6.1.

RBC lysis assay for antibody screening

For antibody screening, human serum (Biopredic) was diluted to 8% with assay buffer comprising HBSS with calcium and magnesium (Gibco) and 0.05% BSA (Sigma). Antibodies were diluted to 40 μg/mL in assay buffer and incubated with equal volume of 8% serum for 30 min, 1 h or 3 h at 37 °C. As a control, human serum was pre-incubated with assay buffer alone, or assay buffer with 10 mM EDTA (Gibco). Sheep red blood cells (Innovative Research) were sensitised with anti-sheep red blood cell stroma antibody at 10 μg/mL (Abcam, ab50674, RRID:AB_881646), rinsed, counted and adjusted to 5 × 108 cells/mL in assay buffer. The antibody/serum mixture was then added to an equal volume of sensitised sheep red blood cells and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C to allow for lysis of the red blood cells. The final concentration of human serum and antibodies in this reaction was 2% and 10 μg/mL respectively. The reaction was stopped with cold assay buffer containing EDTA. The mixture was centrifuged to pellet unlysed cells and the supernatant was withdrawn. Absorbance (OD) at 415 nm was read (Multiskan, ThermoFisher), from which OD at 630 nm was subtracted to analyse the release of haemoglobin. To calculate the percentage inhibition of red blood cell lysis, 0% inhibition was set as the condition where no antibody (buffer only) was added, and 100% inhibition was set as the condition where EDTA was added at a final concentration of 5 mM.

RBC lysis assay of healthy donor and CAD patient plasma

RBC lysis was performed using the components in a CH50 kit (Denka Seiken, 400017). 45 μL of 20% human plasma (Bay Biosciences) and 45 μL of RAY121 diluted solutions were mixed on a 96-well plate. The plate was sealed and incubated in a shaking incubator at 550 rpm, at 37 °C for 190 min. 60 μL of this mixture was mixed with 60 μL of 3.0 × 108 cells/mL sensitised sheep RBC and incubated for 9 min at 37 °C. 40 μL of ice-cold 40 mM EDTA (0.5 M EDTA diluted with HBSS buffer) were mixed with each well of the 96-well plate to stop the reaction. The 96-well plate was then centrifuged at 4 °C at 1000 × g for 2 min. 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a flat bottom 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 415 nm by a microplate reader (Spectramax, Molecular Devices). Relative haemolytic activity was defined as the percentage of absorbance value at i μg/mL relative to the absorbance value at 0 μg/mL RAY121.

Identification of CUB1-EGF-CUB2 binders with C1q displacement function

The C1s protein is a large multi-domain and multi-functional molecule. The domain structure and function are schematically depicted in Fig. 1a. The N-terminal portion comprises two CUB domains and one epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like domain (CUB1-EGF-CUB2). The CUB1-EGF-CUB2 domains mediate the assembly of C1qrs, also known as the C1 complex. The CUB1-EGF-CUB2 domains contains regions that are responsible for calcium-dependent dimerisation of C1s, binding to C1r dimer to assemble the C1r2s2 tetramer, and binding to C1q to form the C1 complex. The C-terminal portion of C1s comprises two complement control protein domains and a serine protease domain (CCP1-CCP2-SP). The CCP1 and CCP2 domains are responsible for binding downstream complement substrates such as C4 and C2. The SP domain is responsible for proteolytic cleavage of C4 and C2 by the activated C1s enzyme. We hypothesised that antibodies that selectively bind the N-terminal CUB1-EGF-CUB2 domains of C1s (hereafter termed CUB binders) may be able to displace C1q from the C1 complex and neutralise CP function. Such C1q displacement antibodies would be able to avoid C1q-mediated clearance and have prolonged half-lives in circulation. The antibody generation workflow to generate and identify CUB binders is described in Fig. 1b. Rabbits were intradermally immunised with recombinant human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer or native human serum-derived C1s. Splenic and peripheral blood B-cells were isolated from these rabbits and frozen down. Human and cynomolgus C1s cross-reactive B-cells were sorted using fluorescent-tagged human C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer and cynomolgus C1s-Flag proteins. The culture supernatants of the sorted B cells were then screened to identify CUB-specific binders using a combination of full-length recombinant C1s-His and a truncated C-term CCP1-CCP2-SP-His construct.Fig. 1Generation of C1s antibodies with C1q displacement function. Characterisation of C1q displacement and blockade of C1 reassembly by SPR

To demonstrate that the CP neutralisation was indeed mediated by displacement of C1q from the C1 complex, we established a sensitive C1q displacement assay using SPR. As SPR allows for real-time monitoring of protein binding and dissociation from the sensor surface, we took advantage of this property to monitor C1q disassembly from C1qrs using the assay format shown in Fig. 2a. Briefly, recombinant C1r2s2 Flag/His tetramer was first captured onto the sensor surface by anti-His antibody. Native human C1q was then injected to form the C1 complex. Finally, the CUB binder was injected continuously. Engineering pH-dependent antigen-binding characteristics of anti-C1s antibodies

Antibodies that have pH-dependent binding to their target have significantly longer half-lives than non-pH-dependent variants.30 This is because they can dissociate from their cognate antigen within the acidic environment of the endosome and recycle back to the circulation as antigen-free antibodies. We assessed the potential of the four neutralising CUB binders to be engineered for pH-dependent binding to C1s. The overall workflow is summarised in Fig. 4a.Fig. 4Antibody engineering of CUB binders. (a) Overview of antibody engineering workflow of CUB binders to generate COS0637pHv2. All antibodies were generated as C1q silent IgG1. SEC: size exclusion chromatography, ECM: Extracellular matrix (b) SPR data showing binding data of non-pH-dependent parent COS0637-TT91R versus pH-engineered COS0637pHv2-TT91R to human and cynomolgus C1r2s2 at pH 7.4 and pH 5.8.

In the initial stage, both antibody humanisation and histidine scanning of the CDRs were conducted in parallel. Antibody humanisation was performed by framework shuffling of the heavy and light chains.31 The best combination of framework regions was selected based on the evaluation of the following four parameters—affinity to hC1r2s2, aggregation tendency by size exclusion chromatography, melting temperature and expression titre. For histidine scanning, each CDR position was sequentially mutated to histidine and assessed for pH-dependent binding to C1s using SPR at neutral pH of 7.4 and acidic pH of 5.8. Approximately 70 histidine substitution variants were made for each antibody. Although all four CUB antibodies could be successfully humanised, we selected COS0637 for further optimisation as it showed the best potential for pH-dependent antigen-binding characteristics.

Next, we systematically mutated each position of the CDRs of COS0637 and the portion of framework that has potential to affect the binding affinity. Each position was mutated to the other 18 amino acids excluding the original amino acid and cysteine, giving rise to approximately 1000 single mutant variants. The variants were evaluated using the assays described and promising mutations were identified for combination. As it was not feasible to test all possible combinations, we prioritised the selection of mutations that showed the best physicochemical profile. Heavy chain combinations and light chain combinations were evaluated separately before being combined. Approximately 700 combination variants were tested. For the final selection, the top six combination variants were extensively evaluated and COS0637pHv2 was selected for evaluation in cynomolgus pharmacokinetics (PK) study. As shown in Fig. 4b, the humanised and pH-engineered variant COS0637pHv2 bound to both human C1r2s2 tetramer at neutral pH, but weakly at pH 5.8. In contrast, the parent COS0637 antibody did not display any pH-dependent binding characteristics.

Subcutaneous dosing of RAY121 in cynomolgus monkey

To investigate the ability of RAY121 to be administered as a subcutaneous drug, cynomolgus monkeys were dosed with RAY121 intravenously at 20 mg/kg, or subcutaneously at 1, 5, and 20 mg/kg and their pharmacokinetic profiles were compared (Fig. 7a). Same as the earlier cynomolgus PK studies, ADA positive points were excluded from the analyses (Supplementary Table S5). At 1 mg/kg subcutaneous dosing, the half-life was significantly shorter at 7.54 days, suggesting that at low antibody concentrations, antigen-dependent accelerated elimination of RAY121 occurs to some extent. At higher doses of 5 and 20 mg/kg subcutaneous dosing, a long half-life of 25.4 to 25.5 days was observed (Table 5). This was almost comparable to a half-life of 26.2 days observed after intravenous dosing at 20 mg/kg. In terms of CP neutralisation, the mean complement activity after a single subcutaneous dose of 1 mg/kg of RAY121 was reduced up to 60% of baseline, followed by recovery of CP activity with elimination of RAY121 (Fig. 7b). At doses of 5 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg, the mean complement activity was reduced below the lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) of 16% in this assay. Notably, CP activity was inhibited for up to 42 days by a single 5 mg/kg subcutaneous dose. Neutralisation of human complement by RAY121

To investigate if RAY121 has the potential to neutralise human complement in a disease setting, we titrated RAY121 and assessed its ability to neutralise CP of healthy donors and CAD patient plasmas. RAY121 showed highly similar neutralisation profile against healthy donor and CAD plasmas to sensitised RBCs (Fig. 8a). Apart from RBCs, we also investigated if RAY121 was able to neutralise CP activation on target cells of other CP-mediated autoimmune disorders. In the skin blistering disease bullous pemphigoid (BP), one of the main pathogenic epitopes is the noncollagenous 16A (NC16A) domain of human collagen 17, also known as BP180. The anti-NC16A autoantibody 3.B6 was cloned by Qiang et al. from patients with BP and is able to induce BP-like disease in human COL17 transgenic mice.29,32 We expressed the 3.B6 antibody with wildtype human IgG1 Fc and established HEK-based 293-Freestyle cells stably expressing full-length human COL17A1. 3.B6-IgG1 bound strongly to the COL17A1 stable 293-F cells, but not to untransfected cells (Fig. 8b). When incubated with human serum, RAY121 potently neutralised C3b deposition on the sensitised target cells to a similar extent as sutimlimab-like IPN009VH2VK3 antibody (Fig. 8c). Cryo-electron microscopy of RAY121 bound to C1s

To decipher the C1q displacement mechanism of RAY121, we performed cryo-Electron Microscopy (cryo-EM) analysis to determine the 3D structure of the C1s CUB domain in complex with RAY121 Fab fragment. The cryo-EM structure revealed that RAY121 binds to the CUB1 domain of C1s, largely recognising a linear stretch of residues from Q70 to E83 in a loop region between two antiparallel beta sheets (Fig. 9a). This epitope is consistent with the observation that RAY121 able to bind SDS denatured C1s in Western blot analysis (data not shown). The cryo-EM structure also revealed the presence of histidine residues on both sides of the binding interface between RAY121 and CUB1 (Fig. 9a). These histidine residues create a hydrogen bond network and are likely to be important for pH-dependent antigen binding of RAY121. To understand how RAY121 may displace C1q from the C1 complex, we superpositioned a previously reported crystal structure of CUB1-EGF-CUB2 with C1q collagen-like peptide (PDB: 4LOR)25 with the RAY121-C1s CUB complex structure determined in this study. Interestingly, we observed that RAY121 binds to a site distinct from the C1q collagen-like peptide binding site but the Fab arm sterically clashes with the rigid C1q collagen-like peptide triple helix (Fig. 9a). To further confirm this, we superimposed our cryo-EM structure onto the C1 complex structure that was constructed based on the cryo-electron tomography map (EMD-16251).33 We again observed that the epitope of RAY121 does not directly compete with C1q for binding to C1s (Fig. 9b) but lies within the area where the C1q collagen arms extends out from the C1 complex. Based on these superpositioned structures, we postulate that RAY121 does not inhibit the interaction between C1s and C1q by directly competing for binding to the C1q binding site on C1s, but rather exerts its displacement activity through steric hindrance. This interpretation is also supported by the SPR results observed in Fig. 2a and b. When COS0637 (RAY121 precursor antibody) was added to the C1 complex, the SPR sensogram exhibited a transient behaviour indicative of COS637 binding to the C1 complex before displacement starts to occur. This aligns with the proposed mechanism of RAY121's displacement mechanism through steric hindrance rather than direct inhibition of the C1s-C1q interaction.

Discussion

Complement proteins are one of the most well-characterised serum proteins. Despite this, the number of approved antibody drugs against complement remain few. One reason is that the high plasma concentration and rapid turnover of complement poses a challenge for drug pharmacokinetics. The first approved anti-complement drug eculizumab requires large intravenous infusion of 900–1200 mg once every two weeks. This dosage is needed because plasma C5 concentration can reach up to 80 μg/mL (0.42 μM). Ravulizumab, a second-generation long-acting version of eculizumab, was subsequently developed and approved. It contains two histidine substitutions to permit dissociation of C5 from the antibody at pH 6.0 as well as Fc mutations to enhance FcRn binding for endosomal recycling.34 Ravulizumab offers a reduced and more convenient treatment schedule of 3000–3600 mg intravenous dosing once every 8 weeks. We previously reported the generation of a pH-dependent anti-C5 antibody called SKY59.35 In cynomolgus monkeys, SKY59 exhibited long-lasting neutralisation of complement,36 paving the way for clinical testing. In the COMMODORE Phase 3 study, SKY59, also known as crovalimab, exhibited sustained complement inhibition in patients with paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH) when administered as a single intravenous loading dose of 1000–1500 mg followed by a maintenance subcutaneous dose 680–1020 mg once every four weeks.37 Crovalimab was recently approved as an anti-C5 drug, validating the utility of pH-dependent binding to reduce the required dose to neutralise complement.38 The anti-C2 antibody, empasiprubart (ARGX-117), also utilises pH-dependent and calcium-dependent binding to achieve dose reduction to neutralise the CP and LP. Empasiprubart demonstrated dose-linear pharmacokinetic profile with extended neutralisation of C239 and is presently in Phase III clinical trials for several autoimmune indications. Since plasma C1s concentration is almost the same as C5 at approximately 0.4 μM, we adopted a similar strategy to generate an anti-C1s antibody with pH-dependent antigen-binding characteristics. RAY121 showed a half-life of 41.2 days in healthy volunteers in Phase 1a trial and CP activity could be suppressed for 4 weeks by a single dose at 4.5 mg/kg.40

During the process of engineering RAY121, we observed that pH-dependency alone was unable to confer a long antibody half-life. As shown in Fig. 6a, an additional step of pI-engineering was required to attain the desired level of PK prolongation. We, as well as others, have previously reported that lowering the antibody's pI by modifying the variable domain can improve its pharmacokinetic profile.41, 42, 43 In this study, we show that pI modulation by modifying the Fc and keeping the variable domain unchanged may also be an effective method. This may be advantageous when mutation of the variable region is not desirable. As we only showed data for one antibody, COS0637pHv8, more examples are needed to judge if this can be applied to antibodies in general. Nonetheless, exchanging the Fc on antibodies is a relatively easy and routine task. Lowering the antibody pI by Fc swapping can be done as a first step to assess its effectiveness before deciding whether it is necessary to undertake the more difficult process of engineering the variable domain.

In this study, we have adopted pH-dependent binding as the strategy to prolong the pharmacokinetic profile of anti-C1s antibodies. Others have adopted a different and interesting strategy to selectively target the activated form of C1s. The proenzyme form of C1s makes up the large majority in circulation and only a small proportion is locally activated at the disease site. By avoiding binding to proenzyme C1s, anti-activated C1s antibodies can minimise TMDD. Riliprubart, also known as SAR445088 or BIVV020, is a second generation anti-C1s antibody from Sanofi that preferentially binds activated C1s. Riliprubart also contains mutations in the Fc to enhance its binding to the neonatal Fc receptor and recycle from the endosome more efficiently. In healthy volunteers, riliprubart did not exhibit TMDD in contrast to its predecessor, sutimlimab.44 In a recent Phase 1b trial with a small number of patients with CAD, a single intravenous dose of Riliprubart at 15 mg/kg demonstrated sustained control of haemolysis and improved anaemia over 15 weeks.45 Dianthus Therapeutics is another company that is developing an anti-activated C1s antibody, DNTH103. It is currently being developed in Phase 2 clinical trial for generalised myasthenia gravis (trial identifier NCT06282159) and for multifocal motor neuropathy (trial identifier NCT06537999). There is currently no published data available for DNTH103 and its clinical efficacy and pharmacokinetics remains to be seen. Since both RAY121 and anti-activated C1s antibodies do not show strong TMDD, it is reasonable to expect that both types of antibodies will be effective in the treatment of CP-driven haematological diseases. However, it is less clear whether anti-activated C1s antibodies will be equally effective in neutralising complement at distal tissue sites where antibody concentrations are lower but the substrates of C1s, complement C2 and C4, may be high due to local complement synthesis. One such tissue is the kidney where C2 and C4 transcripts are highly expressed in the tubular region of normal human kidneys.46 It will be interesting to see if anti-activated C1s antibodies will be able confer therapeutic effect in CP-driven kidney diseases such as SLE and autoantibody-mediated kidney graft rejection.

The mechanism of how RAY121 exerts its C1q displacement activity is interesting but also difficult to interpret as there is no high-resolution structure of the entire C1 complex. A helpful dataset was from Venkatraman et al. who solved the X-ray co–crystal structure of C1s CUB1-EGF-CUB2 bound to a short triple helical collagen-like peptide of C1q.25 The key residues of C1s in contact with C1q collagen helix were E45, E48, Y52, D98, F99, S100. Notably, this region is adjacent to the epitope of RAY121 which is from Q70 to E83. It is hypothesised that RAY121 achieves its displacement activity by causing steric hindrance with this C1q collagen helix. Furthermore, Cryo-electron tomography structures of the C1 complex analysed by Thomas et al.47 and Abendstein et al.33 revealed that RAY121's epitope is located within the hollow core of the C1 complex. This raises the question of how RAY121 can access and bind to the epitope. While interpretation is challenging due to limited data on the flexibility and dynamics of the C1 complex, RAY121's epitope is thought to have a flexible loop-like structure. Additionally, SAXS data of the C1 complex indicates that the C1 complex possesses a partially flexible structure.48 Based on these data, it may be postulated that the RAY121 epitope peptide may adopt a conformation accessible to RAY121 due to its flexibility. This supports the notion that RAY121's epitope is located in the hollow core structure of the C1 complex. Consequently, RAY121 may be able to bind to C1s within the core of the C1 complex by leveraging the flexibility and accessibility of the epitope. To elucidate these details further, additional studies focusing on dynamics are necessary. Such research would be crucial for a comprehensive understanding of the mechanism behind RAY121's interaction with the C1 complex. The question also arises—if RAY121 can bind to the C1 complex without competing with C1q, how does it displace C1q? One possible explanation is that the collagen arms of C1q are more rigid when bound to C1r2s2 and more flexible when C1q is alone. Electron microscopy analysis comparing fixed C1 complex and free C1q reported that the cone angle of C1q globular heads in C1 complex exhibited a restricted range of angular flexibility compared to free C1q.49 Supporting this hypothesis, Zwarthoff et al. reported that C1r2s2 fixes the collagen-like arms to orient the globular head of C1q to favour binding to hexameric IgG clusters.50 We therefore postulate that upon RAY121 binding, the rigid triple helical structure of the C1q collagen-like arm adjacent to C1s are pushed aside by the Fab. As C1q adopts a rigid structure when bound to C1r2s2, it is unable to reorient itself to accommodate the bulky Fab head in the core of the C1 complex. Owing to the high affinity of RAY121 for C1s compared to the weak C1q-C1s interaction, C1q is eventually displaced from C1r2s2.

We note several key limitations in our study: Firstly, we did not conduct further investigation to identify the reason why modulating the antibody pI by swapping the Fc and keeping the variable domain unchanged was effective to significantly reduce antibody clearance. It may likely be due to a reduction in the electrostatic interaction between the immune complex and negatively charged components in the circulation such as glycosaminoglycans and proteoglycans. A detailed characterisation of the immune complexes formed using Fcs of various pIs will be required to shed light on this. Secondly, the mechanism of C1q displacement by RAY121 is modelled on a highly limited number of C1qrs structures. Our hypothesis of how RAY121 accesses the CUB domain in the C1qrs complex and displace C1q is therefore limited. Higher resolution structures of C1qrs, if available in the future, will allow for a better understanding of the interaction between C1s and RAY121 and shed more light on the mechanism of C1q displacement. Thirdly, in the SPR experiments showing C1q displacement by various CUB binders, the set-up utilised C1qrs immobilised to the sensor surface by anti-His antibodies that capture the His-tag on C1s. This may represent an unnatural state of C1qrs where the flexibility of the C1s arms is constrained.

In summary, we present RAY121 as a novel pH and pI-engineered anti-C1s antibody that is able to potently neutralise the CP for an extended duration by virtue of its long half-life. In addition, RAY121 is effective as a subcutaneously administered drug, allowing for convenient patient dosing. Finally, we show that RAY121 possesses a unique mode of action of displacing C1q from the C1 complex to inhibit complement activity. Taken together, these data demonstrate that RAY121 is a promising antibody candidate for treatment of CP-driven diseases. RAY121 is presently under phase 1b open-label basket trial to inhibit CP in immunological diseases (trial identifier NCT06371417).
