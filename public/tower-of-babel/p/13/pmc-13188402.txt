The composition and function succession of lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia drives in situ decomposition of rice straw in paddy fields
Authors: Binhan Zhao, Wenjun Dong, Yang Yu, Xin Zhao, Zhanjun Cai, Haoyue Zhang, Shaojie Li, Xianyun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStraw incorporation into soil can increase soil organic matter content and improve soil structure. However, the in situ decomposition mechanisms of straw in field remain unclear.ResultsThe decomposition mechanisms of straw in paddy fields within the black soil region of Northeast China were investigated by analyzing the composition and functional succession of lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia (LDMC). Straw decomposition predominantly occurred from May to September, coinciding with warmer temperatures. The alternating paddy-upland field patterns and temperature fluctuations significantly influenced soil microbial community diversity and function, thereby impacting straw decomposition. Through literature analysis, microbial isolation, and enzymatic activity assays, LDMC comprising 18 bacterial genera and 41 fungal genera were proposed. A comprehensive analysis of the dynamics of straw decomposition rates, microbial composition succession, enzymatic profiles, and the abundance of corresponding enzyme genes in soil revealed unique functional succession patterns of LDMC. During the paddy field phase (May-August), Pseudarthrobacter, Bacillus, Nocardioides, Tausonia, Mortierella, Pseudeurotium, etc. were the dominant microbial taxa driving straw decomposition. Tausonia, Mortierella and Pseudeurotium primarily contributed to cellulose degradation, while Bacillus and Nocardioides were involved in the breakdown of both cellulose and hemicellulose. In contrast, Pseudarthrobacter played a major role in lignin degradation. This phase was characterized by a high abundance of cellulase and hemicellulase genes, indicating that decomposition primarily targets these components. During the upland field phase in September, Bacillus, Bradyrhizobium, Mortierella, Mrakia, Pseudeurotium, etc. were the predominant decomposers. Mortierella and Mrakia contributed to the degradation of straw lignocellulose, while Bacillus and Pseudeurotium played a key role in the degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose. In contrast, Bradyrhizobium was predominantly involved in lignin degradation. This phase exhibited a higher abundance of ligninase genes, suggesting a shift towards lignin degradation as the dominant process.ConclusionsThese findings provide novel insights into the ecological succession and functional roles of LDMC in rice straw decomposition. They offer a theoretical basis for identifying key lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms active at different phases, thereby laying a foundation for improving the efficiency of rice straw return to fields.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00888-6.Keywords: Paddy field, In situ decomposition of straw, Soil microorganisms, Lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms, Lignocellulose-degrading enzymes

Introduction

The organic substances rich in straw, such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, are gradually transformed into soil organic matter through the processes of decomposition in the soil after straw is returned to the field. Poor soil management and inappropriate tillage methods lead to soil degradation and a decrease in organic matter content, affecting the yield and quality of crops [1]. Straw return to the field can increase soil organic matter content, improve soil structure, and also avoid the waste of resources and environmental pollution caused by open burning of straw, hence its widespread application [2, 3]. Rice is a widely cultivated crop worldwide, and the abundant straw produced after harvest constitutes a key recyclable resource. The incorporation of rice straw into fields offers extensive ecological and agricultural benefits. Research on the in situ decomposition patterns of rice straw and the microorganisms involved in the decomposition process can provide guidance for the scientific return of rice straw to the field.

The main components of straw include cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, and their decomposition in the soil is a process mainly mediated by microorganisms [4, 5]. In this process, microorganisms produce cellulase, hemicellulase, and ligninase to collectively complete the decomposition of straw. The pattern of degradation for different components in this process, as well as the primary microorganisms and enzymes that are involved, has been reported [4–6]. Common microorganisms with cellulose-degrading activity include bacteria such as Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Clostridium, Cytophaga, Sporocytophaga, Cellulomonas, and Streptomyces, as well as fungi such as Trichoderma, Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Phanerochaete chrysosporium [5, 7, 8]. Common microorganisms with hemicellulose-degrading activity include bacteria such as Bacillus, Clostridium, and Streptomyces, and fungi such as Trichoderma and Aspergillus [5, 8]. Common microorganisms with lignin-degrading activity include bacteria such as Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Sphingomonas, and Streptomyces, and fungi such as white-rot fungi and Aspergillus [5, 8, 9].

Unlike other crops, rice grows under flooded or submerged conditions, so its cultivation pattern has a distinct feature of alternating between paddy field and upland field [10, 11]. Before rice transplanting, the field needs to be soaked, making it completely submerged in water, at which point it transitions from upland field to paddy field. Before rice harvesting, the paddy field needs to be drained and exposed to the sun, at which point it reverts to upland field [11, 12]. This unique cultivation pattern leads significant differences in soil water content, oxygen levels, and redox potential (Eh), causing the in situ decomposition of rice straw in paddy fields to undergo sequential “aerobic-anaerobic-aerobic” environmental shifts [13, 14]. Consequently, the microorganisms and related degrading enzymes involved in this process may undergo systematic succession, thereby influencing the progression of straw decomposition. However, currently, the composition, succession, and functions of lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms and related degrading enzymes involved in the in situ decomposition of rice straw in paddy fields throughout a complete decay cycle are not yet clear, and research reports in this regard remain limited.

Northeast China is one of the major rice-producing areas in China. Due to its higher latitude, lower temperatures, and longer freezing periods, the in situ decomposition of rice straw is considerably slower [15]. Understanding the patterns of rice straw in situ decomposition and the microbial composition in Northeast China can provide theoretical support and microbial resources to further improve the decomposition rate. Yan et al. [16] reported that in the long-term straw return paddy fields in Northeast China, the dominant genera include Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Sulfuricurvum, Geobacter, etc. However, high-abundance groups during the straw decomposition process may not directly participate in the degradation of lignocellulose. In the process of lignocellulose degradation, many groups do not possess lignocellulose-degrading activity and survive on the soluble sugars and other intermediate metabolic products produced by lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms [17]. By selecting microbial groups that clearly have lignocellulose-degrading activity and forming a functional group for further analysis, it is possible to better understand the function and changes of microorganisms directly involved in the degradation of lignocellulose during the straw decomposition process.

This study aims to address the following scientific questions: (1) What are the in situ decomposition patterns of rice straw in the black soil region of Northeast China? (2) What are the compositional and functional succession patterns of lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms and enzymes involved in this process? By literature analysis, microbial isolation, and lignocellulose-degrading enzymatic activity assays, we proposed and clarified the lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia involved in rice straw degradation in paddy fields for the first time. Combining omics analysis, we elucidated the in situ decomposition patterns of rice straw in the black soil region of Northeast China, as well as the composition and functional succession of lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms and lignocellulose-degrading enzymes involved in this process. Our findings contribute to understanding the in situ decomposition patterns of rice straw under the alternating paddy-upland field cultivation system, as well as the succession patterns of lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia and their functions involved in this process. This enables the identification of key lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms active at different phases, providing a theoretical basis for improving the efficiency of rice straw return to fields.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and environmental conditions

The experiment was conducted at the paddy field base of Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Minzhu Township, Daowai District, Harbin City, Heilongjiang Province (45°49′N, 126°48′E). The experimental area has a mid-temperate continental monsoon climate, characterized by four distinct seasons, with cold and dry winters and warm and humid summers. The soil type in the experimental area is chernozem.

Design of the in situ rice straw decomposition experiment

Four plots, each with an area of 500 m2, were set up in the paddy field as the experimental area. In early October, when the rice was harvested, the combine harvester chopped the rice straw and left it in the field. Subsequently, a tiller was used to turn the soil, at which point the straw was evenly distributed in the 0–22 cm soil layer. Soil samples were collected monthly from March to September of the following year. Each month, approximately 200 g of soil was collected from each of the four plots (0–22 cm depth) using the five-point sampling method. For each sampling date, four independent soil samples were obtained, representing four biological replicates. Each independent soil sample was divided into two portions: one was stored at −80°C for high-throughput sequencing, and the other was stored at 4 °C for microbial isolation and cultivation. Additionally, soil temperature was monitored using automated temperature recording devices to obtain daily temperatures, which were then aggregated to calculate the monthly average temperature. The paddy field was flooded in early May each year and drained in mid-to-late August, so March, April, and September were considered upland field phase, while May to August were paddy field phase (Fig. 1A).

Measurement of in situ rice straw decomposition rate

The in situ decomposition rate of rice straw was determined according to methods described by Zhao et al. [18]. Specifically, rice straw was air-dried and chopped to small pieces (≤ 10 cm). Then 40 g of rice straw was mixed evenly with 3.9 kg paddy field soil and placed into a PVC pipe (height ~ 22 cm). The pipe was then placed into a mesh bag (80 meshes). To simulate the actual field distribution of straw after plowing, the pipes were buried into holes approximately 22 cm deep in the experimental plots, with a thin layer of soil covering the top. Four pipes were taken per month from March to September. The straw and soil inside the pipe were separated using a sieve. The straw was then washed repeatedly with water to remove attached soil, and the cleaned straw was dried at 105 °C until constant weight. The formula for calculating the straw decomposition rate is: Decomposition rate (%) = (initial dry weight of straw - dry weight of undecomposed straw)/initial dry weight of straw × 100%. Furthermore, a linear fitting was performed between the decomposition rate of each sample and the corresponding monthly average temperature.

DNA extraction, high-throughput sequencing and data analysis

The extraction of total soil DNA and high-throughput sequencing were completed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. DNA extraction and subsequent amplicon sequencing and metagenomic sequencing were performed separately for each of the biological replicates collected monthly. For bacterial amplicon sequencing, the V3-V4 regions of 16 S rRNA gene were amplified by PCR using the universal primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) [19]. For fungal amplicon sequencing, the ITS1F-ITS2R regions of ITS were amplified by PCR using the universal primers ITS1F (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’) and ITS2R (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’) [20]. The amplification conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 3 min, 27 (for the bacterial specific primer pair) or 35 (for the fungal specific primer pair) cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 45 s, and finished by 72 °C for 10 min. In addition, sample with water replacing template DNA was used as negative amplification control. Amplicon sequencing of PCR product was performed on Illumina Next2000 PE300 platform. All raw data from the sequencing were uploaded to the NCBI SRA database (PRJNA1241498). The raw reads were preprocessed and filtered using fastq (v0.19.6). Low-quality reads (reads with base quality values below 20 at the tail, reads shorter than 50 bp, and reads containing more than 5 N bases) were filtered out, and adapter/primer sequences were removed. Then the paired-end clean reads were assembled before being imported into QIIME (v1.9.1) pipeline for operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering based on 97% identity. Taxonomic assignment of bacterial and fungal OTUs was performed using SILVA (release 138) and UNITE (release 8.0) database, respectively. Bioinformatic analyses were conducted using the online platform of Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com). All samples were rarefied to the minimum sequence count, followed by analyses of alpha diversity, beta diversity, and community composition. Specifically, one-way ANOVA was employed to analyze intergroup differences in sobs (the observed richness) index and Shannon index, with Tukey’s test used for multiple comparison correction. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was applied to compare intergroup β-diversity, using Bray-Curtis distances to calculate sample dissimilarity, and analysis of similarity (ANOSIM, with 999 permutations) was performed to test for significant difference between groups [18].

To cover the majority of taxa with relatively high abundance, the top 50 bacterial and fungal genera by relative abundance were screened for each month. For genera from which isolates had been successfully obtained, plate assays were conducted to detect the presence of cellulase, hemicellulase, or ligninase activity. For genera for which no isolates were available, a systematic review of the published literature was performed to determine whether strains within those genera had been reported to exhibit the aforementioned enzymatic activities. Finally, genera corresponding to strains experimentally verified or documented in the literature as possessing cellulase, hemicellulase, or ligninase activity were classified into the lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia. Phylogenetic trees of the selected genera were constructed using the neighbor-joining method in FastTree (v2.1.3). For heatmap visualization, the mean relative abundance (within the total microbial community) of the selected genera for each month was standardized using StandardScaler, and hierarchical clustering was conducted using Euclidean distance with complete linkage method.

Metagenomic sequencing was further performed using the Illumina Novaseq X Plus platform. Raw data from the sequencing were uploaded to the NCBI SRA database (PRJNA1241498). After assembly, ORF prediction and clustering, the constructed non-redundant gene catalog was aligned against Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Carbohydrate-Active enzymes (CAZy) database to obtain functional annotations. Based on the descriptions of each functional family in the CAZy database, functional families related to cellulases, hemicellulases, and ligninases were selected [21]. KEGG functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the online platform of Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com), and the top 20 enriched functions with |Reporter score| ≥1.65 were presented [22]. PCoA was employed to analyze the differences in lignocellulose-degrading enzyme genes at different phases. The Bray-Curtis distance was used to calculate the distances between samples, and the ANOSIM was applied to test for differences between groups.

Microbial isolation, identification, and determination of lignocellulose-degrading enzymes activity

The isolation and cultivation of microorganisms were performed according to the previous description [18]. The collected paddy soil was mixed with sterile water at a ratio of 1:10 (m: v) and shaken for 30 min. The mixture was then subjected to a 10-fold serial dilution, and 100 µL of the appropriate dilution was spread onto different solid media. Bacteria were isolated using nutrient agar (NA) medium, while fungi were isolated using Martin’s medium (K2HPO4·3H2O 1 g/L, MgSO4·7H2O 0.5 g/L, peptone 5 g/L, glucose 10 g/L, rose bengal 0.033 g/L, streptomycin 0.03 g/L, agar 20 g/L) [23]. Media were incubated at 28 °C until colonies appeared on the plates. Bacterial colonies were streaked onto Luria-Bertani (LB) and tryptone soy agar (TSA) solid media to obtain pure colonies. Fungal colonies were inoculated onto potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium to obtain pure colonies. Genomic DNA was extracted from single colonies, followed by PCR amplification. The bacterial primers used were 27 F (AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG) and 1492R (GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT) [24], while the fungal primers were ITS1 (TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG) and ITS4 (TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC) [25]. The amplification conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 5 min, 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 90 (for the bacterial primers) or 55 s (for the fungal primers), and finished by 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were purified and sequenced, and the sequences were compared with nucleic acid sequences in the GenBank database using the BLASTn to determine the taxonomic classification of the strains.

CMC-Na medium (carboxymethyl cellulose-Na 15 g/L, K2HPO4 1 g/L, KH2PO4 1 g/L, MgSO4·7H2O 0.2 g/L, NaCl 0.1 g/L, FeCl3 0.01 g/L, NaNO3 2.5 g/L, CaCl2 0.1 g/L, yeast extract 5 g/L, agar 15 g/L, pH 7.0–7.2.0.2) was used for bacterial cellulase activity determination [26]. CMC-Na medium or microcrystalline cellulose medium (replacing CMC-Na in the CMC-Na medium with microcrystalline cellulose) was used for fungal cellulase activity determination. Xylan medium (xylan 10 g/L, NH4NO3 5 g/L, MgSO4·7H2O 0.5 g/L, NaCl 5 g/L, K2HPO4 2 g/L, (NH4)2SO4 1 g/L, yeast extract 0.3 g/L, agar 15 g/L) was used for xylanase (a type of hemicellulase) activity determination [26, 27]. LB medium supplemented with 0.04% guaiacol and 40 mg/L CuSO4 was used for bacterial laccase (a type of ligninase) activity determination [28]. PDA medium supplemented with 0.04% guaiacol was used for fungal laccase activity determination [29]. PDA medium supplemented with 0.1 g/L aniline blue was used for the determination of bacterial and fungal peroxidase (a type of ligninase) activity [30]. Single colonies of the bacteria or fungi to be tested were picked and suspended in sterile water, and 2 µL of the suspension was inoculated onto the center of the respective medium plates. The plates were incubated at 25 °C. For CMC-Na medium, microcrystalline cellulose medium, and xylan medium plates, after incubating for 2–5 days, the plates were flooded with 1 mg/mL Congo red solution and stained for 30 min. Then the Congo red solution was discarded and the plates were soaked in 1 mol/L NaCl solution for 15 min. After discarding the NaCl solution, the hydrolysis zones and colony diameters were observed and measured. For guaiacol medium plates, after incubating for 3–10 days, the reddish brown color zones were directly observed and measured. For aniline blue medium plates, after incubating for 3–10 days, the decolorization zones were directly observed and measured. The enzymatic activity of the strains was quantified by means of the enzymatic index (EI) calculated from the ratio between the diameter of the hydrolysis/color change/decolorization zone and the diameter of the colony [31].

Results

Changes in soil temperature and straw decomposition rate

The annual average soil temperature in the experimental area was 7.3 °C, with temperatures below 0 °C from December to March of the following year. During the months when the experiment was conducted, the average soil temperature in March and April was below 4 °C. Subsequently, the temperature rapidly increased, with the monthly average soil temperature being above 14 °C from May to September. The highest monthly average soil temperature (23.7 °C) was recorded in July (Fig. 1B).

The total straw decomposition rate is shown in Fig. 1C, with the total decomposition rate showing a continuously increasing trend. From March to April, the total straw decomposition rates were less than 16% (n = 4). From May to July, there was a considerable increase, with total straw decomposition rates of 38.6%, 50.8%, and 75.0%, respectively (n = 4). By the end of September, the total straw decomposition rate reached 89.7% (n = 4). The decomposition rate for each month was further calculated. Since the average monthly temperature was below 0 °C in March, the decomposition rate for that month was set to 0, as it was likely attributable to the preliminary decomposition that occurred from October to November of the previous year. For the remaining months, the decomposition rates in May, June, July, and September were relatively high, at 22.9%, 12.2%, 24.2%, and 13.1%, respectively (n = 4).

Furthermore, the monthly straw decomposition rate was fitted against the monthly average temperature (Fig. S1), revealing a significant positive correlation between temperature and the straw decomposition rate (R2 = 0.2966, P = 0.0027). This indicates that temperature is a key environmental factor driving straw decomposition.

Soil microbial community diversity and structure

For the α-diversity of bacterial communities in paddy soil, no significant difference was observed in the sobs and Shannon index across the months (P > 0.05, n = 4) (Fig. 2A). For the α-diversity of fungal communities in paddy soil, the sobs index showed an initial increase followed by a decrease, with the sobs index during the paddy field phase being higher than that during the upland field phase. The sobs index in May was significantly different from that in March, April, and September (P < 0.05, n = 4). Unlike bacterial communities, the Shannon index of the fungal community in May was significantly higher than in other months (P < 0.05, n = 4) (Fig. 2B). The above indicated that the richness and diversity of soil fungi in the paddy field in May were higher than in other months.

Pairwise PCoA comparisons between consecutive months indicated that there was no significant difference (P > 0.05, n = 4) in bacterial β-diversity between the two months of the upland field phase (March and April). Similarly, during the paddy field phase, no significant difference (P > 0.05, n = 4) was observed between May and June, June and July, and July and August. However, significant differences (P < 0.05, n = 4) were found during the transitions between habitat types, i.e., between April and May (upland field to paddy field) and between August and September (paddy field to upland field) (Fig. 2C). For fungal communities, the PCoA results were similar to those of the bacterial communities, with significant differences (P < 0.05, n = 4) in β-diversity also observed during these transitional periods (Fig. 2D). This indicated that the differences in habitat between paddy fields and upland fields lead to distinct soil microbial community structures and compositions.

Microbial community composition was further analyzed at the phylum and genus levels (Fig. S2). During the upland field phase (March and April), the bacterial community was predominantly composed of Chloroflexi, Actinobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Acidobacteriota at the phylum level. At the genus level, the most dominant groups were Pseudarthrobacter, Intrasporangium, Nocardioides, and Sphingomonas. For fungal communities, at the phylum level, Ascomycota had the highest relative abundance, followed by Basidiomycota and Mortierellomycota. At the genus level, the most dominant groups were Mortierella, Epicoccum, Cladosporium, and Tausonia. During the paddy field phase (May to August), for bacterial communities, at the phylum level, the most dominant groups changed to Chloroflexi, Actinobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Firmicutes. At the genus level, the most dominant groups changed to Pseudarthrobacter, Intrasporangium, Nocardioides, and Bacillus. For fungal communities, at the phylum level, Basidiomycota had the highest relative abundance, followed by unclassified_k__Fungi and Ascomycota. At the genus level, the most dominant groups changed to Tausonia, Mortierella, Holtermanniella, and Pseudeurotium. During the upland phase (September), for bacterial communities, at the phylum level, the most dominant groups changed to Chloroflexi, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, and Acidobacteriota. At the genus level, the most dominant groups changed to Pseudarthrobacter, Thiobacillus, Bacillus, and Intrasporangium. For fungal communities, at the phylum level, Basidiomycota had the highest relative abundance, followed by Mortierellomycota and Ascomycota. At the genus level, the most dominant groups changed to Mortierella, Tausonia, Mrakia, and Pseudeurotium.

Composition and structure of lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia in paddy soil

The top 50 bacterial and fungal genera in terms of relative abundance for each month were selected. Among them, the cumulative relative abundance of the top 50 fungal genera in each month exceeded 97% of the total fungal community, while the cumulative relative abundance of the top 50 bacterial genera remained around 55%. Based on literature reports and the results of enzyme activity assays of isolated microbial strains (Tables 1 and 2), 18 bacterial genera and 41 fungal genera were selected to form the lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia in paddy field soil (Fig. 3). For the lignocellulose-degrading bacterial consortia, they were distributed across the Actinobacteriota (5 genera), Bacteroidota (2 genera), Chloroflexi (1 genus), Proteobacteria (6 genera), and Firmicutes (4 genera) (Fig. 3A). A total of 5 genera (Bacillus, Lysobacter, Pseudarthrobacter, Pseudomonas, Sphingomonas) have yielded isolates, and their specific EI values are presented in Table S1. For the lignocellulose-degrading fungal consortia, the vast majority were distributed in the Ascomycota (26 genera) and Basidiomycota (14 genera), with one additional genus belonging to the Mortierellomycota (Fig. 3B). A total of 8 genera (Acremonium, Aspergillus, Cladosporium, Fusarium, Paracremonium, Penicillium, Talaromyces, Trichoderma) have yielded isolates, and their specific EI values are presented in Table S2.

Furthermore, α and β diversity analyses were conducted on microbial communities composed of 18 bacterial genera and 41 fungal genera at the OTU level (Fig. S3). For the lignocellulose-degrading bacterial communities, the sobs index showed a continuously decreasing trend. The sobs index in September was significantly lower than that in March, April, and May (P < 0.05, n = 4), while the Shannon index did not show significant changes overall, with no significant difference between months (P > 0.05, n = 4). For the lignocellulose-degrading fungal communities, the sobs index was highest in May, significantly higher than in July, August and September (P < 0.05, n = 4), with no significant difference (P > 0.05, n = 4) observed between other months. The Shannon index from March to May was significantly higher than that from June to September (P < 0.05, n = 4). The PCoA results for the lignocellulose-degrading bacterial and fungal communities were consistent with those for the entire bacterial and fungal communities, showing significant differences in β diversity during the transition periods between habitat types, i.e., between April and May (upland field to paddy field) and between August and September (paddy field to upland field) (P < 0.05, n = 4). This indicated that temperature and the alternation of paddy and upland field conditions also had a significant impact on the community structure of the proposed lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia.

Functional succession of lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia in paddy soil

The relative abundance of each genus in the lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia from March to September was normalized to generate a clustered heatmap (Fig. 3). Based on the changes in relative abundance of each genus at different phases, the dominant lignocellulose-degrading microbial groups at each phase were identified. Specifically, during the upland field phase in March-April, the dominant lignocellulose-degrading microbial groups included 11 bacterial genera (Flavobacterium, Cryobacterium, Nocardioides, Bradyrhizobium, Sphingomonas, Pedobacter, Intrasporangium, Arenimonas, Lysobacter, Aeromicrobium, Anaerolinea) and 23 fungal genera (Glaciozyma, Cystofilobasidium, Aspergillus, Vishniacozyma, Nigrospora, Emericellopsis, Phaeosphaeria, Filobasidium, Cladosporium, Sporobolomyces, Bipolaris, Sarocladium, Leuconeurospora, Fusarium, Schizothecium, Gibberella, Cistella, Microdochium, Alternaria, Epicoccum, Coniothyrium, Albifimbria, Paracremonium). In addition, the bacterial genera Pseudarthrobacter, Bacillus, Trichococcus, and the fungal genera Tausonia, Pseudeurotium, Mrakia also had relatively high abundance (Figs. 4 and 5). However, due to the low temperatures during March-April (with soil temperatures below 0 °C in March and an average soil temperature below 4 °C in April), the straw decomposition rate was very low (only 4.7% in April). Therefore, the function of the soil lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia was limited during this phase.

After entering the paddy field phase (May-August), the dominant lignocellulose-degrading microbial groups shifted to 11 bacterial genera (Bacillus, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_13, Pseudarthrobacter, Trichococcus, Pseudomonas, Polaromonas, Anaerolinea, Nocardioides, Cryobacterium, Flavobacterium) and 19 fungal genera (Pseudogymnoascus, Tausonia, Coprinellus, Trichoderma, Acremonium, Papiliotrema, Typhula, Sampaiozyma, Cystobasidium, Holtermanniella, Wardomyces, Talaromyces, Mortierella, Coprinopsis, Penicillium, Pseudeurotium, Glaciozyma, Aspergillus, Vishniacozyma). Combining the composition of the lignocellulose-degrading enzyme systems and their relative abundance within the total microbial community (Tables 1 and 2; Figs. 4 and 5, Fig. S4), it was found that the fungal genera Tausonia (> 11%), Mortierella (> 6%), and Pseudeurotium (> 1%) mainly participated in cellulose degradation; the bacterial genus Trichococcus (> 0.5%) mainly participated in hemicellulose degradation; the bacterial genera Bacillus (> 1%), Nocardioides (> 1%), Pseudomonas, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Cryobacterium, Flavobacterium, Anaerolinea (> 0.5%) mainly participated in the degradation of both cellulose and hemicellulose; and the bacterial genus Pseudarthrobacter (> 5%) mainly participated in lignin degradation. In addition, among the groups with high abundance in March-April but reduced abundance in May-August, the relative abundance of two bacterial genera (Intrasporangium, Sphingomonas) and two fungal genera (Schizothecium, Cistella) remained above or close to 0.5% in May-August. The fungal genus Mrakia also had a relatively high abundance (> 1.9%) during this phase, and this genus may still play a role in straw decomposition. During this phase, the average soil temperature was above 15 °C, and the total straw decomposition rate reached 60%, making it the main phase for rice straw decomposition. The soil lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia played an important role during this phase.

After returning to the upland field in September, the dominant lignocellulose-degrading microbial groups in the soil shifted again to include two bacterial genera (Bacillus, Bradyrhizobium) and 12 fungal genera (Wardomyces, Talaromyces, Mortierella, Saccharomyces, Mrakia, Coprinopsis, Penicillium, Pseudeurotium, Glaciozyma, Emericellopsis, Albifimbria, Paracremonium). Combining the composition of the lignocellulose-degrading enzyme systems and their relative abundance within the total microbial community (Tables 1 and 2; Figs. 4 and 5, Fig. S4), it was found that the fungi Mortierella (> 15%) and Mrakia (> 6%) mainly participated in the degradation of straw lignocellulose; the bacterial genus Bacillus (> 1%) and the fungal genera Pseudeurotium (> 3%), Emericellopsis (> 0.5%) mainly participated in the degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose; and the bacterial genus Bradyrhizobium mainly participated in lignin degradation. In addition, the bacterial genus Pseudarthrobacter (> 4%) and the fungal genus Tausonia (> 7%) still maintained high relative abundance in the September upland field phase. These groups may still play a role during this phase. The average soil temperature during this phase was above 15 °C, and the monthly decomposition rate reached 13%, making it another main period for rice straw decomposition. The soil lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia also played an important role during this phase.

Functional analysis of soil microbiome based on metagenome sequencing

The metagenomic sequencing data were functionally annotated based on the KEGG database, and a differential analysis of the KOs between the upland field phase and the paddy field phase was conducted, obtaining information on the up- and down-regulated KOs in the differential phases, which were ultimately enriched into pathways (Fig. 6A). The pathways enriched during the paddy field phase include methane metabolism, bacterial chemotaxis, starch and sucrose metabolism, and flagellar assembly, while the pathways enriched during the upland field phase include tryptophan metabolism, steroid degradation, degradation of aromatic compounds, and aminobenzoate degradation.

Simultaneously, the metagenomic sequencing data were functionally annotated based on the CAZy database, and the functional families related to cellulase, hemicellulase, and ligninase were selected from the annotated CAZy families. The differences in lignocellulose-degrading enzyme genes between the upland field phase and the paddy field phase were analyzed using principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA). The lignocellulose-degrading enzyme genes were clearly distinguished between the two phases (Fig. 6B). Further, the abundance changes of functional families related to cellulases, hemicellulases, and ligninases were compared from July to September (Fig. 6C). In detail, during the upland field phase in March and April, the reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (RPKM) of functional families related to cellulases decreased from 7087.42 to 6938.41, the RPKM of functional families related to hemicellulases decreased from 13331.13 to 12717.10, while the RPKM of functional families related to ligninases increased from 1567.45 to 1654.47. Entering the paddy field phase (May to August), the RPKM of functional families related to cellulases increased to the range of 7184.37–7500.56, the RPKM of functional families related to hemicellulases increased to the range of 13305.10–13934.01, while the RPKM of functional families related to ligninases decreased to the range of 1424.70–1555.71. When the upland field phase resumed in September, the RPKM of functional families related to cellulases decreased to 7246.23, the RPKM of functional families related to hemicellulases decreased to 13410.54, and the RPKM of functional families related to ligninases increased to 1539.51. Further analysis revealed that the abundance of functional families related to cellulases was significantly higher from July to September than from March to April. The abundance of functional families related to hemicellulases was significantly higher in July to September than in April. Conversely, the abundance of functional families related to ligninases was significantly higher in April than in May to August, and significantly higher in March, April, and September than in August. Overall, the abundance of functional families related to cellulases and hemicellulases was higher during the paddy field phase than during the upland field phase. In contrast, the abundance of functional families related to ligninases was higher during the upland field phase than during the paddy field phase.

Discussions

Northeast China is located in mid-high latitude (115°52′–135°09′ E and 38°52′–53°55′ N), and the vast majority of it falls within the mid-temperate zone, characterized by relatively low temperatures and a long freezing period. This climatic regime suppresses microbial activity and serves as a key environmental constraint on straw decomposition [15, 78]. In this study, straw decomposition primarily occurred from May to September, during which the monthly average temperatures were consistently above 14 °C. Linear fitting analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the monthly average temperature and the monthly straw decomposition rate. This indicates that temperature serves as a fundamental driver regulating microbial metabolic activity and directly determines the active period of rice straw decomposition. Simultaneously, the unique practice of alternating paddy and upland cultivation significantly influenced the biochemical pathways of straw degradation by altering soil redox conditions. In this study, the decomposition rate in August was only 3.16%, while the decomposition rate in September increased to 15.89%. This results from the differences in chemical composition and structure of the straw itself during various decomposition stages, as well as the succession of electron acceptors and microbial metabolic pathways driven by dramatic shifts in soil Eh. In anaerobic paddy field environments, the decrease in soil Eh leads to the replacement of aerobic decomposition by anaerobic fermentation using cellulose and hemicellulose as substrates as the primary decomposition pathway. This drives methanogens and other microorganisms to utilize intermediate products such as H₂/CO₂ or acetate to produce methane, which may explain the enrichment of the methane metabolism pathway in KEGG analysis [14, 79]. However, under anaerobic conditions, the depolymerization of lignin is severely inhibited due to its dependence on strong oxidants [80, 81]. Consequently, as the more readily degradable cellulose and hemicellulose are broken down, the remaining components—such as lignin, which is more resistant to anaerobic degradation—result in an overall lower straw decomposition rate by late August. When the paddy field transitions to upland conditions in September, the soil Eh increases, and oxygen becomes the primary electron acceptor [14]. This shift activates aerobic and facultative anaerobic microorganisms, which produce ligninases such as peroxidases and laccases, thereby accelerating the depolymerization and decomposition of recalcitrant lignin [80]. This likely accounts for the observed enrichment of aromatic compound and aminobenzoate degradation pathways in KEGG during this phase. The further depolymerization and decomposition of lignin subsequently enhance the overall straw decomposition rate. This shift in biochemical pathways aligns with metagenomic data showing a decrease in the abundance of cellulase/hemicellulase-related CAZy families and an increase in ligninase-related families between August and September.

In this study, the richness of bacterial and fungal communities in paddy field soil was higher during the paddy field phase than during the upland field phase, which was related to differences in environmental factors. On one hand, the temperatures during the paddy field phase from May to August were generally higher than during the upland field phase; on the other hand, during the paddy field phase, the paddy fields were in a completely submerged environment with high soil moisture content and low oxygen levels. Yuan et al. [82] indicated that the OTU richness of bacteria and fungi in paddy soil was positively correlated with soil moisture. The Shannon index of bacterial community showed no significant difference from March to September, but the Shannon index of fungal community was significantly higher in May than in other months, indicating that as temperatures rise and the transition from upland field to paddy field occurs, the evenness of fungal communities gradually increases, but as the duration of submersion extends, their community evenness decreases again.

β-diversity analysis confirms that alternating paddy and upland conditions are the primary driver of significant monthly variations in microbial community composition. The underlying cause is the community succession process triggered by drastic fluctuations in soil oxygen levels. Noll et al. [83] have shown that after paddy soil was submerged in water for 6 h, no oxygen can be detected below a soil depth of 2 mm, and anaerobic bacteria such as Clostridium rapidly proliferated to become dominant groups. After the paddy field was flooded in May, the soil rapidly became oxygen-deficient. This led to an increase in the relative abundance of anaerobic or facultative anaerobic groups, such as Clostridium, Trichococcus, and Bacillus [84–86], which subsequently became the dominant groups, while aerobic groups were suppressed. Conversely, after the paddy field reverted to upland field in September, oxygen diffusion resumed, and the abundance of aerobic groups recovered.

The periodic succession of microbial community structure reflects the adaptive strategies of microbial communities to environmental disturbances. This succession also reveals that during the alternating upland and paddy field processes in paddy ecosystems, the selective succession of functional groups maintains the stability of key ecological processes, ensuring the continuous decomposition of rice straw in the fields. Meanwhile, taxa such as Pseudarthrobacter and Mortierella maintained a relative abundance exceeding 4% in all months across both the paddy and upland field phases. These taxa have been reported in the literature or determined through our assays to possess more than one type of lignocellulose-degrading enzyme activity, identifying them as core members of the lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia. Member of Pseudarthrobacter isolated from paddy fields has been reported to possess lignocellulose degradation activity and is key group in the decomposition process of rice straw [18]. These groups are not only adapted to the anaerobic conditions of paddy fields but also function under aerobic upland fields. This provides the rice paddy ecosystem with important functional redundancy, ensuring the stability of ecosystem functions such as carbon cycling amidst drastic environmental changes [87].

Based on the identification of high-abundance microbial groups in straw incorporation soils, this study specifically proposed soil lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia by combining literature analysis, microbial isolation, and enzymatic activity assays. This provides a more precise perspective for directly linking specific taxa with decomposition functions. Temperature and alternating paddy and upland conditions ultimately affect the in situ decomposition rate of rice straw by regulating the abundance and activity of this functional microbial group.

This regulatory mechanism is manifested in two aspects: biochemical selection of metabolic pathways and ecological selection of functional microbial groups. During the warm, flooded anaerobic phase of paddy fields, the soil Eh is notably lower than that in the upland field phase [14]. Most dominant lignocellulose-degrading microbial genera are anaerobic or facultatively anaerobic [84–86]. Among them, microorganisms such as those in the genus Clostridium can produce cellulosomes to effectively degrade cellulose and hemicellulose [5]. However, lignin decomposition is suppressed at this phase [81]. Decomposition is primarily driven by anaerobic fermentation and methanogenesis pathways [14, 79]. When the paddy fields revert to upland fields in September, the majority of dominant cellulose-degrading microbial genera are aerobic [88–93]. At this phase, oxygen serves as an electron acceptor, activating the aerobic lignin biodegradation pathway centered on free radical reactions [14, 80]. Consequently, the abundance of relevant microorganisms and associated enzyme genes increases. This sequential activation of “anaerobic-aerobic” metabolic pathways represents a biochemical adaptation to the decomposition of complex straw components. Different microbial groups, equipped with specialized enzyme systems, function within their respective optimal redox windows, forming a functional relay and synergy. In paddy ecosystems, this selective succession pattern of lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia, shaped by climatic characteristics and farming practices, is crucial for maintaining the stability of straw decomposition under alternating paddy and upland conditions.

It is worth noting that for some taxa within the lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia, no isolates have yet been obtained to analyze the composition and activity of their lignocellulose-degrading enzyme systems. The reported lignocellulose-degrading enzyme activities in the literature may not be comprehensive, which in turn may lead to an incomplete understanding of the functional succession of lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia. Meanwhile, this study employed an OTU clustering method based on 97% similarity. It should be noted that this method offers relatively limited resolution in current high-throughput sequencing data analysis. It may obscure ecological functional differences among certain strains at the species level and affect the accurate detection of low-abundance taxa, thereby limiting, to some extent, the resolution of taxonomic details and the accuracy of functional predictions. Furthermore, amplicon sequencing has revealed high abundances of norank and unclassified taxa in the soil, and it remains to be verified whether these taxa possess lignocellulose-degrading activity. In the future, more culture media and conditions should be explored to isolate and cultivate more strains from lignocellulose-degrading microbial communities and to analyze their enzyme activities. This will enable a more comprehensive and accurate understanding of the composition and functional succession of lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms during the decomposition of straw.

Analyses based on the KEGG and CAZy databases provided potential genetic functional explanations for the aforementioned eco-biochemical processes. It is hypothesized that the enriched methane metabolism pathways during the paddy field phase are linked to the terminal methanogenesis process resulting from cellulose/hemicellulose degradation under anaerobic conditions, whereas the enriched aromatic compounds and aminobenzoate degradation pathways during the upland phase are associated with the aerobic metabolism of lignin and its derivatives [14, 79, 80, 94–96]. The CAZy gene profile indicates that cellulase and hemicellulase genes were more abundant during the paddy field phase, while ligninase genes were more abundant during the upland field phase. This suggests that the redox cycling driven by alternating paddy and upland conditions guides a “division of labor” in catabolism at the gene expression level: the paddy field phase primarily accomplishes the initial depolymerization and anaerobic transformation of cellulose/hemicellulose, while the upland field phase focuses on the aerobic depolymerization of lignin and further mineralization of its aromatic products.

Overall, the community structure and function of the lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia exhibited a regular succession pattern in response to the alternation of paddy and upland conditions. During the low-temperature upland field phase (March-April), although aerobic taxa were predominant, their decomposition activity was constrained by low temperatures. In the warm anaerobic paddy field phase (May-August), with increasing temperature and the shift to anaerobic conditions, anaerobic/facultative anaerobic taxa became the dominant groups. At the functional gene level, this was characterized by an increase in the abundance of cellulase/hemicellulase genes and a decrease in ligninase genes, with this phase primarily involving the degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose. During the aerobic upland field phase (September), the abundance of aerobic taxa increased, accompanied by a corresponding rise in ligninase gene abundance, facilitating the aerobic depolymerization and mineralization of lignin and its aromatic byproducts.

In contrast to previous studies that focused on the effects of environmental factors or agricultural practices on in situ rice straw decomposition efficiency and soil microbial communities in paddy fields, or those examining the impacts of long-term rice straw return to field experiments on soil microbial communities, this study investigates the in situ decomposition patterns of rice straw and the succession dynamics of lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms and their associated functions across different phases within a complete decomposition cycle involving “upland-paddy-upland” transitions. The findings reveal how such regular successions drive the patterns and rates of straw decomposition. Moreover, by integrating literature mining with enzymatic activity validation, this study specifically proposed functional soil lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia, thereby providing a more precise perspective for directly linking specific microbial taxa to their decomposition functions.

Conclusions

The in situ decomposition mechanisms of straw in paddy fields within the black soil region of Northeast China were elucidated by analyzing the composition and functional succession of lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia. Straw decomposition predominantly occurred from May to September, coinciding with warmer temperatures. The alternating paddy-upland field patterns and temperature fluctuations significantly influenced the diversity and function of soil microbial communities, thereby impacting straw decomposition. A distinct succession of lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia and lignocellulose-degrading genes was observed between the paddy field and upland field phase. During the paddy field phase (May-August), Pseudarthrobacter, Bacillus, Nocardioides, Tausonia, Mortierella, Pseudeurotium, etc. were the dominant microbial taxa driving straw decomposition. Tausonia, Mortierella and Pseudeurotium primarily contributed to cellulose degradation, while Bacillus and Nocardioides were involved in the breakdown of both cellulose and hemicellulose. In contrast, Pseudarthrobacter played a major role in lignin degradation. This phase was characterized by a high abundance of cellulase and hemicellulase genes, indicating that decomposition primarily targets these components. During the upland field phase in September, Bacillus, Bradyrhizobium, Mortierella, Mrakia, Pseudeurotium, etc. became the predominant decomposers. Mortierella and Mrakia contributed to straw lignocellulose degradation, while Bacillus and Pseudeurotium played a key role in the breakdown of cellulose and hemicellulose. In contrast, Bradyrhizobium was predominantly involved in lignin degradation. This phase exhibited a higher abundance of ligninase genes, suggesting a shift towards lignin degradation as the dominant process. The sequential activity of these dominant microbial groups and their lignocellulose-degrading enzyme genes ensures the progressive decomposition of straw. The elucidation of these decomposition dynamics provides a theoretical foundation for the identification of key lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms active at different phases, providing a theoretical basis for improving the efficiency of rice straw return to fields.
