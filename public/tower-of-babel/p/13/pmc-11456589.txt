Trajectories and predictors of suicidal ideation in clinical characteristics and pharmacological treatments for major depressive disorder: a study based on a national multi-centered prospective cohort
Authors: Ruoxi Ding, Xuequan Zhu, Lei Feng, Le Xiao, Ling Zhang, Ping He, Gang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Suicidal ideation (SI) is a significant precursor and risk marker for suicide behaviors in major depressive disorder (MDD). Exploration of SI trajectory from a longitudinal framework are essential for treatment guidelines and clinical management of suicide risk. This study sought to explore SI trajectories and its associated clinical, sociodemographic characteristics, and initial treatment among patients with MDD. We used data from a non-interventional, national multi-centered prospective cohort study. 1 461 patients with MDD were included in the growth mixture modeling using SI at baseline, 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 8 weeks, 12 weeks, and 6 months, 9 months, and 12 months as the indicator. A multinomial regression was employed with SI trajectory as the outcome and anhedonia, depressive symptoms, atypical depressive symptoms, pharmacological treatments, and other covariates as the predictors. Four distinct SI trajectories were identified: a consistently low SI trajectory(50.7%), a persistently mild SI trajectory(20.6%), a fast declined SI trajectory(8.9%), and a slowly declined trajectory(19.8%). Compared to those with a consistently low SI trajectory, a higher score of anhedonia was associated with an increased risk of experiencing persistently mild (RRR = 1.20, 95%CI: 1.05, 1.38) and slowly declined SI (1.54, 95%CI: 1.32, 1.80). Severity of depressive symptom was also positively associated with the risk of experiencing persistently mild (1.15, 95%CI: 1.13, 1.18) and slowly declined SI (1.17, 95%CI: 1.14, 1.21). And the risk of experiencing slowly declined SI was higher for those use SSRI(1.49, 95%CI: 1.02, 2.31), and for those use antidepressant and antipsychotic/mood stabilizer combined therapy (3.78, 95%CI: 1.48, 9.61). The findings of this study are potentially useful for clinical practice as critical indicators of profiles and interventions for prognosis among patients with MDD. Further research is warranted to explore potential modifiable factors and the association between SI trajectories and suicide behavior.

Introduction

Suicide has long been a devastating public health concern. It is one of the global leading causes of death and injury—the overall global death toll from suicide was about one million per year, which indicating that one suicide death occurred every 40 s [1]. Suicide is frequently paired with mental health problems, of which major depressive disorder (MDD) remains the most commonly reported conditions associated with suicidal ideation (SI) and suicidal behaviors [2]. Large epidemiological studies have linked suicidal thinking and non-fatal suicide attempts with the presence of depressive symptoms [3, 4], and psychological autopsy showed that ~60% of people died by suicide have depressive disorders [5].

SI appears to precede the decision of suicide acts [6]. More than half of the patients with major depressive disorders experienced SI and 95% of those who attempted suicide had reported SI [7]. A study based on 17 countries showed that 60% of transitions from suicidal ideation to plan or attempts in depression occur within the first year after ideation onset [8, 9]. Though only a minority (<10%) of SI actually progress to suicidal attempts, SI remains a significant precursor and risk marker for suicide behaviors [6]. A better understanding of the pattern and the predictors of SI for patients with MDD may help elucidate the link between depressive symptoms, suicidal thinking, and attempts, which is essential for the clinical management of suicide risk [10].

However, SI is a heterogeneous phenomenon, and little is known about the relation between depressive symptoms and the emergence, as well as the change of SI, which requires exploration of SI trajectory from a longitudinal framework [11]. Recent research indicated that short-term SI trajectories were related to demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as severity of depressive symptoms [12, 13]. Nevertheless, most of existing few studies on SI trajectory were focused on a relatively short period, or only based on adolescent populations. Togerther with the black box warning of antidepressants for children and adolescents, as well as the remained heterogeneity of findings among adults with MDD [14, 15], it further underlines that such investigation is in urgent need for treatment guidelines and prevention for future suicides in depression.

This study sought to explore SI trajectories over 1-year period from a descriptive perspective, and to identify associated clinical, sociodemographic characteristics, and initial treatment among patients with MDD based on a non-interventional, prospective cohort study.We employed growth mixture modeling analysis to identify unique latent trajectories of SI while adjusting for individual variability over time. We hypothesized that four types of SI trajectories would be identified: consistently low SI, fast declined SI, slowly declined or consistently high SI, and fluctuating SI or increased SI. And we also hypothesized that different aspects of depressive symptoms, different types of antidepressant treatment would be associated with the trajectories of SI. The result of this study is expected to provide a predictive pattern for the clinical management of suicide risk among MDD patients.

Methods

Participants

We used data from The Prospective Research to examine the Effect of Initial Treatment choice for depressive episodes (PREIT) study, which was an epidemiological, non-interventional, prospective, cohort study that conducted at 27 sites (6 secondary and 11 tertiary psychiatric hospitals, and 10 tertiary general hospitals) in 19 cities of China mainland between August 2020 to December 2022. Participants were Chinese outpatients or inpatients with depressive episodes, recurrent depressive disorder aged 18 years or above, with a diagnosis of depressive episodes, recurrent depressive disorder based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) and The International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD-10), and have not initiated treatment for the current depressive episode. Depressive symptoms were assessed by 30-item Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology-Self-Report (IDS-SR30) (total score ≥26). Exclusion criteria included interventional clinical trials participation within 3 months, low adherence determined by researcher or level of education below elementary school. Patients in this cohort were observed for 12 months after the baseline investigation. They were followed-up at 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 8 weeks, 12 weeks, and 6 months, 9 months, and 12 months with usual clinical practice. All the information at baseline was collected by face-to-face interview, and the follow-up measures were collected by patients’self-report using Wechat application in cell phone.

All patients provided written informed consent for participation. The PREIT study was in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practices guidelines and with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by Ethics Committee of Beijing Anding Hospital, Capital Medical University (No.2019-90).

Measures

Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology-Self-Report (IDS-SR30) is a 30-item, self-reported rating scale of depressive symptoms, which was measured at baseline and each point of the follow-up period. According to previous literature using IDS-SR30 [2, 16], we defined anhedonia based on the aggregated score of item 21(Pleasure/enjoyment) and 22 (Sexual interest) in IDS-SR30; and atypical depressive symptoms was defined based in threshold rating criteria of specific IDS item 4 (hypersomnia), 8 (reactivity of mood), 12(increased appetite), 14(weight gain), 29 (interpersonal sensitivity) and 30 (leaden paralysis). The aggregated score of rest items (to avoid overlap with outcome, item 18 was excluded) were employed as the variable of severity of depressive symptoms.

Concise Health Risk Tracking scale: a brief self-report is a 7-item self-rating scale (CHRT-SR) that systematically assesses both suicidal thinking and associated thoughts that may indicate the propensity for suicidal attempts [17]. CHRT-SR was measured at baseline and each point of the follow-up period. Suicidal ideation was defined by the item “I have been having thoughts about how I might kill myself”, with those who strongly agree or agree being categorized as having suicidal ideation (=1), and those who strongly disagree, disagree or neither agree nor disagree being categorized as not having suicidal ideation (=0).

The initial pharmacological treatment of the depressive episode in this cohort were recorded at baseline and each point of the follow-up period. It was further categorized as antidepressant non-use, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), selective serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI), other antidepressant, and antidepressant & antipsychotic/ mood stabilizer.

Sociodemographic characteristics included age as continuous variable, gender (male or female), education (junior high or below, senior high or junior college or university or above), income (low, medium or high) and residence (urban or rural). Besides depressive symptoms, clinical characteristics also included episodes (first episode or recurrent). Health behaviors included smoking (never, quit, ≤10 cigarettes per day or >10 cigarettes per day), and drinking (never, quit, moderate or heavy drinker).

Statistical analysis

We conducted descriptive statistics to report the distribution in socio-demographic, clinical characteristics, pharmacological treatment and SI at baseline. Growth mixture modeling analysis was employed to determine a best-fit latent class model using SI at baseline and follow-up period. Models with increasing number of classes were compared using statistical (the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), and the sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC) as well as the Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and the boots-trapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT)), substantive (entropy) and empirical criteria (enough participants (>5%) occupied each class) to determine the best-fitting model. Each participant’s most likely class of SI trajectory were calculated. Then, a multinomial regression was applied, with the classes of SI trajectory as the outcome variable. The predictors were all from baseline survey, including socio-demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, pharmacological treatment and health behavior. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 16.0 for Mac (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

In total, 1 698 participants had at least one IDS-SR30 measurement reported during the 12-months study interval and were included in growth curve modeling to determine SI trajectories. Of these, 1 406 (82.8%) had no missing values in covariates and were included in the multinomial regression analysis comparing SI trajectories. Among these completed cases, the mean age was 32.4 years (SD:11.3), 67.5% were female, 54.3% have education attainment of university or above, 79.5% lived in urban area, and 75.5% were first-episode depression. For pharmacologicl treatment, 50.1% of patients used SSRI, and 34.6% were not using any antidepressants. A substantial proportion of patients (75.5%) had atypical depressive symptoms, and the mean score of anhedonia was 3.0 (SD:1.7). More than 40% of patients with MDD reported suicidal ideation at baseline. Discussion

The current study investigated 1-year longitudinal trajectories of SI and its association with clinical and treatment characteristics among a large sample of patients with MDD. Contrary to previous studies focused on adolescent sample, used limited data points in a short-interval, or left the association with initial treatment unexplored, the present study included eight data points over the course of 1 year and with an all-age-group sample of adults with MDD. Overall, we identified four distinct SI trajectories: a consistently low SI trajectory, a persistently mild SI trajectory, a fastly declined SI trajectory, and a slowly declined trajectory. Compared to those with a consistently low SI trajectory, those with a more severe depressive symptoms or a higher anhedonia score were more likely to experience poor SI trajectories (persistent mild or slowly declined), and those use SSRI or combined therapy of antidepressant and antipsychotic/mood stabilizer were more likely to experience slowly declined SI.

The four types of SI trajectories identified by our analysis are similar to those found in previous studies, and three of the trajectories were close to our hypothesis: a consistently low SI (51.1%), a fastly declined SI (8.8%) and a slowly declined SI (19.9%). Research based on the Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR*D) study also identified four trajectories, with three of them (little to no SI, improving SI, and persistent SI) were consistent with our results [2]. Notably, instead of the variable or increased SI trajectory that identified in most of other studies, we found 20.3% of our sample were classified into the group of a persistently mild SI trajectory. One possible explanation is that the proportion of variable or increased SI trajectory was relatively minor since more than 75% of the participants were outpatients, whose symptoms are relatively mild and moderate. On the other hand, persistently mild SI should also be considered as high risk as slowly declined SI. One study based on military service members indicated that persistently mild (moderate-stable) and slowly declined (high-table) trajectory were associated with greater odds of following suicidal behavior, in comparison of consistently low (low-stable) trajectory [13]. It has been suggested that the persistence of SI was strongly related to the poor response to antidepressants and the low improvement of depression [18], which echoes with our supplementary analysis—the initial depressive symptoms were more severe among persistently mild SI and slowly declined SI group than other two groups. The relatively large proportion (>40%) of persistently mild SI and slowly declined SI from our analysis further highlights the potential risk of suicide among patients with MDD.

Several sociodemographic characteristics were found to be associated with SI trajectories. First, age was negatively associated with the possibility of experiencing fastly decline and slowly declined SI trajectories. Suicidal ideations are comparatively more usual in younger adults in comparison to the older group since elderly people in general were less likely to experience tiredness of life, death wishes or suicidal thoughts [19]. Consistently with our expectation, socioeconomic status were also correlated with SI trajectories. Compared to those with consistently low SI trajectory, patients with higher education (senior high or above) were less likely to experience fastly declined SI. And high income was associated with lower possibility to experience persistently mild and slowly declined SI trajectory than low-income group. The inverse association between education, income and suicidal ideation has been suggested by previous studies [20–22], high educational attainment and financial status often denote high self-esteem and social support, and thus act as protective factors for experiencing high degree of suicidal ideation. The finding indicated that socioeconomic disadvantages may put younger adults with MDD at worse situation of suicide-related outcome.

Different aspects of depressive symptoms are associated with SI trajectory. Patients with more severe anhedonia were more likely to experience persistently mild or slowly declined SI trajectory. Anhedonia has been recognized as a feature of treatment-resistance and a risk factor for suicidal attempts in MDD, and the association between anhedonia and SI trajectory could be explained from different perspectives [23]. Psychological research suggested that people who feel less pleasure have a tendency to focus on efforts to avoid psychological pain, which may contribute to the increased suicidal ideation, as the part of the experiential avoidance strategies [24]. Neurobiological hypothesis argues that motivational anhedonia was linked to decreased dopamine release in the striatal circuit, which was also correlated to suicidal thoughts and behaviors [23, 25]. Besides, as we expected, depressive symptoms were positively associated with the possibility of experiencing persistently mild or slow declined SI [26]. Such significant associations identified from our analysis suggests the treatment or intervention targeted anhedonia and typical depressive symptom are critical for suicidal prevention.

Regarding the association between pharmacological treatment and SI trajectories, we found that the initiation of SSRI failed to decrease the risk of experiencing disadvantaged trajectory of SI (persistently mild or slowly declined SI). Though most of the antidepressants (predominantly SSRI) have shown a substantial effect on reducing the severity of depressive symptoms, its side effects, including emotional blunting, detachment, akathisia, and agitation, as well as the consequent treatment discontinutation [15, 27, 28], may counteract the benefits from improvement of symptoms and function, and thus weaken the positive impact on suicidal outcomes. On the other side, the possibility of non-causal association should be noted. SSRI was mainly prescribed to individuals with modeate to severe depressive symptoms, which was originally a marker of less-favorable disease progression over follow-up. The combination of antidepressant and antipsychotic or mood stabilizer were associated with increased risk of experiencing slowly declined SI. This is contrary to previous inference, which suggested that antipsychotic agents or mood stabilizer have anti-suicidal effect in mood disorders, including reducing overstimulation and lower the risk of inducing agitation and mood switches [29–31]. Similar to SSRI, combined pharmacological therapy is normally prescribed to enhance the treatment effects for patients with severe symptoms or with the poor response to antidepressant monotherapy [32, 33]. The users of combined therapy may have poor prognosis with high emergence of suicidal thoughts and behavior at the beginning. Our results indicated that suicidal ideation is relatively persistent and heterogeneous, and the use of adjunctive mood stabilizer or antipsychotic was not associated with increased efficacy of SI reduction and even negatively affect the trajectory of suicide-related outcomes. Therefore, pharmacological treatment may only play minor role in improving the SI among patients with MDD. Other treatment, such as psychotherapy and physical therapy, should be incorporated to achieve better treatment outcome. Further research from neurobiological perspective is needed to examine the mechanism of the association between mood-stabilizer or antipsychotic agents and the emergence and change of suicide thoughts and attempts.

Our findings have certain clinical and public health implications. First, the distinct trajectories of SI, as well as its significant predictors indicated the necessity of identification of patients with different sociodemographic and clinical characteristics to employ tailored therapy for optimized treatment outcome. Second, the findings were based on a national multi-centered cohort, which to a certain degree, could represent the general profile of SI among patients with MDD in China. From the perspective of public health, considering the established significant association between SI and suicidal attempts, the substantial proportion of subjects with persistent SI in our study underlines the potential burden of depressive disorders, and further highlights the urgent need of policies and mental health resources for suicide prevention.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, our sample might have been insufficient to describe all potential trajectories of SI since the cohort data we employed was not designed to investigate suicide-related outcome. Second, due to the lack of information in this cohort, we failed to include family history of suicide behavior, which has been identified as a putative predictor variable. Third, similarly, the absence of data excludes the possibility to assess the association between SI trajectories and the risk of suicidal attempts during the follow-up. However, the positive association of persisitently mild and slowly declined SI trajectory with suicidal plan from the supplementary analyses could partially compensate this limitation (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, the baseline survey failed to collect the information of other types of treatment (e.g., psychotherapy, transcranial magnetic stimulation), which may also be one of the predictors of subsequent SI trajectories. Additionally, the study design prevents us to examine the causality, and we were unable to analyze the potential impact of factors like treatment adherence or social support on the trajectory of SI. Last but not the least, 17% of the subjects at baseline was lost to follow-up, which may resulted in statistical bias of our analysis. Nevertheless, the bias was minor since the distribution of baseline characteristics of missing cases was roughly similar with that among complete cases (Supplementary Table S3). Despite of the above limitations, this study has worth-mentioning strength, includes a relatively long follow-up time with multiple data points, a sample of adults with MDD across all age groups, and the exploration of the association between pharmacological treatment and SI trajectory.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings delineate four class of patients with MDD regarding SI trajectory in 1 year: consistently low SI, persistently mild SI, fast declined SI and slowly declined SI. And we found SI trajectories differed based on sociodemographic characteristics, such as age and income, as well as clinical symptoms including anhedonia and typical depressive symptoms, and pharmacological treatment, such as the single use or adjunctive use of mood stabilizer or antipsychotic agents. These findings may be useful for clinical practice as critical indicators of profiles and intervention for prognosis. Further studies are warranted to explore potential modifiable factors of worse SI trajectory and to investigate associations between SI trajectories and suicide-related outcomes, such as attempts or death.
