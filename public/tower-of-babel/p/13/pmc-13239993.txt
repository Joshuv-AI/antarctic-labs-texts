Prevalence and Associated Risk Factors for Anxiety, Depression, and Insomnia Among University Students in Bangladesh: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Faiza Fairuz Muskan, Mehzabeen Ifty, Umaira Abdul Khaleque, Asef Raj, Mohiuddin Ahmed Bhuiyan, Md Rabiul Islam, Eva Rahman Kabir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundUniversity students face substantial mental‐health challenges that impair learning, sleep, and overall quality of life and well‐being. This study aimed to assess the mental health status and associated risk factors of students in Bangladesh to prevent mental health disorders and provide better healthcare support.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional, self‐administered survey between September 2023 and February 2024 across ten universities. Standardized tools (GAD‐7, PHQ‐9, AIS‐8) were used to measure anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia. Associations with demographic and health factors were analyzed using binary logistic regression.ResultsIn total, 952 undergraduate and postgraduate students from ten universities in Bangladesh participated in the study. The mean age was 22.3 years, with 51% males and 49% females. The prevalence of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia among the students in Bangladesh was 70.3%, 49.1%, and 61.7%, respectively. Anxiety symptoms were higher in females than males (OR 1.681; 95% CI 1.126–2.509; p = 0.011), higher in married than unmarried students (OR 1.344; p = 0.003), and higher in those with chronic disease (OR 0.332 for no disease vs disease; p = 0.002), while students with siblings had lower odds (OR 0.555; p = 0.045); anxiety also varied by residence area (p = 0.002). Depressive symptoms were more frequent in females (p = 0.038), decreased with higher BMI (OR 0.593; p = 0.029), and was higher in students with chronic disease (OR 0.544 for no disease vs disease; p = 0.012); depressive symptoms also differed by economic status (p = 0.018), residence area (p = 0.007), and number of siblings (p = 0.027). Insomnia was more common in females (OR 1.685; p = 0.038), higher among married students (OR 2.171; p = 0.022), and higher in those without siblings (OR 1.798; p = 0.039). Students with normal‐range BMI had lower odds of insomnia compared to those with higher BMI (OR 0.684; p = 0.006). Insomnia was also associated with smoking (p = 0.029), chronic disease (p < 0.001), and parental relationship status (p = 0.032). We also observed positive correlations among anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia symptoms.ConclusionThe findings reveal that Bangladeshi university students experience alarmingly high rates of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia, with female, married, and chronically ill students particularly vulnerable. Protective factors such as having siblings and a higher BMI were identified. From a clinical perspective, the results underscore the value of early and regular prescreening in university settings, combined with intervention studies to evaluate preventive and therapeutic strategies. Universities and policymakers should prioritize accessible counseling, awareness campaigns, and early screening programs tailored to the demographic risk groups identified in this study.Keywords: anxiety, depression, insomnia, mental health, risk factors, students, young adults

Introduction

University life is one of the most physically and psychologically challenging phases of a person's academic career. During this transitional period, students experience hormonal and psychological changes while developing the capacity to cope with societal and academic challenges associated with preparing for professional life. This immense change can result in crises like loneliness, self‐doubt, social retreat, and low self‐esteem [1]. Students also face multiple stressors, including academic workload, living away from home, financial constraints, social pressures, and broader societal concerns [2, 3, 4]. Universities worldwide have reported a concerning increase in mental health disorders among students, with prevalence rates exceeding those of the general population by 12.9% [2, 5, 6]. In the United States, nearly 44% of college students reported depressive symptoms, and 37% reported anxiety in the 2021–22 Healthy Minds survey, reflecting a heavy burden even in high‐income settings [7]. Students also face significant pressure to perform well in academic assessments, including tests, quizzes, and managing tight class schedules. This can lead to poor time management, sleep deprivation, and, eventually, sleep disorders [8].

The detrimental consequences of mental health disorders render the students more susceptible to issues such as learning problems, substance misuse, and poor performance in schoolwork [9]. They become vulnerable to substance abuse involving psychoactive agents like tobacco and alcohol, which disrupt normal physiological functioning. Importantly, epidemiological evidence consistently associates these substances with increased risks of anxiety, depression, and various other mental health conditions. According to Arifin et al., university students from low‐income families or those experiencing monetary challenges are more inclined to suffer from depression, anxiety, and stress. The results show that 68.9% of the participants experienced moderate to high psychological discomfort, 72.7% had anxiety, and 60.6% were depressed [8].

Another survey conducted on the students in Sialkot, Pakistan, reported that the rates of occurrence of depression were 75% and anxiety were 88.4% [10]. Students suffering from depression and anxiety frequently confront challenges with interpersonal skills, leading to diminished enthusiasm for both academic and personal activities, and sleep disorders [11]. Empirical studies have demonstrated significant gender disparities in mental health prevalence rates. One investigation found that 13% of the participants reported insomnia symptoms, with women showing significantly higher susceptibility than men [12]. A Norwegian study on college and university students revealed markedly higher rates of both generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) (females 16.0% vs males 8.2%) and major depressive disorder (MDD) (females 17.1% vs males 10.8%) among female students. A nationwide survey of data collected from on‐campus mental health services in Canadian college and university students found that 86% of respondents were depressed, 95% were overwhelmed and weary, 81% felt lonely, and 83.7% had anxiety [2]. Another research carried out across multiple South Asian countries determined that a substantial portion of university students, with estimates varying from 35.4% to 70%, experienced insomnia [13].

In Bangladesh, mental health disorders continue to be an immensely neglected and stigmatized concern within the larger population [14]. Among the first‐year medical students in a public medical college in Bangladesh, depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms were reported in 54.3%, 64.8%, and 59.0% of students, respectively [15]. In Bangladesh, approximately 7 million people suffer from depressive disorders, with a recorded prevalence rate of up to 54.3%. Among the female university students in Bangladesh, the incidences of anxiety, loneliness, and depressive symptoms were estimated to be 69.18%, 55.88%, and 45.23%, respectively [14]. Another study revealed that among students, 84.7% suffered from moderate to severe depression, 83.3% from moderate to severe anxiety, and 76.5% from moderate to severe insomnia [16].

The stigma surrounding mental health disorders constitutes both a public health concern and a violation of fundamental human rights in low‐ and middle‐income countries like Bangladesh [17]. The inadequate provision of mental healthcare in Bangladesh can be ascribed to numerous factors, including the lack of public mental health infrastructure, inadequate financial resources, a scarcity of proficient mental health professionals, ineffective execution of mental health policies, and societal discrimination [18]. University students are particularly susceptible to developing symptoms associated with mental health disorders, primarily due to the rigorous academic demands and the ambiguity concerning their possible future paths [19]. Due to this negligence in mental health care, it has become a significant problem among the students [20]. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the key reasons and the underlying causes of mental health disorders. Additionally, due to the lack of understanding and social stigma, it has become a concerning matter for the growing young adults in Bangladesh [21]. Therefore, the goal of the current study was to investigate the prevalence of mental health disorders (anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia) and their contributing factors among Bangladeshi university students. This study primarily analyses the severity of the mental health problems and evaluates the social and demographic variables such as age, sex, living status, financial situations, and tries to observe any association that could contribute to the prevalence of symptoms of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia among students.

In line with prior literature, we selected predictors that have plausible links with student mental health. Family structure and peer support may shape coping resources, and sibling status has been associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms in college samples [22]. Relationship context is also relevant, as large cross‐country evidence shows higher odds of depressive symptoms among unmarried individuals compared with married peers [23]. Physical health indicators such as BMI can relate to mental health, with evidence suggesting elevated depression risk at non‐healthy BMI levels, supporting its inclusion as a predictor [24]. Health behaviors were also considered because smoking has been linked to depression and related psychiatric outcomes, and cohort evidence indicates smoking increases the incidence of insomnia [25, 26]. Based on this evidence, we hypothesized that female students, those with chronic illnesses, and individuals from lower socioeconomic groups would be more likely to experience symptoms of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia. We also anticipated that factors such as the number of siblings and healthier BMI levels would act as protective influences. This study contributes uniquely to the current literature by offering multi‐institutional, in‐person data from Bangladesh, thereby expanding the evidence base on student mental health in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Our findings are expected to add new context‐specific insights that can guide tailored interventions and policy strategies to address mental‐health burdens among university students.

Methods

Participants

The study was carried out between September 1, 2023, and February 28, 2024, among the university students in Bangladesh. We applied a stratified sampling technique and interviewed a representative sample of university students from different universities in Bangladesh. The present study collected data from 10 randomly selected universities (5 public and 5 private) maintaining urban‐rural demographic ratios in Bangladesh. Before data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants to ensure that they fully understood the study's objectives, procedures, and eligibility criteria. The inclusion criteria for participation required individuals to be current university students in Bangladesh, aged between 18 and 30 years, without any prior clinical diagnosis of a mental health condition, willing to voluntarily participate, and capable of providing written informed consent. Students who reported a prior clinical diagnosis of any mental disorder were excluded based on a self‐reported screening item at the beginning of the questionnaire. Additional exclusion criteria included incomplete or insufficient survey responses.

Assessment Parameters

Sociodemographic Information

The survey questionnaire consisted of two sections where the first section of the survey consisted of the consent form and demographic questions, comprising a total of 16 questions including age, sex, BMI, marital status, educational level, academic result, family income, family type, parent's status (separated or not separated), sibling numbers, living status (with or without family), relationship status, smoking habit, residence area and presence of any chronic illness. The second section of the questionnaire consisted of 24 questions based on different psychometric assessment scales commonly employed in scientific research and self‐assessment. We utilized psychometric instruments such as GAD‐7, PHQ‐9, and AIS‐8 to assess symptoms of generalized anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia, respectively.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD‐7)

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD‐7) was used to measure the level of severity of GAD symptoms. It consists of seven criteria that rate the intensity of various symptoms. Respondents respond to each question based on the degree of recurrence on a scale with four points, ranging from “Not at all“ to “Nearly every day,“ with scores ranging from 0 to 3. Scores of four or below indicate normal anxiety, a range of 5–9 signifies mild anxiety, 10–14 specifies a moderate level of anxiety, while scores of 15 or above indicate a severe level of anxiety [27].

Depressive Symptoms (PHQ‐9)

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐9), a 9‐item self‐reported tool, was used to identify and grade the severity of depression, which is based on DSM‐5's nine diagnostic criteria for MDD. Scores of 10 or greater are considered elevated and closely correlate with a diagnosis of major depression; nevertheless, the final diagnosis must be made by a competent therapist. Scores below 10 indicate no depression, a range of 10–15 signifies mild depression, 16–21 specifies a moderate level of depression, while scores of 22 or above indicate a severe level of depression [28].

Insomnia (AIS‐8)

Insomnia symptoms were measured with the 8‐item Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS‐8), which is based on the diagnostic criteria by the ICD‐10 for insomnia. Each item has 4 points ranging from 0 to 3, which shows the insomnia severity from none to severe levels. To get the total score of an individual, the scores on each item of AIS‐8 were added (score 0–24). The total score was then categorized into four levels: cumulative scores < 6, 6–14, 15–21, and 22 or greater for no, mild, moderate, and severe insomnia, respectively [29].

The survey questionnaire's reliability was assessed by utilizing Cronbach's Alpha test, which yielded a score of 0.894, 0.915, 0.883, and 0.906 for GAD‐7, PHQ‐9, AIS‐8 scales, and the whole questionnaire, respectively. This α score indicates that the survey questions were reliable.

Procedure

Universities were selected using a stratified approach to ensure representation across institutional type and geographic setting. First, we created a sampling frame of eligible universities in Bangladesh and stratified them by public versus private and urban versus rural location. From each stratum, universities were selected using simple random selection, resulting in 10 participating universities (5 public and 5 private) and preserving the intended urban–rural balance. To avoid institution‐specific interpretation and to maintain confidentiality, we did not report university‐level identifiers or detailed institutional size metrics; however, the sample included both public and private institutions across multiple districts, with participation from universities offering both undergraduate and graduate programs.

Participant recruitment was conducted on‐site with support from student organizations and university administration. Recruitment announcements were shared through student groups, departmental contacts, and on‐campus approaches, and interested students were invited to participate voluntarily. Data collection was conducted offline using a self‐administered questionnaire in designated on‐campus spaces (e.g., classrooms/common rooms) either individually or in small groups. Data collectors remained present to clarify questions when requested. The survey required approximately 10–15 min to complete. Participation in this study was voluntary and was not paid for. All participants were informed about the anonymity of collected information as well as the maintenance of confidentiality. Before data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants to ensure that they fully understood the study's objectives, procedures, and eligibility criteria. This study received ethical approval from the institutional review board (Research Ethics Committee of University of Asia Pacific; approval number: UAP/REC/2023/201‐S), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards.

Translation and Pilot Testing

All the questionnaires were developed in English, translated into Bangla, and back‐translated into English. Discrepancies were reviewed and resolved before finalization. A pilot test on 20 randomly selected students in a different university confirmed clarity and cultural appropriateness. The survey was administered in Bangla or English based on participant preference.

Data Collection

The required minimum sample size was 385 (based on 50% prevalence, 95% CI, and 5% error margin). A total of 1,000 responses were collected, with 48 excluded due to insufficient information, resulting in 952 finalized responses (Figure 1). Sampling ensured a balanced 1:1 male‐to‐female ratio and a 3:2 rural‐to‐urban ratio to enhance representativeness. To avoid bias in the data, we provided questionnaires to larger or smaller groups of students randomly. In‐person surveys were conducted, allowing clarification of questions and minimizing online survey biases such as non‐responses or social desirability bias. All participants signed informed consent forms, assuring openness and respect for their autonomy. Their responses were kept anonymous, and rigorous data security procedures were put in place to safeguard their privacy. Student organizations and university administrations facilitated participation.

Statistical Analysis

Data was initially entered and cleaned in Microsoft Excel 2016 and analyzed with SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Descriptive statistics summarize demographic and outcome variables. Chi‐square (χ²) tests were used to compare the distribution of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia across groups. Binary logistic regression with 95% CI was conducted to assess associations between sociodemographic factors and mental health outcomes. Binary logistic regression models were run separately for each outcome (anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, and insomnia symptoms), using dichotomized screening status (screen‐positive vs screen‐negative) as the dependent variable. For each model, the same set of predictors was entered simultaneously: age group, sex, BMI category, marital status, education level, economic status, family type, parental separation status, sibling status, relationship status, living status, smoking status, residence area, and chronic disease status. Categorical predictors were dummy‐coded, with reference categories aligned with Table 2. Pearson's correlation analysis was done to examine the interrelationships between anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In addition to p‐values, effect sizes were calculated to estimate the magnitude of associations. Cramer's V for chi‐square tests and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were used for logistic regressions. Reporting effect sizes ensured that the results are interpretable beyond statistical significance and provide insights into the practical relevance of findings.

Results

Sample Demographics

The demographic variables of the individuals are presented in Table 1. Altogether 952 responses were collected, among which the percentage of male and female respondents was 51% and 49%, respectively, and the mean age was 22.3 years. 61% of the respondents had an average BMI in the range of 18.5–25.5. Around 88% of the respondents were undergraduate students. Furthermore, 72% of respondents were living with family, along with parents not being separated (96%). Most of the respondents reported living in a nuclear family (77%). A significant portion of the respondents (60%) came from a family having a low economic background, whereas 35% of the respondents came from a medium economic background. A minority of the participants (9%) were married, and more than half of the participants (65%) were involved in any relationship. More than half of the participants were non‐smokers (90%). Lastly, only 14% of the respondents reported having a chronic disease.

Anxiety

As per the results, the prevalence of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia was 70.3%, 49.1%, and 61.7%, respectively (Figure 2). For clinically more concerning symptom burdens, moderate‐to‐severe symptoms were observed in 37.3% for anxiety, 21.1% for depressive symptoms, and 14.4% for insomnia.

Distribution and severity of symptoms of mental health problems among the university students in Bangladesh.

The prevalence of anxiety is found to be higher among the students compared to depressive symptoms and insomnia. Anxiety was found to be significantly associated with sex (p = 0.017), family type (p = 0.016), residence area (p = 0.002), and chronic disease (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the results also revealed that anxiety was significantly associated with depressive symptoms (p < 0.001) and insomnia (p < 0.001). A binary logistic regression was conducted to determine the relationship between anxiety and sex, and the results showed a statistically significant association (OR 1.681, 95% CI 1.126–2.509, p = 0.011,) indicating that women are 1.681 times more prone to anxiety compared to men. Married individuals have higher odds of experiencing anxiety (OR 1.344, 95% CI 0.272–1.689, p = 0.003). Moreover, individuals having siblings have 0.555 times lower odds of experiencing anxiety compared to those without siblings, and it has a statistically significant correlation (OR 0.555, 95% CI 0.314–1.001, p = 0.045). Respondents who did not report chronic diseases were 0.332 times less likely to suffer from anxiety compared to individuals who reported having chronic diseases (OR 0.332, 95% CI 0.163–0.674, p = 0.002) (Table 2).

Depressive Symptoms

Depressive symptoms had the lowest prevalence compared to anxiety and insomnia, indicating that less than half of the sample population were suffering from this disorder. The statistically significant variables for these parameters were sex (p = 0.038), BMI (p = 0.016), economic impression (p = 0.018), parental relationship status (p = 0.050), smoking habit (p = 0.050), and chronic disease (p < 0.001). Moreover, the statistics also depicted that depressive symptoms were significantly associated with anxiety (p < 0.001) and insomnia (p < 0.001). It was also observed that depressive symptoms had a significant association with family income (p < 0.001). Additionally, it was found to be associated with the number of siblings (p = 0.027). In binary logistic regression, compared with students with BMI > 25 kg/m² (overweight/obese; reference), those with BMI < 18.5 kg/m² (CED) had lower odds of reporting depressive symptoms (OR 0.593, 95% CI 0.370–0.948, p = 0.029) (Table 2). The presence of chronic disease was also found to correlate with depressive symptoms. Respondents without chronic disease had 0.544 times less chance to have depressive symptoms than those who possessed this disease (OR 0.544, 95% CI 0.339–0.873, p = 0.012) (Table 2).

Insomnia

Insomnia has a prevalence rate of 61.7%, indicating that more than half of the sample population suffers from insomnia. It was found that insomnia was significantly associated with variables such as age (p = 0.023), parental relationship status (p = 0.032), number of siblings (p = 0.044), smoking habit (p = 0.029), chronic disease (p < 0.001), anxiety (p < 0.001), and depressive symptoms (p < 0.001). According to the binary logistic regression, females were found to be 1.685 times more likely to experience insomnia compared to males (OR 1.685, 95% CI 0.479–0.979, p = 0.038). Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated a statistically significant association between insomnia symptoms and normal‐range BMI (18.5–25.5 kg/m²) (p = 0.006), suggesting that individuals within this BMI category had 0.684 times less chance of suffering from insomnia compared to those with higher BMI (OR 0.684, 95% Cl 0.367–1.932, p = 0.006). Being married was correlated with insomnia, where married individuals were 2.171 times more prone to experience insomnia compared to unmarried individuals (OR 2.171, 95% CI 1.117–4.217, p = 0.022). The results also depicted that respondents having no siblings were 1.798 times more likely to have insomnia compared to individuals having siblings, and this correlation was statistically significant (OR 1.798, 95% CI 1.049–3.219, p = 0.039). Moreover, people without chronic diseases were found to have 0.842 times less chance to suffer from insomnia (OR 0.842, 95% CI 0.497–1.425, p = 0.521) (Table 2).

As shown in Table 3, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia were significantly and positively correlated, indicating substantial overlap across these symptom domains.

To assess the strength of the statistically significant associations identified by the chi‐square tests, Cramer's V was calculated as a measure of effect size. The results indicated that all significant associations, while statistically reliable (p < 0.05), represented effect sizes in the ‘very small’ to ‘small’ range (Cramer's V = 0.075–0.179) (Table 1). The strongest observed association was between the presence of a chronic disease and depressive symptoms (V = 0.179). This suggests that while these demographic and lifestyle factors are significantly related to mental health symptoms, they individually explain a relatively small portion of the variance, indicating that other unmeasured factors likely play a substantial role.

Of the 952 participants, 41.0% (n = 390) screened positive for all three conditions (anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia) (Table 4). Comorbid anxiety and insomnia were observed in 14.4% (n = 137), while 5.3% (n = 50) had anxiety and depressive symptoms, and 1.3% (n = 12) had depressive symptoms and insomnia. Single conditions were reported by 10.0% (n = 95) for anxiety, 1.6% (n = 15) for depressive symptoms, and 4.9% (n = 47) for insomnia. Only 21.6% (n = 206) reported no disorder.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia among Bangladeshi university students and to identify associated sociodemographic and health‐related factors. Across ten universities, we found that 70.3% of students reported anxiety symptoms, 49.1% reported depressive symptoms, and 61.7% reported insomnia. The associated risk factors for these mental health conditions are sex, age, socioeconomic status, chronic disease, and family structure. Female students, those with chronic diseases, and married individuals consistently showed higher vulnerability, while protective effects were observed for sibling presence and higher BMI. These findings confirm that mental‐health burdens are widespread in this population and highlight specific risk groups requiring tailored support. Unlike prior studies that often focus on a single institution or specific student groups, our offline data collection across ten universities enhances the representativeness and reliability of the findings, offering a broader perspective on the mental health status of university students nationwide.

The present findings are consistent with some previous studies. For example, a study done in 2021, among 449 students, revealed the prevalence of anxiety and depression to be 85.1% and 51.4%, respectively [30]. Similarly, during COVID‐19, a cross‐sectional survey was conducted among public and private university students in Bangladesh, revealing that 53.99% of public university students experienced anxiety, 59.16% had depression, and 46.95% had comorbidity ranging from moderate to severe [31]. Another study, conducted over 5 months among university students in Sialkot, Pakistan, reported high rates of depression (75%) and anxiety (88.4%) [10]. A systematic review on six ASEAN countries (Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, Thailand, and Vietnam) in Southeast Asia's low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) having few mental health resources surveyed among 5,351 students for anxiety and depression and found the median point prevalence to be 29.4% and 42.4% [32]. The higher prevalence of anxiety (70.3%) in our study compared to ASEAN (29.4%) and Pakistani (88.4%) cohorts may reflect unique stressors in Bangladeshi academia, such as competitive exams, financial instability, and limited mental health support. It could also stem from cultural differences in symptom reporting or access to clinical diagnoses. This underscores the need for localized interventions rather than direct cross‐country comparisons.

A study done on 856 university students from Turkey reported that only 104 students had insomnia, having a prevalence of 12.1% [33]. In the Middle East, insomnia is common, with studies indicating the incidence to be 66.7%, 63.9%, 64.4%, 48.4%, 61.4%, and 63% in the UAE, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Qatar, and Bahrain, respectively [34]. In another study, done on university students from South Asian nations, the pooled prevalence range of insomnia was found to be 35.4% to 70% [16]. Our study found that the sex of students is a potential risk factor for both anxiety and depressive symptoms, which is supported by a study conducted among Canadian medical students, where female sex was identified as a potential risk factor for anxiety and other psychological disorders, along with suicide ideation [35]. Another study found a significant correlation between parents’ relationship and insomnia, where they reported that individuals having a restrained family relationship are more likely to experience insomnia and troubled sleeping compared to those who have a healthy family relationship [36]. The heightened vulnerability of our female students aligns with global trends but may be exacerbated in Bangladesh by societal pressures, gender roles, and restricted coping mechanisms. Previous studies suggest women internalize stress more, while men underreport symptoms due to stigma, a critical consideration for gender‐sensitive mental health programs. Interestingly, our data indicated a higher prevalence of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia among participants without chronic illnesses. This contrasts with established findings where 21.3% of patients with chronic diseases experienced depressive symptoms, and 61.3% had anxiety [37]. The strong link between chronic illness and mental health symptoms implies a bidirectional relationship: physical illness may worsen psychological distress, while depression/anxiety can impair treatment adherence. Integrating mental health screenings into chronic disease clinics could mitigate this cycle among students.

Age was found to be a significant factor positively correlated with insomnia, which is supported by a study reporting that 33% of participants experienced chronic insomnia with increased age, and the presence of diabetes was significantly associated with its occurrence [38]. Our study also identified a unique and significant factor associated with both anxiety and the absence of siblings, which showed a positive correlation with these conditions. While some Western studies suggest smoking reduces stress, our findings show it worsens insomnia in Bangladeshi students. This may be because students here use smoking to cope with stress when mental healthcare is hard to access. On the other hand, having siblings appears protective, likely because family support helps manage stress. This highlights the need for better mental health support and stronger peer networks in student interventions.

The strong positive correlation between anxiety and depressive symptoms in our sample aligns with previous studies reporting overlapping risk factors and symptom clusters in student populations [39, 40]. The moderate associations of both anxiety and depressive symptoms with insomnia further emphasize the interconnected nature of mental health disorders, where sleep disturbance often exacerbates or coexists with mood‐related symptoms. The high level of comorbidity (41% reporting all three disorders) highlights the overlapping nature of mental health problems among Bangladeshi students. These findings are consistent with the literature showing that anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia often co‐occur, intensifying functional impairment [41]. These results underscore the need for integrated screening approaches, as identifying one condition may serve as an early indicator for others, supporting targeted interventions in university settings.

In our bivariate analysis, chronic disease was significantly linked with higher rates of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia. However, in the adjusted regression models, chronic disease remained a significant predictor of anxiety and depressive symptoms, but not insomnia. This pattern might reflect that chronic conditions more directly influence mood disorders via physiological stress or inflammation, while their effect on insomnia may be mediated by other factors such as coping strategies or lifestyle variables [42]. Future research should explore these pathways and potential moderators in student populations.

Interestingly, we observed no significant differences in the prevalence of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia between undergraduate and postgraduate students. This may reflect that emotional stressors are present across both academic levels. Undergraduates contend with adaptation, course loads, and identity formation, whereas postgraduates often face pressures around research, publication, and career planning [43]. Future research should investigate whether emotional coping or resilience mediates mental health outcomes differently in undergraduates versus postgraduates.

While our findings align with global patterns, the extreme prevalence rates in Bangladesh demand urgent policy action. Potential drivers include academic pressure, economic instability, and stigma delaying help‐seeking. Future studies should explore causal pathways (e.g., social media use, sleep hygiene) to tailor campus mental health initiatives. This study stands out on its own since it is one of the few studies that examines the prevalence of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia in Bangladeshi students. The majority of research in this area focuses on certain student populations, such as in medicine or nursing [44]. Moreover, we ensured the utilization of Cronbach's alpha analysis, which suggests a commitment to evaluating the consistency and reliability of the study's measuring scales and questionnaires. This statistical technique adds depth to the research methodology, providing insights into the robustness of the questions and enhancing the credibility of the results obtained in this study. The mental health conditions of university students are influenced by multiple demographic factors. We evaluated a wide range of demographic parameters, which are rarely included in similar studies. This allowed a more comprehensive analysis and captured diverse contexts influencing mental health outcomes of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia among university students. Unlike most studies that rely on online surveys, our in‐person data collection enabled direct interaction with participants, improving both the quality and quantity of responses. Sampling from various districts enhanced representativeness. For insomnia assessment, most studies use the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index or the Insomnia Severity Scale. In contrast, we used AIS‐8, which is based on the ICD diagnostic criteria for insomnia, ensuring diagnostic accuracy.

Our survey questionnaire was self‐administered, and we used internationally validated instruments that increase the acceptability of our study. As the survey was anonymous, it resulted in gathering honest opinions from the respondents. Furthermore, conducting the survey offline allowed us to address any ambiguities related to the questions in real time, thereby improving the accuracy of the responses. Since most Bangladeshi students lack awareness regarding mental health, universities can consider establishing dedicated mental health hubs. These hubs can utilize validated screening scales to quickly identify symptoms and provide timely support to students. They can also readily identify mental health issues by establishing workshops, seminars, and campaigns that can help in reducing the social stigmas associated with mental health issues and encourage students to seek treatment when necessary. Early identification of symptoms through these scales allows for effective intervention and treatment, preventing serious consequences. Policymakers can use this data in their campaigns for the inclusion of mental health education and support programs in universities. The study's findings can help influence the development of randomized controlled trials and preventive measures in academic settings. This might include efforts to raise mental health awareness, peer support groups, and counseling services that meet university students’ needs. Collaboration among universities, healthcare providers, and community organizations can help adopt and sustain good mental health practices on campus.

This study has several limitations. The cross‐sectional design prevents causal inference, so associations like smoking and insomnia may be bidirectional or influenced by unmeasured factors such as stress or genetics. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm temporality. Reliance on self‐reported GAD‐7, PHQ‐9, and AIS‐8 may introduce response bias, such as underreporting due to stigma or overreporting linked to transient stressors. Though these tools are validated, clinical interviews would improve accuracy. Findings may not generalize beyond 10 universities, excluding rural or non‐student groups. Environmental factors like workload and social media were unmeasured. Future research should combine longitudinal designs with mixed‐method approaches to mitigate these limitations. While demographic factors were prioritized in this study, future longitudinal studies could explore genetic and environmental interactions.

Conclusion

This study found a high prevalence of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia among university students in Bangladesh, showing a major mental health burden in this group. Risk factors included female gender, low family income, nuclear family structure, absence of siblings, and chronic illness. The absence of siblings emerged as a unique factor linked with both anxiety and insomnia. The use of validated tools and in‐person surveys improved reliability and representativeness. The high rates call for targeted interventions such as university‐based screening hubs, awareness programs, and accessible counseling services. Policymakers and universities must prioritize mental health support to prevent long‐term consequences and promote well‐being among students. Future research should use longitudinal designs to explore causal pathways and test prevention strategies.
