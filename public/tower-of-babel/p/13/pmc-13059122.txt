Alpha-ketoglutarate alleviates acute pancreatitis by restoring energy metabolism
Authors: Peng Li, Chunfeng Shi, Hangshi Wang, Shiyang Zhan, Jiahui Zhang, Dongyao Xu, Yuxun Lv, Furong Yan, Shaobin Xu, Jin Chen, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcute pancreatitis (AP) lacks specific therapies. This study identified dysregulated metabolites in AP and investigated the therapeutic potential of α-ketoglutaric acid (AKG). Metabolomic profiling of human serum revealed 76 AP-specific metabolites compared to acute cholecystitis and normal control, with AKG significantly elevated and correlated with disease severity. In a severe AP mouse model, oral AKG supplementation attenuated pancreatic injury, systemic inflammation, and multi-organ damage. Mechanistically, AKG restored pancreatic energy metabolism and mitochondrial structure while reactivating the Nrf2 antioxidant and adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase signaling pathways. These findings highlight AKG as a specific metabolic signature of AP and a promising therapeutic agent for alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and organ injury.Subject areas: health sciences

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common and potentially life-threatening inflammatory condition of the exocrine pancreas and a leading cause of gastrointestinal-related hospital admissions.1 The global incidence of AP is rising, affecting 13–45 individuals per 100,000 annually.2 Approximately 80% of cases are mild and self-limiting, while the remaining 20% progress to severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), which is associated with a high mortality rate of >30% when complicated by organ failure and infected necrosis.3 However, treatment options remain limited. Current clinical management of AP remains confined to supportive measures, such as fluid resuscitation and nutritional support, and lacks specific therapies that halt its pathological progression.4 Accordingly, it is urgent to discover novel therapeutic targets and intervention strategies.

AP pathogenesis initiates with the premature intra-acinar activation of digestive enzymes, which triggers a cascade of deleterious cellular responses, including mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore and a collapse of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, precipitates a catastrophic cellular bioenergetic crisis.5 This energy failure leads directly to acinar cell death and constitutes the critical stress signal that activates energy-sensing pathways, such as adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK), thereby linking the initial injury to the cellular metabolic response.6,7 Therefore, the modulation of pancreatic energy metabolism presents a promising therapeutic approach to eliminate AP.

Recent metabolomic studies have revealed severe metabolic disturbances in patients with AP. Ornithine metabolism contributes to AP aggravation,8 and palmitoleic acid has potential as a biomarker for the diagnosis of AP.9 Glutamine and malate, which are involved in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, are significantly altered.10 However, the specific metabolic profile of AP remains unclear.

In this study, metabolic profiling revealed that 95 and 40 differential metabolites were detected in AP versus normal control (NC) and acute cholecystitis (AC) versus NC, respectively, and 76 metabolites, including alpha-ketoglutaric acid (AKG), were specifically expressed in AP. AKG level was significantly reduced during hospitalization, and positively correlated with white blood cell (WBC) count and amylase levels. AKG is pivotal in the TCA cycle and essential for cellular metabolism,11 and it exhibits potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.12,13 Oral AKG treatment in mice significantly ameliorated SAP-induced pancreatic injury and multi-organ damage (lung and kidney). Metabolic analysis of mouse pancreatic tissue reveals improved energy metabolism with AKG treatment. SAP-induced severe mitochondrial dysfunction and suppression of Nrf2 and AMPK signaling were significantly improved. Consequently, treatment with specific metabolites involved in energy metabolism may be a promising approach to attenuate AP.

Results

Metabolomic profiling identifies AP-specific metabolic signatures

Given the potential overlap between biliary pathology and pancreatitis, we enrolled patients with biliary AP but excluded those with concurrent AC. Although biliary issues are a common cause of AP, selecting an AC group (without pancreatic injury) as a control allowed us to separate the metabolic noise from biliary tract inflammation. This strategy ensured that the identified differential metabolites were specific to pancreatic injury, rather than the concomitant biliary pathology. Systemic metabolic alterations in AP were investigated. Quality control (QC) samples were grouped in the principal component analysis (PCA) score plot to verify the stability of the analytical platform. The PCA revealed a distinct separation between the AP and NC groups (Figure 1B). Based on the criteria of fold change (FC) > 1.5 or <0.63 and a p-value <0.05, 95 differential metabolites (89 upregulated and 6 downregulated) were identified, as presented in the volcano plot (Figure 1C). The heatmap revealed extensive metabolic perturbations in patients with AP (Figure S1A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis indicated that arginine biosynthesis and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism were the significantly enriched pathways (Figure 1D). The TCA cycle was also enriched, suggesting a strong association between mitochondrial dysfunction and AP development.

We used the AC group as a disease control to evaluate the specificity of these alterations. Comprehensive profiling confirmed that the AC group possessed a distinct metabolic phenotype, with 40 differential metabolites identified compared to controls (Figures S1B–S1D; Table S2). Then, we performed an intersection analysis to isolate AP-specific signatures. Venn diagram (Figure 1E) illustrates that 19 metabolites were shared between “AP versus NC” (95 metabolites) and “AC versus NC” (40 metabolites) comparisons (Figure S1E). Consequently, 76 metabolites were identified as dysregulated exclusively in the AP group. The hierarchical clustering heatmap validated this specificity by demonstrating that these 76 AP-specific metabolites distinguished the AP cohort from NC and AC groups (Figure 1F).

Chemical classification analysis revealed that carboxylic acids were a predominant class among these signatures, accounting for 19 of 76 AP-specific metabolites. To further characterize them, we analyzed their abundance distributions. Violin plot analysis (Figure 1G) revealed a significant and consistent elevation of these 19 AP-specific carboxylic acids in the AP group than in the NC and AC groups. AKG, a core TCA cycle intermediate, was significantly increased in AP (Figure 1H). Accordingly, AKG is a potential key player in AP pathophysiology.

AKG as a key metabolic signature associated with AP severity

To delineate the temporal patterns of metabolic dysregulation, metabolic profiling of patients with AP was performed on days 1, 3, and 7 (D1, D3, and D7) of hospitalization (Table S2). Based on time-series cluster analysis, metabolites were categorized into four distinct clusters (Figure 2A; Table S3). Clusters 1 (n = 20) and 2 (n = 16) exhibited transient changes, while clusters 3 (n = 95) and 4 (n = 21) exhibited decreasing and increasing trends (Figure 2A). Cluster 3 was of particular interest because it exhibited a continuous decline from admission (D1) to discharge (D7), paralleling clinical recovery. Enrichment analysis revealed that carboxylic acids predominantly clustered in this declining pattern (Figure 2B), suggesting that their clearance is linked to disease resolution.

To explore the metabolites strongly associated with clinical indicators in patients with AP, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed (Table S4). According to the significance criterion of p < 0.05, metabolites 1, 18, 10, 20, and 25 were positively correlated with PCT, WBC, NLR, AMS, and NEUT (Figure 2C), while 18 metabolites were negatively correlated with clinical indicators (Figure 2D). Particularly, 19 metabolites were significantly correlated with WBC, an inflammatory marker (Figure S2A), while 21 metabolites were strongly associated with AMS level, an indicator of pancreatic injury (Figure S2B). Six metabolites, including malic acid, galacturonic acid, glucuronic acid, phenylpyruvic acid, benzoic acid, and AKG, were identified as AP-specific carboxylic acids. It was observed that AKG was strongly correlated with WBC (Figure 2E) and AMS levels (Figure 2F). Although AKG exhibited the second-strongest association with disease severity, its critical rate-limiting role in the TCA cycle and its central hub in linking carbon and nitrogen metabolism make it a potential therapy.

AKG attenuated SAP pathophysiology

Since SAP represents the primary clinical challenge with high mortality and a lack of specific therapies, and AKG levels strongly correlate with disease severity, SAP mouse models were used for AKG interventional studies. Male C57BL/6J mice (aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 18–20 g) were randomly divided into four experimental groups (n = 8 per group): NC, AKG treatment (AKG), SAP mice model (SAP), and SAP treated with AKG (SAP + AKG). The SAP mouse model involved seven hourly intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of caerulein (at 100 μg/kg body weight per injection), immediately followed by a single i.p. injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 10 mg/kg body weight). For AKG treatment, mice were treated with 0.5% AKG in drinking water before SAP (Figure 3A). We have measured AKG levels in vivo using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) (Table S5). Levels of AKG in the AKG and SAP + AKG groups were significantly elevated compared to the NC and AKG groups, both in the serum (Figure 3B) and pancreas tissue (Figure 3C). Exogenous AKG supplementation effectively restored its physiological concentrations.

Macroscopic examination revealed that AKG treatment significantly ameliorated the severe necrosis, edema, and hemorrhage in the pancreatic tissue of the SAP group (Figure 3D). Serum amylase and lipase were elevated in the SAP group than in the NC group, but were effectively reduced by AKG treatment (Figures 3E and 3F). Additionally, the levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, exhibited the same behavior as amylase and lipase (Figures 3G–3I).

To further investigate the protective effect of AKG against pancreatic injury at the cellular level, acinar cell death was evaluated. TUNEL staining was performed to detect DNA fragmentation, a hallmark of cell death. The SAP group displayed a dramatic increase in TUNEL-positive cells within the pancreatic parenchyma, whereas AKG administration significantly reduced the number of dead cells (Figures 4A and 4B). Furthermore, we examined the expression of cleaved caspase-3, the final executor of the apoptotic cascade, using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Consistently, AKG treatment significantly attenuated the elevated cleaved caspase-3 levels in SAP mice (Figures 4C and 4D). These results demonstrate that AKG exerts protective effects by inhibiting the inflammatory response and subsequent acinar cell death during SAP progression. Thus, AKG treatment significantly attenuated SAP-induced pancreatic injury and inflammation.

AKG alleviates SAP-induced multi-organ damage

To confirm the protective effects of AKG, the damage to the pancreas and remote organs was evaluated. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated that AKG pre-treatment significantly ameliorated SAP-induced pancreatic damage by reducing acinar necrosis and hemorrhage. Acute lung and kidney injuries are common and severe complications of SAP.14 Thickened alveolar septa and inflammatory infiltrates in the lungs and decreased tubular necrosis and brush border loss in the kidneys were observed (Figure 5A). Quantitative pathological scoring supported these observations, revealing that the injury scores of the pancreas, lung, and kidney were significantly lower in the SAP + AKG group than in the SAP group (Figures 5B and S3). Given that neutrophil infiltration is a key driver of tissue damage in AP,15 the extent of infiltration was assessed using MPO immunohistochemistry. Compared to the widespread and intense MPO-positive staining observed in all organs of the SAP group, it was significantly reduced in the AKG intervention group (Figures 5C and 5D). These findings indicate that the in vivo protective effect of AKG manifests as a dual inhibitory action on direct tissue damage and the secondary neutrophil-mediated inflammatory response induced by SAP.

AKG alters the SAP-induced metabolic reprogramming

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the AKG attenuation in SAP, targeted metabolomics of the pancreas was conducted (Table S6). PCA exhibited a distinct separation between SAP and NC groups, consistent with the metabolic profiling of patients with AP (Figure 6A). AKG treatment (SAP + AKG group) may lead to metabolic alterations, as indicated by PCA performance. We observed that 212 metabolites were significantly changed using the analysis of variance (ANOVA), and they are presented in a heatmap (Figure 6B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that amino acid and energy metabolism were significantly altered (Figure 6C). The metabolic pathway involved in AKG is illustrated in Figure 6D. Analysis of TCA cycle metabolites (Figure 6D) revealed complex metabolic reprogramming in the SAP group. Phosphoenolpyruvic acid and pyruvic acid were significantly downregulated, while citric acid, aconitic acid, AKG, succinic acid, and aspartic acid were significantly upregulated in the SAP group than in the NC group. Their SAP-induced expression profiles were significantly changed with AKG treatment, indicating that AKG supplementation can rebalance TCA cycle metabolism and provide therapeutic potential for SAP intervention. These metabolomic data from pancreatic tissue indicated that the protective effect of AKG was closely associated with metabolic reprogramming, especially in the TCA cycle.

AKG ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction induced by SAP

We investigated the performance of mitochondria in SAP under AKG treatment, which activates the TCA cycle. At the ultrastructural level, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed severe mitochondrial damage in the SAP group, characterized by swollen and fragmented cristae, while AKG intervention significantly improved mitochondrial morphology (Figure 7A). Then, mitochondrial damage was quantified using an established scoring system.16 The results confirmed the protective effect of AKG on mitochondrial structure (Figure 7B). Additionally, ATP levels were significantly lower in the SAP group than in controls and increased upon AKG treatment (Figure 7C). Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number, a critical indicator of mitochondrial biogenesis and integrity, was also significantly reduced in the SAP group, whereas AKG treatment effectively restored its level (Figure 7D). We measured the expression of key proteins of the mitochondrial respiratory chain (MRC) complexes I, II, III, IV, and V. They were significantly downregulated in the SAP group, but AKG treatment effectively restored their expression levels (Figures 7E and 7F). AKG significantly repaired SAP-induced mitochondrial dysfunction.

AKG improved SAP by AMPK pathways

Given that SAP is closely linked to oxidative stress imbalance, we investigated protein expression changes in the endogenous antioxidant defense system.17 Proteins involved in the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, including Nrf2, KEAP1, SOD1, SOD2, and HO-1, were significantly decreased when SAP was detected using western blot (Figures 8A and 8B). AKG treatment significantly alleviated this suppression, effectively restoring its levels (Figures 8A and 8B). Based on previous results, the AMPK pathway, which is linked to energy and antioxidants, may play a potential role in AKG treatment. Then, we detested AMPK and its phosphorylation level using western blot (Figures 8C and 8D) and immunofluorescence (IF) (Figures 8E–8G). AMPK was dephosphorylated in the SAP group compared to NC, whereas AKG treatment stimulated its phosphorylation. AKG treatment was observed to be associated with the restoration of AMPK phosphorylation levels.

Discussion

AP is a critical inflammatory disease characterized by complex pathogenesis and significant systemic complications, but with limited treatment.4 In this study, metabolic profiling of AP, AC, and NC volunteers revealed specific metabolites, including carboxylic acids, expressed in patients with AP. AKG was strongly associated with clinical indicators, and its levels were significantly elevated in patients with AP and SAP animal models. Furthermore, exogenous AKG supplementation effectively ameliorated pancreatic and distant-organ injury in SAP by improving mitochondrial function, activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, and restoring AMPK activity.

Metabolomic analysis of blood samples from patients with AP compared to NC individuals has been discussed. Carboxylic acids, such as hexadecenoic acid,18 (R)-3-hydroxybutyric acid,19 and palmitoleic acid,9 are clinically relevant for diagnosing AP. Amino acids, including valine, isoleucine, and leucine, were altered, consistent with these results.10 Furthermore, ornithine significantly contributes to AP aggravation.8 However, metabolites specifically expressed in AP remain to be elucidated. In this study, AC, a cause of acute abdomen, was used, and it is clinically significant to distinguish between AP and AC. We observed that 76 metabolites were abnormally expressed in AP versus NC compared with AC versus NC, of which were carboxylic acids. The abundance of most carboxylic acids declined during hospitalization as AP improved. Especially, TCA cycle intermediates, including AKG, malic acid, and pyruvic acid, were abnormally expressed in patients with AP. This was also observed in the pancreas of SAP-induced mice. Accordingly, this study elucidated specific metabolites, such as TCA cycle intermediates, expressed in patients with AP and may provide new insights into AP diagnosis and treatment. Energy metabolism is severely disturbed in AP, and targeting it represents an alternative new therapeutic approach. A key observation in this study was that serum AKG levels were positively correlated with WBC and amylase levels. Furthermore, we monitored the dynamic changes during the disease course. As the clinical condition of patients with AP gradually improved during hospitalization (D1, D3, and D7), serum AKG levels decreased correspondingly. This dynamic pattern parallels that of traditional injury markers, such as amylase, suggesting that elevated serum AKG is a sensitive biomarker of the extent of tissue injury and metabolic stress, rather than a causative factor of the disease. These results also demonstrated that exogenous AKG supplementation effectively alleviated pancreatic and distant-organ injury, including the lung, in SAP mice. Accordingly, this study highlights AKG potential as a therapeutic target for AP.

AKG has been reported to exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory functions in various diseases. As an anti-inflammatory agent, it improves inflammatory bowel disease by inhibiting NF-κB signaling20 and presents neuroprotection against cerebral ischemia by suppressing the c-Fos/IL-10/Stat3 pathway.21 As an antioxidant, it reduces mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis22 and cardiovascular disease23 and inhibits ferroptosis in osteoarthritis.24 However, the mechanism of action of AKG in AP treatment remains unclear. In this study, the protective effect of AKG was closely associated with the restoration of mitochondrial homeostasis and the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway. AKG supplementation comprehensively improved mitochondrial ultrastructure, restored mtDNA copy number, recovered the expression of MRC complex proteins, and reactivated the suppressed Nrf2 pathway.

However, AKG has been reported to dictate AMPK translation in cancer cells,25 and AMPK activation has been implicated in the response to AP.26 This suggests that the AMPK energy-sensing pathway might be involved in the effects of AKG treatment in AP. In this study, exogenous AKG supplementation was found to correlate with the reversal of SAP-induced AMPK suppression. Therefore, these data suggest the possibility that AKG exerts its organ-protective effects, potentially by correcting maladaptive AMPK suppression and restoring cellular energy homeostasis. Nonetheless, further studies using specific AMPK inhibitors or genetic models are necessary to definitively establish this regulatory mechanism.

Although this study provides important insights, it has limitations. First, the number of participants in this study was relatively small. Second, the protective effects of AKG were primarily validated in animal models. The precise efficacy, optimal dosage, and safety of AKG in patients with AP require confirmation in future clinical trials.

Conclusion

In summary, metabolic profiling reveals metabolites that are specifically expressed in patients with AP, especially metabolic pathways involved in the AKG. Exogenous supplementation of AKG exerts significant protective effects against SAP and its associated multi-organ injury. The underlying protective mechanisms potentially involve the correction of mitochondrial dysfunction, restoration of AMPK energy sensor activity, and enhancement of Nrf2-mediated cellular antioxidant defenses. These findings provide a new metabolic perspective for understanding AP pathogenesis and developing novel therapeutic strategies that target cellular energy metabolism and mitochondrial function.

Limitations of the study

Although this study provides important insights into the metabolic alterations in AP and the therapeutic potential of AKG, it has limitations. First, the number of clinical participants included in the metabolomic profiling was relatively small. Second, the protective effects and mechanisms of AKG were primarily validated in animal models. The precise efficacy, optimal dosage, and long-term safety of AKG in patients with AP require confirmation in future large-scale clinical trials.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

This study strictly adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University ([2019] Ethics Approval No. 83 and [2025] Ethics Approval No. 533). All participants were fully informed of the study's purpose and procedures and voluntarily signed written informed consent forms before enrollment. From July 2019 to October 2019, 9 patients with biliary AP, 10 patients with AC, and 9 NC of Han Chinese ethnicity who were age- and sex-matched were enrolled in this study. For the AP group, samples were collected on D1, D3, and D7 after admission. Biliary AP patients were enrolled but strictly excluded those with concurrent acute cholecystitis, and they were diagnosed according to the 2012 revised Atlanta classification. AC patients were diagnosed according to the Tokyo Guidelines 2018 (TG18). Besides, CT/MRI confirmed that the AC group had no pancreatic involvement, and the AP group’s primary pathology was pancreatic inflammation. The exclusion criteria were chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic tumors, autoimmune diseases, severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal insufficiency, and pregnancy. The detailed clinical characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1.

Animals

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University ([2025] Ethics Approval No: [533]). Seven-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Hangzhou Medical University. Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free barrier environment at a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, and a 12-h light-dark cycle. They had free access to standard chow and sterile drinking water.

Method details

Animal model and AKG treatment

A total of 32 male C57BL/6J mice (aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 18–20 g) were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8 per group): NC, AKG, SAP, and SAP + AKG. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment (22 ± 2°C, 55% ± 10% humidity) under a 12-h light/dark cycle. AKG and SAP + AKG groups received 0.5% AKG (dissolved in drinking water) for seven days prior to modeling. The SAP model was induced using seven hourly i.p. injections of caerulein (100 μg/kg), followed immediately by a single i.p. injection of LPS (10 mg/kg).27 To ensure model stability and prevent non-specific acute mortality, mice were strictly maintained on heating pads (37°C) during the modeling process. Mice were sacrificed 12 h after the first injection to collect blood and tissue samples.

Metabolic profiling using GC-MS

Metabolites were extracted from serum (20 μL) or pancreatic tissue (10 mg) with methanol containing tridecanoic acid as an internal standard.28 Supernatants were lyophilized and derivatized with methoxyamine and N-trimethylsilyl-N-methyl trifluoroacetamide. Samples were analyzed using a GC-MS-QP2020 coupled with an AOC-20i auto-sampler (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Separation was performed using a DB-5 capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 1 μm; Agilent Technologies). Data were acquired using SIM mode from Smart Database software (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan).

Histopathological examination

Pancreatic, lung, and kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned to a thickness of 4 μm. Sections were stained with H&E. Pathological damage to the pancreas was semi-quantitatively scored using the criteria established by Schmidt et al.29,30 Pancreatic tissues were evaluated for edema (0–3), acinar necrosis (0–3), inflammation (0–3), and hemorrhage (0–3), with a maximum total score of 12. Lung and kidney injuries were assessed using a standard histological scoring system.

IHC and IF

For IHC, paraffin sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary anti-MPO antibody (1:600) or anti-cleaved caspase-3 (1:300; #9661, Cell Signaling Technology, United States), followed by an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Signals were visualized with DAB. For IF, sections were incubated overnight with anti-p-AMPK (1:200) or anti-AMPK (1:200) antibodies, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488 or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies. To evaluate cell apoptosis, sections were processed using a One-step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Green Fluorescence; #MA0223, Meilunbio, China). After deparaffinization, sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and incubated with the TdT enzyme and fluorescent labeling solution at 37 °C for 1 h in a humidified dark chamber. The nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured using a Leica confocal microscope (STELLARIS 8).

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NDUFS3, SDHB, UQCRC1, MT-CO1, ATP5F1A, AMPK, p-AMPK, Nrf2, KEAP1, SOD1, SOD2, and HO-1. Bands were visualized using an ECL kit and quantified using ImageJ software. GAPDH served as the loading control.

TEM

Fresh pancreatic tissues were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmium tetroxide. Samples were dehydrated, embedded in EPON 812 resin, and sectioned (70 nm). Images were acquired using TEM (Hitachi HT7800) at 80 kV.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), ATP, and mtDNA analysis

Serum amylase, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels were measured using commercial ELISA kits. Pancreatic ATP content was determined using an ATP assay kit. Relative mtDNA content was analyzed using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with primers targeting the mitochondrial D-loop and nuclear 18S rRNA.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Two groups were compared using Student's t-test, while multiple groups were compared using a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2). PCA and pathway enrichment were conducted using R (version 4.5.1) and MetaboAnalyst. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
