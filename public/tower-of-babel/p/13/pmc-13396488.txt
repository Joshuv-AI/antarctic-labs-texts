Intermolecular interactions optimization in weakly solvating ether solvents for wide-temperature Na metal batteries
Authors: Mengjie He, Yunsen Liu, Zhiling Wang, Yongzhu Fu, Shuai Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of wide-temperature-range batteries is essential for applications under extreme weather conditions and scientific endeavors such as deep-sea and space exploration. Sodium (Na) metal batteries offer the advantage of weak ion–solvent interactions, which promote easier desolvation at low temperatures compared with lithium batteries. Weakly solvating electrolytes can further decrease desolvation energy barriers, thereby enhancing electrochemical reversibility at low temperature. However, conventional weakly solvating solvents are highly volatile, posing safety concerns for high-temperature applications. Herein, we redesign the molecular structure of typical weakly solvating ethers to reinforce intermolecular interactions, thereby increasing their boiling points while preserving their low solvation characteristics. As a result, the Na metal batteries exhibit stable performance at current densities of at least 0.5 mA cm⁻2 across a wide temperature range from −40 to 70 °C. Moreover, the kinetic study highlights the previously overlooked yet critical role of solvent intermolecular interactions in influencing the desolvation barrier at low temperatures, in addition to ion–solvent interactions. Therefore, optimizing the intermolecular interactions of weakly solvating solvents is proposed to be a facile and promising strategy for achieving wide-temperature Na metal batteries.

Introduction

Rechargeable batteries are being developed to operate across a wide temperature range, driven by increasingly extreme weather conditions and exploration activities in deep-sea and space environments1–3. Sodium (Na) has the larger ionic radius and lower first ionization energy compared with lithium (Li), which can lead to the weaker solvation effect and thus the better low-temperature kinetics4. Moreover, Na metal negative electrode has a high theoretical capacity (1166 mAh g−1) and low equilibrium potential ( −2.7 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)), making Na metal batteries a kind of promising candidate for the wide-temperature-range application5–7.

The plating/stripping reaction for the Na metal negative electrode involves transport of Na cations in the electrolyte, desolvation at interface, and Na+ transport in the interphase. When the working temperature is expanded from room temperature to a wide temperature range, the ionic conductivity and desolvation may be hindered at low temperature, and simultaneously, the interphase instability and the safety issues may arise at high temperature. The desolvation-free batteries like organic batteries or the ion–solvent cointercalation-based batteries, can have good low-temperature or wide-temperature performances, however, the energy density or cycling stability are still not satisfactory8–10. The electrolyte engineering is believed to be the most convenient and cost-efficient strategy to regulate the low-temperature kinetics of Na batteries, irrespective of the reaction mechanism11,12. The ester-carbonate-based electrolytes can promote the wide-temperature performance of Na metal batteries with the aid of interphase modification between Na metal negative electrode and the electrolyte13–16. The reduction of electrolytes can be mitigated, and desolvation of Na+ can be accelerated by the interphase17,18.

Besides, ether-based electrolytes are highly compatible with Na metal negative electrodes due to the high reduction stability, and the Na metal negative electrodes generally display high coulombic efficiency (99.9%) with ether-based electrolytes at room temperature, illustrating the promising application potential19–22. The solvation capability or strength is of vital importance for the kinetics, which mainly refers to the weak ion–solvent interaction and is affected by the polarity, dipole moment of solvents, etc. The weak-solvation ethers can help lower the desolvation barrier at low temperature and thus promote the electrochemical reversibility at below −20 °C. The collocation between the strong-solvation linear ether and the weak-solvation cyclic ether solvents is the general strategy to balance the ionic conductivity and desolvation process at low temperature to achieve favorable cycling stability23–26. The pairing of other ether solvents also promotes the low-temperature performance27–30. However, the weak-solvation solvents adopted in the electrolytes usually have high volatility. For instance, the boiling points of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and tetrahydrofuran (THF) are only 75 °C and 66 °C, respectively, which can pose safety risks when the battery operates at elevated temperatures near the boiling points of the solvents. Although several low-volatility solvents have been in lithium- and potassium-battery researches for interphase modification or voltage tolerance31–35, it remains urgent and challenging to develop a method to design low-volatile and weak-solvation solvents to meet the wide-temperature-range applications (Fig. 1a).Fig. 1Schematic of wide-temperature-range electrolyte design.a Requirements of wide-temperature electrolyte at extreme temperatures and their relation with intermolecular interactions between solvents. In this work, we design high-boiling-point, weak-solvation ethers as the solvents for wide-temperature Na metal batteries, which decreases the volatility of the electrolytes at high temperature and simultaneously improves the reversibility of Na metal batteries at low temperature. The cyclic derivatives 1,3-dioxane (DX) and 4-methyl-1,3-dioxane (MDX) are designed through a facile strategy of upgrading the molecular structure of the existing low-volatile weak-solvation ethers from a long-overlooked aspect of solvent-solvent interaction. It is unraveled that the medium intermolecular interactions between them can increase boiling points and simultaneously maintain the weak-solvation capability (Fig. 1b–d). The Na metal batteries can thus work highly reversibly from −40 °C to 70 °C in the resultant electrolyte. Moreover, the electrolyte solvation microstructure is systematically compared across the wide temperature range and the low-temperature interfacial kinetics of strong- and weak-solvation electrolytes were deciphered in detail. The critical role of intermolecular interactions in dictating the desolvation kinetics at low temperatures is uncovered, acting in concert with solvation capabilities to shape the desolvation process. The work highlights the importance of optimizing intermolecular interactions for the exploration of viable electrolytes for wide-temperature batteries.

Results

Rational design of a wide-temperature electrolyte

The solvents DX and MDX were rationally designed based on the general weak-solvation solvent of DOL, which can facilitate low-temperature performances but suffer from the high volatility at elevated temperatures. When the number of carbon atoms in the ring is increased, and the hydrogen atoms on the ring are substituted with methyl groups, DOL is converted to DX and MDX, respectively. They are expected to retain the low solvation capability of DOL to have good low-temperature performance and reduce the volatile property to increase high-temperature safety (Fig. 2a). The enthalpy of vaporization of different solvents was calculated to illustrate the low volatility of DX and MDX. It can be seen that DX and MDX have higher enthalpy of vaporization than DOL and even the general ester carbonates like dimethyl carbonate (DMC) in commercial lithium-ion batteries (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Data 1). Although dimethoxyethane (DME) has a higher enthalpy of vaporization, it shows a lower boiling point than DX and MDX. The boiling points of DX and MDX are even higher than DMC. The saturated vapor pressure at different temperatures was further predicted according to the Clausius–Clapeyron relation, which confirms that MDX and DX are less volatile than DOL and even DMC solvents (Fig. 2c). The thermogravimetric analysis also confirms the lower volatility of DX and MDX than DOL (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). It is hypothesized that the increased intermolecular interaction for DX and MDX lowers the volatility, compared with DOL. The binary intermolecular binding energies for DOL, DX and MDX verify the hypothesis (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 1). The diffusion ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) and molecular dynamics (MD) analyses were conducted for the mixed and individual solvents of DOL, DX, and MDX (Supplementary Figs. 4–6, Supplementary Data 1), which also confirm the hypothesis36. Besides, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies of DX and MDX are even higher than DOL and other ethers like diglyme (G2), exhibiting that the designed solvents have good thermodynamic stability with the Na metal negative electrode (Supplementary Fig. 7, Supplementary Data 1).Fig. 2The design of a wide-temperature electrolyte.a Boiling points, intermolecular interactions, binding energies of Na+ and electrostatic potential energies of DOL, DME, DX, MDX, G2, EC and PC. b Enthalpy of vaporization of DOL, DME, DMC, DX, MDX, G2, EC and PC. c Saturated vapor pressures of DOL, DME, DMC, DX, MDX, G2, EC and PC at different temperatures. d Binary binding energy between DOL, DX and MDX respectively. e Electrostatic potential mapping of DME, DOL, DX, MDX, G2, EC and PC molecules. In addition, the solvation capability of DX and MDX was evaluated by the electrostatic potential mapping and binding energy between Na+ and solvents (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Data 1). It indicates that the lowest electrostatic potential point of DX and MDX is −1.34 and −1.35 eV, respectively, which is comparable to that of DOL ( −1.32 eV), but significantly lower than the generally strong-solvation DME ( −2.33 eV), G2 ( −2.07 eV) and ester carbonates, like ethylene carbonate (EC) ( −1.70 eV), propylene carbonate (PC) ( −1.74 eV). The binding energy between Na+ and the solvents also shows the same trend. The DX and MDX illustrate slightly higher binding energies with Na+ than DOL, but they are still much lower than DME, G2 and EC etc., confirming that DX and MDX retain the weak-solvation capability (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Data 1, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Therefore, it can be confirmed that the designed DX and MDX solvents have the low volatility due to the high molecular interaction, which is beneficial for the high-temperature safety, and retain weak-solvation capability for improving electrochemical reversibility at low temperature.

The electrolyte with the mixed DX and MDX was initially prepared with NaPF6 salt. However, the 0.5 M NaPF6 cannot be fully dissolved in the solvents due to the weak-solvation capability, and thus the ionic conductivity at various temperatures is too low (Fig. 2g). Therefore, the wide-liquid-range strong-solvation ether G2 was introduced in the electrolyte (denoted as 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX). Moreover, the pure G2-based strong-solvation electrolyte was prepared as the control electrolyte (denoted as 0.5 M NaPF6-G2). The optical photo of the three electrolytes shows that NaPF6 can be fully dissolved after the introduction of G2 (Supplementary Fig. 9). The electrolytes of 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX and 0.5 M NaPF6-G2 exhibit high ionic conductivities from 70 °C to −70 °C (Fig. 2g). In addition, the resultant electrolyte 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX also shows enhanced thermal stability compared with the general weakly solvating electrolytes (WSEs) (Supplementary Fig. 10). Following that, the Na | |Na symmetric cells were assembled with the two electrolytes and evaluated at 50 °C, 25 °C and −40 °C, respectively. It can be found that the Na | |Na symmetric cells with the two electrolytes show stable voltage profiles at 50 °C and 25 °C at the current density of 1 mA cm−2. However, the Na | |Na symmetric cell can still work well at −40 °C with 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX, while the cell exhibits quite high and unstable overpotentials with 0.5 M NaPF6-G2 at the current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 (Fig. 2h, i). The discrepancy demonstrates the efficacy of the weak-solvation solvent of DX and MDX in the amelioration of the low-temperature electrochemical reversibility. It is noted that the 0.5 M NaPF6 can prevent the quick drop of ionic conductivity of electrolytes for the wide-temperature-range compared with 1 M and 2 M NaPF6 and thus 0.5 M NaPF6 is adopted in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Electrochemical performances over a wide temperature range

The long-term Na metal batteries were further checked in the electrolyte of 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX across a wide temperature range. The Na | |Na symmetric cells exhibit stable Na plating/stripping for over 600 and 1700 hours at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 at 50 °C and 25 °C, respectively (Fig. 3a, b). The average overpotentials are as low as ca. 5 and 10 mV, respectively. Moreover, the Na | |Na symmetric cell shows stable Na plating/stripping for 2000 hours at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 at −40 °C with a low overpotential of 12 mV (Fig. 3b). Meanwhile, the replication experiments consistently show good performances (Supplementary Fig. 12). It can be seen that most of the electrolytes in literature can only facilitate either high- or low-temperature performance, while the present electrolyte favors the wide-temperature performances of the Na | |Na symmetric cells with competitive current densities and plating/stripping durability (Fig. 3c)18,23,26,27,37. The Na | |Na symmetric cells with higher active capacities also exhibit stable plating/stripping at −40 °C, illustrating good promise for future applications (Supplementary Fig. 13). The electrolyte also exhibits good anti-oxidation capability in the wide-temperature-range (Supplementary Fig. 14), and the oxidation potential is higher than 4 V vs. Na/Na+ even at 50 °C, which is compatible with most of the electrode materials in Na batteries. The Na | |Na3V2(PO4)3 (NVP) cells show stable cycling for 500 times at 50 °C and −40 °C, respectively (Fig. 3d). The cells still retain the reversible capacity of 110 and 65 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles, respectively. At 25 °C, the Na | |NVP cell can be cycled for 1600 times with the reversible capacity of 102 mAh g−1 (Fig. 3e, f). Likewise, the good performances were reproduced in subsequent experiments (Supplementary Fig. 15, 16). The Na | |NVP cell with a low negative/positive (N/P) electrode capacity ratio displays the reversible capacity of 99 mAh g−1 with stable cycling (Supplementary Fig. 17). In addition to the coin cell tests, the Na | |NVP pouch cells were assembled to further demonstrate the application potential of the electrolyte. It illustrates stable cycling over 400 times at 25 °C (Fig. 3g, h). The reversible capacity still reaches about 10 mAh (83 mAh g−1) even at −40 °C after 50 cycles (Fig. 3i). Both the coin and pouch cells demonstrate the great promise of the electrolyte for wide-temperature applications. In the temperature-dependent test, the Na | |Na symmetric cell and Na | |NVP cell can be stably cycled from −40 °C to 70 °C, respectively (Fig. 3j, k, Supplementary Fig. 18). The performances rival most of those in literature in terms of the working temperature range and reversible capacity (Supplementary Fig. 19)18,37–41. The plating morphology of Na metal negative electrode after Na plating/stripping was checked by optical and scanning electron microscopy. The solvation microstructures of the electrolytes at different temperatures

In order to reveal the origin of the cycling distinction of the two electrolytes (0.5 M NaPF6-G2, 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX) at different temperatures, the solvation microstructures of the electrolytes were thoroughly explored. Firstly, the temperature-dependent 17O spectra of the mixed solvents of G2, DX, and MDX with and without NaPF6 were scrutinized (Supplementary Fig. 21). Both the chemical shift and peak distortion manifest that G2 is dominantly solvated with Na+ than MDX and DX at room temperature42. However, when the temperature decreases to −40 °C, MDX and DX show a slightly increased existence in the solvation sheath (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, the electrolyte solvation structures of the two electrolytes are simulated by the classic molecular dynamics. It is found that both the PF6- anions and G2 exist in the solvation sheath at the three temperatures and the relative amount of G2 increases for 0.5 M NaPF6-G2 as temperature decreases (Supplementary Fig. 22, Supplementary Data 1). The solvation sheaths of 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX are quite similar at 50 °C and 25 °C and PF6- anions and the strong-solvation G2 dominate the solvation sheath (Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Data 1). When temperature drops to −40 °C, more MDX and DX solvents enter the solvation sheath (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Data 1). As shown in Fig. 4d, the ratio of G2 decreases from about 90% at 50 °C and 25 °C to 65% at −40 °C. The total ratio of MDX and DX correspondingly increases from 10% to 35%. In addition, the residence time of solvents in the solvation sheath obtained from the MD trajectories indicates that at 50 °C and 25 °C, DX and MDX exhibit significantly shorter mean residence times within the solvation sheath compared with G2. When temperature decreases to −40 °C, the gap of mean residence times for DX/MDX and G2 becomes smaller (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Figs. 23–25, Supplementary Data 1). It also manifests that DX and MDX exhibit an increased attendance in the solvation sheath at −40 °C compared with that at 25 °C and 50 °C (Fig. 4f). The typical snapshots of the solvation sheath at different temperatures for the two electrolytes are shown in Supplementary Fig. 26. To unravel the reason of the solvation sheath transition at different temperatures, the other two kinds of electrolytes with weak solvents, 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), 2,4-dimethyl-1,3-dioxane (DMDX), were constructed, and denoted as 0.5 M NaPF6-G2/DOL (1:1 in volume) and 0.5 M NaPF6-G2/DMDX (1:1 in volume), respectively. The density functional theory (DFT) calculation shows that binary intermolecular binding energies for the weak solvents increase in the order: DOL < DX < MDX < DMDX (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Fig. 27 and Supplementary Data 1). Moreover, the 0.5 M NaPF6-G2/DOL and 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX electrolytes exhibit a pronounced solvation sheath transition upon cooling from 25 °C to −40 °C, accompanied by increased incorporation of DOL or DX/MDX into the solvation sheath (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 28, Supplementary Data 1). In contrast, 0.5 M NaPF6-G2/DMDX electrolyte shows a weaker solvation sheath transition over the same temperature range and the proportion of DMDX in the solvation sheath is notably lower than that with DOL or DX/MDX at −40 °C. The solvent clustering or freezing can occur instead, according to the optical photo (Supplementary Fig. 29) and mean diffusion coefficients (Supplementary Fig. 30, Supplementary Data 1) of electrolytes at −40 °C. This indicates that excessively strong intermolecular interactions, such as those seen with DMDX, can induce electrolyte freezing or clustering rather than necessitating an effective solvation sheath transition. The significantly slowed down desolvation or ligand exchange process at −40 °C, indicated by the mean residence time (2978.4 ps at −40 °C; 810.7 ps at 25 °C) of G2 in the solvation sheath, can leave some exposed space of the solvation sheath during the ligand exchange process to weak-solvation solvents to get contact to Na+ despite the weaker binding energy. However, if the intermolecular interaction is too strong, solvent clustering or freezing can occur instead of the solvation sheath transition. Therefore, too strong intermolecular interaction between solvents is also not appropriate for the low-temperature batteries (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Data 1). Not only the intermolecular interaction matters, the ion-dipole interaction should also correlate to the desolvation process directly. The binding energy between Na+ and G2 is higher than that of Na+ with DX or MDX from 50 to −40 °C (Supplementary Fig. 31, Supplementary Data 1). As G2 dominates the solvation sheath for both the two kinds of electrolytes at 50 and 25 °C, the desolvation energies of them are close but low at such temperatures. In contrast, the desolvation energy of 0.5 M NaPF6-G2 at −40 °C obviously rises while that of 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX remains at the same level, illustrating the importance of increased weak-solvation solvents in the solvation sheath on the low-temperature desolvation barrier and thus the low-temperature kinetics (Fig. 4i, Supplementary Data 1). The increased intermolecular binding energy also has a direct effect on improving the high-temperature cell performances beyond lowering volatility of solvents. The Na | |NVP cell with 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX shows longer lifespan than that with DOL-based electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 32). The pouch cell with 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX facilitates lower swelling when exposed to elevated temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 33). When the electrolyte is rested at 70 °C, the 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX electrolyte exhibits higher stability from the 1H NMR results (Supplementary Fig. 34). Furthermore, 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX electrolyte illustrates higher stability in terms of the electrolyte and interphase impedance evolution through the electrochemical impedance spectra during cycling at 70 °C (Supplementary Fig. 35, Supplementary Table 2). The more stable and uniform interphase may originate from the relatively lower dissolution or destruction of DX/MDX compared with the DOL-based electrolytes. The cryo-transmission electron microscopic images can confirm the more stable positive electrode | |electrolyte interphase in 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX (Supplementary Fig. 36). Benefiting from the more uniform interphase, the NVP positive electrode in 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX exhibits slightly lower dissolution of vanadium during cycling at 70 °C (Supplementary Fig. 37). The differential electrochemical mass spectra show that 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX electrolyte facilitates a minor gas evolution during charge process (Supplementary Fig. 38). The high-temperature tests illustrate the efficacy of the enhanced intermolecular interactions on the high-temperature battery performances. Therefore, considering both the high- and low-temperature situations, optimizing intermolecular interactions are critical for the wide-temperature performances (Fig.4g).

Interfacial charge transfer process at different temperatures

Based on the different solvation structures, the interfacial kinetics were further explored at different temperatures. The overpotentials of Na | |Na symmetric cells at different current densities were monitored at 50, 25, and −40 °C respectively. The solvation reorganization energy was simulated according to the Marcus-Hush model, based on which the solvation sheath reorganizes at the interface to accept electrons for Na+ during the plating process (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 39). The reorganization energy has a direct influence on the kinetics of interfacial charge transfer43–46. It can be observed that the reorganization energy is comparable for the two electrolytes at 50 and 25 °C. The reorganization energy increases to 0.23 eV for 0.5 M NaPF6-G2 at −40 °C while it just shows a slight increase to 0.11 eV for 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX. It indicates the two electrolytes can have a low and close reorganization energy barrier mainly based on the fast thermal motion of solvents to overcome the resistance of ion-solvent and solvent-solvent interactions at 50 C and 25 °C as the reorganization process corresponds to the solvent displacement in the solvation sheath indeed. However, when the temperature drops to −40 °C, the thermal motion of solvents significantly decreases and thus the reorganization barrier increases for the two electrolytes. As MDX and DX solvents exhibit weaker binding with Na+ compared to G2, the reorganization energy for 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX does not show a significant increase, whereas that for 0.5 M NaPF6-G2 increases substantially, which may hinder interfacial kinetics at −40 °C. It can also be concluded that the free energy parabola can have a steeper slope, corresponding to a higher force constant, at low temperature than room temperature and the weak-solvation solvents can lower the force constant due to the weaker ion-solvent interactions (Fig. 5b)44,47. The low-temperature reorganization or desolvation process at the interface between the Na metal negative electrode and the electrolyte was further simulated using ab initio molecular dynamics (Fig. 5c)18,48. It can be seen that no obvious solvent removal is observed for 0.5 M NaPF6-G2 in the first 3 ps of simulation. G2 partly detaches from the solvation sheath while Na+ is still primarily coordinated by G2 after 20 ps of simulation for 0.5 M NaPF6-G2. In contrast, the MDX solvent starts to escape from the solvation sheath after 3 ps and Na+ is noticeably exposed to the Na electrode after 20 ps of simulation for 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX. Interphase structure between Na metal negative electrode and electrolyte

The electrochemical impedance spectra were obtained for the Na | |Na symmetric cells with the two electrolytes from room to low temperature. The impedance spectra were converted into two-dimensional spectra with the distribution of relaxation time49,50. It can be seen that all the impedances for the ionic transport in electrolyte (Rs), ionic desolvation (Rdes), and charge transfer (Rct) increase as the temperature decreases (Fig. 6a, b). However, the Rdes for 0.5 M NaPF6-G2 increases most apparently at −40 °C, which manifests that the desolvation process is mainly responsible for the reversibility discrepancy between the two electrolytes at −40 °C. The interphase structures of Na metal negative electrode with the two electrolytes were investigated by time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)51. The two electrolytes promote quite similar interphase structures from the TOF-SIMS (Fig. 6c, d). The organic components align mostly in the outer layer and the inorganic NaF, Na2O are distributed from the outer to the inner layer. More specifically, the in-depth XPS results demonstrate similar results (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 40). The Na-O, NaF can be seen from the surface to inner part. In contrast, the organic C-O, C-C species are mainly at the surface and the P-F species also appear on the surface, which corresponds to the trace amount of remaining of Na salts from the electrolyte. Based on the analysis of solvation microstructure and interfacial kinetics above, the intermolecular interaction of solvents can be confirmed to be critical but has been overlooked for the electrolyte of wide-temperature batteries, besides the ion-solvent interactions. On one hand, the enhanced intermolecular interactions can lower the volatility of solvents and thus raise the safety at high temperatures. On the other hand, the too strong intermolecular interactions of weak solvents can not facilitate effective solvation sheath transition at low temperature and thereby hinder the desolvation kinetics. Herein, we note that designing a wide-temperature electrolyte can be achieved through a relatively straightforward method by rationally upgrading commonly used, highly volatile, weakly solvating solvents. This approach focuses solely on the intermolecular interactions of the solvents, whereas modifying strong-solvation solvents requires consideration of both ion-solvent and intermolecular interactions, increasing the complexity (Fig. 6f).

Discussion

In this work, we strengthen the intermolecular interaction of general volatile weak-solvation solvents by expanding the ring of the weak solvent and introducing a methyl group on the ring, generating the ester-carbonate-level low-volatile DX and MDX ether solvents with weakly solvating capabilities. The enhanced intermolecular interaction between weak solvents is confirmed to ameliorate the high-temperature performances and safety, beyond lowering the volatility. When collocating with the strong-solvation ethers, the electrolytes can favor the Na metal cells' highly reversible performances across a wide-temperature-range under decent current densities from −40 °C to 70 °C, far rivaling most of the wide-temperature Na metal batteries in literature. Moreover, we unravel that optimized intermolecular interaction can promote the increased weak-solvation solvents to enter the solvation sheath due to the much slower desolvation process of strong-solvation solvents at low temperature, improving the low-temperature kinetics. The work supplies a potential guidance for the rational solvent design based on the consideration of the intermolecular and ion-solvent interactions together rather than only the latter one for the wide-temperature batteries.

Methods

Materials

The diglyme (G2, >99.8%), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, >99.9%), and NaPF6 (>99.8%) were purchased from Dodochem with battery grade. 1,3-Dioxane (DX, 98%) and 2,4-dimethyl-1,3-dioxane (DMDX, 98%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. 4-Methyl-1,3-dioxane (MDX, >99.0%) was purchased from TCI. The solvents were dried with activated 4 Å molecular sieves for 24 h before use, respectively. The Na metal foils for the coin cell were bought from Dodochem with a diameter of 12 mm and a thickness of 0.4-0.5 mm, which is supported by an aluminum (Al) substrate and protected by a plastic film. Metallic Na ingot (99.8%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. NVP (battery grade) was bought from Tianjin Rongbai Scolande Technology Co., Ltd. N-Methylpyrrolidone (NMP, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}≥99.9%) with analytical grade was bought from Aladdin. Polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, battery grade) and Ketjenblack (KB, battery grade) were purchased from Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.

Preparation of electrolytes

The electrolytes were prepared by dissolving NaPF6 in the pretreated solvents in an argon-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm, Mikrouna). The electrolytes include: 0.5 M NaPF6 in G2/DX/MDX (2:1:1 in volume), denoted as 0.5 M NaPF6-GDX; 0.5 M NaPF6 in G2, denoted as 0.5 M NaPF6-G2; 0.5 M NaPF6 in DX/MDX (1:1 in volume), denoted as 0.5 M NaPF6-DX/MDX.

Preparation of electrodes

The NVP positive electrode for the coin cell was fabricated by mixing the NVP powder, conductive agent (KB), and PVDF with a weight ratio of 8:1:1 using the NMP solvent. After stirring overnight, the obtained slurry was uniformly single-sided coated onto the carbon-coated Al foil using an automatic film applicator (BEVS1811/2, BEVS Industrial Co., Ltd.) with an adjustable doctor blade (Canrd-KTC-150, Canrd), and subsequently dried at 80 °C overnight in a vacuum oven. Following that, the NVP foil was cut into circular disks with a diameter of 12 mm using a manual disc cutter (MSK-T10, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd.). The mass loading of the active materials is about 2 mg cm−2. The NVP positive electrode for the pouch cell was fabricated by mixing the NVP powder, conductive agent (acetylene black), and PVDF binder with the weight ratio of 85:7.5:7.5 using the NMP solvent. After stirring overnight, the obtained slurry was cast on the carbon-coated Al foil, and dried at 80 °C overnight in a vacuum oven. Then, the NVP foil was cut into rectangular foils with a size of 5×5 cm. The mass loading of the active materials is about 5.0 mg cm−2. Sodium metal negative electrodes were prepared entirely in an argon-filled glove box. Commercial sodium foil for coin cells was used directly after removing the surface protective films on both sides without further treatment. For pouch cells, the surface oxide layer and impurities of the sodium ingot were thoroughly scraped to expose fresh sodium metal, which was then pressed into thin foils (800 μm in thickness) and cut into rectangular pieces with required dimensions for subsequent cell assembly.

Electrochemical tests

The Na | |Na symmetric cells were assembled in CR2032 coin-type configurations with 304 stainless steel cases and springs, employing two sodium metal electrodes separated by a Celgard 2400 separator (25 μm, 19 mm in diameter), with 50 μL electrolyte per cell. Galvanostatic cycling of Na | |Na symmetric cells was conducted at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 at −40 °C, and 1 mA cm−2 at 25 °C and 50 °C. Na | |NVP cells were constructed using NVP positive electrodes, sodium metal negative electrodes, and Whatman glass fiber separators (GF/A, 260 μm, 19 mm in diameter), with 100 μL electrolyte injected into each cell. For Na | |NVP full cells, the charge-discharge rate was set to 0.3 C at −40 °C, 1 C at 25 °C, and 5 C at 50 °C, where 1 C is defined as 118 mA g−1. The cut-off voltage window was 2.5-3.8 V for measurements at 25 °C and −40 °C, and 2.5-3.6 V for high-temperature tests at 50 °C. The capacity retention was calculated as the ratio of the discharge capacity at the final cycle to the discharge capacity at the initial cycle. The single-layer pouch cells were constructed using rectangular electrodes (5×5 cm) featuring NVP positive electrodes. The Na metal negative electrode in the pouch cell was prepared by cutting the Na metal ingot into a rectangular foil (5×5 cm) after scraping the oxide layer on the surface. The Whatman glass fiber (GF/A, 6×6 cm) was adopted. After injecting 2 mL of electrolyte, the pouch cell was degassed under vacuum and thermally sealed. All of the cell assembly processes were carried out in the argon-filled glove box (H2O < 0.1 ppm and O2 < 0.1 ppm). All electrochemical cycling tests were conducted in a programmable environmental temperature chamber (BTZ-175E, Espec) with stable temperature control ranging from −70 to 70 °C. The ambient cycling tests were performed at an average temperature of 25 ± 2 °C. The coin cells were held at a designated temperature for a minimum of six hours prior to testing in order to achieve thermal equilibrium. All of the cells were tested using the Lanhe battery testing system (CT3001A). 1 C is defined as a specific current of 118 mA g−1 for all Na | |NVP cells in this work. At least two parallel cells were assembled and tested for each electrochemical experiment. All electrochemical curves displayed in the main text are selected from representative cells with stable and typical performance, which sufficiently reflect the overall electrochemical characteristics of the tested batches. Partial test data of parallel cells are provided in the Supplementary Information for reference.

The ionic conductivity of the electrolyte was determined by introducing the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) with a home-made symmetrical cell. The impedance spectra were obtained in the frequency range from 105 to 0.1 Hz at 0 V with two identical stainless-steel foils as the working and counter electrodes respectively. Linear scanning voltammetry (LSV) measurements were conducted from the open circuit potential to 5.5 V (vs. Na/Na⁺) with a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1. The EIS of the Na | |Na symmetric cell was measured by potentiostatic method at open-circuit potential with an amplitude of 10 mV in a frequency range from 105 to 0.1 Hz, with 10 points per decade. The EIS of the Na | |NVP cell was measured by potentiostatic method at open-circuit potential with an amplitude of 10 mV in a frequency range from 105 to 0.1 Hz, with 10 points per decade. The LSV and EIS measurements were conducted using the electrochemical workstation of Autolab IMP (AUT60052). The EIS data were then transformed into a distribution of relaxation times (DRT) plot with a DRTtools package.

Characterization

To investigate the thermal stability of various solvents, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed with the TGA Q500 V20.13 Build 39 instrument. The tests were conducted under an air atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. The temperature range is from 30 °C to 150 °C. The surface micromorphology of the cycled Na negative electrode was characterized using the Thermo Scientific Helios G4 CX microscope. The cycled Na | |Na cells were disassembled in a glovebox under an inert atmosphere. Subsequently, the harvested Na negative electrodes were washed with 1 mL of THF to remove the residual electrolyte and then dried under vacuum at 25 °C for 2 h. Finally the samples were transferred to the test chamber with a sealed container protected with the Ar atmosphere.

1H DOSY-NMR spectra at different temperatures were acquired with the coaxial NMR tube to avoid the possible effect of deuterated solvent on the solvation structure. Deuterated DMSO or methanol was injected in the inner tube, whereas the samples were placed in the outer tube of the coaxial NMR tube, respectively. The 17O NMR spectra of the electrolyte and the pure solvent mixture were acquired at different temperatures without deuterated solvents. The NMR tests above were based on the NMR (Bruker, Avance NEO, 600 MHz). A differential electrochemical mass spectrometer (QAS100 Li) was used to characterize the gas evolution of the Na | |NVP with argon gas as carrier gas under 1.14 mL min−1. The CEI layer on the positive electrode was analyzed by cryo-TEM (Krios G3i, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The high-temperature dissolution of vanadium was quantitatively evaluated using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES), with the instrument (Agilent 5110).

XPS was performed to determine the composition of the SEI that formed on the Na metal negative electrode after cycling. A PHI 5000 VersaProbe, equipped with a monochromatic aluminum K-alpha (Al Kα) X-ray source, was used for the analysis. Particular attention was paid to preventing air exposure: after disassembling the cells in a glovebox, sodium disks were rinsed with THF and dried under vacuum. The sample was hermetically sealed for transport to the XPS system. To examine the chemical composition as a function of depth, argon ion (Ar+) sputtering was used to etch away the outer layers of the SEI and expose the underlying structure information. The surface compositional analysis was also performed utilizing the PHI nanoTOF II time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometer (TOF-SIMS, PerkinElmer). The primary bismuth ion beam was utilized, with an energy of 30 keV and a raster size of 100 μm ×100 μm.
