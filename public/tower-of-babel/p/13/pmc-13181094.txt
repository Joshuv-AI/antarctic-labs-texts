Digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration in high-tech industries
Authors: Yifang Zhang, Haocan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhancing supply chain resilience and security in high-tech industries is crucial for mitigating risks arising from dependence on critical technologies amid global uncertainties. Using firm- and product-level data from China’s high-tech industries during 2010–2022, this study investigates how industrial digitalization affects upstream supply chain source concentration (USC) at the industry level through a two-way fixed effects model. The results reveal a U-shaped relationship between industrial digitalization and USC, mediated by human capital accumulation and risk-taking capacity. The effect is stronger in labor-intensive and highly concentrated industries, reflecting long-term strategic adjustments rather than short-term fluctuations. The findings call for a balanced digitalization strategy that manages its dual effects, reinforces human capital and risk management capacity, and relies on coherent institutional frameworks to sustain transformation and strengthen supply chain resilience.

Introduction

High-tech industries, founded on major technological breakthroughs and critical development needs, have increasingly played a leading role in shaping long-term global economic and social development. Their innovation and growth rely not only on domestic R&D and manufacturing capacity but also heavily on cross-border access to key technologies and core intermediate goods. With the continued deepening of global value chain division, such dependence on external inputs has become more prominent.When upstream supply sources become overly concentrated, the potential risks to supply chain security and technological stability are significantly amplified Consequently, many governments have integrated diversification of critical supply sources—particularly in upstream inputs— into their strategic and industrial policies to strengthen resilience and reduce vulnerability. For example, the European Union introduced the Critical Raw Materials Act, the United States enacted the CHIPS and Science Act, and China proposed the Industrial and Supply Chain Security Strategy. In practice, the rapid expansion of the digital economy, combined with rising global uncertainties, is reshaping the supply chain landscape of high-tech industries, thereby altering the concentration of upstream supply chain sources. On the other hand, excessive digitalization may intensify concentration as transaction costs associated with coordination, compliance, and technological upgrading rise again with system complexity. Nevertheless, the overall effect of industrial digitalization on upstream supply chain source concentration—and the mechanisms through which it operates—remain underexplored. At the theoretical level, existing studies lack systematic tests on upstream supply chain source concentration and its dynamic evolution, and research focusing specifically on high-tech industries is even more limited. Therefore, investigating how industrial digitalization affects upstream supply chain source concentration not only helps to assess the true role of industrial digitalization in supply chain security but also provides new empirical evidence for international trade and industrial policy.

Related research primarily examines the impact of digitalization on supply chain resilience. Scholars have defined and measured supply chain resilience from different perspectives to explore its relationship with digitalization. For example, Bag et al.1, argue that digital transformation of core firms can enhance supply chain sustainability by strengthening suppliers’ ESG performance and improving collaboration practices. Hald and Coslugeanu2, suggest that the application of technologies such as artificial intelligence and big data can increase supply chain flexibility and visibility, thereby enhancing resilience. Qi et al.3, further verify the positive effects of digital transformation on supply chain resilience from the dimensions of resistance and recovery. These studies indicate that digitalization has become an important driver of supply chain resilience. However, most of the findings focus on resilience as a whole and seldom address critical aspects such as supply chain source concentration.

Within the broader literature on supply chain resilience, upstream supply chain source concentration emerges as a particularly critical yet underexplored dimension. Yet, the literature examining the impact of digitalization on upstream supply chain source concentration remains limited. Most existing studies focus on other determinants. At the micro level, Chod et al.4, find that when firms face expansion in downstream external demand, their upstream supply chain source concentration decreases. Namdar et al.5, argue that under policy uncertainty, firms are more likely to pursue geographical diversification to mitigate risks. At the macro level, Jaimovich6, shows that rising per capita income and population scale reduce the concentration of national import sources, while Ly-My et al.7, find that the WTO’s Aid for Trade initiative significantly lowers import source concentration in recipient countries. A few scholars have attempted to link digitalization with upstream supply chain source concentration8–10, but such studies remain at an aggregate level and lack systematic, dynamic examinations of specific industries—particularly regarding how industrial digitalization affects supply chain source concentration over different time horizons.

Against the backdrop of intensifying global technological competition and geopolitical risks, understanding structural changes in upstream supply chains has attracted growing scholarly attention. Related empirical studies on supply source concentration have developed relatively established measurement and modeling approaches, providing useful methodological references for subsequent empirical research11,12. However, Existing research largely emphasizes overall supply chain resilience, while systematic exploration of upstream supply chain source concentration and its dynamic evolution remains scarce. To fill this gap, this paper makes three contributions. First, in terms of research perspective, unlike most studies that focus on overall resilience, it takes upstream supply chain source concentration and its dynamic evolution as the entry point, systematically testing nonlinear patterns of change in the context of industrial digitalization, thereby extending the boundaries of resilience research. Second, regarding the research object, this study focuses on high-tech industries, which carry both global and strategic significance, thus responding to the practical concerns of digital transformation and supply chain security in international industrial policy. Third, from a research dimension, this study incorporates a temporal perspective by distinguishing between long-term and short-term effects, thereby revealing stage-specific differences in how industrial digitalization influences upstream supply chain source concentration and enhancing the understanding of its reconfiguration dynamics.

Hypotheses development

As shown in Fig. 1, this study focuses on upstream supply chain source concentration in high-tech industries, which refers to the concentration level of import sources for intermediate goods. The development of high-tech industries is inherently subject to considerable uncertainty. According to transaction cost economics13, such uncertainty may significantly increase transaction costs in firms’ governance structures and supplier relationship choices. industrial digitalization, as a factor that can either reduce or raise transaction costs, may have markedly different effects at different stages. In the early stages of industrial industrial digitalization, the use of digital technologies can substantially lower firms’ search and coordination costs, making it easier to access external information, identify potential partners, and engage in cross-border transactions, thereby reducing upstream supply chain source concentration. This corresponds with supply chain resilience theory14, which emphasizes that diversification helps disperse risks and strengthen resilience15. However, as industrial digitalization deepens, the distribution of upstream supply sources becomes more globalized and dispersed, leading to rising cross-regional coordination and governance costs. Firms are then compelled to allocate more resources to technology maintenance, regulatory compliance, and managerial burdens, which further reinforces transaction cost constraints. Under such conditions, firms may shift toward more concentrated sourcing strategies to alleviate the complexity-related pressures. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 1

Digitalization in high-tech industries has a U-shaped effect on upstream supply chain source concentration.

Regarding the underlying mechanisms, this study highlights two intermediary channels under transaction cost constraints: human capital accumulation and risk-taking capacity. For the human capital channel, human capital is regarded as the fundamental capability for effective governance under transaction cost constraints. Human capital theory16 posits that skills and knowledge reserves are essential resources for enhancing productivity and competitiveness. This capability is reflected not only in production efficiency but also in the ability to manage environmental complexity and supply chain governance challenges. In the early stages of industrial digitalization, firms require significant digital talent to implement new technologies, manage systems, and drive transformation. To meet these demands, firms increase investments in employee training and skill upgrading, accelerating human capital accumulation17. Higher levels of human capital enable firms to identify and leverage comparative advantages across multiple sources at lower governance costs, thereby reducing supply chain concentration. However, as industrial digitalization advances, some technologies and managerial processes become standardized, reducing the marginal effect of additional human capital on lowering governance costs. Meanwhile, rigid expenditures related to system upgrades, technical maintenance, and compliance rise sharply, crowding out ongoing investment in human capital. In such circumstances, the marginal returns to human capital decline, weakening firms’ capabilities to manage complex and diversified supply chains, thereby raising transaction costs18. Consequently, firms are more likely to simplify supply chain structures and concentrate sourcing in fewer regions to reduce governance and coordination costs. Based on this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 2

Human capital accumulation mediates the relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration in high-tech industries, leading to a U-shaped effect.

For the risk-taking channel, risk-taking is understood as a firm’s tolerance for and capacity to manage uncertainty under transaction cost constraints. In the early stages of industrial digitalization, improvements in information transparency and governance efficiency generally enhance firms’ risk-taking capacity, enabling them to identify and manage the risks associated with diversified sourcing, thus encouraging them to expand source diversification to mitigate shocks15. However, as industrial digitalization further intensifies, the costs of system upgrades, technology selection, and compliance increase substantially, and the resources required for governing diversified supply chains also rise. Firms’ capacity to bear risks is therefore expected to decline19,20. At this point, transaction cost logic suggests that firms will adopt more cautious strategies, concentrating their sourcing in fewer locations to mitigate managerial complexity and potential risks21. This leads to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3

Risk-taking capacity mediates the relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration in high-tech industries, leading to a U-shaped effect.

It is worth noting that the impact of industrial digitalization on upstream supply chain source concentration exhibits temporal differences. Along the transaction cost logic, the intermediary channels of human capital accumulation and risk-taking capacity may not undergo large-scale changes in the short run, making the overall effect less significant. Over the longer term, however, the sustained accumulation and adjustment of these mechanisms are likely to exert substantive effects on sourcing configurations, rendering the overall impact more significant.

Research design

Sample selection and data sources

As high-tech industries remain a relatively new field, currently available firm–product-level data are somewhat outdated, which to some extent limits scholars’ ability to conduct in-depth analyses of their dynamic development and market responsiveness. Considering data availability, continuity, and completeness, this study selects Chinese data from 2010 to 2022 as the research sample and manually compiles firm–industry and product–industry datasets at the industry level for empirical testing. This choice is justified not only because data on these industries are relatively accessible in China, but also because China intensively introduced relevant industrial and digitalization policies during this period, which created favorable conditions for conducting systematic research. It should be noted that official Chinese documents generally refer to these industries as “strategic emerging industries.” In this study, however, they are referred to as high-tech industries to maintain consistency with international academic literature.

First, we selected 28 secondary industries under nine major high-tech industry categories (see Table 1). Referring to the Shenwan Industry Classification Standard (2021 edition), we matched the “key products and services” identified in the Catalogue for the Classification of Strategic Emerging Industries (2023) and the Classification of Strategic Emerging Industries (2018) with the products that accounted for the largest share of operating revenue among Chinese A-share listed firms by the end of 2022. The following treatments were applied: (i) excluding firms under abnormal status such as ST, *ST, or SST during the sample period; and (ii) excluding firms listed in or after 2018. This yielded 1,782 firms, for which data from 2010 to 2022 were selected as the final sample.

Second, based on the “key products and services” of each industry listed in the Catalogue for the Classification of Strategic Emerging Industries (2023) and the Classification of Strategic Emerging Industries (2018), and with reference to industry chain maps published by institutions such as the China Business Industry Research Institute and Qianzhan Industry Research Institute, we manually compiled a list of 602 upstream key components across 28 industries and identified their HS six-digit codes. For illustration, Fig. 2 presents the upstream supply chain structure of the next-generation information network industry, in which the second layer from the bottom shows the industry’s key products, the third layer represents their key components, and the fourth layer lists the HS six-digit codes of these components.

The firm-level data were obtained from the CSMAR (China Stock Market & Accounting Research) database, CNINFO, the China Research Data Services Platform (CNRDS), and the Wind database. The product-level data were sourced from UN Comtrade, the WTO Tariff Database, and the World Bank.

Empirical analysis

Baseline regression

Table 3 reports the baseline regression results on the impact of industrial digitalization on upstream supply chain source concentration in high-tech industries. All regressions include industry and year fixed effects, with standard errors clustered at the industry level. In Column (1), the coefficient of the core explanatory variable (digit) is statistically insignificant. After introducing the squared term (digit²) in Column (2), the results show that the linear term (digit) is significantly negative at the 1% level, while the squared term (digit²) is significantly positive at the 1% level. This U-shaped relationship remains robust after the inclusion of control variables in Column (3). To further verify whether the effect of industrial digitalization on upstream supply chain source concentration indeed follows a U-shaped pattern, we conducted the U-test following the approach of Haans & Pieters (2016). The results, reported in Table 4, indicate that the turning point of industrial digitalization is 0.807, which lies within the observed value range [0.046, 1.781], with a p-value of 0.021. These findings provide statistical evidence that the relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration exhibits a U-shaped pattern, thereby confirming Hypothesis H1. To further illustrate the nonlinear relationship, Fig. 3 plots the predicted relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration, with the dashed vertical line indicating the estimated turning point.

Endogeneity test

To address potential reverse causality, we adopt an instrumental variable (IV) approach. Following the interaction-based IV design proposed by Nunn and Qian29, we construct the instrument as the interaction between fixed telephone penetration in 1984 and lagged industrial digitalization and its squared term, and estimate the model using two-stage least squares (2SLS). Fixed telephone penetration in 1984 reflects the level of early telecommunications infrastructure and generates persistent effects on subsequent digital development through long-term technological accumulation and network foundations, thereby ensuring a strong correlation with current industrial digitalization. Meanwhile, with the rapid advancement of digital technologies and the replacement of fixed-line systems by modern broadband, mobile networks, and digital platforms, early telecommunications infrastructure mainly captured basic communication capacity in a pre-digital era and exhibits substantial technological discontinuity from contemporary production networks. As a result, historical telephone penetration is unlikely to directly affect current upstream supply chain source concentration in high-tech industries. After controlling for industry and year fixed effects and relevant covariates, the instrument influences upstream supply chain source concentration mainly through industrial digitalization, thereby satisfying the relevance and exclusion restrictions.

To further assess the validity of the instrument, we conduct a series of formal statistical tests. Table 5 reports the relevant results. The Kleibergen-Paap rk LM statistic strongly rejects the null hypothesis of under-identification at the 1% significance level. Moreover, the Kleibergen-Paap rk Wald F statistic exceeds the Stock-Yogo critical values at the 10% significance level, suggesting that the weak instrument problem is not a concern and confirming the validity of the chosen instruments. Importantly, the coefficient of the core explanatory variable remains consistent in sign and significance with the baseline regression, indicating that the U-shaped relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration is robust and not driven by endogeneity.

Robustness tests

Replacing the core explanatory variable

Because the keyword dictionaries used for the five dimensions of digitalization—artificial intelligence, big data, cloud computing, blockchain, and digital technology application—contain different numbers of keywords, potential measurement errors may arise in constructing the digitalization index30. To address this, we follow the approach of Yuan et al.31 and reconstruct the digitalization indicator as follows. First, we separately compute five sub-indices for each dimension (i.e., AI, BigData, Cloud, Blockchain, DigitalApp). Second, each sub-index is normalized annually to eliminate differences in scale. Finally, the normalized sub-indices are aggregated to obtain a new firm-level digitalization frequency index, and the average across all firms within each high-tech industry is taken as the new industry-level digitalization variable (digit_replace), with its squared term denoted as digit_replace². Column (1) of Table 6 reports the results. The coefficient of digit_replace is significantly negative, while that of digit_replace² is significantly positive, indicating the robustness of the U-shaped relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration.

Lagged independent variable

Given that the effects of digitalization in high-tech industries may materialize with a time lag32, we further incorporate the one-period lag of the digitalization variable and its squared term into the regression specification. This lagged structure is motivated by the approach in Zhang and Du33. As reported in Column (2) of Table 6, the estimated coefficients remain consistent in both sign and statistical significance with the baseline results, further confirming the robustness of the results.

Winsorization

Extreme values may distort coefficient estimates and inference, particularly when variables exhibit heavy-tailed distributions. To mitigate this concern, all variables are winsorized at the 1 st and 99th percentiles34. Column (3) of Table 6 presents the corresponding results. After controlling for the influence of outliers, the U-shaped relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration remains intact. The magnitude and significance of the key coefficients are largely unchanged, supporting the robustness of the baseline findings.

Time–shock interaction

To ensure that the estimated relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration is not driven by time-specific disturbances caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, we introduce a time-shock interaction specification. Following the approach used in Ivanov et al.35, we create a dummy variable for the period 2020–2022 to capture potential pandemic-related shocks, and we include interaction terms between this dummy and the digitalization variables (digit and digit²) in the model. The results presented in Column (4) of Table 6 show that the interaction terms are statistically insignificant. This suggests that the effect of industrial digitalization on upstream supply chain source concentration does not exhibit structural changes during the COVID-19 period. Thus, we find no evidence that the identified U-shaped relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration is driven by pandemic-related disturbances. This result further supports the robustness of the U-shaped relationship.

Heterogeneity analysis

Industry factor intensity

Industries are classified based on their factor intensity using 2010 as the benchmark year, when China’s industrial structure and industrial census data became relatively stable and comparable across sectors. Samples with factor intensity values at or above the 2010 median are defined as capital-intensive industries, while those below the median threshold are categorized as labor-intensive industries. All other industries are considered labor-intensive. The grouped regression results are reported in Table 8. The results show that the nonlinear effect of industrial digitalization on upstream supply chain source concentration is more significant in labor-intensive high-tech industries. One possible explanation is that compared with labor-intensive industries, capital-intensive industries rely more heavily on large-scale fixed assets and high-tech equipment. Their supply chains tend to be concentrated among a few technologically advanced or highly efficient partners. Therefore, improvements in industrial digitalization have a less pronounced impact on the concentration of upstream supply sources in capital-intensive industries.

Upstream supply chain source concentration

High-tech industries differ markedly in their degree of supply chain concentration. Some rely on diversified sourcing to mitigate risks, while others pursue concentrated sourcing to strengthen control and coordination. As a result, the direction and magnitude of digitalization’s impact may vary across industries with different levels of upstream supply chain concentration. To capture these heterogeneous effects, quantile regressions are employed to examine the influence of industrial digitalization under low, medium, and high concentration scenarios. The results, reported in Table 9, reveal clear differences across quantiles. When upstream supply chain concentration is low or moderate (τ = 0.10, 0.25, 0.50), the effect of industrial digitalization on supply chain source concentration is statistically insignificant. However, when concentration reaches higher levels (τ = 0.75, 0.90), industrial digitalization exhibits a significant U-shaped effect. This pattern can be interpreted as follows. In high-tech industries characterized by low upstream supply concentration, suppliers are already sufficiently diversified, and transaction costs remain relatively stable; thus, the marginal impact of industrial digitalization is limited. By contrast, in industries with high upstream concentration, industrial digitalization introduces two countervailing forces. In its early stage, digitalization enhances information transparency and coordination efficiency, thereby lowering transaction costs and allowing industries to diversify their sourcing structures by engaging a broader range of suppliers, which mitigates dependency on a few sources. However, as digitalization advances, rising system complexity and regulatory demands—together with diminishing returns to human capital investment—may once again increase transaction costs. Consequently, industries tend to consolidate sourcing around a smaller group of reliable partners. The interplay of these opposing dynamics helps explain why the U-shaped relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply source concentration is more evident in highly concentrated industries.

Long-term and short-term effects

Given that both industrial digitalization and supply chain restructuring in high-tech industries are characterized by time lags and gradual adjustments, this study employs an error correction mechanism (ECM) to distinguish the long-term and short-term effects of industrial digitalization on upstream supply chain source concentration.The estimation results, presented in Table 10, show a significant nonlinear long-term relationship between industrial digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration. The coefficient of the linear term (digit) is significantly negative at the 5% level, while that of the squared term(digit2) is significantly positive at the 1% level, suggesting a U-shaped long-run effect. In the short run, however, the first-differenced terms of digit and digit² are statistically insignificant, implying that industrial digitalization does not immediately alter the configuration of upstream supply sources. Instead, its influence emerges gradually through long-term structural adjustments. The coefficient of the error correction term (ECT) is negative and significant at the 1% level (− 0.477), indicating that when the system deviates from its long-run equilibrium, approximately 47.7% of the disequilibrium is corrected each period. The estimated half-life of adjustment is about 1.84 years, meaning that it takes nearly two years for half of a shock to be absorbed. Overall, these findings imply that the impact of industrial digitalization on supply chain security and resilience is strategic and long-term in nature, rather than a short-term response to market fluctuations.

Conclusions, implications, and limitations

This study focuses on high-tech industries, systematically examining how industrial digitalization influences the concentration of upstream supply chain sources. The main conclusions are as follows: (1) In high-tech industries, digitalization and upstream supply chain source concentration exhibit a U-shaped relationship, a finding that remains robust across multiple model specifications and tests. (2) The influence of industrial digitalization in high-tech industries operates through two mediating mechanisms—human capital accumulation and risk-taking capacity—both of which also show a U-shaped pattern. (3) The nonlinear impact of digitalization is more pronounced in labor-intensive high-tech industries and in sectors with higher initial levels of concentration, indicating heterogeneous effects across different industrial structures. (4) The results of the ECM estimation reveal that the effect of digitalization in high-tech industries is mainly long-term and structural, suggesting that digital transformation reshapes supply chain configurations through gradual adjustment rather than short-term shocks.

These findings provide a foundation for policy formulation aimed at enhancing supply chain security and resilience in high-tech industries under digital transformation. Based on these results, four key policy implications are proposed: First, policymakers should carefully manage the dual effects of digitalization on the concentration of upstream supply chain sources within high-tech industries. Instead of pursuing rapid and unbalanced digital transformation, governments should establish industry-specific standards and regulatory guidelines that promote diversified sourcing in the early stages of transformation, while guarding against over-digitalization that could amplify concentration risks and technological dependency. Second, strengthening human capital development and risk management capacity is essential to mitigate the mediating effects of industrial digitalization on supply chain concentration in high-tech sectors. Since digitalization in these industries influences upstream concentration through the dual channels of human capital accumulation and risk-taking capability, governments should prioritize the cultivation of digital talent and design risk-sharing mechanisms that help enterprises enhance efficiency without intensifying concentration due to limited managerial competence or unbalanced risk preferences. Third, policy efforts should also prioritize labor-intensive and highly concentrated high-tech industries, as these sectors are more sensitive to the nonlinear effects of industrial digitalization. Supporting their digital transformation can facilitate the optimization of upstream supply chain structures, while monitoring mechanisms should be established to prevent new concentration trends arising from platform lock-in or data monopoly. Such targeted policy interventions can help reduce over-reliance on a few dominant suppliers and promote greater supply chain diversification within these key technology-driven sectors. Finally, from a long-term institutional perspective, governments should focus on building stable and coherent policy frameworks that sustain the digital transformation of high-tech industries. Institutional stability and consistent regulation are crucial to enable firms to leverage digital technologies continuously for improving supply chain configuration and mitigating systemic risks associated with excessive source concentration.

Despite these findings, this study is subject to several limitations. First, the analysis focuses on two primary channels—human capital accumulation and risk-taking capacity—through which industrial digitalization affects upstream supply chain source concentration. Other potentially important transmission mechanisms, such as technology diffusion, platform governance structures, or inter-industry coordination effects, are not explicitly examined and warrant further investigation in future research. Second, the present analysis is based on data from China’s high-tech industries, and the findings mainly reflect supply chain adjustments under a specific institutional context. Given cross-country differences in institutional environments, industrial policies, and supply chain governance frameworks, the applicability of the results to other economies remains to be tested in broader empirical settings.
