Association between dietary inflammatory potential and chronic kidney disease among patients at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, Ghana
Authors: Antwi Joseph Barimah, Eugenia Afoakwa, Henry Ofosu Addo, Christopher Larbie, Elvis Nutifafa Agbley, Adansi Whitney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inflammation plays a critical role in the development of kidney disease of all forms while dietary factors have been consistently found to regulate inflammation. The incidence of kidney disease has been on the rise in Ghana in recent years and some hypothesis have emerged suggesting the influence of dietary transition from the typical Ghanaian diet which is rich in vegetables, fruits and whole grains (anti-inflammatory diet) to a more westernized diet high in saturated fats and simple sugars (pro-inflammatory diet) could be implicated in this public health trend. This study highlights the association between dietary inflammatory potential and chronic kidney disease among patients at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, Ghana. The study utilized a retrospective case-control method involving 100 participants with Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) in the case group and 100 participants in the control (non-CKD), determined using the Cochrane formula between August 2022 and October 2022. A structured WHO/FAO food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was adopted and employed to collect data on the dietary patterns of the study participants. The construction of the dietary inflammatory index scores was employed using methods described in other studies. Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) score was calculated using 29 dietary parameters derived from the FFQ and total scores were classified into five quintiles. The results of this study revealed that 20% of all the participants had DII score in the 5th quintile (Highly pro-inflammatory diet). A significantly higher number of participants with CKD (31%) had 5th Quintile scores of DII compared to 9% of the control (non-CKD). The study also revealed that the odds of CKD in participants increased with increasing DII quintiles; odds of the 3rd Quintile (1.23 to 2.82) was [OR = 2.98(1.14–7.79)], 4th Quintile (2.83 to 4.45) [OR = 5.17(1.95, 13.70)] and 5th Quintile (4.46 to 6.12) [OR = 6.54(13.16, 32.40]. This study concluded that the DII score of the diet of participants had a significant relationship with the odds or association with CKD.

Introduction

Numerous literatures have shown that diet plays a significant role towards the regulation of chronic inflammation1. According to Luyckx et al.2., kidney disease is the most neglected chronic disease with a global burden that necessitates more population-based research. In a recent study3, the prevalence of CKD (Chronic kidney disease) in Ghana is 13.3%. A 10-year retrospective review of renal cases in a tertiary institution found that the incidence of hospital admissions for kidney disease is increasing, with over 75% of the affected reporting in the end stages of the disease, leading to the death of up to 50% of patients admitted with kidney disease4,5.

Inflammation plays a critical part in the development of kidney disease in all forms6. Dietary habits have been discovered to regularly control inflammation7,8. Healthy foods such as the Mediterranean diet, which is high in fruits and vegetables have been linked to decrease levels of inflammation, whereas Western-style diets which are high in fat and simple carbohydrates have been linked to higher levels of inflammatory markers9.

The Diet Inflammatory Index (DII) was developed to assess a diet’s inflammatory potential using the pro and anti-inflammatory qualities of its numerous components. Shivappa et al.10., and Yu et al.11., established the DII to give a method for assessing the total inflammatory potential of the diet. Several previous studies used the DII to examine the association between dietary patterns and CKD risk12–16.

Almost all of these studies, however, have been conducted on European and Asian populations13–16, with very little research on the African population. To remedy such gaps in the literature, the current study uses Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital as a case institution to examine the inflammatory potential of diet and its association with renal disease among individuals with Chronic Kidney Disease.

This study therefore, enumerates and evaluates the association between dietary inflammatory potential and chronic kidney disease among patients at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, Ghana.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a retrospective case-control methodology to determine the effect of the inflammatory potential of diet on the risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital. The case files of these patients were handled retrospectively between august and October 2022.

A 10% non-response rate was included, bringing the sample size to 198; approximately 200 participants with equal numbers in each group.

Sampling technique

Non-probability sampling technique, specifically the consecutive sampling technique was employed in selecting the 100-case group and 100 control group for the study. Male and female participants aged 18 years and above who provided informed consent were included in the study. Participants in the case group were individuals diagnosed with chronic kidney disease (CKD), defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² persisting for at least 3 months. Only patients with CKD stages 3–5 were included. Individuals undergoing dialysis or with a history of kidney transplantation were excluded. The sampling was done by recruiting every patient who met the inclusion criteria during the study period until the sample size was reached.

To minimize selection bias associated with non-probability sampling, participants were recruited consecutively using clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data collection methods and instruments

Questionnaire for Assessment of dietary intake

A structured food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was used by adapting FAO, 2018 dietary assessment tools for low-income settings by considering locally consumed foods and food items17. The questionnaire included actual recall and usual recall of food taken by patients. The questionnaire considered pro- and anti-inflammatory factors, such as energy, carbohydrate, protein, fat, Saturated Fatty Acids (SFA), Iron (Fe), cholesterol, and anti-inflammatory factors like alcohol. Alcohol was considered separately due to its context-dependent inflammatory effects, as both pro- and anti-inflammatory properties have been reported depending on quantity and pattern of consumption. This will form the basis of determining the Diet Inflammation Index (DII) and its potential of leading to kidney diseases. Data collection and participant recruitment were conducted between August 2022 and October 202218.

The nutrient analysis of the food composition was done using the USDA FoodData Central Database19. The dietary data was collected using the food frequency questionnaire with standardized measure and weight of specific foods. These standardized intakes were converted to mean daily intakes. These foods in the FFQ were linked to their respective nutritional components from the USDA Food database, which expresses the nutritional components of the foods in units per 100 g of the food. This information was used to extrapolate the total dietary consumption of the participants.

Construction of diet inflammatory index (DII)

The Dietary Inflammation Index (DII), was created and validated in 200911 and updated in 20141 to assess the inflammatory influence of food on health and disease using 29 dietary characteristics derived from the FFQ and was used for DII calculation as described by1. The dietary parameters that were examined were energy, carbohydrates, fat, protein, fiber, cholesterol, saturated fats (SFAs), mono-unsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), folic acid, niacin (B3), riboflavin (B2), thiamin (B1), vitamin B12, pyridoxine (B6), folic acid, zinc, selenium, magnesium, iron, caffeine, pepper, onion, garlic, and green or black tea20.

Dietary intake data were collected using a structured food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) adapted from FAO guidelines and modified to reflect locally consumed foods. Standard portion sizes were used to estimate daily intake of food items.

The reported food items were converted into nutrient intakes using the USDA FoodData Central database, which provides nutrient composition per 100 g of food. These values were used to estimate the daily intake of each dietary parameter included in the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII).

For DII calculation, each nutrient intake was standardized by subtracting the global reference mean and dividing by the global standard deviation to obtain z-scores. These were converted to centered percentile scores to reduce skewness. Each parameter was then multiplied by its respective inflammatory effect score derived from the literature. The overall DII score was obtained by summing the individual component scores, with higher positive values indicating a more pro-inflammatory diet.

Data analysis

Rigorous data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). In this analysis, univariate analysis was performed using various descriptive statistics, such as mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage. For performing bivariate analysis, independent t-tests were used for continuous variables, and chi-square tests were used for categorical variables to compare the differences between cases with CKD and controls with non-CKD. For performing multivariate analysis, logistic regression analysis was used to examine the relationship between DII and CKD, considering various potential confounders, such as age, sex, hypertension, and diabetes. Logistic regression analysis was used for this purpose since the outcome variable is binary. In addition, since a case-control study was conducted, odds ratio is the most appropriate method for this purpose. In case-control studies, odds ratios derived from logistic regression provide valid estimates of association irrespective of disease prevalence. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The DII score data was divided into quintiles for each dietary pattern score and provided as the mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and the number and percentage for categorical variables.

Results

Demographic characteristics of study participants (case and control group)

A total of 200 participants were engaged in this present study, with 100 participants in each group (Control and cases (CKD)). Most of the participants in the study were females representing (63%) and (54%) of the control (non-CKD) and cases (CKD) respectively. Furthermore, most of the participants in the control group (41%) and the case group (36%) belonged to the 26-45-year group. The majority of participants in both control (49%) and case (41%) cohorts had tertiary/postgraduate education respectively. Also, 53% of the control (non-CKD)s and 50% of the cases (CKD) have a history of Diabetes Mellitus. Additionally, 47% and 49% of the control and case cohorts respectively reported a history of hypertension. No significant difference (p > 0.05) was observed between groups based on these characteristics as shown in Table 1.

Mean dietary intake of the study participants

The mean dietary consumption of twenty-nine (29) dietary components was assessed by use of a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) of the 100 participants with CKD and the 100 Control participants as depicted in Table 2. Table 2 presents the mean daily intake of selected dietary components among cases (CKD) and controls (non-CKD). Influence of diet inflammatory index on the development of kidney disease

Table 5 shows the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) for chronic kidney diseases among participants in different quintiles of the dietary inflammatory index (DII), where Q1 represents the least inflammatory diet and was used as a reference category. The participants in Q2 were not found to have a significant association with chronic kidney diseases (AOR = 1.19; 95% CI: 0.42–3.33; p = 0.740). However, there was a significant increase in the risk of chronic kidney diseases starting from Q3 and beyond. Thus, participants in Q3 were found to have approximately three times more risk of developing chronic kidney diseases (AOR = 2.98; 95% CI: 1.14–7.79; p = 0.027). The risk was even more in Q4 (AOR = 5.17; 95% CI: 1.95–13.70; p = 0.001) and was found to be highest in Q5 (AOR = 6.54; 95% CI: 1.316–32.40; p < 0.001). A clear dose-response pattern was observed, indicating that higher DII scores are associated with progressively increased odds of CKD.

Discussion

Dietary scores of participants

In this study, the relationship between the dietary inflammatory potential and chronic kidney disease among patients attending Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital was examined. From the study, it is clear that the relationship between the dietary inflammatory index and chronic kidney disease is significant, since higher scores of the dietary inflammatory index significantly increase the odds of chronic kidney disease. A gradient effect is also seen, where higher scores in the dietary inflammatory index increase the odds of chronic kidney disease compared to lower scores31.

This current study reported the Dietary Inflammatory Index scores of the participants ranged from − 2.97 to 6.12 and additionally, 20% of all the participants had DII in the 5th quintile (highest pro-inflammatory quintile), with 31% of participants with CKD and 9% of those in the control (non-CKD) belonging to this quintile. Conversely, 20% of the participants also belonged to the 1st DII quintile, with 2% of the participants with CKD and 38% of participants in the control group belonging to this quintile. This result implies that the prevalence of participants with CKD with highly pro-inflammatory dietary patterns was higher than participants in the control group. In a study by Moludi et al.21., DII values varied from − 3.3, the highest anti-inflammatory rating, to + 2.82, the highest pro-inflammatory value. This difference could be due to the use of 45 food parameters to determine the DII in their study as compared to the 29 food parameters utilized in this present study. Similarly, Mazidi et al.15., also reported that most participants with CKD (16.4%) belonged in the highest quartile (quartile 4), according to Energy adjusted-DII quartiles.

Association between diet and kidney disease

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), can be brought on by a variety of causes, including lifestyle, and is defined as a gradual modification in kidney function22. The DII score was used in this present retrospective case-control research to evaluate the association between the dietary intake of participants with CKD. A relationship between the DII score and a higher risk of CKD indicates that a pro-inflammatory diet may be the cause of the condition. The results of this study in suggest that compared to participants with DII scores in the 1st Quintile (-2.97 to 0.02 minimally anti-inflammatory), the risk of CKD significantly surges with an increase in DII quintile categories. This present study revealed that the odds of CKD in participants increased with increasing DII quintiles; odds of the 3rd Quintile (1.23 to 2.82; [OR = 2.98(1.14–7.79)], 4th Quintile (2.83 to 4.45; [OR = 5.17(1.95, 13.70)] and 5th Quintile (4.46 to 6.12; [OR = 6.54(13.16, 32.40].

The observed positive association between higher DII scores and CKD suggests that pro-inflammatory diets may contribute to the pathophysiology of kidney dysfunction. Chronic inflammation is known to play a central role in the progression of CKD through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and fibrosis. Diets rich in saturated fats and refined carbohydrates may promote inflammatory processes, while diets rich in fiber and antioxidants may have protective effects.

A study by Mazidi et al.15., involving research participants comprised of those who had completed the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey found that when comparing the highest DII quartile to the least, the likelihood of CKD was 29% greater. Diets with high inflammatory index were linked to both a high incidence of CKD and deteriorating kidney function. This showed that the odds of CKD increased with increasing DII. This finding was similar to a nationwide study in the US by Shivappa et al.1., which reported higher pro-inflammatory dietary patterns, measured with DII, were linked to a higher likelihood of mortality from diseases such as malignancies and CVD. According to a Yan et al.23., study, participants in the highest DII tertile had a higher mortality risk than those in the lowest DII tertile. Their findings supported the notion that pro-inflammatory foods are a factor in the elevated all-cause mortality seen in persons with CKD.

The effect of dietary intake on the concentrations of inflammatory cytokines, which control the body’s inflammatory response, is one possible reason explaining the evident association with the DII score and increased risk of inflammatory disorders including cardiovascular disease and CKD24. This has been observed as inflammatory cytokines are essential to the fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms of diabetes mellitus and its cascading effect on kidney disease and other pathologies25. According to Dunkler et al.26., , diet may be crucial for maintaining chronic inflammation as well as renal function. Better kidney health, a decreased incidence of albuminuria, and a less rapid loss in renal function have all been linked to anti-inflammatory foods such as PUFA, fiber, and several vitamins. On the other hand, saturated fatty acids and sucrose are thought to have pro-inflammatory effects and have been related to decreasing renal function27.

The high protein consumption in patients with CKD in this study is contrary to current recommendations in the clinical setting and is in alignment with the recent literature that has indicated difficulties in following diet. Recent guidelines (including the KDOQI 2020 update) suggest moderated protein status of around 0.55–0.8 g/kg/day in non-dialysis patients with CKD to reduce the incidence of disease development and metabolic load (Ikizler et al., 2020). Nonetheless, it has been indicated that such dietary restrictions are not easily achieved and maintained in real-life situations. As an example, low adherence to low-protein diets (LPDs) has consistently been documented, even in controlled clinical trials, in which the actual protein consumption frequently surpasses the recommended diets (Cunha, 2022).

Likewise, there is consistent data that adherence to protein-restricted diets is not optimal and almost half of the studies incorporated in a recent Cochrane review found unsatisfactory adherence, which hinders the effectiveness of dietary interventions (Cochrane Kidney and Transplant, 2023). This disparity between the advice and practice could be explained by various issues, such as a low level of nutritional counselling, patient education or complexity of the long-term changes in dieting. Even clinical guidelines highlight the value of constant monitoring of the diet and personalised counselling to enhance compliance, especially at the beginning of intervention (Handu et al., 2021).

In addition, increased protein status can also imply late disease presentation, in which strict dietary management is not prioritized in comparison with general nutritional sufficiency and quality of life. New literature controversies also raise the question of the extent of benefit of strict protein restriction, indicating that its effect on the delay of CKD progression can be small or unpredictable, particularly in cases of poor adherence (Cunha, 2022; Cochrane Kidney and Transplant, 2023)28.

Furthermore, this study found that the sociodemographic characteristics; gender, and age were found to be significantly associated (p < 0.05) with DII categories. According to Yamamoto and Yoshitaka29, the process of inflammation is influenced by several variables, such as gender, age, exercise, cigarette smoking, usage of certain drugs, as well as food consumption. These findings suggest that sociodemographic factors are considered to have a significant influence on health since they are linked to inflammation, possibly to an extent30, particularly concerning aging.

Although the general results revealed that an increased DII score was related to CKD, some individual food items showed conflicting results. Of particular interest was the fact that some nutrients were found in larger quantities in patients with CKD. This apparent discrepancy may be due to reverse causation effects in that people diagnosed with CKD tend to eat more healthy food. In addition, recall bias in FFQs may also play a role in this discrepancy.

Conclusion

The purpose of this study was to investigate the link between DII scores and the risk of CKD in patients at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital. This study can be concluded that a positive association between dietary inflammatory potential and chronic kidney disease was established. The results showed that a high DII score increases the odds of CKD, indicating that diet could be a crucial factor. Further studies are recommended for establishing the relationship.

Limitations

The use of non-probability sampling may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias. Retrieving the participants food history via the FFQ, could introduce recall bias as some of the participants might face difficulties providing accurate reflections of their meals.Additionally, there is the possibility that members of the case cohort have had prior exposure to diet therapies which could influence both their current meal intake and responses to present a “healthier” intake. These biases could be addressed in further studies through the use of alternative dietary intake assessment methods that are less subjective.

Recommendations

Based on the critical findings and conclusions from the study, the following recommendations are suggested:

Healthcare providers at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital and similar facilities should incorporate dietary assessment (including DII scoring) into routine screening for patients at risk of Chronic Kidney Disease.Nutrition counselling should emphasize anti-inflammatory dietary patterns, such as increased intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and healthy fats, while reducing processed and high-saturated-fat foods.Dietitians should be integrated more actively into renal care teams to guide individualized dietary interventions.
