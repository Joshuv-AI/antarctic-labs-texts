High SIGLEC10‐Expressing Tumor‐Associated Macrophages Participate in Remodeling the Tumor Immune Microenvironment and Predict Poor Prognosis in Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Chuang Miao, Fei Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSialic acid‐binding immunoglobulin‐like lectin 10 (SIGLEC10), a member of the SIGLEC family, is selectively expressed on multiple immune cell subsets and plays a key role in immune regulation through the recognition of sialylated ligands. Recent studies suggest that SIGLEC10 functions as an emerging immune checkpoint that contributes to the regulation of tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) within the tumor microenvironment (TME).MethodsImmunohistochemistry was performed to assess SIGLEC10 expression in a CRC cohort comprising 202 patients, and the association between SIGLEC10 expression and patient survival and prognosis was evaluated. Immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry were used to characterize the localization and phenotypic features of SIGLEC10‐expressing cells within tumor tissues. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) datasets were analyzed to investigate SIGLEC10‐associated tumor immune signaling pathways. Additionally, single‐cell datasets from Tumor Immune Single‐cell Hub 2 (TISCH2) were used to further investigate the regulatory mechanisms of SIGLEC10high TAMs within the TME. Finally, the therapeutic potential of SIGLEC10 as an immunotherapeutic target in CRC was evaluated.ResultsHigh SIGLEC10 expression predicts poor prognosis in CRC patients and serves as an independent prognostic factor. SIGLEC10 is predominantly expressed in CD68+ macrophages and is associated with TAMs phenotypes. Functional enrichment analyses indicate that SIGLEC10high TAMs are involved in the regulation of multiple inflammatory and tumor immune signaling pathways. Immune infiltration and differential analyses suggest that SIGLEC10high TAMs may facilitate tumor immune evasion by promoting increased infiltration of immune cells and recruiting various immune chemokines. Single‐cell analyses reveal that SIGLEC10high TAMs may remodel the TME through TNFSF13B‐mediated regulation of multiple immune cell populations, including B cells. Finally, SIGLEC10 exhibits considerable potential as an immunotherapeutic target for CRC.ConclusionThis study highlights SIGLEC10 as an independent prognostic factor in patients with CRC and its potential as a promising target for immunotherapy in CRC.Keywords: colorectal cancer, prognosis, SIGLEC10, tumor immune microenvironment, tumor‐associated macrophages

Introduction

CRC is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer‐related mortality [1], with a similar trend observed in China [2]. Current therapeutic strategies for CRC include endoscopic local resection, radical surgical resection, neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy, as well as targeted therapies. Although these approaches have substantially improved survival outcomes in patients with early‐stage CRC, their efficacy remains limited in advanced‐stage disease, particularly in patients with metastatic CRC [3]. The emergence of immunotherapy has provided a promising avenue for cancer treatment by harnessing the host immune system to selectively target and eliminate malignant cells [4]. Among these strategies, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting immune checkpoints such as CTLA‐4 and PD‐1 have shown remarkable clinical efficacy by counteracting tumor‐induced immunosuppression and sustaining the function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [5]. CRC can be broadly classified into two major immunological subtypes based on microsatellite status: microsatellite instability (MSI) and microsatellite stability (MSS). The KEYNOTE‐177 trial demonstrated that patients with MSI‐high (MSI‐H) CRC exhibit significant and durable clinical responses to ICI monotherapy, establishing ICIs as a standard first‐line treatment option for this subgroup. However, MSS tumors, which account for the majority of CRC cases, commonly exhibit immune‐desert or immune‐excluded phenotypes and show limited responsiveness to current ICI monotherapy, representing a major challenge and central focus of ongoing CRC immunotherapy research [6, 7].

Tumor immune evasion represents a major obstacle to the efficacy of current immunotherapies. Immune evasion is a fundamental process that underlies malignant tumor progression [8]. The establishment of an immunosuppressive TME is a prerequisite for immune surveillance escape in most malignancies, including CRC [9, 10]. Multiple cellular components, including tumor cells, stromal cells, and immune cells, have been reported to contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Stromal and immune cell populations play critical roles in facilitating tumor immune evasion. Recent studies have shown that stromal cells can drive immune evasion in CRC through the secretion of exosomes [11]. In addition to stromal cells, immune cell populations, including TAMs, T lymphocytes, B cells, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, and tumor‐associated neutrophils (TANs), are key components of the immunosuppressive TME [12].

As one of the most abundant immune cell populations in tumor tissues, TAMs play a pivotal role in shaping the TME. Owing to their plasticity, TAMs can acquire either antitumor or pro‐tumorigenic phenotypes depending on their activation states, enhancing tumor immune clearance through a pro‐inflammatory M1‐like phenotype or promoting immune evasion and tumor progression through an anti‐inflammatory M2‐like phenotype [13]. Evidence suggests that tumors can remodel the TME by inducing macrophage infiltration and driving M1‐to‐M2 polarization, thereby creating a more permissive environment for tumor survival and immune escape [14, 15]. Distinct TAM subsets defined by specific molecular markers exert specialized functions in tumor biology. For example, DC‐SIGN+ TAMs exert immunosuppressive and pro‐tumorigenic functions in bladder cancer by inducing tolerance in CD8+ CTLs [16]. Recent single‐cell studies have identified multiple functionally specialized TAM subsets in CRC and other solid tumors. Among these subsets, C1QC+ TAMs may be associated with antitumor immune responses, whereas SPP1+ TAMs are linked to angiogenesis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and immunosuppression, and may play critical roles in driving tumor progression, metastasis and immune evasion [17, 18]. However, the precise roles of distinct TAM subsets in CRC progression remain to be further elucidated.

Sialic acid‐binding immunoglobulin‐like lectins (Siglecs) are a family of receptors that participate in immune regulation through the specific recognition of sialic acids on glycoproteins and glycolipids [19]. As a member of the Siglec family, SIGLEC10 consists of five extracellular immunoglobulin‐like domains and a cytoplasmic tail containing three putative tyrosine‐based signaling motifs. Through its sialic acid‐dependent binding capacity, SIGLEC10 is thought to mediate cellular recognition by interacting with sialylated ligands expressed by specific cell populations [20]. Several studies have shown that SIGLEC10 is highly expressed in TAMs. Through its interaction with tumor‐expressed CD24, SIGLEC10 suppresses macrophage‐mediated phagocytosis of tumor cells, thereby promoting tumor immune evasion [21, 22, 23, 24]. Its homolog SIGLEC15 has been identified as a key immunosuppressive factor predominantly expressed in macrophages and cancer cells, and its expression is negatively associated with PD‐L1 expression [25]. Recent studies have shown that SIGLEC10+ macrophages can promote gastric cancer progression by suppressing CD8+ T cell function [26]. However, the role of SIGLEC10 in the TME of CRC remains unclear.

This study demonstrates that SIGLEC10 is an independent prognostic factor in patients with CRC and that high SIGLEC10 expression is predominantly localized to TAMs in CRC specimens. Current studies of SIGLEC10 TAMs in the TME have primarily focused on their regulation of T cell immunity. In contrast, by integrating immune infiltration analyses with RNA‐seq and single‐cell datasets, our study identifies a close association between SIGLEC10high TAMs and B cells in CRC, suggesting that these macrophages may influence antitumor immunity by regulating B cell maturation and differentiation through TNFSF13B. In addition, SIGLEC10high TAMs may not represent a completely distinct macrophage subset within the CRC TME, but rather a marker‐defined population that partially overlaps spatially with C1QC+ TAMs while potentially exerting opposing immunoregulatory functions characterized by negative immune regulation. Finally, correlation analyses showed that SIGLEC10 expression was significantly and positively associated with tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), tumor neoantigen (NEO), and multiple immune checkpoints. Collectively, these findings suggest that SIGLEC10 TAMs have considerable potential as a targetable macrophage population for the development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies in CRC.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Clinical Specimens

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed 202 CRC specimens obtained from patients who underwent radical resection at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University between December 2017 and October 2018. The clinicopathological characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. Postoperative tumor specimens were independently evaluated by two pathologists from the Department of Pathology at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University. Tumor staging was performed according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system. Microsatellite status was determined based on the loss of expression of mismatch repair proteins, including MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2. Owing to limitations in clinical testing availability and incomplete retrospective data for some early enrolled cases, MSI/MMR status information was unavailable for a subset of patients. Follow‐up data were collected from the date of surgery until October 2023. None of the patients included in this study had received preoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from surgery to death or the last follow‐up. A subset of specimens was used for immunofluorescence analysis. Fresh tumor tissue samples for flow cytometric analysis were also collected from the same hospital after obtaining informed consent from the patients. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University (Project No. 2020‐K091‐01), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Immunohistochemistry

The tissue microarrays (TMAs) were prepared by the Department of Pathology at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University using collected CRC specimens. The selected sections were first deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated using a graded ethanol series. To eliminate endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in a pressure cooker with citrate buffer at 121°C for 20 min. Following antigen retrieval, the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum (NGS) at room temperature for 2 h and then incubated overnight at 4°C with a rabbit anti‐SIGLEC10 polyclonal antibody (dilution 1:50, Catalog No. HPA027093, SIGMA). The next day, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG at 37°C for 30 min. Color development was achieved using DAB reagent (DAKO, Denmark), after which the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and examined under a microscope. Immunostaining for each specimen was independently assessed by two pathologists from the Department of Pathology at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University, who were blinded to the clinical data of the patients. Images were captured using a microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software (V1.8.0). The average optical density (mean density = IOD/area) was calculated by measuring the integrated optical density (IOD) and the area of each image. This value reflects the protein concentration per unit area in the sample. For each specimen, the final expression value was determined as the average optical density calculated from 10 randomly selected fields of view.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence, the selected paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and underwent antigen retrieval as described above. The sections were washed three times with PBS and incubated overnight at 4°C with APC anti‐SIGLEC10 fluorescently labeled antibody (diluted 1:200, Catalog No. 347606, BioLegend) and anti‐CD68 antibody (diluted 1:200, Catalog No. 28058–1‐AP, Proteintech). The following day, the sections were incubated at 37°C for 1 h with a secondary fluorescent antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) (diluted 1:200, Catalog No. A0423, Beyotime), followed by staining with 10 mg/mL DAPI in the dark for 5 min. The slides were then mounted using antifade mounting medium containing an anti‐fluorescence quenching agent, and representative fields were captured using a Leica DM5000 B microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Preparation of Single Cell Suspension

The freshly collected tumor tissue was rinsed with saline to remove blood and placed in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), then immediately transported to the laboratory on ice. Tissue dissociation was performed following the manufacturer's instructions (Miltenyi). Briefly, enzymes H, R, and Z were prepared as per the reagent manual. Necrotic tissue, fat, and fibers were removed from the tumor tissue, which was then cut into 2–4 mm pieces. The enzyme mixture and tissue fragments were placed in a gentesmacs C tube, inverted, and mounted onto the sleeve of the gentesmacs Dissociator (Miltenyi). Using the heating module, the sample was incubated at 37°C with continuous rotation for 30 min, repeated twice. After dissociation, the C tube was removed and centrifuged. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm filter, centrifuged again, and treated with red blood cell lysis solution to remove erythrocytes. The prepared single‐cell suspension was then subjected to flow cytometry analysis and cell sorting within 2 h.

Flow Cytometry

For fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS), single‐cell suspensions prepared from fresh tumor specimens, as described above, were incubated with human BD Fc Block (BD Biosciences) to prevent nonspecific binding. The cells were then stained with FITC anti‐CD68 (Catalog No. 562117, BD Biosciences), APC‐Cy7 anti‐CD45 (Catalog No. 557833, BD Biosciences), and APC anti‐SIGLEC10 (Catalog No. 347606, BioLegend) antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. After washing three times with PBS, the cells were resuspended in staining buffer and sorted using a BD FACS Aria3 flow cytometer. TAMs were classified and isolated into SIGLEC10high TAMs (CD45+ CD68+ SIGLEC10high) and SIGLEC10low TAMs (CD45+ CD68+ SIGLEC10low) based on flow cytometric sorting.

RNA Sequencing and Sequence Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the sorted samples using the Qiagen RNeasy kit (Catalog No. 74104), and cDNA libraries were prepared following the Smart‐seq2 protocol [27]. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between SIGLEC10high and SIGLEC10low TAMs were analyzed using R software (V4.2.1) and the limma package, with |log2(FC)| > 1 and p.adj value < 0.05 as the criteria for significance. The pheatmap package was used to generate the cluster heatmap, while the enhancedvolcano package was employed to create the enhanced volcano plot.

Dataset Analysis

The TCGA expression profiles and corresponding clinical data used in this study were obtained from the official TCGA data portal (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE71187 CRC dataset was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://tisch.compbio.cn/home/). The single‐cell CRC datasets used in this study, including CRC_EMTAB8107, CRC_GSE146771_10X, CRC_GSE139555 and CRC_GSE166555, were obtained from the TISCH2 database (http://tisch.compbio.cn/home/).

Functional Enrichment Analysis

The CRC dataset was first downloaded from the TCGA database. SIGLEC10 expression values were extracted from the expression matrix, and samples were stratified into SIGLEC10‐high and SIGLEC10‐low groups using the median SIGLEC10 expression level as the cutoff, followed by data normalization and transformation. Differential expression analysis was then performed using the limma package, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with |log2(FC)| > 1 and p.adj value < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses. GO analysis was used to annotate cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF), and biological process (BP) categories, whereas KEGG analysis was used to integrate genomic, pathway‐level, and disease‐related information. In addition, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted to further investigate the potential role of SIGLEC10 in CRC. Enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID annotation database (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/).

Bioinformatic Analysis

Mechanistic diagrams for this study were created using BioRender (https://app.biorender.com/). The optimal cutoff value was determined using X‐tile software (version 3.6.1). Correlations between different variables were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficients. Immune infiltration analysis of TCGA CRC data was performed using the ESTIMATE and CIBERSORT packages. The TIMER online database (http://timer.cistrome.org/) was used to investigate the association between SIGLEC10 expression and tumor‐infiltrating immune cell populations. The TISCH2 single‐cell database was used to perform UMAP‐based localization analysis of SIGLEC10, TNFSF13B, C1QC and SPP1 expression and to evaluate the association between SIGLEC10 and multiple immune cell populations in CRC single‐cell datasets [28]. For cell–cell communication analysis, the correlation matrix was first downloaded from TISCH2, and correlations between SIGLEC10 and other genes were analyzed. Cells were then classified into SIGLEC10‐high and SIGLEC10‐low groups according to SIGLEC10 expression, followed by cell–cell communication analysis using the CellChat package, which infers intercellular communication based on cellular gene expression profiles and ligand–receptor interactions, together with cofactor interactions [29]. Venn diagrams were generated using an online Venn diagram tool (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/static/others/jvenn/). Single‐cell AUCell scoring was performed using the R package AUCell, a gene set enrichment method based on gene expression ranking, to quantify the activity of predefined gene sets or cell markers at the single‐cell level. The SangerBox data analysis platform (http://sangerbox.com/) was used to assess the correlations of SIGLEC10 expression with TMB, MSI, NEO and multiple immune checkpoints.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.3, SPSS 27.0.1, R 4.2.1, and ImageJ 1.8.0. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method and log‐rank test. In our own CRC cohort, patients were divided into two groups according to the median SIGLEC10 expression level, whereas TCGA samples were stratified using the optimal cutoff value for SIGLEC10 expression. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to evaluate the associations between individual variables and overall survival. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05, p ≤ 0.01, and p ≤ 0.001; ns indicates no statistical significance. The workflow of this study is shown in Figure 1.

Results

High Expression of SIGLEC10 Is Associated With Poor Prognosis and Serves as an Independent Prognostic Factor in CRC Patients

We assessed SIGLEC10 expression by immunohistochemistry in 202 CRC specimens and 20 adjacent non‐tumor tissues. SIGLEC10 was highly expressed in CRC tumor tissues, whereas its expression was relatively low in adjacent tissues (Figure 2A–C). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated the favorable diagnostic potential of SIGLEC10, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.933 (95% CI, 0.899–0.968) (Figure 2D). Consistently, analysis of SIGLEC10 expression in the GSE71187 CRC dataset from the GEO database also showed higher SIGLEC10 expression in tumor tissues than in non‐tumor tissues (Figure 2E). To investigate the association between SIGLEC10 expression and patient prognosis, we analyzed 5‐year survival data from the 202‐patient in‐house cohort, stratified into SIGLEC10‐high (n = 101) and SIGLEC10‐low groups (n = 101), as well as CRC patient data from TCGA, stratified into SIGLEC10‐high (n = 326) and SIGLEC10‐low (n = 183) groups. Patients with CRC exhibiting high SIGLEC10 expression generally had poorer prognosis (Figure 2F,G). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses of the 202‐patient cohort showed that AJCC stage and SIGLEC10 expression were independent prognostic factors (Tables 2 and 3). Additionally, among the 110 CRC samples with defined microsatellite status, the majority were MSS (n = 91), compared with MSI (n = 19). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of SIGLEC10 expression levels or patient prognosis (Figure S1).

SIGLEC10 is Primarily Expressed in TAMs, and SIGLEC10 + TAMs Are Involved in Multiple Tumor Immune Signaling Pathways

Previous studies have shown that SIGLEC10 is predominantly enriched in macrophages [21]. Data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) further supported this expression pattern (Figure S2). To further validate the expression pattern of SIGLEC10 in CRC tissues, dual immunofluorescence staining for SIGLEC10 and CD68 was performed in a subset of clinically collected CRC specimens. The clinicopathological characteristics of these patients are summarized in Table S1. As shown in Figure 3A,B, quantitative analysis of the immunofluorescence images demonstrated that the proportion of SIGLEC10‐expressing TAMs was significantly higher than that of TAMs lacking SIGLEC10 expression. Consistent with these findings, flow cytometric analysis further confirmed this expression pattern, showing that within the CD68‐positive macrophage population identified by surface staining, the proportion of SIGLEC10high TAMs exceeded that of SIGLEC10low TAMs (Figure 3C,D).

(B) Differential distribution of SIGLEC10‐low TAMs and SIGLEC10‐high TAMs. (C) Representative flow cytometry plots of sorted SIGLEC10‐low TAMs and SIGLEC10‐high TAMs. (D) Proportions of SIGLEC10‐low TAMs and SIGLEC10‐high TAMs in tumor samples.

To elucidate the mechanisms by which SIGLEC10‐expressing TAMs regulate the tumor immune microenvironment, we first identified DEGs from the TCGA dataset. To investigate the biological functions of these DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database. GO analysis revealed that the upregulated DEGs were primarily enriched in biological processes (BP) related to signal transduction, cell adhesion, immune response, and inflammatory response. These genes were predominantly localized to membrane‐associated cellular components (CC) and were mainly associated with calcium ion binding and signaling receptor binding in terms of molecular function (MF) (Figure 4A). In contrast, downregulated DEGs were mainly involved in biological processes associated with cell division, were predominantly localized to nuclear cellular components, and were primarily associated with protein binding functions (Figure 4B). Consistently, KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that the upregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, cell adhesion molecule, and chemokine signaling pathways (Figure 4C), whereas the downregulated DEGs were primarily associated with metabolic pathways (Figure 4D).

In addition, GSEA was performed using the clusterProfiler package, which identified the top 10 tumor‐associated signaling pathways most significantly enriched in the SIGLEC10‐high group, including the B cell receptor signaling pathway, chemokine signaling pathway, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, JAK–STAT signaling pathway, NF‐κB signaling pathway, NOD‐like receptor signaling pathway, PD‐L1 expression and PD‐1 checkpoint pathway in cancer, PI3K–Akt signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway, and Toll‐like receptor signaling pathway (Figure 4E). Notably, most of these pathways are involved in the regulation of inflammatory responses and immune signaling. These findings suggest that SIGLEC10high TAMs may exert their functions in CRC by modulating inflammatory responses and reshaping the TME.

SIGLEC10high TAMs May Promote Colorectal Cancer Progression Through Modulation of the Tumor Immune Microenvironment

To validate this hypothesis, RNA‐seq was performed to identify and characterize DEGs between previously sorted SIGLEC10high TAMs and SIGLEC10low TAMs isolated from fresh CRC specimens (Figure 5A,B). Based on the RNA‐seq results, single‐pathway GSEA was conducted to validate the 10 previously identified tumor‐associated immune signaling pathways using adjusted p.adj value < 0.05 and normalized enrichment score (NES) > 1.0 as thresholds. Although the PD‐1 checkpoint pathway, PI3K–Akt signaling pathway, and T cell receptor signaling pathway did not meet the enrichment criteria and were excluded, the results nevertheless confirmed the robust association of SIGLEC10high TAMs with the regulation of the remaining seven tumor‐related immune signaling pathways (Figure 5C).

Immune and stromal cells represent the two major non‐malignant components of the TME and have substantial value in tumor diagnosis and prognostic evaluation. To further investigate the relationship between SIGLEC10 and the TME, the ESTIMATE package in R was used to calculate immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores, and the correlations between SIGLEC10 expression and these scores were evaluated. SIGLEC10 expression was significantly and positively correlated with immune infiltration, stromal infiltration, and overall ESTIMATE scores (Figure 6A). In addition, significant differences in ESTIMATE scores were observed between the SIGLEC10‐high and SIGLEC10‐low groups (Figure 6B). Given the established immunosuppressive receptor function of SIGLEC10 [21], we speculate that elevated SIGLEC10 expression may contribute to stromal remodeling within the TME and facilitate tumor immune evasion through increased infiltration of immunosuppressive immune cell populations.

(B) Significant differences exist between SIGLEC10‐low TAMs and SIGLEC10‐high TAMs in the tumor microenvironment. (C) Analysis from the TIMER database reveals a significant correlation between SIGLEC10 expression and immune cell infiltration in CRC. (D) CIBERSORT analysis shows differential expression of SIGLEC10 in various immune cell types. (E) Correlation analysis between SIGLEC10 expression and infiltration of multiple immune cell types.

We next investigated the associations between SIGLEC10 expression and major immune cell populations using the TIMER database. The results demonstrated that SIGLEC10 expression was significantly and positively correlated with the infiltration levels of multiple immune cell populations in CRC (Figure 6C). To further validate these findings, the CIBERSORT package in R was used to analyze the correlations between SIGLEC10 expression and the infiltration of 22 distinct immune cell types in CRC samples. High SIGLEC10 expression was positively correlated with the infiltration of M1 and M2 macrophages, neutrophils, resting mast cells, naive B cells, and CD8+ T cells, whereas it was negatively correlated with the infiltration of activated mast cells, CD4+ memory T cells, activated dendritic cells, monocytes, and memory B cells (Figure 6D,E). Therefore, although SIGLEC10high TAMs were associated with extensive immune cell infiltration, they appeared to preferentially maintain immune cells in a resting or naive state overall. To further characterize the immune status, correlation analyses revealed that SIGLEC10 expression was significantly and positively associated with multiple immune exhaustion markers, including PDCD1, CTLA4, HAVCR2, LAG3, and TIGIT (Figure S3). Moreover, differential analysis of the RNA‐seq data demonstrated that multiple chemokines associated with immunosuppression and tumor progression, including CCL3, CCL18, and CXCL12, were upregulated in SIGLEC10high TAMs (Figure 5B). Collectively, these findings suggest that SIGLEC10high TAMs may promote tumor progression through their contribution to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Single‐Cell Analyses Revealed a Close Association Between SIGLEC10high TAMs and B Cells

To further characterize the specific immune cell populations associated with SIGLEC10‐expressing TAM infiltration within the TME, we analyzed single‐cell RNA sequencing data from CRC samples obtained from the TISCH2 database. Detailed information for all publicly available patient samples is summarized in Table S2 [30, 31, 32, 33]. We first performed uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis to evaluate the distribution of SIGLEC10 expression across distinct single‐cell populations in four representative datasets, including EMTAB8107, GSE146771_10X, GSE139555, and GSE166555. SIGLEC10 expression was predominantly enriched in monocyte/macrophage populations and B cells (Figure 7A–D). We next calculated the correlations between SIGLEC10 expression and 29 distinct cell types in CRC single‐cell sequencing datasets (Figure 7E). SIGLEC10 expression showed positive correlations primarily with B cells, M1‐like macrophages and monocytes, with the strongest association observed for B cells, whereas negative correlations were observed with malignant cells and regulatory T cells. Together with the prominent enrichment of B cell–related signaling pathways identified by GSEA and the immune infiltration analyses, these findings suggest that tumor‐associated B cells may contribute to the immunosuppressive functions of SIGLEC10high TAMs within the TME.

SIGLEC10high TAMs May Remodel the Tumor Immune Microenvironment by Modulating TNFSF13B Expression and Thereby Influencing B Cell Maturation and Differentiation

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the association between SIGLEC10high TAMs and B cells, we performed cell–cell communication analyses using the EMTAB8107 and GSE146771_10X datasets, which contained more comprehensive sample information (Figure 8A,B). Cell–cell communication source analyses revealed several shared signaling pathways originating from SIGLEC10‐high macrophages that potentially regulate B cell function in both datasets, including TNFSF13B–TNFRSF13B/TNFRSF13C, SPP1–CD44, MIF–(CD74 + CXCR4)/(CD74 + CD44) and LGALS9–P4HB/CD45/CD44 signaling axes. These findings suggest that SIGLEC10high TAMs may influence B cell immune function through the regulation of key mediators, including TNFSF13B, SPP1, MIF, and LGALS9, together with their corresponding receptors or ligands.

To further identify specific regulatory mediators, we compared the key factors identified above with the DEGs derived from the RNA‐seq and TCGA datasets. As shown in Figures 5B and 8C, TNFSF13B and LGALS9 were identified as DEGs in the RNA‐seq dataset. However, only TNFSF13B was consistently present in the intersection of upregulated DEGs from both the RNA‐seq and TCGA datasets. Correlation analysis of TCGA CRC samples further demonstrated a significant positive association between SIGLEC10 and TNFSF13B expression (Figure 8D). Consistently, RNA‐seq data from our in‐house cohort showed that TNFSF13B expression was significantly elevated in the SIGLEC10high TAM population (Figure 8E). Previous studies have identified TNFSF13B as a critical regulator of B cell maturation and differentiation, and its elevated expression has been associated with poor prognosis and altered tumor immune infiltration in CRC [34, 35]. Taken together, these findings suggest that TNFSF13B may serve as a key mediator through which SIGLEC10high TAMs regulate B cell immune function within the TME.

Regulatory B cells (Bregs), a differentiated subtype of B cells, have been identified as immunosuppressive cells that promote immune tolerance and play important roles in inflammation and tumor progression [36]. To investigate the potential presence of this cellular subset, and given the difficulty of precisely clustering Bregs at the single‐cell level, we selected established Breg‐associated markers, including IL10, TGFB1, CD24, and CD1D, as a composite signature [37]. The presence of Breg‐like cells was inferred by identifying B cell populations expressing this signature. Specifically, the composite gene set was analyzed using the Gene Signature Exploration module of the TISCH2 database to quantify expression, and single‐cell AUCell scoring was applied to assess signature activity, thereby indirectly validating the presence of Breg‐like populations within previously identified B cell clusters. The results showed that across all four single‐cell datasets, B cells exhibiting high expression or high activity of the IL10+ TGFB1+ CD24+ CD1D+ signature were consistently detected (Figures S4 and S5). Additionally, we evaluated the associations between SIGLEC10 and TNFSF13B, as well as correlations between TNFSF13B and Breg‐associated markers, including IL10, TGFB1, CD24, CD38, CD1D, PDCD1LG2 (PD‐L2), and CD274 (PD‐L1), which are commonly linked to immunoregulatory or exhausted phenotypes. Strong positive correlations were observed among these features (Figure S6). These results suggest that Bregs or Breg‐like immunosuppressive cell populations are likely present in the tumor single‐cell microenvironment and may contribute to SIGLEC10high TAMs–TNFSF13B–mediated immunosuppression.

In addition, previous studies have shown that TNFSF13B can promote the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) within the TME, thereby suppressing CD4+ and CD8+ T cell–mediated immune responses and facilitating tumor immune evasion [38]. UMAP analyses of TNFSF13B expression across the four datasets further demonstrated that, in addition to its predominant distribution in monocyte/macrophage populations and B cells, TNFSF13B was also expressed in subsets of conventional CD4+ T cells (CD4+ Tconv), exhausted CD8+ T cells (CD8+ Tex), and Tregs (Figure S7). Given that SIGLEC10 is widely recognized as an immunosuppressive receptor, together with the significant positive correlation between SIGLEC10high TAMs and TNFSF13B expression in the TME and the association of elevated expression of both factors with poor prognosis in CRC, we speculate that SIGLEC10high TAMs may remodel the TME and influence CRC prognosis by upregulating TNFSF13B signaling and modulating the functions of multiple immune cell populations, including B cells. Consistently, TNFSF13B, a key activator and regulator of the NF‐κB signaling pathway involved in tumor progression [39], was also identified in single‐pathway GSEA analyses of both TCGA and RNA‐seq CRC datasets (Figure 8F,G), further supporting our hypothesis.

SIGLEC10high TAMs May Represent a Promising Therapeutic Target for the Development of Novel Immunotherapeutic Strategies in CRC

TMB has been widely recognized as a predictive biomarker for responses to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [40]. In patients with CRC, high MSI status has been associated with improved response rates to anti‐PD‐1 immunotherapy [41]. Similarly, NEOs, which arise from nonsynonymous mutations and serve as tumor‐specific markers, represent highly attractive targets for cancer immunotherapy [42, 43]. To evaluate the feasibility of SIGLEC10 as a potential immunotherapeutic target in CRC, we performed a pan‐cancer analysis to investigate the correlations between SIGLEC10 expression and MSI, TMB, and NEO. The results demonstrated that SIGLEC10 expression was significantly positively correlated with TMB in endometrial carcinoma and CRC cohorts, positively associated with MSI in CRC and uveal melanoma cohorts, and significantly correlated with NEO in endometrial carcinoma and CRC cohorts (Figure 9A–C). Compared with other tumor types, CRC was characterized by significant positive correlations between SIGLEC10 expression and all three indicators. These findings suggest that SIGLEC10 may serve as an indirect biomarker for predicting immunotherapeutic responsiveness, indicating that tumors with high SIGLEC10 expression may possess an immune cell–enriched microenvironment and could therefore exhibit increased sensitivity to ICIs.

In addition, our study demonstrated that SIGLEC10 expression in CRC was significantly and positively correlated with multiple representative immune checkpoint molecules, including ADORA2A, CD274, CTLA4, HAVCR2, LAG3, PDCD1, SLAMF7, TGFB1, C10orf54, TNFRSF4 and TNFRSF9 (Figure 9D). These findings further support the potential of SIGLEC10high TAMs as promising targets for the development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies in CRC. Furthermore, to determine whether SIGLEC10+ TAMs represent a distinct macrophage population within the TME, we performed UMAP co‐localization analyses of SIGLEC10, C1QC and SPP1 expression in macrophages. The results showed that SIGLEC10+ TAMs partially overlapped with C1QC+ TAMs, whereas minimal overlap was observed with SPP1+ TAMs (Figure S7). Moreover, previous enrichment analyses indicated that this macrophage population is primarily involved in inflammatory and immune responses, including chemokine and cytokine interactions, which is consistent with the functional characteristics previously reported for C1QC+ TAMs. Taken together, these findings suggest that SIGLEC10+ TAMs may not constitute a completely distinct macrophage subset in CRC, but rather represent a subpopulation that partially overlaps with C1QC+ TAMs while exerting opposing immunoregulatory effects that negatively modulate antitumor immune responses within the TME. Therefore, given the partial overlap in the functional landscapes of these macrophage subsets, combinatorial strategies aimed at regulating the balance between SIGLEC10+ and SPP1+ macrophage populations may help overcome the limitations of current immunotherapeutic approaches.

Discussion

Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade, has provided new therapeutic opportunities for patients with cancer, including those with CRC. However, the clinical efficacy of immunotherapy remains limited in MSS‐CRC, which accounts for the majority of CRC cases [44, 45, 46]. Emerging evidence suggests that members of the SIGLEC family can function as immune checkpoints and play critical roles within the TME [47, 48]. Previous studies investigating the SIGLEC family, including SIGLEC10, have primarily focused on ovarian and breast cancers, whereas their roles within the CRC TME remain poorly understood. In the present study, we identified SIGLEC10 expression as being predominantly enriched in a specific TAM subset in CRC, a finding that has not previously been reported. As a key immune receptor, SIGLEC10 has been implicated in the progression of multiple malignancies [49]. Previous studies have demonstrated that tumor‐resident CD24 can function as a ligand for SIGLEC10 expressed on TAMs, thereby promoting immune evasion and driving the progression of ovarian and breast cancers [22]. In gastric cancer, SIGLEC10 has been reported to suppress tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells through the Akt/P38/Erk signaling pathway, thereby facilitating tumor progression [26]. In the present study, we further demonstrated that SIGLEC10high TAMs in CRC were associated with the activation of multiple inflammatory and immune‐related signaling pathways, including B cell receptor signaling, cytokine–receptor interactions, chemokine signaling, Toll‐like receptor signaling, JAK–STAT, NF‐κB and NOD‐like receptor signaling pathways.

Chronic inflammation is widely recognized as a critical driver of cancer progression [50]. In addition to the chronic inflammatory signaling pathways identified above as being associated with SIGLEC10high TAMs, our RNA‐seq analysis further identified multiple inflammation‐related chemokines among the DEG, including CCL3, CCL18 and CCL24. Moreover, during inflammatory responses, macrophages can polarize into either classically activated M1 or alternatively activated M2 phenotypes, thereby shaping distinct inflammatory microenvironments [51]. Through this process, macrophages may sustain chronic inflammation and contribute to tumor initiation and progression. Within tumor tissues, TAMs frequently exhibit both M1‐ and M2‐like characteristics, thereby establishing a pro‐inflammatory microenvironment that promotes tumor growth and invasion while simultaneously limiting immune‐mediated tumor cell clearance [52]. In particular, M2 polarization has been closely associated with tumor immune evasion through the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines and suppression of T cell–mediated immunity [53]. In our immune infiltration network analysis, SIGLEC10high TAMs exhibited a predominant M2‐like phenotype while partially retaining M1‐like characteristics. Given the established role of M2‐like TAMs in the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, we speculate that SIGLEC10high TAMs may contribute to immune evasion in CRC, thereby highlighting their potential clinical value as therapeutic targets in CRC immunotherapy.

Over the past decade, the role of T cells in tumor immune surveillance within the TME has been extensively investigated. In contrast, the contribution of B cells has received comparatively less attention. In recent years, however, the importance of B cells in antitumor immunity has increasingly been recognized. Previous studies have demonstrated that B cells can exert antitumor effects by presenting tumor antigens to CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, secreting cytokines that promote antitumor immune responses and releasing granzyme B to directly target tumor cells [54, 55]. In the present study, GSEA, immune infiltration analyses and single‐cell analyses consistently revealed a significant association between SIGLEC10high TAMs and B cell infiltration. Subsequent analyses further suggested that SIGLEC10high TAMs may remodel the tumor immune microenvironment by upregulating TNFSF13B expression and thereby modulating the functions of multiple immune cell populations, including B cells. TNFSF13B, a member of the tumor necrosis factor superfamily and a critical regulator of B cell maturation and differentiation [56, 57], has been shown to play a pivotal role in tumor immune infiltration and to be associated with poor prognosis in CRC. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that TNFSF13B can promote IL‐10 secretion by B cells in chronic lymphocytic leukemia [58]. TNFSF13B has also been reported as a key activator and regulator of the NF‐κB signaling pathway involved in tumor progression [39]. Single‐pathway GSEA of the NF‐κB signaling pathway also identified this key factor, suggesting that TNFSF13B may serve as an important mediator of the immunosuppressive function of SIGLEC10high TAMs in the CRC immune microenvironment. In addition, our single‐cell analyses consistently detected B cell populations enriched for Breg‐like or immunosuppressive signatures across datasets. SIGLEC10 was also strongly positively correlated with TNFSF13B and multiple Breg‐associated and immune exhaustion markers. Collectively, these findings suggest that Bregs or Breg‐like cell populations may represent an important component in SIGLEC10high TAMs–TNFSF13B‐mediated immune regulation and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment.

Our findings indicate that although SIGLEC10high TAMs are associated with extensive immune cell infiltration, they predominantly maintain immune cells in a resting or naive state. Moreover, despite the association between elevated SIGLEC10 expression and poorer prognosis in patients with CRC, these patients may exhibit enhanced responsiveness to immunotherapy. These observations highlight the complex dual role of SIGLEC10high TAMs in CRC. Their presence may reflect a highly active yet functionally impaired immune microenvironment. This microenvironment is enriched with immune cells, thereby showing significant associations with TMB and MSI, while simultaneously being dominated by SIGLEC10‐mediated immunosuppressive signaling. In the absence of immunotherapeutic intervention, this immunosuppressive environment appears to predominate, promoting tumor progression to an extent that outweighs the favorable prognostic effects generally associated with MSI, thereby facilitating immune evasion and resulting in poorer clinical outcomes. Conversely, treatment with ICIs may reactivate this pre‐existing but functionally suppressed immune response reservoir. Consequently, despite their intrinsically suppressive microenvironment, tumors with high SIGLEC10 expression may exhibit a greater likelihood of responding to ICIs than tumors lacking a pre‐existing immune cell infiltrate. These findings suggest that elevated SIGLEC10 expression may define a state of reversible immunosuppression, in which immunosuppressive signaling promotes tumor progression under baseline conditions but may be therapeutically reversible following targeted immunomodulatory interventions, including immunotherapy. Recent studies have similarly demonstrated that CSF1R‐targeted immunotherapies may selectively deplete protective C1QC+ TAMs while sparing pro‐tumorigenic SPP1+ TAMs, thereby limiting their clinical efficacy [59, 60]. Our findings further suggest that although SIGLEC10+ and C1QC+ TAMs partially overlap spatially, they may exert opposing roles in the regulation of immune responses. Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at coordinately modulating the balance between SIGLEC10+ and C1QC+ TAM populations may help overcome the limitations of current immunotherapeutic approaches.

Our analyses revealed that SIGLEC10high TAMs may influence CRC prognosis by modulating TNFSF13B expression and thereby regulating B cell differentiation and antitumor immunity. Previous studies have primarily focused on the role of SIGLEC10 at the interface between tumor‐associated macrophages and T cells, whereas our study extends the immunoregulatory functions of SIGLEC10high TAMs to the B cell compartment, thereby expanding the current understanding of SIGLEC10‐mediated immune regulation. Although these findings have not yet been fully validated by mechanistic cellular experiments, they provide a conceptual framework for future investigations. Moreover, our findings may offer a novel therapeutic avenue for patients with CRC who are insensitive to PD‐1 blockade therapy. We do not propose that SIGLEC10 should replace classical immune checkpoints such as PD‐1/PD‐L1. Rather, we emphasize its unique value as a complementary therapeutic target in specific clinical contexts. Previous studies have shown that classical immune checkpoints represented by PD‐1/PD‐L1 mainly function during the terminal exhaustion stage of T cells. In contrast, SIGLEC10 is positioned at the interface between TAMs, which mediate innate immunity, and adaptive immune cells such as T cells. Compared with direct hyperactivation of T cells, which may increase the risk of cytokine release syndrome, blockade of SIGLEC10 may restore macrophage‐mediated innate immune activity and antigen presentation capacity in a more moderate and upstream manner, thereby indirectly reactivating antitumor adaptive immunity. This strategy may therefore achieve a more favorable balance between immune activation and safety and has the potential to serve as a lower‐toxicity partner in combination immunotherapy.

Finally, we acknowledge several limitations of the present study. Owing to limitations in sample availability and clinical conditions, the potential effects of microsatellite status and postoperative adjuvant therapy on patient prognosis could not be fully excluded, which may have introduced bias into the results. Future studies incorporating more comprehensive cohorts are therefore warranted for further validation. In addition, limitations in the detection of intracellular CD68 protein may have prevented the SIGLEC10high TAMs analyzed in this study from fully representing the entire macrophage population, thereby potentially introducing data bias. Future investigations should adopt more rigorous staining protocols together with more specific surface markers to enable more accurate characterization of these macrophage subsets. The single‐cell datasets used in this study exhibited heterogeneity in clinical characteristics, including tumor stage and MSI/MSS status. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting these findings, and further validation in independent cohorts with more uniform clinical features will be necessary. At present, some of the conclusions of this study, including the proposed involvement of regulatory B cells, remain speculative and are not fully supported by the current data. Further clarification will require additional comprehensive functional experiments.

Conclusion

This study highlights the prognostic value of SIGLEC10 in CRC and supports its potential role as an independent prognostic predictor in CRC. SIGLEC10 is predominantly expressed in TAMs, conferring distinct phenotypic characteristics upon this immune cell population. SIGLEC10high TAMs play critical roles in modulating tumor immune evasion and shaping the tumor immune microenvironment through multiple immune‐related signaling pathways. SIGLEC10high TAMs may remodel the tumor immune microenvironment by regulating TNFSF13B expression and thereby modulating the functions of multiple immune cell populations, including B cells. Furthermore, the development of immunotherapeutic strategies specifically targeting SIGLEC10high TAM populations may provide new opportunities to overcome the current limitations of immunotherapy efficacy in CRC.
