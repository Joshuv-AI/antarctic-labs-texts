Click-like Aldol Polycondensation in air for plastic electronics and beyond
Authors: Hongru Chen, Hongyuan Fu, Xiaofan Shi, Shichao Wu, Pinyu Chen, Zhengkai Li, Ming Zhang, Mingyang Cui, Fanchang Meng, Shixin Meng, Lingwei Xue, Yuze Lin, Zitong Liu, Xugang Guo, Ming Lei, Zhi-Guo Zhang, Yongfang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyaromatic materials, characterized by extended C–C/C = C conjugation, hold transformative potential for next‑generation plastic electronics. Herein, we present a click-like C = C bond-forming Aldol polycondensation that proceeds with exceptional efficiency and operational simplicity in air. Central to this finding is an enolate-mediated mechanism within a dual-catalytic process. Strategic control of adjacent heteroatom and ring geometry precisely modulates the proton transfer and the energy spans in the enol/enamine tautomerization pathways, thus the reactivity of ketone and imide monomers. This approach establishes both a design roadmap and a versatile tool box for tailored functionality. Notably, the in situ-generated acetic acid increases reactant oxidation potential enables air-tolerant operation, unlike conventional metal-catalyzed C–C couplings requiring oxygen-free conditions. This methodology facilitates rapid (typically within 30 min), modular assembly of multidimensional conjugated polymers, offering transformative potential for emerging plastic electronics and beyond.Subject terms: Polymers, Conjugated polymers, Polymers

Introduction

The demand for polymeric materials with precise structural and functional control is intensifying in industry and academia, particularly for preparing polyaromatic materials in plastic electronics1–4. Click chemistry, introduced by Sharpless5 and Meldal6 in 2002, offers a modular synthetic approach that meets these stringent requirements. Notably, this widely adopted approach enables the creation of tailored macromolecular materials for various applications. Recent advancements in click chemistry have expanded its scope, facilitating the straightforward synthesis of well-defined, multifunctional polymer architectures, albeit the reactions primarily form carbon-heteroatom bonds (Fig. 1)7.

Nevertheless, conventional synthesis of polyaromatic materials, which feature consecutive carbon-carbon single and double bonds, generally relies on metal-catalyzed cross-coupling8–10. However, achieving efficient and selective C–C bond formation remains challenging for high-performance plastic electronics11. Click chemistry’s potential to revolutionize the polyaromatic materials field is significant and underdeveloped. This approach promises to simplify synthesis, and more efficient, robust, and environmentally sustainable alternatives to traditional methods. Recent advances in the adaptation of azide-alkyne cycloaddition and thiol-ene click reactions, demonstrate the powerful for constructing fascinating polymers via carbon-heteroatom bonds, further highlighting the potential of this click approach (Fig. 1b). While effective, these methods generate heteroatom-based linkages and create discrete connections between building blocks that are not optimal for constructing highly conjugated structures essential for high-performance plastic electronics (Fig. 1d).

Combining the efficiency and versatility of click chemistry with the need for highly conjugated structures provides an opportunity to develop new pathways for the development of advanced polyaromatic materials. This innovation has the potential to significantly impact the design and manufacture of next-generation plastic electronics, combining superior performance with sustainable practices. The ability to streamline the synthesis process and reduce the environmental impact makes click chemistry as a game-changer in the field of polymer science.

To address this challenge, we revisited the classical Aldol polycondensation and discovered a click-like version. The high efficiency of this polycondensation arises from the Lewis acid-mediated in situ generation of enol or enamine tautomers respectively from carbonyl or imide units (Fig. 1c), under a dual catalyst process. Furthermore, we established a rational design strategy for bifunctional monomers, and successfully tailored the reactivity of ketone- and imide-based monomers by strategically modulating adjacent heteroatoms and ring geometry. This approach enables rapid polymerization under mild conditions. Notably, the operational simplicity and air tolerance provide a distinct advantage over conventional metal-catalyzed C–C couplings, which require strict oxygen-free environments. The resulting C = C linkages are well-defined and highly conjugated, advantageous for designing polyaromatic materials, including structures inaccessible via traditional cross-coupling methodologies, and for creating custom functional polymers for emerging technologies.

Results

Design with Aldol Polycondensation

The classic aldol reaction typically involves the combination of two carbonyl compounds to form β-hydroxy carbonyl motifs. Subsequent dehydration produces a carbon–carbon double bond, yielding the sp2 carbon-conjugated Knoevenagel product with methylene-based carbonyl substrates or the Claisen–Schmidt product with ketone-based substrates12. However, under conventional conditions, the reversible nature of this reaction and the associated side reactions in protic solvents, such as self-condensation, make it unsuitable for synthesizing polyaromatic materials.

In our exploring strategies to enhance efficiency of such reaction, we noticed an interesting report describing Indium (III)-promoted aldol condensation of active methylenes, such as dimethyl malonate and malononitrile. Notably, this system employed toluene, rather than protic solvents typically used in base-catalyzed protocols, providing an optimal solvent for polyaromatic synthesis. However, these methods achieved only moderate yields (60-80%) and required extended 8-h reaction times13. Significantly, we recently discovered a rapid, quantitative coupling of indandione-based substrates with aryl aldehydes, enabling a low-cost synthesis of photovoltaic materials14. The discrepancy in yields of different substrates, particularly the enhanced reactivity of the indandione-based ketones compared to those reported by Sakai et al., prompted us to investigate the fundamental mechanism of this condensation and establish a screening rule for identifying substrate scope. This approach also allows us to strategically control of adjacent heteroatom and ring geometry to precisely modulate the reactivity of ketone and imide monomers, establishing both a design roadmap for tailored functionality.

Scope of Substrates

To investigate the fundamental mechanism, we focus on the active methylene component (Fig. 2) to probe the substrate scope, which spans a broad pKa range of 3 to 42 (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1), covering most active methylene groups. These groups can typically undergo classic base or acid-catalyzed condensation with aryl aldehyde (D) substrates, and can function as acceptor (A) units in constructing semiconductors. The electron-withdrawing nature of the carbonyl group adjacent to methylene enables tuning the photophysical properties. However, the low efficiency and reversible nature of such reactions limit its potential for polymerization reactions, with only a few successful examples being realized15. In this study, we explore whether the reaction efficiency could be enhanced using our method. Here, GaCl3 was used as Lewis acid to facilitate comparison of the results with the DFT calculations (Supplementary Discussion 1).

Our earlier research demonstrated the high reaction efficiency of 1,3-indandione (A1) using the current protocol14. Building on this, we investigated halogenated A2 and dicyano-substituted A3, along with its enol acetate (A4) and silyl enol ether (A5) counterpart. Each reaction proceeded smoothly, affording yields above 98%. It is well known that the classic Mukaiyama aldol reaction facilitates the reaction between non-conjugated, aliphatic silyl enol ethers with aldehydes in the presence of a Lewis catalyst16,17. However, a control experiment with A4 or A5 in the absence of acetic anhydride did not furnish any aldol product, suggesting that the reaction process differs from that of the Mukaiyama aldol reaction. Similarly, thio and sulfonyl modifications of A1 led to consistently high efficiency for A7 and A8, respectively. However, when the remaining carbonyl groups in A3 and A7 were further functionalized with dicyano groups, resulting in A6 and A9, no desired product was detected. This observation implicates the relative stability of the anion and thus underscores the critical role of the carbonyl group in providing the necessary reactivity. A similar trend was observed with methylene-based compounds such as malononitrile (A10) and fluorene (A11), which typically exhibit reactivity in conventional addition reactions.

For 1-indanone (A12), its condensation with aldehyde remains consistently high, despite the absence of one carbonyl group compared to its structural counterpart A1. Similarly, halogenation, alkylation and boronic esterization of A12 lead to excellent efficiency in A13, A14 and A15. Notably, breaking the cyclic structure slightly reduces the reactivity of acetophenone (A17) and its derivatives (A16, A18), but it remains high, above 80%, despite the reduced acidity of their α-carbon.

The above results indicate the critical role of the carbonyl group. It is well known that heteroatoms like oxygen and nitrogen can effectively modulate the photophysical properties of aromatic materials while typically resonating with an adjacent carbonyl group via lone pair donation. This feature prompted the investigation of their reactivity in condensation with aldehydes. Unfortunately, preliminary studies with cyanoacetate (A19), and cyanoacetamide (A20) failed to furnish any condensation products. Encouragingly, we removed the heteroatom with alkyl and phenyl groups, and screened 3-oxopentanenitrile (A21) and benzoylacetonitrile (A22), both of which proceeded with quantitative yield. Notably, the linear β-diketone counterparts (A23-A26), all of which are completely unreactive in this process. We speculated that this inconsistency arises from the formation of a stable bidentate complex between the linear β-diketone and the Lewis catalyst, which sequesters the catalyst and hampers the condensation18,19. In contrast, the spatial separation of the two carbonyl groups in the cyclic β-diketone, like A1, prevents the formation of a bidentate coordination complex. Encouragingly, their heterocyclic derivatives (A27-A30), featuring O, S, or N atoms adjacent to the carbonyl oxygen, demonstrate nearly quantitative condensation20. One possible explanation for this phenomenon is that the enolate tautomer is stabilized by the presence of a ring. The high reactivity of the heterocyclic ketone structure is further demonstrated in A31 and its thiol analogue A32.

These results highlight the critical role of the carbonyl oxygen group in the substrates and its ability to tautomerize to its enol form. Since enamines and enols have similar properties, due to their structural characteristics and reactivity patterns21, we further explored a series of heteroaromatic derivatives. Interestingly, the thiazole- and pyridine-functionalized compounds retain good reactivity, provided that the following conditions are met: at least one nitrile group, the α-carbon exhibits proper acidity, and there is no bidentate chelation with the Lewis catalyst. This is exemplified by substrates A33 and A36 compared to A34, A35 and A37.

Critical requirements for the substrates

Motivated by the observed substrate-dependent variations, we investigated the Lewis acid-promoted condensation of carbonyl and imine substrates (A-units). Specifically, we examined the primary role of the Lewis acid in facilitating enol or enamine formation to reveal the reaction scope. While this is crucial for the formation of carbon-carbon bonds leading to aldol products, as highlighted in earlier study, the underlying mechanism remains underexplored. A recent study showed that aryl aldehyde-derived geminal diacetates generate the base species GaCl3(OAc)- through low-barrier C–O bond cleavage22. Building on this, we investigated the keto-enol tautomerism with a dual-catalyst (GaCl3 and GaCl3(OAc)-) using density functional theory (DFT) at ωB97X-D/6-311 + + G(d,p)//ωB97X-D/6-31 G(d,p) level (Supplementary Fig. 2-5, Supplementary Note 1-2 and Supplementary Table 2-3)23, and propose the reaction mechanism shown in Fig. 3a.

Mechanism study.

a Proposed reaction pathways for the aldol condensation under GaCl3(OAc)--catalyzed keto-enol tautomerism pathways, the connected aromatic groups are labeled with different colors. b Free energy profiles of A20 versus A21, c Free energy profiles of A23 versus A27. d Free energy profiles of A33 versus A34. For the substrates, lower profiles are highlighted in red.

To elucidate heteroatom effects, we compared the reactivity of α-cyanoketone substrates A20 and A21, where A20 features a nitrogen atom adjacent to the carbonyl group. The free energy profiles for their tautomerization are shown in Fig. 3b, using INT1 GaCl3(OAc)- as the relative zero point. Initially, A21 forms an exergonic complex (INT2, −4.8 kcal/mol) with GaCl3 and GaCl3(OAc)-. In this complex, coordination of GaCl3 to the carbonyl group (but not the cyano group) of A21 greatly enhances the acidity of the α-carbon protons, facilitating subsequent deprotonation at the methylene position via TS1 (ΔG‡ = 6.1 kcal/mol) with GaCl3(OAc)-, generating enolate ion of INT3. For INT3, coordination of GaCl3 reduces the electron density of the carbonyl group, hindering proton transfer to the oxygen atom. This difficulty is overcome when GaCl3 transfers to the cyan group, leading to the formation of INT4 (endoergic by 5.4 kcal/mol). Subsequently, the -OH moiety of GaCl3(HOAc) vibrates and approaches the carbonyl group, facilitating proton transfer from GaCl3(HOAc) to the carbonyl oxygen via TS2 with a low energy barrier of 4.2 kcal/mol. Overall, the formation of the enolate product INT5 from A21 is an exergonic process, with a low energy span of 11.6 kcal/mol (INT2 → TS2). Additionally, the keto-enol isomerization of A21 in other processes, catalyzed by GaCl3 or HOAc, is unfavorable due to the high energy barrier (Fig. 3b). The keto-enol tautomerism of A20 follows a similar mechanism, with a high energy span of 24.8 kcal/mol, making the entire process endoergic. These results suggest that the ease of keto-enol isomerization in A21, with the removal of the heteroatom, is closely related to its reactivity.

The analysis also suggests a dual-catalysis mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Note 3) in which GaCl3 and GaCl3(OAc)- play distinct roles: promoting deprotonation at the methylene position via TS1 and facilitating proton transfer to the carbonyl group via TS2 (and TS3 for certain substrates). To further investigate heteroatom effects, we compared the reactivity of A23 and A27, focusing on how the heterocyclic geometry of A27 enhances reactivity (Fig. 3c). For A27, TS1 has a lower energy barrier (7.2 kcal/mol) than A23 (14.7 kcal/mol). Proton transfer in A27 occurs via TS2 in a synchronous step, with a low energy barrier of 4.4 kcal/mol. In contrast, A23 follows a stepwise pathway involving TS2 (vibration of the -OH moiety) and TS3 (proton transfer), with TS2 being significantly hindered by a high energy barrier of 17.2 kcal/mol. The keto-enol tautomerism of A27 is entirely exergonic, whereas that of A23 is endergonic, with a higher total energy span of 17.2 kcal/mol. These results highlight the influence of heterocyclic geometries in facilitating keto-enol isomerization, with A27 following a more favorable pathway. Similarly, the enhanced reactivity of 1-indanone (A12) relative to A16, attributed to its cyclic structure, is reflected in an energy span of 14.8 kcal/mol and a TS1 barrier of 12.6 kcal/mol, both lower than the corresponding values of 15.0 kcal/mol and 13.7 kcal/mol for A16 (Supplementary Fig. 5). This confirms that cyclic geometries promote keto-enol isomerization in all substrates. For the imine substrates, the high reactivity of A33 is well understood, as evidenced by its low energy span of 8.9 kcal/mol. In contrast, the formation of the enolate product for A34 is strongly endoergic, with a barrier of 28.0 kcal/mol, consistent with its low reactivity (Fig. 3d). Likewise, the enolate product of A35 could not be located, likely due to the low acidity of its methyl group, suggesting that enolization is difficult.

Our results suggest that substrate acidity influences the enolization process. Following the strong acid to weak acid concept, we hypothesize that the pKa values of the substrate-GaCl3 complex must be lower than that (3.7) of released HOAc(GaCl3) to facilitate deprotonation of the α-carbon via TS1(Supplementary Fig. 1b). Calculations reveal that less reactive substrates, such as A35(GaCl3), exhibit value of 4.35, which is significantly higher than 3.7. This is consistent with their poor reactivity. In contrast, substrates like A20(GaCl3) and A34(GaCl3) possess pKa values of −5.28 and 1.55, respectively, both well below 3.7 (Supplementary Table 1). However, their high energy spans, exceeding 15 kcal/mol, greatly diminish their reactivity. These findings suggest that α-carbon acidity, relative to this threshold, may provide insight into substrate reactivity and enolization efficiency.

Based on DFT results, we established key structural criteria for substrate reactivity. First, carbonyl or imine groups are essential, as their coordination with Lewis acid (without forming stable bidentate complex) enhances α-proton acidity, facilitating deprotonation and proton transfer with the assistance of GaCl3(OAc)-. In contrast, heteroatoms directly bonded to the carbonyl group stabilize the keto form, reducing acidity and hindering proton transfer. Additionally, a cyclic structure promotes keto-enol tautomerism. By applying these criteria, we can effectively screen and design for a variety of suitable substrates to use as a toolbox for click-type reactions. With this established toolbox, this reaction has all the desirable features of a click reaction, being highly efficient, simple to execute with no side products and proceeding rapidly to nearly quantitative yields.

Application in Polymerizations

With the established condensation in hand, we investigated its feasibility for condensing aldehyde derivatives with various linkers base on structure of screened substrates (Fig. 4). These linkers are either readily available (L3, L4, L5) or can be easily prepared (L1, L2, L6, L7). In general, the polymerizations are conducted under catalytic conditions using BF3·OEt2 and acetic anhydride in toluene typically within 30 min, notably preforming in air. During the reaction, acetic acid is released (Fig. 3a), leading to a higher oxidation potential for the reactants, as described by the Nernst equation (Supplementary Discussion 2 for Nernst equation), thereby making them more resistant to oxidation. Unlike the Stille and Suzuki reactions, which require oxygen-free conditions, this approach offers significant advantages by simplifying the procedure, enhancing robustness, and improving scalability, making it highly suitable for large-scale industrial production.

Initially, dialdehyde-derived D1 and its alkyl chain-extended analogue, D2, were used to test the reactivity of different linkages (Fig. 4a, b). In all cases, the polycondensation initiated almost immediately, as indicated by the increased viscosity of the solution. The number-average molecular weights (Mn) of most resulting polymers exceed 20 kDa and polydispersity index (PDI) around 2.0, indicating the effectiveness of the polymerizations via the C = C formation with different linkages. Branched alkyl groups in L1 may have sterically hindered the polymerization process, yet the resulted P1 still had a Mn over 11.81 kDa (Fig. 4c). Notably, when L4 was used as the linker, its polymerization with D1 and D2 each reached a gel station within 30 min (Supplementary Movie 1 and 2), resulting in the formation of a syrupy solution with ultrahigh molecular weights (Mn = 21.9 kDa for P4 and 23.0 kDa for P5).

With the aldol polycondensation, conjugation is extended along the main chain network via the alternation of single and double bonds, offering useful electronic properties for plastic electronics11,24–26. The different electron-accepting properties of the imine and carbonyl-based linkages allow for fine tuning of the polymer bandgap, resulting in variations in absorption wavelength ranges and optical bandgaps (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 4). Given the growing interest in how chirality affects light-matter interactions in such soft materials, the development of new methods for synthesizing chiral conjugated polymers is highly desirable, with potential to simultaneously control charge, spin, and light in bio-inspired applications27. As a proof of concept, commercially available R-1,1′-bi-2-naphthol (R-BINOL) was utilized as a chiral backbone, functionalized at the 9-positions with a thiophene-based aldehyde (D4), and condensed with L4 to afford the polymer P8. The resulting P8 films exhibited broad charge-transfer transitions with an absorption edge at 671 nm in films. Notably, strong Cotton effects were observed at 600 nm, with dissymmetry factors (gabs) estimated at 0.003 (Fig. 5d). The distinct optical activity of the chiral polymers is attributed to the intrinsic asymmetry of the binaphthyl linkage and the excitonic coupling of the D-A chromophores, driven by Davydov splitting and the rotational strength of the chiral coupled excited-state dipoles28. This approach to main-chain chirality opens up possibilities for creating tailor-made chiral polymers with precisely defined properties.

In plastic electronics, π-electron delocalization not only tunes the optical properties but also regulates carrier transport in organic field-effect transistors (OFETs) and affects exciton dynamics in photovoltaic devices, thereby influencing overall optoelectronic performance11. This highlights the need for designing n-type polyaromatic with suitable electron-deficient heteroaromatic linkages. In line with this, we find that P4 and P5 exhibits unipolar n-type characteristics, with a high mobility up to 0.65 cm2V-1s-1 with increased side chains in P5 (Fig. 5f). The multiple S···O conformational locks endow the polymer with a highly planar conjugated backbone, leading to strong crystallinity29. Thin films of P5 shows an face on orientation with a π-π stacking distance as short as 3.43 Å (Fig. 5e, and Supplementary Table 5), which explains the observed high mobility30.

Notably, the versatility of our synthetic strategy is further demonstrated by polymer P9 as Organic Photocatalyst (OPC), a material inaccessible through conventional cross-coupling methodologies. An optimized PM6:P9 blend (3:7, w/w) achieving a record hydrogen evolution rate of 1235.5 mmol h−1 g−1, the highest value reported for organic photocatalysts (Fig.5b, g-h, Supplementary Fig. 8–10 and Supplementary Table 6-7)31,32. This success highlights the unique potential of C = C click-type polycondensation for developing advanced functional polymeric materials beyond the scope of traditional synthetic routes. In polymer solar cells (PSCs), polymerized small molecular acceptors (PSMAs) combine the advantages of SMAs with the properties of polymers, leading to enhanced mechanically robust and stability33. Traditionally, their synthesis relies on Stille coupling reactions under reflux in toluene for 24–72 h. Using the silyl-mediated version of aldol condensation, we synthesized P11 at 60 °C in 1 h with a Mn of 14.22 kDa, higher than that (8.02 kDa) obtained via Stille coupling reactions within 24 h. To overcome the molecular weight limitations intrinsic to the helical structure of P11, which impede chain growth, we synthesized its linear analog, P11a (Supplementary Fig. 11). Alleviating these structural constraints enabled the synthesis of P11a with a high molecular weight exceeding 30 kDa (Supplementary Note 4). In contrast, conventional Suzuki and DAHP methods failed to synthesize PSMA, as the requisite strong basic conditions decompose the Knoevenagel-based monomer. This result underscores the high reactivity and effectiveness of our method for developing sustainable energy materials. Additionally, polymers with vinyl linkages prepared by our method exhibit good stability (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Note 5). Notably, the broad functional group tolerance of our method is further demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 13. In device, the power conversion efficiency reached 18.43% (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Table 8) after device optimization, higher than obtained from conventional Stille coupling reactions34,35. The higher molecular weight and absence of metallic impurities critically enhance device performance. Additionally, the lower synthesis costs and straightforward procedures of this approach offer significant advantages for sustainable energy.

We further extended this strategy to synthesize conjugated microporous polymers (CMPs) (Supplementary Fig. 14-16 and Supplementary Table 9), a unique class of materials that combine extended π-conjugation with a permanently microporous skeleton36. Typically, the synthesis of CMPs requires high temperatures, long reaction times, and sometimes high pressure. To our delight, our method successfully produced P12 within 15 min in air, achieving excellent yields (>90%). As shown in the FTIR and NMR analyses in Supplementary Fig. 14, it confirms the absence of residual aldehyde groups in the synthesized CMPs, further confirming the high reactivity of the method. Importantly, traditional condensation under high temperature often suffer from issues with cis-trans isomerization for the C = C bond37. In contrast, our method consistently yields the trans-isomer under a lower temperature (Supplementary Fig. 15). This significant advantage of our approach ensures greater structural uniformity and potentially enhances the properties and performance of the materials38,39. Thus, the simple preparation under milder conditions facilitates the sustainable and large-scale synthesis of the desired CMPs. Despite that, systematic studies are needed to elucidate the roles of steric and electronic effects in controlling stereoselectivity, which would further enhance the rational design of CMPs with tailored properties and functions39,40.

Discussion

In this work, we revisit the classic Aldol condensation and demonstrate a click-like version via C = C bond formation polymerization, implemented using a Lewis acid catalyst with assistant of acid anhydride in air. This method overcomes the reversible nature of classic Aldol condensation, being highly efficient, simple to execute, and rapidly completing polymerization. A general design roadmap was established for methylene monomers based on an enolate-mediated mechanism via a dual-catalytic process. Strategic manipulation of adjacent heteroatoms and ring geometry enables precise modulation of their reactivity. Performing DFT calculations reveals two key criteria for the design roadmap: (1) Lewis acid activation is producing substrate complexes with pKa <3.7 to enable proton transfer, and (2) readily accessible enol/enamine tautomerization pathways with energy spans ≤15 kcal/mol. Notably, the air compatibility of this polymerization system simplifies the process, a distinct advantage over conventional metal-catalyzed C–C couplings that require oxygen-free conditions. These findings establish a conceptual framework for a rapid, programmable, modular synthesis of structurally diverse polyaromatic materials. The resulting C = C bond linkages provide well-defined, highly sp2-conjugated structures that permit precise tailoring their electronic properties. This approach lays a foundation for the next generation of sustainable and high-performance materials. The implications of this work extend far beyond the laboratory, offering transformative solutions for the advancement of emerging plastic electronics and beyond.

Synthesis of compound D1, D2 and D4

Compound A (3 g, 11 mmol) and B (10 g, 25 mmol) were added to a 100 mL flask, (3.5 g, 25 mmol) potassium carbonate, (2 g, 13 mmol) potassium iodide, 50 ml DMF, and heated at 120 °C for 12 h to obtain 10.0 g Compound C, yellow liquid, yield 95%. Subsequently, compound C (5 g, 5.3 mmol) and D (2.07 g, 13.3 mmol) were added to a 100 mL three-neck flask, followed by potassium carbonate (1.8 g, 13.3 mmol) and XPhos Pd G3 (50 mg). Synthesis of compound L1

General synthesis of polymers

Monomer D (0.05 mmol) and linker L (0.05 mmol) were added to a single-neck flask and dissolved in toluene (1.0 mL) with stirring for 2 min. Acetic anhydride (20 μL, 0.20 mmol) was then introduced, and the mixture was preheated to 110 °C. After 2 min, BF3·OEt2 (5 μL, 0.04 mmol) was added, and the reaction was maintained at 110 °C under continuous stirring. The reaction was terminated upon the appearance of visible film formation on the vessel wall. The crude polymer was precipitated into methanol and subsequently purified by Soxhlet extraction with methanol, n-hexane, dichloromethane, chloroform, and chlorobenzene in sequence. The detailed synthetic procedures for P1-P11 are provided in the Supplementary Methods, high temperature GPC measurements of P1-P11 are shown in Supplementary Fig. 97–107.

General synthesis of CMPs

For the synthesis of CMPs (P12, TFPPy-ICTO-COF, and BHT-2), monomer D (0.05 mmol) and linker L (0.05 mmol) were placed in a single-necked flask, followed by the addition of toluene (1.0 mL). The mixture was stirred for 5 min to achieve a uniform dispersion. Acetic anhydride (20 μL, 0.20 mmol) was then added, and the reaction mixture was preheated at 110 °C. After 2 min, BF3·OEt2 (5 μL, 0.04 mmol, V/Vtoluene = 1/50) was introduced, and the reaction was stirred at 110 °C. The synthetic routes illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 108.

The polymerization was terminated when a large amount of precipitate formed, and the supernatant became colorless and transparent. Methanol was added dropwise to the reaction mixture under stirring to quench the reaction. The resulting solid was collected by filtration and sequentially washed with THF, acetone, DMF, DMSO, acetic acid, and chloroform. After drying under vacuum, CMPs were obtained as pale powders. The reaction was typically completed within 15 min, yielding the product in >90% isolated yield. The reaction can occur in an open system without the need to perform additional operations such as maintaining moisture- and oxygen-free conditions. It can also obtain a product even by grinding (Supplementary Fig. 109).

Material characterization

1H NMR and 13C NMR were recorded on a Bruker Avance-400 spectrometer with chloroform- d or DMSO-d6 as solvent. The chemical shifts were recorded as δ value (ppm) relative to an internal tetramethylsilane (TMS) standard. Solid-state NMR spectra were recorded on a JNM-ECZ600R/S3 (Jeol, Japan) (600 MHz). Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) experiments were performed on an Autoflex III instrument (Bruker Daltonics, Inc.). High temperature gel permeation chromatography (GPC) measurements were carried out on Agilent Technologies PL-GPC−220 at 160 °C, where 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene was used as the solvent and polystyrene as the standard. UV-Vis spectra were obtained on a Hitachi U2910 spectrophotometer. FT-IR spectra of the CMPs were collected with a Spectrum 100 FTIR spectrometer(PerkinElmer) in the 500–3500 cm−1 range, all samples were tested in ATR mode. CD spectra were recorded on a J-815 circular dichroism spectropolarimeter (JASCO, Japan). Grazing incidence wide angle x-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurement was conducted at the beamline of 7.3.3 at the Advanced Light Source (ALS). Samples were prepared on Si/PEDOT: PSS substrates. TEM was conducted by JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan). The films were spin-coated on PEDOT:PSS substrates under optimal fabrication conditions, and then transferred to 300 mesh Cu carriers for measurement.

Device fabrication and characterization of the PSCs

The PSCs were fabricated with a structure of ITO/2PACZ/PM6:P11 (total weight ratio is 1:1)/PDINN/Ag. The ITO substrates were ultrasonic treated for 20 min in a sequence of water, deionized water, and ethanol, respectively. Then the substrates were dried in 90 °C oven for 20 min and treated in an ultraviolet ozone cleaner (Ultraviolet Ozone Cleaner, Jelight Company, USA) for 15 min. The 2-PACZ was dissolved in ethanol and string for 2 h with a 0.27 mg/ml concentration. After that, the 2-PACZ solution was spin coated on UVO treated ITO substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s in glove box. Then the 2-PACZ coated ITO substrates were thermal annealed at 100 for 10 min. PM6:P11 was dissolved in chloroform with a total concentration of 16 mg/ml. The solution was stirred at room temperature for 3 h, after that, 2% volume ratio 1-chloronaphthalene (1-CN) was added as additives and stirred for 20 min. The active layers were spin-coated at ~3500 rpm and form a film with ~100 nm thickness. The processed films were thermal annealed at 100 °C for 5 min, after cooling, PDINN (methanol, 1 mg/ml) was spin-coated on the top of active layer at 3000 rpm for 30 s and formed a film thickness 5 ~ 10 nm. Finally, Ag was thermal evaporated under as mask when the vacuum pressure is ~1 × 10-6 mbar. The aperture area of fabricated device is measured to be 0.06 cm2. The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the OPVs were measured in nitrogen glove box with a Keithley 2450 Source Measure unit. Oriel Sol3A Class AAA Solar Simulator (model, Newport 94023 A) with a 450 W xenon lamp and an air mass (AM) 1.5 filter was used as the light sources. The light intensity was calibrated to 100 mW cm−2 by a Newport Oriel 91150 V reference cell. The scan voltage was from -0.2 V to 1 V with a step width of 10 mV and delay time of 1 ms. Incident photon-to-current efficiency (IPCE) was measured by Solar Cell Spectral Response Measurement System QE-R3-011 (Enli Technology Co., Ltd., Taiwan). The light intensity at each wavelength was calibrated with a single-crystal Si photovoltaic cell.

OFET characterizations

To investigate the carrier transporting properties, top-gate/bottom-contact (TGBC) OFFTs devices were fabricated. A heavily doped n-type Si wafer was used as a gate electrode, while a layer of dry oxidized SiO2 (300 nm, with roughness lower than 0.1 nm and capacitance of 11.5 nF cm-2) was employed as the gate dielectric layer. The drain-source (D-S) gold contacts were fabricated by photo-lithography. The channel width and channel length of all OFET devices are 1440 μm and 50 μm, respectively. The substrates were cleaned by sonication in acetone and water and isopropyl alcohol sequentially. 5 mg of polymers were put into o-dichlorobenzene (1 mL), respectively, which were in ultrasound for 4 h and then heated at 150 °C for 2 h. Then, the supernatant liquids were spin-coated on Au/SiO2/Si substrate at a speed of 3500 r/min. After thermal annealing at different temperatures (120 °C, 140 °C, 160 °C, 180 °C, 200 °C, and 220 °C) for 10 min for the polymer thin films, a polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA, Mw = 996 kDa) solution in n-butyl acetate (60 mg/mL) was used to fabricate insulator layer on the surface of thin films and dried at 90 °C for 20 min and 110 °C for 40 min on a hotplate (PMMA thickness ~900 nm, unit-area capacitance Ci = 2.56 nF/cm2). At last, Al (~100 nm) gate electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation through a shadow mask.

DFT Calculations on mechanistic

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations on mechanistic were performed using the Gaussian 09 package at the ωB97X-D/6-311 + + G(d,p)//ωB97X-D/6-31 G(d,p) level of theory under standard conditions (298.15 K, 1.0 atm). Solvent effects were accounted for using the SMD implicit solvation model with toluene as the solvent. Detailed computational procedures and information on the polyacid systems are provided in the Supplementary Methods.
