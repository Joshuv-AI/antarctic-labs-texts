Stokes–Adams Syncope and Ventricular Standstill
Authors: Khalid Shakfeh, Takumi Yamada, Aditya Saini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stokes–Adams syncope has various etiologies including atrioventricular conduction delays, ischemia, or vagal mechanisms. Careful EKG analysis including serial EKGs, may help identify contributory mechanisms to the etiology of syncope, even after permanent pacemaker implantation.

A 77‐year‐old woman with a right bundle branch block (RBBB) (Figure 1) presented with recurrent syncopal episodes over several days without prodromal symptoms. No significant electrolyte abnormalities or structural heart disease were identified. An electrocardiogram (ECG) was obtained (Figure 2A).

Baseline electrocardiogram. ECG with sinus tachycardia, incomplete right bundle branch block (QRS 118 ms), normal axis (approximately 45°), and first‐degree AV block (PR interval 209 ms). No other conduction abnormalities are identified.

(A) 12‐lead ECG demonstrating atrial tachycardia with 2:1 AV block during 3 beats, followed by a ventricular standstill. (B) Zoomed‐in rhythm strip highlighting the P–P intervals (red arrows) and P waves (green arrows) buried in the T waves. The P wave axis is unusual, as there is no distinct P wave in Lead I, with a clear P wave leads II (upright) and V1 (negative). The lack of an upright P wave in Lead I in this regular tachycardia (P–P interval 480 ms, atrial rate 125 bpm) favors an ectopic rhythm over sinus rhythm.

The ECG was recorded when the patient suffered from Stokes–Adams syncope. At first glance, the baseline rhythm before the ventricular standstill might appear as sinus rhythm with 1:1 conduction with a RBBB. However, upon closer inspection, there are P waves buried within the T waves, suggesting the underlying rhythm is atrial tachycardia (AT) with 2:1 atrioventricular block (AVB). The ventricular standstill then occurred with a complete AVB during persistent AT. The ectopic rhythm is better visualized after pacemaker implantation (Figure 3).

ECG with upper rate tracking behavior. Note the difference in P wave morphology in leads I and V1 in this ECG compared to the baseline ECG (Figure 1). Whereas the baseline P wave in V1 is biphasic, it is negative in this atrial tachycardia. There is no clear P wave in lead I in comparison to the baseline ECG. These suggest an ectopic focus near the crista terminalis.

There are several possible mechanisms to explain the occurrence of the ventriclar standstill. First, significant infra‐hisian conduction disease might have manifested as phase IV block. This is characterized by spontaneous diastolic depolarization of the His‐Purkinje system and loss of available voltage gated sodium channels. Due to this spontaneous depolarization, there is failure of adequate repolarization and subsequent depolarization [1]. While phase IV blocks are usually preceded by a premature atrial complex and subsequent prolonged refractory period, in this case the prolonged delay in impulse transmission may have been due to the upstream AVB. The block continues until there is an appropriately timed escape rhythm or premature beat, and thus highly unstable. As phase IV block typically presents as a left bundle branch block, the combination of her baseline first‐degree AVB and RBBB would result in complete block, and is a strong possibility for her presentation.

Second, an overdrive pacing from persistent AT is another possible mechanism. Supraventricular overdrive pacing is a physiologic phenomenon from either the sinoatrial node or atrial focus that suppresses idioventricular pacemaker cells [2]. This is achieved by hyperpolarizing idioventricular pacemaker cells via increased Na/K ATPase activity. When sinus or atrial impulses suddenly fail to propagate through the ventricle, it may take several seconds (or longer in diseased conduction systems) for those cells to take over the pacemaker role. This effect is more profound in sudden changes in conduction such as complete AV block in this patient [3].

Third, the ventricular standstill might have persisted from acute myocardial ischemia and lack of coronary perfusion [2]. Following a pause or block from nodal or infra‐hisian disease, lack of coronary perfusion exacerbates the condition. In this case, high sensitivity troponins were elevated but flat at 68 ng/L without a positive delta. This mechanism remains speculative in this particular case, however.

Fourth, increased vagal tone might have increased coincidentally and contributed to the ventricular standstill. Increased vagal tone has greater effect on atrial pacemaker cells compared to idioventricular cells, but may unmask diseased conduction. If atrial cells are inhibited by increased vagal tone, ventricular pacemaker cells may assume their role [2]. If those cells are diseased, a consequent ventricular standstill can occur due to lack of generated impulses. An electrophysiologic study was not performed as it would not have changed the decision to proceed with permanent pacing. She underwent placement of a permanent pacemaker and was 100% ventricular paced without any syncopal episodes beyond 3‐month follow up.
