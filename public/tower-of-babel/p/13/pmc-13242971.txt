Pineal Gland Volume, Sleep Quality, and Psychiatric Disorders: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Sophie Bolwig, Kristian H R Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDisturbances in sleep and circadian rhythms are features of several psychiatric disorders. The pineal gland regulates these rhythms via melatonin, with pineal gland volume (PGV) serving as a structural proxy. We conducted a meta‐analysis to synthesize evidence on differences in PGV between patients with psychiatric disorders and healthy controls and the association between PGV and sleep outcomes.MethodSystematic searches of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science were performed (August 2025). Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale. Random‐effects meta‐analyses using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation were conducted for correlation and group‐comparison outcomes. Subgroup analyses were performed by diagnostic category.ResultsFourteen studies compared PGV between psychiatric patients and healthy controls, and seven studies examined associations between PGV and sleep outcomes. PGV was significantly lower in patients with psychiatric disorders compared with healthy controls (1055 patients, 712 controls, Hedges' g = −0.54 [−0.68, −0.41], p < 0.001). Reductions were most pronounced in schizophrenia spectrum disorders (g = −0.63 [−0.81, −0.46], p < 0.001), and were approximately twice the magnitude observed in mood disorders (g = −0.33 [−0.59, −0.06], p = 0.027). The pooled association between larger PGV and better sleep was weak and nonsignificant (47 patients, 713 controls, Z r = 0.18 [−0.08, 0.43], p = 0.136), with substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 82%), likely reflecting variability in sleep assessment and study populations.ConclusionAcross psychiatric disorders, PGV is reduced relative to healthy controls, with the largest and most consistent reductions observed in schizophrenia spectrum disorders. In contrast, evidence for an association between PGV and sleep outcomes remains inconclusive. These findings suggest that reduced PGV may represent a trait‐like structural alteration linked to psychiatric illness, particularly schizophrenia spectrum disorders, rather than a direct marker of sleep disturbance. Introduction

Sleep disturbances are common in patients with psychiatric disorders. In severe mental disorders, sleep disturbances are linked to lower quality of life, a reduction in cognitive and clinical functioning, suicide attempts, as well as higher relapse rates in mood disorders specifically [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. As many as 92% of patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) experience sleep disturbances [7], including difficulty falling asleep and staying asleep, poor sleep quality, nightmares, and daytime sleepiness [8], with longitudinal studies identifying insomnia as a risk factor for new‐onset MDD across age groups [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Sleep disturbances are also highly prevalent in patients with schizophrenia (SCZ) [14], including but not limited to insomnia and reversed sleep–wake cycles [15]. Moreover, insomnia is a prodromal symptom related to decompensation and relapse of MDD, bipolar disorder (BD), and SCZ [16, 17, 18]. Notably, sleep disturbances are the most common prodrome of bipolar mania and the sixth most common prodrome of bipolar depression [19].

The pineal gland synthesizes and secretes the hormone melatonin, a hormone involved in regulating circadian rhythms and sleep [20, 21]. Melatonin secretion is regulated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, following a 24‐h cycle [22]. Disturbances in melatonin production can disrupt the sleep–wake cycle [23, 24], due to pineal dysfunction or disrupted SCN signaling [25]. The pineal gland comprises approximately 80% of melatonin‐producing pinealocytes [26], establishing a direct link between gland structure and melatonin‐producing capacity. Notably, this relationship is strongest for parenchymal (noncystic) volume, which directly reflects functional pineal tissue [27, 28, 29]. This suggests that volumetric measurements may serve as a proxy for melatonin‐producing capacity. This structural–functional relationship provides biological plausibility for investigating pineal volume as a potential biomarker in psychiatric disorders characterized by circadian and sleep disturbances. Pineal gland alterations may result from neuroinflammation [30], neurodevelopmental abnormalities [31, 32], and HPA axis dysregulation [33, 34], potentially reflecting both shared and disorder‐specific mechanisms.

Yet, changes in pineal gland volume in psychiatric disorders remain unclear, with contradictory findings across studies. Zhao et al. found decreased parenchymal PGV and a higher prevalence of cysts in patients with MDD compared to healthy controls [35], whereas Fındıklı et al. found no difference [36]. Similarly, Bersani et al. found a significant difference in total PGV between SCZ and healthy controls [37], whereas Rajarethinam et al. did not [38]; however, the matter remains unresolved [39]. Meta‐analysis can resolve these inconsistencies by pooling effect sizes and examining sources of heterogeneity.

Aims of the Study

We therefore conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis to synthesize evidence on PGV differences between psychiatric patients and healthy controls, and to examine whether PGV is associated with sleep measures. We hypothesized that patients would exhibit lower PGV than controls, with potential diagnostic specificity, and that greater PGV would correlate with better sleep.

Methods

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was registered at PROSPERO (CRD420251138624).

Literature Search

Systematic searches were performed using the electronic databases PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science. We also used Consensus (www.consensus.app) and snowballing. The last literature search across all databases was completed on August 15, 2025. No filters or restrictions were used. See PRISMA diagram (Figure S1).

For the first research question: (“Pineal Gland”[MeSH] OR “pineal gland” OR “corpus pineale” OR “pineal body”) AND (“Magnetic Resonance Imaging”[MeSH] OR “magnetic resonance imaging” OR MRI) AND (“Mental Disorders”[MeSH] OR “Mood Disorders”[MeSH] OR “Anxiety Disorders”[MeSH] OR “Obsessive‐Compulsive Disorder”[MeSH] OR “Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders”[MeSH] OR “Attention Deficit Disorder with Hyperactivity”[MeSH] OR “Autism Spectrum Disorder”[MeSH] OR “Stress Disorders, Post‐Traumatic”[MeSH] OR “Personality Disorders”[MeSH] OR psychia* OR depress* OR “affective disorder*” OR “mood disorder*” OR bipolar* OR anxiety OR “anxiety disorder*” OR OCD OR “obsessive compulsive disorder” OR schizo* OR ADHD OR ADD OR autism OR “autism spectrum disorder” OR “attention deficit hyperactivity disorder” OR PTSD OR “post‐traumatic stress disorder” OR “borderline personality disorder”).

We used the second research question: (“Pineal Gland”[MeSH] OR “pineal gland” OR “corpus pineale” OR “pineal body”) AND (“Magnetic Resonance Imaging”[MeSH] OR “magnetic resonance imaging” OR MRI) AND (“sleep” OR “insomnia”).

Animal studies, studies on children, and systematic reviews were excluded. Covidence was used for title and abstract screening. Screening and full‐text review were done independently by S.B. and K.J., who were blinded to each other's decisions, and conflicts were resolved by consensus. The studies were divided based on the questions they could answer, with some eligible for both groups.

Resolving Potential Data Overlap

To avoid double‐counting participants, we examined potential sample overlap in four of the five studies by Takahashi et al. [40] and Takahashi et al. [41], and Takahashi et al. [42, 43]. We evaluated study characteristics, including recruitment periods and sample demographics (see Methods S1). To avoid double‐counting, we excluded the smaller SCZ data reported in [41], but retained the schizotypal disorder (SZT) data [41].

Characteristics and Effect Sizes

As descriptive data, we extracted the following: country, number of cases and controls, % females, age, diagnosis, diagnostic classification system, and % of cases medicated. Effect sizes were calculated using the online Effect Size Calculator [44], JASP [45], or manually.

For research question one, we extracted differences in PGV between cases and controls. We used Hedge's g to assess the strength of group differences while minimizing bias from small studies. We extracted raw means and standard deviations. In Bumb et al. [46] and Zhao et al., the effect size to calculate Hedges' g came from the ANCOVA F‐statistic [35, 46]. In Takahashi et al. [47], we calculated Hedges' g for the MDD and BD cohorts using the adjusted Cohen's d value [47]. For the dichotomous pineal gland cyst prevalences between groups, we calculated odds ratios (ORs). For research question two, we extracted correlations between sleep outcomes and PGV from cases and controls. Fisher's Z r was used to determine the association strength. Pearson's correlation was extracted from the articles, except for Sigurdardottir et al. and Park et al. [48, 49], where standardized regression coefficients were extracted, yielding partial correlations [48, 49, 50]. Zhao et al. did not report effect sizes for their nonsignificant correlations with self‐report sleep quality (PSQI: Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index), and inconsistencies were found in the reported polysomnography data [35]. Despite several attempts to contact the authors, these inconsistencies could not be resolved, necessitating the exclusion of these findings from research question two.

Quality and Potential Publication Bias

The quality and methodology of the studies were assessed using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale [51]. Three items (i.e., selection item 4 and outcome items 2 and 3) were excluded because all the studies were retrospective. Each study could thus obtain a maximum of six points (i.e., three in selection, two in comparability, and one in each outcome). Publication bias was examined by visual inspection of Funnel Plots; asymmetry was tested with Egger's regression only for the first question due to insufficient studies in question two [52].

Meta‐Analysis

Meta‐analyses were conducted using JASP (version 0.95.4) employing a random‐effects model to account for expected heterogeneity across studies [45].

We employed random‐effects meta‐analysis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation to account for expected heterogeneity in study populations, measurement methods, and clinical characteristics, since REML estimates the between‐study variance (τ 2) with reduced bias compared to older methods such as the DerSimonian–Laird method [53]. Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests were calculated using the Knapp–Hartung adjustment, which provides more conservative inference given the small number of studies included [54]. Between‐study heterogeneity was quantified using τ 2 and the I 2 statistic and assessed for statistical significance using the residual heterogeneity test (Qₑ). A 95% prediction interval was also calculated to illustrate the expected range of true effects in similar populations.

Pineal Gland Volume in Psychiatric Patients

In our main analysis comparing total PGV in psychiatric patients, we excluded the study by Rajarethinam et al. due to poor quality (Table 2). However, we included the study in a sensitivity analysis. In available studies, we also compared parenchymal PGV and the prevalence of cysts (> 2 mm) in psychiatric patients and controls to examine whether volumetric differences reflect changes in functional or structural tissue.

Pineal Gland Volume in Schizophrenia Spectrum Versus Mood Disorders

We conducted a subgroup analysis of schizophrenia spectrum disorders (i.e., 6% SZT, 29% clinical high‐risk [CHR], 65% SCZ) and mood disorders. We tested whether the absence of heterogeneity was due to the REML estimator being overly conservative with a small number of studies by repeating the analysis using the DerSimonian–Laird method. Lastly, we compared the difference in the total PGV reduction between SCZ spectrum disorders and mood disorders using Cochrane's Q‐test.

Pineal Gland Volume and Sleep Outcomes

Given the high heterogeneity among the studies, we performed a sensitivity analysis limited to those that used the PSQI. In an exploratory analysis, a meta‐regression was conducted with study mean age as a moderator, given the wide range in the study populations, which may contribute to the high study heterogeneity. Model fit statistics (BIC) and reductions in τ 2 were used to assess whether age improved model fit and explained between‐study variability.

Smaller Pineal Gland Volume in Psychiatric Patients

We identified 14 studies comparing psychiatric patients to healthy controls. Rajarethinam [38] was excluded due to methodological concerns (Table 2), leaving 13 studies, 1055 psychiatric patients, and 712 healthy controls. The patients had a significantly lower total PGV than controls (g = −0.54 [−0.68, −0.41], p < 0.001; Figure 1A). The low heterogeneity across studies was nonsignificant (I 2 = 9.6%, Q(14) = 19.8, p = 0.136). When we included Rajarethinam et al. in a sensitivity analysis, the group difference became less pronounced (g = −0.50 [−0.66, −0.34], p < 0.001; Figure S3), and heterogeneity increased significantly across studies (I 2 = 37%, Q(15) = 26.6, p = 0.03).

Moderate leftward asymmetry in the funnel plot suggests potential publication bias primarily driven by SCZ studies (Figure S2A). However, this was not confirmed by Egger's test (t = 0.29, p = 0.78).

Smaller Parenchymal Pineal Gland Volume in Psychiatric Patients

We examined the difference in parenchymal PGV between psychiatric patients and healthy controls. There was moderate but nonsignificant study heterogeneity (I 2 = 34%, Q(4) = 6.03, p = 0.197). In this subset of studies, we observed that patients not only had a significantly lower total PGV but also a significantly lower parenchymal PGV compared to controls with a similar effect size (g = −0.54 [−0.82, −0.26], p = 0.006; Figure 1B).

Similar Pineal Gland Cyst Prevalence in Psychiatric Patients

Lastly, we investigated whether psychiatric patients had a higher prevalence of cysts (> 2 mm). In the seven cohorts, cyst frequency ranged from 19% to 62% in patients and from 18% to 40% in controls (Figure 1C). The highest frequencies were reported in the only 1.5 Tesla study by Zhao et al. [35]; without this study, frequency ranges were 19%–31% and 18%–30% for patients and controls, respectively. Meta‐analytically, patients had a similar frequency of cysts (OR = 0.89 [0.55, 1.45], p = 0.58). There was moderate, but nonsignificant, study heterogeneity (I 2 = 41%, Q(6) = 9.85, p = 0.13).

Diagnostic Subgroup Analyses

In a subgroup analysis of the seven schizophrenia‐spectrum disorder studies (comprising 65% SCZ, 29% CHR, and 6% SZT), the heterogeneity was low (I 2 = 0%, Q(6) = 7.55, p = 0.273). Patients had a significantly lower total PGV than controls (g = −0.63 [−0.81, −0.46], p < 0.001, Figure 2). To ensure that the absence of heterogeneity was not due to the REML estimator being overly conservative with few studies, we repeated the analysis using the DerSimonian–Laird method. I 2 remained low (21%), supporting the robustness of the heterogeneity reduction across estimation methods.

Pineal Gland Volume and Sleep Outcomes

We identified seven studies examining PGV and sleep outcomes, including 713 controls and 47 cases. The studies used self‐reported measures of sleep outcomes, except for Carpenter et al., who used actigraphy [59]. Park et al. [49] employed the REM sleep behavior disorder screening questionnaire (RBDSQ), which, unlike the PSQI and the Schlaffragebogen B Sleep Questionnaire (SF‐B), isn't a general measure of sleep quality [49] as the PSQI. Sigurdardottir et al. used a self‐developed binary questionnaire assessing the presence of very severe sleep disruption and adjusted their analysis for urine melatonin levels [50]. However, melatonin may be a mediator rather than a confounder, and estimates may therefore be subject to overadjustment bias.

We observed a weak, nonsignificant correlation between larger PGV and better sleep (Z r = 0.18 [−0.08, 0.43], p = 0.136, Figure 3). However, there was significant heterogeneity among studies (I 2 = 82%, Q(6) = 28.85, p < 0.001). We therefore performed a sensitivity analysis limited to studies using the PSQI, thus also excluding Carpenter et al.'s patient study and Sigurdardottir et al.'s possibly biased study. I 2 was reduced to 0.02%, and the estimated correlation remained the same (Z r = 0.15 [−0.13, 0.43], p = 0.15).

Forest plot of pineal gland volume correlations with sleep quality. Studies examining PGV correlations with sleep quality using 1.5 Tesla or 3 Tesla MRI. SF‐B, Schlaffragebogen B Sleep Questionnaire; RBDSQ, REM Sleep Behavior Disorder Screening Questionnaire; PSQI, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. *Included patients with MDD, BD, and ANX.

The studies have mean ages ranging from the 20s to the 70s (Figure 3). We performed a meta‐regression that included age as a predictor to estimate whether age influences the pooled effect size. τ 2 increased from 0.05 to 0.07, and age did not improve model fit (ΔBIC = −2.05).

Moderate rightward asymmetry in the funnel plot suggests potential bias (Figure S2B). Bumb et al. [58], one of the earliest studies, is an outlier with the largest effect size and a small sample [58]. Park et al. [49] had the largest sample and the second‐largest effect size; however, their results fall outside the confidence intervals [49].

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta‐analysis, we investigated the relationships among pineal gland volume, psychiatric disorders, and sleep. We found that patients with psychiatric disorders exhibited significantly lower total and parenchymal PGV compared to controls. This reduction was twice as large in schizophrenia spectrum disorders compared to mood disorders. Furthermore, our results indicated a weak, nonsignificant correlation between a larger PGV and better sleep.

Smaller Pineal Gland Volume Across Psychiatric Disorders

We observed significantly smaller total and parenchymal PGV in psychiatric patients compared to controls, with limited heterogeneity across studies. The similar reductions in both total and parenchymal volume, combined with no difference in cyst prevalence between patients and controls, indicate that volumetric alterations reflect changes in functional pineal tissue rather than cystic degeneration. This finding remained robust despite methodological limitations, e.g., most studies used 1.5 Tesla MRI with manual segmentation, which may introduce measurement inconsistencies. However, the young average age of participants (22–42 years) minimizes potential confounding from disease‐related brain atrophy.

Importantly, while smaller pineal gland volumes were present across diagnostic categories, including disorders in which sleep disturbances are not core features, such as OCD and OCPD [56, 57], the magnitude of the reduction varied substantially by diagnosis. This diagnostic specificity, detailed below, suggests that pineal alterations may reflect disorder‐specific pathophysiological mechanisms rather than a simple transdiagnostic phenomenon. The paucity of studies examining disorders beyond schizophrenia and mood disorders (Figure 1), particularly adult autism spectrum disorder, despite pediatric evidence of reduced PGV [62], highlights a gap warranting future investigation.

Smaller Pineal Gland Volume in Schizophrenia Than in Mood Disorders

We found that patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders and mood disorders had significantly smaller PGV compared to healthy controls. Takahashi et al. [40] didn't find an association between PGV and medication status in schizophrenia spectrum disorders [40]. Similarly, Fındıklı et al. found no correlation between treatment duration and PGV for schizophrenia and mood disorders [36]. Thus, there is evidence to support that PGV is independent of medication. Interestingly, we observed that patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders had twice as large reductions compared to patients with mood disorders (Figure 2).

Circadian rhythm disturbances are common in schizophrenia and mood disorders, appearing more severe in psychosis‐spectrum conditions. A meta‐analysis comparing remitted SCZ and BD patients found that SCZ patients had significantly greater circadian disruption, as measured by actigraphy, including longer total sleep time, increased sleep latency, and greater wake after sleep onset, though both groups showed significant rhythm instability compared to controls [63]. This parallels our finding of pineal volume reductions that are present in both mood and psychotic disorders, but twice as large in schizophrenia spectrum disorders. Studies in CHR states support this gradient: circadian rhythm disruption predicts worse psychosis outcomes [64, 65] and more severe affective symptoms [66, 67], with particularly fragmented and irregular patterns emerging in those who later develop psychosis. These findings suggest that PGV, and thus melatonin production capacity, may influence circadian rhythm regulation more than subjective sleep quality per se, with greater pineal alterations potentially contributing to the more severe and heterogeneous circadian disturbances characteristic of schizophrenia. Takahashi et al. found that the reduction in PGV was already observed in individuals who are at high risk of developing psychosis, with similar reduced PGV observed in patients with chronic schizophrenia [43]. Notably, the schizophrenia spectrum subgroup in the present meta‐analysis comprised patients distributed across the schizophrenia continuum, from SZT (6%) through CHR states (29%) to SCZ (65%), and the consistent PGV reduction observed across these groups argues against cumulative illness‐related structural change as the primary explanation. Together with the lack of association with medication [36, 40], this supports the interpretation that smaller PGV represents a neurodevelopmental trait marker of vulnerability rather than a consequence of illness progression. In line with this, a meta‐analysis of five studies found that SCZ was associated with reduced midnight plasma melatonin levels, resulting in reduced melatonin secretion curve amplitude in these patients and that this too were independent of disease duration and of treatment factors [39].

Recent genome‐wide association studies have identified the genetic architecture of pineal gland volume, demonstrating 19% heritability and 34 genome‐wide significant loci [68]. Notably, several PGV‐associated genes overlap with established psychiatric risk loci. Genes regulating synaptic function, neuronal migration, and insulin‐like growth factor signaling (including THADA, NCKAP1, RIMS2, and IGF1R) are associated with both schizophrenia and mood disorders [69, 70, 71]. Additionally, genes with stronger schizophrenia‐specific associations include MIR206, COL25A1, and IMMP1L, which regulate synaptic plasticity, axonal guidance, and mitochondrial function [71]. The genetic overlap suggests that pineal volume reduction in psychiatric disorders may reflect shared biological pathways affecting both brain structure and disease vulnerability. The preponderance of schizophrenia‐associated loci may partially explain our observation of twice the effect size in schizophrenia spectrum disorders compared to mood disorders (Figure 2), indicating condition‐specific genetic contributions to pineal morphology.

The smaller PGV in schizophrenia spectrum disorders compared to mood disorders could reflect several other mechanisms. The chronic, persistent nature of schizophrenia, contrasted with the episodic course of mood disorders, may result in cumulative structural changes, potentially accelerating pineal aging. However, this chronicity hypothesis remains speculative. Alternative explanations include neurodevelopmental origins, given that PGV reductions appear in at‐risk individuals before psychosis onset [43], or disorder‐specific pathophysiological processes such as differential inflammatory burden or dopaminergic dysregulation [72, 73]. A smaller PGV may affect downstream melatonergic processes, potentially explaining limited therapeutic responses to melatonin in these populations [74] and necessitating sedative antipsychotics.

Our finding of diagnostic group different pineal volume reduction aligns with broader neuroimaging evidence from large‐scale consortia demonstrating greater structural brain alterations in schizophrenia compared to mood disorders [75, 76]. The convergence of structural (reduced PGV), functional (lower melatonin levels), and clinical (more severe circadian disruption) findings in schizophrenia suggests that pineal pathology may represent one component of more extensive neurodevelopmental or neurodegenerative processes in psychotic disorders. Whether pineal volume reduction contributes causally to illness pathophysiology or serves as a biomarker of broader brain abnormalities remains open.

Nonetheless, several mechanisms may contribute to this pattern. Neuroinflammatory processes, recognized in both schizophrenia and mood disorders [30], may affect pineal structure through microglial activation and oxidative stress [77, 78, 79]. Neurodevelopmental abnormalities implicated in schizophrenia may extend to the pineal gland, which develops from the diencephalon and reaches full size by age two [31, 32]. HPA axis dysregulation may additionally influence pineal morphology through downstream effects on circadian regulation [33, 34]. Whether these alterations are transdiagnostic or disorder‐specific in their etiology remains an open question, but the magnitude gradient observed here suggests that at least some mechanisms are preferentially operative in schizophrenia spectrum disorders.

Pineal Gland Volume and Sleep Outcomes

We observed a weak, nonsignificant correlation between larger PGV and better sleep outcomes (Z r = 0.18 [−0.08, 0.43]). This finding aligns with Zhao et al., who reported nonsignificant correlations that could not be included in the study [35]. The evidence base remains limited, with substantial heterogeneity across the seven studies, suggesting considerable inconsistency that may be due to differences in sleep assessment methods, populations, and age ranges. Sensitivity analyses restricted to studies using the PSQI did not alter the conclusion, nor did meta‐regression adjusting for sample age, despite aging's known effects on both sleep and pineal calcification [80, 81]. These null findings suggest that the weak PGV–sleep relationship is genuine rather than an artifact of methodological heterogeneity.

Several biological factors may explain this weak correlation. First, melatonin secretion patterns are determined not only by pineal volume but also by pinealocyte activity, receptor sensitivity, and metabolic clearance rates. None of which are captured by volumetric measurements. Second, sleep is multifactorial, influenced by psychological, environmental, and neurobiological factors beyond the circadian system. Third, the relationship between structural measures and functional output may be nonlinear, suggesting a threshold of pineal tissue may be sufficient for adequate melatonin production. Notably, a case study of 17 patients who underwent pineal tumor removal and had no measurable urinary melatonin found that all maintained clear 24‐h sleep–wake rhythms, with only three experiencing poor sleep quality [82], suggesting melatonin's influence on sleep is limited compared to other factors. This clinical evidence suggests that while melatonin plays a role in circadian timing, its contribution to subjective sleep outcomes is limited relative to other regulatory mechanisms.

Collectively, these findings indicate that structural pineal alterations do not directly translate to sleep impairment, highlighting the complexity of sleep regulation beyond melatonin‐dependent pathways. However, variance in both the PGV and sleep outcomes is lower in healthy individuals than in psychiatric populations, which would reduce statistical power to detect a true association and compress effect sizes toward the null. Finally, no studies directly examine the association between PGV and circadian rhythm measures, representing a gap warranting future investigation.

Methodological Limitations

Several limitations must be considered when interpreting our findings.

First, the cross‐sectional design of all included studies precludes causal inference. We cannot determine whether PGV reductions represent neurodevelopmental trait markers present before illness onset, progressive changes during disease course, or consequences of treatment. Evidence from CHR populations [43] suggests pre‐existing alterations, but longitudinal studies tracking pineal volume from the prodrome through the chronic phases are needed to establish temporal relationships.

Second, measurement variability across studies may have introduced bias. All but one study used manual segmentation, and several were unblinded (Table 1), potentially leading to inconsistencies and biased volume estimates. Additionally, most studies used 1.5 Tesla MRI rather than 3 Tesla scanners, limiting spatial resolution. E.g., the notably high cyst prevalence reported by Zhao et al. [35] may reflect a methodological artifact due to the lower spatial resolution of the 1.5 Tesla MRI scanner used compared to those in other studies in the meta‐analysis (Figure 1C). However, the consistency of findings despite these heterogeneities strengthens confidence in the overall pattern of results.

Third, limited adjustment for confounders may affect estimation. Only five studies adjusted for intracranial volume, yet potential confounding from age‐related brain atrophy is limited since most samples are young (≤ 42 years). Medication effects cannot be entirely excluded, though available evidence suggests PGV is independent of medication status [36, 40].

Finally, the high heterogeneity (I 2 = 82%) in the sleep analysis, driven by diverse sleep assessment methods, limits the reliability of the pooled estimates, and the low variance in healthy samples likely further attenuates the observed association. Missing data from Zhao et al. [35] and potential overadjustment bias in Sigurdardottir et al. [50], who adjusted for urinary melatonin (likely a mediator rather than a confounder), further constrained interpretation.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrates that pineal gland volume is significantly reduced in psychiatric disorders, with diagnostic specificity: reductions in schizophrenia spectrum disorders are approximately twice those in mood disorders. This gradient parallels the severity of circadian rhythm disturbances across diagnostic categories, suggesting that pineal alterations may contribute to the circadian dysregulation characteristic of schizophrenia. In contrast, the weak, nonsignificant correlation between pineal volume and sleep quality indicates that structural pineal changes do not directly translate to functional sleep disturbances, highlighting the multifactorial nature of sleep pathophysiology beyond melatonin‐dependent mechanisms. These findings suggest that reduced pineal volume represents a trait‐like structural alteration linked to psychiatric illness, particularly schizophrenia, rather than a direct biomarker of sleep disturbance.
