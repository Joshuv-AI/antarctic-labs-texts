Promotive effects of 830-nm Photobiomodulation on corneal epithelial wound healing and rho-gtpase expression in a rat alkali burn model
Authors: Esther Jang, Hoon Kim, Kyongjin Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction: Corneal injuries can lead to permanent damage that disrupts the integrity of the cornea and impairs vision. Photobiomodulation (PBM) is a non-invasive therapy that uses specific wavelengths of light to stimulate biological processes, leading to a range of therapeutic benefits. In this study, the effect of PBM on the alkali burn model was investigated, with a focus on its potential to promote cell proliferation and migration and reduce inflammation using 830 nm of light. A wavelength of 830 nm was used for PBM treatment. After Alkali burn induction, rats were treated with 830 nm light every 12 h and the healing area was observed. Immunofluorescent staining of Ki67 demonstrated the proliferation of corneal epithelial cells. Clinical examination of corneal neovascularization and the opacity was assessed using a slit lamp. Immunohistochemistry was performed to compare the expression of inflammatory cytokines. Western blot analysis revealed a difference in the level of Rho-GTPase between the control and PBM group. PBM treatment promoted wound closure after alkali burn induction. The number of Ki67 positive cells increased in the PBM group than in the control. New blood vessel growth expanded from the limbal to the cornea after a week following the alkali burn in both groups, and no significant difference was observed in neovascularization score. However, corneal opacity was significantly reduced in the PBM group compared with the control group at 1 week (p = 0.029). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry revealed significantly reduced expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the PBM group compared with the alkali burn control at 48 h, and corresponding mRNA expression levels of all three cytokines were also significantly decreased at both 48 h and 1 week. The expression of selected Rho-GTPase proteins (RhoA, RhoC) was increased in whole corneal tissue of the PBM group during the early healing phase. 830-nm PBM accelerated early corneal re-epithelialization by enhancing corneal epithelial cell migration and proliferation in vitro and in vivo. This recovery was accompanied by increased expression of selected Rho-GTPases (RhoA/RhoC), suggesting a potential involvement of cytoskeletal signaling in epithelial repair. Crucially, PBM established a robust anti-inflammatory environment by significantly downregulating key pro-inflammatory cytokines at both the transcriptional and translational levels, which successfully translated into a significant alleviation of subacute corneal opacity. Together, these findings support 830-nm PBM as a highly effective, non-invasive supportive strategy that facilitates therapeutic recovery after chemical corneal injury.Clinical Trial Number: Not Applicable.Keywords: Corneal alkali burn, Chemical injury, Photobiomodulation, Corneal wound healing

Introduction

The cornea, the outermost layer of the eye, plays a crucial role in maintaining clear vision. However, the cornea is easily damaged by chemical and mechanical injuries because it is exposed to external stimuli [1, 2]. In addition to direct tissue damage, corneal chemical burns can also cause long-term complications, such as scarring, corneal ulceration, and second inflammation [3, 4]. These corneal injuries can lead to permanent damage that disrupts the integrity of the cornea and impairs vision [5].

The corneal re-epithelization occurs in the wound healing process, through various cellular events and signaling pathways such as migration, proliferation, and differentiation of epithelial cells adjacent to the wound [6, 7]. Minor corneal epithelium injuries are recovered by the wound healing process, but severe injuries delay wound healing, which can lead to neovascularization and corneal opacity [8]. However, to maintain the transparency of the cornea, it is essential to promote rapid re-epithelialization.

Photobiomodulation (PBM) is a non-invasive therapy that uses the power of specific wavelengths of light to modulate biological processes and has been reported to support tissue repair and symptom relief in various conditions [9, 10]. PBM has been reported to promote energy production within cells, which can enhance cellular function and promote healing [11]. Proposed mechanisms include modulation of cellular bioenergetics and redox signaling, as well as downstream effects on pathways involved in cell migration, proliferation, and inflammatory responses [12, 13]. However, the relative contribution of these mechanisms can vary by tissue context and irradiation parameters. Red and near-infrared wavelengths are commonly used because of their tissue penetration depth and their reported ability to influence mitochondrial and non-mitochondrial signaling cascades [14, 15].

In ophthalmology, PBM has been explored in several ocular surface conditions using different wavelengths and treatment regimens [16]. Previous studies have shown the effectiveness of PBM, such as improved eye conditions and reduced inflammation in dry eye and blepharitis models using different wavelengths [17, 18]. Building on this background, we investigated whether 830-nm PBM facilitates corneal epithelial wound closure after alkali burn injury. It should be noted that the anti-inflammatory effects of PBM are not uniformly consistent across studies, and outcomes appear to be highly dependent on irradiation parameters, tissue context, and injury model [19, 20]. Rather than focusing on mitochondrial endpoints, we examined epithelial-level outcomes including proliferation, migration, and Rho-GTPase expression patterns — given that corneal epithelial migration and proliferation depend critically on cytoskeletal reorganization mediated by Rho-GTPase family members [21, 22] — with inflammatory cytokines as secondary observations.

Methods

PBM treatment

Photobiomodulation was delivered using an 830-nm continuous-wave LED device (HEALITE II, Lutronic, Korea). The actual irradiance at the target surface was measured using a power meter (Vega, Ophir Optronics, Israel) and confirmed to be 8.9 mW/cm². The exposure parameters were chosen with reference to prior PBM literature indicating that near-infrared wavelengths around 810–830 nm and low-to-moderate fluence ranges are biologically active for wound healing and inflammatory modulation [23–27]. Furthermore, our pilot study using the same device and alkali burn model demonstrated that a lower irradiance of 5 mW/cm² accelerated initial wound closure but was associated with increased neovascularization, corneal opacity, cataract formation, and elevated inflammatory cytokines, highlighting the importance of irradiance optimization [20]. The irradiance was therefore set at 8.9 mW/cm² in the present study to achieve a sufficient biological stimulus while avoiding the adverse effects observed at the lower setting.

Because no standardized PBM protocol has been established for corneal alkali burn in our experimental setting, the final condition was defined as a pilot-study parameter set. Under the measured device output and fixed irradiation geometry, the exposure duration was set at 560 s, corresponding to a delivered fluence of 4.98 J/cm². The LED treatment head was positioned at a fixed distance above the corneal surface to avoid direct contact and to minimize thermal influence. The same parameters were applied in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. For animal experiments, each rat was anesthetized with alfaxan (Jurox Inc., USA), placed in a customized chamber, and irradiated under identical geometric and power conditions.

Cell culture

Human corneal epithelial cells (hCECs) maintained as a laboratory stock were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12 [1:1]; Corning, USA) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Corning, USA), 1× penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; Welgene, Korea), 500 ng/mL hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 5 µg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells within passages 5–10 after thawing were used for experiments, and passage-matched cells were used for comparisons within each experimental set. Mycoplasma contamination was screened using a Mycoplasma Stain Kit (MYC1; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and no fluorescence pattern suggestive of contamination was observed in the cultures used for the experiments.

Cell proliferation assay

hCECs were seeded into 96 well plates, and the next day, cells were treated with PBM. WST reagent (EZ-Cytox, DoGenBio, Korea) was added to the cells, and then the cells were incubated for 30 min. Cell proliferation assay was performed at 6, 12, and 24 h after PBM treatment. The proliferation of cells was determined by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland).

BrdU staining

hCECs were treated with 10 μm of BrdU (Thermo Fisher Science, USA). The cells were exposed to PBM once then the cells were fixed with 4% PFA (Biosesang, Korea) after 6 h. The cells were incubated in 1 M HCl for 30 min at 37 °C and were neutralized with 0.1 M borate buffer (Thermo Fisher Science, USA). After washing with PBS, the BrdU antibody (Abcam, UK) was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Then, the cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with Cy-3 conjugated secondary (Thermo Fisher Science, USA) and counterstained with Hoechst 33,258 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Images were exposed on the fluorescence microscope (Axio imager D2, Carl Zeiss).

Cell wound healing assay

The wound was mechanically generated with a white tip. After scratching, the cells were treated with PBM every 12 h. The wound images were taken by microscopy from random areas of each well every 12 h. The decreasing wound area was measured using the ImageJ program (National Institute of Health, USA).

Animals

Eight-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats (Dahan BioLink, Korea) were used in this study. In total, 70 rats were included, with 35 animals assigned to the control group and 35 animals assigned to the PBM group. Fluorescein-based wound healing observation and slit-lamp opacity/neovascularization scoring were performed serially on all animals without sacrifice and are therefore not counted separately. For terminal tissue collection, 10 animals per group were used for Ki67 immunostaining, 7 animals per group for immunohistochemistry, 10 animals per group for qPCR, and 8 animals per group for Western blot analysis. All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Dankook University (IACUC No. DKUH 22 − 001) and were performed in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Clinical assessment and tissue collection timeline

Clinical slit-lamp examination was performed to assess corneal opacity and neovascularization after alkali burn induction. Corneal tissues were collected at 12 h and 24 h for Ki67 immunostaining, at 48 h for cytokine immunohistochemistry, at 48 h and 1 week for qPCR analysis, and at 24 h and 48 h for Western blot analysis.

Time points were selected based on the biological relevance of each outcome measure after alkali burn injury. Ki67 immunostaining and Western blot analyses were performed at earlier time points (12–24 h or 24–48 h) to capture early proliferative and signaling responses associated with epithelial repair. Clinical slit-lamp outcomes and cytokine IHC were evaluated at 48 h to assess early tissue-level injury and inflammation, while 1-week measurements were included for neovascularization, opacity, and qPCR analyses to examine subacute progression and persistence of inflammatory responses.

Corneal alkali burn model

Rats were anesthetized with Zoletil (Zoletil®, Virbac, France; 30 mg/kg) and Rompun (Rompun®, Bayer, USA; 10 mg/kg), and then 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride (Alkaline, Alcon, USA) was applied to the cornea. Alkali burn generated by a 3.5 mm diameter paper disc soaked in 3.5 µl of 1 N NaOH (Invitrogen, USA). It was placed on the corneal center for 30 s then the cornea was irrigated with 10 ml of PBS. The injured area was monitored by staining with 0.05% fluorescein sodium (Fluorescite inj 10%, Alcon, USA) every 12 h. The fluorescein area was measured and quantified by using the Image J program (NIH, USA).

Assessment of opacity and neovascularization

Clinical slit-lamp examination was performed after alkali burn induction to assess corneal opacity and neovascularization. Corneal opacity and neovascularization were evaluated at 48 h after injury. The cornea was examined under a slit lamp (Topcon, Japan). Corneal opacity was scored from 0 (transparent) to 4 (severe) according to the following criteria: 0, transparent cornea; 1, mild corneal opacity; 2, moderate corneal opacity; 3, severe corneal opacity with visible iris; and 4, severe opacity with invisible iris [28]. For neovascularization assessment, the cornea was divided into four quadrants and further subdivided into three equal zones from the center to the limbus. Newly formed vessels in each quadrant were scored from 0 to 3, and the scores were summed to obtain a maximum total score of 12 [29].

Immunostaining

For immunofluorescence staining, the eyeballs were fixed in 4% PFA, were transferred into sucrose (Biosesang, Korea), and embedded in OCT compound (Sakura, Japan). Cryosection was cut at 12 μm thickness with a cryostat (Leica, Germany). The tissue sections were post-fixed and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After blocking in 2% bovine albumin serum, the sections were incubated with the primary antibodies of Ki67 (Vector, USA) and secondary antibody of Alexa flour 555 (Invitrogen, USA). Nuclei were counter-stained with Hoechst 33,258. Images were exposed on the fluorescence microscope (Axio imager D2, Carl Zeiss).

For immunohistochemistry, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody of IL-1b, IL-6, and TNF-a (Abcam, UK) overnight at 4 °C, and then were incubated with a secondary antibody of Goat-anti-Rabbit IgG, Biotinylated (Vector, USA) for 1 h. These sections were incubated with ABC reagent (Vectastain, USA) for an hour and washed. The sections were incubated in peroxidase substrate solution until the desired stain intensity developed and washed in running tap water. Nuclei were counter-stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Dako, USA). The sections were dehydrated and mounted with Permount® (Thermo Fisher Science, USA). Images were exposed to the microscope (DP73, Olympus, Japan). The staining area was quantified by using the Image J program.

Western blot

The rats were sacrificed at 24 and 48 h after alkali burn induction. RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor was used to lysis protein. The protein was quantified by using BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Science, USA). The proteins were loaded onto pre-made SDS-polyacrylamide gels (10% or 12% gels), performed electrophoresis, and the samples were transferred onto PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA or 5% skim milk (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA) and incubated with primary antibodies of RhoA, RhoC, Cdc42, Rac1-3, p-ERK/t-ERK (Cell signaling, USA), CyclinD1, GAPDH (Invitrogen, USA) and appropriate secondary antibody of m-IgGκ BP-HRP and Goat anti-Rabbit IgG, HRP (Thermo Fisher Science, USA). The immunoreactive bands were developed with chemiluminescence detection (Clarity, Bio-Rad). Images were analyzed by using Chemidoc XRS+ Imager (Bio-Rad, USA). The target protein expression was normalized to β-actin and Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). The expression of proteins was quantified by using the Image J program.

q-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from the entire cornea tissues using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) reagent. The following steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed using a Superscript III kit (Enzynomics, Korea) and random hexamers. cDNA was mixed with Sybr Green Q-PCR Master Mix with Low Rox (Smart gene, Samsung Bioscience, Korea) and specific primers (IL-1b: (F) 5’-CAC CTC TCA AGC AGA GCA CAG-3’ (R) 5’-GGG TTC CAT GGT GAA GTC AAC-3’, IL-6: (F) 5’-CCC ACC AGG AAC GAA AGT CA-3’ (R) 5’-GAA GTA GGG AAG GCA GTG GC-3’, TNF-a: (F) 5’-GAC CCT CAC ACT CAG ATC ATC TTC T-3’ (R) 5’-TGC TAC GAC GTG GGC TAC G-3’, GAPDH: (F) 5’-TTC AAC GGC ACA GCC AAG G-3’ (R) 5’-CTC AGC ACC AGC ATC ACC-3’). A melting curve of the product was used to analyze the specific amplification of the PCR product. The GAPDH gene was used as an endogenous control to normalize the gene expression.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24 (IBM Corp., USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, the independent t-test was applied for normally distributed variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-parametric data. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

The effectiveness of 830 nm on hCECs proliferation and migration

To examine the potential of PBM treatment in promoting the proliferation of human corneal epithelial cells (hCECs), the cells were once exposed to 830 nm light for 560 s. The proliferation of cells was evaluated at time intervals after PBM treatment. Notably, PBM treatment increased cell proliferation 6 h and 24 h after treatment (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, a BrdU assay was conducted to further investigate the effects of PBM treatment on hCECs. The hCECs were treated with 830 nm light, and an increase in the number of BrdU-positive cells was observed 6 h post-treatment as a result of PBM (Fig. 1B). Quantitative analysis confirmed a statistically significant elevation in the number of BrdU-positive hCECs following PBM treatment (Fig. 1C).

(A) Cell proliferation was assessed by WST assay at 6, 12, and 24 h after PBM treatment. (B) Representative immunofluorescence images of BrdU-positive cells. (C) Quantitative analysis of BrdU-positive cells. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 10). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 versus control. Scale bar, 100 μm

A scratch was generated on hCECs to evaluate the wound healing process (Fig. 2A). The hCECs were treated with 830 nm light every 12 h, and wound closure was monitored. At 12 h post-treatment, cells treated with PBM demonstrated a faster healing rate than the control group, with the most significant difference observed at 24 h (Fig. 2B). These findings demonstrate that PBM treatment using 830 nm light stimulates the proliferation and migration of hCECs.

Acceleration of corneal wound healing by PBM

To evaluate the effect of PBM on corneal wound healing, an alkali burn of uniform size was induced on the rat cornea. The PBM group received 830 nm light exposure for 560 s every 12 h. The wound healing process was evaluated by staining the cornea with fluorescein and observing the wound area under blue light at 12-hour intervals (Fig. 3A). The PBM group demonstrated accelerated wound closure compared to the control group at 12 h. A notable difference was observed at 24 h following alkali burn induction (Fig. 3B), and the wound had almost completely healed after 48 h. These results indicate that PBM treatment promotes corneal wound healing during the re-epithelialization process.

Rats in the PBM group were irradiated with 830-nm light for 560 s every 12 h. (B) Quantitative analysis of wound closure rate. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 10 per group). Mann–Whitney test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 versus control

Increased proliferation of corneal epithelial cells by PBM

To determine whether the accelerated wound healing in the PBM group was associated with altered epithelial proliferation, corneas were immunostained for Ki67 at 12 and 24 h after alkali burn induction (Fig. 4). In uninjured corneas, the number of Ki67-positive cells did not differ significantly between the control and PBM groups (Fig. 4B). After alkali burn induction, Ki67-positive cells increased in both groups relative to the uninjured condition. No significant between-group difference was observed at 12 h. At 24 h, the PBM group exhibited a significantly greater number of Ki67-positive cells than the control group (Fig. 4B). While the representative images showed some spatial heterogeneity in Ki67-positive cell distribution, the quantitative analysis performed across the analyzed corneal epithelium supported an increase in epithelial proliferation in the PBM group at 24 h. Together with the wound-healing data (Fig. 3B), these findings suggest that PBM may facilitate early corneal epithelial repair by enhancing proliferative activity after alkali burn injury.

Representative images are shown for illustration and may not fully reflect the spatial heterogeneity observed among individual sections. (B) Quantification of Ki67-positive cells across the analyzed corneal epithelium. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 10 per group). Mann–Whitney test. *p < 0.05. Scale bar, 200 μm

Examination of corneal neovascularization and opacity

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of PBM on corneal alkali burns, corneal neovascularization and opacity were assessed at 48 h and 1 week after injury. At 48 h, newly formed blood vessels were mainly confined to the peripheral cornea in both groups (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis showed no significant difference in neovascularization score between the control and PBM groups (Fig. 5B). Severe corneal opacity was also observed in both groups at this early time point (Fig. 5C), and the opacity score was not significantly different, although the PBM group showed a modest tendency toward lower opacity than the control group (Fig. 5D).

Effects of PBM on corneal neovascularization and opacity. The score of neovascularization and opacity was measured after alkali burn induction at 48 h and 1 week. (A) The neovascularization was limited in the corneal periphery at 48 h. However, the new blood vessel invaded from periphery to center after a week. (B) The graph represented no significant difference in neovascularization score between the control and PBM group. (C) The severe corneal opacity was observed after alkali burn induction. (D) The opacity score was improved after 48 h (p = 0.075) and significantly after 1 week (p = 0.029). Mann–Whitney test. *p < 0.05

At 1 week, corneal neovascularization had progressed further, with vessels extending toward the central cornea in both groups (Fig. 5A). Consistent with the 48-hour findings, the neovascularization score remained comparable between the control and PBM groups, indicating that PBM did not significantly suppress vascular ingrowth during the observation period (Fig. 5B). In contrast, corneal opacity was visibly reduced in the PBM group compared with the control group at 1 week (Fig. 5C). Quantitative analysis confirmed that this difference was statistically significant in the opacity score analysis (p = 0.029; Fig. 5D). Taken together, these findings suggest that while repeated 830-nm PBM treatment did not significantly affect corneal neovascularization, it significantly alleviated corneal opacity during the subacute healing phase after alkali burn injury.

Inflammatory cytokine readouts after PBM

To assess the anti-inflammatory effects of PBM, immunohistochemical analyses were performed on rat corneal tissue at 48 h after alkali burn induction (Fig. 6). The expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α was evaluated by measuring the percentage of stained area within the corneal epithelium. PBM treatment significantly reduced the expression of all three inflammatory cytokines compared with the alkali burn control group. The reductions were highly significant for IL-1β and IL-6 (both p < 0.001) and significant for TNF-α (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6B, D, and F), indicating that PBM suppressed inflammatory protein expression in the injured cornea.

PBM reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines in corneal tissues after alkali burn injury. Representative IHC images and quantitative analysis of IL-1β (A, B), IL-6 (C, D), and TNF-α (E, F) in the alkali burn control and PBM-treated groups. PBM treatment significantly reduced the expression of all three inflammatory cytokines compared with the alkali burn control group. Data are presented as mean ± SD. ***p < 0.001 and *p < 0.05 vs. alkali burn control group

To further validate these findings at the transcriptional level, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis was performed at 48 h and 1 week after alkali burn induction (Fig. 7). In perfect agreement with the protein-level observations, the PBM-treated group exhibited a statistically significant reduction in the mRNA expression levels of all three pro-inflammatory cytokines—IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α—at 48 h compared with the injured control group (p < 0.05; Fig. 7A). This robust anti-inflammatory effect was maintained and became even more pronounced at 1 week post-injury, where the mRNA expression of IL-1β and IL-6 was markedly suppressed (both p < 0.001), and TNF-α expression remained significantly lower in the PBM group (p < 0.05; Fig. 7B). Together, these results demonstrate that 830-nm PBM exerts a powerful and sustained anti-inflammatory effect at both the mRNA and protein levels during the early and subacute phases of corneal alkali injury.

Compared with the injured control group, the PBM-treated group showed significantly lower expression of all three cytokines. Data are presented as mean ± SD. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001 vs. injured control group

The PBM effect on Rho-GTPase expression

To investigate molecular changes potentially associated with the wound-healing effects of PBM, we evaluated the expression of Rho-GTPase-associated proteins in whole corneal lysates collected at 24 and 48 h after alkali burn induction (Fig. 8). The proteins analyzed included RhoA, RhoC, Cdc42, and Rac1/2/3. At 24 h, the PBM group showed significantly higher expression of RhoC and Cdc42 than the control group (Fig. 8B), whereas RhoA and Rac1/2/3 did not differ significantly between groups. At 48 h, RhoA and RhoC expression were significantly increased in the PBM group compared with the control group (Fig. 8D), while no significant difference was observed for Cdc42 or Rac1/2/3. Overall, these results indicate that PBM was associated with altered expression of selected Rho-GTPase-related proteins during the early phase after alkali burn injury. However, because whole corneal tissue was analyzed, the observed changes cannot be attributed specifically to corneal epithelial cells.

Discussion

Corneal injuries directly affect vision and can cause blindness. It is important for corneal transparency to induce re-epithelialization quickly to prevent side effects and maintain corneal homeostasis. Steroids, antibiotics, and artificial tears are commonly used to treat chemical injuries, which can prevent serious side effects. Recently, PBM has shown potential for treatment in various medical fields and is a treatment method that is clinically used [30, 31]. It is a non-invasive therapy that has demonstrated therapeutic effects in promoting wound healing, and reducing inflammation and pain and is being explored as a potential treatment option in ophthalmology [17, 32].

In this study, the PBM group showed a faster rate of wound closure in in vitro and in vivo models compared to the control group. PBM effectively increases the proliferation and migration of corneal epithelial cells after treatment. Previous studies have reported that the increasing proliferation and migration by PBM presented diverse cell types such as fibroblast, keratinocytes, and endothelial cells. Besides, in alkali burn induction, the differences were significant at 12 h, and there was an increase in the number of Ki67-positive cells at 24 h. The corneal epithelial wound healing process involves the initial migration of cells from the limbus to cover the corneal wound surface, followed by rapid cell proliferation to generate new cell layers [33, 34]. Considering this process, it suggests that PBM treatment enhanced the migration of epithelial cells during the first 12 h after the alkali burn, although there was not much difference in cell proliferation. In the subsequent 12 h, PBM treatment increased the number of proliferating cells. Furthermore, the positive effects of PBM on wound closure have been demonstrated in various injury models, including diabetic mouse models and thermal burn models [10, 35]. Therefore, PBM treatment also demonstrated beneficial effects on cell proliferation in the cornea.

An anti-inflammatory action is frequently discussed in the PBM field [17, 36, 37]; therefore, we evaluated inflammatory cytokine readouts after alkali burn induction. In the present study, PBM significantly reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines at the protein level in corneal tissue at 48 h, which was thoroughly validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis showing robust and statistically significant decreases in the mRNA expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α at both 48 h and 1 week (Figs. 6 and 7). These concurrent findings at both the transcriptional and translational levels provide strong and complementary evidence supporting the potent anti-inflammatory efficacy of 830-nm PBM in the injured cornea. However, because the present analyses did not include uninjured controls and were limited in their ability to define deeper stromal inflammatory processes, the results should still be interpreted with appropriate caution [36, 37].

Proinflammatory cytokines play a crucial role in corneal immune responses, angiogenesis, and wound healing. Increased or prolonged expression of these cytokines is associated with various corneal disorders, tissue destruction, and delayed wound closure [38, 39]. Characteristically, the expression of inflammatory cytokines can reach peak levels or persist up to a week after severe alkali burn induction, leading to chronic stromal remodeling and corneal haze [40]. Our chronological analysis demonstrated that 830-nm PBM successfully suppressed the acute-phase inflammatory surge at 48 h and, furthermore, maintained this targeted down-regulation up to 1 week post-injury, particularly showing a highly pronounced inhibition of IL-1β and IL-6 mRNA. By demonstrating consistent anti-inflammatory efficacy across different molecular layers (mRNA and protein) and multiple time points, our study addresses a common challenge in the PBM field—namely, the parameter-dependent inconsistency of biological responses [19]. In our own earlier work using identical equipment but a lower irradiance (5 mW/cm²), PBM paradoxically elevated corneal inflammatory cytokines and worsened structural outcomes, underscoring the critical importance of parameter optimization [20]. The robust and reproducible anti-inflammatory patterns observed under the current 8.9 mW/cm² protocol confirm that optimized 830-nm PBM parameters can establish a highly favorable microenvironment for corneal repair.

Importantly, this robust early molecular protection translated into meaningful structural benefits during subacute corneal healing. Our data indicate that 830-nm PBM not only accelerated initial re-epithelialization—as reflected by accelerated wound closure and increased proliferative activity within the first 24–48 h—but also significantly alleviated corneal opacity at 1 week (p = 0.029; Fig. 5D). This subacute reduction in corneal haze suggests that the early suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the rapid restoration of epithelial integrity may collectively mitigate subsequent aberrant stromal remodeling. Although corneal neovascularization scores remained comparable between the control and PBM groups during the 1-week observation period, the significant improvement in corneal clarity indicates that PBM holds substantial therapeutic potential as an early supportive intervention following chemical injury. To fully evaluate whether these beneficial effects are sustained, longer follow-up studies extending beyond 1 week will be necessary to assess chronic outcomes, including stromal fibrosis, myofibroblast activation, and long-term corneal endothelial cell density [8, 40, 41].

Corneal endothelial integrity and lenticular transparency were not directly assessed in the present study; however, within our observation period, PBM did not worsen corneal opacity and was associated with improved opacity scores at 1 week, suggesting that the applied parameters did not exert obvious adverse effects on deeper ocular structures. Dedicated studies will be required to evaluate PBM’s effects on stromal remodeling, endothelial cell density, and lens clarity after alkali injury.

PBM is known to stimulate biological activities by activating cellular signaling pathways [12, 42]. In this study, PBM treatment was found to promote both the proliferation and migration of corneal epithelial cells. Because functional inhibition or knockdown experiments were not performed, the present data do not establish the necessity of the Rho-GTPase axis for PBM-induced epithelial repair. Rather, the observed upregulation of RhoA/RhoC should be viewed as a mechanistic correlate that motivates future loss-of-function studies. Rho-GTPases are involved in various cellular events such as the cell cycle, cell migration, and actin cytoskeletal rearrangement [21, 43]. Their coordinated activity plays a pivotal role in wound healing, migration, and proliferation [41, 44].

Especially, previous research reported that Rho is important in corneal epithelial cell proliferation and the cell cycle while Rac/Cdc42 induces cell expansion during cell migration [22]. Inhibiting Rho activity has been shown to decrease the proliferation of corneal epithelial cells, ultimately impairing corneal epithelial wound healing [21]. Rho is involved in the downstream activation of pathways that modulate the actin cytoskeleton in the corneal wound model [43]. Additionally, Rho/ROCK signaling regulates cell cycle progression by upregulating cyclinD1 in rabbit corneal epithelial cells [45]. On the other hand, silencing Cdc42 has been reported to delay cell migration in wound healing assays, suggesting that Cdc42 plays a complementary role alongside RhoA in coordinating the cytoskeletal dynamics required for epithelial sheet movement.

Recently, the use of PBM in combination with other drugs has shown better results in reducing inflammation, wound healing, and biological activities compared to using the drugs alone in various models and clinical studies [46]. Although anti-inflammatory drugs are effective in treating corneal injuries, they can have significant side effects and are not suitable for long-term use [47]. Considering the recent studies on the positive effects of combination therapy of PBM and drugs and our findings which is decreasing the pattern of inflammatory cytokines, such a strategy may also have a synergistic effect on the cornea, reducing inflammation and improving corneal conditions.

This study has several limitations that constrain mechanistic interpretation. First, mitochondrial endpoints (e.g., ATP content, membrane potential, or respiration) were not measured; therefore, mitochondrial mechanisms frequently discussed in the PBM literature cannot be concluded from our data. Second, upstream signaling events linking PBM to changes in cytoskeletal regulators were not interrogated. Third, functional blocking experiments were not conducted, and thus the necessity of the Rho-GTPase pathway for PBM-associated epithelial repair remains unproven. Fourth, Ki67 immunostaining was not performed at 48 h, which limits temporal interpretation of the PBM-associated proliferative response and prevents direct comparison with the wound closure data at the same timepoint. Fifth, uninjured control tissue was not available for inclusion in the IHC or qPCR analyses, which limits the ability to establish the absolute magnitude of PBM-induced cytokine modulation relative to the normal uninjured state. Sixth, inflammatory assessments were limited and largely epithelial in the current analysis, and while subacute opacity was significantly alleviated at 1 week, chronic stromal outcomes and long-term neovascularization dynamics require further evaluation under extended follow-up periods. Finally, while the 1 N NaOH alkali burn model used here has been validated for early-phase corneal injury research [20, 28], the degree of injury severity may differ from clinical scenarios involving more concentrated or prolonged alkali exposure, and the generalizability of the present findings to more severe injuries should be evaluated in future studies. These limitations define clear priorities for future work.

In conclusion, 830-nm PBM accelerated early corneal epithelial wound closure in a rat alkali burn model and enhanced corneal epithelial cell migration and proliferation in complementary in vitro assays. PBM was accompanied by increased expression of selected Rho-GTPases (RhoA/RhoC), suggesting a potential involvement of cytoskeletal signaling during epithelial repair, and was further characterized by a significant downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines at both the transcriptional and translational levels. Taken together, our results support PBM as a non-invasive strategy that may facilitate early re-epithelialization after chemical injury, and they provide a basis for future mechanistic studies incorporating mitochondrial endpoints and functional pathway interrogation.
