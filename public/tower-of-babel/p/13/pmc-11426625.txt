Exploring the Link between Sleep Disorders and Ocular Health: A Comprehensive Clinical Study
Authors: Deepankar, Sunil Kumar, Rohit K. Mahato, Neha Kiran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Sleep disorders and ocular conditions are prevalent health concerns with significant impact on the quality of life. However, the relationship between them remains underexplored. Additionally, therapeutic interventions targeting both sleep and eye health are explored.Materials and Methods:A prospective clinical study was conducted involving 500 participants aged between 30 and 65 years, recruited from an outpatient clinic specializing in sleep disorders and ophthalmology. Participants underwent comprehensive assessments including polysomnography for sleep disorders diagnosis and ophthalmic examinations for ocular condition evaluation. Sleep quality was assessed using standardized questionnaires, while ocular health was evaluated through clinical examination, including measurements of intraocular pressure, tear film break-up time, and retinal imaging. Statistical analyses were performed using Chi-square tests and logistic regression models to determine associations between sleep disorders and ocular conditions, adjusting for potential confounders.Results:Among the participants, 35% were diagnosed with sleep apnea, 25% with insomnia, 20% with dry eye syndrome, 15% with glaucoma, and 10% with diabetic retinopathy. Significant associations were found between sleep disorders and ocular conditions. Participants with sleep apnea were more likely to have dry eye syndrome (odds ratio [OR] = 2.5, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.8–3.4, P < 0.001) and glaucoma (OR = 1.8, 95% CI = 1.3–2.5, P = 0.002). Similarly, insomnia was associated with dry eye syndrome (OR = 2.2, 95% CI = 1.5–3.1, P < 0.001) and diabetic retinopathy (OR = 1.6, 95% CI = 1.1–2.3, P = 0.012).Conclusion:This study highlights the significant association between sleep disorders and ocular conditions, suggesting a potential bidirectional relationship.

INTRODUCTION

Sleep disorders and ocular conditions are two prevalent health issues affecting millions of individuals worldwide and are known to significantly impact overall well-being and quality of life.[12] Sleep disorders encompass a wide spectrum of conditions ranging from sleep apnea to insomnia, each characterized by disturbances in sleep patterns and quality.[3] However, ocular conditions such as dry eye syndrome, glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy represent a diverse group of disorders affecting different structures of the eye, leading to visual impairment and potential blindness if left untreated.[456]

While both sleep disorders and ocular conditions have been extensively studied in isolation, the potential interplay between them has gained attention only recently.[78]

Through rigorous examination and analysis, this study seeks to contribute to the growing body of knowledge surrounding the relationship between sleep disorders and ocular conditions and pave the way for more integrated and holistic approaches to patient care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This prospective clinical study involved 500 participants recruited from an outpatient clinic specializing in sleep disorders and ophthalmology. Participants aged between 30 and 65 years were enrolled after providing informed consent. Individuals with a history of significant ocular trauma, surgery, or systemic conditions affecting ocular health were excluded from the study.

Assessment of sleep disorders

Participants underwent comprehensive assessments for the diagnosis of sleep disorders, including obstructive sleep apnea and insomnia. Polysomnography, the gold standard for sleep disorders diagnosis, was conducted to evaluate sleep architecture, respiratory events, and nocturnal oxygen saturation levels. Additionally, standardized questionnaires such as the Epworth Sleepiness Scale and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index were administered to assess subjective sleep quality and daytime sleepiness.

Evaluation of ocular conditions

Ophthalmic examinations were performed to assess ocular health and identify any existing ocular conditions. Clinical evaluations included measurements of intraocular pressure using Goldmann applanation tonometry, assessment of tear film stability through tear film break-up time measurement, and fundus examination using slit-lamp biomicroscopy and fundus photography. Participants were also screened for the presence of dry eye syndrome, glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy based on the established diagnostic criteria.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using appropriate methods to determine the association between sleep disorders and ocular conditions. Chi-square tests were utilized to assess the categorical variables, while logistic regression models were employed to adjust for potential confounders such as age, gender, and comorbidities. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated to quantify the strength of associations between sleep disorders and ocular conditions. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Similarly, the prevalence of glaucoma was 15% among all participants, with higher proportions observed in individuals with sleep apnea (20%) than those with insomnia (10%) or without a sleep disorder (10%).

Regarding diabetic retinopathy, 10% of participants were diagnosed with this condition, with similar proportions across sleep disorder categories, although slightly elevated prevalence was noted among individuals with insomnia (15%).

DISCUSSION

The observed higher prevalence of dry eye syndrome among individuals with sleep apnea and insomnia aligns with previous research highlighting the impact of sleep disturbances on tear film dynamics and ocular surface health.[12] Sleep apnea, characterized by intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation, has been implicated in ocular surface inflammation and tear film instability, contributing to the development of dry eye syndrome.[3] Similarly, insomnia, often associated with increased sympathetic nervous system activity and altered tear production, may exacerbate ocular surface discomfort and dryness.[4]

Our study also found an elevated prevalence of glaucoma among participants with sleep apnea, consistent with previous literature suggesting a potential link between obstructive sleep apnea and glaucomatous optic neuropathy.[56] The mechanisms underlying this association may involve nocturnal fluctuations in intraocular pressure, altered ocular blood flow dynamics, and oxidative stress, all of which could contribute to optic nerve damage and glaucoma progression.[7]

Interestingly, while diabetic retinopathy was not significantly associated with sleep apnea in our study, individuals with insomnia exhibited a higher prevalence of this ocular complication. This finding is consistent with emerging evidence suggesting a bidirectional relationship between chronic sleep disturbances and diabetic retinopathy, possibly mediated by systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and microvascular dysfunction.[89]

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our findings highlight the significant association between sleep disorders and ocular conditions, emphasizing the need for comprehensive assessment and management of both aspects of health in clinical practice.
