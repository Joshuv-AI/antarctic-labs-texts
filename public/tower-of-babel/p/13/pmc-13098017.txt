Antimicrobial Resistance in Cancer Care: Challenge and Path Forward
Authors: Majani Edward, Ameh Wisdom Owoicho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global health crisis that poses a devastating and specific threat to immunocompromised cancer patients, who are heavily reliant on antibiotics to navigate high‐risk treatments. The rapid emergence of multidrug‐resistant (MDR) pathogens compromises the efficacy of prophylaxis and empirical therapy, leading to increased morbidity, mortality, and economic burden. This article aims to critically analyze the unique challenges of AMR in oncology and evaluate the translational readiness of innovative therapeutic modalities as a sustainable path forward.MethodsWe synthesize current literature to articulate the clinical and economic burden of AMR specifically within the oncology setting. The article goes beyond conventional antibiotic stewardship discussions to critically appraise non‐traditional strategies, including the clinical and regulatory barriers facing bacteriophage therapy, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), and CRISPR‐Cas systems.ResultsAMR threatens the safe delivery of systemic cancer therapies, as resistant infections necessitate dose modifications and treatment delays. Traditional antibiotic development is insufficient. The path forward requires a paradigm shift: coupling robust stewardship and rapid diagnostics with a critical investment in innovative therapies. While promising, these novel modalities are not “silver bullets” and face significant hurdles in standardization, manufacturing, and regulatory approval that must be addressed for clinical integration into cancer care.ConclusionThe fight against AMR is inseparable from the fight against cancer. To safeguard the future of oncology, a concerted effort is required to advance non‐traditional anti‐infectives through the translational pipeline, while ensuring immediate, tailored infection management for this vulnerable population.

Introduction

Cancer care exemplifies the devastating impact of this looming crisis [1, 2]. When resistance undermines these essential drugs, the consequences are severe—higher rates of sepsis, prolonged hospital stays, escalating medical costs, and increased mortality [3]. According to surveys, about half of doctors worry that significant cancer treatments may become impractical due to drug resistance. This is concerning because oncologists are becoming more worried that resistant infections could compromise the safe delivery of chemotherapy [4]. As global health leaders pursue ambitious initiatives to transform cancer care, including the Biden Administration's “Cancer Moonshot,” the rise of AMR threatens to derail these efforts, underscoring the inseparable link between infection control and effective cancer treatment [5].

The biological basis of antibiotic resistance further highlights the magnitude of the problem [6]. Resistance may be innate or acquired, mediated by genetic mutations [7], horizontal gene transfer, or adaptive mechanisms that enable bacteria to survive exposure to drugs that once proved lethal [8]. These changes can confer resistance to single drug classes or, more alarmingly, lead to multidrug resistance (MDR), defined as resistance to three or more antibiotic categories [3]. Particularly concerning are the ESKAPE pathogens—Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species—named for their ability to “escape” the effects of antibiotics and cause serious hospital‐acquired infections [9]. These pathogens are frequently implicated in infections among cancer patients, where their resistance profile makes treatment exceedingly difficult and often ineffective [10].

The escalating burden of AMR in cancer care makes clear that reliance on traditional antibiotics is unsustainable [11]. Protecting vulnerable patients requires a paradigm shift in how resistant infections are prevented, diagnosed, and treated [12]. Stewardship programs must be strengthened to maximize the lifespan of existing antibiotics, while investment in novel antibiotics, antimicrobial peptides, bacteriophage therapies, and other innovative modalities is urgently needed [13]. Equally critical advances in rapid diagnostic technologies that allow clinicians to tailor therapy precisely, avoiding unnecessary exposure to broad‐spectrum agents [14]. Infection prevention measures, including stringent hospital hygiene and tailored prophylactic strategies, must be integrated into oncology practice [15].

The fight against cancer cannot be separated from the fight against AMR [16]. Unless novel therapeutic strategies are prioritized and aggressively pursued [17], the hard‐won progress in cancer treatment risks being undone by the silent but relentless advance of resistant infections. The challenge is immense, but so too is the opportunity: by coupling breakthroughs in oncology with innovative approaches to combat AMR, it is possible to safeguard the future of cancer care and ensure that patients continue to benefit from the full promise of modern medicine [18]. Methodology

This article employed a narrative literature review methodology to conduct a critical analysis and strategic appraisal of the intertwined crisis of Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) and cancer care. The aim was not simply to summarize findings, but to synthesize evidence and evaluate the translational readiness of non‐traditional therapeutic solutions. A comprehensive literature search was executed across major academic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search strategy utilized a combination of focused keywords such as “Cancer,” “Oncology,” and “Immunocompromised” paired with “Antimicrobial Resistance,” “Multidrug Resistance (MDR),” and “ESKAPE” to establish the clinical and economic burden. Simultaneously, terms like “Bacteriophage therapy,” “Antimicrobial Peptides (AMPs),” and “CRISPR‐Cas” were used to identify literature on innovative anti‐infective modalities.

The selection criteria prioritized primary research, systematic reviews, and consensus guidelines published primarily between 2015 and the present, focusing specifically on how resistant infections impact cancer treatment schedules and patient outcomes. The extracted data underwent a critical, qualitative synthesis. This synthesis involved a three‐part appraisal: first, detailing the severity and cost of AMR in the oncology setting; second, conducting a comparative evaluation of each novel modality (phages, AMPs, CRISPR) based on its efficacy potential and current clinical trial status; and third, performing a strategic analysis to identify the key regulatory, manufacturing, and standardization hurdles that must be overcome to integrate these solutions into routine cancer care. This systematic approach allowed the review to move beyond conventional antibiotic stewardship discussions to propose a concrete, future‐oriented path for safeguarding vulnerable cancer patients.

Challenge of AMR in Cancer Care

Increased Morbidity and Mortality Associated With AMR Infections in Cancer Patients

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most pressing issues in cancer care because patients undergoing chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or hematopoietic stem‐cell transplantation are highly immunocompromised and heavily dependent on antibiotics for infection prevention and treatment. According to recent research, up to 40% of bloodstream infections in patients with hematologic malignancies are caused by multidrug‐resistant (MDR) Gram‐negative bacteria, specifically Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter spp., and Klebsiella pneumoniae [19, 20]. In patients with solid cancers, these microbes are linked to increased infection‐related mortality, prolonged neutropenia, and more therapy delays. Infections caused by MDR organisms have been associated with longer hospital stays [21], greater treatment expenditures, and poorer survival rates when compared to infections caused by susceptible strains. The growing resistance to last‐line drugs like colistin and carbapenems complicates the clinical treatment of cancer patients worldwide [22].

Challenges in Antibiotic Prophylaxis and Empirical Therapy

Prophylactic antibiotic use remains an essential component of supportive cancer therapy, particularly during chemotherapy‐induced neutropenia [23]. However, widespread resistance to fluoroquinolones and beta‐lactams has significantly reduced the efficacy of standard prophylactic regimens [24]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (2022) recommends fluoroquinolone prophylaxis for high‐risk neutropenic patients, yet emerging resistance and Clostridioides difficile infections have limited its benefit [25]. This dynamic demands a careful balance between preventing infection and minimizing resistance selection pressure. Empirical therapy is likewise challenged by MDR pathogens, as delayed or inappropriate initial antibiotic coverage has been correlated with increased mortality in neutropenic sepsis [26]. These therapeutic dilemmas highlight the urgent need for individualized antibiotic stewardship programs within oncology settings.

How AMR Complicates Cancer Therapies

AMR Affects Nearly Every Modality of Cancer Treatment

Chemotherapy: Resistance reduces the effectiveness of prophylaxis during neutropenia, increasing the frequency of febrile episodes, treatment interruptions, and dose reductions [27]. Radiotherapy: Mucosal barrier injury predisposes patients to bacterial translocation, and infection with resistant organisms delays tissue recovery and prolongs hospitalization [28]. Surgical oncology: Resistant postoperative infections following tumor resections or catheter placements raise morbidity and extend recovery time [29]. Stem‐cell and bone‐marrow transplantation: Profound immunosuppression exposes recipients to multidrug‐resistant bacterial and fungal infections that are often refractory to conventional therapy [21].

Antimicrobial resistance complicates cancer therapy through multiple interconnected pathways. Mechanistically, resistant pathogens prolong infection clearance and delay recovery, which disrupts treatment schedules and reduces chemotherapy dose intensity—both of which are linked to poorer survival outcomes [30]. Clinically, AMR infections often necessitate the use of last‐resort antibiotics that have narrow therapeutic windows and severe side effects, further compromising patient tolerance to ongoing cancer therapy [31]. The need for isolation precautions and prolonged hospital stays also delays subsequent treatment cycles and limits access to critical care resources.

Healthcare System Challenges and Economic Burden

It is commonly acknowledged from numerous research papers that managing AMR among healthcare personnel is difficult [32]. This problem stems from the possible ignorance or insufficient access to resources of healthcare personnel [33]. Ninety‐two percent of medical students in the United States said that knowledge of antibiotics is vital for all healthcare professionals. However, 90% of respondents expressed a strong desire for more education regarding the appropriate use of antimicrobials, and around one‐third felt sufficiently knowledgeable about the guidelines governing their use [34]. Healthcare workers who operate in resource‐constrained situations deal with a difficult conundrum on a daily basis. They may come into circumstances where the risk of infection is increased because of insufficient infection prevention and control measures or poor hygiene and sanitation, even though they recognize the significance of avoiding indiscriminate antibiotic prescriptions. Improving the healthcare system as a whole and guaranteeing a smooth supply chain for drugs to treat infections are essential to combating AMR. According to Khameneh et al. (2016), severe sepsis, which calls for the use of the right medicines, will cause the death of 8.5% of cancer patients [35]. Because short‐duration antibiotics are not profitable, people are turning to chronic diseases instead. Low‐cost antibiotics can be expensive, and doctors tend to keep new medications for emergencies, which delays the return on investment [36]. As a result, new treatment development is delayed. Pharmaceutical companies' return on investment when developing new treatments is consequently further postponed [37]. Understanding the use of antibiotics and patient illnesses is essential for managing and reducing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) while recommending appropriate medications.

In addition to spending their time treating infected patients, healthcare professionals go above and beyond to obtain funding for required testing and treatments [38]. Global estimates suggest that by 2050, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) could impose economic losses ranging from approximately $300 billion to over $1 trillion annually [39]. However, these figures should be interpreted with caution, as they stem from complex macroeconomic modeling rather than direct expenditure data. Such projections often depend on assumptions about infection prevalence, healthcare costs, and productivity losses that vary significantly across regions and income levels [40]. Studies employing computable general equilibrium (CGE) and health‐economics simulation models yield wide‐ranging outcomes depending on their underlying parameters [41]. Therefore, while these estimates illustrate the potential scale of the economic threat, they represent scenario‐based forecasts rather than fixed financial predictions.

The majority of cancer patients are aware that chemotherapy weakens their immune systems and makes them more vulnerable to viral and bacterial infections [42]. They would prefer to employ telemedicine; this technology improves the way cancer care is delivered because it can reduce hospitalization rates, pain, anxiety, and sleep issues [43]. These discussions, which are currently held via online video conferencing, involve interdisciplinary tumor boards and a variety of medical specialists due to the intricacy of cancer treatment options [44]. This guarantees that duties are distributed properly when choosing a course of therapy.

As a result, physicians are unable to provide antibiotics to cancer patients due to the prevalence of AMR [45]. Our understanding of possible losses is hampered by the lack of information on the precise costs related to the many secondary consequences of AMR. It is admirable how committed they are to making sure patients receive thorough care [46]. Global change will result from the development of strategies to continue providing care for illnesses impacted by antibiotic resistance while maintaining patient and healthcare worker safety.

AMR Effect on the Cost of Cancer Treatments

Growing resistance raises the expense of specialist equipment, longer hospital stays, patient isolation measures [47], and more costly antibiotics (infections that develop resistance to first‐line treatments must be treated with second or third‐line drugs, which are nearly always more expensive). This has a detrimental effect on patients' outcomes from therapy and raises the cost of cancer care [48]. Antimicrobial‐resistant cancer patients frequently require intensive care and other medical procedures during their prolonged hospital stays. Due to these realities, healthcare prices have significantly increased, placing a financial strain on healthcare systems, providers, and patients alike. According to one investigation, the annual economic cost of illnesses linked to AMR in the USA alone is $2 billion [49].

In order to treat antimicrobial‐resistant infections in cancer patients, new, costly antibiotics and antifungal medications must be administered; as a result, the cost of antimicrobial agents, in addition to other cancer treatments, is a significant portion of the total cost of cancer treatment [50]. The management of infections in cancer patients is complicated by antimicrobial‐resistant organisms, which raises the risk of treatment failure and death. Inappropriate antimicrobial drugs also complicate cancer treatment tolerance, which usually leads to dosage reductions and therapy delays or termination [51]. Treatment failure is noteworthy because it can lead to both greater healthcare expenses and ineffective treatment [52].

The lengthening of hospital stays is another significant impact of AMR on cancer care that places patients and healthcare systems in difficult financial situations [53]. Consequently, the overall economy is also impacted. AMR complicates the treatment of infections in cancer patients and frequently necessitates longer, more involved medical procedures, sometimes involving hospitalization [53]. The length of hospitalization brought on by AMR‐related infections directly raises direct healthcare expenditures, which include hospitalization, diagnostic tests, antibiotic treatment, and supportive care costs [54]. According to the research, each additional day spent in the hospital has a significant financial impact, ranging from hundreds to thousands of dollars per day for an individual [55]. All facets of a household's health expenses, including direct, indirect, and intangible costs, should be taken into account when planning and developing health system policy [56]. Patients find it challenging to maintain their routines during extended hospital stays because it interferes with their capacity to work, take care of their families, and socialize [57]. In order to combat AMR‐related illnesses, a greater variety of antibiotics—particularly second‐line therapies—are frequently needed; however, these medications are more costly and less efficient than first‐choice medications [58]. Long hospital stays for cancer patients become more expensive due to the soaring cost of antibiotics [59]. The Lee et al. study found that those with multidrug‐resistant illnesses received two to three times as many antibiotic prescriptions as people with simple susceptible infections [60].

AMR causes more diagnostic tests to be used in order to detect resistant bacteria and make treatment decisions, even while it makes managing tumors more difficult [61]. To fight AMR infections, doctors heavily rely on tests such as pathogen culture, antibiotic resistance testing, and treatment regimen assignment [62]. In order to effectively combat antibiotic‐resistant microorganisms, further tests are required to detect and describe these species [63]. Numerous techniques, including as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing, culture and sensitivity assays, next‐generation sequencing (NGS), and others, can be used to achieve this [63]. Because these tests necessitate specialized facilities, lab equipment, and trained workers, their utilization increases hospital expenditures [64].

Antimicrobial resistance testing usually uses antibacterial resistance detectors, such as specific diagnostic kits, reagents, and consumables [65]. The cost of buying and maintaining cancer treatment supplies is particularly high in underdeveloped locations where access to state‐of‐the‐art diagnostic equipment may be limited [66]. Furthermore, patients and healthcare systems may incur additional expenditures due to the diagnostic testing fees charged by outside laboratories [67]. When choosing a course of treatment for cancer patients, diagnostic tests that reveal antibiotic resistance are crucial. Treatment delays and longer hospital stays may arise because clinicians cannot begin their specialized antimicrobial treatment until test results are available [67]. On the other hand, these delays worsen patient outcomes and increase the cost of medical care, including extended hospital stays, supportive care, and further interventions [68].

Future Directions

The fight against antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in cancer care requires a multifaceted approach that addresses the unique vulnerabilities of this patient population. The path forward must involve the development of novel therapeutic strategies, advancements in diagnostics, and a commitment to robust antimicrobial stewardship.

The conventional approach of developing new broad‐spectrum antibiotics is becoming less effective due to the rapid emergence of resistance. Therefore, a paradigm shift is needed towards innovative modalities that circumvent traditional resistance mechanisms.

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs): These are small, naturally occurring peptides that have potent and broad‐spectrum antimicrobial activity. They act by disrupting bacterial cell membranes, a mechanism that is less prone to resistance development compared to traditional antibiotics. AMPs can also modulate the host immune response and may have synergistic effects with conventional antibiotics. Research into synthetic AMPs and their application in cancer patients, particularly in topical formulations or for systemic infections, holds significant promise [69].

Bacteriophage therapy: Bacteriophages, or “phages,” are viruses that specifically target and kill bacteria. Each phage is highly specific to a particular bacterial species or strain, which minimizes disruption to the patient's beneficial microbiota. Phage therapy, which was used before the discovery of penicillin, is experiencing a resurgence. Phage cocktails can be tailored to target specific multidrug‐resistant (MDR) pathogens like the ESKAPE group. Clinical trials are now exploring the use of phages to treat resistant infections in immunocompromised patients, including those with cancer [70].

Vaccines: Preventing infections in the first place is a powerful way to reduce the need for antibiotics and, consequently, the development of resistance. The development of vaccines targeting high‐risk pathogens, such as Staphylococcus aureus or Pseudomonas aeruginosa, could significantly lower the burden of infections in cancer patients and reduce their reliance on prophylactic and therapeutic antibiotics [71].

CRISPR‐Cas systems: Genome editing technologies, like CRISPR‐Cas, are being explored as a revolutionary new way to combat AMR. This technology can be used to target and destroy resistance genes within bacteria, effectively “re‐sensitizing” them to existing antibiotics. It can also be engineered to kill specific pathogenic bacteria while leaving the beneficial microbiota unharmed. While still in its early stages, this approach offers a highly precise and powerful tool for treating resistant infections [65].

While innovative strategies such as antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), bacteriophage therapy, CRISPR‐Cas genome editing, and vaccine development represent exciting frontiers in the fight against antimicrobial resistance, each carries notable limitations that must be critically considered. AMPs, though broad‐spectrum and less prone to resistance, often face challenges related to toxicity, instability, and high production costs, limiting large‐scale application. Bacteriophage therapy requires individualized bacterial matching and remains constrained by regulatory uncertainties, inconsistent clinical protocols, and limited randomized trials [72]. CRISPR‐Cas technologies, while offering precision in targeting resistance genes, are largely experimental and have yet to demonstrate consistent efficacy or safety in oncology‐specific contexts [73]. In a similar vein, vaccinations against resistant pathogens are still in the early stages of development, and it's unclear how beneficial they will be for cancer patients with compromised immune systems. In order to get these medicines from concept to practical reality, it is imperative that regulatory innovation and translational research continue.

Conclusion

The intertwined challenges of cancer care and antimicrobial resistance pose a grave threat to modern medicine. Cancer patients, who are inherently immunocompromised, face a heightened risk of serious infections, which are increasingly caused by multidrug‐resistant pathogens. The unchecked rise of AMR not only increases morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs but also jeopardizes the very foundation of safe and effective cancer treatment. The current reliance on traditional antibiotics is unsustainable, necessitating a collective and urgent response. To safeguard the future of cancer care, a paradigm shift is essential. This shift requires a global commitment to strengthening antimicrobial stewardship, investing in rapid and accurate diagnostic tools, and, most critically, pioneering novel therapeutic strategies that circumvent existing resistance mechanisms. By integrating innovative approaches like antimicrobial peptides, bacteriophage therapy, and CRISPR‐based technologies, alongside robust infection prevention measures, we can protect vulnerable patients and ensure that the hard‐won progress in cancer treatment is not undone by the silent, relentless threat of AMR. The fight against cancer is inseparable from the fight against AMR, and only a united effort can secure the full promise of modern medicine.
