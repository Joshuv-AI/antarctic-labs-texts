Machine learning predictions of climate change effects on nearly threatened bird species (Crithagra xantholaema) habitat in Ethiopia for conservation strategies
Authors: Tadele Bedo Gelete, Diriba Tulu, Kalid Hassen Yasin, Erana Kebede
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Endemic and endangered bird species, such as Salvadori serin (C. xantholaema), are vulnerable to environmental and anthropogenic changes. Understanding the impact of climate change on ecological niches is essential for effective conservation. This study employed advanced ML algorithms to model the current and future suitability of C. xantholaema under two scenarios (SSP245 and SSP585) for the years 2050 and 2070. The four machine learning models, namely, Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Extreme Gradient Boost (XGBoost), predicted habitat suitability using 188 presence occurrence data and 15 environmental factors. Model performance was assessed using AUC-ROC, accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, kappa, and F1 score, with ensemble modeling techniques enhancing reliability. The current analysis indicated high predictive accuracy, with XGBoost achieving the highest AUC (0.99), followed by RF (0.98), SVM (0.97), and MaxEnt (0.92). Regarding habitat suitability, 75.3% of Ethiopia’s land was unsuitable for C. xantholaema, with only 3.9% classified as highly suitable. By 2050, 61.82% and 57.14% of areas were projected to be unsuitable under SSP245 and SSP585, respectively. By 2070, unsuitable habitats may increase to 65.24% (SSP245) and 60.17% (SSP585), further decreasing habitat suitability. High-suitability habitats are expected to decline by 80.8% in 2050, covering approximately 8,259.95 km2, and by 73.2% in 2070, covering about 11,584.6 km2. Precipitation during the driest month (Bio14) was the most crucial predictor of habitat suitability, with importance values ranging from 32.5% (XGBoost) to 100% (SVM and RF), while temperature-related factors, particularly annual mean temperature (Bio1), contributed differently across ML models. According to this study, climate factors impact habitat changes. The findings emphasize the urgent need for conservation strategies to mitigate C. xantholaema habitat loss. Future research should include local data and other human-related factors to enhance the effectiveness of conservation efforts and improve predictions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20952-4.

Introduction

Biodiversity, which encompasses the variety of life forms and their interactions within ecosystems, is essential for the resilience and functionality of natural ecosystems1. However, global warming has emerged as a serious threat to biodiversity, increasing existing pressures and creating new challenges2. Additionally, habitat loss and fragmentation, especially intensified by global warming, are significant threats to species distributions and populations and are projected to worsen shortly3,4.

Birds are among the most susceptible species to climate change, particularly those with limited ranges, mobility limitations, small populations, or additional conservation challenges5,6. Climate change is expected to alter the geographical ranges of bird species, with tropical birds migrating to higher altitudes and northern temperate species moving north7. These changes in species distribution and abundance have broad impacts on biodiversity, making it crucial to understand climate-induced changes in habitat suitability to develop effective conservation strategies. Accurate predictions of how climate change affects species distributions enable the prioritization of areas that will become suitable for the species in the future8,9.

Although these worldwide shifts and trends are alarming, this study focuses on Ethiopia, a biodiversity hotspot facing unique challenges due to climate change, particularly for endemic bird species such as the Salvadori serin (C. xantholaema)10. Ethiopia, a global center of endemism, protects much of its biodiversity in highland areas that are more vulnerable to climate change and human influences11,12. There is a significant research gap in predicting the distribution and habitat suitability of the country’s endemic bird species concerning climate change, with few studies conducted on species such as the Ethiopian bush crow (Zavattariornis stresemanni) and white-tailed swallow (Hirundo megnensis)13, four highland birds14, Ruspoli Turaco (Tauraco ruspolii) and white-cheeked Turaco (Tauraco leucotis)15, and spot-breasted lapwing (Vanellus gregarious)16. Salvadori, one of Ethiopia’s 19 endemic bird species and classified as near threatened is found in the central Harar, northern Bale, and central Sidamo regions. Its habitats include subtropical or tropical dry forests and high-altitude shrublands10,17. Understanding the effects of climate change on the distribution and habitat suitability of Salvadori seedeaters, as well as projecting shifts in the species, is vital for managing populations and promoting successful conservation policies and programmes18–20.

Several studies have focused on predicting the impacts of climate change on bird species worldwide. These predictions use species distribution modeling (SDM) techniques, including advanced machine learning, to assess changes in distribution and abundance21–25. Although tropical bird species are especially vulnerable to global climate change, they receive significantly less attention than birds from temperate regions26,27. In the tropics, the general trend indicates that species may shift to higher elevations, reducing their distribution area as temperatures rise in the Great Plains28–30.

Species distribution models (SDMs) are effective tools that combine statistical and machine-learning approaches to identify suitable habitats and geographical locations of species30,31. These models can accurately predict the current distribution through some bioclimatic factors and can be applied to project future potential expansions under different climate change scenarios32. Machine learning techniques such as random forest (RF), generalized linear models (GLMs), flexible discriminant analysis (FDA), support vector machines (SVMs), multivariate adaptive regression splines (MARS), and maximum entropy (MaxEnt) have shown predictive accuracy in comparison to traditional statistical methods33,34. These models have emerged as major instruments for forecasting the habitats of different animal and plant species35–37.

In recent years, comparisons of SDMs and ML models, such as the CTA, RF, MARS, FDA, GLM, GBM, GAM, artificial neural network (ANN), and their ensembles, have shown that the RF model outperforms other models in forecasting the global distributions of plant species38–42. Ensemble models, which incorporate selected high-performance models, are effective in evaluating SDMs and are often very precise and realistic, significantly reducing uncertainty43,44. Although modern ML algorithms can capture complicated interactions and increase prediction accuracy, identifying adequately complex models that capture actual species–environment correlations remains challenging34. However, quantitative species–habitat relationships of SDMs, especially those based on AI approaches, have great potential for promoting environmental management and policy, but their potential needs to be fully exploited. Therefore, this study aimed to (1) examine the ability of four machine learning methods (RF, MaxEnt, SVM, and XGBoost) with varying degrees of complexity to predict the habitat suitability of the bird species C. xantholaema; (2) map the current and future distributions and habitat suitability of C. xantholaema under two climate change scenarios (SSP245 and SSP585) at periods (2041–2060 (2050) and 2061–2080 (2070)); and (3) determine habitat changes for future suitability projections for C. xantholaema in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods used

Description of the study area

Ethiopia is Africa’s largest landlocked country, covering 1.13 million km2 and located between 3° 40 and 15° N latitude and 33° and 48° E longitude, respectively (Fig. 1). The extraordinary topography, which ranges from 126 m below sea level in the Afar Depression to 4,620 m above sea level in Ras Dashen, greatly contributes to its ecological variety45. The avifaunal richness of Ethiopia is especially remarkable, with 821 bird species spread among 342 genera, 89 families, and 26 orders. This assemblage comprises 665 landward species (81%), 156 waterbird species (19%), 546 resident species (67%), and 275 migratory species (33%)46. The 17 breeding endemic species are particularly significant, accounting for 2% of the country’s total avifauna10. The 36 globally threatened species with diminishing numbers indicate conservation problems. These species include seven severely endangered, 12 endangered, and 17 vulnerable species.

Salvadori serin (Crithagra xantholaema) is one of Ethiopia’s indigenous avifauna species that stands out. Initially classified as Serinus, phylogenetic research using mitochondrial and nuclear DNA sequences led to its reclassification as Crithagra47. This rare finch, which measures approximately 11 cm in length, has distinctive morphological characteristics, such as grayish-brown plumage, a bright yellow throat, and a prominent black breast band that may occasionally be reduced to lateral points. Salvadori serin inhabits subtropical and tropical dry forests, as well as high-altitude shrubland, with a preference for Juniperus and Acacia-Commiphora forests at elevations ranging from 1000 to 1500 m48. The species is under significant anthropogenic pressure, particularly due to agricultural expansion and habitat degradation caused by fuel and construction material deforestation. These threats have been classified as near-threatened by the International Union for Conservation of Nature10.

Bird-occurrence data

The occurrence records of the target bird species (C. xantholaema) were retrieved from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) website, covering the period from January 2001 to April 2023, which was accessed on November 10, 202450. To improve SDM accuracy, historical data dating back to 2001 were included, considering species population decline10 and environmental consistency over time51. To mitigate spatial autocorrelation, the “grid Sample” function was used in the ‘disco’ package of R52 and uncertainty-free data sets resulted in 188 records for subsequent analysis, meeting the requirements for SDM and minimizing prediction errors53,54. The geographical independence of the occurrence data is critical for model training and evaluation because spatial clustering typically results in overfitting and reduces forecast performance by incorporating environmental biases from cluster regions55,56.

Preparation of predictor variables

Environmental predictor variables

Environmental predictor variables for modeling the current and future habitat suitability of C. xantholaema in Ethiopia were systematically selected and compiled from globally documented, widely accessible sources to increase methodological accuracy and ecological significance11,57–59. Nineteen bioclimatic variables (Table 1) were obtained from the WorldClim version 2.1 dataset (https://www.worldclim.org/) at a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (~ 1 km2) for the present-day baseline period (1970–2000)60. For future climate projections (years 2050 [2041–2060] and 2070 [2061–2080]), data were sourced from the HadGEM3-GC31-LL model (accessed via the WorldClim v2.1 (https://www.worldclim.org/future), a CMIP6 global circulation model (GCM), under two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways: SSP245 (moderate mitigation) and SSP585 (high emissions)61. The HadGEM3-GC31-LL model was selected through multi-model comparisons, illustrating its strong performance for Africa and Europe contents62. Similarly, the SSP245 and SSP585 scenarios were selected based on their validated performance in simulating climate paths63,64, their ability to represent the most probable future socio-economic pathways, and their capacity to capture different warming magnitudes (2.4 °C vs. 4.4 °C by 2100), which are critical for analyzing the vulnerability of endemic species65.

Land use, land cover, NDVI, and topographic variables

Land use/land cover (LULC) data at 30 m resolution were obtained from the Global Land Cover Product (http://geosimulation.cn/GlobalLUCCProduct.html) developed by66, which has been validated for high classification accuracy (overall accuracy: 85%; forest-class F1 score: 0.82). Although the Ethiopia-specific national LULC datasets were unavailable, the spatial and thematic resolution of this global product was considered appropriate for characterizing the habitat requirements of C. xantholaema. These LULC data were resampled to a 1-km resolution using bilinear interpolation, which was applied using the raster package52 in R version 4.2.267. Topographic variables, including elevation, slope, and aspects derived from the 30 m resolution Copernicus Digital Elevation Model, accessed through the Copernicus Open Access Hub (https://scihub.copernicus.eu/)68. Terrain analyses were conducted using the terrain() function within the raster package52 in R version 4.2.267.

Vegetation dynamics were measured using the MODIS Terra Vegetation Indices product (MOD13Q1), which provides NDVI data at a 250 m spatial resolution with a 16-day compositing cycle69. These data were resampled to a consistent 1 km resolution using bilinear interpolation applied via the project Raster() function from the raster package52 in R version 4.2.267. Bilinear interpolation calculates output cell values as weighted averages of the four nearest input cell centroids, making it particularly appropriate for continuous variables such as NDVI. All 24 predictor variables, including 19 bioclimatic variables, 3 topographic features, 1 land use/land cover (LULC) layer, and the NDVI, were standardized to a unified coordinate reference system (WGS 1984; EPSG:4326), resampled to a 1 km2 grid, and spatially clipped to the extent of Ethiopia (3°40′–15° N, 33°–48° E) using the project Raster() function with bilinear resampling67.

Selection of predictor variables

Accurate and ecologically meaningful SDMs critically depend on the appropriate selection of environmental predictor variables. Including highly correlated variables can induce spatial autocorrelation, resulting in model overfitting and reduced reliability70. To ensure model robustness, selecting an optimal variable subset is essential71,72. In this study, we compiled an initial set of 24 environmental variables to model habitat suitability for current bird species in Ethiopia. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients to minimize redundancy and improve ecological interpretability. Pairwise correlations were calculated within the R statistical environment using the ‘Corr plot’ package73,74. Absence of data development

A total of 188 presence data records of C. xantholaema were used to develop the species distribution model. In the absence of established absence data, pseudo-absence points were generated using the random Points() function from the disco R package52. The pseudo-absences were constrained within Ethiopia’s terrestrial ecoregions, following78, to establish ecological significance while avoiding unrealistic environmental conditions. Following the best practices in species distribution modeling, the number of pseudo-absence points was set to twice the number of presence records (376 pseudo-absences). This 1:2 ratio is commonly used to increase the discriminatory power of the model and enhance its ability to differentiate between suitable and unsuitable habitats, particularly when true absence data are inaccessible79–81. Random selection of pseudo-absences was spatially restricted within the known or potential range of the species and a minimum inter-point distance of 1 km was maintained to ensure spatial independence82,83. Moreover, to reflect the ecological niche of the species, pseudo-absence points were stratified across the altitude range of 500–4500 m, aligning with the elevation distribution documented for C. xantholaema48. The resulting dataset, comprising both presence and pseudo-absence records, was subjected to stratified random partitioning using the createDataPartition () function from the caret package84, allocating 70% of the data for model training and 30% for validation, while maintaining class balance85.

Model evaluation and accuracy

Various evaluation metrics are used when evaluating the performance of a model, including threshold-dependent and threshold-independent indicators (Table 2). A confusion matrix is a basic assessment that categorizes predictions into true positives (TP), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN), and true negatives (TN)86. Several performance parameters were derived from the confusion matrix. For the threshold-independent analysis, the area under the curve (AUC) of the ROC curve was calculated using the package R ‘pROC’87. AUC values range from 0 to 1, with values greater than 0.9 indicating exceptional performance, 0.7–0.9 indicating moderate performance, and 0.5 to 0.7 indicating suboptimal performance, while 0.5 corresponds to random prediction88,89. Implementation and evaluation were performed using the caret package84 in R version 4.2.267.

Machine learning models

Four modeling approaches, namely, maximum entropy, random forest, SVM, and extreme gradient boost, were used to evaluate the distribution of C. xantholaema based on climatic factors, considering that different SDMs have distinct predictive capabilities90,91. These models were selected on the basis of their demonstrated effectiveness in ecological modeling, as validated in previous studies15,16,92.

Maximum entropy (MaxEnt)

MaxEnt is an SDM that determines habitat suitability and predicts species occurrence93–95. The maximum entropy principle, which seeks the most uniform probability distribution for species present under environmental constraints, is used. This study demonstrates particular effectiveness with presence-only data, addressing a common limitation in ecological research96,97.

Here, S denotes entropy, P is the prior distribution under known conditions, and Pi is the probability of i.

Model development followed standardized parameterization protocols that commonly used algorithms. The MaxEnt model incorporated linear, quadratic, hinge, product, and threshold (LQHPT) feature classes with a retained multiplier set to 1.033. The model was optimized through ten-fold cross-validation to balance model complexity and predictive performance while minimizing overfitting, following best practices in ecological modeling99.

Random forest (RF)

The RF model, a nonparametric ensemble method, constructs a “forest” of decision trees for predictions and is widely used in ecological studies for regression and classification100,101. Its robustness stems from aggregating individual tree predictions, mitigating overfitting, and enhancing consistency, making it suitable for variable and uncertain ecological data. RF assesses predictor importance through Gini impurity and accuracy loss metrics, providing insights into variable contributions102. The SDM package in R was used for spatial data analysis in ecology103. Model development followed standardized parameterization protocols that commonly used algorithms. The RF model was configured with 500 decision trees, try = 4, and a minimum node size of 5102. The model was optimized through ten-fold cross-validation to balance model complexity and predictive performance while minimizing overfitting, following ecological modeling bestpractices99.

Support vector machine (SVM)

The SVM is a supervised learning method that demonstrates effectiveness in classification and regression analysis104. Originally developed for binary classification, SVM addresses ecological applications by distinguishing species’ presence and absence across environmental gradients. Model development followed standardized parameterization protocols that commonly used algorithms. Its strength lies in creating an optimal hyperplane for class separation, enhancing prediction, and generalization. SVMs were implemented using a RBF kernel with cost = 1 and gamma = 0.5105. The model was optimized through ten-fold cross-validation to balance model complexity and predictive performance while minimizing overfitting, following best practices in ecological modeling99. SVMs manage multicollinearity among predictors and minimize overfitting, which is beneficial in the ecology of correlated variables. This study used SVM via the SDM package in R for species distribution analysis.

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)

XGBoost is an advanced ensemble-supervised learning algorithm that enhances traditional gradient boosting with regularization and optimization techniques106. XGBoost iteratively constructs trees, addressing residual errors of previous trees107,108. Model development followed standardized parameterization protocols that commonly used algorithms. The XGBoost algorithm was applied using a learning rate of 0.01, a maximum tree depth of 6, and 1,000 boosting rounds106.The model was optimized through ten-fold cross-validation to balance model complexity and predictive performance while minimizing overfitting, following best practices in ecological modeling99. The regularization terms mitigate overfitting and enhance robustness, optimizing an objective function that balances loss and complexity. The hyperparameter tuning strategies optimize the model109,110.

Ensemble model

Ensemble modeling combines predictions from multiple models to improve prediction reliability and accuracy39, leveraging their strengths to outperform single model approaches111. Using the caret ensemble package in R85. Predictions from four models were aggregated using a weighted average technique that is based on individual model evaluation metrics. BIOMOD2 and caret packages in R facilitated species distribution modeling85,112.

Habitat suitability and change analysis

Habitat suitability analysis produced continuous probability maps from 0 to 1. This scale is classified into unsuitable (< 0.25), low (0.25–0.50), moderate (0.50–0.75), and high (> 0.75) classes according to established guidelines113–117. ArcGIS 10.8 was used to generate suitability layers, with areas quantified using pixel-based analysis. To assess habitat changes for C. xantholaema, these continuous maps were further converted into binary presence-absence outputs using the MaxSSS (maximum training sensitivity plus specificity) threshold, which effectively balances omission and commission errors to enhance model accuracy118–120. Habitat loss was identified as areas currently suitable becoming unsuitable under future climate scenarios (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 for 2050 and 2070), while habitat gain referred to new areas becoming suitable. These dynamics were analyzed alongside trends in mean suitability values, highlighting that an overall decline in habitat suitability is a key indicator of extinction risk, particularly for range-restricted and vulnerable bird species10,13,16.

Results

Multicollinearity assessment

The multicollinearity assessment removed nine highly correlated environmental variables, leaving a final set of 15 environmental predictors, including nine bioclimatic variables (Bio1, Bio3, Bio4, Bio7, Bio12, Bio14, Bio15, Bio18, and Bio19), three topographic features (elevation, slope, and aspect), NDVI, and LULC (Fig. 2). This reduced set minimizes redundancy and ensures that each predictor provides SDMs with only relevant information. The consistent use of these predictors across all model ensembles improves the reliability of habitat suitability predictions and allows for better interpretation of critical ecological drivers.

Model performance and validation

The predictive performance of the four ML models was assessed using multiple metrics, including accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, kappa, the F1 score (Table 3), and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (Fig. 3). All models demonstrated strong predictive performance across the evaluated metrics, though their strengths varied. SVM and RF achieved the highest sensitivity values (0.97), indicating their ability to properly classify presence records. In contrast, XGBoost had the highest specificity (0.98), reflecting its effectiveness in finding acceptable absence points and minimizing false-positive predictions. Precision values were consistently high across models, with SVM (0.95) and MaxEnt (0.94) performing best, indicating strong prediction reliability. The F1 score, which balances sensitivity and precision, was highest for SVM (0.96), followed by XGBoost and RF (0.95), while MaxEnt performed comparatively lower (0.87) because of its weaker sensitivity.

XGBoost outperformed the other models in terms of overall classification accuracy (0.96), followed by SVM (0.95) and RF (0.94), with MaxEnt having a lower accuracy (0.86). The kappa statistics supported these outcomes, with XGBoost (0.92) and SVM (0.90) displaying the strongest agreement beyond chance. The AUC-ROC results further confirmed the high discriminatory performance of all models, with XGBoost achieving the highest AUC (0.99), followed by RF (0.98), SVM (0.97), and MaxEnt (0.92). Although AUC values above 0.90 across all ML models indicate excellent classification and prediction ability, considering sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score offers a more balanced assessment of model performance. Generally, these results show that all four ML models can produce reliable predictions for the distribution of C. xantholaema, with XGBoost excelling in specificity and overall accuracy, SVM performing particularly well in sensitivity and balanced prediction (F1 score), and RF providing consistent results across metrics. MaxEnt, although achieving strong precision, showed relatively lower sensitivity and specificity, reducing its overall performance compared to the ML approaches. These findings emphasize the importance of using multiple evaluation metrics in model validation to confirm robust and ecologically meaningful predictions rather than relying solely on AUC.

Relative influence of environmental predictors

The current study revealed that bioclimatic variables, especially precipitation in the driest month (Bio14), had a significant impact, with values ranging from 32.5% in XGBoost to 100% in both the SVM and RF models (Fig. 4). Similarly, annual precipitation (Bio12) and precipitation in the coldest quarter (Bio19) had significant impacts, with the RF model having a significance of 58% and 57%, respectively, followed by XGBoost (15.5% and 20%), SVM (100% and 45.6%), and MaxEnt (10.3% and 2.9%), respectively. Unfortunately, temperature-related factors had various degrees of importance in the models (Fig. 4). For example, Bio1 was highly significant in the SVM (62.03%) and RF (49.1%) models; however, it was less significant in the MaxEnt (11%) and XGBoost (8.3%) models. Temperature seasonality (Bio4) was significantly important in the RF model (36.9%), although only slightly important compared with XGBoost (8.2%) and MaxEnt (0.6%).

Similarly, topographic factors, such as slope and elevation, showed varying levels of importance between models (Fig. 4). The slope was relatively low in the MaxEnt (0.8%), SVM (4.93%), and XGBoost (1.4%) models; however, it was significantly important in the RF model (10.1%). Elevation was more important in the SVM (69.47%) and RF (26.5%) models than in the MaxEnt (2.4%) and XGBoost (0.4%) models (Fig. 4). The NDVI indicated moderate to low values in most models, with the RF (31.2%) and SVM (33.27%) models indicating that they are more important than XGBoost (3.2%) and MaxEnt (0.1%) (Fig. 4). The LULC was significant in the SVM (44.89%) and RF (31.5%) models; however, it was very low in the XGBoost (1.7%) and MaxEnt (16.2%) models. In general, the variables had the greatest impact on all ML models, with temperature-related parameters, vegetation indices, land cover, and topographic factors contributing differently. Thus, ensemble modeling methods can provide more comprehensive knowledge of the important elements that influence a species’ modeled distribution and habitat suitability.

Current distribution and habitat suitability of C. xantholaema

A comparison of the machine learning models used to determine habitat suitability for C. xantholaema revealed considerable differences in the expected area coverage between suitability classes (Table 4; Fig. 5, and Supplementary Table S1). According to the MaxEnt model (Fig. 5A), 76.5% of Ethiopian land is considered unsuitable for the species, with 12.8% classified as low suitability, 7.2% classified as moderate suitability, and only a small proportion (3.5%) classified as highly suitable. The RF model (Fig. 5C) supports this trend, identifying 69.7% of the region as not suitable, while only 3.8% are classified as having high suitability. In contrast, the XGBoost model (Fig. 5D) predicted 86.7% of the areas as not suitable, with lower percentages for low (5%), moderate (4.1%), and high (4.2%) suitability, suggesting that the XGBoost model is more conservative in estimating suitable habitats for C. xantholaema. The SVM model (Fig. 5C) indicates that unsuitable areas comprise 68.2%, while low-, moderate-, and high-suitability areas constitute 15.5%, 12.4%, and 3.9%, respectively. Ultimately, the findings reveal that the area is primarily classified as unsuitable for the species, with varying degrees of low, moderate, and high suitability among the models. The RF and XGBoost models identify smaller regions in the north, although suitable habitat areas are largely found in the southern and southwestern parts of the research area. This variance emphasizes the need for model selection and calibration in ecological forecasting and the importance of carefully interpreting habitat suitability data to help determine conservation and resource management strategies for this species.

Future distribution and habitat suitability of C. xantholaema

Ensemble projections indicate that 75.3% of Ethiopia is currently unsuitable for C. xantholaema, with significant differences in habitat suitability under SSP245 and SSP585 scenarios projected for 2050 and 2070 (Table 4; Fig. 6), indicating an overall decline in suitable habitat. Under the SSP245 scenario, approximately 61.8% of the habitat is projected to be unsuitable by 2050, whereas 57.1% of the habitat is predicted to be unsuitable under SSP585. The SSP585 scenario predicted a higher proportion of marginal habitats (areas of low suitability) (27.48%) than SSP245 (22.34%), suggesting that SSP585 incorporates slightly more potential habitat areas. Moderate suitability is generally stable at approximately 15%, whereas high suitability is negligible, accounting for only 1.35% and 0.10% in SSP245 and SSP585, respectively, by 2050, indicating the limited availability of ideal conditions. By 2070, the estimated extent of unsuitable habitat will increase further to 65.24% under the SSP245 scenario (Fig. 6A) and 60.17% under the SSP585 scenario (Fig. 6B) by 2070. The low suitability areas of SSP245 are expected to fall somewhat to 21.34%, whereas those of SSP585 remain largely stable at approximately 24%. Furthermore, by 2070, moderate suitability will decrease to 12.39% in SSP245 (Fig. 6C) and 14.84% in SSP585 (Fig. 6D), indicating a decrease in the number of places with intermediate viability. High suitability is unusual in both cases and is seldom highest at 1% during this period. As unsuitable regions expand, C. xantholaema faces significant habitat limits, and the future of suitable habitats remains uncertain.

Discussion

Climate change poses a significant challenge to species distribution and conservation in the 21st century, particularly for endemic and endangered species121,122. Ensemble machine learning modeling has emerged as the main technique for predicting distribution changes and advancing conservation strategies for species under climate change scenarios40,51. Therefore, precise distribution maps of globally endangered species are critical for successful management and can be generated through ML approaches123. Machine learning algorithms are important in ensemble modeling because of their flexibility and accuracy, and they often require less time than traditional approaches124. The present study investigated the application of four machine learning models, MaxEnt, RF, XGBoost, and SVM, to generate distribution and habitat suitability maps under current and future climate scenarios to determine suitable conservation strategies for the near-threatened C. xantholaema bird species in Ethiopia.

Model performance and environmental drivers of the distribution of C. xantholaema

The AUC is essential for evaluating the accuracy of predictive models in ecological studies125. In this study, XGBoost showed the highest performance with the AUC (0.99), accuracy (0.96), and kappa (0.92), attributable to its gradient boosting architecture that effectively models complex ecological relationships while minimizing overfitting through regularization126. SVM showed strong precision (0.95) and F1 score (0.96), benefiting from its robust handling of high-dimensional data, while RF’s slightly lower performance (accuracy: 0.94) may show sensitivity to dataset characteristics like class imbalance or correlated predictors127. Notably, both SVM and RF achieved the highest sensitivity (0.97), indicating excellent true-positive detection, whereas MaxEnt trailed in sensitivity (0.80), likely due to its presence-only modeling approach. The variations in model performance indicate that algorithm-specific strengths interact with dataset properties, with XGBoost evolving as the most balanced performer across metrics. These findings align with broader ecological modeling literature23,128–131, while focusing on the importance of selecting algorithms based on specific study objectives and data characteristics. All models exhibited strong predictive capability125, supporting their utility in species distribution modeling. However, practitioners should consider XGBoost for optimal overall performance, SVM for precision-focused applications, and RF’s trade-offs between accuracy and sensitivity in ecological locations23,132,133.

Understanding the influence of environmental factors on the distribution of endemic species is crucial for effective conservation planning134,135. Precipitation during the driest month (Bio14) was the primary determinant of suitable habitat for C. xantholaema, consistent with findings for other Ethiopian endemic bird species, such as Rouget’s Rail (Rougetius rougetii)136 and White-cheeked Turaco (Tauraco leucotis)15. This strong dependence on precipitation indicates its critical role in maintaining plant diversity and food resources137. Although responses vary significantly among species, some birds show altitudinal migration in response to drought138. Other endemic bird species in Ethiopia, such as spot-breasted lapwings (Vanellus melanocephalus), remain unaffected by precipitation variables16. The differences between species may arise from variations in ecological niches and local adaptations across Ethiopia’s diverse agroecosystems.

Temperature variables, particularly the annual mean temperature (Bio1), emerged as the second-most important predictor group, especially in the SVM and RF models. Highland species have been shown to be sensitive to thermal regimes, with colder temperatures in dry quarters relating to higher habitat suitability139,140. Similar temperature dependencies have been documented for R. rougeti136 and T. leucotis15, suggesting widespread thermal constraints among Ethiopia’s montane avifauna. Vegetation structure (NDVI) and land use patterns moderately influenced habitat predictions, especially in RF and SVM models, indicating food availability and shelter quality141,142. C. xantholaema prefers moist evergreen montane forests and Acacia-Commiphora woodlands45, demonstrating how changes in land cover can directly affect habitat suitability.

Topographic factors, such as elevation, significantly influenced model performance, accounting for 69.47% of the variation in SVM models, compared to 26.5% in RF models. This disparity arises from the elevation’s character in limiting species distributions and shaping microclimates143,144 and differences in how these models handle correlated predictors134,145. The topographic complexity of Ethiopia’s landscapes further alters local bioclimatic conditions, making microrefugia that influence the distribution of various species146. These findings suggest that environmental differences affect species-environment interactions among endemic birds in Ethiopia. The variation in the importance of variables across different modeling approaches highlights the necessity for multi-model frameworks to guide conservation efforts in complex landscapes. Generally, ML is a promising tool for species distribution modeling due to its nonparametric approaches and sparsity to overcome difficulties arising from high dimensions of environmental data and sparse data on occurrence, particularly in rare, threatened, or endangered species.

Current and future distributions of C. xantholaema and habitat suitability

Endangered endemic bird species with narrow ecological niches, such as C. xantholaema, face elevated extinction risks due to climate change and human-induced habitat alteration147,148. Species survival depends on their ability to adapt, migrate to suitable habitats, or face extinction95,149. Based on ensemble projections integrating four machine learning models (RF, MaxEnt, SVM, and XGBoost), currently, only 3.9% (43,698.4 km2) of Ethiopian land is highly suitable for C. xantholaema, while 75.3% (853,674.5 km2) is unsuitable. This indicates a severe spatial divergence with the species’ known occurrence range (154,000 km2) reported by10. This variation arises from methodological limitations in topological range mapping150, whereas SDMs suggest further ecologically relevant predictions151,152. Despite SDMs identifying reduced suitable areas, they provide a more accurate conservation framework153,154. However, many of these suitable habitats are outside protected zones and vulnerable to anthropogenic threats155.

Ensemble-based projections under future climate scenarios (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5) indicate a radical decline in highly suitable habitats by 2050 and 2070, with losses of 80.8% and 73.2%, respectively. This will result in significant ecological pressure on the feeding and breeding behaviors of the species and supports documented shifts in habitat suitability for other endemic Ethiopian bird species such as T. ruspolii, R. rougeti, and V. melanocephalus under similar scenarios45,156–158. Although the study showed a 20.8% and 16.4% decrease in unsuitable areas by 2050 and 2070, respectively, this trend is not associated with enhanced suitability, as the overall reduction in highly suitable zones overshadows any gains159,160. These habitat reductions, driven by climatic uncertainty, reduce ecological connectivity and increase the risk of local extinctions28,35,161.

Projected shifts in climate niches suggest potential migrations to more favorable environments; however, such range shifts may not be possible for range-restricted endemic species like C. xantholaema148,162. Species response is further complicated by physiological, behavioral, and ecological factors, including land-use change and interspecies interactions24,163. Notably, low-elevation species, such as C. xantholaema, are extremely affected, with rapid climatic shifts worsening range loss and extinction risk116,164–166. Despite its current IUCN status as Near Threatened, the study demonstrates that C. xantholaema faces increased risk due to projected habitat loss13,167,168.

Implications and strategies for conservation

The projected decline in suitable habitats for C. xantholaema under future climate scenarios necessitates urgent and targeted conservation actions. With highly suitable areas expected to decrease by over 70% by 2070, immediate efforts should prioritize the protection and restoration of critical habitats, particularly in Ethiopia’s southern and southwestern parts. Given the species’ sensitivity to precipitation and elevation, conserving microclimatic refugia, such as moist montane forests, is essential for mitigating the effects of climate change. Establishing ecological corridors between fragmented habitats will facilitate potential range shifts and maintain genetic connectivity. In addition, the creation of buffer zones and the maintenance of natural corridors are vital for enhancing habitat resilience. Community-based conservation, especially in areas beyond formal protection, should be highlighted. This includes promoting sustainable land-use practices, bird-friendly agriculture, environmental education, and ecotourism to promote local stewardship. Reducing pesticide use further supports biodiversity and ecosystem health. In newly suitable but currently unoccupied regions, assisted colonization may be considered following rigorous ecological risk assessments. Continuing monitoring and improved ecological modeling using locally sourced data are crucial for refining conservation strategies. Interdisciplinary collaboration is essential in addressing climate-related threats. Ultimately, an integrated approach that combines climate adaptation, habitat protection, and active community participation will be critical for ensuring the long-term survival of bird species15–17,150.

Limitations and prospects

This study provides an approach to projecting the regional distributions of bird species through current climate variables. However, limitations and uncertainties must be considered. From the beginning, the research relied solely on GBIF data, and the use of survey data can improve the accuracy and reliability of predictions. Furthermore, the absence of soil data is notable because soil conditions have a substantial impact on the distribution of plant species and, consequently, the suitability of bird habitats. Other critical factors, including anthropogenic activities, biological interactions (such as those with parasites, predators, and competitors), dispersal distances, and ecological limitations, can also influence species distribution patterns and are not fully included in this study169–171.

Future research should explore the relevance of different ML models, such as deep learning approaches, to further expand the scope of species distributions172,173. Furthermore, including physiological and behavioral flexibility in macroecological frameworks is critical for understanding the effects of climate change on certain populations174,175. Furthermore, investigating the role of microhabitats and microrefugia176 in population survival is crucial, particularly in regions smaller than the 1 km pixels used in this analysis.

Conclusion

The increasing extinction risk faced by several endemic bird species indicates the critical need for science-based conservation strategies that utilize species-occurrence data to protect habitats. This study reveals that ML algorithms, particularly MaxEnt, XGBoost, SVM, and RF, thoroughly evaluated through performance metrics, significantly outperform traditional methods in habitat suitability modeling. Applied to the endangered C. xantholaema, the findings reveal severe habitat degradation: only 3.9% of the current range is highly suitable, projected to collapse to 0.1% by 2070 under combined SSP245 and SSP585 climate scenarios. While the proportion of unsuitable areas may decrease to 61.8% (2050) and 57.1% (2070), this trend is offset by a concerning expansion of low-suitability zones (11.5% to 22.3 and 27.5%), indicating habitat fragmentation and suitability declines. Precipitation seasonality, temperature extremes, and topographic heterogeneity emerged as the primary drivers of habitat suitability for the species. Despite potential adaptive capacity through elevational shifts, climate change, synergized with anthropogenic pressures, threatens disastrous range reduction. The current existence of marginally suitable habitats outside protected areas, combined with these increasing threats, requires the urgent implementation of targeted conservation strategies. Future research should incorporate localized environmental factors, such as land-use intensity and microclimate data, to enhance predictive models and generate effective mitigation measures for this endemic bird species.
