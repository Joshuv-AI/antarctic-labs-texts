Quantification of global gray matter amyloid‐β plaque from 18F‐Florbetaben PET/MRI and correlation to BAPL score in patients with cognitive impairment
Authors: Adam Farag, Seyed Ali Mirshahvalad, Claudia Ortega, Carmela Tartaglia, Patrick Veit‐Haibach, Ur Metser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONMultiple standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) and Centiloid (CL) thresholds exist for amyloid‑β (Aβ) positron emission tomography (PET) to classify scans as negative or positive, yet none reliably stratify brain amyloid plaque load (BAPL). This study proposes a global gray matter–based SUVR/CL model to determine amyloid status and quantitatively differentiate BAPL scores.METHODSTwo 18F‐Florbetaben (FBB) PET datasets were analyzed to derive SUVRs and CL values from global gray and white matter. SUVR/CL were integrated with visual assessment and BAPL scoring, resulting in CL thresholds to distinguish amyloid status and cognitive stage. The CL thresholds were validated in an independent local cohort.RESULTSGlobal gray‐to‐white matter CL best stratified amyloid status and BAPL scores, yielding CL thresholds of < 21 and > 38 and high diagnostic accuracy.DISCUSSIONThe integrated global CL–BAPL model reliably stratified amyloid positivity and BAPL level, suggesting that it could serve as a complementary tool to visual assessment.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, brain β‐amyloid plaque load, Centiloid, 18F‐Florbetaben, PET/MRI

INTRODUCTION

The population of people with dementia reported by Alzheimer's disease (AD) International is growing and could reach as high as 139 million in North America by 2050. 1 This growing burden underscores the need for fast, accurate, and efficient assessment methods to facilitate initial screening and longitudinal follow‐up of affected patients.

Historically, AD diagnosis relied exclusively on clinical symptomatology, with definitive confirmation only possible through post‐mortem examination. Advances in understanding the disease pathophysiology, particularly the central role of amyloid‐β (Aβ) plaque deposition, have led to the development of novel diagnostic tools. AD is characterized by a prolonged preclinical phase during which pathological changes progress silently until clinical symptoms emerge. 2 This prolonged pre‐clinical stage allows for early and potentially impactful therapeutic intervention. Hence, reliable diagnostic tools allow for early identification of AD patients or at‐risk patients for AD, who could benefit from therapy. Furthermore, the prolonged mild cognitive impairment (MCI) stage is where many new drugs are being tested. 3 Thus, reliable diagnostic methods are important for drug development and therapy monitoring. 4

One of the most promising diagnostics of AD is Aβ–positron emission tomography (PET) imaging which uses one of the clinically approved radiotracers and allows trained readers to identify the accumulation of Aβ plaques in the cerebral cortex. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Aβ plaques has been shown to correlate with presence of neuritic Aβ plaques at post‐mortem histopathology. 5 The current clinical standard for interpreting Aβ PET is visual assessment (VA). VA uses regional cortical tracer uptake (RCTU) across four cortical regions for AD interpretation, where uptake is classified into three stages: no uptake, moderate uptake, and pronounced uptake. The highest RCTU is then used in the brain amyloid plaque load (BAPL) scoring system to provide an overall summary of Aβ plaque load. In this context, BAPL offers a quick, visual, semiquantitative classification of amyloid burden that is practical for routine clinical decision‐making, such as determining eligibility for anti‐amyloid therapy. Nevertheless, BAPL is prone to inter‐reader variability, and additional quantitative methods can complement it for more precise and reproducible assessment. Despite excellent inter‐reader agreement between readers and across radiotracers, 9 qualitative assessment remains susceptible to human error and is limited in objectively quantifying changes in Aβ plaque load over time. For this reason, reproducible quantitative PET measures are needed to complement visual interpretation. Current quantitative measures include standardized uptake value ratios (SUVR) 6 , 10 between the cortex and reference regions with stable radiotracer uptake (e.g., cerebellum or pons), Centiloids (CL) for harmonizing PET interpretation across different tracers 8 , z‐scores comparing patients to the normal population, 11 Aβ load, 12 Aβ index, 13 and AMYQ. 14 For these quantitative measures to effectively enhance diagnostic confidence, they must be integrated into routine clinical practice alongside methods such as VA and RCTU/BAPL. While the z‐score has been correlated and integrated with RCTU, 15 this has not been done for SUVR and CL. Furthermore, all these quantitative measures typically use four cortical gray matter regions (frontal, temporal, parietal, and cingulate cortex) as targets, and the whole cerebellum (WC) or a composite of WC + pons + brainstem as reference regions. Although these regions are widely used, their sensitivity to early‐stage disease and anatomical specificity is limited. 16 , 17 Furthermore, studies have shown that white matter lesions critically contribute to biomarker interpretation, and including the white matter in the assessment would improve the accuracy of AD diagnosis. 18 We hypothesize that utilizing whole gray and white matters amyloid plaque load using fused PET/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is feasible and would correlate well with standard methods of VA. The aims of the current study were to: (1) evaluate 18F‐Florbetaben (FBB) SUVR and CL obtained from global gray matter (GGM) to anchors (reference regions), as a biomarker for Aβ deposition in the cortex; (2) integrate the SUVR/CL with the RCTU by means of modeling the correlation of SUVR/ CL to visual assessment of PET to the BAPL scoring system and to a dichotomous scale (positive vs negative scan).

This institutional research ethics board‐approved study included publicly available datasets from the Global Alzheimer's Association Interactive Network (GAINN) and a cohort of 133 patients referred for a FBB PET/MRI due to cognitive impairment and clinical suspicion of AD. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Subject recruitment

Two sources of patient data were used in this work. The first source is the GAAIN obtained from the GAAIN open source at https://www.gaain.org/centiloid‐project. This dataset includes FBB PET DICOM images acquired 90–110 minutes after radiotracer injection, along with corresponding T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid acquisition gradient‐echo (T1‑MPRAGE) MRI scans. A total of 35 subjects were included, comprising 25 elderly participants and 10 young controls younger than 45 years (mean age 33 ± 8 years).

The second dataset was acquired locally between March 2024 and April 2025 and consisted of patients older than 18 years who were referred for FBB PET imaging by physicians from a memory clinic due to MCI or dementia. A total of 133 patients were retrospectively identified through the institutional Radiology Information System. PET‐MRI acquisition

All local patients underwent simultaneous PET/MRI imaging on an mMR‐Biograph, Siemens 3.0 T (Erlanger, Germany), approximately 90 minutes after injection of 300 ± 60 MBq of FBB. PET acquisition time was 20 minutes. PET images were acquired simultaneously with T1‐ MPRAGE MRI acquisitions at a resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3 voxels.

Segmentation, labeling, and image processing pipeline

All image processing, modeling, and statistical analyzes were performed using in‐house tools developed in MATLAB 24.2.0 (The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). An overview of the processing pipeline is shown in Figure 1. Deep‐learning–based volumes of interest (DL‑VOIs) were generated using a pretrained 3D U‑Net segmentation model (SynthSeg; https://github.com/BBillot/SynthSeg), developed by Billot et al. 19 and trained on synthetic data generated from a segmentation‐conditioned generative model. The resulting segmentations were labeled using the CANDI ground‐truth label volume, which defines 32 anatomical brain structures. 20

Schematic of the proposed pipeline for MRI‐based DL PET image segmentation. GGM/WM and GGM/WC were used to estimate SUVR and CL, then, recalled in the BAPL model to score the Aβ load.

Following auto segmentation and labeling, T1‐MPRAGE was parcellated into four cortical targeted regions; frontal, lateral temporal, parietal cortices, and precuneus, as previously defined. 8 The orbitofrontal cortex was defined using the Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas (AAL3), spatially normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) 21 and available at https://www.oxcns.org/aal3.html. The AAL3 was co‐registered to the T1‐MPRAGE using Affine transformation and a regular step gradient descent optimization space, 22 and affinely registered to the subject's T1‑MPRAGE. This combined approach enables evaluation of gray and white matter across the whole brain, supports analysis of all 32 anatomical classes, and facilitates selection of regions consistent with the RCTU scoring system.

PET images reconstructed using conventional ordered‐subset expectation maximization (OSEM) were co‐registered to the T1‐MPRAGE using the same Affine transformation method as above, including translation, rotation, scaling, and shear. This process generated co‐registered PETreg images with resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3. The segmented‐labeled DL‐based map, together with the transformed AAL3, were applied to PETreg images to extract the SUVs, which were volume‐normalized prior to calculation of the SUVRDL‐VOI. SUVRs were computed for two purposes: 1‐ Validating the DL‐VOIs, SUVRs were calculated using the four cortical regions, as targets and the WC, brainstem (B), and cerebellum gray matter (CG) for references. 7 , 8 , 23 2‐ For model development, using the gray‐to‐white matter SUVR, global gray matter as target region and the global WM (GGM/WM), WC (GGM/WC), brainstem (GGM/B), and CG(GGM/CG) as reference regions. Additionally, GM/WM SUVRs for the frontal and lateral temporal cortices were included in the modeling.

Visual Assessment

All FBB PET images from the GAAIN and local cohorts (35 GAAIN and 133 local subjects; total n = 168) were visually assessed independently by two experienced readers (U.M. and S.A.M.; > 10 years’ experience) using dedicated clinical software (Hermes Medical Solutions, Stockholm, Sweden), following standard institutional workflow. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus and, when needed, adjudicated by a third reader (P.V.H.). Visual interpretation followed the RCTU system, and BAPL scores were independently assigned by all three readers. For cases with discordant BAPL scores, a final score was determined by consensus. VA scores were categorized as negative (BAPL = 1), moderate (BAPL = 2), or significant (BAPL = 3). Additionally, scans were classified as Aβ negative (BAPL = 1) or Aβ positive (BAPL = 2 or 3). All GAAIN cases and 55 randomly selected local cases were used for model development, while the remaining 78 local cases were withheld to enable blinded validation of the SUVR–BAPL model.

Validation of DL‐VOIs and GAAIN VOIs

GAAIN 90‐ to 110‐minute FBB PET and corresponding MRI DICOM images from 25 elderly subjects and 10 young controls were processed using the proposed pipeline. SUVRs derived using deep‐learning–based VOIs (SUVRGAAIN‐DL‐VOI) were compared with published GAAIN VOI‐based SUVRs (SUVRGAAIN‐VOI). 23 To validate the DL‐VOIs suitability, SUVRGAAIN‐DL‐VOI was correlated to SUVRGAAIN‐VOI, for WC+B and CG.

Statistical analysis

Accuracy, sensitivity and specificity were evaluated using normal approximation. Correlations were calculated using Spearman correlation coefficients with 95% CIs. The statistical significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.

As for GGM/WM, the model produced precise cutoffs SUVR = 0.69 for BAPL1 vs 2 and SUVR = 0.75 for BAPL2 vs 3. The cutoffs for BAPL1 vs 2, were confirmed by the ROC analysis with 0.87 sensitivity, 0.95 specificity, and for the BAPL2 vs 3 cutoff, the ROC was 0.82 sensitivity, 0.95 specificity, while for BAPL1 vs 3 the ROC was 0.98 sensitivity, 0.99 specificity and accuracy of 97.5%.

SMOTE analysis yielded a balanced dataset with a total of n = 81 cases, including n = 27 cases for each BAPL score. Additional modeling using the SMOTE‐based synthetic balanced dataset resulted in no change in the cutoff estimated from the local cohort dataset (n = 55).

DISCUSSION

In this work, we used a previously developed deep‐learning segmentation model with 32 labeled brain regions and anatomical structures to build an image‐processing pipeline for extracting SUVRs from integrated FBB PET/MRI images. MRI‐based segmentation enabled accurate global gray matter delineation, allowing extraction of whole‐brain gray matter FBB uptake. The pipeline was validated by comparing SUVRs with established GAAIN values. Site‐specific SUVR‐to‐CL conversion was derived using local subjects. While SUVR cutoffs could not distinguish visual BAPL scores, modeled BAPL produced clear SUVR thresholds for GGM/WM and GGM/WC.

Lecanemab is an United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) ‐approved monoclonal antibody targeting amyloid‐β protofibrils and has demonstrated efficacy in reducing amyloid plaque burden on PET imaging while slowing cognitive decline in patients with early AD. 25 As with other targeted therapies, amyloid PET is used to select patients for therapy and has been used in multiple trials as a secondary endpoint to show decrease in Aβ plaque burden. Consequently, reliable and reproducible quantification of amyloid plaque load is essential for objective assessment of longitudinal changes during therapy.

While visual interpretation remains the clinical gold standard for amyloid PET assessment, quantitative measures provide valuable adjunctive information, particularly in visually equivocal cases and for tracking temporal changes in amyloid deposition. Prior studies have reported concordance rates exceeding 90% between visual and quantitative assessments. 26 , 27 Discordance may arise for several reasons. First, focal amyloid positivity detected visually may not exceed thresholds based on global quantitative measures. Second, abnormalities in target regions (e.g., large cerebral infarcts) or reference regions (e.g., cerebellar infarcts) can lead to under‐ or overestimation of amyloid burden. The use of patient‐specific MRI‐based GM segmentation, rather than atlas‐based regions of interest employed by some commercial software, may mitigate these effects. Additionally, combining global quantitative metrics such as CL with regional assessments like modeled BAPL scores may provide a more comprehensive evaluation.

Unlike the four‐cortical‐regions method currently used, which forces image readers to make assumptions about where disease or developmental effects occur, the global metrics (i.e., GM and WM) approach does not limit the analysis to only four cortical regions. It also reduces reader bias, which can be especially valuable for diffuse conditions (e.g., normal aging and AD), where atrophy is widespread rather than localized. Furthermore, global metrics minimize false positives caused by subjective ROI selection, as seen in the four‐cortical‐regions method.

A notable effort to quantitatively model AD progression was proposed by Whittington et al., 28 who introduced a logistic growth model describing amyloid accumulation over time, using longitudinal SUVR data and a maximum amyloid capacity constraint. In their approach, SUVRs were calculated using CG as the reference region, and no relationship between BAPL scores and CL was reported. 12 Furthermore, Chong et al., 29 proposed a modified RCTU metric based on the BAPL scoring system to quantify AD progression and demonstrated its ability to depict asymmetric amyloid deposition that could potentially predict conversion from MCI to AD. However, an optimal cutoff distinguishing MCI from AD was not established.

Current image‐processing pipelines for amyloid quantification and CL standardization often require manual selection of target and reference volumes of interest and rely on multi‐step workflows involving software such as FreeSurfer 30 pipeline, statistical parametric mapping (SPM), or PMOD. 1 , 23 , 31 , 32 FreeSurfer in particular, has been reported to occasionally mislabel ventricular regions as white matter in cases of severe ventricular enlargement. 33 The pipeline presented in this study offers a fully automated solution capable of producing standardized CL values integrated with the BAPL scoring system within approximately 5–6 minutes, without user intervention. The resulting CL values are consistent with recent reports for FBB PET using the GGM/WC reference, with a positivity cutoff of ≥21. 23 , 34 Based on local data, a CL cutoff of < 38 was found to discriminate negative from positive amyloid PET for the GGM/WM reference. Although this cutoff appears higher than values commonly reported for standard regions (CL ≈ 25–35), GGM/WM represents a novel target—reference pairing that warrants further validation. 35 The pipeline successfully processed both GAAIN and local datasets using the BAPL–SUVR–CL model, yielding accurate and consistent results, suggesting feasibility for use at other sites following limited site‐specific calibration. Although mean SUVR values were higher in AD dementia compared with MCI due to AD, no clear quantitative threshold separating these clinical groups was identified, likely reflecting diagnostic heterogeneity and imperfect clinical reference standards. MCI and dementia may arise from AD or non‐AD pathologies, underscoring the distinction between AD pathology and clinical syndrome. However, a larger dataset will be required to further address this issue.

This study has few limitations. Its cross‐sectional design involved a single visit with no follow‐up PET scan, precluding longitudinal assessment. Repeat scans of the same patients, particularly after disease‐modifying therapy, would help confirm the model's ability to capture temporal change and are planned. The automated pipeline was evaluated only with FBB PET, and performance with other tracers remains unknown. Broader validation using data from multiple scanners and centers is needed. It also remains unclear whether the derived quantitative measures better discriminate MCI due to AD from AD dementia.

In conclusion, in this work we used GGM/WM and GGM/WC as an alternative to 4‐region analysis commonly used for SUVR and present a robust, fully automated segmentation and scoring model (BAPL and CL) for FBB PET/MRI. Future studies will be performed to validate this pipeline on different scanners and evaluate its ability to track amyloid changes longitudinally.
