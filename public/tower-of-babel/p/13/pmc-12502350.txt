Understanding the issues of women’s empowerment and fertility in Bangladesh
Authors: Shanjida Chowdhury, Md. Aminul Haque
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional cultural norms in Bangladesh often restrict women’s autonomy to decide and voice their opinions regarding fertility. This study tried to understand how Bangladeshi women’s perceptions, views, and experiences regarding women’s empowerment (WE) influence their total number of children, desired number of children, birth spacing, and gender preferences.MethodsA qualitative research design was used to investigate the impact of WE on fertility in Dhaka City. The study conducted seven focus group discussions (FGDs) and twenty-nine in-depth interviews (IDIs) with 93 married women aged 15–49 years. A purposive sampling technique was used to select the study participants, and thematic analysis was conducted to identify key patterns and insights.ResultsThe results show that higher levels of Women’s empowerment were associated with a preference for smaller family sizes and delayed childbearing. Empowered women exhibit greater self-confidence and independence in expressing opinions and making decisions in both household and societal settings. Economic empowerment enhances women’s bargaining power and decision-making capabilities regarding the timing and number of children. However, the prevalent preference for sons still significantly impacts these choices.ConclusionThe study identified significant sociocultural barriers, including family pressure, gender preference, lack of critical consciousness, and misconceptions about contraceptives that hinder WE toward fertility decisions. Addressing these enduring cultural and societal barriers requires immediate and thorough interventions focused on economic, psychological, and sociocultural empowerment. Increasing autonomy in fertility decision-making is crucial for achieving more equitable reproductive health outcomes.

Introduction

Bangladesh, a South Asian country with a population of 169.8 million, has experienced significant demographic shifts in recent decades. A remarkable transformation is the substantial decline in the fertility rate. Bangladesh’s total fertility rate (TFR) decreased from 6.3 children per woman in 1975 to 2.3 in 2014, which remains at 2.3 according to the latest reports [1, 2]. The decrease in this phenomenon has been attributed to multiple factors, including socioeconomic development, family planning programs, and women’s empowerment.

Women’s empowerment refers to various aspects of their lives that enable them to make strategic decisions that shape their futures. Several researchers have defined women’s empowerment as their capacity to exercise agency, make decisions, access resources and opportunities, and possess power [3–6]. It is a “process of change by which those who have been denied the ability to make choices acquire such an ability” [7]. This definition highlights empowerment as an active and dynamic process encompassing interconnected elements, including achievement, agency, and resources [7]. The capability of individuals to translate their decisions into actions to achieve desired outcomes signifies their empowerment [6]. Sen’s capability approach also underscores the importance of providing women with opportunities and resources, focusing on the adaptability required to attain the desired outcomes [8, 9].

Empowering women is crucial for shaping their reproductive goals. It has direct and indirect impacts on achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly in the areas of health (SDG 3), gender equality (SDG 5), and economic growth (SDG 8). Women’s empowerment is essential for the overall progress of society, as educated and empowered women are more likely to seek employment, access healthcare, and effectively manage family planning. Overcoming obstacles that hinder women’s empowerment is crucial for achieving the SDGs and developing an equitable society. Therefore, creating a thoughtful and equitable society that promotes women’s education and empowerment is essential. Nationally and internationally, substantial research has been conducted on the relationship between the increased empowerment of women and lower fertility rates [10–14]. Several studies have shown a correlation between women’s education, employment level, involvement in decision-making processes, and lower fertility rates [1, 10]. In addition to quantitative studies, qualitative research provides deeper insights into women’s experiences, perceptions, and perspectives. However, few qualitative studies have been conducted on this topic. In Bangladesh, few quantitative studies have employed nationally representative surveys, such as the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Surveys (BDHS), to investigate the relationships between various indicators of women’s empowerment (including education, employment, and decision-making autonomy) and fertility outcomes [15–20]. These studies highlight the intricate interplay of cultural, societal, and economic factors influencing women’s reproductive choices and empowerment. Despite a dramatic reduction in fertility, traditional gender norms and religious beliefs continue to limit their ability to make childbearing decisions [21, 22]. While there is extensive research on women’s empowerment and fertility [10–12, 23], there remain gaps in understanding the nuanced ways in which empowerment influences fertility decisions in different contexts. The multidimensional nature of empowerment is often not fully captured, with many studies focusing on specific domains, such as household decision-making, while neglecting others [11]. The main objective of this study is to understand how women’s empowerment influences their total number of children ever born, the desired number of children, birth spacing, and gender preference. Also, the study identified the barriers and facilitators of women’s empowerment and their implications for fertility outcomes.

Conceptual model

In a society, women’s empowerment is a gradual process that unfolds over time. It encompasses various aspects such as education, employment, and personal growth, enhancing a sense of self-worth. Resources facilitate the empowerment process, but do not inherently grant power, control, or the ability to make choices. Instead, they create circumstances that enhance women’s decision-making ability and act accordingly. Resources can be classified into various types, including material resources (e.g., money), social resources, and human resources (e.g., education) [7]. The agency is the capacity to establish objectives, make autonomous choices, and take action, driven by significance, desire, and purpose, and based on women’s feelings [7]. It is driven by relevance, desire, and purpose and is based on women’s feelings of agency or internal power. This study considers that empowerment comes from two essential components: resources and agency (Fig. 1) [7, 24]. A similar approach has been used by other researchers [25]. Women’s empowerment includes achievements in successfully attaining predetermined objectives [7, 26], such as deciding on childbearing or utilizing contraceptives [7, 26]. These achievements are tangible measures of progress and success on the journey of empowerment. This study adopted Kabeer’s concept [7] due to its comprehensive approach to measuring women’s empowerment, which focused on resources, agency, and achievements. Methods

Study design

After reviewing existing studies on women’s empowerment, the researcher found that most were quantitative and based on Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data. This study employed a qualitative research design to gain a deeper understanding of women’s personal experiences and perceptions regarding their empowerment in their lives and its impact on their fertility choices. This approach also allows for capturing personal experiences and perceptions. In-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted to gather primary data. IDIs allowed individual women to share personal and detailed insights into their fertility and empowerment. FGDs permitted the exploration of group dynamics and collective experiences. The study was conducted in various urban areas to ensure diversity in terms of income, educational level, and cultural background.

Study population and sampling

Seven focus group discussions (FGDs) with 8–10 women in each group and 29 in-depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted in seven mohallas in Dhaka City for qualitative methods (Fig. 2). This study focused on urban areas, and the Mohallas were selected randomly. Interviews were recorded with the participants’ permission. FGDs and IDIs included women aged 15–49 with at least one child living with their husbands in the same home. Pregnant women were excluded from the study because their empowerment levels and experiences change throughout pregnancy. The participants were selected purposively. As a qualitative study, the study depends on saturation points during data collection. Moreover, the researcher and her team were present in the field during the data collection, and after consultation on the field situation and consensus on data saturation, they decided that 7 FGDs and 29 IDIs were sufficient for the study.

All FGDs and IDIs were transcribed and analyzed according to identified themes (e.g., perceptions of women’s empowerment, the relationship between women’s empowerment and fertility).Fig. 2Study area map [27]

Data collection

The interview questions were developed through a pilot interview conducted in an urban area. Feedback from these sessions improved the clarity of the language and vocabulary used in the questions. Before data collection, a research assistant obtains informed consent. FGDs and IDIs were carried out in a confidential environment selected by the participants. Throughout FGD sessions, one moderator, a note-taker, and the researcher were present. FGD and IDI guidelines are provided in Appendix 2.

The research was a part of a PhD study, and the assistants had a master’s in social sciences and 3–4 years of experience conducting qualitative data collection and analysis. The research team was employed for 5 months and continuously communicated through WhatsApp, email, and messaging. Initially, they met twice a week and later sat at least once a week. The researcher also had regular weekly meetings with the PhD supervisor. Data were collected between February 2022 and April 2022 in seven areas of Dhaka City. Interviews were conducted in Bengali. Each IDI/FGD session lasted for approximately 35–40 min. Interviews were recorded digitally with the participants’ consent. Some interviews were recorded by taking notes, as respondents hesitated to record digitally.

Data analysis

A thematic analysis technique was employed [28], which involved familiarizing the data, coding, developing, and refining themes to minimize bias. We organized peer debriefing sessions. The audio and written notes of the IDIs and FGDs were carefully transcribed into English. The interviews were conducted in Bangla. The researcher read the interview transcripts repeatedly and developed a codebook based on the research objectives and themes that emerged from the data. A triangulation strategy was used to ensure consistency and quality of the data analysis. The first author read the interview text sentence by sentence and identified topics related to the theme under study. The second author assisted with validating the coding framework.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the principles of research ethics, which included ensuring participant anonymity and obtaining informed consent. All participants were free to participate and discontinue at any time. This is a part of the doctoral research conducted within the Department of Population Sciences. The study involving human participants received approval from the Department of Population Sciences at the University of Dhaka. The studies were conducted following local laws and institutional protocols. During data collection, researchers were aware of their biases and ensured that their passion, learning, educational background, and position would not influence the respondents’ responses. The team established rapport with the participants before conducting the interviews. Research assistants never tried to lead any responses or correct a respondent’s reaction. Moreover, research assistants underwent training to address these issues and ensure effective data collection.

Results

The qualitative component of this study consisted of seven focus group discussions (FGDs) with 8–10 participants each and 29 in-depth interviews (IDIs) with urban women of diverse ages, incomes, and socio-economic and cultural backgrounds. Ninety-three women from seven areas in Dhaka City participated in the study.

Most participants (approximately 40.0%) were between 20 and 30 years old, and most were Muslim (see appendix 1). Their educational status varied from illiterate (23.7%) to higher secondary or higher (22.6%). Nearly 35.5% of the participants did not use any contraceptives. Among the respondents, 58.1% were not working and had one to two children (57.0%). Four sub-themes related to knowledge about women’s empowerment (KWE) were identified through thematic analysis. The four sub-themes that emerged from the study are household empowerment, economic empowerment, socio-cultural empowerment, and psychological empowerment (Table 1).

Theme 1: knowledge about women’s empowerment (KWE)

Based on the participants’ responses from FGDs and IDIs, it is evident that women’s empowerment spans various spheres. According to the eighty participants, decision-making is vital to women’s empowerment. In response to the question, “Could you please share your personal views regarding the word ‘women’s empowerment’?” We identified four sub-themes and nineteen categories regarding women’s empowerment (Table 1). When asked if they felt empowered, more than half of the women responded positively or stated that they felt empowered to some extent. They see empowerment as the ability to choose one’s own life goals, make independent decisions, express their views without any hesitation, have their voice, economic solvency, prioritize decision-making, have a sense of self-worth and self-esteem, overcome barriers, can make one’s own identity, and are free from violence and leadership capacity.

Household empowerment

Household decision making

The participants acknowledged freedom of choice and decision-making as fundamental aspects of empowerment. They also emphasized the importance of autonomy in decision-making, which involves selecting one’s life goals and path, and making choices free from external influences. Three IDI participants highlighted the empowerment of choosing their career pathway. In contrast, others were engrossed in freedom to make decisions for themselves, their families, and their children.

Equality in rights and responsibilities

Many interviewed women acknowledged the need for equality as the foundation for empowerment. They defined empowerment as the equitable treatment of males and females, free of gender-based constraints, biases, or discrimination, encompassing equal responsibilities in both personal and professional settings. When equality is incorporated into social, economic, and political dimensions, it opens up new opportunities for women to access, make informed decisions, and pursue their goals without fear of gender-based constraints. Women identified their sense of empowerment with their capacity to execute these rights and live free of the confines of traditional gender roles, which frequently limit their actions:

“Women’s empowerment- a woman will make her own decisions. In this regard, it is not a woman who has no voice in the household, even if she is a woman. She must have equal rights to her husband to express an opinion on childcare and economic issues. Economic empowerment

Economic solvency, participate in household expenses, control over resources and assets

Participating in economic decision-making in a family is another essential component of women’s empowerment, as identified by a group of women. However, women’s decisions regarding financial issues, particularly family budgeting, are denied in most family situations. In addition, women who earn money have more freedom to make decisions than those who do not. Thirty-five participants mentioned contributing their income to family expenditure as a source of pride. As described by the study participants, women’s economic solvency impacts their empowerment in decision-making issues.

Socio-cultural empowerment

Freedom of movement

Women recognized that their ability to move freely increased their likelihood of participating in activities that broadened their horizons, including education and employment. It was also highlighted that from a young age, women are restricted from leaving the house without obtaining permission from their fathers, elder brothers, or husbands. Men can go outside anytime, whereas women are often limited. More cases are only permitted to go to their children’s schools and hospitals, and there are no social ideals for women. They identified that restrictions on mobility hindered their ability to be empowered. One housewife participant in FGD stated, “I was not allowed to work or study after marriage, and my in-laws expected me to have a child as early as possible after marriage. It was difficult to argue against this because I was financially dependent on them. If I had more autonomy, I would have made different choices.”

“I need to get permission if I go far from home. Sometimes, going outside requires money. As he was the only person earning money, I couldn’t go to the market; I needed him to purchase the items when he had the money. Also, he doesn’t like to go outside because it requires spending money.” [FGD, Age: 28, Education: Higher Secondary].“If a son wants, he can work as a night guard, but if a daughter wants to work as a night guard, she can’t. Psychological empowerment

Enhancing well-being/better life

Thirty-two participants in this study reported that empowerment leads to an improved quality of life, increased fulfillment, and enhanced self-esteem, satisfaction, and happiness. Well-being is defined as a subjective experience that arises from possessing the ability to exercise choices and live a life that is true to oneself. Twelve participants emphasized the importance of collective well-being, encompassing the well-being of individuals, their families, and society. They emphasized how empowering women can benefit individuals and communities, resulting in a more harmonious and thriving culture. The survey participants generally highlighted that well-being is a vital outcome of empowerment and is closely linked to individual and societal well-being. For example, one IDI participant aged 36 mentioned, “Despite rapid societal changes, the perspective on women’s issues remained unchanged. I believe women’s empowerment means struggling to live a better life.”

Own identity

Discussion

The study explored women’s perceptions and experiences regarding empowerment regarding their fertility. In this study, we applied the conceptual framework proposed by [7] and [25] in successfully attaining predetermined objectives [7, 26], such as deciding on childbearing or utilizing contraceptives. These achievements are tangible measures of progress and success on the journey of empowerment. The narratives shared by participants are divided into different themes and subthemes. The current study extends previous research by pursuing an FGD and IDI to incorporate the perspectives of women from different sociocultural backgrounds at various stages of their reproductive lives to investigate the role of women’s empowerment on their fertility. Most importantly, it reports on single women’s views, largely absent in the empowerment literature [13, 31], especially for Bangladesh. While quantitative studies on similar issues often identify different societal and cultural factors as barriers, these qualitative findings reveal how women navigate these barriers and face challenges. Understanding these dynamics can facilitate the development of broader and more effective strategies for women’s reproductive empowerment.

Findings revealed that due to a lack of critical consciousness, women fail to identify the limitations that society, traditional, and cultural norms impose on them. Consequently, they may accept these constraints as usual, reducing their ability to make decisions that align with their desires or well-being. A study based on minority and immigrant women also identifies a lack of critical consciousness as a reason for individual failings [32]. Our study result also aligns with the study conducted in Nepal [33].

Participants reported economic independence, i.e., the ability to earn and contribute to household finances, as a critical theme closely linked to women’s decision-making power within the home. It empowers them to make independent decisions regarding their reproductive preferences. When women earn, they often invest in education or careers before having children. This economic independence also allows them to afford contraception, giving them more control over birth spacing. Some women, particularly those who are not working, emphasize that the financial condition of their paternal house plays a significant role in their decision-making process. This aligns with research emphasizing economic empowerment as a key factor in determining reproductive autonomy [11, 34–36]. Another important indicator is that women are focused on their decision-making capacity in the household. Most women mentioned that households are the place where the process of empowerment began. Outside the home, individuals may have a voice; ultimately, their house is the primary sanctuary where they find tranquility, inspiration, and a sense of belonging. Studies on women’s empowerment got similar results, emphasizing household empowerment of women [37, 38]. Household empowerment of women depends on their participation in household-related decision-making. However, household decision-making varies significantly based on household structure (whether a joint family or not), the husband’s characteristics, economic condition, and educational level. Household decision-making capacity of women associated with increased birth spacing and the total number of children, contributing to better health outcomes and improved maternal and child well-being. Previous studies support our findings [12, 39]. There is a relationship between women’s empowerment and their fertility, as empowered women possess the ability to make informed decisions regarding family planning, contraception, and the timing and desired number of children, thereby demonstrating autonomy in reproductive health. The findings of this study align with the existing literature that provides detailed insights into the specific ways empowerment shapes fertility and reproductive choices [10–12, 14, 40–42]. A cross-sectional study in Mozambique emphasized the importance of implementing a domain-focused approach to estimate and comprehend empowerment accurately [43]. Equal rights were another important theme; participants highlighted the importance of legal recognition, property ownership, and access to education and employment. Furthermore, they also emphasized the importance of their psychological well-being and a violence-free life as a means of empowerment.

Despite the positive impacts of women’s empowerment, participants described social and cultural pressures, including pressure from partners and family members (in-laws), on women’s reproductive choices. Women who lack critical consciousness may feel pressured by cultural or familial norms regarding their childbearing decisions, including family sizes and birth spacing, without fully considering their autonomous choices. Thirty-seven reported concerns regarding the preference for a son and the expectation to bear children soon after marriage. They also noted that families often feel pressured to have sons because sons are typically perceived as the bearers of the family name and sources of financial strength. Recent studies conducted in Bangladesh have revealed comparable findings, as despite a stated decrease in son preference, actual fertility choices continue to reflect a bias favoring sons [44–46]. This study shows that empowered women also prefer sons, but they tend to prioritize gender balance. Our results on women’s empowerment and reproductive behavior can strengthen research conducted in Bangladesh, demonstrating similar constraints on women’s empowerment regarding pregnancy and family planning decisions. Participants noted that empowerment enabled them to make informed decisions about their reproductive health, improving health outcomes and overall satisfaction. This finding aligns with the broader literature linking women’s empowerment to improved health behaviors and outcomes, having met their fertility goal and having had a child according to their desired timing without external pressure [13, 42, 43, 47–49]. Respondents mentioned that when women are empowered to drive change and express their opinions, it leads to an improved quality of life and increased self-esteem and self-worth. Another study also revealed a similar outcome [50].

The present study identified various barriers that hinder women’s empowerment in determining fertility, including financial dependency, entrenched gender norms, family pressure, lack of critical consciousness, religious belief, and lack of access to necessary resources. A study conducted by group of researchers pointed out gender norms and cultural traditions [43, 51], family and community expectations [51, 52], economic constraints and financial dependency [11, 53], lack of awareness and education about reproductive rights [43, 51], and misinformation about contraception and family planning [51, 52] as a barrier. Qualitative studies in Tanzania and Western Kenya emphasize men as the primary decision-makers in reproductive matters [54, 55], which this study also identified.

Beyond education, access to information, economic independence, and empowerment resources amply described in the literature, this study identified other facilitators like misconceptions regarding contraceptive use, lack of critical consciousness, and family pressure to have a baby earlier after marriage that deserve further attention in research and interventions.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study is its ability to explore deep, context-rich women’s perceptions, motivations, and barriers regarding their empowerment and fertility. The study reveals how traditions, gender expectations, and interpersonal dynamics influence these aspects. The main strength of this study is its ability to capture the complex, lived experiences and nuanced perspectives of women, which are often missed by quantitative research.

This study, like any others, has its limitations. We acknowledge that questions on these sensitive topics related to fertility in FGDs may have limited the scope of discussions among women, potentially limiting the information we obtained. However, we attempted to mitigate this potential bias by conducting focus group discussions (FGDs) with women from diverse socio-economic and cultural backgrounds to maximize comfort and facilitate open dialogue. Second, we interviewed only female of reproductive age and did not consider their male partners, so there may be chances of desirability bias. Third, the study area was urban. There is a potential for future research to include a comparative analysis that considers women from rural settings.

Implications for practice and research

The results of the present study suggest enhancing decision-making autonomy, economic empowerment, and mobility for women while promoting gender equality and reproductive health autonomy. Programs that improve financial literacy include budgeting workshops, savings strategies, and investment education. Additionally, these initiatives’ fostering entrepreneurship and promoting safe social participation are essential components. Targeted plans could focus on particular groups, such as young women entering the workforce, low-income women, and women in business. Furthermore, boosting women’s self-esteem and well-being through targeted interventions is crucial for improving their psychological empowerment. Furthermore, understanding the effects of gender norms on empowerment and assessing fertility behavior is imperative, as these cultural and social factors significantly influence women’s empowerment and life choices. This comprehensive strategy has the potential to foster a more equitable and empowered society for women by addressing the various barriers they encounter and creating supportive environments for their development.

Conclusion

Through women’s perceptions, meanings, and lived experiences, this study offers a nuanced interplay between different dimensions of women’s empowerment and fertility. This study found that higher levels of women’s empowerment were rooted with a preference for smaller family sizes and delayed childbearing. Empowered women exhibit greater self-confidence and independence in expressing opinions and making decisions in both household and societal settings. Participants underscored that economic empowerment is a crucial element of empowerment. The study deepen the understanding about significant sociocultural barriers, including family pressure, son preference, lack of critical consciousness, and misconceptions about contraceptives that hinder women’s empowerment toward fertility decisions. The findings also highlight the potential for change because addressing family pressure and social barriers could significantly improve women’s empowerment and fertility outcomes. These changes are crucial for advancing gender equality and achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly in women’s empowerment and reproductive health. Empowering women is not only about improving their circumstances but also about shifting societal structures that perpetuate inequality. Interventions and programs should be designed to address specific constraints and improve women’s capacity to acknowledge their rights and exercise choices to achieve their reproductive goals.
