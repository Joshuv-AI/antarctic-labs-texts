Impact of lung cancer discovery mode on cancer outcomes: data from the KBP-2020-CPHG study
Authors: Emmanuel Grolleau, Sébastien Couraud, Cyril Maurer, Jean-Philippe Kraemer, Jean-Michel Peloni, Stéphane Raymond, Hervé Le Floch, Nicolae Banciu, Alexia Letierce, Didier Debieuvre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Early-stage detection significantly improves prognosis, but most diagnoses are made at advanced stages due to limited early symptoms. This real-world study from the KBP-2020-CPHG cohort evaluated clinical characteristics and overall survival according to the mode of lung cancer discovery.Patients and methodsKBP-2020-CPHG is a nationwide prospective cohort of 8999 patients with newly diagnosed lung cancer in non-academic French hospitals. Patients were categorised by mode of lung cancer discovery as symptomatic presentation, incidental finding or individual screening. Baseline characteristics and survival were compared across groups.ResultsAmong 8943 patients with available data, 6404 (71.6%) were diagnosed symptomatically, 2178 (24.4%) incidentally and 361 (4.0%) through opportunistic screening in a non-organised context. Overall, 71.0%, 82.5% and 89.1% of patients, respectively, had performance status 0/1 (p<0.0001), and 3.4%, 6.4% and 6.9% had lepidic adenocarcinoma (p<0.001). Early-stage disease (stages 0–II) was diagnosed in 54.6% of screened patients, 40.5% of incidentally diagnosed patients and just 10.6% of symptomatically diagnosed patients (p<0.0001). Median overall survival was 45.6, 30.0 and 11.5 months, respectively. Corresponding 3-year overall survival rates were 59.0%, 46.3% and 26.4%.ConclusionThe mode of lung cancer discovery was associated with stage at diagnosis and survival. Compared with symptomatic diagnosis, screening and incidental detection were associated with earlier stage of diagnosis and significantly better overall survival. These findings support the broader implementation of structured early-detection strategies, including screening programs, to improve the prognosis of patients with lung cancer.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer and of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. The strongest prognostic factor for lung cancer survival is TNM stage at diagnosis [2, 3], with localised stages being associated with a far better prognosis than advanced stages or metastatic disease. We have previously reported that, in the French KBP-2020-CPHG study, the 3-year overall survival rate decreased from 84.0% in patients with stage I disease, to 65.9%, 49.5% and 21.3% in patients with stages II, III or IV disease, respectively [4].

The early detection of lung cancer is therefore of great importance. However, early-stage lung cancers are poorly symptomatic, and an early diagnosis of lung cancer is usually made by chance from chest computed tomography (CT) scans that have been taken for another reason (e.g. trauma), with the resulting finding of incidental pulmonary nodules (IPNs). There is then the challenge of correctly managing such nodules and avoiding loss of sight of them, which can result in nodule progression into unresectable or metastatic disease. Some teams are thus developing IPN management programs [5]. Another way of diagnosing early-stage lung cancers is obviously through lung cancer screening programs [6]. In France, the proportion of lung cancers diagnosed at stage I/II is approximately 20%, whereas in the DEP KP80 screening study, 71.4% of identified cancers were stage I/II [6, 7].

The KBP-2020-CPHG study collected data on all patients with newly diagnosed lung cancer within general hospitals in France from 1 January to 31 December 2020. In the current publication, we sought to assess patient and tumour characteristics, and their impact on the overall survival, based on the method of early detection of lung cancer: by developing symptoms, by incidental discovery or through individual screening.

Method

The KBP-2020-CPHG study

The KBP-CPHG (French College of General Hospital Pulmonologists) studies are prospective cohort studies that have been conducted each decade since 2000 in non-academic public hospitals in France. The studies aim to collect data on all patients with newly diagnosed lung cancer in general hospitals in France. In 2020, 82 participating centres were enrolled in the KBP-2020-CPHG study and provided data on 8999 patients, corresponding to approximatively 20% of all lung cancer cases in France.

The methodology and main results of the 2020 edition have been previously published [7]. The KBP-2020-CPHG protocol was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud Méditerranée VI and the French National Drug Authority (Agence Nationale de Sécurité du Médicament et des Produits de Santé; ANSM) (ID RCB 2019-A00943-54). The study protocols were approved by French data protection commission (Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés; CNIL) on 19 November 2019 (ID 919267). The ClinicalTrials.gov identifier is NCT04402099. An information sheet was provided to the patients and their non-opposition was obtained. The data were anonymised in the electronic case report form (eCRF).

Discovery mode

In the current analysis, we focused on the mode of discovery of lung cancer for patients in the KBP-2020-CPHG study. This variable was displayed in the eCRF and completed by investigators. Three modalities were listed: symptomatic; incidentally found (i.e. discovered through an examination prescribed for another reason); or found following individual screening (i.e. discovered through a low-dose CT scan for screening purposes). In 2020, there was no organised lung cancer screening program in France. However, individual (opportunistic and non-organised) screening was encouraged in people aged 55–74 years who had smoked for ≥30 pack-years (PY), and who were current smokers or had quit <15 years previously [8].

Among the 82 participating centres, one failed to provide robust data regarding discovery mode and was excluded from this analysis. Thus, we analysed data from 81 centres and 8944 patients.

Statistical analysis

A comparative analysis between the discovery modes was performed. The characteristics of patients and tumours at baseline were categorised and compared as follows: “symptomatic mode”, “incidental mode” and “screening mode”. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and proportions, and continuous variables as mean±sd. Statistical comparisons were made using the chi-squared or Fisher tests for categorical variables and ANOVA as appropriate. Two groups were sometimes pooled and compared with the third group using a t-test or Wilcoxon tests. p-values <0.05 were considered significant. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was fitted to evaluate the association between discovery mode and overall survival after adjustment for relevant clinical variables; because the proportional hazards assumption was not entirely satisfied, results were interpreted as exploratory.

Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared among the three groups using a log-rank test. Statistical analyses were conducted using R v.4.2.0 [9]. Median duration of follow-up of the cohort, at the time of analysis, was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method described by Schemper & Smith [10].

Results

The discovery mode of lung cancer was recorded in 8943 of the 8944 patients. Overall, 6404 (71.6%) cases of lung cancer were identified following symptoms, 2178 (24.4%) were incidentally found and 361 (4.0%) resulted from individual screening. This distribution varied substantially among different French administrative regions (supplementary table S1).

The main patient and tumour characteristics in the three groups are shown in table 1. There was no major difference in sex distribution between the three discovery groups, or in smoking status. Number of years since quitting smoking and PYs were similar between the symptomatic and incidental groups, but the screening group contained significantly more patients with >20 PYs (n=270, 90.0%) compared with the symptomatic and incidental groups (82.9% and 83.8%, respectively; p=0.007). Similarly, time since quitting smoking was significantly lower in the screening group, at a mean±sd of 12.9±11.2 versus 16.6±12.7 and 16.5±12.5 years in the symptomatic and incidental groups, respectively (p=0.03). Compared with the symptomatic group (4.0%), the proportion of cannabis smokers was lower in the incidental group (2.6%) and the screening group (1.7%) (p=0.002).

Age distribution differed in the screening versus the symptomatic and incidental groups, with fewer patients aged ≥75 or <50 years. There were no differences in age between the symptomatic and incidental groups. We found significant gradients, from the symptomatic group to the screening group, in Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status at diagnosis and weight loss. Thus, 60.4% of patients in the symptomatic group had lost weight at diagnosis (24.8% with weight loss >10%), as compared with 42.4% in the incidental group (13.6% with weight loss >10%) and 33.4% in the screening group (8.4% of with weight loss >10%). A similar trend was found for performance status at diagnosis, with a performance status of ≥2 in 29.0% of patients in the symptomatic group, 17.5% in the incidental group and 10.9% in the screening group.

Histology was also significantly different among the groups. We found more adenocarcinomas in the screening and incidental groups (62.9% and 61.3%, respectively) compared with the symptomatic group (53.9%; p=0.01 versus the two other pooled groups). Conversely, small-cell lung carcinomas were found in 14.0% of the symptomatic group, 9.3% of the incidental group and 7.5% of the screening group.

Regarding variables potentially associated with overdiagnosis, we found that the proportion of patients with lepidic adenocarcinoma was around two-fold higher in the incidental and screening groups (6.4% and 6.9%, respectively) versus the symptomatic group (3.4%). The proportion of stage 0 and occult cancers similarly increased across the groups but remained very low overall (0.1% in the symptomatic group, 0.2% in the incidental group and 0.9% in the screening group; table 2).

We found that 54.6% of cancers were stages 0–II in the screening group, compared with 40.5% in the incidental group and 10.6% in the symptomatic group (p<0.001; table 1).

The median follow-up duration of the cohort was 40.8 months (95% CI 40.5–41.1). Median overall survival (all stages) was significantly different between the three groups, with the longest survival in the screening group (45.6 months, 95% CI 43.4–not reached), followed by the incidental group (30.0 months, 95% CI 27.4–33.2) and the lowest in the symptomatic group (11.5 months, 95% CI 10.9–12.1) (table 3 and figure 1). Corresponding 3-year overall survival rates were 59.0%, 46.3% and 26.4%. Regarding TNM stages, we found similar median overall survival and 3-year overall survival rates for patients with stage I/II cancer across the three discovery modes; however, we again found a significant gradient (from symptomatic to screening) in the overall survival of patients diagnosed at stages III/IV (table 3 and supplementary figure S1).

In the exploratory multivariable Cox analysis adjusted for age, sex, histology, stage at diagnosis, ECOG performance status and smoking status, the mode of discovery was associated with overall survival (supplementary table S3). Although the proportional hazards assumption was not fully met, compared with symptomatic detection, incidental discovery was associated with a lower risk of death (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.80–0.91), whereas screening detection showed a similar association (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.60–0.84).

Discussion

In this analysis, we found that the mode of lung cancer discovery was associated with disease stage, with a higher proportion of stage I cancers found among patients diagnosed through opportunistic screening compared with those with incidentally found or symptomatic cancers (supplementary table S2). This stage shift was associated with longer observed overall survival in this group.

As expected, patients’ main characteristics (i.e. sex, age and smoking status) did not significantly differ between the symptomatic and incidental groups. However, overall survival was significantly longer in the incidental versus the symptomatic group. This finding is probably associated with the TNM stage distribution, with a significantly higher proportion of localised stages in the incidental group. Our results mirror those reported by Osarogiagbon et al. in the Mississippi DELUGE trial [11]. These results illustrate that IPN management programs are a useful tool. Recently, Grolleau et al. [12] showed that artificial intelligence-based software reading of CT reports may help to identify IPNs.

In contrast, patient characteristics did differ between the screening and symptomatic groups. This was expected, as lung cancer screening is generally restricted to eligible patients. In 2020, eligibility criteria were those from the National Lung Screening Trial: age 55–74 years, who had smoked for ≥30 PYs, and who were current smokers or had quit <15 years previously [8, 13]. Unfortunately, we found that a substantial proportion of patients from the screening group did not meet these eligibility criteria. As example, 9.4% (34 of 360, 1 missing) were never-smokers, or 23.5% were above 75 years old. This may reflect the absence of an organised lung cancer screening program in France at that time and may have resulted in heterogeneous screening practices and variable adherence to eligibility criteria, potentially influencing patient selection within the screening group. Nevertheless, these findings underscore the need for improved education of healthcare providers and patients, as well as for further evaluation of structured screening pathways to ensure appropriate eligibility adherence [14], an objective now being actively addressed through national pilot initiatives such as the IMPULSION program [15]. Overdiagnosis is a crucial concern in patients with IPNs and screening [16] and is mainly related to the management of pure ground-glass opacities [17, 18]. When persistent (around 50% disappear spontaneously), this nodule subtype is usually diagnosed as lepidic adenocarcinoma [19]. However, the key point is the level of invasiveness of the lesion, from in situ to invasive carcinoma, with a watch-and-wait strategy appropriate for in situ carcinoma, whereas invasive carcinoma should be treated. Interestingly, we found a two-fold higher rate of lepidic carcinoma in both the screening and incidental groups versus the symptomatic group, which could suggest an overdiagnosis bias in these groups. Moreover, looking at disease stages, we observed an increase in the proportion of non-clinically relevant stages (occult and stage 0) from the symptomatic group (0.1%) to the incidental (0.2%) and screening (0.9%) groups, although numbers remained low. Taking this into account, we postulate that a small proportion of lepidic carcinomas may be over-diagnosed, whereas others contribute to early diagnosis and could be affected by lead-time bias (which is defined by the fact that survival time seems longer because diagnosis is done earlier) [17]. Moreover, Akinbobola et al. [20] have highlighted the limited role of lead-time bias in lung cancer screening and IPN management with a stage-stratified analysis of patients in their IPN program. As for screening, prospective randomised studies are awaited in the field of IPN management.

The distribution of histological subtype with respect to the proportion of small-cell lung carcinomas also differed according to the mode of discovery in the present study. Small-cell lung carcinoma was identified in 14.0% of the symptomatic group, compared with 9.3% and 7.5% of the incidental and screening groups, respectively. This observation is consistent with the aggressive biological behaviour of small-cell carcinoma, which tends to progress rapidly and present with early symptoms, a pattern that could influence its mode of detection, although our data do not allow definitive conclusions and further investigation is needed.

This study provides high-quality data, given the large cohort and the low rate of missing data. However, it does have certain limitations. Discovery mode was classified by investigators, which might have led to classification or memorisation bias. The widespread use of chest CT scans and radiography for the evaluation of COVID-related respiratory symptoms (particularly in older populations also at increased risk for lung cancer) likely increased the detection of IPNs, potentially inflating the proportion of incidentally diagnosed cancers compared with routine practice. Concurrently, pandemic-related disruptions to healthcare systems and preventive activities may have delayed diagnostic work-up in symptomatic patients and limited access to opportunistic screening [21–22]. Consequently, the relative distribution of discovery modes observed in this study may not fully reflect current practice patterns, which should be considered when interpreting the generalisability of our results. Finally, the pandemic may also have acted as a proper structural confounder by simultaneously increasing incidental detection and delaying diagnosis and care among symptomatic patients, potentially amplifying survival differences between discovery modes. Nevertheless, the associations observed between discovery mode, stage at diagnosis and survival remain internally consistent and biologically plausible, supporting the overall validity of our findings.

Overall mortality analysis by stage helps to understand that point. Indeed, there were no significant differences in overall survival across the three groups for early-stage disease (stages I/II), although the screening group had a numeric increase in the 3-year overall survival rate versus the incidental and symptomatic groups. However, we found a significant difference in overall survival for patients diagnosed at stages III and IV. These results suggest that over and above stage, a patient's general condition or local complications due to tumour invasion can affect their prognosis. Moreover, our exploratory multivariable Cox analysis showed that the mode of discovery remained associated with overall survival after adjustment for major clinical factors, which was consistent with the descriptive and stage-stratified analyses. The similar prognosis observed between stage I and II across the three modes of discovery is compatible with a limited impact of overdiagnosis. Importantly, given the observational design of this study, the association between lung cancer discovery mode and overall survival cannot be interpreted as causal, and discovery mode should not be considered an independent prognostic factor. Despite adjustment for major clinical variables, including stage at diagnosis and performance status, lead-time bias cannot be fully accounted for and may partly explain the longer observed survival in the screening and incidental discovery groups. In addition, residual confounding related to unmeasured factors, such as comorbidities, frailty, healthcare-seeking behaviour or access to diagnostic and therapeutic pathways, may persist and contribute to the observed survival differences.

In conclusion, in the present study, we have shown that IPN management programs may be useful in detecting lung cancer at early stages, which was associated with longer overall survival. This finding underlines the important role of IPN management programs, which should be further evaluated. We also found that opportunistic, non-organised lung cancer screening is associated with favourable outcomes, despite a poor knowledge of eligibility criteria among healthcare providers and patients. However, given the heterogeneity and limited representativeness of this subgroup, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. This suggests that the healthcare provider community, as well as the general population, would benefit from further education about lung cancer.
