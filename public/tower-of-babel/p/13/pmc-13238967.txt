Effects of exercise therapy on dynamic posture control and self-report function in individuals with chronic ankle instability: a systematic review with pairwise and network meta-analysis
Authors: Peng Chen, Huiwu Zuo, Yixue Gong, Lulu Yin, Lin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to compare the effects of different exercise therapies on dynamic postural control and self-reported function in individuals with chronic ankle instability (CAI) and provide an evidence-based medical basis for selecting the optimal exercise therapy.MethodsA systematic search for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving exercise interventions in CAI individuals was conducted across five electronic databases, encompassing data from their inception up to December 2024. This review process was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024620821).ResultsA total of 48 RCTs including 1,630 individuals with CAI were included. Seven exercise therapies were involved: balance training, strength training, comprehensive training, plyometric training, whole-body vibration training, blood flow restriction training, and visual occlusion balance training. All exercise therapies significantly improved dynamic posture control. Visual occlusion balance training was superior to plyometric training (SMD = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.01–1.43). Six exercise therapies significantly improved self-reported function, except for whole-body vibration training, for which evidence was lacking. Blood flow restriction training (SMD = 1.49, 95% CI: 0.59–2.40) and visual occlusion balance training (SMD = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.06–1.75) were superior to strength training.ConclusionsVisual occlusion balance training may be the most effective exercise therapy for improving dynamic postural control in CAI individuals. Blood flow restriction training and visual occlusion training may be the most effective exercise therapies in improving self-reported function. However, interventions should be chosen with caution because the certainty of evidence is very low.

Introduction

Acute ankle sprain is a common injury in sports activities (1). Up to 40% of individuals develop chronic ankle instability (CAI) following a sprain and suffer lifelong sequelae, including self-reported instability, ‘giving way’, and functional decline (2, 3, 4, 5). Prolonged joint instability may also lead to post-traumatic osteoarthritis and a significant decrease in work efficiency (6). CAI causes considerable suffering to individuals, affecting their quality of life and level of physical activity, and the high cost of treatment imposes a huge economic burden on individuals and the society (6). Exploring effective treatments for CAI is remarkably important for improving individuals’ quality of life and reducing the social medical burden.

Many studies suggest that deficits in postural control are one of the most important risk factors for recurrent sprains in individuals with CAI (7, 8). Postural control requires a combination of sensory systems (visual, proprioceptive, and vestibular systems), central nervous system, and motor systems. Compared with healthy individuals, individuals with CAI have sensorimotor deficits, such as impaired proprioception, arthrogenic muscle inhibition, and decreased muscle strength (4, 9). Together, these factors contribute to postural control deficits and recurrent ankle sprains in individuals with CAI. Therefore, interventions for CAI should focus on emphasizing the restoration of sensorimotor function. In recent years, different types of exercise therapy have been widely used in the rehabilitation of CAI individuals (10, 11). Exercise therapy, in general, has been shown to be effective in improving sensorimotor function (e.g. proprioception) and posture control in CAI individuals compared with non-exercise therapy (10, 11, 12).

However, whether specific types of exercise therapy are more effective for CAI has not received enough attention. Clinicians and physical therapists must choose between multiple exercise therapies when making decisions. Pairwise meta-analyses comparing exercise therapies to blank controls only partially inform this decision. Network meta-analysis combines direct and indirect information from a network of randomized clinical trials to estimate the relative effectiveness of multiple treatments. This type of study includes evidence from blank controlled trials and comparative effectiveness trials. In 2018, two reticulated meta-analyses explored the effects of nonsurgical interventions on dynamic postural control and self-reported functioning in individuals with CAI (13, 14). Notably, these two studies included a relatively small number of studies and did not impose strict limits on the number of trials of interventions, with a large number of interventions considered in one trial only. From a statistical perspective, this largely limits the validity of the results. In addition, previous evaluations have failed to focus on some of the more recently developed interventions (e.g. visual occlusion balance training (15, 16) and blood flow restriction training (17, 18)). Ultimately, the optimal exercise therapies that can be used to manage CAI are unknown, suggesting a significant gap in the current evidence.

In this systematic review, reticulated meta-analysis was used to explore the effectiveness of different types of exercise therapy in improving dynamic postural control in individuals with CAI. Self-reported function was assessed as a secondary outcome indicator. This study aimed to provide a basis for selecting the optimal exercise therapy for CAI.

Method

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension statement for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA) (19). This study is registered with the International Prospective Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO registration number: CRD42024620821). The PRISMA-NMA checklist is listed in Table S1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article).

Search strategy

PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, and EBSCO (CINAHL, Medline, and SPORTDiscus) were systematically searched to identify research articles evaluating the effects of exercise therapy in individuals with CAI. All relevant articles published globally in any language from the time the database was created until December 12, 2024, were included. Searches were conducted using a combination of subject terms and keywords. The search terms included the following: ‘Chronic ankle instability’, ‘CAI’, ‘ankle instability’, ‘ankle sprain’, ‘Functional ankle instability’, ‘FAI’, ‘Exercise Therapy’, ‘Rehabilitation’, ‘Exercise’, and ‘Therapeutics’. Detailed search strategies can be found in Table S2. The search was conducted by two independent researchers following the search formula. In case of disagreement, a third researcher was consulted. A list of previous reviews and references of the included trials was searched. EndNote X9 software was used to screen and manage the above reports.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: i) patients: individuals with CAI. Individuals with a history of at least one ankle sprain who had recurrent ankle sprains and/or functional ankle instability were included. For trials eligible for inclusion, authors had to confirm the diagnosis or quantify the function of the unstable ankle by using assessment and/or discrimination tools prior to treatment (20). ii) Intervention: a comparative study of different exercise therapies or a comparative study of an exercise intervention group versus a control group. Exercise was defined as planned, purposeful, and repeatable physical activity. At least three literature studies on the same exercise therapy for this exercise therapy were included to ensure the high quality and validity of the analysis. On the basis of the results of the initial search, exercise was categorized into seven different types. The specific exercise types are defined in Table S3. Exercise interventions usually require a duration of at least 4 weeks to establish a stable therapeutic effect due to the initial adaptation of the patient and the development of lasting physiological changes (21). Therefore, the duration of the intervention is at least 4 weeks (21). iii) Comparison: received a blank control (maintained their current lifestyle) or routine health education. iv) Study type: randomized controlled trials (RCTs). v) Outcome: primary outcome: star excursion balance test (SEBT) distance; secondary outcome: self-reported score. The exclusion criteria were as follows: i) lack of information on endings; ii) conferences, experimental protocols, and systematic reviews; iii) duplicate publications; iv) serious flaws in study design; and v) gray literature.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two researchers. In case of disagreement, a third researcher was involved in the decision-making. The following data were extracted from the included studies: i) basic study information, i.e. first author’s name, year of publication, country, sample size (intervention/control group), and individual characteristics (age and gender); ii) interventions: duration and frequency; iii) outcome metrics: main outcome metrics: the star excursion balance test, a functional test that allows for the assessment of dynamic postural control of the lower limbs by measurable reach distance, was selected. This study focused on the results of the assessment in the posteromedial direction because it is considered the most representative (14, 22). When SEBT was performed in the literature but the posteromedial direction was not reported, the corresponding author of the test was contacted up to three times to obtain the data. When seeking was unsuccessful, a composite score was selected for assessment. Secondary outcome indicator: self-report function. For studies reporting two or more indicators of self-reported function at a given time point, the following hierarchy was used for extraction: Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT), Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM), and Foot and Ankle Disability Index (FADI).

Quality assessment

Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias for the included studies by using the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool (version 2). The overall quality of the evidence was summarized by two researchers with the use of a summary of results table generated by Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) (19). Each outcome was scored as ‘high’, ‘moderate’, ‘low’, and ‘very low’ depending on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirect evidence, impression, and publication bias. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher was consulted to assist in judgment.

Statistical analysis

Pairwise meta-analyses

Pairwise meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager (version 5.3) software. The pooled effect estimates were computed from the change in values between the baseline and the end of intervention, their standard deviations, and the number of participants. The random-effect model was used for the pooled meta-analysis only when more than three (n ≥ 3) outcomes were included to ensure the high quality and validity of the analysis. Standardized mean difference (SMD) was used as the effect size indicator, and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated. The heterogeneity of studies was examined using the I2 statistic. Studies with I2 values above 50% were considered highly heterogeneous (23). Whether studies had publication bias was intended to be determined by assessing the asymmetry of the corrected funnel plot (24). However, the statistical power is usually too low to distinguish chance from true bias because most paired meta-analyses have fewer than 10 trials (data points), so funnel plot bias was not tested.

Network meta-analyses

Network meta-analysis was performed using Stata (version 16.0). SMD and 95% CI were used as effect size indicators. Nodal analysis was used to test for inconsistency, with P > 0.05 suggesting that the results of direct and indirect comparisons are more consistent, and the consistency model was used for analysis. On the contrary, the inconsistency model was used for analysis, and the source of heterogeneity was explored by sensitivity analysis. Local inconsistency tests were performed using the node splitting method. The area under the cumulative ranking probability curve (surface under the cumulative ranking, SUCRA) was used to visualize the likelihood of each intervention being the best intervention, and the interventions were ranked in order of superiority on the basis of the SUCRA values. The value of SUCRA ranges from 0 to 100%, where 100% represents the greatest optimal probability, and 0 represents the lowest optimal probability. A minimum of three studies is needed to assess an intervention type for it to be included in network meta-analysis (25).

Network meta-regressions

Network meta-regressions were conducted to explore the potential modifying effects of the moderating variables and whether they affected the model fit. All interventions were assumed to share treatment effects. The variables of interest were all continuous and included the intervention duration, proportion of male, age, and publication date. Missing data were supplemented using the mean substitution method (26).

Results

Literature selection and characterization

The initial search yielded 4,788 articles, and 961 duplicate publications were excluded. The abstracts were read, and 3,263 documents that were evidently irrelevant were excluded. After the specific content of the remaining articles was read, 440 articles were excluded. Further screening resulted in the inclusion of 48 published RCTs (Fig. 1). The main characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table S4. Forty-eight studies included 1,630 individuals (900 males) with CAI. The Detailed International Ankle Consortium standard rating scales are presented in Supplementary Table S5. The studies were conducted in the United States (n = 13), Europe (n = 8), Asia (n = 26), and Africa (n = 1). The mean age of the individuals was 14.1–38.4 years. The intervention periods ranged from 4 to 12 weeks.

Literature review flow chart.

Risk of bias

Out of the 48 studies included, four studies were classified as low risk (8%), 20 studies as moderate risk (42%), and 24 studies as high risk (50%, Fig. S1). Thirty studies specified their randomization methods, primarily involving computer-generated random sequences or random number tables. Seven studies reported no deviations from the intended interventions, indicating a higher risk in this aspect. Forty-six studies had no missing outcome data, reflecting a lower risk in this regard. Thirty-three studies appropriately measured outcome indicators, and 23 studies explicitly stated the absence of selective reporting of results (Fig. S2).

Pairwise meta-analysis

Dynamic posture control

Figure 2 illustrates the results of the pairwise meta-analysis of dynamic postural control. The study contained eight pairwise comparisons. Compared with control, balance training (SMD = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.57–1.07), strength training (SMD = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.62–1.23), comprehensive training (SMD = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.52–1.26), plyometric training (SMD = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.15–1.14), and whole-body vibration training (SMD = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.06–0.66) were able to significantly improve SEBT distance. Visual occlusion balance training (SMD = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.02–0.76) was superior to balance training in enhancing SEBT distance (Figs S3 and S4).

Pairwise meta-analysis of dynamic postural control. Con, control group; BT, balance training; ST, strength training; CT, comprehensive training; PT, plyometric training; WBVT, whole-body vibration training; VOBT, visual occlusion balance training.

Self-report function

Figure 3 illustrates the results of the paired meta-analysis of self-reported functioning. The study contained six pairwise comparisons. Compared with control, balance training (SMD = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.43–1.24), strength training (SMD = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.39–1.13), comprehensive training (SMD = 1.45, 95% CI: 0.61–2.29), and plyometric training (SMD = 1.44, 95% CI: 0.33–2.55) were able to significantly improve self-reported function (Figs S5 and S6).

Self-reported function

Figure 5 shows the main evidence for specific movements. The treatment effects of different exercises were ranked using the SUCRA line. Blood flow restriction training had the highest SUCRA (96.3%), followed by visual occlusion balance training (77.5%), plyometric training (62.3%), comprehensive training (52.7%), balance training (37.9%), and strength training (23.3%).

(A) Network diagrams and (B) ranking panel plots. Con, control group; BT, balance training; ST, strength training; CT, comprehensive training; PT, plyometric training; BFRT, blood flow restriction training; VOBT, visual occlusion balance training.

Self-reported function was reported in 29 studies involving 1,046 subjects. Consistent results (P = 0.73) showed that compared with control, balance training (SMD = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.53–1.41), strength training (SMD = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.31–1.24), comprehensive training (SMD = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.63–1.80), plyometric training (SMD = 1.39, 95% CI: 0.61–2.17), visual occlusion balance training (SMD = 1.68, 95% CI: 0.86–2.50), and blood flow restriction training (SMD = 2.27, 95% CI: 1.38–3.16) significantly improved self-reported function. Visual occlusion balance training was superior to strength training (SMD = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.06–1.75). Blood flow restriction training was superior to balance training (SMD = 1.29, 95% CI: 0.36–2.23), strength training (SMD = 1.49, 95% CI: 0.59–2.40), and comprehensive training (SMD = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.20–1.90; Table 1).

Meta-regression

After regression analysis, no significant effect of all covariates was found on either outcome, indicating that the heterogeneity of the study did not stem from the intervention duration, proportion of male, age, or publication date (Table S6).

Level of evidence

We first rated the certainty of evidence in direct comparison. The certainty of the evidence ranged from low to very low across the different outcomes and comparisons (Figs. 3 and 4). The main reasons for downgrading were high risk of bias and inconsistency. Table S7 summarizes the certainty of evidence in indirect comparison. The certainty of evidence for two outcomes was very low.

Discussion

This study included 48 RCTs, and two networks were constructed to investigate the effectiveness of seven exercise therapies in improving dynamic posture control and self-reported function in individuals with CAI, combining direct and indirect evidence. The results showed that all seven exercise therapies significantly improved SEBT distance compared with control, with visual occlusion balance training being the most effective in improving SEBT distance. All six exercise therapies significantly improved self-reported function, except for whole-body vibration training, for which evidence was lacking. Blood flow restriction training was the best measure for improving self-reported function.

Main findings

Based on the results of this network meta-analysis, most of the exercise therapies showed similar effect sizes in improving dynamic postural control. Only visual occlusion balance training was superior to another exercise therapy (plyometric training). The results of paired meta-analysis showed that visual occlusion balance training was superior to balance training in improving dynamic postural control. The benefits of balance training for CAI are well documented (10, 27, 28). Balance training stimulates local proprioceptors, which, in turn, improves proprioception and postural control. Notably, due to proprioceptive deficits, individuals with CAI have an increased reliance on visual information for postural control (29). Although balance training improves proprioception, it does not reduce visual dependence in individuals with CAI (30). Therefore, a growing number of studies point out that balance training under visual occlusion conditions should be considered (31, 32). Restricting incoming visual information on the basis of balance training forces the central nervous system to generate adaptive strategies by increasing the weight of proprioception. This phenomenon contributes to a rational allocation of neural resources, reduces overreliance on visual feedback, and induces enhanced proprioceptive and postural control improvements (33). The results of the present study confirmed this view. Overall, visual occlusion balance training seems to be a promising exercise therapy in improving dynamic postural control in CAI individuals.

Blood flow restriction training and visual occlusion balance training were superior to strength training in improving self-reported function. The use of tourniquet cuffs during training can partially restrict arterial blood flow and block venous return to the active muscle (34), leading to an ischemic/hypoxic environment that increases the level of metabolic stress and recruitment of rapidly contracting muscle fibers (35). In addition, hypoxia and metabolic stress due to blood flow restriction trigger the mammalian target of rapamycin and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways (36, 37). The mammalian rapamycin target protein pathway is a key regulator of anabolic and catabolic signaling in skeletal muscle (38). Thus, blood flow restriction training may contribute to improvements in muscle activation and muscle strength and further improvements in postural control and self-reported function. Some recent evidence points to the superiority of blood flow restriction training over conventional rehabilitation in improving muscle strength and self-reported function in individuals after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (39, 40). In addition to proprioception, neuromuscular control and restoration of muscle strength are the physiologic bases for effective CAI rehabilitation. Therefore, measures aimed at enhancing strength may be beneficial in CAI. The results of this study confirmed that blood flow restriction training helps improve postural control and self-reported function in CAI.

Comparison with other studies

Two studies published in 2018 systematically reviewed the effects of nonsurgical treatments on dynamic postural control and self-reported function in CAI, respectively (13, 14). Their findings indicated that exercise interventions, such as balance training and comprehensive training, significantly improved dynamic postural control and self-reported function. The results of this study, which included a large sample of individuals with CAI and a wider range of exercise modalities, found that strength training, augmentative training, whole-body vibration training, visual occlusion balance training, and blood flow restriction training were beneficial for dynamic postural control in individuals with CAI. The results of several other pairwise meta-analyses (10, 11, 28, 41, 42) showed that balance training, whole-body vibration training, and comprehensive training were superior to controls in improving dynamic postural control in CAI. Koshino et al. (11) showed that strength training did not significantly improve dynamic postural control in individuals with CAI compared with controls. This finding contradicts the results of the present study, and this discrepancy may be due to the inclusion of a larger number of literature in the present study.

Clinical implications

For the management of CAI, the results of the current network meta-analysis support the effectiveness of exercise therapy. Although the ideal duration has not been specified, a duration of 4–6 weeks has been shown to be sufficient to improve postural control and self-reported function and has been recommended for clinical use (43). Most of the intervention durations used in the included literature are also 4–6 weeks. A notable detail that visual occlusion balance training was the only intervention that outperformed another type of exercise therapy on both outcome measures. In other words, if a busy physician is choosing a treatment, visual occlusion balance training is most likely to be the best choice. Although the neurophysiological mechanisms behind this phenomenon are still unclear, when balance training is performed under visual occlusion conditions, individuals with CAI are speculated to require cross-modal sensory reweighting from the visual to the somatosensory system to accommodate the postural control demands generated by balance training, which could increase somatosensory load and induce enhanced training effects (44, 45). This finding also emphasizes the importance of targeted interventions for altered neuroplasticity in individuals with CAI.

The results of this study showed that balance training was similar to strength training in improving both outcome measures. This finding is interesting because the two interventions have different goals for the therapy (balance training focuses on improving proprioception, whereas strength training focuses on strengthening the ankle muscles typically affected by ankle sprains). This finding may reflect the beneficial effects of strength training on sensorimotor function. Improving the strength of ankle muscles may allow individuals with CAI to compensate for changes in ankle ligament mechanoreceptor activity (46). However, traditional strength training may not be optimal. Supplementing strength training with blood flow restriction may be more clinically effective because findings have shown that blood flow restriction training is superior to strength training in improving self-reported function.

Several studies have proposed that physiological changes induced by vibratory stimuli may lead to more proprioceptive feedback (47, 48). In addition, most whole-body vibration training programs include balance tasks. The combined effect of vibratory stimulation and balance training may be beneficial for postural control (49). However, the results of this study do not support this idea considering that whole-body vibration training did not differ significantly from balance training on dynamic postural control metrics. This result is similar to that of a recent meta-analysis showing that whole-body vibration training was not superior to balance training in improving static and dynamic postural control (11). In individuals with CAI, balance training appears to provide sufficient sensorimotor stimulation, which may limit the added benefits of whole-body vibration (11). In addition, a notable detail is that whole-body vibration training is costly and imposes a considerable financial burden on individuals. Therefore, it is not recommended as a preferred intervention.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several key strengths. First, this study combined pairwise and network analyses to examine the effects of exercise therapy on individuals with CAI. The combination of these two approaches allows for the determination of the optimal form of exercise, helping clinicians and people with CAI prioritize evidence-based interventions and then make more informed decisions. Second, a systematic and comprehensive search strategy based on multiple databases was used. The search was not limited by publication date nor language, allowing the inclusion of a sizable number of RCTs (48 trials; 1,630 individuals) and a wide range of exercise modalities, thereby providing adequate statistical efficacy and more options for comparison.

This study also has several limitations. First, the source studies contained a limited number of long-term observational reports and static postural control outcomes, and these could not be statistically pooled. The importance of long-term follow-up and static postural assessment was emphasized because clinicians must consider both in their decision-making process. Second, regarding the analysis of self-reported score results, the results of three different self-administered questionnaires (CAIT, FAAM, and FADI) were combined. This diversity of instruments is thought to be related to the introduction of clinical heterogeneity. The results of the included trials were standardized before combining them for analysis, as recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration (13). Third, some neurophysiological measures that could further elucidate the underlying mechanisms were not included. Future studies should explore the underlying mechanisms by which exercise therapies improve postural control and ankle function through neurophysiological assessments. In addition, the article did not consider the intensity of the exercise analyzed because this information was missing in most of the studies. Finally, the interventions included in the studies in comprehensive training were difficult to categorize due to the different types of intervention combinations, which increased the heterogeneity of the results.

Conclusion

This comprehensive meta-analysis confirmed the effectiveness of different exercise therapies in improving dynamic postural control and self-reported function in individuals with CAI. All seven exercise therapies improved dynamic postural control in CAI individuals compared with control. No significant difference was found in the efficacy of the exercise therapies except for visual occlusion balance training, which was significantly better than plyometric training. In terms of improving self-reported function, all six exercise therapies were superior to the control, except for whole-body vibration training, which lacked evidence. In addition, visual occlusion balance training and blood flow restriction training improved self-reported function significantly more than strength training. However, interventions should be chosen with caution because the certainty of evidence is very low. Future large-scale RCTs are needed to examine the effect of different exercise levels on individuals with CAI and how long this effect lasts.
