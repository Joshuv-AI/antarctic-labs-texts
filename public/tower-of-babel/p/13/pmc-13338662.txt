Longitudinal and Daily Links Between Impulsivity and Prosocial Behavior: The Moderating Role of Positive Risk‐Taking
Authors: Weiyu Edith Chen, Hao Zheng, Yao Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThere is a growing interest in positive risk‐taking (PRT) during adolescence and young adulthood. Emerging evidence has documented positive associations of PRT with multiple positive adolescent socioemotional developmental outcomes, including prosocial behavior. Impulsivity, despite its link with PRT, has shown mixed relations with prosocial behavior. The joint association between PRT and impulsivity with prosocial behavior remains underexplored, especially their prospective associations in longitudinal designs and daily contexts.MethodsParticipants were 177 Canadian university students (M age = 20.3, 74.6% female, 69.7% non‐White), who first completed a baseline survey in 2022, followed by a month‐long daily diary report and a follow‐up survey 6 months later. Multiple linear regression examined the longitudinal associations of PRT, impulsivity, and their interaction with prosocial behavior over a 6‐month period. Dynamic structural equation modeling investigated the daily associations between impulsivity and same‐day prosocial behavior and whether these daily links varied by participants' levels of PRT.ResultsThere was minimal evidence for the longitudinal associations between PRT, impulsivity, and prosocial behavior over a 6‐month period. However, higher daily impulsivity was associated with lower same‐day prosocial behavior. The negative association was less salient among participants with higher levels of PRT, such that high levels of PRT could buffer the negative influences of young adults’ daily impulsive tendencies that deter them from engaging in daily prosocial behavior.ConclusionsFindings highlight the importance of distinguishing short‐ and long‐term developmental processes and underscore PRT as a context‐sensitive construct relevant in youth's social functioning in daily life.Keywords: daily diary, impulsivity, positive risk‐taking, prosocial behavior

Introduction

Theoretically, positive risk‐taking (PRT) refers to socially acceptable or encouraged risky behaviors, such as initiating a new friendship, traveling someplace new, or enrolling in challenging courses (Duell and Steinberg 2020). Recent empirical research has demonstrated the associations between PRT and a wide range of positive developmental correlates among adolescents and young adults, such as better socioemotional functioning (Crone and Dahl 2012; Duell and Steinberg 2020), higher school engagement (Duell and Steinberg 2020), and better academic achievement (Wood et al. 2013). However, most previous studies only examined concurrent associations between PRT and these correlates using cross‐sectional designs, with scant evidence on the prospective associations involving PRT in longitudinal designs or in daily contexts.

Among investigated positive developmental correlates, one notable variable that has received growing attention is prosocial behavior (Duell and Steinberg 2021; Patterson et al. 2023), which plays a critical role in adolescent socioemotional development. Although PRT has been found to be positively associated with prosocial behavior in cross‐sectional (Fryt and Szczygiel 2021) and recently longitudinal (Gao et al. 2025; Tian et al. 2026) studies, it remains unclear whether such positive associations manifest under both daily and longitudinal contexts.

Moreover, impulsivity has been linked to both PRT and prosocial behavior (Fryt et al. 2021; May et al. 2021), but few studies have explored the associations of impulsivity and PRT jointly with prosocial behavior. Furthermore, whereas PRT is conceptualized as a relatively stable trait, impulsivity can fluctuate from day to day (Tomko et al. 2014; Zheng and Zheng 2026) and may demonstrate both immediate and longitudinal associations with prosocial behavior (May et al. 2021), with PRT potentially playing different roles depending on the timescales. Using a month‐long daily diary design and a 6‐month follow‐up, this study investigates the moderating role of PRT in both the short‐term (i.e., daily) and longitudinal (i.e., 6‐month) associations between impulsivity and prosocial behavior among a group of late adolescents and young adults.

Negative and PRT During Adolescence

Risk‐taking behavior tends to increase during adolescence and young adulthood, particularly in college years, which are characterized by transitions and new opportunities (Duell and Steinberg 2019; Duell et al. 2024). Although risk‐taking has conventionally been associated with harmful outcomes (e.g., unprotected sex, illicit substance use, risky driving), it is not inherently negative (Telzer et al. 2022). In fact, risky behaviors can play a constructive role in normative developmental processes, particularly in fostering autonomy and identity formation (Blakemore and Mills 2014; Nelson et al. 2005). Accordingly, recent research has begun to differentiate between negative and PRT—the latter not only supports key developmental milestones but also, in theory, involves less severe potential harm or long‐term negative consequences (Duell and Steinberg 2019, 2021).

Despite being a relatively new concept, PRT falls within the broader risk‐taking framework alongside the well‐studied negative risk‐taking (Duell and Steinberg 2019). The dual systems model attributes adolescents' increased risky behaviors to neurodevelopment processes, particularly the asynchronous maturation of the limbic system, which is primarily in charge of reward‐sensitivity, and the later matured prefrontal cortex, which is responsible for self‐regulation (Steinberg 2008). This imbalance heightens sensation seeking and limits impulse control (Duell et al. 2022; Steinberg 2010), leading to higher risk‐taking.

While higher levels of impulsivity have been consistently linked with more negative risk‐taking (Steinberg 2010), the relation between impulsivity and PRT is less clear. On the one hand, the dual systems theory posits a positive association between impulsivity and PRT (Steinberg 2010). Like negative risk‐taking, PRT also requires a degree of reward sensitivity and impulse to initiate action and to overcome hesitation or fear of uncertainty. Impulsivity—particularly in the context of positive emotions (e.g., seeking social connections)—may facilitate engagement in prosocial risks (Wood et al. 2013). On the other hand, PRT could be negatively associated with impulsivity as the reward to positive risks is often not spontaneous, which requires more deliberate planning as compared to actions due to lack of control (Gao et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2025).

It should be noted that although some earlier literature uses the term prosocial risk‐taking interchangeably with PRT (Wood et al. 2013), they are not the same. Prosocial risk‐taking refers to behaviors that benefit others (e.g., lying or cheating for a friend; Armstrong‐Carter and Telzer 2025; Zhang and Wang 2025). In contrast, PRT is theoretically defined by whether the behavior itself is socially acceptable or even encouraged. As such, prosocial risk taking may overlap with PRT in certain instances, but it is not a subcategory of PRT, nor could prosocial outcomes be assumed for PRT (Rieder et al. 2026; Tian et al. 2026).

When examining the five subdimensions of impulsivity as assessed by the UPPS‐P Impulsive Behavior Scale (Cyders et al. 2014), sensation seeking has consistently shown a positive association with PRT (Duell and Steinberg 2020; Gao et al. 2025; Te Brinke et al. 2022), whereas lack of perseverance shows a negative association (Gao et al. 2025). However, the relations of positive urgency, negative urgency, and lack of premeditation with PRT remain largely unexplored. The limited understanding of the relations between subdimensions of impulsivity and PRT may partly explain the inconsistent findings in the literature. Hence, the mixed findings highlight the need for a better understanding of how impulsivity, especially its different subdimensions, and PRT jointly influence the psychosocial development in adolescents and young adults.

The Associations Among PRT, Impulsivity, and Prosocial Behavior

Prosocial behavior—voluntary actions that intend to benefit others without expecting rewards to oneself (Do et al. 2017)—supports psychological well‐being during adolescence and young adulthood (Hui et al. 2020). Because some PRT behaviors (e.g., defending a peer) involve prosocial risks (Do et al. 2017), it is not surprising that PRT is positively associated with prosocial behaviors (Fryt and Szczygiel 2021; Park and Gentzler 2025; Tian et al. 2026). This association may be driven by social responsibility, a key feature of PRT that is reinforced by social rewards and support (Fryt and Szczygiel 2021; Rieder et al. 2026) and greater sensitivity to social norms among those with high levels of PRT (Fryt and Szczygiel 2021; Park and Gentzler 2025). However, in contexts of heightened impulsivity, people may prioritize immediate peer approval over long‐term social rewards, sometimes resulting in socially aversive behavior (May et al. 2021). Therefore, it is unclear whether the association between impulsivity and prosocial behavior is the same among people with high versus low levels of PRT.

Impulsivity generally shows a negative association with prosocial behavior (Bansal et al. 2024; Côté et al. 2002; Memmott‐Elison and Toseeb 2023). One explanation is that impulsive individuals tend to prioritize immediate self‐interest, whereas prosocial actions require greater deliberation due to their delayed rewards (Malesza and Kalinowski 2021; Yamagishi et al. 2016). However, impulsivity may not always hinder prosocial behavior. For example, when adolescents seek social interactions, impulsivity can fuel behaviors such as initiating conversations that constitute the beginning of a prosocial action (Armstrong‐Carter et al. 2022). Drawing on the PRT framework, impulsivity may serve as a form of behavioral activation, driving individuals to social engagement. Young adults with higher levels of PRT may utilize such moments to channel their impulsivity into socially constructive actions. Thus, it is equally important to further explore the link between impulsivity and prosocial behavior in joint consideration of PRT.

Daily Links Among PRT, Impulsivity, and Prosocial Behavior

To our best knowledge, only five extant pertinent studies on PRT have examined adolescents (age 13–16, Duell et al. 2022; age 12–15, Gao et al. 2025; age 12–18, Lee et al. 2025; age 12–15, Tian et al. 2026; M age = 12.6, Zhang and Wang 2025), while the majority was conducted among young adults (age range: 18–30; Duell and Steinberg 2020; Duell et al. 2024, 2025; Fryt and Szczygiel 2021; Fryt et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2025; Te Brinke et al. 2022; Wong and Yang 2020). This pattern partly reflects the developmental salience of the college transition period, which introduces a broader and more autonomous context for engaging in PRT (Duell et al. 2024). Meanwhile, the links among PRT, impulsivity, and prosocial behavior were primarily investigated with cross‐sectional designs. Only Gao et al. (2025) and Tian et al. (2026) adopted conventional longitudinal design (spanning 2 years and 1 year, respectively), identifying relatively longer‐term patterns of associations.

Although PRT is conceptualized as a relatively stable trait—given that the behaviors it captures (e.g., joining a new club or traveling somewhere new) are unlikely to vary over short timescales such as daily (Duell and Steinberg 2020)—recent research has suggested that both impulsivity (Tomko et al. 2014; Zheng and Zheng 2026) and prosocial behavior (Arbel et al. 2022; Schacter and Margolin 2019; Snippe et al. 2018) exhibit substantial within‐person short‐term fluctuations in daily contexts. Impulsivity, for instance, is contextually influenced, with peer pressure in group activities or competitive settings amplifying impulsive tendencies (Armstrong‐Carter et al. 2022). These momentary shifts in impulsivity may be directly linked to fluctuations in prosocial behavior, along with the opportunities presented and emotional reinforcement (Armstrong‐Carter et al. 2022; Wu and Hong 2022). Despite these theoretically plausible dynamic patterns, the short‐term daily associations between impulsivity and prosocial behaviors, and the potential role of PRT, remain unexplored.

Daily diary methods offer a valuable complementary approach to conventional longitudinal designs by capturing the dynamic, day‐to‐day fluctuations that characterize people's everyday lives (Sliwinski 2011). Besides minimizing recall bias through reporting in daily contexts, it facilitates the detection of short‐term variations that may be obscured in conventional long‐term or retrospective designs (Bolger et al. 2003). As such, daily diary designs offer a nuanced lens through which to examine how PRT may potentially moderate the association between daily impulsivity and prosocial behavior in daily contexts. Moreover, daily diary data can reveal meaningful between‐person heterogeneity in these daily short‐term processes (Zheng and Zheng 2024), and further explore what individual characteristics (e.g., PRT) could account for the heterogeneity in these daily processes.

The Present Study

Previous studies have examined how PRT and impulsivity were linked to prosocial behavior separately, while few have explored their associations with prosocial behavior jointly, especially in daily contexts. The present study aims to examine both the short‐term (i.e., daily) and longitudinal (i.e., 6‐month) associations between impulsivity and prosocial behavior, and to investigate the potential moderating role of PRT. We used month‐long daily diary data with reports of daily impulsivity and prosocial behavior among a group of late adolescents and young adults, who also reported their general PRT, impulsivity, and prosocial behavior at baseline survey and 6 months later. We first examined whether adolescents and young adults’ daily prosocial behavior co‐fluctuates with day‐to‐day changes in impulsivity at the daily within‐person level (Q1a), and whether impulsivity and PRT are associated with prosocial behavior at 6 months later at the longitudinal between‐person level (Q1b). Second, we explored whether PRT as a person‐level characteristic could moderate these daily and longitudinal associations between impulsivity and prosocial behavior (Q2).

Method

Participants and Procedures

The final analyzed sample consisted of 177 Canadian university students (M age = 20.3 years, SD = 0.65, range = 19–25 [only 7 participants older than 21], 74.6% female). Participants' ethnic‐racial backgrounds were 30.7% White, 54.0% Asian, 4.5% Black, 4.5% multiracial, 1.1% Latino or Hispanic, and 5.1% Others. Each participant completed a baseline survey and at least five daily entries in a 30‐day daily diary study. Missing data were handled using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method implemented in Mplus with the Bayesian estimation (Asparouhov and Muthén 2022). There were minimal missing data on baseline measures (1.1%–1.5% per variable). A total of 33 participants did not complete the follow‐up survey; however, there were no significant differences (ps > 0.12) between those who did and did not complete the follow‐up survey in impulsivity, prosocial behavior, PRT, age, sex, and race‐ethnicity, indicating no evidence of differential attrition. At the daily level, there are 3985 observations (M = 22.51 days, SD = 6.95, with 74.6% completing ≥ 20 days). Observations (33 total daily reports) from participants (n = 17) who provided fewer than five daily entries were excluded to maintain reliable within‐person fluctuations and avoid estimation issues (Asparouhov and Muthén 2022). Study procedures and materials were reviewed and approved by the research ethics committee at the University of Alberta.

The current dataset was drawn from a larger multi‐year longitudinal project examining the socioemotional development and adjustment of Canadian university students during their transition into and throughout the university. All first‐year undergraduate students were eligible for inclusion for initial recruitment. Participants were recruited through online advertisements, campus posters, and brief presentations in undergraduate classes. For data used in current analyses, between February and May 2022, participants first completed a baseline survey (~45 min), followed 3 days later by daily surveys (~10 min each) over 30 consecutive days, and a follow‐up survey after 6 months. Daily survey links were emailed to participants at 7 p.m., with completion requested by bedtime. Each participant received a $75 e‐gift card upon completing the baseline and daily surveys, and a $15 e‐gift card for completing the follow‐up survey as compensation (see Zheng and Zheng 2025, 2026 for more information on recruitment procedures). Survey instruments were developed and administered using REDCap (Harris et al. 2019).

Measures

PRT Behavior

In the baseline survey, participants reported their engagement in PRT behavior using eight items selected from the PRT Scale (Duell and Steinberg 2020). Patterson et al. (2023) refined the original 14‐item scale to 11 items based on interviews with adolescents, and we selected eight items to reduce participant burden. Questions probed PRT behavior in general on a 5‐point Likert scale (0 = never to 4 = always). Example behaviors included “Tell a secret about yourself” (disclosure), “Stand up for what you believe is right, either socially or academically” (autonomy), “Try out for a team, joined a new club or activity, or audition for a play” (joining‐in), “Travel someplace new” (novelty‐non‐interpersonal), and “Initiate a new friendship” (novelty‐interpersonal; Patterson et al. 2023). Scores were averaged over all items, with higher scores reflecting engaging in more PRT behaviors (ω = 0.96; Revelle and Condon 2019).

Baseline Impulsivity

In the baseline survey, participants reported their impulsivity using the 20‐item Short UPPS‐P Impulsive Behavior Scale (Cyders et al. 2014). Participants rated their positive urgency, negative urgency, lack of premeditation, lack of perseverance, and sensation‐seeking levels in general on a 5‐point Likert scale (0 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree). Perseverance and sensation‐seeking have been found as defining features of PRT (Duell and Steinberg 2020; Gao et al. 2025). To capture the non‐shared variance or the unique aspects of impulsivity non‐overlapping with PRT, these two subscales were removed from analyses. Scores were averaged across the other three subscales (i.e., positive urgency [4 items], negative urgency [4 items], lack of premeditation [4 items]), with higher scores reflecting higher impulsivity (ω = 0.82). Since emerging research suggests that positive urgency might also overlap with PRT (Wood et al. 2013), another version of average scores of impulsivity was calculated with the subscales of negative urgency and lack of premeditation, excluding positive urgency (ω = 0.67).

Daily Impulsivity

Guided by impulsivity symptoms outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th ed.; American Psychiatric Association 2013) and prior research (Purpura and Lonigan 2009; Tomko et al. 2014), daily impulsivity was assessed using three items: “Excitable, impulsive.” “Failed to finish things that started.” and “Distractible or attention span was a problem.” Participants reported their impulsivity on that day on a 3‐point scale (0 = not at all, 1 = just a little, 2 = pretty much/very much). These items capture core aspects of impulsivity as conceptualized in the field, specifically targeting dimensions such as lack of impulse control, premeditation, and inattention. They are consistent with prior studies using intensive longitudinal designs to assess impulsivity (e.g., Pihet et al. 2017; Tomko et al. 2014; Zheng and Zheng 2026). Scores were averaged across the three items, with higher scores reflecting a higher level of impulsivity (ordinal ωw = 0.53, ωb = 0.71).

Baseline and Daily Prosocial Behavior

In the baseline and follow‐up surveys and daily surveys, prosocial behavior was assessed with three items (“I tried to be nice to people. I cared about their feelings.” “I was helpful if someone was hurt, upset, or feeling ill.” and “I offered to help others.”) from the prosocial subscale of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (Goodman and Goodman 2009). Participants responded on a 3‐point scale (0 = not true, 1 = somewhat true, 2 = certainly true) on how well these items described them on that day. Scores were averaged across the three items; a higher score reflected higher level of prosocial behavior (baseline: ordinal ω = 0.70; follow‐up: ordinal ω = 0.74; daily: ordinal ωw = 0.73, ωb = 0.89).

Analytic Strategy

All analyses were conducted in Mplus 8.11 (Muthén and Muthén 1997–2019). To investigate the long‐term associations between PRT, impulsivity, and prosocial behavior, a multiple linear regression analysis using the Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) estimation was conducted to examine the main and interactive effects of baseline PRT and impulsivity on follow‐up prosocial behavior, controlling for demographic covariates including sex (0 = female, 1 = male), age, ethnicity‐race (0 = White, 1 = Others), as well as baseline prosocial behavior.

The short‐term daily association was examined with Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling (DSEM; McNeish and Hamaker 2020). The repeated measures of daily prosocial behavior and impulsivity yielded a hierarchical data structure, with daily observations nested within each individual. Both daily measures were decomposed into within‐ and between‐person level (i.e., random intercepts) components. The within‐person‐only (level‐1) models were first built, wherein daily fluctuations in impulsivity were examined in relation to daily changes in prosocial behavior while controlling for the autoregressive effect of the previous day's prosocial behavior. All daily predictors were mean‐centered, and person‐level means were included as covariates. Both intercepts and slopes at level‐1 have random effects (i.e., random variances) to capture differences at the between‐person level reflected in the level‐2 analysis (indicated by black dots in Figure 1). Fixed effects of the days (coded 0–29, first day = 0) were included to control for potential linear time trends.

Conceptual multilevel model. NU = negative urgency; PU = positive urgency.

Next, cross‐level interaction models were built. Specifically, baseline PRT and impulsivity were included as moderators at level 2 to explain variance in the random slope of daily impulsivity predicting prosocial behavior. At the between‐person level, person‐average means of impulsivity and prosocial behavior were freely correlated to reflect covariance between the random intercepts of these two components across individuals (Figure 1). These person‐average means, along with sex, age, and ethnicity‐race, were also included when modeling the random slopes. All DSEMs were estimated with the default Bayesian estimation with the MCMC using uninformed priors in Mplus by default. Under the Bayesian estimation, Mplus reports one‐tailed p values. Model convergence was assessed using the Potential Scale Reduction (PSR) statistic, trace plots, and autocorrelation plots (Hamaker et al. 2021).

Results

Descriptive Statistics

At the between‐person level, however, they were positively correlated (r = 0.18, p = 0.026). Additionally, person‐average daily prosocial behavior was positively correlated with PRT at the between‐person level (r = 0.26, p < 0.001), but not with baseline impulsivity (r = −0.06, ns). In contrast, at the between‐person level, the person‐average of daily impulsivity was positively correlated with baseline impulsivity (r = 0.30, p < 0.001), but not with PRT (r = 0.08, ns). Daily Links Between Impulsivity and Prosocial Behavior

The level‐1 analysis, which incorporated only daily variables, examined the daily link between impulsivity and prosocial behavior (Table 3). After accounting for the previous day's prosocial behavior (B = 0.12, one‐tailed p < 0.001), when participants reported higher than their average levels of impulsivity, they tended to report lower than their average levels of prosocial behavior on the same day (B = −0.04, one‐tailed p = 0.015). There were significant between‐person variations in this association, as evidenced by the significant random slope estimate (0.027). The standard deviation (SD) of the random effect of impulsivity on prosocial behavior was 0.164 (the square root of the variance [0.027]; fixed effect = −0.041). This suggests that for 68% (±1 SD) of the participants, the link between impulsivity and prosocial behavior falls between −0.205 to 0.123 (−0.041 ± 0.164). These findings suggested that on average participants reported lower than their average levels of prosocial behavior on days when reporting higher impulsivity, yet there were significant between‐person differences in the magnitude and direction of this association.

Cross‐Level Moderation of Within‐Day Associations

The cross‐level interaction model (Table 3) suggested that after controlling for sex, age, and ethnicity‐race, PRT was positively associated with the negative associations between daily impulsivity and prosocial behavior (B = 0.10, one‐tailed p = 0.018). These results indicate that while daily prosocial behavior co‐fluctuates with impulsivity, higher PRT was associated with a reduction in this negative daily association. Figure 2 depicts the simple slopes of this association for participants at low (−1.5 SD), mean, and high (+1.5 SD) levels of PRT. Participants with a lower level of PRT exhibited a negative association between same‐day impulsivity and prosocial behavior (B = −0.17, one‐tailed p < 0.001). This link was weaker in participants with a mean level of PRT (B = −0.07, one‐tailed p < 0.001) and became non‐significant in participants with a higher level of PRT (B = 0.03, one‐tailed p = 0.305). Baseline impulsivity did not exhibit a significant association with this daily slope (B = −0.02, one‐tailed p = 0.285), regardless of whether positive urgency was included in its calculation. We further compared the effects of PRT and baseline impulsivity on the daily association and found that PRT and baseline impulsivity indeed exhibited significantly different effects (difference estimate = 0.12, one‐tailed p = 0.005). Notably, the moderating effect of PRT emerged only when baseline impulsivity was measured using negative urgency and lack of premeditation, and was not significant when positive urgency was included (B = 0.09, one‐tailed p = 0.035), although with a similar trend.

Positive risk‐taking moderating the daily association between daily impulsivity and prosocial behavior. ***p < 0.001.

Moreover, higher person‐average levels (i.e., random intercept) of prosocial behavior was associated with a heightened negative link between daily impulsivity and prosocial behavior (B = −0.21, one‐tailed p < 0.001). Thus, participants who generally exhibited lower levels of prosocial behavior displayed weaker within‐day links between daily impulsivity and prosocial behavior. Person‐average levels of impulsivity were not related to the daily association between impulsivity and prosocial behavior (B = 0.16, one‐tailed p = 0.032) when baseline impulsivity was calculated with negative urgency and lack of premeditation, but was related to the daily association (B = 0.17, one‐tailed p < 0.001) when positive urgency was included into the baseline impulsivity calculation.

Sensitivity analyses also examined the same cross‐level models with and without controlling for covariates using the Bayesian estimator as well as the Maximum Likelihood estimator with robust standard errors (MLR). The results were consistent across both estimators in terms of parameter magnitude and significance level.

Discussion

Previous research on the correlates of PRT has primarily focused on between‐person differences using cross‐sectional designs. This study employed a 6‐month longitudinal design along with a month‐long daily diary design to investigate the associations of impulsivity with prosocial behavior and the moderating role of PRT in both daily and longitudinal relations. Answering Q1a, higher levels of daily impulsivity were linked to lower prosocial behavior on the same day. The same‐day associations displayed substantial between‐person differences in magnitude and direction. Addressing Q1b, impulsivity was not linked to prosocial behavior 6‐month later. Similarly, PRT was not associated with prosocial behavior 6‐month later, either, after controlling for baseline prosocial behavior. Regarding Q2, higher‐than‐group‐average PRT was associated with a lower negative link between daily impulsivity and prosocial behavior, while no moderating effect of PRT was found at 6 months. To our best knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively examine the role of PRT at different levels using the daily diary design to understand the role of PRT on daily dynamics, and is among the first few efforts employing a longitudinal design.

Daily Links Among Impulsivity, Prosocial Behavior, and PRT

Consistent with previous diary studies on impulsivity (Tomko et al. 2014; Zheng and Zheng 2026) and prosocial behavior (Arbel et al. 2022; Schacter and Margolin 2019; Snippe et al. 2018), both constructs showed substantial day‐to‐day variability in the present study, highlighting the importance of exploring their dynamics on micro timescales. At the within‐person level, young adults engaged in fewer prosocial behaviors on days when they experienced higher than their person‐average levels of impulsivity. Extending previous research on macro timescales (Bansal et al. 2024; Côté et al. 2002; Memmott‐Elison and Toseeb 2023), this finding supports the link between impulsivity and reduced capacity for goal‐directed, other‐focused behavior, potentially due to a heightened focus on immediate self‐reward (Malesza and Kalinowski 2021; May et al. 2021; Yamagishi et al. 2016), on a micro timescale. More importantly, current findings revealed substantial between‐person differences in the within‐person link between impulsivity and prosocial behavior. Impulsivity is not universally negative or subversive for young adults (Armstrong‐Carter et al. 2022; Wu and Hong 2022). It highlights the need to better understand individual characteristics that may moderate this short‐term dynamic process to promote prosocial behavior among young adults.

In young adults with higher PRT, the negative daily association between impulsivity and prosocial behavior was smaller. PRT is closely linked with sensation seeking, which motivates young adults to engage in risky behaviors while disregarding the costs (Duell and Steinberg 2020; Gao et al. 2025; Te Brinke et al. 2022;). Compared to individuals with lower PRT, those with higher levels of PRT are more sensitive to social norms (Duell and Steinberg 2019; Fryt and Szczygiel 2021) and are more inclined to seek challenges within the rules (Tian et al. 2026). These challenges, such as telling a secret about oneself, can take time to be rewarded, and may sometimes conflict with immediate self‐rewards (Duell and Steinberg 2021). For example, individuals may face immediate risks of rejection, being ignored or misunderstood, or the discomfort of re‐exposing themselves—costs that stand in contrast to the longer‐term goal of developing a close and trusting relationship. Thus, engaging in PRT behaviors can require higher levels of self‐control to deliberately carry out the action (Gao et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2025; Wood et al. 2013). Hence, one possible explanation for this finding is that young adults high in PRT demonstrate greater self‐control, allowing them to sustain prosocial behavior even when such actions conflict with immediate self‐interests. However, as direct self‐control or self‐regulation was not measured in this study to corroborate this explanation, it remains speculative, and future research should include explicit relevant measures to test this potential mechanism.

Alternatively, these young adults may exhibit heightened sensitivity to social rewards, which may increase the perceived value of prosocial actions relative to self‐focused incentives (Duell and Steinberg 2019; Fryt and Szczygiel 2021). Importantly, such social rewards may reflect perceptions held by youth themselves (e.g., behaviors valued by their peer group) as well as those endorsed by adults. Although the theoretical definition of PRT—particularly behaviors considered acceptable or encouraged by society—implicitly assumes that these rewards are positively evaluated by adults, positive peer norms and peer engagement in PRT are also associated with higher levels of PRT among youth (Fryt et al. 2021). Future research would benefit from explicitly assessing social reward perceptions to better understand the underlying mechanisms.

In the context of heightened sensitivity to social rewards, the decision to engage in prosocial behavior may stem less from deliberate self‐regulation and more from a motivational bias toward socially rewarding outcomes. For youth with higher PRT, prosocial actions may yield immediate and salient rewards, such as approval or positive appraisal from peers and parents, particularly when recognition is tied to the act of taking initiative itself rather than to its eventual outcomes (Rieder et al. 2026). These immediate social reinforcements may increase the subjective value of prosocial behavior, effectively buffering the potential costs associated with overriding competing impulses. As a result, individuals may still engage in prosocial behavior even in the presence of impulsive urges, as the anticipated social rewards outweigh the appeal of more self‐focused or negative alternatives. For example, an impulse to seek social connection or recognition may be expressed through constructive behaviors, such as standing up for a peer, offering help, or contributing to a group effort, rather than through maladaptive alternatives like attention‐seeking disruption or risky group‐based behaviors. In this way, prosocial behavior may reflect not only reduced impulsivity but also the redirection of underlying motivational drives toward socially valued and adaptive outcomes. Altogether, this finding suggests that PRT may serve as a regulatory or motivational suppressor, allowing individuals to maintain social responsiveness even during periods of heightened impulsivity.

The magnitude of the daily impulsivity–prosocial behavior link also varied based on young adults' overall prosocial behavior. Specifically, in young adults who reported lower person‐average prosocial behavior, the negative association between impulsivity and same‐day prosocial behavior was smaller. This may reflect a floor effect, whereby there are simply fewer prosocial activities to be inhibited by impulsivity. Conversely, young adults who are generally more prosocial appear more behaviorally reactive to daily changes in impulsivity, underscoring the importance of motivational and self‐regulatory flexibility in shaping social behavior (Malesza and Kalinowski 2021; Yamagishi et al. 2016). Future research should further explore how stable prosocial orientation interacts with momentary self‐regulatory demands to shape behavioral responses.

It is important and interesting to note that the moderating role of PRT on daily slopes became non‐significant when positive urgency was included in the calculation of baseline impulsivity. In other words, the moderating role of PRT manifested when only controlling for lack of premeditation and negative urgency. Building upon previous findings that lack of perseverance and sensation seeking were highly related to PRT (Duell and Steinberg 2020; Gao et al. 2025; Te Brinke et al. 2022), current finding provides preliminary evidence on potential conceptual and empirical overlap between PRT and positive urgency, both involve strong reward sensitivity and heightened approach motivation through a shared responsiveness to socioemotional incentives (Cyders et al. 2014; Duell and Steinberg 2019). Given the scarce literature, research is needed to replicate current findings and to disentangle the shared and unique contributions of these constructs to daily social decision‐making.

Longitudinal Links Among Impulsivity, Prosocial Behavior, and PRT

Young adults with high PRT are more prone to social rewards and thus more likely to engage in behaviors that benefit others (Duell and Steinberg 2019; Fryt and Szczygiel 2021). Its interpersonal nature may further reinforce social skills and foster greater sensitivity to others’ needs (Duell and Steinberg 2020). After controlling for baseline prosocial behavior, however, PRT was not significantly related to prosocial behavior 6 months later. These results provide minimal evidence for the longitudinal or prospective relation between PRT and prosocial behavior. It is possible that while PRT can support prosocial development over time, a 6‐month period might be insufficient to observe substantial behavioral changes beyond initial individual differences. Future longitudinal studies with more follow‐ups and longer time intervals are needed to further explore these relations.

There was no significant longitudinal association between impulsivity and prosocial behavior, nor the potential interactive role of impulsivity and PRT. These findings call for a more nuanced understanding of their links beyond cross‐sectional designs. This pattern suggests that the interplay among these constructs may be more dynamic and context‐dependent than can be captured at longer timescales. Given that impulsivity during this developmental period can be episodic and context‐dependent (Tomko et al. 2014; Zheng and Zheng 2026), its behavioral consequences may be more detectable on micro timescales rather than across more extended time intervals.

The lack of a longitudinal moderation—unlike the observed daily effects—suggests that the role of PRT may rely more on access to immediate opportunities for action and social reinforcement. Research on prosocial risk‐taking indicates that such behaviors are shaped by situational affordance—such as opportunities to help—and by the presence of social feedback, including peer evaluation and reputational concerns (Armstrong‐Carter and Telzer 2025). In this sense, PRT may reflect a general propensity to engage in socially acceptable risks, but whether this propensity translates into prosocial behavior may depend on whether relevant situations arise in daily life.

As a result, the moderating role of PRT may be more detectable at the daily level, where individuals encounter concrete opportunities to act and receive immediate social feedback. Over longer periods, however, variability in contextual exposure (e.g., differences in peer interactions or opportunities to help others) may attenuate these effects, leading to the absence of stable longitudinal associations (Wood et al. 2013). This interpretation underscores the role of both individual tendencies and situational contexts, and suggests that future research should examine whether daily PRT processes translate into longer‐term prosocial behavior through indirect pathways.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several methodological and conceptual strengths. Most prior relevant research has relied on cross‐sectional designs, with only two known longitudinal studies (Gao et al. 2025; Tian et al. 2026). The present study addresses this gap by employing a 30‐day daily diary design with a large number of daily observations and a 6‐month follow‐up. This design allowed us to disentangle within‐person fluctuations from stable between‐person differences, and provided insights into the micro‐level processes through which individual characteristics interact with daily experiences to shape prosocial outcomes, thereby extending recent longitudinal research. Conceptually, this study is among the first to integrate impulsivity with PRT in relation to prosocial behavior, offering a more comprehensive understanding of how the imbalance between impulsive drives and regulatory capacity shapes young adults’ social development. Future research should continue to explore these dynamics across different fine‐grained timescales (e.g., momentary vs. daily) and developmental stages.

Strengths bearing in mind, several limitations should also be noted. First, the observational nature of the study precludes any causal inferences. Second, the survey's self‐report nature may introduce social desirability bias, particularly in the assessment of prosocial behavior, which may result in inflated reports. Although our analytic approach disentangles within‐ and between‐person variance and focuses on within‐person fluctuations of prosocial behavior, which may partially reduce such response biases that reflect more at the between‐person level as general tendencies, these concerns cannot be fully ruled out (e.g., momentary response biases). Additionally, shared method variance remains possible with only self‐reports. Future research should explore the multi‐informant approach to measure these constructs.

Third, the use of brief daily measures (three items each for impulsivity and prosocial behavior), while necessary to reduce participant burden, may have constrained the depth of construct assessment and the consistency with the baseline measure for impulsivity. In particular, the daily impulsivity measure included one item assessing inattention. Although this is consistent with current practice in assessing impulsivity (e.g., Purpura and Lonigan 2009; Tomko et al. 2014), caution is warranted in interpreting the negative association between daily impulsivity and prosocial behavior, as heightened inattention may reduce the likelihood of noticing others’ needs. Regarding the moderation of PRT on this negative daily association, the current measure may suggest that the effect may be partly driven by increased attention to social rewards among individuals with higher PRT. Future research developing more accurate and concise measures of daily impulsivity is much needed.

Fourth, the sample consisted primarily of Canadian university students, which may limit the generalizability of findings to more diverse populations. Future research should replicate these results in larger and more diverse samples and explore the role of other contextual factors (e.g., peer norms, cultural values) that may shape the meaning and function of PRT across groups. Lastly, future studies should employ longer‐time follow‐up intervals to better capture the longitudinal links among impulsivity, PRT, and prosocial behavior. Given the limited literature on PRT, there is relatively little empirical basis to draw on when explaining how it may influence youths' behavior, highlighting the need for further research to confirm these interpretations.

Implications

This study contributes to emerging perspectives that view impulsivity as context‐dependent rather than inherently maladaptive (Tomko et al. 2014; Zheng and Zheng 2026). By demonstrating that PRT can buffer the short‐term negative links between impulsivity and prosocial behavior, the findings underscore the value of interventions that redirect impulsivity toward constructive outcomes (e.g., prosocial behavior). These results suggest that educational and intervention programs targeting young adults can benefit from incorporating strategies that promote deliberative forms of risk‐taking—such as encouraging initiative—rather than solely focusing on risk avoidance or impulse control. Promoting PRT may be especially valuable for individuals who struggle with impulsivity, as it offers an adaptive pathway to meet social and emotional goals, yet further studies are needed to understand the applicability.

Conclusion

Current findings underscore the importance of investigating within‐person processes that shape prosocial behavior in daily life and highlight the role of potential individual characteristics in moderating these dynamic associations. By identifying PRT as a factor relevant to the negative link between daily impulsivity and prosocial behavior, the findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of impulsivity as a context‐sensitive construct, whose impact on behavior is contingent on concurrent motivational traits. Future research would benefit from exploring the developmental trajectories and contextual moderators of this interplay, with particular attention to how factors such as PRT may be leveraged in intervention efforts to enhance adaptive social engagement during the transition to adulthood.
