Complications of emergency airway management: Improving rapid sequence induction with the SALAD technique
Authors: James DuCanto, Olivia Dow, Luca Ünlü, Cliff Reid
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Fifty-six years after Dr. Peter Safar and Dr. William Stept first described rapid sequence induction (RSI) to prevent aspiration during emergency anaesthesia,[1] clinicians still face the fundamental challenge of airway contamination from regurgitated gastric contents. Despite the introduction of fast-acting muscle relaxants and advanced airway devices, aspiration remains a leading cause of anaesthesia-related mortality, accounting for nearly 50% of deaths during emergency procedures.[2] Debates regarding the utility of cricoid pressure in emergency airway management are current topics of debate in anaesthetic and emergency medicine literature, with a publication in this issue of the Indian Journal of Anaesthesia demonstrating the superiority of cricoid pressure to paratracheal pressure, without substantial degradation in the Percentage of Glottic Opening score.[3] Recent developments in supraglottic airway device technology may obviate the need for immediate and mandatory endotracheal intubation during both anaesthetic and emergency airway procedures in patients at greater risk for regurgitation and aspiration such as obese patients, however, further research with supraglottic airways in these cases is needed, and definitive techniques to reduce or eliminate aspiration during airway management are required.[4]

The Fourth National Audit Project (NAP4) demonstrated that aspiration continues to be a major source of morbidity and mortality in emergency airway management.[5] In prehospital settings, approximately one-third of tracheal intubations show evidence of blood or fluid contamination during laryngoscopy,[6] with airway contamination identified as one of the top two predictors of failed first-pass intubation.[7] This persistent problem demanded a systematic solution.

THE SALAD TECHNIQUE

The Suction-Assisted Laryngoscopy and Airway Decontamination (SALAD) technique, developed by Dr. James DuCanto in 2014, represents the first standardised approach to managing airway contamination during laryngoscopy.[8] SALAD involves the proactive insertion of a rigid suction catheter into the airway during laryngoscopy to continuously clear vomitus, blood, or other fluids.

The technique addresses two critical issues simultaneously: preventing massive aspiration of gastric contents into the lungs while maintaining clear visualisation of the vocal cords by keeping video laryngoscopy optics clean. This real-time decontamination approach enables successful intubation in circumstances that would otherwise result in failed attempts and potential catastrophic outcomes.

EVOLVING UNDERSTANDING: THE THREE TRAJECTORIES

As clinical experience with SALAD has expanded, understanding of its application has become increasingly nuanced. Reid has identified three distinct trajectories by which contaminant fluid reaches the pharynx, each requiring tailored modifications to the basic SALAD approach.[9]

Trajectory 1 involves contamination ascending from the gastrointestinal tract (haematemesis, bowel obstruction, regurgitation), where the classic SALAD positioning with suction catheter placement in the hypopharynx/proximal oesophagus is optimal. Trajectory 2 describes contamination ascending from the respiratory tract (pulmonary oedema, haemoptysis, drowning), which presents unique challenges as continuous suction may worsen pulmonary oedema. Here, techniques such as directing the suction toward the source of froth or using the bougie-through-suction-catheter method may be required.[9] Trajectory 3 involves contamination descending from the upper respiratory tract (epistaxis, maxillofacial trauma, post-tonsillectomy bleeding), where suction positioning above and lateral to the larynx may be more effective than deep hypopharyngeal placement.

This trajectory-based approach represents a significant evolution in SALAD methodology, moving beyond a one-size-fits-all technique toward situation-specific airway management strategies.

EVIDENCE FOR BENEFIT

Simulation studies have demonstrated that SALAD is easily learned and significantly improves clinician confidence in managing contaminated airways.[8] The SALAD technique reduces the number of intubation attempts required, thereby decreasing the risk of hypoxia, aspiration pneumonia, and cardiac arrest. Clinical case reports support these simulation findings, with anecdotal evidence suggesting improved outcomes in cases of massive airway contamination.

TRAINING AND IMPLEMENTATION

As Epictetus, the Greek Stoic philosopher, stated: “Let any danger loom—and you will discover what you have practised and trained for.” This wisdom is particularly relevant to emergency airway management, where regurgitation can rapidly escalate to an airway crisis.

Training in the SALAD technique is accessible and cost-effective. Teams can develop proficiency using standard airway manikin heads combined with materials readily available in emergency departments or operating theatres.[10] This low-cost approach makes SALAD training feasible for all emergency and critical care services, from resource-limited settings to advanced tertiary centres.

We recommend using Macintosh-style video laryngoscopy for emergency airways, particularly when contamination is anticipated, as this provides both indirect camera visualisation and direct laryngoscopy backup if the lens becomes soiled.

CURRENT CONTROVERSIES AND LIMITATIONS

The optimal timing of SALAD implementation remains debated. Some prehospital services advocate for the routine use of all laryngoscopy attempts, citing a high prevalence of contamination and its association with reduced first-pass success. Others express concern about the potential for soft tissue injury from the blind insertion of large-bore suction catheters. In the absence of definitive evidence, clinicians must make case-by-case decisions while ensuring the availability of large-bore suction for all emergency intubations.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Despite encouraging simulation and anecdotal evidence, high-quality clinical studies examining the impact of SALAD on patient outcomes remain limited. Future research might compare outcomes between SALAD-trained teams with conventional approaches, including first-pass success rates, incidence of aspiration, and patient morbidity. Additionally, studies examining optimal timing, equipment specifications, and training protocols would enhance the evidence-based implementation of this approach.

CONCLUSION

The SALAD technique represents a significant advancement in emergency airway management. Its ability to maintain airway visualisation while preventing aspiration addresses a fundamental challenge that has persisted since the dawn of emergency anaesthesia. Given the substantial risks associated with airway contamination and the technique’s demonstrated feasibility, any serious prehospital or in-hospital critical care team should incorporate SALAD training into their standard airway management programmes.
