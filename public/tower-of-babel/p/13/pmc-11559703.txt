Validation of the Arabic version of the Obstructive Sleep Apnea-18 quality of life questionnaire for evaluating children with sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome
Authors: Montaha Al-Iede, Mohammad Ali Alshrouf, Abdallah Al-Ani, Alaa Alkurdi, Areej H. Jaber, Omar Husain, Batool Abusabra, Nihad A. Almasri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) poses a significant health concern, affecting the well-being and quality of life of affected children. This study focuses on the translation, adaptation, and validation of the OSA-18 questionnaire for Arabic-speaking pediatric patients, aiming to contribute to the understanding of OSA and its implications on the quality of life in this population.METHODS:A study was conducted at Jordan University Hospital, involving 186 Arabic-speaking pediatric patients with suspected OSA. The OSA-18 questionnaire was translated and culturally adapted following the World Health Organization guidelines. Overnight polysomnography was conducted to assess OSA severity. Internal consistency, construct validity, convergent validity, and predictive/discriminant validity were analyzed.RESULTS:The study sample (68.8% of males, median age: 10.87) showed a median Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI) of 7.0, OSA-18 score of 46.5, and Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire (PSQ) score of 7.0. Internal consistency of OSA-18 was acceptable, except for the “physical symptoms” domain. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses revealed five factors corresponding to different OSA-18 domains. Convergent validity was supported by significant correlations between OSA-18 and PSQ scores. However, no correlation was found between OSA-18 scores and AHI. OSA severity was associated with higher PSQ scores but not with AHI.CONCLUSION:This study provides valuable insights into pediatric OSA in Arabic-speaking children. While the translation and validation of the OSA-18 questionnaire demonstrated acceptable reliability and convergent validity, the lack of correlation between OSA-18 scores and AHI raises questions about the survey’s ability to capture the full impact of OSA objectively. Future research should focus on developing more reliable diagnostic tools, especially in resource-limited settings.

Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) represents a significant health concern, exerting a considerable impact on the overall well-being and quality of life of affected children, with potential long-term implications for cardiovascular health and neurocognitive and behavioral development.[12] Previous studies have reported prevalence rates ranging around between 1 and 5% among school-aged children.[34] The peak prevalence of OSA occurs throughout ages 2 to 8 years.[3] Additionally, the literature shows that obesity increases the risk of OSA by 4 to 5 folds.[34] The condition can have a moderate to large impact on the quality of life of affected children, as measured by the OSA-18 survey.[5]

The gold standard for diagnosing and assessing the severity of pediatric OSA is overnight polysomnography (PSG). Nevertheless, PSG is expensive, and its practicality is restricted, since children are harder to cooperate.[6] In response to the intricate challenges posed by this sleep-disordered breathing condition, a variety of tools, including the OSA-18 questionnaire, the Sleep Apnea Quality of Life Index (SAQLI), the Sleep-Related Breathing Disorder Scale of the Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire (SRBD-PSQ), the BEARS sleep screening tool, among others, have been developed to comprehensively evaluate the association between quality of life and sleep-related pathologies.[78910]

Among these, Franco et al. developed and published the OSA-18 quality of life questionnaire in 2000, to assess the impact of OSA on children and their caregivers by using 18 questions.[7] The OSA-18 survey is a straightforward and quick-to-complete questionnaire by the child’s caregivers. It is applicable in both clinical and research environments and consists of five distinct sections: sleep disturbance, physical symptoms, emotional symptoms, daytime function, and caregiver concerns.[7] Each item is assigned a score ranging from 1 to 7, with 1 indicating “never” and 7 indicating “always.”

The OSA-18 questionnaire has been previously adopted in Chinese, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Greek, and Thai.[615121311] Despite its popularity, there exists no Arabic adaption of the OSA-18. This limits the ability to compare findings from Arabic-speaking populations to those of the international literature. Therefore, in this comprehensive translation and validation initiative, our study aspires to measure the reliability and validity, and contribute to the linguistic adaptation, of the OSA-18 questionnaire. This study holds promise in advancing the understanding of pediatric OSA and its implications for quality of life in Arabic-speaking children, providing a foundation for more accurate clinical assessments and targeted interventions tailored to the unique needs of this population. This study entails the translation, adaptation, and validation of the OSA-18 questionnaire to assess the pediatric OSA-18 questionnaire in the Arabic-speaking population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional study conducted at the Jordan University Hospital, between November 2020 and January 2024. Jordan University Hospital is a 600-bed tertiary teaching hospital with a two-bed sleep laboratory. The sleep laboratory is used to perform both adult and pediatric sleep studies with a rate of 5 nights per week for adult sleep studies and 1 night per week for pediatric sleep studies. The study included Arabic-speaking pediatric patients who had sleep problems or were suspected to have OSA from the respiratory, pediatric, and otorhinolaryngology clinic.

Inclusion criteria included all parents of children who were undergoing a sleep study at the Jordan University Hospital and agreed to participate and fill out the questionnaire during the night of sleep study.

Translation and cultural adaptation

We have received permission from the authors of the OSA-18 to translate the questionnaire into Arabic. The questionnaire was accurately translated into Arabic following the rules set by the World Health Organization. The translation was conducted at an authorized translation agency in Amman by a qualified translator and a multilingual health-care practitioner with relevant clinical and research expertise. Back translation has been performed to verify accuracy, and adjustments were made as necessary to ensure linguistic and cultural equivalence.

A bilingual expert panel, proficient in both English and Arabic, has conducted the translation, with consideration to the linguistic and cultural nuances specific to the Arabic-speaking population. Furthermore, a proficient team comprising experienced pediatric surgeons and multilingual medical physicians fluent in both English and Arabic meticulously reviewed both the translated and original questionnaires. To assure the translated version’s face and content validity, the committee extensively reviewed its conceptual equivalence, clarity, and comprehensibility.

Calculation of sample size

Taking into consideration the lack of a specific theoretical framework for estimating the sample size or power calculation in the validation of questionnaires, we have relied on a general rule of thumb for calculating our sample size. Based on this regulation, it is advised to have a minimum of 5–10 times the number of questionnaire questions as the number of participants.[14] We took the upper limit, which is 10 per each question in the survey with a final sample size of 186. Furthermore, the validation criteria for self-reported scales were met with a sample size of 100 individuals.[16]

Polysomnography study

The examination was carried out by proficient certified polysomnographic technicians utilizing the Phillips Alex 6 equipment, which was then assessed by specialists specialized in sleep medicine at the sleep unit at Jordan University Hospital. Participants underwent overnight PSG, which began within 1 h of the patient’s usual bedtime. The study used bilateral electrooculogram channels, namely the LOC–A1 and ROC–A1 channels, as well as submental electromyogram channels. The respiratory channels consisted of a nasal cannula, a pressure sensor for airflow monitoring, and plethysmography for monitoring breathing movements of the chest and belly. The levels of oxygen saturation (SaO2) and pulse were measured using a pulse oximeter.

The collected variables encompass a range of physiological and sleep-related parameters using PSG. For the physiological aspects, we examined key blood gas parameters including pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide, bicarbonate concentration, and base excess; all of which provide insights into the respiratory and metabolic status of the participants.

The sleep characteristics included sleep efficacy, total sleep time, sleep onset, and the distribution of sleep stages. The latter is composed of N1, N2, N3, and REM sleep. Sleep staging was assessed by four electroencephalogram leads: two leads each for the central and occipital regions. The leads used were C3–A2, C4–A1, O1–A2, and O1–A1. Additionally, participants were examined for their arousal index, which reflects the frequency of awakenings during the sleep cycle. The Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI) and its subtypes, namely central, obstructive, and mixed apnea, were scrutinized to quantify the severity and nature of respiratory events during sleep. We also considered hypopneas, awake arterial SaO2, average arterial oxygen desaturation during sleep, and the nadir of SaO2, shedding light on oxygenation levels throughout the sleep period. In addition, we explored parameters associated with sleep-disordered breathing symptoms, such as snoring time, the percentage of sleep duration affected, and the type of ventilation employed. The cardiac rhythm was measured using electrocardiogram leads. Finally, the conclusion of the study encompasses a comprehensive synthesis of our findings, providing valuable insights into the pediatric population’s sleep and respiratory health.

OSA was classified as having an AHI of 1 or more episodes per hour. The severity of OSA was classified as follows: mild if the AHI was 1 < AHI < 5, moderate if the AHI was 5 ≤ AHI < 10, and severe if the AHI was AHI ≥ 10.[17]

Obstructive Sleep Apnea-18 questionnaire

During the PSG, the parents were asked to complete the survey. The OSA-18 questionnaire has 18 questions categorized into 5 domains, with each category being evaluated using a 7-point ordinal scale from “none of the time” to “all of the time.”[17] The OSA-18 domains included sleep disturbance (4 items), physical symptoms (4 items), emotional distress (3 items), daytime function (3 items), and caregiver concern (4 items). The overall OSA-18 score has a range of 18–126 and is used to categorize the extent of influence on quality of life into three levels: mild (score below 60), moderate (score ranging from 60 to 80), and severe (score over 80).[7] The total score and subscores were calculated by summing the score of the questions. The OSA-18 was shown to be a valid and reliable tool in its original publication.[78]

Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire

The PSQ, which has been previously validated in Arabic,[18] assesses 22 items that include the presence and intensity of snoring, the occurrence of obstructive apneas and breathing difficulties, sleepiness, and additional symptoms associated with pediatric OSA. Participants are required to indicate their responses using a three-point scale: affirmatively with a value of 1, negatively with a value of 0, or do not know, which is equivalent to missing. The total score was calculated by dividing the positive answer by the total count of items receiving either positive or negative responses, which ranged from 0 to 1. The denominator excludes items with missing replies and those answered with “do not know.” Scores exceeding 0.33 suggest a heightened risk of a sleep-related respiratory issue in children.[19] The PSQ was shown to be a valid and reliable tool in its original publication.[19]

Ethical consideration

The study adheres to ethical guidelines and ethical approval for this project was provided by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of University of Jordan (IRB #: 2019/296). This study was done in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before starting the survey, we explained the aim of the study and the survey for the parents. Informed consent has been obtained from the parents or legal guardians.

Statistical analysis

All data were cleaned and analyzed using R (version 4.1.0, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria, 2020). Descriptive statistics were used to describe the data (i.e., frequencies for categorical variables and median (interquartile range) for continuous variables). Internal consistency of the OSA-18 questionnaire was measured using Cronbach’s alpha. Exploratory factor analysis was conducted using “principle axis factoring” with an “oblimin” rotation. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of the original OSA-18 was conducted using the lavaan’s “CFA” function. Path diagrams were drawn using the “lavaanPlot” package. Correlations between different scales (e.g., OSA-18 and PSQ) were measured using the Pearson r correlation coefficient. Pearson r values were interpreted as follows: 0.0–0.1, negligible; 0.1–0.39, weak; 0.40–0.69, moderate; 0.7–0.89, strong; and 0.9–1.00, very strong.[20]

Associations between OSA-18 (categorized) and PSQ or AHI were explored using the Chi-squared test. Raw total OSA-18 score differences among different AHI tertiles were examined using the independent sample t-test. All graphics were drawn on R using relevant packages. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all conducted analyses.

Results

Characteristics of the studied sample

We included a total of 186 participants in the final analysis. Of the included sample, 68.8% were males while 31.2% were female (2.2-1). The median age for the entire sample was 10.87 (7.43–13.93). The median AHI for the sample was 7.0 (4.0–17.0). In terms of quality-of-life metrics, the median OSA-18 was 46.5 (33.75–61.25) while the median PSQ score was 7.0 (5.0–10.0).

Internal consistency

The internal consistency of the OSA-18, as measured by Cronbach’s α, was 0.861 indicating appropriate reliability. Acceptable levels of internal consistency were observed for all domains of the OSA-18 for the exception of the “physical symptoms” domain, which has a Cronbach’s α value of 0.582 [Table 1].

Construct validity

The Arabic version of the OSA-18 was examined for construct validity using both exploratory and CFA. The Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant at P < 0.001, and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure was 0.825. Using principal axis factoring with oblimin rotation, a total of 5 factors were extracted for the OSA-18 [Figure 1]. Factors were extracted using parallel analysis. The 5 extracted components corresponded to the “sleep disturbance,” “physical symptoms,” “emotional symptoms,” “daytime function,” and “caregiver concerns” domains of the OSA-18. Across extracted factors, all items had a factor loading > 0.4 for the exception of items 5 and 8 of the “physical symptoms” domain and item 9 of the “emotional symptoms” domain [Table 2]. The exploratory model fit indices are as follows: χ2/df = 1.684; TLI = 0.904; RMSEA = 0.0603.

Convergent validity

The OSA-18 was tested for convergent validity by comparing its scores against the PSQ. The total OSA-18 scores and scores of its 5 individual subdomains were significantly correlated with PSQ score. Pearson r values ranged from 0.31 to 0.49, all of which indicated a weak-to-moderate strength of positive correlations [Figure 3].

Factor correlation table for Obstructive Sleep Apnea-18 questionnaire subscales. OSA: Obstructive sleep apnea, PSQ: Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire, AHI: Apnea–Hypopnea Index

Predictive and discriminant validity

OSA was significantly associated with PSQ (odds ratio [OR] =7.219; confidence interval [CI] =3.405–15.305; P < 0.001). However, it was not associated with AHI (OR = 1.261; CI = 0.611–2.601; P = 0.530). Furthermore, total OSA scores nor its domains did significantly differ across different AHI tertiles for the exception of the “sleep disturbance” domain (df = 177; t = −2.755; P = 0.006).

Discussion

This study contributes to the understanding of pediatric OSA-18 quality of life questionnaire in an Arabic-speaking population. The questionnaire’s internal consistency, as measured by Cronbach’s α, was acceptable for most of its domains except for the “physical symptoms” domain as it was poor. The correlation between OSA-18 scores and the PSQ suggests appropriate convergent validity, affirming the questionnaire’s ability to capture related constructs. However, there was no correlation between OSA-18 scores and AHI.

OSA is one of the most common causes of sleep disturbance in pediatrics and a concerning health issue with significant implications for the overall well-being and development of affected children.[121] Certal et al. demonstrated that children with OSA have lower scores of overall health compared to healthy children and that the effect of pediatric OSA on quality of life has been greatly overlooked.[22] Possible treatments for OSA include weight loss and oral appliance therapy.[23] However, the main treatment for children with OSA is adenotonsillectomy which has been found to improve the growth, development, quality of life, and comprehensive cognitive abilities.[2324] Other treatment options include intranasal steroids, montelukast, continuous positive airway pressure, and bony or soft-tissue upper airway surgery.[23] Furthermore, a systematic review reported that treating OSA has a beneficial impact on quality-adjusted life years and can reduce health-care use and other expenses, underscoring the potential advantages of successful OSA treatment.[25]

Overnight PSG is widely considered the gold standard for diagnosing and assessing the severity of pediatric OSA.[26] However, there are practical challenges and expenses associated with PSG, particularly in pediatric populations. Moreover, PSG may not be readily accessible in all health-care settings, leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation for children with suspected OSA. The development and validation of alternative tools provide a more feasible and cost-effective approach for assessing the impact of OSA on children’s quality of life.[78910] Previous research showed that the OSA-18 measure had low sensitivity and negative predictive value for identifying moderate-to-severe OSA, and it was advised that physicians and surgeons not rely on it and that a new instrument be created.[27] In the Greek OSA-18 version, which had a sample size of 141 participants, it showed a sensitivity of 53.4%, suggesting poor predictive reliability compared to PSG. In addition, there was only a significant difference between mild and severe OSA but not between moderate and mild OSA or moderate and severe OSA.[14] Furthermore, a meta-analysis of the clinical evaluation of OSA indicated that both individual symptoms and combinations of symptoms and signs exhibit low diagnostic accuracy in predicting pediatric OSA.[22]

In clinical practice, the lack of correlation between OSA-18 scores and AHI prompts a reevaluation of the survey’s reliability in capturing the overall impact of OSA in pediatrics. AHI is a crucial parameter derived from overnight PSG, representing the number of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep. This finding might be explained by the discrepancy between subjective and objective measures, which stem from the challenges in capturing the full spectrum of the impact of OSA on a child’s life through physiological parameters alone. AHI predominantly focuses on the frequency and severity of respiratory events during sleep, providing an objective assessment of the physiological disturbances caused by OSA.

In contrast, the OSA-18 questionnaire assesses subjective experiences, encompassing broader dimensions such as emotional well-being, daytime functioning, and caregiver concerns. In addition, the reliance on parents to report their child’s symptoms and quality of life and parental perceptions may not always align with the child’s experiences. Parents or guardians, who served as proxies for younger participants, may have provided responses influenced by their own perceptions or other external factors. However, the aforementioned could be considered secondary to the true purpose of the OSA-18 as it was not designed to be correlated with specific clinical variables such as AHI.[27]

Across the present literature of studies validating the OSA-18 in a number of different languages, a number of methodological concerns arise.[615121311] Most studies, for the exception of Chiner et al., assume that the structure of the OSA-18 is consistent without any sort of testing.[12] In other words, only Chiner et al. performed some form of factor analysis (i.e., CFA) to test the structural validity of OSA-18. In their findings, the Spanish OSA-18 did not 100% match the original as its “poor attention span” item saturated the “emotional distress” domain rather than its original “daytime problems” domain. The various other studies comprising the literature on the validity and reliability of cross-culturally adapted OSA-18, only measures reliability using methodologies that might be contested among statisticians. For example, Kang et al., Mousailidis et al., and Kuptanon et al. use the Pearson r test to examine test–retest reliability when other methods (e.g., interclass correlation coefficient or Bland–Altman analysis) are better recommended.[11141528]

Considering the aforementioned, the current literature on the psychometrics of OSA-18 only proves the stability of the questionnaire through two different time points. Even the factor analysis provided by Chiner et al. was conducted on 60 participants examining a tool of 18 participants.[12] Thus, per the recommendations presented by Mundfrom et al., their model might be too underpowered to showcase appropriate factor loadings.[29] Our EFA results are similar to that of Chiner et al., in that it shows that the OSA-18 structure is not stable. In fact, “mouth breathing,” “dysphagia,” and “mood swings” failed to saturate any of the five factors.[12] The inconsistency of the OSA-18 could be attributed to the fact that it is marketed as a universal tool for a wide spectrum of ages and often filled by parents, which may project their own biases to an already subjective measure.

Therefore, a number of recommendations could be suggested. First, practitioners dealing with pediatric OSA should attempt to compare the OSA-18 to tools of better psychometric properties such as the SRBD-Pediatric Questionnaire or SAQLI.[3031] Second, future researchers should aim to develop scales that assess quality of life through either objective measures or integrate objective clinical markers into their scoring, as means to allow for better diagnostic and predictive ability. Third, findings of the OSA-18 should be confined to the fact that it is a measure of parents’ perceptions of their children’s quality of life and not be used for clinical extrapolations. Finally, the OSA-18 requires analysis of invariance, particularly per age groups, as it is naïve to assume that the spectrum of pediatric age groups deal with or are subjected to similar quality of life hindrances.

This is the first study that translated the OSA-18 quality of life questionnaire to Arabic and tested the reliability and validity of this questionnaire. The study’s sample size of 186 exceeded those reported in similar validation studies; thus, enhancing the statistical power and confidence in our findings.[615121311] The study provides valuable insights; however, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The study’s cross-sectional design limits its ability to establish causation or explore the longitudinal effects of pediatric OSA. In addition, all the participants were recruited from a single center; however, it is one of the biggest tertiary hospitals, which gets referrals from all over the country. Additionally, the study could explore the longitudinal implications of pediatric OSA and the responsiveness of the OSA-18 questionnaire to interventions.

Conclusion

This study significantly contributes to the understanding of pediatric OSA-18 in Arabic-speaking children. Our psychometric validation of the Arabic OSA-18 implies that the tool is not psychometrically reliable method for the assessment of quality of life. Moreover, the tool fails to predict relevant clinical outcomes. Ultimately, this research sets the stage for more accurate clinical assessments and targeted interventions tailored to the unique needs of Arabic-speaking children with OSA. We recommend that future study to develop a more reliable tool that can be used for the diagnosis of OSA in patients without the need of PSG, especially in countries with limited resources.
