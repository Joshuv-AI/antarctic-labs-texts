Disposition at Equipoise: A Qualitative Study of Emergency Physicians' Decision‐Making About Hospitalizing People With Dementia
Authors: Anita N Chary, Annika R Bhananker, Rebecca Franks, Jason Bowman, Adrian D Haimovich, Victoria Martell, Elizabeth M Schoenfeld, Cameron J Gettel, Shan W Liu, Kei Ouchi, Maura Kennedy, Aanand Naik, Manish N Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeople with dementia are often hospitalized from the emergency department (ED) for conditions that could potentially be treated in the outpatient setting. In scenarios of clinical equipoise—where either admission or discharge may be appropriate—little is known about how emergency physicians make disposition decisions.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study with attending emergency physicians at a single academic medical center. Interviews included open‐ended questions about disposition decision‐making in general as well as structured case scenarios using a think‐aloud protocol. We used an inductive approach to code interview data and elucidate themes about the factors emergency physicians consider in disposition decisions for people with dementia in situations of clinical equipoise.ResultsWe interviewed twenty‐one physicians with an average of 10.5 years of clinical practice (range 5–21 years). Five themes emerged: (1) assessing availability of, reliability of, and burden faced by caregivers; (2) anticipating clinical trajectory and feasibility of outpatient follow‐up; (3) identifying acute cognitive changes that may reflect serious underlying illness; (4) navigating medicolegal concerns; and (5) weighing potential harms of hospitalization. Among these, caregiver assessments and clinical trajectories were discussed most frequently, with physicians describing informal and variable approaches to caregiving assessments. While many physicians recognized the risk of discharging a delirious patient, few acknowledged that hospitalization itself could contribute to delirium or other harms. Overall, emergency physicians’ decisions were shaped not only by patient characteristics, but by the surrounding health system's capacity to support timely and safe outpatient care.ConclusionsED disposition decisions for people with dementia often depend on caregiving context and health system constraints. Improving alignment of admission decisions with patients’ and care partners’ values and preferences may require structured approaches to assessing caregiver capacity and communicating risks of hospitalization. Health system investment in outpatient infrastructure and alternatives to admission may better support emergency physicians in making safe, patient‐centered decisions.Keywords: dementia, disposition, emergency department, geriatrics, hospitalization, qualitative, qualitative research

Introduction

People with dementia are hospitalized from the emergency department (ED) 1.3 times more frequently than older adults without dementia [1]. Forty percent of these admissions occur for ambulatory care‐sensitive conditions, that is, conditions that could potentially be treated in the outpatient setting [2]. Hospitalization carries elevated risks for people with dementia, including delirium, functional and cognitive decline, and falls [3, 4, 5, 6], as well as cycles of readmissions and facility placement [7, 8, 9].

Avoiding potentially unnecessary hospitalization is important to people with dementia and their care partners [10, 11], but identifying which admissions are necessary can be challenging. Seventy percent of admissions go through the ED [12], placing ED clinicians at the center of disposition decisions. In many cases, ED clinicians must decide between discharge without timely outpatient follow‐up or admission for observation when patients do not have “definite medical acuity” requiring admission but do have factors making safety after discharge questionable [13, 14]. Disposition decision‐making in such cases may be especially challenging for people with dementia in the ED due to time constraints, diagnostic ambiguity, and incomplete knowledge of a patient's baseline cognition, function, support system, and care preferences [11, 15, 16]. Despite the high stakes of admission decisions, little is known about how ED physicians reason through gray areas regarding disposition decisions for people with dementia [17]. The Geriatric Emergency Department Guidelines, developed by national emergency medicine and geriatrics organizations, outline evidence‐ and consensus‐based recommendations to reduce potentially avoidable admissions and provide structured care transitions for older adults [18]. However, they do not offer specific guidance on how emergency physicians should approach admission decisions for people with dementia.

Our objective was to explore how emergency physicians approach disposition decisions for people with dementia in situations of clinical equipoise, that is, where either admission or discharge could be considered clinically reasonable. We sought to understand emergency physicians' perspectives on the factors influencing their disposition decisions, with the goal of informing both system‐level efforts to reduce potentially unnecessary admissions and initiatives that promote care aligned with the values of people with dementia and their care partners.

Methods

Study Design

We used a qualitative design combining semi‐structured interviewing techniques with a think aloud protocol [19], with each physician interview treated as a case study of decision‐making [20]. Research is reported per the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (checklist available as Supplement S1). Baylor College of Medicine provided ethics approval (H‐53024).

Study Setting

The study took place with attending emergency physicians affiliated with an academic ED in the Southern United States. Physicians worked across four EDs with a combined annual visit volume of 135,000, including a high‐volume public safety net ED and three private tertiary care EDs with majority geriatric patient populations.

Participant Selection

We used random selection to identify emergency physicians from the department's faculty roster. We took this approach to minimize selection bias within a relatively small group of approximately 45 faculty. Potential participants received an email invitation to participate in the study and scheduled a videoconference interview if interested. We aimed to interview approximately 20 emergency physicians spanning a range of years in practice to ensure elicitation of diverse perspectives. Prior work suggests thematic saturation, that is, the point where no new themes emerge from participant responses, is typically reached with this sample size [21].

Data Collection

We developed an interview guide (Supplement S2) based on research team priorities and literature review [22]. Interviews consisted of two sections. First, we asked open‐ended questions to understand participants' general decision‐making related to disposition of people with dementia in situations of clinical equipoise. This section centered on three core questions and associated prompts to elicit factors impacting disposition decisions, based on qualitative methodology findings that focused questioning can yield rich and meaningful data [23]. Second, we walked each participant through a case scenario of a person with dementia with a condition that could be reasonably managed in the inpatient or outpatient setting: syncope, heart failure exacerbation, or pneumonia, selected based on literature review [2, 24]. Emergency physicians on the research team developed the cases using case study materials from a geriatrics educational course for clinicians and adapted them for the ED context. We retained the patients' medical histories, medications, comorbidities, and vital signs (within normal limits) from the original teaching cases, but modified the presentation and added laboratory results and imaging to reflect typical ED encounters in which either admission or discharge could be clinically reasonable. We intentionally designed the cases to simulate the full clinical reasoning process—from initial patient presentation through disposition—rather than isolating the moment of admission or discharge. This approach allowed us to capture how emergency physicians integrate evolving diagnostic information and care partner input into final disposition decisions. Participants were assigned one of three cases based on the date of their interview. We used a think aloud protocol [19] to assess clinicians' decision‐making. The think aloud protocol for this study was adapted from a prior study conducted by one of the senior research team members (ADN) about outpatient clinical decision‐making and was reviewed by a psychologist with expertise in cognitive interviewing techniques. The interview guide was piloted tested with one emergency physician and questions were modified for clarity.

Interviewers were an anthropologist and a medical student, both trained in interviewing by an investigator with qualitative methods expertise. One‐on‐one interviews were performed by videoconference from November to December 2023. Interview participants were compensated $100. Audio recordings were professionally transcribed and reviewed for accuracy.

Data Analysis

Data analysts included anthropologists, medical students, and emergency physicians with clinical experience relevant to the study topic. We used an inductive approach to identify factors that informed decision‐making from participants' responses to the first section of the interview, that is, discussions about clinical equipoise in abstract. Based on team review of the first ten transcripts, a team of eight analysts developed a codebook over the course of three team meetings (Supplement S3). The team refined the codebook after all analysts independently coded two or more transcripts and met to compare coding, resolve differences, and revise code definitions. We then used a deductive approach to analyze the case scenarios by applying the codes. Two researchers (ARB, RF) independently coded each transcript using Microsoft Excel, and a senior researcher resolved coding discrepancies (ANC). All researchers reviewed coded data, and we elucidated themes through consensus discussions. Data analysis occurred after all data were collected, based on analyst availability. Thematic saturation occurred by six interviews. We performed a separate analysis of how physicians reported communicating with patients and caregivers during the thinkaloud protocol, which is reported elsewhere [17].

Of 65 eligible faculty members, 31 were randomly selected to receive invitations to participate in the study. Twenty‐three agreed to participate, and 21 participants completed the interview. Two lacked mutual availability with interviewers. Average interview length was 33 min.

Assessing Availability, Reliability of, and Burden Faced by Caregivers

Physicians reported assessing caregiver reliability and burden when determining whether to admit a person living with dementia. In general, clinicians expressed that people with dementia may not be able to adhere to treatment recommendations—and particularly medication regimens—without support. Physicians therefore saw caregivers as essential to ensuring execution of a treatment plan after ED discharge:

It's all about their support, because I'm concerned if I send you out with these medications for outpatient, are you even going to remember to take it?… If I can't ensure that, then you're getting admitted. (020)

Physicians also perceived caregivers as crucial to monitoring patient trajectory and watching for signs of decompensation.

In some cases, physicians described assessing supports at assisted living or skilled nursing facilities. They reported informally evaluating whether a facility could provide adequate monitoring or treatment based on care handoff information received when a patient was transferred to the ED or from familial caregivers.

Physicians did not simply consider whether a caregiver was available, but evaluated the depth of the support provided. A robust, engaged caregiving network allowed physicians to feel more confident in choosing outpatient management:

A big thing is family support. If they have a bunch of people at home, a bunch of strong muscular people, lots of people who are very good at adhering to appointments, medications, and will come back if there's something concerning happening, then I may be a little bit more understanding if they wish to return home. (005)

Physicians recognized the demands placed on caregivers and reported informally assessing for strain by asking about employment and obligations towards family members other than the care recipient. If they perceived caregivers were overburdened, participants viewed admissions as a means of providing respite and avoiding unsafe discharge situations:

The caregiver, a lot of times, will just feel overwhelmed…So I think that tends to be the most common reason why these patients get admitted—to provide a little bit of respite for the caregiver. (004)

Anticipating Clinical Trajectory and the Feasibility of Outpatient Follow‐Up

About half of physicians (n = 10, 47%) reported that they were more likely to admit people with dementia whom they anticipated would decompensate clinically or in terms of functional status following discharge. They closely tied this concern to their assessment of the patient's ability to attend a follow‐up care appointment and whether reliable monitoring could be ensured in the outpatient environment.

For something complicated like congestive heart failure exacerbation, and the patient has a high risk of decompensating at home—high‐risk cases—I don't usually discharge home. (009)

Concerns about anticipated trajectory were often entangled with questions about the availability of timely follow‐up. Participants noted that even when patients had established outpatient providers, insurance coverage, and transportation, the health system might not facilitate rapid follow‐up within a safe timeframe—making outpatient management less viable.

Identifying Acute Cognitive Changes That May Signal Underlying Illness

Physicians described acute changes in cognition as a key factor that increased the likelihood of admission for people with dementia. These changes raised concern for underlying delirium, prompting clinicians to pursue further diagnostic evaluation and adopt a more cautious approach to disposition. If physicians could not confirm if a patient was at their cognitive baseline, they expressed discomfort with discharging someone who might be acutely ill:

Sometimes we err on the side of caution thinking that a medical condition is what is causing confusion… So we end up admitting because we're just afraid that we don't have enough of a good history… Are they truly at their baseline? We're not sure. (003)

Navigating Medicolegal Concerns

Physicians described how medicolegal risks influenced their decision‐making. When concerned about the possibility of clinical deterioration following discharge, physicians saw admission as a means of ensuring patient safety while mitigating potential litigation from family members or designated decision‐makers.

You want to act in the patient's best interests, for sure, but you always have this concern of getting sued … You don't want to send somebody home if they are going to have a bad outcome and lead to opening yourself up for litigation issues. (010)

Physicians viewed this concern as especially salient for patients with dementia due to their medical complexity and elevated short‐term mortality. Concerned these factors increased the likelihood they would be blamed for poor outcomes, physicians expressed they were more likely to admit patients with dementia even where outpatient management might be reasonable.

Weighing the Potential Harms of Hospitalization

Participants explicitly cited potential harms of hospitalization as influencing their disposition decisions. They described iatrogenic risks including hospital‐acquired infections, pressure ulcers, delirium, and falls.

I would sort of stress the risks of being in the hospital. So falls, confusion, delirium, and hospital‐acquired infections. I find that patients really don't like those, and family members really don't like those. So I try to stress that, ‘Hey, I think it would actually be safer for him or more comfortable for him to be at home.’ (006)

These physicians emphasized the high risk of hospital‐associated delirium in people with dementia and felt it tipped the balance in favor of discharge when outpatient management was clinically reasonable.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored how emergency physicians approach disposition decisions for people with dementia in scenarios of clinical equipoise. Our findings highlight the centrality of caregiver context, limited consideration given to potential harms of hospitalization, and physician risk tolerance in these decisions. These insights have implications for shared decision‐making and future health systems‐based interventions.

Shared decision‐making between clinicians and patients is often proposed as a strategy to align care with the values and care preferences of older adults [25]. Shared decision‐making refers to the process by which clinicians, patients, and care partners communicate collaboratively to come to a treatment decision when there is more than one clinically reasonable course of action. Our study suggests that for ED patients with dementia, clinicians must understand caregiving context to determine if shared decision‐making with patients and care partners is appropriate. While clinical equipoise is often thought of in medical terms, in these cases, it also encompasses psychosocial factors that shape the feasibility and safety of discharge. Most emergency physicians in our study reported assessing for caregiver presence, reliability, and burden. However, they did so informally and idiosyncratically, without the support of structured tools or workflows. Furthermore, physicians did not describe awareness of how hospitalization itself might place new logistical or emotional demands on caregivers [26], such as time off work or extended bedside presence. Currently, there is no widely adopted method in the ED to capture caregiver identity, responsibilities, or level of strain. Validated tools such as the Zarit Burden Interview, which quantifies caregiver burden [27], and even abbreviated versions [28], may not be feasible for clinicians to administer in time‐constrained acute care settings. More efficient approaches may include brief screening questions and electronic health record tools. For example, asking a care partner how many hours per day they provide care or about their geographic proximity to a patient—questions supported by recent literature—may offer a rapid insight into caregiving depth and reliability [29, 30]. A Veterans Health Administration hospital developed a “Best Possible Caregiver History” banner for the electronic health record, which summarizes information about caregiver identities and frequency and types of caregiving support available to patients to help inform ED disposition [31]. Geriatric dashboards could also support more consistent data gathering and value‐concordant care [32].

A key component of shared decision‐making is the transparent discussion of risks and benefits of particular treatment options. Prior research has reported on emergency clinicians' tendency to admit older patients and those with cognitive impairment in the face of diagnostic uncertainty [15]. Emergency clinicians may overestimate the benefits of hospitalization [33] and underestimate the iatrogenic risks associated with inpatient care [34]. In our study, though some physicians did recognize that hospitalization can carry risks for older adults, these concerns were rarely voiced during case‐based discussions. This disconnect may reflect the gap between intellectual recognition of patient safety issues and real‐time practice, where time pressure and cognitive load limit reflection. System‐level interventions, such as brief electronic health record alerts, could help emergency clinicians recognize when hospitalization may carry significant risks [35, 36]. Such tools could help prompt emergency physicians to clearly communicate risks of hospitalization, but must be thoughtfully integrated into clinical workflows to avoid alert fatigue and ensure they effectively aid in patient‐centered decision‐making [35].

An important implication of our study is that improving ED disposition decisions for people with dementia will require strengthening system‐level capacity for outpatient follow‐up. Physician risk tolerance, often conceptualized as an individual trait, is framed by what the health system can realistically support. Recent research shows wide variation in emergency physicians' admission patterns [37], even within the same institution [38], suggesting that individual clinical judgment plays an important role in ED disposition decisions. Our findings align, but further reveal that clinicians assess the feasibility of timely outpatient follow‐up alongside the perceived likelihood of a patient's decompensation after ED discharge. Health systems must bolster outpatient care infrastructure and establish reliable follow‐up pathways or alternatives to admission for emergency clinicians to make safe, patient‐centered decisions in cases of clinical equipoise. For people with dementia, home hospital programs are associated with decreased behavioral disturbances and caregiver strain and represent promising alternatives to brick‐and‐mortar hospital admission [39].

Finally, there is a growing opportunity for developing guidelines and communication tools to support clinicians in admission decisions at clinical equipoise. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services recently created an age‐friendly measure, which requires establishing goals of hospital‐level care for older adults—including those with dementia [40]. Conversation guides to elicit patients' and caregivers' goals for ED and hospital care are needed and could inform shared decisions about admission [41]. Additionally, the forthcoming revision of the Geriatric Emergency Department Guidelines [42] could incorporate recommendations for disposition decision‐making in its dementia section. Notably, however, standardized and evidence‐based ED interventions for people with dementia are lacking [43].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Because all interviews were conducted at a single urban academic institution, findings may not reflect the perspectives of clinicians practicing in community or rural environments, where resources and admission thresholds may differ. Additionally, our study took place in a state with a relatively favorable medicolegal climate, including malpractice caps; physicians practicing in higher‐liability regions may place more weight on medicolegal concerns. Participants described their self‐reported and hypothetical practices rather than behaviors observed in real‐time clinical settings, and there may be a gap between reported and actual behavior. Many participants reported personal or family experience caring for a person with dementia, which could have influenced physicians' decision to participate or their perspectives on these topics.

Conclusion

When deciding whether to admit people with dementia in cases of clinical equipoise, emergency physicians consider multiple factors. They prioritize a patient's caregiving context and connect their clinical judgment with the feasibility of outpatient care. Interventions that formalize caregiver assessments, strengthen outpatient care pathways, and support values‐based communication could help ensure that hospital admission decisions align with what matters most to patients and care partners.
