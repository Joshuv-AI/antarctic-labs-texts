GPT-4.1 and Llama 3.3 70 fail to detect clinically relevant errors in radiology reports in zero-shot evaluation
Authors: Tugba Akinci D’Antonoli, Lisa C Adams, Jannik Lübberstedt, Markus M Graf, Christian J Mertens, Felix Busch, Sebastian Ziegelmayer, Marcus R Makowski, Keno Bressem, Ina Luiken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Workload in radiology continues to rise with consistently increasing numbers of radiography, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations that are performed annually worldwide. This increasing clinical workload is associated with greater susceptibility to errors in radiology reports [1]. Driven by their impressive performance in general language tasks, large language models (LLMs) have also attracted interest for radiology tasks [2–4]. They have recently been evaluated on a wide range of applications, including, but not limited to, information extraction, structuring, summarization, decision support, and error detection [5–12].

However, high performance in linguistic fluency does not necessarily equate to clinical safety. As LLMs are increasingly considered for integration into clinical workflows, a key concern is their ability to identify and interpret medically significant errors, especially mistakes that may otherwise go unnoticed and result in diagnostic delays or patient harm [13]. Moreover, LLMs could be misused for cyberattacks by injecting false medical data into radiology reports to profit from unnecessary tests or follow-ups [14].

Studies have underscored this gap, emphasizing that while LLMs can mimic expert phrasing, they often lack the domain-specific reasoning, e.g., potentially leading patients to draw harmful conclusions when used to simplify radiology reports [15–17]. More recently, the evaluation of LLMs for detecting clinically relevant errors in radiology reports has become an area of increasing interest, reflecting the increasing reliance on automated systems for medical documentation and diagnostic support, with findings suggesting a disconnect between generative fluency and factual reliability [18, 19]. Furthermore, while models with multimodal and retrieval-augmented generation capabilities have an improved contextual understanding, they have not resolved fundamental limitations in logical consistency and clinical prioritization [15]. Few studies have benchmarked these tools across diverse modalities, and to our knowledge, among them, only a few have systematically evaluated Llama models in this context [12].

A critical distinction exists between errors that follow formulaic patterns, such as contradictory recommendations, and errors that require domain-specific reasoning, such as recognizing physiological impossibilities. LLMs may perform well at detecting the former while failing to identify the latter, yet most evaluations have not systematically tested this distinction.

In this study, we propose an evaluation framework for systematically assessing LLM error detection in radiology reports and apply it to GPT-4.1 and Llama 3.3 70B as an initial benchmark. Using 256 radiology reports across CT, MRI, and X-ray, each modified with four categories of clinically relevant errors (1024 total), we evaluate whether LLM performance is governed by error type, testing the hypothesis that a gap exists between pattern-based and reasoning-dependent error detection. The evaluation framework and dataset are made publicly available to facilitate benchmarking of future models.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective analysis was conducted with ethics approval (2024-590-S-CB, and informed consent was waived due to the synthetic nature of the data). We evaluated structured radiology reports created to test clinically relevant error detection capabilities. A schematic illustration of the study workflow is provided in Fig. 1.

Study Overview. A A set of 256 original radiology reports was compiled, including 104 MRI, 104 CT, and 48 X-ray reports. These reports served as the baseline reference. B Each report was quadrupled, with each copy containing a single, deliberately introduced error from one of four predefined error categories (E1: anatomical mislabeling, E2: physiologically impossible or nonsensical findings, E3: diagnostic inconsistencies, E4: Inappropriate recommendations). This process generated a total of 1024 falsified reports. C The complete set of 1024 falsified reports was evaluated for error detection (presence or absence of an error), followed by error classification into one of the four categories and a two-stage success rate, where detection and classification were correct, using GPT-4.1 and Llama 3.3 70b

Dataset construction

Reports were retrospectively selected from anonymized routine clinical examinations at a single academic medical center. In total, 256 radiology reports distributed across three imaging modalities were included. These reports were retrospectively selected from anonymized routine clinical examinations conducted at a single academic medical center. CT examinations (n = 104) included thoracic (n = 28), abdominal (n = 28), cranial (n = 24), and musculoskeletal (n = 24) studies. MRI examinations (n = 104) comprised brain (n = 28), spine (n = 28), abdominal (n = 24), and musculoskeletal (n = 24) regions. Radiographic studies (n = 48) included chest (n = 24) and musculoskeletal (n = 24) examinations.

For each report, we created four report variants, each containing different types of errors (n = 1024). Each error (E) variant was labeled 1 through 4 (e.g., E1). The errors were created and injected into the reports by one board-certified radiologist (T.A.D.) and a 4th-year radiology resident (I.L.). E1 (anatomical mislabeling) placed pathology in incorrect anatomical locations that could lead to inappropriate procedures. E2 (physiologically impossible or nonsensical findings) described findings that violate fundamental biological or physical principles. E3 (diagnostic inconsistencies) contained contradictory statements affecting diagnosis and staging. E4 (inappropriate recommendations) suggested management contradicting the described findings (Table 1). The original dataset was in German and translated into English using large language model–based translation tools (i.e., DeepL). All translations were manually reviewed and verified for accuracy by the authors during the error injection process. The resulting English version served as the base dataset for all subsequent error variations. The dataset is publicly available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17529445.

Two board-certified radiologists (L.C.A. and K.K.B.) independently verified that each error represented a plausible mistake with potential clinical consequences. Disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion.

Model evaluation

We evaluated GPT-4.1 (release 2025-04-14, OpenAI) and Llama 3.3 70b instruct (Meta), which were selected as representative models from the two most widely deployed LLM families in medical AI research, enabling comparison between proprietary and open-source architectures. The inclusion of an open-source model is particularly relevant for clinical deployment scenarios where data privacy regulations may necessitate local hosting and preclude the use of cloud-based proprietary APIs. Each model received standardized prompts requesting error detection (binary decision) followed by error classification (four categories) (Fig. 2).

Standardized prompt requesting error detection (binary decision) followed by error classification (four categories) received by both models

Models were accessed via the OpenAI API (GPT-4.1) and Together AI API (Llama 3.3 70b at half precision without quantization). Each radiology report was processed as an independent API call. For all API calls temperature was set to 0, and all other parameters were kept at their default values. The models were evaluated in a zero-shot setting without training examples, medical context, or prompt optimization, to assess baseline model performance. The exact prompts used are provided in the Supplementary Material. A single evaluation run was performed for each model.

Statistical analysis

Primary endpoints included error detection accuracy (proportion of reports with errors correctly identified as containing errors), error classification accuracy (proportion of detected errors correctly categorized into E1-E4 categories), and two-stage success rate (proportion of reports where both detection and classification were correct). Performance metrics were calculated overall and stratified by imaging modality (CT, MRI, and X-ray) and error category (E1–E4). Differences in proportions between error categories, modalities, and models were assessed using chi-squared tests, with Fisher’s exact test applied where expected cell counts were below 5. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All reported p-values are two-sided. Descriptive statistics with proportions and counts were used to summarize model performance. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the percentile bootstrap method (10,000 bootstrap samples), providing distribution-free CI for proportion estimates. A formal a priori power analysis was not performed, as the dataset size was determined by clinical availability. However, with 1024 error-containing reports and observed effect sizes exceeding 25 percentage points for the primary comparisons (E4 vs E2 detection), the study provides sufficient statistical power to detect the magnitude of differences reported, as confirmed by non-overlapping 95% bootstrap CI. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1.

Results

Primary performance metrics

Both models showed intermediate primary performance metrics using the percentile bootstrap method. Error detection accuracy was 66.5% (95% CI: 63.6–69.1) for GPT-4.1 and 54.0% (95% CI: 50.9–57.0) for Llama 3.3 70B. Error classification accuracy was 65.4% (95% CI: 62.4–68.3) and 48.5% (95% CI: 45.4–51.8) for GPT-4.1 and Llama 3.3, respectively. Both models showed the lowest primary performance in the two-stage success rates (48.2% for GPT-4.1 and 36.0% for Llama 3.3). Primary performance metrics are shown in Table 2.

Error type analysis

Performance differed significantly across error categories E1-E4, revealing specific knowledge gaps. E4 detection rates exceeded E2 detection rates for both GPT-4.1 (72.1–85.4% vs 33.7–47.9%, p < 0.001 across all modalities) and Llama 3.3 (61.5–72.9% vs 25.0–39.6%, p < 0.001). GPT-4.1 achieved significantly higher detection rates than Llama 3.3 across all error categories (overall: 66.5% vs 54.0%, p < 0.001). CT-based detection was significantly higher than MRI-based detection for E1 errors (GPT-4.1: 81.7% vs 49.0%, p < 0.001) and E2 errors (GPT-4.1: 46.2% vs 33.7%, p = 0.048).

E1 errors (anatomical mislabeling) demonstrated stark modality differences. CT showed detection rates of 81.7% (GPT-4.1) and 67.3% (Llama 3.3 70b), while MRI yielded only 49.0% (GPT-4.1) and 33.7% (Llama 3.3 70b). The overall success rate for detecting MRI E1 errors was 33.7% (GPT-4.1) and 19.2% (Llama 3.3 70b), indicating that most anatomical errors that could lead to wrong-site procedures would remain undetected.

Error category E2 (physiologically impossible findings) showed uniformly poor detection. For CT, detection rates were 46.2% (GPT-4.1) and 32.7% (Llama 3.3 70b). Performance deteriorated further for MRI (33.7% and 25.0%) and X-ray (47.9% and 39.6%). Even when detected, classification accuracy was quite low: only 35.4% of detected E2 errors in CT were correctly classified by GPT-4.1, dropping to 25.7% for MRI.

Error category E3 (diagnostic inconsistencies) achieved intermediate performance. GPT-4.1 detection rates were 88.5% (CT), 71.2% (MRI), and 75.0% (X-ray). However, classification accuracy varied significantly, particularly for X-ray, where only 55.6% of detected errors were correctly categorized.

Both models showed the highest performance for detecting E4 errors (inappropriate recommendations). GPT-4.1 achieved detection rates of 72.1% (CT), 79.8% (MRI), and 85.4% (X-ray), with excellent classification accuracy when detected (86.7%, 91.6%, and 95.1%, respectively). In comparison, Llama 3.3 70b achieved detection rates of 61.5% (CT), 67.3% (MRI), and 72.9% (X-ray), with classification accuracy of 71.9%, 80.0%, and 91.4% for detected E4 errors.

Modality-specific performance

Model performance for detecting errors in CT reports was consistently better than for other modalities across most error categories. The performance gap was most pronounced for E1 and E2 errors. For E1 errors, the overall success rate in CT exceeded MRI by 33.6 percentage points for GPT-4.1 (67.3% vs 33.7%) and 27.0 percentage points for Llama 3.3 70b (46.2% vs 19.2%). A performance overview by imaging modality is depicted in Fig. 3C.

Two-stage analysis

Across all reports, both models demonstrated highly variable performance dependent on error type and modality. GPT-4.1 achieved overall success rates ranging from 8.7% to 81.2% (MRI E2 and X-ray E4, respectively). Llama 3.3 70b showed consistently lower performance with rates from 1.9% to 66.7% (MRI E2 and X-ray E4, respectively). Notably, the proportion of reports where both detection and classification were correct revealed substantial performance degradation. Even when initial detection rates appeared reasonable, poor classification accuracy resulted in low overall success rates. This was most evident for E2 errors across all modalities, where already modest detection rates were compounded by classification failures, resulting in overall success rates below 17% for GPT-4.1 and below 2% for Llama 3.3 70b. Discussion

Our systematic evaluation of GPT-4.1 and Llama 3.3 70b across 1024 error-containing radiology reports reveals a pronounced performance hierarchy governed by error type rather than overall model capability. Both models performed best on inappropriate recommendations (E4: up to 81.2% overall success) and worst on physiologically impossible findings, despite representing obvious violations of natural laws (E2: as low as 1.9%), with anatomical mislabeling (E1) and diagnostic inconsistencies (E3) falling between these extremes. However, the exact rank ordering between E3 and E4 varied by model and modality, and absolute performance levels differed substantially, with CT consistently outperforming MRI and X-ray. This pattern suggests that error type is the primary determinant of performance, with E2 errors uniformly representing the greatest challenge, while modality modulates the absolute magnitude. The consistently higher performance on CT reports may reflect the more standardized anatomical terminology and reporting conventions characteristic of CT reporting, which may align more closely with statistical patterns in the models’ training data. These results reflect the baseline capabilities of general-purpose LLMs and should not be interpreted as the upper bound of achievable performance. Domain-specific fine-tuning, prompt engineering, or hybrid architectures integrating structured medical knowledge may alter the observed performance hierarchy.

Our findings reveal a fundamental limitation in current LLMs: the inability to distinguish between linguistically plausible yet medically impossible text. With overall success rates of 8.7% (MRI) for GPT-4.1 and 1.9% (MRI) for Llama 3.3 70b, these models miss more than 90% of physiologically impossible findings. This likely reflects that LLMs operate through statistical next-token prediction [20] without encoding domain-specific knowledge, while inappropriate recommendations align more closely with formulaic training patterns [21, 22]. This distinction between linguistic plausibility and medical possibility represents a significant challenge to autonomous clinical deployment.

The performance variability of GPT and Llama across error types and imaging modalities observed in our study aligns with findings from Gertz et al, who demonstrated that GPT-4 achieved an overall detection rate comparable to human radiologists (82.7%), particularly for more surface-level errors such as spelling mistakes or omissions [23, 24]. Furthermore, as in our findings, performance declined markedly for more complex error types. Schmidt et al found that GPT-4 outperformed LLaMA-2–70b-Chat in identifying various types of clinically relevant speech recognition errors, achieving a precision of 76.9% vs 62.5% [11]. Notably, the only condition in which model performance approached published human benchmarks was GPT-4.1 on inappropriate recommendations (E4) in X-ray reports, where 81.2% overall success approximated the 82.7% detection rate reported for human radiologists by Gertz et al [23]. For all other error categories, particularly physiologically impossible findings (E2), both models fell far below expected human performance, with overall success rates as low as 1.9% (Llama 3.3, MRI E2) for errors that a radiology trainee would immediately identify.

Notably, our two-stage analysis highlights a critical limitation in current LLM-based systems: even when initial detection performance appears acceptable, poor classification drastically reduces clinical utility. Taken together, our findings and those from existing literature indicate that while GPT-4-based models can approach human-level performance in basic proofreading tasks, they fall short in reliably interpreting complex clinical content or contextual inconsistencies. Therefore, current LLMs may have value as supportive tools (for tasks like proofreading or documentation templates), but should not be deployed autonomously for clinical quality assurance without human oversight, particularly for modalities and error types where contextual reasoning is essential. These known limitations, including hallucination [25] and susceptibility to adversarial misuse [3, 14, 26], are compounded by evidence that fine-tuning (Sun et al [12]: F1 = 0.828) and prompt optimization (Schmidt et al [11]: 5–15% F1 improvement) improve detection of surface-level errors but have not been tested on the reasoning-dependent errors where our models failed most dramatically.

Both models exhibited the same performance hierarchy despite differing architectures, suggesting the pattern-recognition vs reasoning gap is a fundamental limitation of current LLM approaches rather than an artifact of model size or training data [27].

One of the limitations of this study is the use of baseline model configurations. While this allowed us to assess fundamental capabilities, it may underestimate the performance achievable through domain-specific fine-tuning or prompt engineering. As such, our findings reflect baseline diagnostic reasoning and should not be interpreted as the upper bound of model performance. Additionally, a single evaluation run was performed per model. Although the temperature was set to 0 to enforce deterministic decoding and minimize output variability, minor variance in online API outputs due to server-side implementation factors cannot be fully excluded. Repeated runs with statistical aggregation could provide more stable performance estimates, though the magnitude of the observed effect sizes (e.g., 36–69 percentage point differences in overall success between E4 and E2 errors) suggests that stochastic variation is unlikely to alter the primary conclusions. Another limitation of this study is the use of synthetic error cases. While this approach ensures consistency across test conditions, it may limit generalizability to real-world variability in radiologic reporting. However, these cases were systematically constructed and validated by board-certified radiologists to reflect plausible and clinically relevant mistakes. Moreover, a formal blinded comparison with human radiologists was not performed in this study. While two board-certified radiologists verified that all injected errors were detectable and clinically consequential, a direct human performance benchmark under conditions comparable to the LLM evaluation would provide additional context and is planned as a priority for follow-up work. Furthermore, this study focused solely on errors in radiology reports, without evaluating consistency with corresponding images. Future research should address this gap to better assess the clinical value of LLMs. Lastly, these findings are specific to GPT-4.1 and Llama 3.3 70b in their baseline, zero-shot configurations. Given the rapid pace of LLM development, newer models, fine-tuned variants, or models with retrieval-augmented generation capabilities may perform differently. The specific performance figures reported here may be superseded, while the evaluation framework and error taxonomy proposed in this study retain their value for benchmarking future models.

In conclusion, when applied to GPT-4.1 and Llama 3.3 70b in zero-shot settings, the proposed evaluation framework reveals a systematic performance hierarchy when evaluating radiology reports for clinically significant errors; up to 81.2% overall success for inappropriate recommendations that follow formulaic patterns, but as low as 1.9% for physiologically impossible findings that require domain-specific reasoning. This hierarchy persisted across CT, MRI, and X-ray modalities and across both proprietary and open-source architectures, suggesting it reflects a fundamental gap between linguistic competence and medical reasoning in current LLMs. Until this gap is addressed through domain-specific training or hybrid architectures that integrate medical knowledge, LLMs cannot be relied upon for autonomous quality assurance of radiology reports. These findings apply to general-purpose LLMs evaluated in a zero-shot setting. Domain-specific fine-tuning, prompt optimization, or hybrid architectures integrating medical knowledge bases may yield different performance profiles and should be evaluated using the framework proposed here.
