Left ventricular ejection fraction and outcomes following PCI in STEMI and other acute coronary syndromes: ACC NCDR registry in India
Authors: Hemant Chaturvedi, Rajeev Gupta, Sanjeev K Sharma, Jitender S Makkar, Krishna Kumar Sharma, Vishnu Natani, Raghubir S Khedar, Ajeet Bana, Samin Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess influence of echocardiographic left ventricular ejection fraction(LVEF) on outcomes following percutaneous coronary intervention(PCI) in ST-elevation myocardial infarction(STEMI) and other acute coronary syndromes(oACS).MethodsSuccessive patients with ACS who underwent PCI from April’2019 to March’2022 were enrolled, while those who did not undergo PCI were excluded. In all patients, LVEF was obtained at hospital admission and classified into <30.0%,30.0-39.9%,40.0-49.9% and ≥50.0%. Details of risk factors, coronary angiography, interventions, management, and in-hospital and 3-year outcomes were obtained. Multivariate Cox-proportionate hazard ratios(HR) and 95% confidence intervals(CI), adjusted for age, sex, risk factors, presentation, angiography, interventions, management, and hospitalisation, for major adverse cardiovascular events(MACE) and deaths were calculated.ResultsData of 3789 patients (men 3011, women 778) with ACS (STEMI = 1601; oACS = 2188) were available at baseline and 3.0yr (IQR 2.1-4.0) median follow-up. STEMI patients were younger, with more men, smoking, hypercholesterolemia, impaired LVEF, vasopressor support, hospitalisation duration, and in-hospital deaths vs oACS (p < 0.01). Important determinants of reduced LVEF were smoking, 3-vessel, left-main or left anterior descending CAD (p < 0.01). In both groups, patients with lower LVEF(<30.0%,30.0-39.9%) had more in-hospital deaths (p < 0.05). At 3-year follow-up, compared to LVEF >50.0%, multivariate-adjusted HR(95%CI) in LVEF<30.0% for MACE [STEMI 8.55(3.04-24.03); oACS 2.71(1.39-5.29)] as well as CV deaths [STEMI 16.50(3.64-74.71); oACS 3.72(1.40-9.85)] were significant (p < 0.001). For LVEF 30.0-39.9% group, HRs were of borderline significance [STEMI 2.26(0.86-5.89);oACS 1.34(0.76-2.36)] and not significant for LVEF 40.0-49.9% group. Outcomes were not significantly different in men and women.ConclusionsSTEMI and other ACS patients undergoing PCI with LVEF<30% have significantly higher in-hospital and 3-year major adverse cardiovascular events and deaths.Keywords: Acute coronary syndrome, Coronary artery disease, Left ventricular ejection fraction, Percutaneous coronary interventions, ST elevation myocardial infarction

Introduction

Reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is the most important marker of adverse cardiovascular outcomes following acute coronary syndromes (ACS).1 Multiple strategies to reduce adverse cardiovascular outcomes have been evaluated. In ACS, the interventions include primary percutaneous intervention (PCI), early coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery, drug therapies to reduce atherothrombosis, and cardioprotective medications.2,3 Primary PCI is associated with better outcomes in STEMI as well as in NSTEMI and other ACS.4 However, clinical trials of PCI in patients with reduced LVEF in ACS and chronic coronary syndromes (CCS) are inconclusive and large randomized controlled trials do not show significant benefit of PCI in patients with reduced LVEF as compared to optimal medical therapy.5, 6, 7

Previous reports from India have identified that both ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and other ACS patients have more extensive and multivessel disease than studies from developed countries.8, 9, 10 ACS registries from India have reported higher in-hospital and short-term-follow-up death rates in STEMI compared to NSTEMI.11, 12, 13 The time to intervention (symptom-to-door time or door-to-balloon time) is longer in patients in India as compared to the developed countries.14,15 This may have a bearing on outcomes following STEMI. There are limited studies in India regarding the in-hospital and medium-term influence of PCI on outcomes among STEMI, NSTEMI and other ACS patients.11, 12, 13,16,17 No study has evaluated the influence of PCI in reduced LVEF patients on in-hospital and long-term cardiovascular outcomes. As part of the American College of Cardiology-National Cardiovascular Database Registry (ACC-NCDR),18 we prospectively obtain data on all the patients who undergo PCI (CathPCI Registry) at our hospital.19 The present study has been performed to evaluate the in-hospital and long-term influence of PCI in patients with different levels of LVEF in patients with STEMI and other ACS (NSTEMI or unstable angina).

Methods

The Cath-PCI Registry at the hospital is part of the ACC-NCDR Centre of Excellence program.18 The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Government of India, CDSCO Registration No. ECR/615/Inst/RJ/2014/RR-20). Informed consent was obtained from each participant in the registry with specific permission for the inclusion of anonymized data.19 The protocol of the study is available at American College of Cardiology- National Cardiovascular Data Registry website: https://cvquality.acc.org/NCDR-Home/registries/hospital-registries/cathpci-registry.18

Patients: Successive patients undergoing PCI at our institution are registered in the CathPCI Registry. Patients with STEMI or oACS, where angiography or PCI was not performed, have not been included. For the present study, we included patients from April 2019 to March 2022 (n = 4045) to ensure a significant follow-up (Fig. 1). Clinical data were prospectively obtained from admission, at coronary intervention and discharge from the hospital and entered into the NCDR database by research assistants. Details of the methodology have been previously reported.19 Briefly, we obtained details regarding age and sex, cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors-hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemias, tobacco use, chronic kidney disease, other laboratory investigations, clinical presentation- ACS (STEMI or other ACS) and CCS. All the CCS patients have been excluded from the present analyses (Fig. 1). An echocardiographic evaluation was performed in the emergency room for all patients with ACS at admission, before any intervention, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was determined according to Simpson's formula. Time to presentation (pain-to-door time) and door-to-balloon time were recorded for STEMI. The location of myocardial infarction in STEMI patients was not recorded. We also recorded details of the arterial location of CAD and its extent (single, double or triple vessel disease) and the number of stents deployed. Almost all stents deployed (>99%) were drug-eluting. Details of in-hospital management with a specific focus on the use of pharmacological vasopressors (noradrenaline, dopamine, vasopressin, etc.), cardiac-support devices (intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP), miniature ventricular assist devices (MVAD) and post-discharge medications were recorded.

Flow chart of the acute coronary syndrome patients in the study.

Outcomes: In-hospital follow-up included duration of hospitalisation (hours) and all-cause and CVD deaths. We performed long-term follow-up of all the patients. In-person follow-up was recommended every 3 months, but due to the Covid-19 epidemic, many patients were not available. Telephonic outcome ascertainment using the validated methodology in previous studies at our centre.19 Outcomes were locally adjudicated by trained personnel. The co-primary outcomes in follow-up were: (i) major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), a composite of cardiovascular deaths, myocardial infarction, stroke, coronary revascularisation, CVD hospitalisation, or all-cause deaths; and (ii) cardiovascular deaths. Individual components of primary outcomes were specified as secondary outcomes.

Statistical analyses: All the data are available at the ACC-NCDR CathPCI registry website.18 The data used in the present study were downloaded from this site and transferred to MS Excel worksheets. CAD patients have been divided into two groups according to the presence of STEMI or other ACS. Data have also been tabulated according to sex for the whole study, but as the outcomes were almost identical for men and women, they are not reported in the main article. Supplementary files contain the sex-specific data. Data analyses have been performed using SPSS software. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± 1 SD and categorical variables as per cent. Inter-group comparisons have been performed using the t-test or ANOVA for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. Significant clinical determinants for reduced LVEF were calculated using logistic regression with LVEF group >50.0% as the comparator (odds ratio = 1). For outcomes data, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis have been performed and hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI calculated. To identify the importance of inter-group differences in various variables such as clinical presentation, pain-to-door time and door-to-balloon time for STEMI patients, angiographic findings, disease severity, clinical management, duration of hospitalisation, and in-hospital outcomes in successive years, we calculated unadjusted; age-sex adjusted; and multivariate-adjusted (age-sex, risk factors, presentation, LVEF, location and extent of CAD, number of coronary stents, in-hospital therapies including vascular support, and duration of hospitalisation) odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The LVEF group ≥50% was taken as the index (HR = 1.0). P values < 0.05 are considered significant.

Data availability: The data have been periodically uploaded at the ACC-NCDR CathPCI website and the full data are available at their website (https://cvquality.acc.org/NCDR-Home/registries/hospital-registries/cathpci-registry). Data are available only to researchers registered with ACC-NCDR and are available on reasonable request.

Results

ACC-NCDR CathPCI registry has been enrolling patients at this hospital since April 2018. For the present study, we included patients with any ACS recruited from April 2019 to March 2022 (n = 4020) to ensure at least a 12-month follow-up. Of these patients, 80 with CCS and 29 with missing LVEF data were excluded, and 122 were not available for follow-up (Fig. 1). Clinical data of the remaining 3789 patients (men 3011, women 778) with any ACS (STEMI n = 1601, other ACS n = 2188) are shown in Table 1. Baseline data for men and women with STEMI and other ACS are in Supplementary Table 1. This shows no significant sex-specific differences in demographic profile, risk factors, previous cardiovascular status, LVEF, extent of CAD, symptom-to-door and door-to-balloon times, number of coronary stents deployed, duration of hospitalisation, and discharge medications.

The baseline characteristics for the overall group (Table 1) show that patients with STEMI vs other ACS were younger (mean age 59.5 ± 11.3 vs 60.9 ± 10.6, p < 0.001) and had more smoking/tobacco use (12.4 vs 7.4%, p < 0.001), raised total cholesterol ≥170 mg/dl (49.1 vs 25.9%, p < 0.001) and non-HDL cholesterol >100 mg/dl (74.5 vs 50.6%, p < 0.001) with lower hypertension and diabetes. Symptom-to-door time was 7.3 h (IQR 3.3-13.1 h) in the overall cohort and was significantly lower in STEMI (5.9, 2.8-11.2 h) vs other ACS (9.8, 5.2-16.4 h) (p < 0.001). STEMI vs other ACS patients had lower mean LVEF (38.8 ± 7.5% vs 49.8 ± 9.3%, p < 0.001) with more patients in LVEF groups <30.0, 30.0-39.9 and 40.0-49.9% while other ACS patients were more in EF >50% group (p < 0.001). The extent of CAD was similar in the two groups, according to the number of vessels involved (p > 0.20). Single stent deployment was more in STEMI compared to other ACS patients (72.8 vs 63.8%, p = 0.010) while two or more stents were deployed more in other ACS patients (34.0 vs 23.4%, p < 0.001). STEMI patients required more pharmacological or mechanical vasopressor support, and the duration of hospitalisation was significantly greater than the other ACS patients. At discharge from the hospital, more than 97% of patients received dual antiplatelet therapy and statins and more than three-fourths received beta-blockers. Use of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) blockers was less than 50% in both STEMI and other ACS groups. In-hospital deaths were significantly more in STEMI (n = 44, 2.7%) compared to other ACS (n = 14, 0.6%) patients (p < 0.001). The in-hospital deaths were lower in men vs women (STEMI 2.4 vs 4.3%, other ACS 0.5 vs 1.0%), but these outcomes were not statistically different (p > 0.05).

The ACS patients were classified into 4 categories of LVEF (<30.0%, 30.0-39.9%, 40.0-49.9%, and ≥50.0%) and subcategorized into STEMI and other ACS patients. The number of women was small in lower LVEF categories, therefore, data in the overall groups are presented (Table 2). This shows the prevalence of risk factors, presentation details, clinical characteristics, angiographic findings and interventions in various LVEF categories. Table 2 shows that STEMI patients in all the LVEF groups have more tobacco use and higher total and non-HDL cholesterol. The median pain-to-door time in all ACS patients and door-to-balloon time in STEMI patients are not statistically different in various LVEF categories. The extent of CAD is also not statistically dissimilar across LVEF groups. Other ACS patients had slightly more left main CAD in higher LVEF groups. Patients with lower LVEF (<30.0% and 30.0-39.9%) had significantly greater involvement of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery (p < 0.05). STEMI patients with lower LVEF (<30.0% and 30.0-39.9%) required more vasopressor support and had a longer duration of hospitalisation. Following the hospital discharge, beta-blockers or RAS blockers were prescribed to fewer STEMI patients in the lowest LVEF group. Data for men and women in these four groups are in Supplementary Table 2. This shows that the prevalence of risk factors, clinical and angiographic findings across the LVEF groups is similar in men and women (Supplementary Table 2). In-hospital deaths were significantly more among STEMI patients compared to other ACS patients in the group with LVEF <30.0% (19.4 vs 1.3%, p < 0.001) and marginally higher in other LVEF groups (Table 2).

Discussion

Reduced left ventricular function is the most important marker of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in CAD. This has been known for decades.1,2 In the present study of a contemporary cohort of CAD patients with STEMI and other ACS who underwent PCI, we found that a reduced LVEF of <40% at hospital admission is associated with a higher incidence of in-hospital deaths. The 3-year follow-up data show significantly more major adverse cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in patients with LVEF <30%. These findings emphasise the need to develop other clinical interventions for low LVEF groups to reduce adverse cardiovascular events. In the present study, a low use of beta blockers and RAAS blockers at discharge was observed in categories with lower LVEF and could be an important confounding factor.

Several coronary intervention registries from the USA, Europe and East Asia have reported outcomes following PCI in STEMI and other acute coronary syndromes. Inohara et al compared trends and in-hospital outcomes in the NCDR data from the USA and J-PCI study in Japan.20 The PCI volume increased in NCDR-USA from 550872 in 2013 to 637650 in 2017 while in Japan it increased from 181750 to 247274 in corresponding years. In NCDR USA 81.2% PCI was performed for ACS while it was less than 40% in Japan. The observed annual in-hospital mortality rates were about 2% in USA and 0.8% in Japan, similar to the present study. Cimci et al compared outcomes and regional differences in practice in a worldwide registry involving 35389 patients.21 The deaths at one-year follow-up in South American patients were in 4.1% compared to 2.0% in Europe, Asia, and Africa. A larger study in China (n = 144659) among military veterans with ACS reported low in-hospital mortality (<1%).22 Data from other large registries in North America and Europe have reported outcomes similar to the present study in patients undergoing PCI.2, 3, 4,23 Low adverse outcomes are associated with higher use of evidence-based therapies in STEMI and other ACS patients as reported in SWEDEHEART registry.24 Recent registries from India and other Asian countries have also reported outcomes similar to the present study.14, 15, 16,25, 26, 27, 28 A higher annual mortality rate following PCI was reported in older studies in India.13,17 In the present study, while the overall deaths were similar in men and women, more women died in the lowest EF groups (Fig. 2). This is not explained by the delay in presentation or more severe CAD in this group of women (Supplementary Table 2). Left anterior descending (LAD) artery disease is slightly more common in women than men in low LVEF groups (Supplementary Table 2) but the numbers are small to be conclusive. In the present study, adverse cardiovascular events in the lowest LVEF group (<30%) persist for up to 3 years as shown by follow-up data after exclusion of in-hospital deaths These findings are similar to recent national and international registries where significantly greater mortality has been reported following PCI in low LVEF individuals early in the phase of presentation.2, 3, 4, 5, 6,27, 28, 29, 30, Randomized clinical trials of PCI versus coronary bypass surgery among low LVEF individuals have reported equivocal results with no benefit of either, compared to optimum medical management.2,5,7,31,32

Reduced LVEF is a well-known marker of adverse outcomes in CAD patients.33 In STEMI, reduction of LVEF is an acute event and is associated with large infarcts that in the short-term lead to malignant cardiac arrhythmias, acute left heart failure and death.33 On the other hand, in NSTEMI and other ACS the prevalence of reduced LVEF is due to a larger ischemic burden. In addition to reduced systolic function, an abnormal diastolic function may contribute to symptoms and higher deaths.33 In the present study the patients with lower LVEF had more left-main or LAD disease, this may contribute to a larger area of infarction and more adverse outcomes. We do not have data on left ventricular diastolic functions or natriuretic peptides and cannot comment on the presence of preserved ejection fraction heart failure (HFpEF). Data on serum troponin levels are also unavailable for all the patients; hence, we cannot comment on the size of myocardial damage in these patients. Greater hemodynamic instability and increased pharmacological and mechanical vasopressor support in patients with reduced LVEF in STEMI and other ACS suggest more severe CAD and explain the higher mortality (Table 2). In the present study, longer symptom-to-door time in STEMI and other ACS patients with reduced LVEF (Table 1) is an important finding and suggests that pre-hospital delay could be an important contributing factor for higher deaths in this group. The door-to-ballon time in patients with STEMI in various groups (Table 2) and in men and women (Supplementary Table 2) was not significantly different and does not explain the greater mortality in the reduced low LVEF patients.

Limitations of the study, other than those mentioned above, include the absence of details on the quality of risk factor control and dosages of various cardioprotective medications at admission and discharge. We do not have details of the social determinants of health that are important community and individual determinants of delays in clinical presentation (longer symptom-to-door time) as well as clinical outcomes.34 In a previous study from this database, we have reported that lack of health insurance is a significant determinant of adverse outcomes.19 Studies from India have reported greater cardiovascular deaths despite a lower prevalence of risk factors compared to high-income countries due to multiple social factors.35 This has been attributed to community-level social factors such as location, educational status, poverty as well as lower availability, affordability and accessibility to health care.36 We have no data on these variables and this is a study limitation. Lack of details of biomarkers of infarct size and cardiac function and very high event rates and deaths in those with low LVEF have already been discussed. Lower mortality in ACS with LVEF ≥40% compared to previous Indian registries,11, 12, 13, 14 could be due to the inclusion, in the present study, of patients with acute coronary syndromes undergoing PCI and exclusion of such patients who were hospitalised with STEMI and other ACS and did not undergo PCI. This was done to follow the protocol of the ACC-CathPCI registry,18 and could have contributed to the low in-hospital and long-term MACE and deaths compared to contemporary Indian registries. The external validity of the data can be, therefore, challenged. However, the mortality rates are similar to those in studies in Europe and North America.4,5,20,21 Only a small number of patients underwent coronary bypass surgery at follow-up. Randomised trials have shown no benefit of this strategy in improving prognosis.5, 6, 7 Important interventions to improve prognosis in patients with reduced LVEF include early initiation of optimal medical therapy, including dual anti-platelet therapy, beta-blockers, RAAS blockers and SGLT-2 inhibitors.1,2 In the present study the use of dual antiplatelets and statins was high. Use of beta-blockers and RAS blockers (including ARNIs) was lower in those with reduced EF and no details of use of SGLT-2 inhibitors are available. The drug therapy could have changed over the follow-up period, and the non-availability of these data is also an important study limitation. High rates of follow-up (97%) and consistency of outcomes across various groups suggest high internal validity of the data and are strengths of the study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study shows a high mortality in a select group of patients (post-PCI) with STEMI and other ACS with reduced LVEF at admission compared to normal LVEF groups. Randomised trials of PCI or coronary artery bypass surgery to improve prognosis in such patients are mostly inconclusive and require patient-specific interventions.2,3,6 Guidelines have suggested that adherence to long-term cardioprotective drug therapies, including anti-platelets, statins, beta-blockers, RAS blockers (including ARNIs), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors in low LVEF patients improves prognosis.2,5 Large randomised trials of lifestyle interventions, including cardiac rehabilitation and yoga; appropriate and long-term use of existing and novel pharmacological agents, and emerging non-pharmacological approaches, are required to alleviate the major adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with reduced LVEF following acute coronary syndromes.37
