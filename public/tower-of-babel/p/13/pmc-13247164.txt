Genomic diversity, functional differentiation and prophage dynamics in Lactiplantibacillus paraplantarum
Authors: Ruirui Lv, Wenxin Ma, Yan Lei, Mengjun Cui, Xia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lactiplantibacillus (L.) paraplantarum is widely distributed in fermented foods and the gastrointestinal tract of mammals. However, its genomic diversity, evolutionary relationships, functional potential, and ecological adaptation strategies remain poorly understood. In this study, 42 genomes [isolated in this study (n = 20) + retrieved from NCBI (n = 22)] were analyzed to characterize species-level genomic structure, metabolic versatility, and prophage diversity. Pan-genome analysis revealed an open pan-genome with extensive accessory gene content. Phylogenomic and Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analyses showed that strains clustered by clade rather than by isolation habitat. KEGG and CAZy databases indicated enrichment in carbohydrate metabolism and core glycoside hydrolase families, consistent with a fermentative lifestyle, with distinct clade-associated functional signatures. Prophage ANI analysis demonstrated lineage-structured phage diversity closely aligned with host phylogeny. Prophage-associated functional genes also exhibited clade-specific distribution patterns. Phenotypic validation of selected strains exhibited pronounced condition-dependent phenotypic variation, with carbohydrate source, environmental pH, and simulated gastrointestinal stress significantly influencing acid production, growth performance, and survival in a strain-specific manner. This study provides a genomic and phenotypic framework for understanding clade-associated diversification in L. paraplantarum and supports its application in fermentation and probiotic development.

Introduction

Lactiplantibacillus (L.) paraplantarum is a Gram-positive lactic acid bacterium widely distributed across various ecological niches, including sauerkraut, dairy products, plant surfaces, and animal intestines1. Previous studies have demonstrated that L. paraplantarum exhibits diverse functional properties. During fermentation, it produces a range of bioactive metabolites, including lactic acid, antioxidant peptides, and short-chain fatty acids2. In addition, L. paraplantarum has been reported to enhance intestinal barrier function, modulate immune responses, and degrade harmful compounds such as cholesterol and bile salts in the gastrointestinal tract, thereby contributing to host digestive health3,4.

Beyond its probiotic potential, L. paraplantarum plays a significant role in improving the nutritional and sensory quality of fermented foods. For example, Huang et al.5 isolated L. paraplantarum M23 from Gannan navel oranges and showed that its fermentation significantly increased vitamin C, total phenolics, total flavonoids, and hesperidin in navel orange juice while enhancing antioxidant and anti-glycation activities. Anumudu et al.6 found that L. paraplantarum can ferment to produce gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) under anaerobic conditions, thereby conferring probiotic properties such as anti-depressive, anxiolytic, and antihypertensive effects to foods. Additionally, L. paraplantarum possesses bacteriocin-producing capabilities, which can effectively inhibit the growth of harmful bacteria in food7. Therefore, L. paraplantarum is regarded as a promising candidate strain for the development of functional foods and probiotic formulations, gradually becoming a focal point in the fields of microbiology and food science8.

With the rapid advancement of genome sequencing technologies, an increasing number of lactic acid bacterial genomes have become publicly available. Researchers found that even within a single species of lactic acid bacteria, strains isolated from different ecological niches exhibit substantial variation in genome content and functional potential9. For example, Lactiplantibacillus (L.) plantarum strains from pickles, fermented sauces, and human feces harbor distinct sets of unique genes, suggesting niche-dependent genetic variation10. Similarly, Limosilactobacillus pontis strains isolated from fermented milk versus other sources cluster separately in phylogenomic analyses and differ in the presence of carbohydrate-active enzymes and bacteriocin loci, indicating functional divergence linked to ecological origin11. This genomic diversity has been found to correlate with their functional, including the production capacity of organic acids, exopolysaccharides, and antimicrobial peptides. However, the genomic diversity and its functional implications in L. paraplantarum remain insufficiently characterized. Therefore, a comprehensive comparative genomic analysis of L. paraplantarum strains from diverse sources is essential to elucidate patterns of genomic diversity and their potential links to ecological adaptation.

Moreover, many previous genomic studies have primarily focused on evolutionary relationships or descriptive genomic diversity, with limited integration of genomic features and food-relevant functional traits. In the context of food science, genome-based predictions—such as KEGG pathway reconstruction and CAZyme profiling—should be interpreted in relation to fermentation performance and substrate utilization in specific food matrices. They should also be considered in relation to stress tolerance under processing conditions (e.g., acidity, osmotic pressure, and temperature), metabolite production, and potential contributions to sensory and nutritional quality. A systematic comparison of these functional genes across strains from diverse sources is therefore essential to clarify how genomic variation translates into practical performance in food systems.

In this study, we analyzed a total of 42 L. paraplantarum genomes, including 22 genomes retrieved from NCBI and 20 newly sequenced isolates. By integrating phylogenetic reconstruction, pan-genome analysis, and functional gene annotation, we aimed to elucidate the genomic diversity of this species and uncover the adaptive differences associated with distinct ecological niches. Identify niche-associated functional genes related to carbohydrate metabolism, stress resistance, and other traits relevant to food fermentation, and explore the potential implications of these genomic features for performance in food matrices and processing environments. These findings provide a valuable foundation for advancing our understanding of the ecological adaptation mechanisms of L. paraplantarum and for exploring its potential applications in food fermentation and probiotic development.

Phylogenetic analysis

To better understand the evolutionary relationships among L. paraplantarum strains, a core-gene phylogenetic tree was constructed (Fig. 3A). The results indicated that L. paraplantarum could be categorized into four clades: Clade1 (n = 7), Clade2 (n = 10), Clade3 (n = 4), and Clade4 (n = 17). L. paraplantarum strains from Asia were primarily found in Clades 1 and 4, while those from Africa were mainly in Clades 3 and 4. Notably, the strains did not group according to their isolation habitat. Instead, they were randomly distributed across the clades. Clustering analysis based on whole-genome ANI (Fig. 3B) was consistent with the phylogenetic inference. The ANI values among the isolates ranged from 96% to 100%, indicating that all 42 genomes belong to the same species. Functional annotation

The metabolic capacities of bacterial strains are closely linked to their ability to adapt to diverse environments. Therefore, KEGG annotation was employed to characterize the metabolic functions of L. paraplantarum strains (Fig. 4A). KEGG annotation identified a total of 54 metabolic pathways. Among them, “Protein families: genetic information processing” contained the largest number of annotated genes, with an average of 541 ± 2.61, highlighting the critical role of this module in cellular metabolic activity and environmental adaptation12. This was followed by “Protein families: signaling and cellular processes”, which contained an average of 492 ± 3.75 genes, underscoring the central role of this module in cellular signaling and information-processing networks12. Moreover, the “Carbohydrate metabolism” pathway contained an average of 254 ± 2.26 genes, reflecting its role in supporting carbohydrate utilization. Pathways related to amino acid metabolism, energy metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and lipid metabolism comprised an average of 166 ± 0.83, 90 ± 0.55, 88 ± 0.51, and 74 ± 0.51 genes, respectively. The “Membrane transport” pathway contained an average of 160 ± 1.01 genes, underscoring its importance in maintaining intracellular–extracellular homeostasis and precisely regulating the transport of molecules across the cell membrane13. These pathways collectively play essential roles in cellular metabolism by ensuring the proper breakdown and turnover of amino acids, proteins, and lipids, participating in glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, fermentation, and DNA/RNA synthesis and repair. Based on the Venn diagram (Fig. 4D), the numbers of identified KEGG Ortholog (KO) gene families in Clades 1–4 were 1461, 1398, 1422, and 1600, respectively. When comparing strains from different isolation sources (Fig. 4C), PCoA showed a significant separation between dairy-derived and fermented plant-derived strains (R2 = 0.159, P = 0.002), with dairy strains displaying a more dispersed distribution and fermented plant strains forming a tighter cluster. The total number of KO gene families was comparable (1515 in dairy strains and 1491 in fermented plant strains), and most gene families (n = 1441) were shared, indicating a highly consistent overall genomic framework. Nevertheless, each source harbored a small set of source-specific gene families (74 in dairy strains and 50 in fermented plant strains), suggesting limited but detectable functional differentiation associated with ecological niches.

Genomic diversity and functional features of prophages

By deeply analyzing the genomic sequences of 42 L. paraplantarum strains, this study aimed to systematically reveal the distribution characteristics of prophages in this species and their potential implications (Table S2). Phage-related sequences were detected in 97.6% (41/42) of the strain genomes. A complete prophage was identified only in L. paraplantarum 20-19, which belongs to Clade 2. To evaluate the genetic relatedness and infer the evolutionary patterns of predicted prophages, ANI analysis was performed on prophage genomes identified from 42 L. paraplantarum strains (Fig. 6). The ANI values exhibited a wide distribution, with most prophages showing moderate or low pairwise similarity and only a few forming small clusters with high ANI. Hierarchical clustering revealed that these prophages could be grouped into several relatively distinct clusters, within which ANI values were often close to 100%, whereas similarities between clusters were markedly lower. This pattern indicates substantial genomic diversification and clear subgroup structure among the prophages. Notably, ANI were independent of isolation source, as prophages from different habitats frequently displayed high similarity, suggesting extensive cross-strain and cross-environmental mobility in natural settings. In contrast, high-ANI prophages corresponded closely to the bacterial clade structure: in most cases, genomes within the same clade shared the same branch grouping and showed ANI values above 95%, with only a few exceptions. Overall, these findings demonstrate pronounced prophage genomic diversity, with ANI-based clustering largely mirroring host phylogenetic clades but showing little association with the ecological origins of the host strains.Fig. 6Hierarchical clustering and pairwise ANI among prophage genomes.The heatmap shows pairwise ANI values (%) between prophage genomes, with color intensity indicating the level of genomic similarity.

In addition, prophage-associated functional genes related to metabolic capacity, stress response, and potential adaptive traits were summarized (Fig. S2). Clade1 was mainly associated with functions involved in carbohydrate metabolism and transport, such as phosphotransferase systems (PTS), glycosyl hydrolases, and glycosyl transferases. In addition, genes related to ion transport and oxidative stress response, including sodium-proton antiporter and glutathione reductase, were also detected. Clade2 exhibited a relatively diverse functional repertoire. In addition to carbohydrate metabolism–related genes (e.g., PTS, phosphoglycerate mutase, and alpha-amylase), several genes associated with stress response and transcriptional regulation, such as MarR family regulators, cold shock proteins, and glutathione reductase, were frequently observed. Moreover, genes linked to cell envelope and surface structure, including Asp23 family proteins and LPXTG-motif cell wall anchor proteins, were also detected, indicating potential roles in host cell envelope stability and interactions with the environment. Clade 3 showed a comparatively narrower functional distribution. Clade4 displayed a broad spectrum of predicted functions, particularly genes associated with stress adaptation and cell envelope maintenance, including universal stress proteins, small heat shock proteins (HSP20), cold shock proteins, and Asp23 family proteins. Overall, prophage functional genes showed a clear clade-associated distribution.

Discussion

L. paraplantarum has attracted considerable attention due to its metabolic versatility and probiotic properties. It is known to improve the flavor, texture, and nutritional value of fermented foods, produce bioactive peptides, modulate the gut microbiota, and enhance immune function. Nevertheless, studies on its genomic diversity and functional characteristics remained limited. Previous studies demonstrated that L. paraplantarum strains from different habitats exhibited significant differences in growth characteristics, metabolic capacity, and environmental adaptability18,19. These differences were likely associated with variations in the distribution and regulation of functional genes within their genomes. In this study, 20 strains of L. paraplantarum were isolated from dairy and fermented product. Together with 22 strain genomes from NCBI, these strains were used to analyze the species’ genomic diversity and functional gene distribution patterns, revealing potential mechanisms underlying gene-level differences. The pan-genome curve exhibited an upward trend with a power-law exponent of 0.26, indicating that L. paraplantarum possessed an open pan-genome. This suggested that the inclusion of additional genomes would likely lead to the identification of more novel genes, thereby increasing genomic diversity and highlighting the presence of numerous strain-specific genes. This interpretation was further supported by the power-law exponent (−0.59) of the new gene curve. This pattern is likely linked to ecological differences associated with the origins of the strains. Isolates from distinct sources or geographic locations may require specialized adaptive functions—such as carbohydrate utilization, stress response capacity, or interactions with hosts and surrounding microbiota—to survive and proliferate effectively within their respective ecological niches20.

Despite this extensive accessory gene pool, the overall genome architecture of L. paraplantarum appears relatively constrained. Genome sizes and GC contents showed only modest variation among strains. This indicated that L. paraplantarum maintain a stable core while tuning niche performance through relatively small sets of accessory loci rather than wholesale genome remodeling16. Larger and more variable genomes in African strains may reflect the accumulation of accessory genes associated with a broader or more heterogeneous range of habitats. In contrast, the more compact genomes observed in Asian isolates could indicate genome streamlining in more specialized or relatively stable environments. However, the overall similarity in genome sizes and mean GC contents across isolation sources (dairy, fermented plant, fermented meat) suggests that adaptation to different isolation sources is not primarily mediated by large-scale changes in genome size or base composition.

Bacteria often exhibit substantial intraspecific diversity driven by ecological selection and horizontal gene transfer21. In L. plantarum, large-scale comparative genomics has repeatedly shown a “nomadic” nomadic lifestyle: core-genome phylogeny is only weakly associated with isolation habitat, whereas phenotypic divergence is largely mediated by accessory gene repertoires19. For example, Martino et al.19 analyzed over 50 L. plantarum genomes and found that strains from fermented foods, human gut, and plant materials were interspersed across phylogenetic clades rather than forming habitat-specific clusters. Li et al22. reported that differences in carbohydrate utilization capacity among L. plantarum strains are primarily associated with variability in accessory gene content, particularly genes encoding sugar transport and carbohydrate metabolism functions. In our dataset, L. paraplantarum similarly did not cluster by isolation habitat, and all strains showed high species-level coherence (ANI > 96.95%). These observations suggest that, like L. plantarum, L. paraplantarum conforms to a broadly nomadic population framework. However, whether functional diversification within this species is randomly distributed or structured along phylogenetic lineages requires further resolution at the pathway and gene-family levels.

Lactic acid bacteria optimized their growth strategies by modulating key metabolic pathways19. KEGG annotation revealed that each clade contained a large number of genes involved in “Carbohydrate metabolism” and “Energy metabolism”. Specifically, enrichment in “Carbohydrate metabolism” pathways indicates an enhanced capacity for the uptake, transport, and breakdown of diverse carbohydrates into central metabolic intermediates (e.g., glucose-6-phosphate and pyruvate), thereby supplying carbon substrates and reducing equivalents for downstream energy-generating processes23. Meanwhile, enrichment in “Energy metabolism” pathways reflects a stronger genetic potential for ATP generation and redox balance maintenance through processes such as glycolysis-linked fermentation pathways, and other electron transfer reactions24. These pathways are directly responsible for converting metabolic intermediates into usable cellular energy25. Similar clade- or lineage-specific enrichments of carbohydrate and energy metabolism genes have been reported in comparative genomic analyses of other LAB, where they have been linked to fine-scale adaptation to distinct food matrices or host-associated niches26.

This study identified 34 CAZymes that were annotated in all 42 L. paraplantarum strains, suggesting that these enzymes were essential for the basic carbohydrate metabolism and survival of the species. The substantial enrichment of carbohydrate-metabolism pathways underscores the strongly fermentative lifestyle of L. paraplantarum, consistent with the central role of sugar catabolism in lactic acid bacteria25. Moreover, significant differences in the types and numbers of CAZymes were observed across different clades and isolation sources, with certain clades annotated with unique CAZymes. For example, in Clade1, CE2, carbohydrate esterase, was primarily responsible for hydrolyzing ester bonds in glycosidic compounds and was involved in the degradation of plant cell walls27. CBM67, a carbohydrate-binding module, primarily bound to specific carbohydrates such as glycoside hydrolases, enhancing the enzyme’s substrate affinity and thereby improving its catalytic efficiency28. The remaining enzymes belonged to the Glycoside Hydrolase family, each responsible for hydrolyzing different glycosidic bonds and involved in carbohydrate metabolism or the degradation of plant cell walls29. GH70 and GT14 were annotated in Clade3. GH70 enzymes are glucansucrases that catalyze the transfer of glucosyl units from sucrose to produce α-glucans with diverse α-glucosidic linkages. These enzymes play an essential role in the biosynthesis of exopolysaccharides (EPS), which are directly associated with texture modification, viscosity enhancement, and stability improvement in fermented foods30. GT14 glycosyltransferases primarily catalyze the transfer of galactosyl residues to form β-1,4-glycosidic linkages, playing an essential role in the extension and modification of glycoprotein and glycolipid glycans31. Additionally, GH13_3 was annotated in Clade4 and is commonly found in lactic acid bacteria isolated from fermented plants. This enzyme hydrolyzes α-1,4-glycosidic bonds and exhibits α-amylase activity. It participates in the degradation of starch and other α-glucans, thereby potentially conferring an advantage in starch-rich substrates such as cereal fermentations32. Previous studies have shown that differences in the repertoire and abundance of CAZymes are closely linked to microbial adaptation to specific nutrient environments, particularly in the human gut microbiota9,33. Duar et al.34 found that different lineages within the genus Lactobacillus underwent extensive selective gene loss during evolution, with some lineages losing genes related to complex carbohydrate metabolism, revealing metabolic diversity. This indicated the role of evolutionary adaptation and genetic diversity in shaping the metabolic capabilities of bacterial species across different environments. These findings provided important references for further investigation into their ecological functions, metabolic mechanisms, and potential applications.

The PCoA and Venn analyses provide additional insight into how this functional potential is organized within the species. The strong segregation of clades at the gene-content level (R2 = 0.528) combined with a large set of shared KO families (1338 core functions), indicates that L. paraplantarum maintains a highly conserved core functional genome upon which clade-specific accessory gene sets are superimposed. By contrast, the much lower R2 for separation by isolation source (0.159) and the high overlap in KO gene families between dairy and fermented-plant isolates (1441 shared families) argue that ecological origin has driven limited functional divergence. This agrees with large-scale genomic surveys showing that closely related LAB lineages can circulate between food and host environments while retaining a broadly similar functional profile. Niche specialization appears to be mediated by relatively small sets of accessory genes rather than by wholesale remodeling of the genomic framework19,34,35. Together, these findings portray L. paraplantarum as a genetically cohesive but functionally structured species. A conserved metabolic core supports clade-specific adjustments in information processing, signaling, and energy metabolism, which likely underlie its successful adaptation to diverse habitats.

ANI provides an effective metric for distinguishing closely related phage genomes and for capturing genomic divergence among different lineages36. The ANI analysis of the predicted prophage genomes indicates that L. paraplantarum harbors a highly diverse phage population that is structured into a few relatively coherent genomic lineages rather than a single homogeneous pool. The predominance of low-to-intermediate ANI values, with only small clusters of phages showing very high identity, is consistent with the well-documented pattern of bacteriophage diversity. In such systems, closely related “species-like” lineages coexist with a broad halo of more divergent genomes. These high-ANI clusters likely represent recently diverged or actively exchanging phage lineages, whereas the numerous more distant relationships reflect the strong modularity, recombination, and long-term diversification that characterize phage evolution. The fact that ANI-based clustering aligns well with host phylogenetic clades but shows little correspondence with isolation source suggests that prophage populations are shaped more by host lineage history than by the immediate ecological habitat. Similar patterns have been reported in other bacteria, where prophages make lineage-specific contributions to the accessory genome and track host phylogeny more closely than geography or niche labels37,38. In this study, phages associated with the same L. paraplantarum clade tend to share branch color and maintain ANI values above 95%, indicating tight co-evolution within particular host lineages, with only a few exceptions. Together, these results portray the prophage complement of L. paraplantarum as genomically diverse and structured into host-linked lineages, with ANI-defined phage largely mirroring host phylogeny while remaining decoupled from sample source.

Prophages are commonly integrated into the genomes of lactic acid bacteria. Their induction may lead to cell lysis and consequently pose a risk of fermentation failure. Prophage-associated genes, including those involved in adaptation or metabolic enhancement, may contribute to host fitness and serve as important drivers of environmental adaptation39. The functional profile of Clade1 suggests that prophages in this clade may contribute to host metabolic activity and environmental adaptation. Genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism and transport, such as PTS, glycosyl hydrolases, and glycosyl transferases, may facilitate efficient utilization of available carbohydrates40. In addition, genes related to ion transport and oxidative stress response, including sodium proton antiporters and glutathione reductase, may help maintain cellular homeostasis under stressful environmental conditions. Clade2 contained diverse genes associated with metabolism, stress response, and surface-associated functions. This suggests that strains from this clade may possess enhanced resilience to fermentation-related stresses, such as acidification and salt exposure, during food processing. For example, genes encoding Na+/H⁺ antiporters and cold shock proteins may help maintain cellular homeostasis and metabolic activity under fluctuating temperature or osmotic conditions41. At the same time, the presence of PTS-mediated sugar transport systems and carbohydrate metabolism–related enzymes, such as phosphoglycerate mutase and α-amylase, may facilitate efficient utilization of available carbohydrates and rapid initiation of acid production in complex fermentation environments42. Clade 4 was characterized by the prominence of genes associated with stress response and cell envelope homeostasis. For example, universal stress proteins, small heat shock proteins (HSP20), cold shock proteins, and members of the Asp23 family were detected in this clade. These genes are commonly linked to bacterial survival under adverse environmental conditions43, suggesting that prophages in Clade 4 may potentially contribute to enhanced environmental tolerance of their bacterial hosts.

To validate the functional predictions derived from comparative genomics, some strains were subjected to phenotypic assays. The carbohydrate-dependent acidification profiles provide functional evidence that clade-level genomic divergence translates into measurable fermentation performance. Robust acidification on glucose across all strains suggests that core glycolysis and primary lactic fermentation routes are highly conserved in L. paraplantarum, consistent with the shared “core” carbohydrate metabolism inferred from comparative genomics. By contrast, the uniformly weaker acidification on galactose points to plausible differences in galactose uptake, indicating that galactose may be a suboptimal substrate for rapid acidification in this species under the tested conditions. A strong strain-to-strain contrast was observed in lactose acidification. In particular, the Clade2 isolate L. paraplantarum 15–27 exhibited markedly reduced lactose acidification relative to its performance on glucose, suggesting that variation in lactose-specific modules can constrain acidification even within the same clade and highlighting an important layer of intra-clade functional heterogeneity44. Overall, integrating clade-guided strain selection with substrate-specific acidification assays strengthens the genotype–phenotype link and provides a practical framework for choosing L. paraplantarum starters tailored to process-relevant carbohydrate matrices.

The selected L. paraplantarum strains share a relatively narrow tolerance for strong acid stress under the tested conditions, with near-complete growth arrest at pH 2–4 suggesting that severe proton stress overwhelms baseline homeostasis systems. The limited growth at pH 5 implies that moderately acidic environments remain restrictive. In contrast, robust growth at pH 7–9 points to effective pH homeostasis and membrane/ion-transport capacity in neutral–alkaline environments. Moreover, the higher final biomass of the Clade1 L. paraplantarum 671 and the Clade4 L. paraplantarum 20-3-1 suggests that distinct phylogenetic backgrounds can converge on strong performance in processing-relevant neutral–alkaline niches45. Taken together, these results suggesting that clade-informed selection can capture strains with different growth strategies across pH regimes, enabling targeted use as starters depending on whether a process prioritizes rapid early dominance or higher biomass/robust persistence during fermentation.

All strains suffered substantial losses during the simulated gastrointestinal transit, indicating that stress tolerance is a major differentiating trait among clade-selected L. paraplantarum isolates. The superior gastric survival of the Clade1 L. paraplantarum 671 suggests more effective acid/bile-associated protection mechanisms46. After intestinal treatment, the Clade4 L. paraplantarum 20-9-2 maintained the highest viable counts, pointing to enhanced tolerance to intestinal stresses, whereas the Clade2 L. paraplantarum 15–27 showed the weakest survival, underscoring intra-species functional heterogeneity among strains selected from different clades. Overall, these data provide a practical basis for tailoring strain choice to food or probiotic applications.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The strain collection size was limited, and most isolates were obtained from specific ecological niches, which may restrict broader ecological inference. In addition, functional predictions were primarily based on genomic annotation, and only a subset of strains was experimentally validated.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive genomic characterization of L. paraplantarum, revealing that the species possesses an open pan-genome, substantial accessory gene diversity, and a highly conserved functional core. Although genome size and GC content varied only modestly across strains, clear phylogenetic structure emerged, with four major clades displaying distinct functional signatures, particularly in energy metabolism and CAZyme repertoires. Prophage ANI analysis further demonstrated that prophage diversity mirrors host phylogeny more closely than isolation source. Prophage-associated functional genes displayed clear clade-specific patterns. Selected strains showed clear condition-dependent and strain-specific differences in acid production, growth under different pH conditions, and tolerance to simulated gastrointestinal stress. Together, these findings portray L. paraplantarum as a genetically cohesive yet functionally structured species. The functional gene differences identified lay a foundation for the future development of targeted functional strains and the optimization of fermentation applications.

Methods

Isolates and Genomes of L. paraplantarum

A total of 42 L. paraplantarum genomes were analyzed in this study, including 20 strains preserved in the National Feed Microbial Germplasm Resource Bank (Inner Mongolia) and 22 publicly available genomes retrieved from NCBI. All genomes selected from NCBI exhibited completeness values greater than 90% and contamination levels below 5%. Detailed information on the strains is provided in Table S3.

DNA preparation and sequencing

The bacterial strains were inoculated into MRS broth and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by subculturing at a 2% (v/v) inoculation rate. The third-generation cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted by using the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, USA)47. The purity, integrity, and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a UV spectrophotometer, agarose gel electrophoresis, and a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer. The qualified genomic DNA was subsequently sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). All sequencing procedures were performed at Novogene Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China.

Genome assembly

Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp to generate high-confidence clean sequences. The filtered reads were assembled using SPAdes v3.14.1 with the parameters “-careful, -cov-cutoff auto, -k 21,33,55,77,99”48. The quality of the genome assemblies was assessed using CheckM v1.1.349. Genomes with completeness greater than 90% and contamination below 5% were retained for downstream analyses.

Pan- genome and core-genome analysis

Pan- and core-genomes analyses were performed using the Roary pipeline (v3.11.2) at a 90% identity threshold50. Good’s coverage was employed to assess the number of strain-specific genes detected within each genome, and was calculated using the following formula:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C=1-\frac{{n}_{1}}{N}$$\end{document}C=1−n1N

C: gene coverage;

n1: The number of genes present in a single strain;

N: total number of gene occurrences.

Average nucleotide identity and phylogenetic analysis

FastANI v1.33 was used to calculate the ANI between genome pairs, with an alignment fraction of at least 60%. For phylogenetic analysis, Lactobacillus gallinarum DSM 10532T was used as the outgroup strain. Phylogenetic reconstruction was carried out using the maximum-likelihood program RAxML-NG v0.9.0 with a GTR + G model and 1000 bootstrap iterations of evolution. The phylogenetic tree was visualized using iTOL v7 (https://itol.embl.de/)36.

Genome annotation

Genomic annotation was implemented using the Prokka v1.14.551. The KEGG database using usearch (v.11.0.667_i86linux32) with -usearch_local -id 0.3 -query_cov 0.7 options52. The carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were annotated by the dbCAN2 database using HMMER v3.3.253.

ANI analysis and genomic functional annotation of prophage

CheckV (v1.0.1) was employed to identify phage-derived sequences within the bacterial genomes and to evaluate their completeness. The completeness of each predicted phage was estimated based on either average amino acid identity (AAI) or hidden Markov model (HMM)–based approaches. Integrity assessment range: High (≥90%), Medium (80–90%), or Low (<80%)54. In CheckV, phage sequences are classified as medium-quality, high-quality, or complete. Phage fragments with a completeness of ≥ 80% were considered intact phages. MAFFT (version 7.505; –auto) was employed to align the prophage sequences included in the analysis, ensuring consistency across all alignments55. FastANI (v1.34) was used to calculate pairwise ANI values36. Self-comparisons of prophage genomes automatically yielded 100% ANI, while values below the 60% threshold between two prophages were designated as NA56. Visualization of the results was performed using the pheatmap package (v1.0.12). Coding regions within prophage genomes were identified using Prodigal v2.6.357. The resulting protein sequences were functionally characterized and categorized against the eggNOG database58. Functional assignments were determined based on the top-scoring alignments between query sequences and database entries, applying an E-value cutoff of 105 59.

Acidification capacity of strains under different carbon source conditions

Based on the KEGG and CAZymes results described above, selected strains were chosen for experimental validation. The acidification capacity of the strain under different carbohydrate conditions was assessed by measuring the decline in culture pH during incubation60. A carbohydrate-free basal medium was individually supplemented with glucose, galactose, or lactose at a final concentration of 2% (w/v). The media were sterilized and cooled to room temperature prior to inoculation. Strains were inoculated into different media at a final concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL and incubated at 37 °C. The pH of each culture was measured at designated time points. Acidification ability was expressed as the decrease in pH (ΔpH = pH0 − pHt), where pH0 represents the initial pH and pHt represents the pH at a given time point. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Effects of different pH conditions on strain growth

To evaluate the effect of environmental pH on bacterial growth dynamics, growth curves were determined under different pH conditions. Briefly, culture media were adjusted to the desired pH values. Strains were inoculated at a final concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL. Aliquots (200 μL) were transferred into sterile 96-well microplates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in an automated growth analyzer (Bioscreen C, Oy Growth Curves Ab, Finland). Optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was recorded at 1 h intervals.

Survival of strains in simulated gastric and intestinal fluids

The tolerance of the strain to simulated gastrointestinal conditions was evaluated using a simplified in vitro assay as previously described with minor modifications61. Briefly, overnight cultures were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended to approximately 108 CFU/mL. For the simulated gastric fluid (SGF) treatment, cells were inoculated into sterile saline containing 0.3% (w/v) pepsin and adjusted to pH 2.0, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Samples were collected at 0 and 2 h, serially diluted, and plated on appropriate agar for viable cell enumeration. For the simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) treatment, cells were transferred into sterile saline containing 0.3% (w/v) bile salts and 1 mg/mL pancreatin at pH 8.0 and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Viable counts were determined as described above. Survival was expressed as log10 (CFU/mL) and calculated relative to the initial cell count. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis and data visualization

Violin plots, Venn diagrams, frequency histograms, principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and statistical analyses were visualized using R software (version 4.0.3). Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to check whether the data conformed to the normal distribution. Our dataset was not normally distributed. Thus, non-parametric statistical analyses, such as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, were performed in this study to evaluate differences between sample groups. Based on the insights obtained from the PCoA results, we further employed the PERMANOVA method to rigorously test whether there were significant differences in the quantity and composition of functional features among different sample groups. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05, indicating a significant difference between the two. All visualizations were further edited using Adobe Illustrator CC2020.
