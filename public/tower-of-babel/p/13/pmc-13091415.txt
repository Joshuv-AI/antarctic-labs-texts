Plasma-derived small extracellular vesicle miR-660-5p as a predictor for carotid plaque vulnerability and postoperative major adverse cardiovascular event risk
Authors: Xin Xu, Taoyuan Lu, Dechao Wang, Yuanyuan Ji, Yabing Wang, Peng Gao, Bin Yang, Yan Ma, Yanfei Chen, Jian Chen, Dianwei Liu, Yanxin Zhao, Lebin Chen, Liqun Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBuilding on our previous isolation of plaque-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) from carotid atherosclerotic stenosis (CAS) patients, this study aimed to identify plasma sEV-encapsulated miRNAs that reflect plaque vulnerability and predict clinical outcomes, based on differentially expressed miRNAs identified in sEVs from stable versus vulnerable plaques.MethodsCAS patients were retrospectively enrolled into four complementary cohorts: (1) a sequencing cohort (n = 12) for miRNA profiling of plaque-derived sEVs; (2) a discovery cohort (n = 62) to identify candidate plasma-derived sEV miRNAs associated with plaque vulnerability; and (3) validation cohort 1 (n = 180; endarterectomy cases) and (4) validation cohort 2 (n = 326; stenting cases) for validating the diagnostic efficacy of the identified plasma-derived sEV miRNAs. Candidate miRNAs were quantified using a High-throughput nano-bio chip integrated system (HNCIB). Their associations with plaque vulnerability and 3-year post-endarterectomy major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were analyzed.ResultsMiRNA sequencing revealed significant upregulation of miR-660-5p in vulnerable plaque-derived sEVs. Its expression levels showed a significant positive correlation between matched plaque- and plasma-derived sEVs from the same patient. Moreover, plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p levels were independently associated with carotid plaque vulnerability in both validation cohorts, defined either by histopathological criteria (endarterectomy cases) or the clinically validated Plaque-Reporting and Data System (RADS) criteria (stenting cases). Additionally, elevated plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p levels were also associated with a higher risk of 3-year post-endarterectomy MACE.ConclusionPlasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p represents a promising non-invasive biomarker for assessing carotid plaque vulnerability and predicting 3-year MACE risk following carotid endarterectomy, offering potential for improved risk stratification and therapeutic targeting.Keywords: Carotid atherosclerotic stenosis, Extracellular vesicles, microRNA, Plaque vulnerability, Major adverse cardiovascular event

Introduction

Carotid atherosclerotic stenosis (CAS), accounting for approximately 15-20% of ischemic stroke causes, is primarily managed according to clinical guidelines emphasizing luminal narrowing severity and symptomatic presentation [1,2]. Growing evidence, however, suggests that plaque vulnerability features critically contribute to both initial pathogenesis and recurrent cerebrovascular events, challenging the conventional heavy reliance on stenosis severity for therapeutic decisions [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Pathologically, vulnerable plaques are characterized by features including a thin or ruptured fibrous cap, a large lipid-rich necrotic core (LRNC), intraplaque inflammation and neovascularization, intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH), surface ulceration, and intraluminal thrombus [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Advances in non-invasive and invasive carotid imaging modalities has revolutionized detailed plaque characterization for clinical risk assessment [6,7]. Concurrently, molecular profiling using advanced multi-omics approaches has provided novel insights into plaque biology [2,8,9]. Despite these advances, a critical gap persist in identifying and validating circulating biomarkers that accurately reflect plaque vulnerability status and predict clinical outcomes [2,10,11].

Small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), comprising exosomes originating from multivesicular bodies (MVBs) and small membrane-shed microvesicles, act as critical mediators of intercellular communication by delivering bioactive cargo to adjacent and distant cells [12,13]. Mechanistic studies reveal that sEVs from diverse cellular sources transport microRNAs (miRNAs) to regulate key pathological pathways in atherogenesis [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Notably, circulating sEV-encapsulated miRNAs have emerged as clinically relevant biomarkers, showing considerable promise for diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic monitoring applications in atherosclerosis [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. However, persistent discrepancies in miRNA expression profiles between plaque tissues and those in circulating sEVs have raised concerns regarding the biological validity of previously identified differentially expressed (DE)miRNAs, as most studies lack conclusive evidence direct evidence linking circulating sEVs to atherosclerotic plaques [[19], [20], [21]]. To address this gap, our recent work directly isolated plaque-derived sEVs from patients with asymptomatic CAS, thereby providing insights into tissue- and disease-specific pathobiology that circumvents the inherent biological ambiguity of sEVs isolated from either biofluids or cell culture media [21]. In that study, from candidate DEmiRNAs identified between stable and vulnerable plaque-derived sEVs, we validated five plasma-derived sEV miRNAs of plaque origin, using a High-throughput nano-bio chip integrated system for liquid biopsy (HNCIB) system, as potential biomarkers for plaque vulnerability [21]. That preliminary study, however, was limited by its restrictive definition of vulnerability—based solely on fibrous cap rupture (FCR) or IPH—which excluded other high-risk plaque features and mixed vulnerable phenotypes [2,21].

In this study, to enhance clinical relevance and further explore the diagnostic potential of plasma-derived sEV miRNAs as indicators of plaque vulnerability, we designed four complementary cohorts: a sequencing cohort, a discovery cohort, and two validation cohorts. These cohorts included both asymptomatic and symptomatic CAS patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy (CEA) or carotid stenting. Plaque vulnerability was evaluated based on a composite of histopathological features in CEA specimens, such as thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA), active inflammation, intraplaque neovascularization, IPH, or fibrous cap rupture (FCR) [22,23], or according to the Plaque-Reporting and Data System (RADS) scoring system for stenting cases [6]. Additionally, we preliminary explored the prognostic utility of validated plasma-derived sEV miRNAs for predicting the risk of 3-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) following CEA.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using prospectively maintained clinical databases and biobank resources from the Department of Neurosurgery, Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (Beijing, China). Consecutive patients with asymptomatic or symptomatic CAS who underwent revascularization for severe stenosis (≥70%) at the distal common carotid artery or the extracranial internal carotid artery were screened between April 2019 and December 2021. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥18 years and (2) complete clinical data with available matched plaque and blood specimens. Exclusion criteria were: (1) re-stenosis post-revascularization or radiation-induced stenosis; (2) recent (<6 months) history of acute coronary syndrome or acute peripheral vascular occlusion; (3) active malignancies, defined as malignant disease diagnosed or treated within 12 months before or during the index admission, or known untreated due to patient preference or palliative care stage, excluding precancerous conditions, non-invasive skin cancer, and myelodysplastic syndromes [24]; and (4) severe hepatic (serum alanine aminotransferase or aspartate transaminase >2 times of the upper limit of normal value) or renal (serum creatinine >1.5 times of upper limit of the normal value) failure.

These two validation cohorts were designed to assess the diagnostic efficacy of the identified plasma-derived sEV miRNAs. In addition, validation cohort 1 was also used to preliminary explore the association of the identified plasma-derived sEV miRNAs with 3-year MACE following CEA. This study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committees of the Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (KS2019-074), and was performed according to the Principles of Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was reported in accordance with STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [25].

Clinical data collection

The following clinical data were collected: (1) demographics; (2) vascular risk factors; (3) CAS laterality and the degree of stenosis quantified based on the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial (NASCET) criteria [26]; (4) clinical presentations, with symptomatic status defined as the occurrence of ipsilateral cerebrovascular ischemic events including amaurosis fugax, transient ischemic attack (TIA) or ischemic stroke within the preceding 6 months; (5) preoperative medication regimens; and (6) baseline laboratory results obtained upon hospital admission.

Three-year MACE assessment

Patients undergoing CEA were monitored for 3-year MACE through structured telephone interviews and scheduled outpatient re-examinations at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 months following revascularization. The composite MACE endpoint included: (1) fatal or non-fatal ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke; (2) fatal or non-fatal myocardial infarction, and (3) cardiovascular death of other causes including fatal aortic aneurysm rupture, fatal heart failure and sudden cardiac death [27,28]. All clinical assessments and imaging interpretations were performed by experienced certified neurologists and radiologists.

Histopathological and imaging assessment of plaque vulnerability

For patients undergoing CEA, carotid plaques were assessed histopathologically for vulnerability according to established criteria [22,23]. Vulnerable plaques were characterized by ≥ 1 of the following features (1) TCFA defined as a large necrotic core (area ratio >40%) covered by a thin fibrous cap (≤65 μm) with massive infiltration of macrophages and T lymphocytes (active inflammation); (2) intraplaque neovascularization or IPH; or (3) FCR. For patients undergoing carotid stenting, plaque vulnerability was classified using the Plaque-RADS scoring system based on multimodal imaging, including duplex carotid ultrasound, magnetic resonance angiography, and computed tomography angiography [6]. Vulnerable plaques were defined as Plaque-RADS 4 exhibiting ≥1 high-risk feature: IPH (4a), FCR (4b), or intraluminal thrombus (4c), irrespective of plaque thickness. All assessments were conducted by experienced certified neurologists, pathologists, and radiologists.

Plaque-derived sEV isolation

Freshly resected carotid plaques were processed immediately following our previously established protocol [21]. The culprit lesion (segment with maximal plaque burden) was used for sEV isolation, while adjacent proximal/distal regions (5-μm transverse sections) were preserved for histopathological evaluation [22,23]. Approximately 550 mg of carotid plaque tissue was embedded in O.C.T. medium, cryosectioned into 200-μm thick slices, and enzymatically digested using the Miltenyi Human Tumor Dissociation Kit (Cat. No. 130-096-929; Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germary) for 15 min at 37 °C. The resulting supernatant was sequentially passed through a 70-μm MACS® SmartStrainer (Miltenyi Biotec) and centrifuged under the following conditions: 300g for 10 min, 2,000g for 20 min, and 10,000g for 20 min, all at 4 °C. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22-μm syringe filter (Millipore, Temecula, CA, USA) and ultracentrifuged twice at 150,000g for 2 h at 4 °C using a SW 32 Ti swinging-bucket rotor (k-factor 238.6) and 38.5-mL Open-Top Thinwall Ultra-Clear tubes in an Optima™ XPN-100 ultracentrifuge. The pelleted material was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using Exosupur® columns (Cat. No. Echo9101; Echobiotech, Beijing, China) and concentrated using Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filters (100 kDa; Millipore).

Plasma-derived sEV isolation

Peripheral venous blood (6 mL) was collected in EDTA-containing tubes upon hospital admission prior to carotid revascularization. Cell-free plasma was obtained using a sequential centrifugation protocol: first at 120g for 20 min at room temperature (RT), then at 1,500g for 20 min at RT, followed by 13,000g for 5min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. For plasma-derived sEV isolation, cell-free plasma samples underwent parallel processing through 0.22-μm syringe filtration, two rounds of ultracentrifugation (150,000g for 2 h at 4 °C), SEC purification, and centrifugal concentration, using the same parameters and instruments as described for plaque-derived sEVs [21].

MiRNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis

Small RNA sequencing of plaque-derived sEVs was performed by a commercial service provider (Echobiotech, Beijing, China) [21]. Total RNA was extracted and purified using the miRNeasy® Mini Kit (Cat. No. 217004; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit (Cat. No. 5067-1511; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Small RNA libraries were prepared from 1 to 10 ng RNA inputs using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (Cat. No. 331502; Qiagen), with unique barcode assigned to individual samples. Index-coded samples were clustered on a cBot Cluster Generation System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Cat. No. PE-401-3001; Illumina), and then sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform to generate 150bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were subjected to rigorous quality control through sequential alignment against the Silva, GtRNAdb, Rfam, and Repbase databases using Bowtie (v1.2.1.1) to remove ribosomal RNAs, transfer RNAs, small nuclear RNAs, small nucleolar RNAs, and other non-coding RNAs. Clean reads were mapped to the Homo sapiens GRCh38 reference genome for annotation. Known miRNAs were annotated against miRBase v22 database (http://www.mirbase.org/) [29], and novel miRNAs were predicted using miRDeep2 (version 2.0.5) [30]. Differential expression analysis between stable and vulnerable plaque-derived sEVs was performed using the “EdgeR” R package (version 3.12.1) [31], with thresholds set at log2 fold-change (FC) > 0.58 and p-value <0.05.

Quantification of miR-660-5p expression by HNCIB

The expression levels of miR-660-5p in plaque-derived and plasma-derived sEVs were quantified using a HNCIB platform (Bixionbiotech Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China) [21,32]. Briefly, isolated sEVs were re-suspended in 50 μL of phosphate buffer solution and loaded onto a nano-biochip (Bixionbiotech) that had been pre-functionalized with biotinylated anti-CD63 antibody (Cat. No. ab134331; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 1 h at 37 °C. sEVs were captured and enriched via antigen-antibody interaction, after which unbound vesicles were removed. Subsequently, 50 μL of cationic nanoparticles encapsulated molecular beacons [MBs; containing 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM) dye as the fluorophore and Black Hole Quencher 1 (BHQ1) dye as the quencher] were added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Electrostatic interactions between cationic nanoparticles and negatively charged sEV membranes facilitated nanoparticle-sEV fusion and MB delivery for miRNA detection. After washing, single-sEV fluorescence signals were captured using total internal reflective fluorescence microscopy (TIRFM; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and the fluorescent intensity was quantified using the fully automated data processing module (Bixionbiotech). The MB sequence for miR-660-5p detection (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) was 5′-6FAM-CCAACTCCGATATGCAATGGGTAGAGTTG-BHQ1-3’.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) with statistical significance set at a two-tailed p < 0.05. Quantitative variables were expressed as the mean (standard deviation; SD) or median (interquartile range; IQR), with normality assessed via Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student's t-test (parametric) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric). Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage), and intergroup differences were analyzed using Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. The association between plaque- and plasma-derived sEV miRNA levels was assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient analysis. Associations between plasma-derived sEV DEmiRNAs and plaque vulnerability were analyzed using multivariate logistic regression models adjusted for covariates achieving p < 0.05 in univariate logistic regression analysis. Given the limited number of MACE events, the associations between plasma-derived sEV DEmiRNAs and 3-year MACE were analyzed using Firth's penalized likelihood logistic regression to minimize small-sample bias. The predictive performance of identified plasma-derived sEV DEmiRNAs was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with area under the curve (AUC) reported alongside 95% confidence intervals (CI). To evaluate the stability of model discrimination, internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling.

Results

Expression profiles of plaque-derived sEV miRNAs associated with plaque vulnerability

To identify plaque-derived sEV miRNAs associated with plaque vulnerability, we first performed miRNA-seq in the sequencing cohort (n = 12; Table S1). Following quality control and miRNA annotation, a total of 2033 miRNAs (1669 known and 364 novel) were identified. Comparative analysis between sEVs isolated from 6 stable plaques (3 asymptomatic and 3 symptomatic status) and 6 vulnerable plaques (3 asymptomatic and 3 symptomatic status) revealed 31 DEmiRNAs, of which 10 were upregulated and 21 were downregulated (Fig. 2A–B and Table S2).

Validation of DEmiRNAs in plaque-derived sEVs and matched plasma-derived sEVs

Subsequently, we evaluated the diagnostic potential of plasma-derived sEV miRNAs for plaque vulnerability, based on the DEmiRNAs identified through miRNA-seq of plaque-derived sEVs. We recruited a discovery cohort of 62 consecutive CAS patients who underwent CEA (mean age 64.7 [7.7] years; 14.5% females; 29 with stable plaques and 33 with vulnerable plaques; 29 asymptomatic and 33 symptomatic), and performed parallel sEV isolation from both plaque tissues and matched plasma samples. Baseline characteristics were comparable between the groups, except for higher levels of neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and homocysteine (Hcy) in the vulnerable plaque group, as detailed in Table S3. Based on the miRNA-seq results, we prioritized the top five candidate DEmiRNAs (miR-660-5p, miR-320c, miR-379-5p, miR-576-5p, and miR-30a-3p; Fig. 2A) for further quantification using the HNCIB platform. The results showed significantly elevated miR-660-5p expression levels in both plaque- and plasma-derived sEVs obtained from patients with vulnerable plaques compared to those with stable plaques (Fig. 2C and Table S4). In plaque-derived sEVs, miR-379-5p was significantly upregulated and miR-576-5p was significantly downregulated in the vulnerable plaque group; however, these differential expression patterns were not observed in the corresponding plasma-derived sEVs (Table S4). The remaining two candidate DEmiRNAs, miR-320c and miR-30a-3p, exhibited comparable expression profiles between plaque subtypes in both sEV sources (Table S4). Importantly, Pearson correlation coefficient analysis demonstrated a significant positive correlation between miR-660-5p levels in matched plaque- and plasma-derived sEVs from individual patients (r = 0.723, p < 0.001; Fig. 2D). Finally, these findings were validated using parallel quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Consistent with the HNCIB results, miR-660-5p expression remained significantly elevated in vulnerable plaque- and plasma-derived sEVs (Fig. S1A), and a significant correlation was confirmed between matched samples (r = 0.571, p < 0.001; Fig. S1B). Moreover, the expression levels of miR-660-5p measured by the HNCIB platform and qRT-PCR showed strong agreement (plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p: r = 0.827, p < 0.001; plaque-derived sEV miR-660-5p: r = 0.861, p < 0.001; Fig. S1C). Collectively, these results identify plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p as a promising candidate biomarker for carotid plaque vulnerability.

Associations of plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p with histopathologically defined plaque vulnerability

To validate the diagnostic performance of plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p, a total of 180 consecutive CAS patients who underwent CEA (median age 66 [IQR, 60-71] years; 11.7% females; 71 asymptomatic and 109 symptomatic status) were enrolled in validation cohort 1. Histopathological evaluation classified 87 (48.3%) plaques as stable and 93 (51.7%) as vulnerable. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Univariate logistics regression analyses revealed significantly higher levels of plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p (OR, 1.24; 95%CI, 1.14-1.35; p < 0.001; Fig. 3A and Table S5), NLR (OR, 1.55; 95%CI, 1.16-2.07; p = 0.003; Table S5), and Hcy (OR, 1.22; 95%CI, 1.11-1.34; p < 0.001; Table S5) in patients with vulnerable plaques compared to those with stable plaques. After adjustment for potential confounders, plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p (adjusted OR, 1.23; 95%CI, 1.13-1.34; p < 0.001), NLR (adjusted OR, 1.46; 95%CI, 1.05-2.05; p = 0.026), and Hcy (adjusted OR, 1.2; 95%CI, 1.09-1.33; p < 0.001) remained independently associated with vulnerable plaque (Table S5). Further ROC curve analysis (Fig. 3A) showed that AUC values for plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p, NLR, and Hcy in distinguishing vulnerable from stable plaques were 0.726 (95%CI, 0.651-0.801), 0.606 (95%CI, 0.524-0.688), and 0.692 (95%CI, 0.615-0.769), respectively. Their incorporation improved the diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC of 0.808 (95%CI, 0.745-0.872).

Associations of plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p with Plaque-RADS defined plaque vulnerability

To further validate the aforementioned findings, we established a validation cohort 2, comprising 326 consecutive CAS patients undergoing carotid stenting (mean age 67.1 [7.2] years; 21.5% females; 145 asymptomatic and 181 symptomatic status). According to Plaque-RADS categories, 110 (33.7%) patients were classified as having vulnerable plaques. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Univariate logistics regression analyses indicated significantly elevated plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p (OR, 1.24; 95%CI, 1.16-1.32; p < 0.001; Fig. 3B and Table S6), NLR (OR, 1.29; 95%CI, 1.05-1.58; p = 0.017; Table S6), and Hcy (OR, 1.13; 95%CI, 1.06-1.2; p < 0.001; Table S6) levels in patients with vulnerable plaques compared to those with stable plaques. After adjustment, plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p (adjusted OR, 1.25; 95%CI, 1.17-1.34; p < 0.001) and Hcy (OR, 1.16; 95%CI, 1.08-1.24; p < 0.001) remained independently associated with vulnerable plaque, while NLR did not retain significance (OR, 1.19; 95%CI, 0.94-1.5; p = 0.15; Table S6). In ROC analysis (Fig. 3B), AUC values for plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p, NLR, and Hcy in distinguishing vulnerable from stable plaques were 0.729 (95%CI, 0.668-0.79), 0.563 (95%CI, 0.496-0.63), and 0.625 (95%CI, 0.56-0.691), respectively. A combined model integrating miR-660-5p, NLR, and Hcy improved the diagnostic performance, yielding an AUC of 0.782 (95% CI, 0.729-0.836).

Associations of plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p with 3-year MACE

Given the substantial residual risk of future MACE in CAS patients following CEA [27,33], we further explored the prognostic potential of plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p for 3-year MACE in validation cohort 1. During the 3-year follow-up period, 18 of the 180 patients (10%) developed MACE. Baseline characteristics were comparable between MACE and non-MACE groups, except for significantly elevated total cholesterol (TC) and low density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) levels in in patients who developed MACE (Table 1). Quantification via the HNCIB platform revealed significantly elevated plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p levels in MACE patients compared to non-MACE patients (Fig. 4). Univariate Firth's penalized likelihood logistic regression analyses identified four parameters associated with 3-year MACE: TC (OR, 1.84; 95%CI, 1.2-2.97; p = 0.006), LDL-C (OR, 1.96; 95%CI, 1.18-3.49; p = 0.011), triglyceride (TG; OR, 1.74; 95%CI, 1.02-2.88; p = 0.043), and plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p (OR, 1.48; 95%CI, 1.3-1.77; p < 0.001) (Table S7). After adjustment, plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p retained its significant association with an elevated risk of 3-year MACE (OR, 1.46; 95%CI, 1.27-1.75; p < 0.001; Table S7), although these estimates should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number events. This was validated using ROC analysis, and AUC value for plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p in discriminating 3-year MACE occurrence was 0.832 (95%CI, 0.719-0.945). Moreover, Bootstrap internal validation (B = 1000 resamples) produced an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.833 (95% CI 0.705-0.929; Fig. 4), suggesting stable moderate discrimination despite the limited event count.

Discussion

In this study, we initially performed miRNA profiling of plaque-derived sEVs from patients with histopathologically confirmed stable or vulnerable carotid plaques to identify DEmiRNAs as potential biomarkers. Utilizing a multi-phase approach and the HNCIB system for rapid and high-throughput single-EV analysis, we identified plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p as an independent biomarker of carotid plaque vulnerability, defined either by histopathological criteria in CEA cases or by the clinically validated Plaque-RADS scoring system in stenting cases. Furthermore, an exploratory analysis suggested that elevated levels of plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p were also associated with a higher risk of 3-year MACE following CEA. However, this latter observation was constrained by the limited number of endpoint events and warrants validation in larger, prospective studies.

While several studies have investigated the potential role of certain plasma-derived sEV miRNAs as predictors of carotid plaque vulnerability, conclusive evidence directly linking these miRNAs to the plaques is often lacking [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Tissue-derived sEVs, which are isolated directly from the interstitial space surrounding various cell types, provide a more comprehensive profile of tissue microenvironmental changes and exhibit higher tissue specificity compared to other sources [34,35]. Our prior work, directly isolating sEVs from plaque tissues of asymptomatic CAS patients, identified five plasma-derived sEV miRNAs of plaque origin predictive of vulnerable plaques defined by IPH or FCR [21]. Building upon this foundation, the present study advances this field in two key respects: (1) the inclusion of both symptomatic and asymptomatic CAS patients, and (2) the adoption of a composite definition of plaque vulnerability based on multiple clinically relevant high-risk features. This composite definition holds greater clinical relevance, as vulnerable plaques in practice are frequently characterized by mixed pathological characteristics rather than a single isolated feature [2,3]. Furthermore, such an approach better reflects the dynamic and multifactorial nature of plaque progression [2,3]. Here, we demonstrated a strong correlation between plaque- and plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p levels within the same patients. Critically, elevated plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p exhibited an independent association with histopathologically confirmed vulnerable plaques, a finding that remained robust in separate analyses of symptomatic and asymptomatic subgroups (data not shown). Furthermore, elevated plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p independently correlated with vulnerable plaques classified by the Plaque-RADS scoring system in patients undergoing carotid stenting. This recently introduced standardized classification system for carotid plaques has demonstrated superior stroke risk stratification compared to traditional stenosis grading [6,36,37]. Additionally, we found that two laboratory markers, NLR and Hcy, previously demonstrated to be associated with plaque vulnerability [38], also showed significant correlations in our cohort. A combined predictive panel integrating these two routine parameters with plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p effectively improved the predictive value for identifying vulnerable plaques. Moving forward, integrating traditional risk factors, noninvasive Plaque-RADS imaging characteristics, and plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p holds promise for enhancing predictive models of vulnerable plaques, warranting further investigation [2]. Notably, despite differences in vulnerability criteria between the two studies, we observed several overlapping DEmiRNAs in plaque-derived sEVs, particularly miR-497-5p. We previously demonstrated that miR-497-5p independently distinguishes plaque vulnerability and that vulnerable plaque-derived sEVs promote inflammatory endothelial dysfunction and atherogenesis, in part through the delivery of miR-497-5p to suppress the atheroprotective uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) [21]. Although these overlapping miRNAs—ranked outside the top five in the current analysis—were not further examined here, they merit future exploration as part of a multi-marker panel for predicting plaque vulnerability. Other top DEmiRNA candidates identified in plaque-derived sEVs (miR-320c, miR-379-5p, miR-576-5p, and miR-30a-3p), previously associated with plaque vulnerability [[39], [40], [41], [42]], were not significantly altered in our cohort. This discrepancy could arise from the compartmentalization of miRNAs, as their expression profiles often differ between EV-encapsulated and free forms [18]. For instance, Jansen et al. [43] demonstrated that EV-encapsulated miRNAs, but not free miRNA, predicted cardiovascular events in patients with stable coronary artery disease. Additional contributing factors may include differences in patient populations, variations in miRNA detection methodologies, and potential biological heterogeneity in plaque vulnerability mechanisms across different cohorts.

Despite undergoing CEA, patients remain at high residual risk for future MACE [27,28]. Reliable predictive biomarkers to guide secondary prevention strategies are thus urgently needed. Current prognostic models for post-CEA outcomes face limitations, including suboptimal performance with AUC<0.8, methodological issues, and inadequate external validation [2,33]. In our exploratory analysis, elevated levels of plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p were associated with a higher risk of 3-year MACE following CEA, exhibiting an AUC exceeding 0.8. This finding may reflect residual systemic atherosclerotic vulnerability, as surgical carotid plaque removal does not alter inherent plaque instability in other vascular territories [22,27]. Indeed, patients with one vulnerable plaque are likely to have vulnerable plaques elsewhere [44]. Notably, our findings align with prior cardiovascular research. Bye et al. [45] identified circulating miR-660-5p as an indicator for future fatal myocardial infarction (MI) in healthy individuals over a 10-year period. Moreover, elevated circulating miR-660-5p levels independently predict 1-year MACE (encompassing cardiac death and recurrent MI) in ST-segment elevation MI (STEMI) patients, and significantly correlate with no-reflow phenomenon following primary percutaneous coronary intervention [46,47]. The prothrombotic potential of miR-660-5p may mechanistically underpin these clinical associations. In vitro studies demonstrate that miR-660 overexpression enhances platelet activation [48], providing a molecular rationale for its link to thrombotic complications central to MACE pathogenesis. Supporting this, elevated circulating miR-660-5p levels are observed in acute venous thromboembolism [49]. It should be noted, however, that although we applied Firth's penalized-likelihood method—a recommended approach for small sample sizes—and performed bootstrap internal validation, the observation remains constrained by the limited number of endpoint events. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger prospective cohorts. Since the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the predictive value of plasma-derived miRNAs for plaque vulnerability, the validation cohort 1 was not prospectively powered for 3-year MACE analysis—a consideration for future study design.

The primary and distinctive advantage of the HNCIB platform is its capability for multi-parametric and high-dimensional analysis at single-EV level, enabling simultaneous detection of both surface markers and intravesicular cargo components [32]. Key technical attributes of the system include: minimal sample consumption (∼90 μL of plasma), rapid processing time (∼6 h), and high-throughput capacity (up to 384 samples). Compared to traditional PCR-based methods, the HNCIB workflow is markedly less labor-intensive and prone to fewer pre-analytical variabilities, thereby enhancing operational robustness. Moreover, the system integrates a fully automated data processing module that standardizes analytical procedures and enhances reproducibility [32]. Should future large-scale studies establish validated reference ranges for plaque vulnerability, the HNCIB system holds considerable promise as a tool for early high-risk CAS patient screening.

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective study with a modest sample size and no external validation, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, this design remains appropriate for the initial, exploratory aims of this study. Thus, our work provides critical hypotheses and references for future rigorous validation in prospective, large-scale, multi-center studies. Second, the sequencing cohort was small, potentially limiting statistical power to detect subtle expression differences. Third, the remaining 26 DEmiRNAs identified by miRNA-seq were not pursued in subsequent validation. Fourth, although multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for established confounders, residual confounding factors cannot be entirely excluded. Fifth, the observed associations between plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p and plaque vulnerability or MACE do not necessarily indicate causation. Our study did not investigate the biological mechanisms of miR-660-5p in atherosclerosis progression, as this extends beyond our current scope, further bioinformatics analysis and functional validation are warranted to elucidate its precise roles in plaque vulnerability and cerebrovascular events.

Conclusion

In conclusion, plasma-derived sEV miR-660-5p serves as a potential non-invasive biomarker for both plaque vulnerability and postoperative 3-year MACE following CEA, demonstrating potential for refining risk stratification and guiding targeted secondary prevention strategies. Additionally, miR-660-5p may represent a plausible therapeutic target for attenuating atherosclerosis progression, although its underlying mechanisms require further investigation.
