Transdiagnostic behavioral and sociodemographic influences on the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap in neurodivergent children
Authors: Eric Wan, Melanie Penner, Jennifer Crosbie, Jessica Brian, Elizabeth Kelley, Russell Schachar, Marc Woodbury-Smith, Jessica Jones, Robert Nicolson, Ryan A. Stevenson, Julia Frei, Elisabetta Trinari, Paul D. Arnold, Mohammad Ayub, Jason P. Lerch, Evdokia Anagnostou, Azadeh Kushki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adaptive functioning typically aligns with cognitive ability in the general population, but this relationship appears more complex in neurodivergent populations. Individuals with autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) frequently show a discrepancy between cognitive ability and adaptive functioning. However, the behavioral and sociodemographic factors associated with this cognitive-adaptive functioning gap remain unclear. Data were collected from the Province of Ontario Neurodevelopmental Disorders Network (POND) and included 902 participants aged 6–21 years (autism = 409, ADHD = 210, OCD = 36, neurotypical = 214, other = 33). Cognitive functioning was assessed using full-scale IQ from the Wechsler tests, and adaptive functioning using the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System-II (General Adaptive Composite). The cognitive-adaptive functioning gap was calculated as the difference between FSIQ and ABAS-II GAC scores. This gap was modeled transdiagnostically with phenotypic (social communication, ADHD and OCD traits, internalizing and externalizing symptoms) and sociodemographic (age, sex, race, household income, caregiver education) features. Three classifiers (i.e. logistic regression, CatBoost, and Explainable Boosting Machine) were trained to distinguish children with above- versus below-median cognitive-adaptive functioning gap scores. Feature importance was assessed for the selected model using SHAP values. Sensitivity analyses repeated the performance analysis and feature-importance assessment using a 15-point FSIQ–ABAS GAC gap threshold and a continuous-outcome model of the numerical FSIQ–ABAS GAC gap. Logistic regression, CatBoost, and Explainable Boosting Machine models showed comparable performance (ROC–AUC = 0.698–0.700). CatBoost was selected for SHAP-based interpretation because it achieved competitive performance and allowed efficient tree-based SHAP estimation. SHAP analysis identified internalizing problems, social communication challenges, and OCD symptoms as the strongest predictors. Behavioral factors contributed more to the gap than sociodemographic variables. Sensitivity analyses using a 15-point gap threshold and a continuous gap outcome retained the same core behavioral contributors: internalizing problems, social communication challenges, and OCD symptoms. Internalizing symptoms and social communication difficulties were most strongly associated with the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58625-5.

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental conditions (NDCs), including autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) are characterized by differences in brain development that can impact cognitive and adaptive functioning. Cognitive functioning, often measured via the intelligence quotient (IQ), entails mental processes such as perception, attention, memory, language, problem-solving, and reasoning1. Adaptive functioning quantifies an individual’s capacity to perform daily tasks, adapt to environmental changes, and meet the demands of everyday life2.

Cognitive functioning varies widely across neurodivergent individuals. For example, among autistic children, an estimated 33% have an intellectual disability (ID)3–5, while approximately 13.7% score in the superior intelligence range5,6. ADHD and other NDCs can also co-occur with ID7–9. Adaptive functioning is also highly varied in NDCs. Autism has been associated with decreased adaptive functioning relative to other NDCs, although large variation in these skills exist8,10–14. Specific domains of adaptive functioning, such as socialization, communication, and daily living skills, tend to be more impaired in children with autism, particularly those with co-occurring autism and ADHD, compared to children with ADHD, who exhibit more pronounced difficulties in motor adaptive functioning15.

For the general population, adaptive behavior and cognitive ability are assumed to be positively associated; however, in NDCs, a significant discrepancy can exist between cognitive and adaptive functioning8,16–20. This discrepancy often manifests as lower adaptive functioning relative to cognitive abilities, and tends to persist from toddlerhood through adolescence and young adulthood, though its trajectory beyond this stage remains unclear18,21,22. The discrepancy is clinically important because cognitive ability is an imprecise proxy for adaptive functioning in autism, particularly among individuals without intellectual disability23. Early work similarly showed that IQ and adaptive behavior differed significantly among autistic individuals with IQ scores above 70, whereas cognitive and adaptive scores were more comparable among those with lower cognitive ability24. Large cohort studies further suggest that children with higher IQ may show substantial adaptive-functioning difficulties, with adaptive scores often falling one to two standard deviations below expected levels despite average-range cognitive ability25. These findings indicate that the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap may capture clinically meaningful support needs that are not evident from IQ alone23,25. Despite the established presence of the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap, the underlying phenotypic and sociodemographic factors associated with this gap are still not fully clear.

Prior studies have identified several candidate contributors to the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap, but the evidence remains fragmented. Cognitive level and age appear particularly important, as the gap is often larger among individuals with higher IQ and may widen with age as everyday demands increasingly exceed adaptive-skill development21,25–27. Executive-function difficulties may also help explain why cognitive ability does not consistently translate into organized, flexible, goal-directed behavior27. Social-communication challenges have been among the more consistent correlates of lower adaptive functioning and larger cognitive-adaptive functioning gaps. Their inclusion as a candidate behavioral contributor to the gap is thus well supported. Evidence for restricted and repetitive behaviors and mental health symptoms is less settled. Some studies report limited associations with anxiety or RRBs and others linking the gap or adaptive-functioning difficulties to psychiatric comorbidities such as anxiety and depression20,22. Recent work further suggests that internalizing and externalizing problems are meaningfully related to adaptive-skill domains, particularly socialization, and that higher cognitive skills may be associated with greater internalizing problems among autistic youth without intellectual disability28. Developmental, sex-specific, and sociodemographic patterns may further shape these associations, as the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap has been observed early in development. This gap may differ in magnitude and developmental course by sex, and has been examined in relation to caregiver education and household income, although findings are not always consistent across studies21,26,29. The evidence motivates a joint examination of social-communication challenges, ADHD and OCD traits, internalizing and externalizing symptoms, and sociodemographic indicators in a transdiagnostic sample of neurodivergent and neurotypical children.

Existing findings on the influence of sociodemographic factors on cognitive-adaptive functioning gaps are less consistent than those for cognitive and behavioral features. Prior studies have examined age, sex, race and ethnicity, caregiver education, income, and family background in relation to adaptive functioning, but results vary across samples and analytic approaches25,30. Measurement choices may also influence observed sociodemographic patterns. For example, incorporating adaptive functioning alongside cognitive functioning scores substantially reduced estimates of co-occurring intellectual disability in autistic children and reduced, though did not eliminate, racial disparities in those estimates31. Sociodemographic factors thus should be considered alongside cognitive and behavioral features when studying the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap.

In addition to these gaps, much of the existing literature remains organized around diagnosis-specific samples, with autism receiving substantially more attention than other neurodevelopmental conditions. This limits generalizability because many candidate predictors of functioning cut across diagnostic boundaries32. Cross-diagnosis investigations remain limited despite evidence that many predictors of functioning and quality of life may be shared across neurodevelopmental conditions32. A transdiagnostic approach may therefore better capture shared sources of everyday functional difficulty than analyses organized primarily around categorical diagnoses33–35.

A further methodological limitation of the existing literature is that many prior studies have examined predictors individually or within relatively constrained statistical frameworks, which may not fully capture the multivariable and potentially non-linear structure of neurodevelopmental profiles. Transdiagnostic, data-driven approaches have been proposed as a way to characterize shared and heterogeneous patterns across neurodevelopmental conditions beyond categorical diagnostic boundaries34. Because, cognitive-adaptive functioning gap likely reflects the joint influence of cognitive, behavioral, and sociodemographic factors, machine-learning approaches offer a useful complement to traditional hypothesis-driven analyses. They can estimate the relative contribution of correlated predictors and accommodate non-linear associations among them.

The present study used a transdiagnostic, data-driven modeling approach in a sample of 902 neurodivergent and neurotypical children and youth. Our objective was to evaluate cognitive, behavioral, and sociodemographic factors across a heterogeneous cohort of participants, including those with autism, ADHD, OCD, and neurotypical individuals, without grouping them by diagnostic labels. Machine learning modeling techniques were applied to estimate the relative importance of cognitive, behavioral, and sociodemographic features to neurodevelopmental profiles. This strategy allows us to model multivariable, potentially non-linear associations between behavioral, sociodemographic, and cognitive-adaptive functioning gap features.

Methods

Participants

For this study, a subset of the data from the Province of Ontario Neurodevelopmental Network (POND), exported on March 26, 2024, was used. The dataset included 3,380 participants, between ages of 0 and 21, who were neurotypical or neurodivergent (neurotypical = 335, autism = 1,307, ADHD = 1,083, OCD = 291, other = 353, 4 siblings without a diagnosis, 16 missing diagnoses). The “other” category included anxiety, sub-threshold ADHD, sub-threshold OCD, Down syndrome, Fragile X, ID only, Rett syndrome, social communication disorders, and Tourette syndrome. Validated assessments were used to support the clinical diagnoses. Specifically, the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule-2 (ADOS-2) and Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R) were used for autism, Parent Interview for Child Symptoms (PICS) was used for ADHD, and the Kiddie-Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (K-SADS) and the Children’s Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (CYBOCS) were used for OCD. For participants with ID, intellectual functioning was assessed using validated measures, such as the Stanford-Binet (SB), Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI), Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence (WPPSI), or Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS), as appropriate for age.

A subset of the above dataset was selected for the present analysis based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) age between 6 and 21 (n = 2,281), (2) neurotypical or a diagnosis of autism, ADHD, OCD, or other (n = 2,277), (3) complete data for the phenotypic measures used in this study (full scale IQ, adaptive functioning, mental health symptoms, and autism, ADHD, and OCD features) (n = 1,764), and (4) complete data for race, household income, and primary caregiver’s education (n = 902). The final sample consisted of 902 participants.

Measures

Full scale IQ was assessed using the Wechsler family of measures, including the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence, Second Edition (WASI-II)36, the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fourth (WISC-IV)37 and Fifth Editions (WISC-V)38 the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence, Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV)39, or the Stanford-Binet Scale40, as appropriate for developmental stage.

Adaptive functioning was quantified using the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System (ABAS)-II (Harrison and Oakland 2003), a parent-report measure with scores along domains of Communication, Community Use, Functional Academics, Home Living, Health/Safety, Leisure, Self-Direction, Social, and Work, through norm-referenced scaled scores. Our analysis used the General Adaptive Composite (GAC) score, representing overall adaptive functioning, encompassing all subscales. The cognitive-adaptive functioning gap was defined as full-scale IQ minus ABAS GAC score.

Autism, ADHD, and OCD features were quantified using the Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ)41, the Strengths and Weaknesses of ADHD Symptoms and Normal Behavior Scale (SWAN)42, and the Toronto Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (TOCS)43, respectively. Mental health symptoms were characterized using the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL), internalizing and externalizing subscales44.

Sociodemographic characteristics included in the study were sex (biological), age, household income, race, and the education of the primary caregiver. The racial categories were Black, East Asian, Indigenous, Latino, Middle Eastern, other, South Asian, Southeast Asian, and White. Household income was initially categorized as follows: lower than $10,000; $10,000 to $24,999; $25,000 to $49,999; $50,000 to $74,999; $75,000 to $99,999; $100,000 to $149,999; $150,000 to $199,999; and $200,000 or more. Primary caregiver’s education was categorized as lower than Bachelor’s degree and Bachelor’s degree or higher.

For analysis, household income was consolidated into three levels: low (under $50,000), middle ($50,000 to $100,000), and high ($100,000 or more), to increase statistical power, simplify interpretation, and reflect meaningful socioeconomic distinctions relevant to the study. Race was categorized as White or racialized, and education as lower than Bachelor’s degree or Bachelor’s degree and higher.

Group differences

To characterize the sample, descriptive statistics were computed and compared differences in sociodemographic and behavioral measures across diagnostic groups using Kruskal-Wallis tests with Bonferroni correction for continuous variables and Chi-squared tests for categorical variables. The p-values for multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Post-hoc analysis was conducted using Dunn’s procedure.

Differences in the cognitive–adaptive functioning gap across diagnostic (autism, ADHD, OCD, other diagnoses, neurotypical), sociodemographic (sex, race, income, education), and phenotypic (IQ) groups were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with Bonferroni corrections applied for multiple comparisons.

For feature groups showing significant differences (p < .05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Effect sizes were calculated for both tests to quantify the magnitude of group differences.

Modeling cognitive-adaptive functioning gaps

To identify predictors of cognitive-adaptive functioning gaps, a classification framework was implemented. The primary outcome, cognitive-adaptive functioning gap, was dichotomized by using the sample median cognitive-adaptive functioning gap value as the threshold: participants with a cognitive-adaptive functioning gap above the median were labeled ‘above-median gap’ and those at or below the median ‘below-median gap.’

The median split was used as the primary threshold to support a balanced classification model and to identify participants with relatively elevated cognitive-adaptive functioning gap within the sample. This threshold was not intended to define a clinical category. We conducted a sensitivity analysis using a scale-based 15-point gap threshold to follow up on this sample-dependent definition.

Three classifiers were evaluated: logistic regression45, CatBoost46, and Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM)47. Logistic regression was used as a baseline due to its simplicity, interpretability, and widespread use in binary classification tasks. To capture non-linear relationships and improve predictive performance, CatBoost was employed, a gradient boosting algorithm shown to outperform other tree-based methods on structured data while handling categorical variables natively. Finally, we included EBM, an interpretable tree-based ensemble model for tabular data that uses gradient boosting to capture non-linear relationships while maintaining transparency, achieving competitive performance. Each model’s hyperparameters were optimized using OptunaSearchCV48 with 50 trials and 5-fold inner cross-validation. Input features included numerical phenotypic variables (SCQ, SWAN, TOCS, CBCL internalizing and externalizing symptoms) and sociodemographic features (age, sex, race, first caregiver’s education, household income), with categorical features one-hot encoded as required. One-hot encoding converts categorical variables into binary format by creating new columns for each unique category, where 1 indicates the presence of that category and 0 indicates its absence, allowing machine learning algorithms to process categorical data numerically.

10-fold stratified cross-validation was conducted with a 90 − 10 train-test split in each fold, while preserving distribution across diagnostic categories, adaptive functioning (ABAS GAC 10-point bins), and sex. Performance metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [ROC-AUC], and area under the precision-recall curve [PR-AUC]) were computed at both the full-sample level and within diagnostic and sociodemographic subgroups. The best-performing model was selected based on overall F1 performance.

To determine whether there were statistically significant differences in model performance across models/configurations, F1 scores from 10-fold cross-validation were compared for each configuration. Since the F1 scores were derived from independent folds across different configurations, a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to test for differences between the groups, where each group represented the set of F1 scores from a specific model configuration. Upon detecting a significant group-level effect (p < .05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test with Bonferroni correction. This allowed us to identify which model configurations differed significantly in their distributions of F1 scores and to rank them accordingly.

To interpret the contributions of individual features to model classification, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were employed. SHAP, derived from cooperative game theory, provides an interpretable framework for understanding how each feature contributes to the predictions of machine learning models by assigning importance scores49,50. Positive SHAP values indicate that a feature increases the model’s estimation, whereas negative values indicate a decrease in the estimation. SHAP dependence plots further illustrate the relationship between individual feature values and the predicted IQ-ABAS gap, allowing us to capture specific contributions across the feature space. This method has been widely adopted in healthcare research to explain model estimations and understand feature importance51,52. SHAP values were computed across the held-out test sets of the 10-fold stratified cross-validation. The SHAP values from all folds were then aggregated to obtain a representation of feature attributions.

To evaluate the associations between different features and the individual feature’s SHAP values, appropriate statistical methods based on the nature of the data were used: For continuous features (e.g., CBCL scores), Spearman’s rank correlations were used to measure the strength and direction of associations, where correlations greater than +/-0.60 were considered strong. The zero-crossing point, defined as the feature value at which the predicted SHAP value equals zero, was estimated for each continuous feature. A quadratic model was fit and the relevant root within the observed feature range was selected. Nonparametric bootstrapping (1,000 resamples) was applied to obtain percentile-based 95% confidence intervals for the crossing estimate. For ordinal features (e.g., household income, caregiver education), Kendall’s rank correlations, were applied with the same threshold of τ > |0.60| for strong associations. Finally, for nominal features (e.g., sex, race), a t-test was conducted to compare group differences in the IQ-ABAS gap. These methods allowed us to explore how each feature type was associated with the observed IQ-ABAS gap.

To evaluate whether the primary findings depended on the outcome definition, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. First, because the median split was sample-dependent and not intended to define a clinical category, we repeated the classification model evaluation using a 15-point FSIQ–ABAS GAC gap threshold, corresponding to one normative standard deviation. Given that dichotomizing the gap may obscure variation in the magnitude of the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap, we also repeated the modeling using the FSIQ–ABAS GAC gap as a continuous outcome. Both analyses used the same predictor set, complete-case sample, cross-validation framework, hyperparameter tuning approach, and SHAP-based feature-importance procedure as the primary analysis.

Statistical analyses

Our analyses were conducted using Python (v3.12.7). The modeling pipeline was developed using scikit-learn (v1.6.1) for preprocessing, model implementation, and evaluation; CatBoost (v1.2.8) for gradient boosting models; and Optuna (v4.3.0) for hyperparameter tuning via Bayesian optimization. Data handling and numerical operations were performed with pandas (v2.2.3) and numpy (v1.26.4). SHAP analysis was performed using the SHAP (v0.46.0) library49.

All statistical tests were implemented using the scipy (v1.13.1) module for the Kruskal–Wallis test and statsmodels (v0.14.4) for Tukey’s HSD with Bonferroni adjustment.

Group comparisons

The distribution of the IQ-ABAS gap is illustrated in Fig. 1, disaggregated by diagnosis and sociodemographic identities. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests with the Bonferroni correction were used to test differences of median IQ-ABAS gap from zero. The gap was significantly greater than zero for the pooled sample (Mdn = 17.50, W = 346163.50, p < .001) as well as each diagnosis group (autism: Mdn = 26, W = 74899.50, p < .001; ADHD: Mdn = 18, W = 19599.50, p < .001; OCD: Mdn = 20, W = 631.50, p < .001; NT: Mdn = 6.50, W = 15446.00, p < .001), except the Other NDCs group (Mdn = 4, W = 369.50, p = .25).

A Kruskal-Wallis test indicated that there was a significant difference in IQ-ABAS gap across the diagnostic groups (Autism: median = 26, IQR = 33; ADHD: median = 18, IQR = 23.75; OCD: median = 20, IQR = 20.75; NT: median = 6.5, IQR = 22.75; Other: median = 4, IQR = 24; χ²(4, N = 902) = 118, adj-p < .001). Post-hoc comparisons using Dunn’s Test with Bonferroni correction revealed that the autism group had the highest IQ-ABAS gap, significantly higher than ADHD (Z = 3.52, p = .002), NT (Z = 10.40, p < .001), and other NDCs (Z = 4.68, p < .001) groups. The OCD group had the second-highest gap, significantly higher than both NT (Z = 3.80, p < .001) and other NDCs (Z = 2.72, p = .033). ADHD showed a significantly higher gap than NT (Z = 5.96, p<.001) and other NDCs (Z = 2.93, p = .017). However, there were no significant differences between the discrepancies of the ADHD and OCD groups (Z = -0.59, p = 1), autism and OCD groups (Z = 1.11, p = 1), or between NT and other NDCs group (Z = -0.165, p = 1).

Model performance

Classification performance was evaluated across the models (Logistic Regression, CatBoost, Explainable Boosting Machine) using five metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 Score, ROC AUC, and PR AUC. Each model was assessed across 10 cross-validation folds (Table 2). Kruskal-Wallis tests were conducted for each performance metric to examine statistical differences across models. No statistically significant differences were observed for any metric: Precision, H(2, N = 902) = 0.49, p = .784; Recall, H(2, N = 902) = 0.44, p = .802; F1 Score, H(2, N = 902) = 0.74, p = .691; ROC-AUC, H(2, N = 902) = 0.05, p = .973; and PR AUC, H(2, N = 902) = 0.63, p = .730. These results suggest that model performance did not differ significantly across metrics. Given the equivalent predictive performance across all models, CatBoost was selected for subsequent interpretability analysis using SHAP. This choice was motivated by CatBoost’s optimal compatibility with SHAP’s TreeExplainer, which provides fast and exact computation of SHAP values by leveraging the tree structure, rather than approximations required by model-agnostic approaches.

Examining predictors

To interpret the contributions of individual features to model estimations, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) was applied on the trained CatBoost classification model. SHAP values show how each feature affects the model’s prediction, where a positive SHAP value indicates the feature pushes the prediction toward a more positive cognitive-adaptive functioning gap and a negative value indicates it pushes toward a more negative cognitive-adaptive functioning gap. Feature importance (Fig. 2A) was assessed using the mean absolute SHAP values. The features that contributed most to the model’s estimation were internalizing problems (mean |SHAP| = 0.292, SEM = 0.006), social communication challenges (mean |SHAP| = 0.275, SEM = 0.006), and OCD symptoms (mean |SHAP| = 0.203, SEM = 0.006). Complete feature importance values with SD, SEM, and 95% CI are reported in Supplementary Table 7.

Sensitivity analysis

The primary median-split threshold classified 451 participants (50.0%) as having a high cognitive-adaptive functioning gap, whereas the 1-SD threshold classified 489 participants (54.2%) as having a meaningful gap. The 1-SD threshold model showed similar cross-validated performance to the primary model (F1 = 0.72 ± 0.04; ROC-AUC = 0.69 ± 0.05). Feature-importance rankings were highly consistent between the primary and 1-SD threshold models, with Spearman’s ρ = 0.96 for both feature ranks and mean absolute SHAP values. The top contributors in the 1-SD sensitivity analysis were internalizing problems, social communication challenges, and OCD symptoms. These results matched the top three features from the primary analysis.

The continuous-outcome sensitivity model also showed a consistent pattern. Cross-validated regression performance was modest, with MAE = 15.39, RMSE = 19.22, R² = 0.18, and Spearman ρ = 0.41. The top contributors to the continuous FSIQ–ABAS GAC gap were social communication, internalizing problems, and OCD symptoms. Thus, the same core behavioral domains were retained when the gap was modeled using a 1-SD threshold and when it was modeled directly as a continuous outcome.

Discussion

This study examined the correlates of the gap between cognitive and adaptive functioning in a neurodiverse sample of children, using both univariate testing and group comparisons, as well as a multivariate machine learning approach. Our findings demonstrate that the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap is observed across NDCs and typical development, although the widest gaps were found in autism, ADHD, and OCD. This is consistent with previous findings in autism21,53 and extends the understanding to other neurodevelopmental conditions. These findings motivate the investigation of shared, transdiagnostic factors that may impact the IQ-adaptive functioning gap.

Our univariate analyses indicated that IQ significantly contributes to the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap, with individuals possessing higher IQ scores more likely to experience a higher gap between their cognitive abilities and adaptive functioning. These findings have clear real-world implications. Prior studies have shown that even among individuals with average or superior IQ, adaptive skills such as daily living, organization, and social functioning often lag markedly behind intellectual ability. In autism, adolescents with typical or high IQ frequently demonstrate daily living scores within the impaired range, and this gap can widen with age17,26. Similar patterns are reported in ADHD, where adaptive functioning is substantially lower than expected from cognitive level, particularly in higher-IQ youth54. In OCD, average or above-average intelligence rarely mitigates the functional burden of time-consuming habits and avoidance, which limit daily performance and independence55. These examples illustrate that high cognitive ability does not guarantee adaptive functioning performance; rather, the gap itself reflects everyday challenges that affect autonomy and quality of life.

The cognitive-adaptive functioning gap may occur because IQ captures structured cognitive potential, whereas adaptive functioning requires the flexible, self-initiated, and socially embedded use of daily living skills. Therefore, emotional distress, social-communication difficulties, rigidity, attentional problems, and executive-function demands may interfere with the translation of cognitive ability into daily performance17,22,27,53.

From a measurement perspective, this may be related to the fact that at higher IQ levels, a wider range of discrepancies is numerically possible, whereas both our IQ and ABAS measures had minimum values (floor) at 40.

Consistent with prior literature, IQ alone did not fully account for adaptive functioning29. Our multivariate models identified phenotypic and demographic features that further modulated the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap, with internalizing problems contributing most strongly. Internalizing symptoms, such as anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal, can negatively impact a child’s quality of life56. These challenges are common in neurodivergent populations, with prevalence estimates in autistic youth reaching approximately 20% for anxiety disorders and 11% for depressive disorders, substantially higher than in neurotypical peers57. Such symptoms may directly widen the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap by undermining the performance of real-world tasks. For example, anxiety may promote avoidance of social and practical situations where adaptive skills are practiced, while depressive symptoms may reduce initiative and persistence in daily tasks. This may be especially relevant for youth without intellectual disability, for whom greater self-awareness may make the mismatch between cognitive ability and adaptive difficulty more visible, increasing social comparison, frustration, anxiety, and withdrawal22,53. Over time, this may create a reinforcing cycle in which internalizing symptoms limit adaptive functioning, while repeated adaptive-functioning difficulties increase vulnerability to internalizing symptoms28.

However, this association should be interpreted with caution because internalizing symptoms and the adaptive-functioning component of the FSIQ–ABAS gap were both measured using caregiver-report instruments. Shared informant effects may inflate associations when the same reporter provides information on both the predictor and outcome-related construct. For example, caregiver stress, expectations, or global perceptions of child difficulty could contribute to higher scores of internalizing symptoms while also contributing to lower scores of daily adaptive functioning. Thus, the prominence of internalizing problems in the model may reflect both clinically meaningful links between emotional distress and real-world functioning and method-related covariance between the caregiver-reported predictor and the caregiver-reported adaptive-functioning component of the gap score.

The temporal ordering between internalizing symptoms and the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap cannot be determined due to the present study’s cross-sectional design. Although internalizing symptoms may reduce adaptive functioning by limiting participation, initiation, and persistence, the reverse pathway is also plausible. Children with relatively high cognitive ability but lower daily adaptive performance may repeatedly encounter situations in which they understand expectations but cannot consistently meet them. These experiences may contribute to frustration, anxiety, avoidance, or social withdrawal over time. Clinically, this interpretation suggests that the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap should not be understood only as a consequence of internalizing symptoms. It may also increase risk for internalizing problems. Supports that reduce environmental mismatch, scaffold daily living demands, and lower repeated experiences of failure may therefore be as important as symptom-focused intervention.

Longitudinal analyses will be needed to test whether internalizing symptoms precede widening cognitive-adaptive functioning gaps, emerge as a consequence of repeated adaptive-functioning challenges, or operate bidirectionally over developmental stages.

Social communication challenges were another major contributor to the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap. This finding may reflect that social communication is not only a social skill domain, but also a mechanism through which children learn and apply everyday adaptive skills. Cognitive ability may support explicit or “academic” understanding of social rules, but this knowledge may not translate easily into real-time social judgment, flexible problem-solving, or spontaneous interaction in daily living53. Social-communication difficulties may also reduce naturalistic learning opportunities, because many adaptive skills are acquired through observation, imitation, reciprocal interaction, and feedback from others. The impact of social communication may extend beyond the socialization domain. Everyday tasks such as navigating community settings, asking for help, arranging appointments, participating in school or work routines, and solving practical problems often require communication and social problem-solving. Prior studies have similarly linked social-communication challenges to daily living skill difficulties, including among youth with average or above-average cognitive ability8,17. In the current study, social communication was the second strongest contributor after internalizing problems, and higher SCQ scores showed a strong positive association with SHAP values. The estimated zero-crossing point was approximately 7.3, suggesting that even relatively mild social-communication differences may begin to contribute positively to the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap in this sample. This supports the interpretation that adaptive functioning difficulties may emerge not only from limited cognitive capacity, but from difficulty translating cognitive potential into socially embedded, real-world performance.

OCD symptoms also contributed to the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap, but this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Prior transdiagnostic studies do not suggest that OCD diagnosis is consistently associated with adaptive-functioning impairment. Groups primarily composed of children with OCD often show relatively high adaptive functioning, and many individuals with OCD cluster closer to neurotypical controls than to other neurodevelopmental groups11,34. Thus, our finding may reflect dimensional obsessive-compulsive traits across the full sample rather than the effect of OCD diagnosis itself. These traits may increase the gap when rigidity, avoidance, time-consuming routines, or difficulty shifting interfere with the flexible application of cognitive skills in daily life. Given the small OCD subgroup, this interpretation should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Inattention also showed a positive association with the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap, although its contribution was smaller than internalizing problems, social communication, and OCD symptoms. This pattern is clinically plausible because adaptive functioning often depends on sustained attention, task initiation, organization, and follow-through. Even when cognitive ability is intact, inattentive symptoms may interfere with the consistent execution of daily routines, school-related responsibilities, and independent living skills.

Externalizing problems and hyperactivity showed weaker contributions than the leading behavioral features, suggesting that they may play a secondary role in this sample. Their weaker contribution does not mean they are unrelated to adaptive functioning, but rather that, once internalizing symptoms, social communication, OCD symptoms, inattention, and sociodemographic factors were considered jointly, they explained less of the model’s estimation of the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap.

Behavioral and emotional features may have ranked higher than sociodemographic features because they are closer to the day-to-day behaviors captured by adaptive functioning. The cognitive-adaptive functioning gap reflects difficulty applying cognitive ability in real-world settings, and symptoms affecting initiation, flexibility, social learning, and task completion are likely to map more directly onto that gap than sociodemographic features such as household income, caregiver education, or race. This interpretation is consistent with prior work showing that, when demographic variables and behavioral symptoms are considered together, social-communication symptoms can explain daily living skill difficulties more directly than maternal education or sex17. Thus, these variables may operate more indirectly, through access to supports, intervention history, family resources, environmental demands, or measurement processes that were not fully captured in the current feature set.

Sociodemographic features showed more modest effects overall. Age displayed the clearest pattern: younger children tended to have a positive gap, with the trend reversing around age 11.5. This contrasts with some longitudinal studies that report widening gaps over time21,29. One possible explanation is that our cross-sectional design captures a snapshot influenced by age-related interventions or environmental supports that may reduce the gap in older participants, whereas longitudinal designs track within-individual changes. Another possibility is that sample differences, such as diagnostic composition or socioeconomic distribution, alter developmental trajectories across studies.

Household income was only slightly related to the gap, with children from higher-income households showing marginally more negative contributions. The effect was small and unlikely to be practically significant. Caregiver education presented an apparent inconsistency: although it showed some association with the gap in preliminary analyses, this effect became negligible once other factors were included in the multivariate model. This illustrates the value of considering all estimators jointly, as bivariate associations may diminish when controlling for correlated features.

Sex showed a larger effect than other sociodemographic variables, with males more likely to have a positive gap, consistent with previous findings26. This may reflect true developmental differences or diagnostic and sampling patterns, but it may also reflect measurement limitations related to behavioral masking. Female neurodivergent participants may use compensatory strategies, such as imitation, rehearsal, or suppression of visible difficulties, that make everyday functioning appear more intact to caregivers despite substantial internal effort or distress. As a result, parent-reported adaptive-functioning measures may underestimate functional struggles in some females, particularly when difficulties are managed privately or are expressed as exhaustion, anxiety, or withdrawal rather than observable skill deficits. This could attenuate the measured IQ–ABAS gap and reduce the model’s ability to identify female participants whose lived experience includes substantial adaptive functioning challenges26,57,58.

It is also important to consider that part of the observed cognitive-adaptive functioning gap may reflect regression to the mean, a statistical tendency for extreme scores on one measure to be accompanied by more average scores on another. Children with exceptionally high FSIQ values, for instance, are less likely to show equally extreme adaptive functioning scores even when both measures are valid. Consequently, some portion of the IQ-adaptive functioning gap at higher cognitive functioning levels may arise from measurement variability rather than true difference. Nonetheless, our analysis demonstrated significant differences in cognitive-adaptive functioning gap across the neurodivergent groups. And it examined the correlates of this cognitive-adaptive functioning gap rather than its absolute magnitude. The persistence of the patterns of associations suggests that they represent meaningful behavioral contributors rather than purely statistical artifacts.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, most sociodemographic variables in our study were dichotomized (e.g., race dichotomized to White vs. racialized), which likely reduced their variability and weakened their predictive power. However, this does not fully account for their relatively low importance in our models. For example, age, analyzed as a continuous variable, also demonstrated low importance, suggesting that sociodemographic features as a group may have limited predictive power for the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap. Future research could examine whether alternative variable representations, such as continuous measures or more fine-grained categories, yield different results and clarify how these differences interact with the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap.

The cross-sectional design limits inference about developmental change or causality. In addition, the sample composition was skewed (male-heavy, small OCD subgroup), which may restrict the generalizability of findings. Moreover, both internalizing symptoms and the adaptive functioning component of the FSIQ–ABAS gap were measured using caregiver-report instruments, raising the possibility of shared method variance. The influence of caregiver perceptions and stress on both CBCL internalizing symptom scores and adaptive-functioning scores potentially inflates the association between internalizing symptoms and the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap.

Several modeling limitations should also be acknowledged. The absence of an independent test set means that generalization to a fully external sample was not evaluated. In addition, the median split was sample-dependent and it simplified a continuous construct. However, sensitivity analyses using both a 1-SD discrepancy threshold and a continuous-outcome model suggested that the main behavioral contributors were not driven solely by the median-based outcome definition. The moderate model performance indicated that additional factors such as intervention history, school supports, medical complexity, family background, or executive-function measures, may be needed to better explain the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap.

While both cognitive and adaptive measures are standardized and reliable, any difference between them may partly reflect regression to the mean. Because our primary analyses examined correlates of this difference rather than predicting its raw magnitude, the potential impact of this artifact is expected to be small.

Conclusion

This study identifies that cognitive-adaptive functioning gap was associated with higher FSIQ, internalizing problems, social-communication differences, and to a lesser degree ADHD and OCD traits as well as externalizing problems. Sociodemographic factors, including sex, age, and sociodemographic status, were also related to the gap, reflecting the diverse influences on cognitive and adaptive functioning.

These findings improve our understanding of the cognitive-adaptive functioning gap in neurodivergent populations and suggest that focusing support on key associated factors, such as internalizing problems may help to reduce this gap. Screening for mental health symptoms in neurodivergent youth, even at subthreshold levels, could provide opportunities for early support that improves real-world functioning.

Future research should explore these factors in longitudinal designs, test finer-grained sociodemographic measures, and evaluate additional variables such as medical history, therapy participation, and school environment. External validation in independent datasets will also be critical to confirm the generalizability of our model’s findings.
