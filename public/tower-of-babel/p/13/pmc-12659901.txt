Comparative evaluation of the fracture resistance and mode of failure of new esthetic post and core systems with different lengths of remaining gutta-percha: An in vitro study
Authors: Prerna Jangir, Neetu Jindal, Renu Aggarwal, Grusha Gupta, Shubha V. Samorekar, Kushal Bhuskute
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aim:This study aims to evaluate and compare the fracture resistance and mode of failure of endodontically treated teeth restored with new esthetic post and core systems with different lengths of remaining gutta-percha.Materials and Methods:Ninety-one extracted human mandibular premolars were used in this study. Following endodontic treatment, the samples were divided into four groups: A positive control group (n = 13) and three experimental groups (Glass Fiber Post, Quartz Fiber Post, and Dentapreg Pin Post) (n = 26 each). Each experimental group was further subdivided into two subgroups based on the length of remaining gutta-percha (4 mm or 6 mm). The samples then underwent post-space preparation, post-cementation, and core build-up, followed by fracture resistance testing under a universal testing machine and evaluation of the mode of failure using radiovisiography.Results:Data were statistically analyzed through SPSS at a confidence interval of 95%. Significant differences were found among groups (P < 0.001). Glass Fiber Post with 4 mm gutta-percha showed the highest fracture resistance (240.04 ± 12.07 N), while the control group had the lowest (50.79 ± 7.67 N). The mode of failure among Dentapreg and glass fiber posts was partial core debonding, but quartz fiber posts showed a mixed mode of failure (partial and complete core debonding and root fracture).Conclusion:Glass fiber posts outperformed quartz fiber and Dentapreg pin posts at 4 mm gutta-percha, but showed nonsignificant differences with Dentapreg posts at 6 mm gutta-percha.

INTRODUCTION

The loss of tooth structure due to preexisting decay can cause biomechanical failure in teeth that have undergone endodontic treatment. Additional factors contributing to increased mechanical and biological failures include the interaction between bacteria and dentin, the effects of irrigants, and diminished tactile sensitivity, which can make it difficult to detect functional overload.[1] Thus, a good coronal restoration is crucial for endodontically treated teeth, as it minimizes coronal leakage and restores function.[2] Good case selection is necessary to determine the need for a post in a decayed tooth. The amount of remaining tooth structure plays a key role in selecting the appropriate post.[3]

Cast post systems (custom-made) were first introduced in 1930. Customised post- and core systems have the benefit of being easy to remove and fitting properly in the root canal space, even within oval canals.[4] It reduces the possibility of core separation and functions as a single unit. However, these systems have some drawbacks, including root fracture due to high modulus of elasticity,[56] discoloration, increased chairside time, temporisation, and the potential for opaque metal posts to show through thin cervical sections and semi-translucent all-ceramic crowns, affecting the restoration’s esthetic result.[7]

The introduction of prefabricated post systems in the 1930s marked a significant development, with various materials being utilised, including metal, carbon fiber, zirconia, quartz fiber, polyethylene fiber, and glass fiber.[8] These posts offer several advantages, including reduced clinical time, simplified placement, cost savings, and enhanced esthetic appeal.[910]

However, the drawbacks encountered after fiber post placement were difficulty in removal due to good bond strength with the root canal, lower tensile strength, and the potential for radiolucent properties to make them difficult to retrieve when concealed under composite or all-ceramic restorations.[11]

Dentin thickness is a crucial factor in determining the success of endodontic treatment. During root canal shaping, residual dentin thickness (RDT) plays a significant role in limiting instrumentation to prevent weakening of the root canal wall. Studies have shown a direct correlation between root strength and RDT. Adequate dentin thickness, particularly a minimum of 1 mm circumferentially along the entire root length, is essential to prevent complications such as strip perforation and vertical root fracture.[12] A thorough understanding of root anatomy and regional dentin thickness is therefore vital for effective treatment planning.[13]

When placing the post, the minimum length for the post should be two-thirds of the root length, leaving a minimum of 3–5 mm of gutta-percha to provide resistance to occlusal loads, maintain an adequate seal, and ensure optimal stress distribution.[1415] Numerous studies suggest retaining 4–5 mm of apical gutta-percha to maintain a better apical seal.[1617] Cementation of the post using dual-cure resin cement provides greater bond strength and a positive impact on fracture resistance.[18]

Innovative pin posts were introduced in the early 2000s for tooth rebuilding after endodontic therapy by Dentapreg. These posts are known for their safety, rapidity, pioneering design, and relatively minimal invasiveness. Due to their flexibility, they can follow the natural contour of the root canal without requiring additional instrumentation, thereby conserving root dentin. By maintaining the tooth’s natural biomechanics, this adaptable design concept reduces the risk of root fracture.[19]

Despite the many advantages of fiber post systems, the longevity of post systems remains a concern. Core debonding may be a cause of fiber post failure, which could ultimately lead to crown or core failure and microleakage. Both the core and cement are affected by stress.[20]

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the fracture resistance and failure modes of endodontically treated teeth restored with different esthetic post systems (glass fiber, quartz fiber, and Dentapreg pin posts) and core build-ups, considering two varying lengths of remaining gutta-percha (4 mm and 6 mm).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 91 extracted human mandibular premolars with a single root were included in this study. Teeth exhibiting visible fracture lines in the root, previous endodontic treatment, or developmental anomalies were excluded from the study.

Preparation of the samples

Samples were stored in distilled water at room temperature for subsequent procedures. Each tooth was decoronated horizontally 2 mm coronal to the cementoenamel junction (CEJ), perpendicular to its long axis, using a diamond disc at high speed with a straight handpiece for the experimental groups and 5 mm for the positive control group. The working length was established 1 mm short of the apical foramen. Root canals were prepared using a crown-down technique up to a master apical file size F3 (Dentsply Maillefer). After each instrumentation, canals were irrigated with 10 mL of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) and dried with paper points. Canals were obturated with F3 ProTaper gutta-percha cones and AH Plus sealer.

Post cementation and core build-up for groups 2 and 3

The post space was conditioned with ParaBond Non-Rinse Conditioner (Coltene Whaledent, USA) for 30 s using the applicator tip. Excess conditioner was dried with a three-way syringe for 2 s. ParaBond Adhesive A and B were then applied for 30 s, dried for 2 s, and light-cured (Woodpecker LED curing light, China) for 20 s. The post was cemented with ParaCore dual-cure resin cement (Coltene Whaledent, USA) and light-cured for 30 s. Core formers (Prime Dental Products Pvt Ltd.) were used to build up the core to 4 mm from the CEJ and light-cured for 30 s.

Post cementation and core build-up for group 4

A self-cure bonding agent (ParaBond Adhesive A and B) was applied to the canal and light-cured. The canal was then filled with dual-cure resin cement (ParaCore Dual Cure Resin Cement), starting from the apex and progressing coronally. Dentapreg Pin Posts were gently inserted into the canal and light-cured for 30 s. The core was built up using a core former [Figure 1].

Testing procedure

Each sample was embedded in an acrylic block and subjected to a fracture resistance test using a universal testing machine. A static compressive load was applied perpendicular to the long axis of the tooth, and the initial failure load was recorded in Newtons. The mode of failure was evaluated radiographically [Figure 2].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, followed by two-way analysis of variance and Tukey’s post hoc test.

RESULTS

Significant differences were observed among the groups (P < 0.001). Group 2a (Glass Fiber Post) exhibited the highest fracture resistance (240.04 ± 12.07), followed by Group 4a (Dentapreg Pin Post) (175.03 ± 2.93), Group 4b (Dentapreg Pin Post) (134.64 ± 2.71), Group 2b (Glass Fiber Post) (133.54 ± 9.99), Group 3a (Quartz Fiber Post) (116.23 ± 5.82), Group 3b (Quartz Fiber Post) (77.14 ± 4.77), and Group 1 (Positive Control Group, without post) showed the lowest fracture resistance (50.79 ± 7.67) [Table 1 and Graph 1].

The mode of failure was evaluated clinically and radiographically. The modes of failure observed were as follows. In Group 1 (Positive Control Group), the failure mode was a complete core fracture. Group 2a (Glass Fiber Post) exhibited partial core debonding, Group 2b (Glass Fiber Post) also showed partial core debonding. In Group 3a (Quartz Fiber Post), both partial and complete core debonding and fracture were observed. Group 3b (Quartz Fiber Post) experienced a complete core fracture and root fracture. Finally, Groups 4a and 4b (Dentapreg Pin Post) both demonstrated partial core debonding.

DISCUSSION

Endodontically treated teeth that are severely decayed often require a post to enhance fracture resistance and retain the core restoration. While post dislodgement can be managed with re-cementation, root fractures usually result in tooth loss. The reduced fracture resistance in endodontically treated teeth is attributed to several factors, including loss of tooth structure, changes in dentinal properties, and the effects of endodontic procedures.[21]

The selection of a post and core system with a suitable modulus of elasticity is crucial for ensuring optimal fracture resistance and retention. Esthetic posts, including zirconia posts, quartz fiber posts, polyethylene fiber posts, glass fiber posts, etc., offer several benefits, notably the preservation of remaining dentin thickness and good elastic properties.[8] However, these posts also have drawbacks, such as core debonding or root fracture. This study investigated the fracture resistance and modes of failure of three esthetic post systems: Glass fiber posts, Quartz fiber posts, and Dentapreg pin posts.

The results of this study demonstrate that, with 4 mm of gutta-percha remaining in the apical third, Glass fiber posts exhibit maximum fracture resistance (240.04 N), followed by Dentapreg pin posts (175.03 N) and Quartz fiber posts (116.23 N). Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference among the groups (P < 0.001). In contrast, with 6 mm of remaining gutta-percha, Dentapreg pin posts and Glass fiber posts show comparable fracture resistance values (134.64 N and 133.54 N, respectively), whereas Quartz fiber posts exhibit lower resistance (77.14 N). The differences among the 6 mm groups were non-significant.

Numerous studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between fiber post length and fracture resistance in endodontically treated teeth. Moreover, retention is directly influenced by post length, with longer posts generally providing greater retention. Isidor et al. have highlighted the importance of post length, demonstrating a direct correlation between increased post length and improved survival rates, likely due to enhanced retention and optimal stress distribution.[22] The ability of a post to absorb stress is influenced by its volume and length. Longer posts with greater volume tend to distribute forces more effectively, reducing stress concentrations on the root dentin. In contrast, shorter posts may compromise retention and increase fracture risk due to inadequate stress distribution.[20] The present study suggests that preserving 4 mm of gutta-percha enhances fracture resistance compared to 6 mm of gutta-percha.

A monoblock approach was employed to fabricate the post and core, using a single composite resin material for both post cementation and core construction. This technique promoted a consistent bond between the post and tooth, minimising interfacial variability. The restoration is subject to a range of stresses, including tension, compression, and torsion. A horizontal compressive force was selected for this study due to its potential to generate substantial stresses within the root dentine, which can lead to restoration failure.[23]

All failure modes, including endodontic failure, fracture of the crown or post, crown displacement, post debonding, and root fracture, were deemed absolute failures. Restoration and tooth assessment were conducted through clinical evaluation and radiographic examination.[24] Complete core fracture was observed in the positive control group, while glass fiber posts with 4 mm and 6 mm of remaining gutta-percha exhibited partial core debonding. Quartz fiber posts with 4 mm of remaining gutta-percha exhibited both partial and complete core debonding, as well as core fracture. In contrast, those with 6 mm of remaining gutta-percha showed complete core fracture and root fracture. Dentapreg Pin posts with both 4 mm and 6 mm of remaining gutta-percha showed partial core debonding. When the post-core system is subjected to loading, bond failure can compromise its integrity, rendering the post and remaining root more susceptible to stress.

In the present study, glass fiber posts exhibited partial core debonding as the mode of failure in both subgroups. Significantly, no root fractures were observed, likely due to the flexibility of the posts. Their modulus of elasticity, being close to that of dentin, enabled effective stress absorption around the root.[25] Applied forces generated microfissures that did not propagate to the cemento-dentinal junction.

The Dentapreg pin post also exhibited partial core debonding. This may be attributed to the lower stress distribution in the middle third of the pin compared to the coronal third. The flexibility of the pin allows it to follow the root’s natural anatomy, providing micromechanical retention through luting cement and the addition of multiple pins. This design reduces the risk of root fracture by preserving the tooth’s original biomechanics. Furthermore, the use of multiple Dentapreg pin posts may have enhanced post reshaping by forming a uniform unit. The increased surface area helps to reduce fissure formation by distributing tensile stress over a larger area of dentin or luting cement, thereby creating a monoblock that reinforces the tooth structure.[26]

Quartz fiber posts with 4 mm of remaining gutta-percha exhibited both partial and complete core debonding, as well as core fracture. In contrast, samples with 6 mm of remaining gutta-percha displayed complete core fracture and root fracture. This may be attributed to the uneven distribution of forces along the root length, potentially due to the inherent rigidity of quartz fiber posts, which are composed of pure silica crystals.[27]

Post retention and fracture resistance are influenced by several factors, including crown ferrule, post material, post length, and the type of resin cement used. This study focused specifically on the effects of post material and post length. The placement of a crown ferrule is an important factor in enhancing fracture resistance in teeth. However, ferrules and crowns were excluded from this study’s methodology to prevent additional variables from complicating the interpretation of load testing results, as crown restorations can cancel out differences between post systems.[28] Raygot et al. noted that post and core restorations are the primary source of crown retention in clinical cases where minimal coronal tooth structure remains, and crown lengthening or a suitable ferrule is not feasible.[15]

A key limitation of this study lies in its use of static loading tests to evaluate fracture resistance. Future studies would benefit from a more comprehensive approach, incorporating dynamic loading and thermocycling, alongside long-term clinical trials to assess the performance of fiber-reinforced posts.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this study, it can be concluded that the fracture resistance of endodontically treated teeth is significantly influenced by post material and post length. The present study demonstrated better fracture resistance with 4 mm of remaining gutta-percha compared to 6 mm in all post systems, with glass fiber posts showing significantly better performance than all other groups.
