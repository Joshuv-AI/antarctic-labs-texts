Barriers for adherence to diabetic retinopathy screening among Saudi adult population, 2023
Authors: Abdulrahman M. A. Alamri, Manar M. H. Alamri, Rayan A. Alolayani, Shaima Y. A. Assiri, Rayan F. Z. Alshehri, Saud Q. A. Alshabab, Shatha S. M. Alshahrani, Razan S. Aloufi, Amjad M. Al Thamr
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:One common and blinding consequence of diabetes is diabetic retinopathy. However, adherence to regular screening remains a challenge for Saudi adults. The goal of this study is to investigate and identify the barriers that hinder Saudi diabetic adults from adhering to screening for diabetic retinopathy.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This cross-sectional study was conducted in a sample of adult diabetic patients throughout Saudi Arabia. Data was collected using a self-administered validated questionnaire comprising following five primary sections: sociodemographic information, profile of diabetes, knowledge of diabetic retinopathy, behaviors, and hurdles to diabetic retinopathy screening. Sample selected using snowball sampling technique. Questionnaire was converted into Google Forms and was shared with study participants via E-mail and several social media sites, including Facebook and WhatsApp during November and December 2023. Data analyzed using SPSS; mean and standard deviation were calculated for continuous variables, whereas categorical data was presented as frequency and percentage. Logistic regression analysis determined barriers for adherence to screening for diabetic retinopathy.RESULTS:The mean age of the study group was 61.6 ± 15 years, and 58.3% were female. Out of 393 respondents, 71% had undergone retinopathy screening in the past few months; 29.0% had a history of diabetic retinopathy. Participants who knew about retinopathy were eight times more likely to have eye screening compared to those who did not know about retinopathy (odds ratio = 8.05, P < 0.001). Less screening chance was observed among those who had acquired their knowledge from the Internet (odds ratio = 0.23, P = 0.002) or relatives (odds ratio = 0.21, P = 0.002) compared to those who had been advised by their physicians.CONCLUSION:These findings underscored the multifaceted nature of barriers to regular screenings within the Saudi Arabian healthcare system. By understanding and addressing these barriers, healthcare systems can enhance the effectiveness of screening programs and improve health outcomes.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy, a microvascular consequence of diabetes mellitus, continues to be the primary cause of blindness and visual impairment globally.[1] The illness develops slowly, frequently with no outward signs in the beginning. One very successful treatment for preventing vision loss caused by diabetic retinopathy is laser photocoagulation.[2] Quick and precise diagnosis of sight-threatening retinopathy before the onset of symptoms is critical to the effectiveness of this strategy.[3] Regular screenings are imperative for early identification and prompt action in order to prevent irreversible vision loss.[4] According to established guidelines, all patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and those diagnosed with type 1 diabetes mellitus 5 years previously should undergo an annual fundal examination.[5] Despite these guidelines, not much progress has been made toward the achievement of the St. Vincent Declaration’s 1989 goal of reducing global blindness from diabetic retinopathy by one-third in 5 years.[678]

Saudi Arabia has experienced an alarming rise in the incidence of diabetes mellitus in recent decades. The incidence has rapidly increased owing to a combination of factors: genetic predispositions, dietary changes, and sedentary lifestyles.[910] According to recent epidemiological studies, nearly one in four persons in Saudi Arabia suffer from diabetes, making it a serious public health concern.[11] With such a high prevalence, the risk of diabetes-related complications, including diabetic retinopathy, becomes a looming threat.

Prior research has found that adherence rates of Saudi individuals were much below the ideal, pointing to a serious weakness in preventive healthcare practices.[12] Because of these low screening rates, opportunities to stop diabetic retinopathy-related visual loss are missed. Adherence to screening is significantly influenced by one’s ability to access healthcare services, particularly specialized eye care facilities.[13] Geographic barriers, coupled with availability and distribution of screening centers, may impact the ability of individuals to undergo regular screenings.[14] Furthermore, socioeconomic factors, such as financial constraints and healthcare costs, can act as significant barriers to preventive healthcare services.[15] Assessing the impact of socioeconomic status on adherence to diabetic retinopathy screening can identify vulnerable populations. Furthermore, the absence of a standardized nationally based screening program results in screening practices being influenced by a number of variables, such as funding issues, historical events, and local moral policy.[16] As a result, a large number of patients do not undergo screening. Although digital retinal photography is thought to be the best modality for a program of systematic screening, areas that lack this technology rely on the regular physician-led opportunistic screening efforts.[17] Thus, understanding the unique sociocultural, economic, and healthcare system-related factors that contribute to low adherence rates is essential for the development of effective strategies to address this public health challenge.

Therefore, this research revealed factors that affect adherence to diabetic retinopathy screening by Saudi diabetic adults. By comprehensively examining cultural dynamics, healthcare accessibility, and socioeconomic factors, the study aimed to contribute nuanced insights that would inform the development of tailored interventions to improve screening adherence.

Materials and Methods

An analytical cross-sectional research was carried out on a sample of adult diabetic patients throughout Saudi Arabia from November 16 to December 31, 2023. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board Vide Letter No. ECM#2023-3211 dated 15/11/2023, and written informed consent was taken from all participants in the study.

The study’s eligibility requirements included being citizens and residents of Saudi Arabia, at least 18 years old, and living with either type of diabetes. Participants had to have a computer or a mobile phone and educated enough to fill out the survey on their history on their own. Illiterate participants were excluded. The participants were recruited using snowball sampling approaches and convenience. Data collectors from all parts of Saudi Arabia disseminated a validated questionnaire to members of their community.

A G*Power was used to determine the sample size. Assuming the population's estimated percentage of diabetic retinopathy of 19.7% based on a prior study,[1819] a power of 95%, alpha error of 0.3, effect size of 0.1, a minimum calculated sample size was 269. The sample size was increased to 400 participants to compensate for nonresponse or missing data.

A self-administered survey was taken from the study by Dervan et al.[20] Following both forward and backward translation, the completed form was piloted with 20 adult volunteers to ensure its face validity, and any necessary modifications were made. Reliability for questions testing knowledge was 0.7 using Cronbach’s α test. The questionnaire was converted into an electronic format using Google Forms, which was then shared via E-mail and several social media sites, including Facebook and WhatsApp. The questionnaire comprised the following primary sections: sociodemographic information, a profile of diabetes, an evaluation of knowledge of diabetic retinopathy, and hurdles to diabetic retinopathy screening.

The sociodemographic data included information on age, gender, education level, region, and place of residence. Diabetes mellitus type, duration of diabetes, history of diabetic retinopathy, family history of diabetic retinopathy, and presence of cataract, glaucoma, or other eye conditions were all included in the diabetic profile. Information regarding diabetic retinopathy covered topics such as participants' prior eye screening history, sources of information, awareness of diabetic retinopathy's visual problems, available treatments, and how frequently participants had their eyes examined. The final section asked participants about obstacles to getting their eyes screened. Prior to beginning the data collection, the research team carried out a pilot study to confirm the viability and accessibility of the web tool.

Based on a self-reported history of having a dilated eye test within the last 12 months, participants were divided into two main groups. The screened group was made up of individuals who said they had had an eye examination in the previous year. On the other hand, individuals who had not had an eye examination in the previous few months were put in the unscreened group. We measured knowledge, attitude, and barriers toward diabetic retinopathy screening in both the groups.

SPSS software version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was utilized for data management and analysis. Numerical variables were characterized by the mean and standard deviation, whereas nominal and categorical variables were described by frequency and percentage. To determine the differences between people who had undergone screening and those who had not, univariate and multivariate analyses were performed. The predictors impacting the chance of getting the right screening were evaluated using odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For the multiple logistics’ regression, variables that showed significance (P < 0.05) in the univariate analyses were included.

Results

A total of 393 patients participated in our study. Of the surveyed participants, 279 (71%) had reported that they had undergone an eye examination in the past year, whereas 114 (29%) had not had any eye screening. The prevalence of a history of diabetic retinopathy in our study was 29.3%. Of our diabetic participants, 114 (29%) had never undergone an eye examination. Nearly a quarter (22.9%) of the participants were 45–54 years old. The mean age of the study group was 61.6 ± 15 years, 58.3% of whom were female. Educational levels varied; 48% had a bachelor’s degree, 32.6% had completed high school or less, and 12.2% had a diploma. The majority of the study participants lived in urban areas (80.2%), 34.1% were from the central region, 28% from the Western region, 22.4% from the Eastern province, and 15.3% from the Southern area [Table 1].

Of the participants, 46.6% reported that they had type 2 diabetes mellitus but did not require insulin, 24.2% had type 2 diabetes mellitus requiring insulin, and the remaining (29.3%) had type 1 diabetes mellitus. The mean duration of diabetes was 14 years [Table 1].

The demographics of participants who reported having undergone eye screening included being older (24%), female (58.5%), having a bachelor's degree (48%), living in the central region (33.7%), being from an urban area (81.4%), and having type 2 diabetes mellitus (44.8%) not requiring insulin. From the screened participants, 36.2% had a history of diabetic retinopathy, 31.2% had a family history of diabetic retinopathy, 38.4% had cataract, 8.2% had glaucoma, and 22.2% had other eye diseases [Table 1].

The unscreened participants were more likely to have no knowledge of the definition of diabetic retinopathy compared to the screened group (81.6% vs. 35.5%, P < 0.001). However, the unscreened group was less likely to have gotten their knowledge from a physician compared to the screened group (29.7% vs. 62.2%, P < 0.001). Furthermore, 43.9% of them did not know treatment options for diabetic retinopathy compared to 22.2% from the screened group [Table 1].

For unscreened participants, the main obstacles were the lack of knowledge about the diabetic retinopathy (54.4%) and high prices (16.7%). For the screened participants, the major obstacle to not going for eye examination was lack of knowledge about diabetic retinopathy (51.4%) followed by inability to access eye care (25.2%) [Table 2].

Table 3 shows the factors related to undergoing screening for diabetic retinopathy. Patients who answered “Yes” to the question “Do you know about retinopathy?” were 8.05 times more likely to undergo screening than those who answered “No” (odds ratio = 8.05, 95% CI [4.72–13.72], P < 0.001). Those who answered “I don’t know” were 0.08 times less likely to undergo screening than those who answered “No” (0.08 [0.04–0.17], P=<0.001). Less chances of undergoing screening were observed among those who learnt about retinopathy through Internet (odds ratio = 0.23, 95% CI [0.09–0.58], P = 0.002) or relatives (odds ratio = 0.21, 95% CI [0.08–0.57], P = 0.002) compared to those who learnt about retinopathy from their physician. No other significant associations were observed.

Discussion

The rate of diabetes in Saudi Arabia is now very alarming,[11] which makes early detection of diabetic retinopathy crucial for the prevention of vision impairment and blindness. Our findings have shed light on various barriers that contribute to low rates of adherence to screening and provide insights that could inform targeted interventions to improve screening rates.

In our study, the prevalence of self-reported diabetic retinopathy was 29.3% of our diabetic participants. This aligns with data from the International Diabetes Federation, which projects that around one-third of all diabetics globally develop diabetic retinopathy.[21] Some studies have explored ethnic and racial disparities in the prevalence of diabetic retinopathy.[222324] Variations in genetic factors and healthcare access may contribute to the differences in diverse populations.[25]

Our study reveals that 29% of the diabetes patients had never undergone an eye examination, indicating that a significant proportion neglected the screening guideline. The literature has reported varied screening rates for diabetic retinopathy over the last two decades. In the early 2000s, rates ranged from 30% to 50%, reflecting limited awareness and healthcare infrastructure.[26] Progress was noted in the mid to late 2000s, with rates between 50% and 70%, attributed to increased awareness and technological advancements.[27] Recent years have seen the integration of technology, yet challenges such as economic factors and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic have introduced complexities to screening rates.[28]

One significant barrier identified in our study was the underestimation of the significance of screening for diabetic retinopathy. Although diabetes is very common in Saudi Arabia, 48.9% of individuals remain unaware of diabetic retinopathy. Besides, the lack of awareness regarding the importance of diabetic retinopathy screening has been a significant barrier to regular screening, as highlighted in several studies.[29303132] This lack of screening has been reported to be prevalent among diabetic patients in various countries, including China, Saudi Arabia, and India,[303334] and associated with delayed diagnosis and management of diabetic retinopathy.[3536] These studies emphasized the need for increased awareness of diabetic retinopathy by diabetic patients and the significance of yearly ophthalmic eye examinations. Additionally, it has been suggested that the promotion of facilities suited for relevant groups and practice-oriented education can help in raising awareness of diabetic retinopathy.[37] Several studies have also highlighted the impact of awareness programs and educational interventions on diabetic retinopathy screening. For instance, the implementation of diabetic retinopathy referral networks, along with public awareness activities, has led to a significant increase in the number of patients screened for diabetic retinopathy.[38] Moreover, the utilization of posters, pamphlets, videos, and educational sessions has been effective in changing the behavior of physicians and increasing awareness of diabetic patients.[39]

Moreover, this study reports that less eye screening was observed among those who acquired their knowledge from the Internet or relatives compared to those who had been advised by their physician. This is consistent with earlier findings that poor screening service utilization has been linked to healthcare professionals' inability to implement ongoing patient education programs about diabetic retinopathy screening.[40] This further emphasizes the importance of healthcare providers’ education of diabetic patients on the significance of regular screening for diabetic retinopathy. Strengthening the communication between healthcare providers and patients, together with incorporating routine screening discussions into diabetes management plans, can contribute to a more supportive healthcare environment.

Financial constraints emerged as another prominent barrier, for 16.7% of unscreened participants reported concerns about the cost associated with diabetic retinopathy screening. Interventions aimed at reducing financial barriers, such as subsidized screening programs or insurance coverage for diabetic retinopathy screening, could be effective in increasing adherence of those facing economic challenges.[41] Depending on the resource availability, screening for diabetic retinopathy in diabetics could be initiated.

In addition, healthcare system-related barriers were also evident in our study. Of the unscreened participants, 14.9% cited a lack of accessibility to screening centers as a hindrance to regular screenings. Collaborative efforts between healthcare providers, policymakers, and community organizations are essential for the improvement of the infrastructure and accessibility to screening services. Telemedicine and mobile screening units must be utilized to overcome geographical barriers and significantly improve accessibility to diabetic retinopathy screening centers.

In our study, the prevalence of eye screening was significantly different among various age groups (P = 0.003). A higher prevalence of screening was found in participants aged 45–54 years. A previous study showed that individuals aged 70 years and above with diabetes had a higher likelihood of undergoing dilated fundoscopy compared to those under 40 years old (odds ratio = 1.9, 95% CI = 1.5–2.6).[42] Multiple studies have indicated that older individuals with diabetes are more likely to undergo screening.[4344] It was noted that the utilization of eye care services within a 12-month period was lower for younger individuals (age = 20–39 years, odds ratio = 0.1, 95% CI = 0.01–0.70, P < 0.05).[45] Similarly, a UK-based study demonstrated that younger age was associated with nonattendance (18–34 years, adjusted odds ratio = 1.4, 95% CI = 1.1–1.7; 35–44 years, odds ratio = 1.4, 95% CI = 1.2–1.7).[46]

It was found that the prevalence of eye screening was higher in participants with type 2 diabetes mellitus not using insulin (44.8%). The difference in screening was significantly different according to type of diabetes (P = 0.021). This aligns with a previous study that found that eye care utilization by individuals with diabetes was higher when the treatment involved oral antidiabetic agents and insulin (odds ratio = 2.8, 95% CI = 1.1–7.4, P = 0.161).[45]

Our participants who had cataracts, glaucoma, or other eye diseases showed a significantly higher prevalence of screening. Similarly, individuals with other eye diseases (odds ratio = 1.2, 95% CI = 1.1–1.6) and those who believed that eye examinations were necessary every 6 months (odds ratio = 1.2, 95% CI = 1.1–1.4) had higher odds of undergoing diabetic retinopathy screening.[20] A study by Moss et al., showed that a history of cataract (odds ratio = 2.9, 95% CI = 1.9–4.4, P < 0.0001) was associated with previous dilated eye examination.[47] Hwang et al., in Canada demonstrated that increased eye screening was associated with visual impairment (odds ratio = 2.6, 95% CI = 1.7–3.9, P = 0.00).[48]

However, gender, education level, and region of residence were not significant factors in screening uptake. This was contrary to some previous studies that suggested that younger, male, and lower socioeconomic individuals were less likely to adhere to screening guidelines.[49]

One limitation of this study is the small sample size, which does limit the generalizability of the findings. The data collected in this study relied on self-reported information from participants, which could introduce bias and inaccuracies. As a result, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to the broader population. Additionally, illiterate individuals were excluded from our study. Because the online self-administered questionnaire was self-reported, only individuals who can read, are familiar with Internet technologies, and have Internet access could respond. A larger and more diverse sample size would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the issue and increase the reliability and validity of the study results.

Conclusion

This study has shed light on the significant impact of diabetic retinopathy on public health and emphasized the importance of preventive measures and early detection in mitigating the burden of this vision-threatening complication of diabetes. Several important recommendations for future action have emerged from the analysis of current trends and challenges in the management of diabetic retinopathy. These include the importance of yearly ophthalmic eye examinations and the need for diabetic patients to become more aware of diabetic retinopathy. Additionally, we encourage the promotion of facilities suited for relevant groups and practice-oriented education that can aid in raising awareness of diabetic retinopathy. Strengthening communication between healthcare providers and patients, and incorporating routine screening discussions into diabetes management plans, can contribute to a more supportive healthcare environment. Furthermore, interventions aimed at reducing financial barriers, such as subsidized screening programs or insurance coverage for diabetic retinopathy screening, could increase adherence by those facing economic challenges. By prioritizing these recommendations and coordinating efforts, we can have a future of a minimized burden of diabetic retinopathy and diabetic individuals with better visual health and quality of life.
