Physical function decline due to rehospitalization in older patients with heart failure and its contributing factors
Authors: Takahiro Shimoda, Shinzi Suzuki, Daisuke Mizukoshi, Tomoko Terai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder patients with heart failure often experience decreased physical activity, which reduces their ability to perform activities of daily living. Additionally, this physical function decline is exacerbated with each rehospitalization. However, the extent of this decline with rehospitalization in older patients with heart failure remains unclear, as do the characteristics of this patient group. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the extent of physical function decline due to rehospitalization in older patients with heart failure and identify the factors associated with it.MethodsPatients with heart failure aged ≥65 years who were hospitalized twice between September 2019 and June 2022 were enrolled. Patients were evaluated using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) at each admission and discharge. Analysis of variance was used to examine the impact of rehospitalization on physical function.ResultsFifty-seven patients were evaluated in this study. SPPB scores were significantly lower at the second discharge than at the first discharge (P = 0.009). Overall, the SPPB score at the second discharge was 92 % (67–100 %) of that at the first discharge. Furthermore, patients with decreased SPPB scores at the second discharge, compared to those at the first discharge, had significantly lower SPPB scores at the second admission than those who maintained their scores.ConclusionsOlder patients with heart failure experienced a 10 % decline in physical function due to rehospitalization. Moreover, those with decreased physical function at the second discharge had already exhibited reduced physical function at the time of the second admission.Keywords: Physical function decline, Older patients, Heart failure, Frailty, Rehospitalization

Introduction

The population of patients with heart failure (HF) in Japan is rapidly aging [1]. Older patients with HF often experience malnutrition and decreased physical activity, which reduces their ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs). Therefore, comprehensive, multifaceted disease management, as well as appropriate nutritional interventions and exercise interventions, is necessary alongside the treatment of the primary disease [1,2]. Older patients with HF experience sarcopenia and frailty at high rates [3,4], with physical frailty being particularly prevalent, posing significant clinical challenges.

Physical frailty in patients with HF is associated with poor prognoses and frequent hospitalization [3,5]. Current heart rehabilitation guidelines recommend assessing and addressing physical frailty in patients with HF [2]. In this patient group, physical frailty is linked to age, left ventricular ejection fraction, comorbidities, anemia, renal function, and nutritional status [[6], [7], [8]]. Additionally, patients with HF experience physical function decline with each rehospitalization [9]. However, the extent of physical function deterioration with repeated admissions in older patients with HF remains unclear. Moreover, the characteristics of this patient group remain unknown.

This study aimed to clarify the extent of physical function decline associated with rehospitalization in older patients with HF and identify the contributing factors.

Material and methods

Patients

The study included patients with HF aged ≥65 years who were admitted twice to the cardiology department of our hospital between September 2019 and June 2022 with an HF diagnosis and who underwent rehabilitation interventions. Patients who declined physical function assessments, could not be assessed for physical function or had severe cognitive, visual, or hearing impairments that made physical assessment difficult were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital. As this study involves the secondary use of clinical data, approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee, and patient consent was acquired through an opt-out procedure. The study protocol conforms to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

Physical function was assessed using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) [10,11] at each admission and discharge. The SPPB consists of three components—gait speed, a five-time chair stand test, and a balance test—yielding a score out of 12 points, with higher scores indicating better physical function [10]. Clinical background factors were collected from medical records for each admission and included age, sex, body mass index, left ventricular ejection fraction, the New York Heart Association classification, systolic blood pressure on admission, presence of comorbidities (including hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], cerebrovascular disease, and myocardial infarction), previous hospitalization for HF, serum albumin, hemoglobin, brain natriuretic peptide, C-reactive protein, estimated glomerular filtration rate, Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index, Clinical Frailty Scale score at admission, prehospitalization ADLs (Katz index), presence of dementia, Mini-Cog score, care level, presence of institutionalization, history of falls, length of stay, and outcomes. The Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index was calculated using the following formula: 14.89 × serum albumin +41.7 × (current body weight/ideal body weight) [12].

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze clinical background factors for patients admitted twice during the study period. Continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR], 25th–75th percentile) values, and categorical variables as counts (%). Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare total SPPB scores at the first admission, first discharge, second admission, and second discharge. Multiple comparisons were made using the Shaffer method. The SPPB score at the first discharge was 100 %, and the ratio of SPPB scores at subsequent discharges was calculated. Patients whose SPPB scores at the second discharge were lower than those at the first discharge were classified as the decline group. In contrast, patients who maintained their scores or showed improvement were classified as the non-decline group. To determine the factors associated with a decline in SPPB scores, continuous clinical background factors were analyzed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient, while categorical clinical background factors were analyzed using the χ2 test. All statistical analyses were performed using the R and EZR statistical software programs [13], with the significance level set at 5 %.

Results

During the study period, 512 patients were hospitalized, of whom 89 were admitted twice. Of these 89 patients, SPPB assessments were possible at each admission and discharge for 57 individuals. Supplemental Table 1 presents the clinical background factors of the 89 and 57 patients included in the analysis. The median duration until readmission for the 57 patients was 95 days (IQR, 46–228 days). Based on ANOVA, the SPPB score at the time of discharge after the second hospitalization was significantly lower than that at discharge after the first hospitalization (P = 0.009; Fig. 1). Furthermore, when the SPPB score at the time of discharge after the first hospitalization was set at 100 %, the corresponding score after the second hospitalization was 92 % (67–100 %; median change: −1 [−2 to 0]).

Changes in the total Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) score at admission and discharge.

Among the 57 patients who were hospitalized twice, a decrease in SPPB scores at the time of discharge after the second hospitalization compared to that at discharge after the first hospitalization was noted for 30 patients. Table 1 presents the clinical background factors associated with changes in SPPB scores. None of the factors were found to be statistically and significantly associated with changes in SPPB. Table 2 presents the clinical background factors for the decline and non-decline groups. The decline group had a lower prevalence of hypertension and higher Clinical Frailty Scale scores than those of the non-decline group. Additionally, the decline group exhibited a decrease in the total SPPB score and the scores for all three sub-items at the time of the second hospitalization (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In older patients with HF, maintaining physical function is crucial for disease management. The guidelines for managing chronic HF indicate that physical function declines during acute exacerbations and that although function improves with remission, it does not return to pre-exacerbation levels. However, the extent to which physical function deteriorates with rehospitalization remains unclear. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to clarify the impact of rehospitalization on physical function decline in older patients with HF and investigate the associated factors. According to the results of the ANOVA, physical function in the older patients with HF in this study declined by 10 % upon rehospitalization. Furthermore, a comparison between patients whose physical function declined and those with no decline in physical function at discharge after the first hospitalization revealed decreased function levels at the time of admission for the second hospitalization. This finding highlights the importance of preventing rehospitalization, implementing cardiac rehabilitation during hospitalization, and continuing cardiac rehabilitation or providing guidance after discharge for older patients with HF.

In this study, the SPPB score was used as an indicator of physical function. The SPPB consists of the walking speed component, five-time chair stand test, and balance test. Since it does not require any special equipment and is easy to perform, it is widely used in clinical practice [14,15]. The SPPB score has also been reported to be associated with prognosis in patients with heart disease [16], and it serves as the main outcome measure in large-scale clinical trials focusing on older patients with HF [17]. In a previous study, the minimum clinically important difference in the SPPB score was set at 0.5–1.0 [18]. In our study, SPPB improved by more than the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) during hospitalization in both the decline and non-decline groups. While clinical improvement in physical function was observed during hospitalization, the median difference in the SPPB scores between the first and second discharges was −1, indicating a decline greater than the MCID. However, Morisawa et al. reported that prehospitalization physical function did not recover in 21.1 % of patients who underwent major cardiac surgery [19]. Additionally, studies on HF cases have indicated that 41.2 %–50.9 % of patients are discharged in a state requiring care [20,21]. The present study evaluated physical function at discharge for each hospitalization in chronic HF cases requiring treatment for acute exacerbations. The results revealed that 52.6 % of patients who were rehospitalized experienced a physical function decline of approximately 10 %. In general, physical function decline leads to frailty and sarcopenia, resulting in limitations pertaining to ADLs and poor survival. Therefore, it is necessary to intervene in cases of physical function decline among older patients with HF before they reach a state requiring care.

In the present study, patients whose physical function declined upon rehospitalization exhibited decreased total SPPB scores and scores for all three sub-items at the time of the second admission. The median SPPB score at the first discharge in the decline group was 9, indicating a state of frailty [10], which is defined as a vulnerable state due to reduced physical reserve [22]. In this study, patients discharged in a frail state exhibited a decline in function upon rehospitalization.

The results of the present study indicate that some patients experience a significant physical function decline from the time of discharge after the first hospitalization to the time of admission for the second hospitalization. Notably, their physical function remains reduced despite cardiac rehabilitation during hospitalization. Furthermore, 37.1 % of the patients with HF are unable to regain their pre-hospitalization ADL levels after discharge [23]. In the present study, the participants started the rehabilitation intervention at a median of 1 day after admission. Although early rehabilitation intervention was possible through collaboration with the attending physicians, 52.6 % of the patients still experienced a decline in physical function. Therefore, early rehabilitation intervention alone may be insufficient, and further investigation is warranted. Reduced activity levels owing to prolonged bed rest during hospitalization could be one of the factors contributing to the decline in physical function. In Japan, rehabilitation is typically provided in 20-min units; thus, even with early rehabilitation intervention, activity levels outside the scheduled sessions remain important. In particular, patients with HF often require syringe pumps for medication management, and the use of syringe pumps has been reported to reduce physical activity levels [24]. Therefore, strategies to maintain higher activity levels during hospitalization are needed. One such strategy is monitoring physical activity. In fact, monitoring the physical activity of hospitalized cardiac patients undergoing cardiac rehabilitation can potentially improve physical activity levels and increase the six-minute walk distance [25]. Moreover, for older patients with HF, it is essential to continue rehabilitation after discharge, develop more effective exercise programs to improve physical function during hospitalization and provide exercise and lifestyle guidance after discharge. Excess salt intake and poor medication adherence are common causes of HF rehospitalization [26], and cardiac rehabilitation focused on exercise therapy and patient education to effectively prevent rehospitalization [[27], [28], [29], [30]] is recommended [2]. However, in Japan, only 33 % of patients with HF undergo rehabilitation during hospitalization, and 7 % continue with outpatient rehabilitation [31]. Promoting outpatient cardiac rehabilitation or continued rehabilitation is necessary, utilizing the long-term insurance system. Moreover, interval training and cognitive tasks combined with aerobic exercise are more effective in improving physical function than moderate-intensity training.26–28 Kitzman et al. reported that individualized exercise therapy focuses on muscle strength, balance, gait, and physical fitness in older patients having HF with an average age of 73.1 ± 8.5 years [17]. In the present trial, the program was tailored to each patient's physical function level and implemented with a progressive loading approach, resulting in high adherence. Our findings suggest that, even in older patients with HF, initiating rehabilitation during hospitalization, building a trusting relationship, and providing an individualized program may help maintain high adherence. Furthermore, given the high prevalence of frailty and sarcopenia among older patients with HF in Japan, individualized exercise therapy focusing on muscle strength, as recommended in the guidelines [2], is likely to be effective. Furthermore, studies on older adults have demonstrated that individuals with frailty are more likely to improve in physical function compared to those who are robust [32]. Studies focusing on patients with HF have similarly reported that patients with frailty are more likely to improve their physical function and achieve better outcomes [17,33]. However, future studies are needed to establish evidence-based interventions in exercise therapy and cardiac rehabilitation for patients with frailty with HF.

In recent years, not only standard cardiac rehabilitation but also neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) and nutritional interventions have been recognized as important aspects of rehabilitation. A meta-analysis of electrical stimulation therapy demonstrated that NMES improves exercise tolerance, muscle strength, and quality of life in patients with HF. Furthermore, a study targeting frail HF patients aged 75 years or older examined the additional effect of NMES combined with exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation initiated early during hospitalization and reported improvements in muscle strength and SPPB scores [34]. In addition, no adverse events associated with early in-hospital NMES were observed. Regarding nutritional interventions, synergistic effects with exercise therapy are expected; however, evidence pertaining to older, frail patients with HF remains limited [35]. With the aging population, an increasing number of patients may not achieve sufficient improvement in physical function with conventional cardiac rehabilitation programs alone, highlighting the need for further evidence supporting these intervention strategies in the future.

This study has some limitations, including its sample size and its single-center design. Consequently, we were unable to fully examine the factors associated with a decline in SPPB scores. A large-scale, multicenter cohort study is needed in the future. A more detailed analysis should be conducted to identify risk factors for physical function decline due to rehospitalization. Furthermore, the number of previous hospitalizations for HF before the study period is unknown. Therefore, research monitoring changes in physical function over time in patients with newly diagnosed HF is necessary. Additionally, rehabilitation for the study participants was provided based on HF treatment guidelines, early mobilization programs, and cardiac rehabilitation guidelines. However, specific rehabilitation content and frequency were individually prescribed by the attending therapist. Furthermore, post-discharge rehabilitation was not evaluated, and information on whether the participants received cardiac rehabilitation or rehabilitation under the long-term care insurance system after discharge was not available. Hence, further investigation, including the details of rehabilitation during hospitalization and post-hospitalization, is required. This study only evaluated physical function during hospitalization, and post-discharge changes in physical function are unknown. Physical function may have changed with the implementation of rehabilitation and changes in activity levels after discharge. Evaluating the transition in physical function and living conditions after discharge could facilitate strategies for maintaining physical function in older patients with HF. Moreover, this was a retrospective study that evaluated only patients who were rehospitalized. To clarify the decline in physical function due to rehospitalization, a prospective study comparing changes in physical function between patients who were rehospitalized and those who were not is necessary.

Conclusion

Physical function in older patients with HF declined by 10 % upon rehospitalization. Patients whose physical function declined after rehospitalization exhibited decreased physical function at the time of admission. Therefore, continuing rehabilitation after discharge or developing exercise therapy programs during hospitalization aimed at improving physical function more effectively is necessary for older patients with HF.
