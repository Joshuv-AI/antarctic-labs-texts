A ddPCR method for simultaneous detection and quantification of Salmonella enterica, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenes, and Bacillus cereus in foods
Authors: Xueqi Sun, Jian Xiao, Haiyan Wei, Kai Chen, Meidan Liang, Zhishen Huang, Jiaqian Lao, Xiuming Lin, Shanmei Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The traditional methods for the detection and quantification of foodborne bacteria are time-consuming with the potential for false negative pathogen-free results. Rapid and effective detection and quantification of foodborne pathogenic microorganisms are crucial in prevention and control of foodborne diseases. In the last decade, the technique of droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) has been widely applied for the precise quantification of gene expression. The aim of this study is to establish a quadruplex ddPCR method to detect and quantify simultaneously four major bacterial pathogens, Salmonella enterica serotype Typhi, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenesand Bacillus cereus, potentially co-existing in instant food. A strong linear correlation (r2>0.999) was observed in the ranges of 33-21500 copies/20µL for S. Typhi, 28-18400 copies/20µL for S. aureus, 25-27000 copies/20µL for L. monocytogenes and 15-15600 copies /20µL for B. cereus respectively. For this established ddPCR method, the lower detection limits were 8 copies/20µL (S. Typhi), 7 copies/20µL (S. aureus), 9 copies/20µL (L. monocytogenes) and 7 copies/20µL (B. cereus) respectively. For its evaluation this method was also compared in parallel with the plate counting method using the artificially contaminated food samples with the added pathogens mentioned above. Unpaired t-test showed the results from the two methods were not statistically different but the ddPCR method is superior to the plate counting method with shorter turnaround time, lower detection limit, and more robust reproducibility.

Introduction

Many food safety incidents are reported worldwide every year, and the diseases caused by these incidents have seriously threatened world public health1,2. In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported that 550 million people worldwide had diarrhea caused by consuming contaminated food, among which about 230,000 people died (at a death rate of 0.04%). Foodborne diseases have become the most harmful disease to public health3. There are dozens of known foodborne pathogens, among which the most prominent are Salmonella spp., S. aureus, L. monocytogenes, B. cereus, Escherichia coli O157:H7, Shiga toxin-producing E. coli, Vibrio spp. and etc3. Among those foodborne pathogens, Salmonella spp. are significant cause agents of the global zoonosis. WHO reported that among 582 million cases of 22 foodborne diseases during 2010 to 2015, there were 351,000 deaths and 52,000 deaths were originated by Salmonella spp. making a death ratio of 14.8% and this type of death ranking in the first place4,5. Foodborne Salmonella spp. infection is reported every year around the globe after consuming contaminated food such as contaminated milk powder, meat, crayfish, eggs and melon. Foodborne Salmonella spp. infection is so important but currently the food surveillance of this pathogen has not been sufficiently implemented yet. For example, the detection rate of Salmonella spp. within retail meat products in Shanghai was only 26.1% in 20166. S. aureus is ubiquitously found in nature and produces heat-resistant enterotoxins during its proliferation in food. When the enterotoxin content in 100 g of food reaches 18 µg, food poisoning could be caused. In recent years, the foodborne disease outbreaks caused by S. aureus and its enterotoxins have made this infection among the top three ranked in China7. Food poisoning incidents caused by B. cereus have been reported worldwide8,9. This bacterial contaminant is generally found in rice and noodle products but also detected in eggs, meat, dairy products, and aquatic products with a detection rate of 25.5%10,11. According to the U.S. Center for Disease Control and Prevention, L. monocytogenes can survive and proliferate in refrigerated food for an extended period and is the third dominant cause of death from foodborne diseases in the United States, characterized by insufficient available medical treatments and high mortality. Wang, et al.12. found that the contamination rate of L. monocytogenes in raw materials and intermediate product samples in instant food factories was 16.1%, which shows this pathogen is a significant safety hazard. The current detection of the above four foodborne pathogens mainly relies on the techniques such as selective cultivation and biochemical identification, which are complicated and time-consuming with low accuracy and a high risk of misdetection, especially for the detection with instant food. Therefore, a more rapid, accurate, and high throughput method is urgently needed to monitor the pathogenic risks to food safety.

ddPCR is considered a rapid, accurate, and absolute DNA quantitative PCR method developed in recent years as the third-generation nucleic acid amplification technology13. In its reactions, the individual target DNA molecules are firstly distributed into a such large quantity of droplets as micro-reaction units, to maintain one or no copy of the target DNA molecule in each micro-reaction unit, then the DNA amplification and fluorescence signal detection are carried out, and finally the absolute quantitation of the target DNA molecule is achieved based on the Poisson distribution principle without the need for standard curves12,14,15. At present, ddPCR is mainly used for medical gene mutation analysis16food-derived component analysis17transgenic analysis18and the quantitative detection of some foodborne pathogens19,20. In the literature so far with the best of our understanding, ddPCR has been reported mostly as a singleplex or duplex method for the quantitative detection of pathogens and there are no reports on the simultaneous detection of four bacterial pathogenic bacteria using a quadruplex ddPCR technology for the detection of multiple target genes in the same PCR reactions with a variety of samples of food origins21.

In this study, we developed a quadruplex ddPCR-based method for simultaneous and sensitive detection and quantitation of four health-related pathogens of generic interest: S. Typhi, S. aureus, L. monocytogenes, and B. cereus using a two-channel ddPCR instrument (QX200, Bio-Rad). We demonstrated that this quadruplex ddPCR method is equivalent to the conventional plate counting method in reliability but less time- and labor-consuming.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

The strains used in this study as the target pathogens are S. Typhi (CMCC(B) 50071), S. aureus (ATCC 6538), L. monocytogenes (ATCC 19115), and B. cereus (CICC 10352). The reference strains used for the primer/probe specificity test are E. coli (ATCC 25922), E. coli O157:H7 (NCTC 12900), Enterobacter sakazakii (ATCC 29544), Campylobacter jejuni (CICC 22936), Proteus mirabilis (CMCC(B) 49005), Vibrio vulnificus (ATCC 27562), Vibrio alginolyiicus (ATCC 33787), Vibrio Parahaemolyticus (ATCC 17802), Shigella dysenteriae (CMCC(B) 51105), Shigella flexneri (CMCC(B) 51572), Enterobacter aerogenes (ATCC 13048), Yersinia enterocolitica (CMCC(B) 52204), Shigella sonnei (CMCC(B) 51592), Bacillus circulans (ATCC 4516), Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 6633), Listeria innocua (ATCC 33090), Listeria seeligeri (CICC 21671), Listeria ivanovii (ATCC 19119), Staphylococcus epidermidis (CMCC(B) 26069), Rhodococcus equi (ATCC 6939), Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC 29212), and Streptococcus agalactiae (CICC 10465) from China National Center for Medical Culture Collections (CMCC), China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC), National Collection of Type Cultures (NCTC) of England, or the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC).

Preparation of bacterial cultures and genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction

The 26 bacterial strains listed in Sect. 2.1 were individually inoculated into 10mL nutrient broth medium (HKM, China) and then incubated for 16–18 h at 36℃. The bacterial DNA was extracted by using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China), and finally eluted into 50 µL TE buffer. gDNA samples that were at the required concentration, i.e. 10–100 ng/µL, and met the purity criteria, i.e. A260/A280 = 1.7–1.9, were used in the following experiments.

Selection and in Silico validation of primers and probes

PCR Primers and probes targeting genes invA22 fimY23 ttrA/ttrC24, the gene encoding glutamate synthase (GltS) FMN-binding domain25 COA19 the gene encoding invasion-associated endopeptidase25 hlyA26 essC27 and gyrB28 were subjected to an in silico validation by using the DNAStar 7.1 software (DNASTAR, Inc.,USA). After excluding the genes of which their primers/probes form “hairpin” structures, four single-copy genes namely the ttrA/ttrC gene encoding tetrathionate reductase of S. Typhi, the GltS FMN-binding domain gene of S. aureus, the invasion-related endopeptidase gene of L. monocytogenes, and the essC gene encoding a type VII secretion protein of B. cereus were selected as the targets for the development of the method (Table 1). The probes of S. Typhi, S. aureus, and B. cereus are conjugated to the 6-carboxy-fluorescein (FAM) group, whereas the probe for L. monocytogenes is labeled with hexachloro 6-methyl fluorescein (HEX) group. All the primers and probes were synthesized by BGI (Shanghai, China).

Primer/probe specificity analysis in the qPCR system

gDNA samples from all the 26 strains listed in Sect. 2.1 were used to verify the specificity of the primers/probes (Table 1) designed for the four target pathogens in a qPCR system. Each 25 µL qPCR reaction comprised of 12.5 µL of 2×qPCR Mix (Toyobo Life Science, Japan), 0.75 µL of 10 µmol/L primers, 0.5 µL of 10 µmol/L probe, 2 µL of template gDNA, and 8.5 µL of DNase/RNase-free deionized water. The qPCR reactions were performed with a CFX 96 Deep Well™ Real-Time System (BIO-RAD, USA) under the following conditions: 15 min at 95℃, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 15 s and 60℃ for 1 min.

Establishment of the singleplex DdPCR

The singleplex ddPCR methods were established for S. Typhi, S. aureus, L. monocytogenes, and B. cereus respectively. All the purified gDNA samples were diluted serially by 10 folds (100−10−6). Each PCR reaction comprised of 10 µL of ddPCR Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA), 1.8 µL of 10 µmol/L primers, 0.5 µL of 10 µmol/L probe, 1 µL of template DNA, and DNase/RNase-free deionized water to a final volume of 20 µL. Droplets were then generated with the QX200™ droplet generator (Bio-Rad, USA) followed by transferring the droplets to a 96-well plate for the DNA amplification in a T100™ Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA). The reaction conditions were 95℃ for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 ℃ for 30 s, 60 ℃ for 1 min, denaturation at 98 ℃ for 10 min, and storage at 4 ℃, with a ramp rate of 2 °C/s at each step. The fluorescence signals of the amplification were determined by using the QX200™ Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad, USA) and then analyzed by using the QuantaSoft™ Analysis Pro software (v1.0.596.0525, Bio-Rad, USA, https://www.bio-rad.com) to obtain the copy numbers of the individual target genes.

Establishment and optimization of the quadruplex DdPCR assay

A duplex ddPCR system detecting S. Typhi and L. monocytogenes was established firstly under the conditions of the singleplex ddPCR (described in Sect. 2.5). The primers and probe for B. cereus were then added to the duplex system to develop a triplex ddPCR method. Lastly, the primers and probe for S. aureus were added to the triplex system to establish a quadruplex ddPCR method. The optimal annealing conditions for the quadruplex method were determined with variable periods of time (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 min) and a range of temperature (55–65℃).

Linear range, detection limit, and repeatability of the quadruplex DdPCR method

The mean value of copy number, standard deviation, and relative standard deviation (RSD) were calculated to determine the reproducibility of the quadruplex method. In the meantime, the quantitation results from singleplex PCR method were applied to benchmark the quadruplex ddPCR method.

20 µl gDNA extracts of the individual four bacteria (S. aureus, L. monocytogenes, B. cereus and S. Typhi) were mixed at concentrations of 884.3 pg/µL for S. Typhi, 308.5 pg/µL for S. aureus, 245.3 pg/µL for L. monocytogenes, and 446.0 pg/µL for B. cereus. The DNA mixture was 4-fold serially diluted to a total of 8 concentrations before the DNA amplification in the 20 µl single (n = 1) or quadruplex ddPCR reactions (n = 5). Standard curves were generated with the logarithm of the mean copy number from five measurements as the vertical coordinate, and the logarithm of template DNA concentration as the horizontal coordinate, to determine the linear range and detection limit of the quadruplex ddPCR.

Detection of the food samples artificially contaminated with four target bacteria with the established quadruplex DdPCR method

To prepare the homogenized food suspension, 25 g food samples of pork jerky, juice of sugar cane and water chestnuts, rice vermicelli, and bread were homogenized respectively with 225 mL sterilized saline. 1mL of each food suspension was used for the extraction of bacterial gDNA and after further purification, the purified gDNA samples were used for the quadruplex ddPCR for the preparation of the baselines of the target pathogens.

All four bacteria were inoculated respectively into 10 mL nutrient broth medium and incubated at 36℃ for 16–18 h. The CFUs/mL of the four individual pathogens were then determined with the plate counting method and then the four individual cultures (at approximately 108 CFU/mL each) were added into 1 ml of homogenized food suspension to prepare the diluents of artificially contaminated samples. Finally, gDNA was extracted from all the diluents of the artificially contaminated food samples and eluted into 50 µL TE buffer.

For the ddPCR assay, 10 µL per gDNA sample was taken and mixed with 40 µL diluent (DNase/RNase-free deionized water) in tube #1, then 10 µL was taken from the tube #1 and mixed well with 30 µL diluent in tube #2, finally 4-fold serial dilutions were prepared as tubes #3-#6. 2 µL of the final dilutions was used as the template for the quadruplex ddPCR. After the ddPCR, the copies of the pathogen of the individual pathogens in the 1 ml artificially contaminated food samples homogenate were calculated with the following formula.

Copies of the pathogen in 1 ml artificially contaminated food sample = (Measured copies/µL ×A/B) × dilution factor ×C.

A: PCR reaction volume, 20 µL;

B: Template volume in the PCR reaction system, 2 µL;

C: Total volume of extracted DNA, 50 µL.

The CFUs of each bacterium in the artificially contaminated food homogenate were also quantified by calculation with the volume and cell density(CFU/mL)of individual bacteria added into 1 ml of the homogeneous solution, and then compared with the gene copies obtained from the ddPCR assays to evaluate the reliability of the latter method.

Statistic analysis

RSDs of 5 replicates of each dilution were calculated to describe the repeatability and linear range. For the established quadruplex ddPCR method, RSD ≤ 25% was generally accepted as the cut-off29 when evaluating the reproducibility of a molecular biological method28. The unpaired t-test was conducted to evaluate the significant difference between the results from the singleplex and the quadruplex ddPCRs (n = 5) by using the software SPSS 18.0.

The selected primers/probes described in Sect. 2.1 were used to amplify the amplicons of its target bacteria and the other 25 strains and the results are shown in Fig. 1. The amplification profiles of the two replicates of individual gDNA samples of the target pathogens were very similar while no amplification was observed with the 25 reference strains. These results showed the high specificity of these primers/probes.

Development of a quadruplex DdPCR method

The quadruplex ddPCR system was achieved by adding the template gDNA of the fourth target bacterium S. aureus into the developed triplex system. Cluster aggregation and separation were achieved and improved by further optimizing the annealing temperature at 61℃ and the annealing time for 2 min as shown in Fig. 5. In line with the expectation, there were 16 (24) clusters, representing all the possible combinations of the microdroplet populations generated from the homogenized gDNA templates of the four bacterial pathogens. The positive clusters were concentrated and well-separated for further quantitative analysis.

Profile of the quadruplex ddPCR system established to detect the four pathogenic bacteria simultaneously. NTC: Negative droplet cluster; Sal+: S. Typhi ; Sa+: S. aureus; Lm+: L. monocytegenes; Bc+: B. cereus.

The optimized PCR conditions for the quadruplex assay were 95℃ for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 ℃ for 30 s, 61 ℃ for 2 min, and denaturation at 98 ℃ for 10 min with a ramp rate of 2 °C/s at each step. When the reactions finished, they were stored at 4 ℃. Each reaction contained the following components:10 µL of ddPCR Supermix, 0.72 µL of the individual primers (25 µmol/L), 0.2 µL of the probe (25 µmol/L), 2 µL template DNA and deionized water to a final volume of 20 µL.

For the quadruplex ddPCR method, reactions with the most concentrated gDNA samples failed (Tables 2, 3 and 4, and 5), indicating the DNA concentrations were too high to get well-separated clusters for quantitation. On the contrary, when the gDNA concentration was too low as described in Tables 2, 3 and 4, and 5, the RSDs would be greater than 25% which is not acceptable as the rule29.Therefore, those two utmost concentrations were excluded when the reproducibility and the linear range assays were further carried out, which resulted in that all the RSDs were below the 25% threshold, demonstrating the robust repeatability of the quadruplex ddPCR method.

The detectable limits of the established quadruplex ddPCR method for the individual target pathogens were obtained by calculating the gene copy numbers in the 20 µL reaction which were 8 copies for S. Typhi, 7 copies for S. aureus, 9 copies for L. monocytogenes, and 7 copies for B. cereus, in line with the results by using the singleplex ddPCR method20.

The results of the single and quadruplex ddPCRs (the average values of 5 measurements) from Tables 2, 3 and 4, and 5 were compared by using the unpaired t-test. Discussion and conclusion

Foodborne diseases have become one of the most concerning threats to public health3. The most prominent foodborne pathogens include Salmonella spp., S.aureus, L.monocytogenes, B.cereus and etc3. The traditional microbial detection methods mainly rely on the techniques such as selective medium separation and biochemical identification, which are time- and labor-consuming. According to the Chinese national food standards, the inspection times (including strain identification) of S. Typhi, L. monocytogenes, S. aureus, and B. cereus are at least 6 days, 6 days, 3 days, and 9 days respectively30–33. These methods are also complicated prone to risks of false negative results especially when the quantities of the target bacteria are low.

Rapid and accurate detection of these foodborne pathogenic microorganisms is the prerequisite key to effective prevention and control of foodborne diseases. With the widespread applications of new technologies in the areas of metabolomics, immunology, molecular biology, biosensors, laser technology, nanotechnology, and computer technology, a series of rapid microbial detection methods have been developed. Ernest, et al.34. used visible near-infrared (Vis NIR) hyperspectral imaging spectroscopy and partial least squares regression (PLSR) algorithm to monitor the contamination of E. coli O157 and S. aureus in pork. Yang, et al.35. quantitatively detected foodborne L. monocytogenes using surface enhanced Raman spectroscopy, with a detection limit of 7.8 CFU/mL. Zhang et al.36. used optimized PMA-m PCR technology to detect viable B. cereus cells in noodles, with a detection limit as low as 22 CFU/g. Yun, et al.37. developed a rotary reaction sliding chip (Rn R-Slip Chip) in combination with the LAMP technology, that can simultaneously detect B. cereus and four other common gastrointestinal foodborne pathogens within 1 h, with a detection limit of 7.2 copies/µL. Abu-Halaweh, et al.38 established a quadruplex qPCR for the detection and discrimination of four Campylobacter species from others. Shi, et al.39. developed a quadruplex RT-qPCR to detect 4 Feline viruses, with a lower detection limit of 110 copies/reaction. It is obvious that speed, accuracy, and high-throughput are the current directions of developing innovative inspection technologies, among which the quadruple ddPCR established in this study is a showcase. Villamil et al.22. constructed a ddPCR quantitative method based on five specific genes (ttr, invA, hilA, spaQ, siiA) of Salmonella spp., with a detection sensitivity of 0.5 copies/µL. While the quadruplex ddPCR method established in this study demonstrated lower detection limits for four bacterial species, 8 copies/20µL (S. Typhi), 7 copies/20µL (S. aureus), 9 copies/20µL (L. monocytogenes) and 7 copies/20µL (B. cereus) respectively. The detection sensitivity of this quadruplex ddPCR method is equivalent or superior to other LAMP, qPCR, or multiplex ddPCR methods developed. As a 3rd generation PCR technique, ddPCR is sensitive and thus could greatly reduce the risk of false negative results. The ddPCR process generally takes no more than 5 h (including 2 h for the preparation of gDNA extracts and 3 h for the droplet generation, PCR, and signal detection). If this technology is applied in the food safety area for the simultaneous and accurate quantification of foodborne pathogens, it would be a cost- and time-effective approach.

The established quadruplex ddPCR method demonstrated robust reproducibility with RSDs ≤ 25% (n = 5), broad linear ranges with a nearly 1000-fold variation in gDNA concentration. The readouts from our quadruplex ddPCR were equivalent to these from the singleplex ddPCR evaluated by using the Student’s t-test. The detection limits of this quadruplex ddPCR method were also equivalent to those reported by using the singleplex ddPCR method20. The results from our work also show that the simultaneous amplification of amplicons of multiple bacteria in one reaction didn’t compromise the linear range and sensitivity of the method, indicating the broad feasibilities of the quadruplex ddPCR technology in the area of food microbial detection.

One of the major disadvantages of ddPCR assay is its high cost on reagents as compared to the plate counting assay, but this can be mitigated by establishing multiplex ddPCR methods20. The quadruplex ddPCR method we developed in this study is a showcase in regard to this consideration and the development of methodologies. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on the development of a quadruplex ddPCR for the quantification of four pathogens in one reaction. Previously, Du, et al.40. developed three triplex amplification systems for the detection of 5 target pathogens.

The concentrations of bacteria added to the food samples in our study seem higher than those of naturally contaminated bacteria. The reasons for that are due to the following facts. Firstly, the microbiological contamination of instant food such as catering samples is usually at high level, much higher than that of production samples. Yang, et al.41. found the aerobic plate count (APC) in catering samples was relatively high, with 77.5% greater than 105 CFU/g (or mL) and 17.5% greater than 107 CFU/g (or mL). We added about 108 CFU bacteria into 1 ml sample homogeneous liquid for the purpose of validating the quadruplex ddPCR method. Secondly, a high level of microbial contamination requires proper dilution before testing. We thus added relatively high numbers of the target pathogens into the sample solution to mimic the situation. One of the practical advantages of the ddPCR is its broad dynamic range, nearly 1000-fold difference, which increases the chance to capture the right dilution for the quantitation of the bacteria at variable levels. For example, in a scenario with contaminating bacteria at low levels, the bacterial cells could be enriched quickly using techniques such as immunomagnetic beads for the gDNA extraction and then the quadruplex ddPCR.

For the quantification of the bacteria in the artificially contaminated food samples, we noted that the gDNA copy numbers of B. cereus from the quadruplex ddPCR were always higher than the empirical CFUs quantified by using the plate counting method. We also observed that a certain number of B. cereus cells in its cultures (36 °C, 16–18 h) were in the endospore form. The formation of endospores may reduce the development of colonies on agar plate as describe by Frenzel, et al.42. though the mechanism has not be elucidated yet, resulting in the underestimation of CFU of B. cereus when the plate counting method was applied. This also implied that the ddPCR method would be more appropriate than the plate counting method for the quantification of B. cereus in samples.

In the NCBI database, it is described that the essC gene is present both in B. cereus and L. monocytogenes. However, our work showed that the primers/probes used in this study are specific to the essC gene of B. cereus but not to that of L. monocytogenes (as shown in Fig. 1D). In addition, B. cereus, B. thuringiensis (ATCC 10792) and B. mycoides (ATCC 10206) are difficult to be differentiated by the traditional biochemical assays due to their phylogenic similarities. While the qPCR technology with the essC primers/probes used in this study were able to differentiate them from each other by different Ct values (data not shown).

False negatives from inhibitory effects are significant given the complexity of food samples. For ddPCR, we suggest using 2–3 dilutions (4-fold gradients) of DNA samples to keep copy numbers within the assay’s linear range and check for inhibitors by assessing the linear relationship between readouts and dilution. However, this approach may not detect weak inhibitors but could mitigate the inhibition of the inhibitors by their concentrations. It would be good to have an internal amplification control in our quadruplex ddPCR assay and we will be working on this to improve our assay for its adoption with more confidence.

The established quadruplex ddPCR method in our work can accurately quantify four major foodborne pathogens (S. Typhi, S. aureus, L. monocytogenes, and B. cereus) simultaneously in one reaction, which is superior to the plate counting method with shorter turnaround time, lower LODs, and more robust reproducibility. This quadruplex ddPCR method has demonstrated it is a high-throughput and rapid detection technique in the surveillance of food safety.
