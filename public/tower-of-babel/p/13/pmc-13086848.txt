Primary dysmenorrhea and associated factors among female adolescents in Jinka town, Southern Ethiopia, 2024
Authors: Habtamu Wondmagegn, Kokobe Nigusu, Habtamu Esubalew Bezie, Biniyam Demisse Andarge, Sayih Mehari Degualem, Tsegazeab Ayele Meshesha, Edlework Wondmagegn Atlaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary dysmenorrhea is a major public health problem and the commonest cause of morbidity in reproductive age women. It affects the quality of life of adolescent girls causing decreased academic performance and productivity. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of Primary dysmenorrhea and associated factors among female adolescents in Jinka Town. A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from July 30–August 20, 2024. Census was conducted to identify all eligible participants and simple random sampling technique was used to select 562 female adolescents. Data were collected via structured questionnaires and analyzed using binary and multivariable logistic regression. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05. The prevalence of primary dysmenorrhea was 71.5% with 95% CI (68.0–75.0%), Family history of dysmenorrhea AOR = 1.81 (95% CI (1.15–2.83), having premenstrual syndrome (AOR = 3.13 (95% CI (1.97–4.98)), sleeping ≤ 6 h per day (AOR = 2.73 (95% CI (1.23–6.03)), heavy menstrual periods AOR = 3.35 (95% CI (1.5–7.38)), skipping breakfast (AOR = 3.26 (95% CI (1.31–8.11)) and history of female genital mutilation AOR = 2.97 (95% CI (1.47–6.03)) had statistically significant association with the primary dysmenorrhea. This study found a high prevalence of primary dysmenorrhea. The factors associated with primary dysmenorrhea were family history of dysmenorrhea, the presence of premenstrual syndrome, skipping breakfast, sleeping ≤ 6 h per day, heavy menstrual flow, and history of female genital mutilation. Thus, efforts to prevent primary dysmenorrhea should focus on health education for adolescents and parents on modifiable lifestyle factors such as eating habitual meals regularly, getting adequate rest, and avoiding female genital mutilation.

Introduction

Dysmenorrhea is the commonest menstrual disorder and it’s a severe, painful, lower abdominal cramping pain occurring just before or during menstruation1,2. It can be categorized into two broad categories: primary and secondary dysmenorrhea. Primary dysmenorrhea (PD) is defined as menstrual pain in the absence of any pelvic pathology which starts 6–12 months after menarche with normal ovulatory cycles3. Secondary dysmenorrhea refers to painful menstruation associated with other known pelvic abnormalities4. Globally, the prevalence of primary dysmenorrhea among reproductive-age women ranges from 45 to 95%, with up to 29% experiencing severe pain5,6. As a leading cause of morbidity, depression, and anxiety, it significantly impairs the quality of life for adolescent girls, frequently interrupting daily activities and causing a paramount loss of productivity7,8.

In Africa, studies have reported high prevalence rates, such as 76.1% in Egypt and 85% in Ghana1,9. Within Ethiopia, the burden remains high but varies across regions; studies among university and preparatory students in Gondar, Debre Birhan, and Bahir Dar have identified prevalence rates ranging from 62.3% to 85.4%10–12. The most widely accepted explanation behind PD is excess production of prostaglandins causing increased uterine contractions, peripheral nerve hypersensitivity, and ischemia of the myometrium3,13. There are factors that have been studied in some countries which are risk factors for PD including early age of menarche, irregularity of menstrual period, consumption of coffee, Coca-Cola, chocolate and alcohol, a previous history of depression or disruption of social network, null parity, family history of dysmenorrhea, and obesity10,14,15.

Since the prevalence of primary dysmenorrhea varies greatly across the world, even within the same country due to the use of different diagnostic criteria and the subjectivity nature of symptoms, small area studies are required16. Although adolescents are the most frequently affected populations, there are scarcities of studies among adolescents in Ethiopia which are limited compared to its public health importance. Moreover, lifestyle factors such as, sleeping patterns, skipping meals, and history of female genital mutilation have not been well investigated among female adolescents in Ethiopia. A complete understanding of PD among adolescent girls is very important in developing appropriate management and preventive strategies. Therefore, this study determined the prevalence and associated factors of PD among female adolescents in Jinka town where no previous study was conducted.

Methods

Study setting, design and period

The study was conducted in Jinka town which is the capital city of South Omo Zone, Southern Ethiopia. According to the town administration there are 6 kebeles and total population of 31,849 of which 16,294 were women and 6500 estimated households in 2019. According to EDHS 2016 using conversion factor 0.255 for female adolescents in a household there are an estimated total number of 1,658 female adolescents in Jinka town.

A community based cross-sectional study was conducted to determine the prevalence of primary dysmenorrhea and associated factors among female adolescents in Jinka town, Southern Ethiopia, from July 30–August 20, 2024.

Population

All female adolescents aged (10–19 years) who experienced menses for the last 3 consecutive months living in Jinka town were the source population whereas female adolescents who experienced menses for the last 3 consecutive months and living in the selected kebeles of Jinka town and fulfilled the inclusion criteria during the data collection period were the study population.

Eligibility criteria

Female adolescents who experienced menstruation at least for 3 consecutive months before the data collection period were included while those who had chronic pelvic and back pain unrelated to menses, and those with history of use of intrauterine devices for the last 3 months were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

Sample size was determined using single population proportion formula by taking the prevalence of primary dysmenorrhea 69.26% from a study among adolescent school girls in East Hararghe Zone, Eastern Ethiopia, with a 4% margin of error and a 95% confidence interval17. By adding 10% of non-response rate the final sample size was 562.

According to the town administration there are six kebeles and an estimated 6,500 households in Jinka town. Out of a total of 6 kebeles 2 were randomly selected. First census was conducted to get the list of all female adolescents with their respective households. Second new codes were assigned for each adolescent, and a sampling frame was constructed. By using these codes a random table was constructed by SPSS and simple random sampling technique was used to draw study participants. Proportionate allocation method was used to assign the number of adolescents to the selected kebeles based on the number of female adolescents in each kebele. In case of more than one female adolescent in a household, lottery method was used to select one.

Operational definition and terms

Primary dysmenorrhea: the presence of primary dysmenorrhea was defined as having one or more of abdominal pain, groin/pelvic pain, back pain or thigh pain 1-2 days before and/or during menstrual periods for the last 3 consecutive months4,18.

Age Category: Adolescents were categorized into early adolescence (10–14 years) and late adolescence (15–19 years) based on the World Health Organization (WHO) classification to reflect distinct developmental and physiological stages.

Premenstrual syndrome: having at least one of the following affective symptoms (depression, irritability, disappointment, mood change) and somatic symptoms ( headache, lower abdominal pain ,back pain, vomiting, nausea , chill , fatigue, poor appetite, breast tenderness, acne) 1 week or less before menses in each of the three previous menstrual cycles18.

Physical exercise: physical exercise was measured as not at all, irregularly (if < 3times a week) or regularly(> 3-4times a week)19.

Sleeping patterns: Short sleeping hours- sleeping hours (≤ 6 h/day), recommended 7–10 h/day, excessive- if > 10 h/day20.

Data collection tools and procedure

Data was collected by a structured face to face interview. Data was collected by 3 well-trained health extension workers. Training was given for 2 days to data collectors and supervisor by the principal investigator prior to the study. The questionnaire items were adapted from previous studies and modified to fit the research objective1,9,10,12,14. It has three major parts which focus on socio demographic characteristics, lifestyle and behavioral factors, and reproductive and menstrual pattern related factors.

Data quality control

To assure the quality of data, properly designed data collection instruments was provided after appropriate training for data collectors and supervisors. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and then translated into Amharic version (local language) by different fluent speakers of both languages and then to English to check its consistency. The questionnaire was pretested before the actual data collection on 5% of female adolescents in Arbaminch town. Based on the pretest findings, minor modifications were made to the phrasing of questions regarding lifestyle factors. After pretest, questions were clarified and amended to make them easier to answer. The internal consistency of the multi-item scales such as the symptoms checklist and behavioral factors was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which yielded a value of 0.82, indicating good reliability of the instrument. During data collection, the supervisor received questionnaires from data collectors and review for completeness, accuracy, and consistency.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data was coded, and entered into Epi-Data version4.4. 3.1 And was exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistical methods such as frequencies, percentages, proportion, mean and standard deviation were used to summarize the various characteristics of the study participants.

Cross tabulation and bivariate logistic regression were used to explore the relation between PD and the different independent variables using crude odds ratio with 95% C.I. Finally, to determine the independent factors associated with PD, multivariable logistic regression model was done, and presented using Adjusted ORs with 95% Confidence Interval.

The Chi-square test was used to assess the association between each categorical independent variable and primary dysmenorrhea. Variables that violated the Chi-square assumptions specifically those where more than 20% of the cells had an expected frequency of less than five were excluded from further multivariable analysis. This included variables such as alcohol consumption, khat chewing, history of pregnancy, and history of childbirth, which exhibited zero or extremely low cell counts, potentially undermining the stability of the logistic regression model.

Variables with p-value < 0.25 during bivariate analysis were taken into the multivariable logistic regression model. Model fitness was checked using log likelihood and Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p-value 0.234). To check for potential multi-collinearity among independent variables, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance tests were performed. The majority of the participants 540(96.9%) were students of which 376(55.3%) were in grade nine. The majority of the participants 538 (96.6%) were single. On multivariable analysis, family history of dysmenorrhea, having premenstrual syndrome, sleeping less than 6 h, heavy menstrual periods, skipping breakfast and history of FGM were statistically significant factors with the primary dysmenorrhea (Table 6).

Adolescents with family history of dysmenorrhea were 1.81 times more likely to have primary dysmenorrhea in comparison with their counterparts (AOR = 1.81, 95%CI; 1.15–2.83). Participants’ who had premenstrual syndrome were 3.13 times more likely to have PD compared to their counterparts (AOR = 3.13, 95%CI; 1.97–4.98). Adolescents who had heavy menstrual flow were 3.35 times more likely to have PD compared to those who had light menstrual flow (AOR = 3.35, 95%CI; 1.5–7.38). Adolescents who skipped breakfast sometimes and everyday were 2.03 times and 3.26 times more likely to have PD than adolescents who always had breakfast (AOR 95%CI = 2.03(1.27, 3.25), 3.26 95%CI; 1.31, 8.11) respectively. Adolescents who slept ≤ 6 h a day were 2.73 times more likely to have PD than those who slept more than 10 h per day (AOR 95% CI = 2.73(1.23, 6.05). Adolescents who had history of FGM were 2.97 times more likely to have PD in comparison with their counterparts (AOR = 2.97, 95% CI; 1.47–6.03) (Table 6).

Discussion

In this study the prevalence of PD among female adolescents was (71.5%) with 95% CI (68%, 75%). The proportions of mild, moderate and severe dysmenorrhea were 45%, 33%, and 22%, respectively. The factors associated with PD were family history of dysmenorrhea, the presence of premenstrual syndrome, heavy menstrual flow, history FGM, sleeping less than 6 h per day, and skipping breakfast.

The prevalence in this study was similar to the results obtained in the previous researches conducted in Asia 72.8% in Japan, 71.5% in Nepal, and also in Arab countries in Saudi Arabia 74.4%21–23. The finding is also consistent with studies among high school students in Debremarkos and Harerge zone in Ethiopia which were 69.3% and 69.26% respectively14,17.The prevalence of PD in this study was higher than the studies conducted in Bangladesh (59.8%) and Egypt (34%)24. This could be due to the difference in age, life style and socio-demographic characteristics of study participants. However, it was lower than studies conducted in Bahir Dar (85.1%)25, Kuwait (85.6%)15 and Iran (93.7%)26.This might be because pain is a highly subjective symptom and difficult to measure, there is no internationally agreed definitions for dysmenorrhea. As a result, different researchers used different data collection methods. Some of these researches assessed the prevalence of a one-year interval15 and others used a six months25 while this study assessed a three months interval prevalence as used by WHO to minimize recall bias. Some studies didn’t clearly state the definition27.

This study showed that adolescents who had family history of dysmenorrhea were 1.81 times more likely to have primary dysmenorrhea compared to their counterparts. This is consistent with studies conducted in Debremarkos14, Bahir Dar25, and Greece8. The possible explanation for the consistency might be due to the genetic similarity or learned experience of pain and other behavioral factors of daughters from mothers. As a result daughters react about menstruation and complain the same way as their mothers4,17.

In this study, adolescents who had premenstrual syndrome were 3.13 times more likely to have primary dysmenorrhea than their counterparts. This finding is similar with studies conducted in India and USA28,29. The exact cause of PMS is not clearly understood however currently it’s believed that the symptoms of PMS are related to the depletion of serotonin, which is a neurotransmitter in the brain that regulates mood and other affective symptoms. Serotonin is affected by estrogen and progesterone. Low level of progesterone during premenstrual phase results in insufficient serotonin in brain which triggers PMS. Therefore this suggests that low level of progesterone results in depleted levels of serotonin and high prostaglandin production causing PMS and primary dysmenorrhea respectively30,31.

Heavy menstrual flow was significantly associated with primary dysmenorrhea. Adolescents who changed 5 or more sanitary pads per day were 3.35 times more likely to have PD than adolescents who used 1 or less pads per day. This is supported by studies conducted in Debremarkos town and Harerge zone among high school students in Ethiopia14,17. The similarity could be due to the fact that PD is mainly due to the increased production of prostaglandins, which are inflammatory factors, produced in endometrial cells as progesterone level decreases during menstruation. These chemicals cause increased uterine contractions that contribute both for the pain and for the blood flow during menstruation. Therefore this suggests that high prostaglandin production results in both severe pain and high blood flow during menstruation3,15. Regarding the reported use of contraceptives (10.4%) in this study, Oral Contraceptive Pills (OCPs) are frequently utilized by adolescents as a primary therapeutic intervention for menstrual regulation and the management of primary dysmenorrhea symptoms, rather than solely for pregnancy prevention29. Given the high prevalence of dysmenorrhea in our sample, it is highly probable that these methods are being used as a form of symptom relief.

Adolescents who had history of FGM were 2.97 times more likely to have PD compared to their counterparts. This is consistent with a study conducted among Bahir Dar University students in Ethiopia25. The significant association between Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) and primary dysmenorrhea can be explained through two distinct mechanisms. Anatomically, FGM particularly Type II and III can lead to pelvic scarring and narrowing of the vaginal orifice. This physical obstruction interferes with the natural flow of menstrual blood, leading to its accumulation and subsequent increased intrauterine pressure and painful uterine contractions results in protracted and painful periods32,33. Psychologically, the trauma associated with the procedure can lead to long-term anxiety and post-traumatic stress, which are known to lower the pain threshold and exacerbate the subjective experience of menstrual cramping34,35. These findings highlight that the impact of FGM extends far beyond the immediate procedure, affecting reproductive health throughout adolescence.

Adolescents who slept 6 h or less per day were 2.73 times more likely to have PD in comparison with adolescents who slept more than 10 h per day. This study is consistent with prior studies in Japan and Iran21,26. A systematic review on PD from different countries among adolescents identified short sleeping hours (< 6 h/day) were associated with moderate-severe dysmenorrhea6. Another study in Indonesia among adolescents identified that PD can cause sleeping disorders36.The possible explanation for this might be due to the fact that menstrual cycle is controlled by several hormones such as progesterone, estrogen, prolactin, and growth hormones. These hormones also control sleep cycle. As a result, alterations of these hormones affect both dysmenorrhea and sleep. Since this study is cross-sectional study design it failed to identify the antecedent factor21,26.

Adolescents who skipped eating breakfast every day and sometimes were 3.26 and 2.03 times more likely to have PD than their counterparts respectively. This is supported by studies conducted in the Japan21, Georgia37, and Iran26. The reason might be related to the effect of nutritional deficiencies on the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis. The nutritional status influences sexual steroids and inadequate intake of nutrients affects hormonal levels due to shortage of energy. However, it is also possible that a lack of appetite often a systemic symptom of the premenstrual phase or the onset of pain leads to breakfast skipping as a consequence of the disorder rather than a primary cause.

Primary dysmenorrhea is strongly associated with conditions such as FGM, high monthly flow, and skipping breakfast, which have important public health implications. In terms of policy, the relationship to FGM emphasizes the importance of ongoing, community-led advocacy and integrated health services that offer survivors with comprehensive care, including pain management and psychological support. Furthermore, the impact of skipping breakfast and irregular sleep shows that low-cost, school-based nutritional and hygiene teaching programmes could be effective primary preventative interventions. Addressing these modifiable lifestyle factors has the potential to reduce the prevalence of menstruation pain while also improving teenage girls’ general academic performance and quality of life, hence lowering the broader socioeconomic cost of school absenteeism.

Limitations of the study

Although this study provides valuable insights into the prevalence and associated factors of dysmenorrhea among adolescents in Jinka Town, it is not without limitations. First, as a community-based cross-sectional study, it can only identify statistical associations and temporal relations could not be established between the identified factors and the onset of menstrual pain.

Second, the diagnosis of “primary” dysmenorrhea was presumptive, as it relied on participant self-reporting and the cyclical nature of symptoms rather than clinical examinations or pelvic imaging. Furthermore, while we excluded those with known chronic pelvic or back pain unrelated to menses, we acknowledge that adolescent girls may not always be able to differentiate between menstrual-related pain and other chronic causes without a professional medical evaluation. Third, some aspects of the data collection relied on subjective assessments, such as the categorization of sugar intake and the reporting of menstrual flow intensity, which may introduce information bias. Additionally, certain topics explored such as sexual activity and substance use are considered sensitive in the local cultural context, which may have led to social desirability bias and underreporting despite the use of anonymous questionnaires.

Conclusion

This study found that more than two third of female adolescents had primary dysmenorrhea. The factors associated to primary dysmenorrhea were: family history of dysmenorrhea, the presence of premenstrual syndrome, skipping breakfast, sleeping 6 h or less per day, heavy menstrual flow, and history of female genital mutilation.

Recommendations

Based on the findings the following recommendations are forwarded.

Health care planners: Health care programmers should focus on designing specific strategies and practical interventions that helps for the prevention of the negative consequences of primary dysmenorrhea. Planning health education programs for female adolescents and parents about some modifiable lifestyle and behavioral factors such as avoiding female genital mutilation, eating habitual meals every day, and getting adequate sleeping hours is very important to reduce primary dysmenorrhea. There is a critical need for holistic care for FGM survivors. Clinical management should move beyond physical symptom relief to include psychological support and specialized gynecological counseling to address the complex pain pathways associated with FGM-related scarring.

To schools and teachers

Teachers should educate female adolescents about life style and behavioral factors including avoiding female genital mutilation, eating habitual meals every day, and getting adequate sleeping hours for the prevention of primary dysmenorrhea. In addition schools should also design strategies to academically support female students who have PD.

To researchers

Further longitudinal studies are necessary to investigate the temporal relationship of variables.
