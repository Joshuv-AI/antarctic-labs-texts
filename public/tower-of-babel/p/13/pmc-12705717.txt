Timing and survival benefits of cytoreduction in patients with recurrent leiomyosarcoma
Authors: Mehmet Tunç, Hüseyin Akıllı, Esra Kuşçu, Ali Ayhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeLeiomyosarcoma is characterized by its aggressive behavior, poor prognosis, resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs, and high recurrence rate. This study aimed to identify prognostic factors affecting the effectiveness of cytoreductive surgery (CRS) on overall survival (OS) in patients with recurrent leiomyosarcoma (LMS).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted at Başkent University Ankara Hospital from 2007 to 2016, involving 59 patients with uterine LMS who underwent surgery. We assessed demographic and clinical variables, disease recurrence intervals, and treatment outcomes.ResultsThe median age of the patients was 50.0 (23–78) years, and the median follow-up time was 25.0 (2–87) months. Median disease-free survival (DFS) and OS were 15.0 and 37.0 months, respectively. Disease recurrence occurred in 44 patients (74.6%). After recurrence, 34 (77.3%) patients underwent CRS and chemotherapy (CT), while 10 (22.7%) patients received only CT. The median OS was 19.0 months for patients who underwent CRS and received CT and 15.0 months for those who received only CT (p = 0.132). Notably, OS was significantly longer for patients whose recurrence occurred after 6 months compared to those with early recurrence (19 versus 8 months, p = 0.049).ConclusionCRS provided a modest survival benefit for patients with recurrent LMS, although statistical significance was not achieved. The results of this study indicate that the timing of recurrence plays a crucial role in survival.

What does this study add to the clinical work

1. Timing of Recurrence Matters: Patients with recurrent leiomyosarcoma who experience recurrence after 6 months have significantly longer overall survival compared to those with early recurrence. 2. While cytoreductive surgery in combination with chemotherapy showed a trend toward improved survival in patients with recurrent LMS, statistical significance was not achieved, indicating that the benefits may be modest.

Introduction

Uterine sarcomas constitute 3–9% of uterine malignancies, with leiomyosarcoma (LMS) being the most common histologic subtype [1]. According to the American Cancer Society, there are approximately 65,000–70,000 new uterine corpus tumors annually, and 2–5% of these malignancies are uterine sarcomas [2]. Despite their rarity, LMSs are noted for aggressive behavior, poor prognosis, resistance to chemotherapeutic agents, and a high rate of recurrence [3].

Diagnosing uterine sarcoma is challenging due to the lack of specific clinical presentations, and it is often done postoperatively with myomectomy or hysterectomy specimens [4]. Surgery remains the primary treatment option and total abdominal hysterectomy (TAH) is the cornerstone of treatment [5]. However, the effectiveness of adjuvant therapy is limited for LMS patients; neither chemotherapy (CT) nor radiation therapy provides significant benefits, and most patients experience tumor recurrence [4].

Patients with LMS recurrence are candidates for a wide range of agents, most of which are still investigational [6]. Recent pharmacological advances, such as monoclonal antibodies, multi-kinase inhibitors, and superoxide producers, have not shown the desired impact. Thus, surgery remains the gold standard for this patient group [7].

The influence of factors such as the stage at initial diagnosis, initial surgical interventions, disease-free interval (DFI), and CT administration on tumor recurrence remains controversial, as do the benefits of surgically removing recurrent masses, primarily due to the rarity of the disease [8]. Generally, chemotherapy has a limited impact on survival, and optimal cytoreductive surgery in patients with recurrent disease may prolong survival [9]. However, there is limited data in the literature regarding the effectiveness of cytoreductive surgery in this setting [10]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the impact of cytoreductive surgery (CRS) on survival outcomes in cases of recurrent LMS. The primary aim was to evaluate whether secondary cytoreduction influences overall survival (OS) in patients with recurrence, while secondary outcomes included an evaluation of the optimal timing for surgery in cases of early recurrence and identification of possible prognostic factors.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Başkent University Ankara Hospital’s Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Division of Gynecologic Oncology, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the relevant institutional review board. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients and their confidentiality was ensured.

Between February 2007 and February 2016, 68 patients diagnosed with LMS were identified in the hospital records. Data on disease recurrence and intervals between diagnosis and recurrences were key focuses. Patients with primary tumors outside the uterus (n = 6) and those lacking surgical or follow-up notes (n = 3) were excluded, leaving 59 eligible participants who underwent surgery for LMS.

The study variables included demographic and clinical variables such as age, lifestyle factors, cancer history, exposure to tamoxifen and pelvic radiation, subtype of LMS, fertility, comorbidities, previous surgeries, diagnostic and surgical details, primary tumor location, and follow-up data. Disease staging followed the FIGO-2009 staging for uterine sarcomas, and treatment impacts on DFS and OS were analyzed [11]. While stages I and II were classified as early-stage disease, stages III and IV were defined as advanced-stage disease.

The follow-up protocol consisted of gynecological examinations every 3 months in the first 3 years, every 6 months for the next 2 years, and annually thereafter. It included ultrasound and computed tomography scans of the pelvis, abdomen, and chest, as well as complete blood count and major biochemical tests. Patient selection for treatment was evaluated by a multidisciplinary gynecological oncology board, considering individual clinical factors (e.g. performance status, patients’ preference, tumor characteristics) and current treatment guidelines to ensure personalized care. The chemotherapy regimens administered primarily included 3 to 6 courses of adriamycin plus ifosfamide with mesna (AIM), gemcitabine plus docetaxel (Gem/Doce), and etoposide plus cisplatin (EP).

Recurrences occurring within 6 months were classified as early recurrences, whereas those appearing after 6 months were considered as late recurrences, based on existing literature [1]. Recurrences detected in the pelvis were categorized as local recurrences, while those in other regions—such as the upper abdomen, lymph nodes, lungs, etc.—were considered distant recurrences.

Statistical assessments included descriptive statistics, univariate analysis via log-rank test, and multivariate analysis using Cox regression. The Kaplan–Meier method was used for survival rate calculations, with model fits evaluated through residual analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 and computations were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 68 patients were identified and after excluding 9 patients, 59 patients were included in the analysis, with a median age of 50.0 years (range: 23–78) and a median follow-up duration of 25.0 months (range: 2–87). The median DFS and OS times of the patients were 15.0 months (range: 10.65–19.36) and 37.0 months (range: 22.16–51.84), respectively. After the initial surgeries, 44 recurrences were detected (74.6%). Among these patients, 34 (57.6%) underwent cytoreductive surgery and CT, while 10 (16.9%) received only CT. The median OS following recurrence for patients who underwent cytoreductive surgery and received CT and for those who received only CT was 19.0 months (range: 7.48–30.52) and 15.0 months (range: 8.06–21.95), respectively (p = 0.132). Complete cytoreduction was achieved in all patients who underwent cytoreductive surgery. Postoperative complications occurred in 2 patients (5.9%), with one case of bowel perforation and one case of bacterial sepsis.

The median OS following disease recurrence was 8.0 months (range: 3.35–12.65) for patients with recurrence within 6 months, and 19.0 months (range: 12.70–25.30) for those with recurrence after more than 6 months (p = 0.049) (Fig. 1). The median OS after secondary CRS was 8.0 months (range: 4.4–11.6) in patients with recurrence within 6 months (n = 6) and 21.0 months (range: 11.7–30.3) in patients with recurrence after > 6 months (n = 28) (p = 0.091). In the subgroup of patients with recurrence within 6 months, the median OS was 8.0 months for the CRS + Chemotherapy group (n:6) and 6.0 months for the chemotherapy-only group (n:4) (p = 0.873). For those with recurrence after more than 6 months, the median OS was 21.0 months for CRS + Chemotherapy (n:28) and 15.0 months for chemotherapy (n:6) alone (p = 0.193). Discussion

This study investigated prognostic factors and the survival benefit of CRS in patients with recurrent LMS. Although CRS was associated with a 4-month increase in OS in patients with recurrent LMS, this finding was not statistically significant (p = 0.132). Patients whose recurrence occurred more than 6 months after cytoreductive surgery had an OS extension of 13 months compared to those with earlier recurrences, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.091). This study has identified recurrence within 6 months and advanced-stage disease at diagnosis as significant prognostic factors for OS in patients with recurrent LMS. Despite the lack of statistically significant findings, the observed trends suggest that CRS may offer a modest survival benefit, particularly in patients with later recurrences.

The median DFI was 15 months in this study, in line with the literature [8, 12]. After disease recurrence, median survival was 4 months longer in patients who underwent secondary CRS compared to the chemotherapy subgroup, although this finding was not statistically significant. Additionally, when patients were divided into early and late recurrence subgroups, our study examined the median OS times for those in the CRS + Chemotherapy and chemotherapy-only subgroups, and no significant survival difference was observed between these groups. Cybulska et al. reported a significant improvement in survival for patients with complete secondary cytoreduction, similar to other previous findings [8, 13]. Recently, Yuan et al. reported significantly better 5-year OS rates in the CRS subgroup compared to the chemotherapy subgroup (72.8% vs 28.5%, respectively; p = 0.001), however, such differences were not observed in our study [10].

In patients for whom recurrence occurred after more than 6 months, OS was nearly 1 year longer after recurrence. Several studies have indicated that a shorter disease-free interval is related to worsened survival outcomes in patients with recurrent LMS [8, 9, 14]. A recent study, which used a 12-month cutoff for the time to first recurrence, reported shorter OS in patients who experienced recurrence within 12 months [10]. The study reported a hazard ratio of 4.60 (95% CI: 1.49–14.41; p = 0.008) for patients with earlier disease recurrence, indicating a significantly increased risk of mortality in this group [10]. The shorter survival observed in patients with early recurrences (0–6 months) could indicate more aggressive tumor biology, resulting in generally worse prognoses and potentially more aggressive disease behavior [15]. This highlights the importance of exploring novel therapeutic approaches for these high-risk patients.

The key limitations of this study include its retrospective design, which may introduce biases related to data collection and patient selection. Patients who did not undergo cytoreductive surgery after disease recurrence may have been primarily due to poor performance status or patient preference, which could introduce a selection bias in the study. Additionally, this study was conducted in a single-center setting, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. The relatively small sample size, due to the rarity of LMS, may also reduce the statistical power to detect significant differences or associations, thereby impacting the robustness of the conclusions drawn from the results—such as those comparing cytoreductive surgery plus chemotherapy versus. Chemotherapy alone. However, this study provides valuable insights given its significant cohort of LMS recurrence cases, and it is one of the largest series from a single center focused on CRS and time to first recurrence in this context.

Future multicentric studies with larger populations and meta-analyses of current studies are needed to validate these findings. Additionally, exploring new therapeutic modalities for patients with early recurrence could improve outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while cytoreductive surgery may extend survival, especially in patients with prolonged recurrence intervals (> 6 months in this study), the small number of patients included in this study makes it difficult to report a direct conclusion, but meta-analyses to be created with this and similar studies will guide the selection of surgical patients in this patient group.
