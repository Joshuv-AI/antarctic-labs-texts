AI adoption in Spain (2023–2025): A web-derived dataset based on LLMs
Authors: Ana Pastor-Merino, Xavier Martínez-Barbero, Josep Domenech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article introduces a nationwide dataset that maps how 112,814 Spanish firms communicate and implement artificial intelligence (AI) on their corporate websites in 2023 and 2025, resulting in 225,628 firm-year observations. Using a systemic pipeline based on large language models (LLMs), website text is segmented, semantically filtered, and evaluated with a structured rubric to identify explicit evidence of AI use in internal processes and in products or services.The dataset offers a detailed portrait of AI adoption across regions (NUTS 3), industries, and firm size categories. For each province–sector–size combination, it reports whether firms adopt AI, whether they apply it internally, whether it is embedded in their offerings, and how many firms have valid website content. This multi-dimensional structure enables users to explore territorial patterns, sectoral differences, and size-related disparities in the uptake of AI.By providing indicators for two benchmark years, the dataset supports the study of how AI adoption evolves across the Spanish business landscape. It offers a reproducible and scalable foundation for research on technological diffusion, regional digitalisation, and industry-level transformation, and can be readily extended to future years or adapted to other countries.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Large language models, Web mining, Corporate websites

Background

Corporate websites have become an increasingly valuable source of firm-level information for innovation and technology studies. Compared with surveys or administrative records, which are often costly, infrequent, and subject to non-response bias, website content is continuously updated and reflects firms’ external communication strategies, digital capabilities, and technological positioning. Prior research shows that website-based indicators can complement or, in some cases, substitute traditional innovation measures by providing more granular and timely insights. Studies such as Ashouri, Hajikhani, Suominen, Pukelis, and Cunningham [1]; Axenbeck and Breithaupt [2]; Blazquez, Domenech, Gil, and Pont [3]; Héroux-Vaillancourt, Beaudry, and Rietsch [4]; and Kinne and Axenbeck [5] demonstrate that textual and structural website features effectively capture innovation activity, digitalisation patterns, and aspects of technological risk. This approach aligns with the broader shift toward big-data-based innovation indicators [6], enabled by large-scale web scraping and text mining.

Language models further enhance the analytical potential of website data. Evidence shows high agreement with human coders in classification and annotation tasks across domains [[7], [8], [9], [10]], and model-based quality assessments correlate with expert judgement [11]. However, methodological caution is required due to risks related to bias, prompt sensitivity, and hallucinations [12,13,10], underscoring the importance of prompt design [14] and transparent evaluation frameworks [15]. Despite growing evidence, no publicly available dataset currently offers nationwide measures of AI adoption from corporate websites distinguishing AI in processes versus products. The dataset introduced here fills this gap by providing reproducible, multi-dimensional indicators of AI use across Spanish provinces, sectors, and firm-size categories.

All numerical results and complete matrices for both years are accessible through the repository files. Users may easily aggregate the data to broader levels (e.g., province–sector or size-only) by recomputing counts and proportions.

The objective of this data article is to share a reproducible and openly accessible dataset that captures AI adoption patterns among Spanish firms using web-derived information. The dataset is designed as a flexible research resource that can be analysed on its own or linked to complementary statistical sources. It enables descriptive, comparative, and longitudinal assessments of AI diffusion across provinces, sectors, and firm size categories, providing a solid empirical foundation for studying digital transformation at scale.

Compared with small-scale LLM-based website studies or survey-based innovation indicators, this dataset offers substantially larger coverage (112,814 firms), balanced longitudinal structure (2023–2025), and fine-grained territorial disaggregation (NUTS-3 × NACE × size). Its fully automated and reproducible pipeline enables scalable monitoring and longitudinal analytical applications that are difficult to implement with manually coded or survey-based data.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the first step of the methodology consists of collecting the input text. The overall workflow can be conceptually divided into three layers: (i) data acquisition, (ii) preprocessing and semantic filtering, and (iii) classification and aggregation. Corporate websites were crawled in 2023 and 2025 using a uniform configuration to ensure temporal comparability between the two collection waves. The crawler retrieved HTML pages and publicly accessible PDF documents while respecting robots.txt, limiting maximum depth, and applying bandwidth and time constraints to minimise server load and ensure ethical data collection.

LLM-based pipeline for detecting AI adoption from corporate website text and generating aggregated indicators.

Corporate websites may undergo structural changes between waves. The same crawling parameters and preprocessing pipeline were applied in both periods, ensuring that the n_companies indicator is constructed using consistent technical inclusion criteria and remains comparable across years.All retrieved content was converted to plain text and processed through a standardised cleaning pipeline. Boilerplate elements—such as navigation menus, cookie banners, repeated headers and footers, legal disclaimers, and code fragments—were removed where possible. The cleaned text was then segmented into fixed-size fragments of 1000 bytes. This byte-based chunking strategy ensures that the units subsequently evaluated by the language model remain within a manageable context window while retaining sentence-level coherence in most cases.

Each fragment was embedded using the text-embedding-3-small model. To focus classification on the most relevant content, cosine similarity was computed between every fragment and a reference embedding representing artificial intelligence. As cosine similarity operates in semantic vector space, it identifies conceptually related descriptions even when firms use non-standard or varied terminology to refer to AI. For each firm, the fragments with the highest similarity scores were retained and, when appropriate, extended with neighbouring text to reconstruct concise and meaningful passages rather than isolated sentences.

The selected fragments were evaluated with GPT-4o-mini across both data collection waves, using a theory-derived rubric designed to (i) identify explicit, concrete evidence of implemented AI use and, when present, (ii) determine whether such evidence relates to internal processes, products or services, or both. This conceptual distinction follows the definitions of the 2018 Oslo Manual [16] and is operationalised in line with Pastor-Merino et al. (2025) [17]. Under this framework, only non-ambiguous descriptions of real AI functionality—such as machine learning models deployed in production, predictive analytics integrated into operations, computer vision systems for quality control, recommendation engines, or chatbots used internally or externally—are considered valid. Generic marketing language, aspirational claims, or mentions of AI without implementation details are coded as non-adoption.

All classifications are generated through a single GPT-4o-mini prompt, fully documented in Appendix A. The internal structure of this promptintegrates a theory-based rubric, formal operational definitions, and domain-specific instructions informed by the literature on innovation and AI adoption. Within this unified prompt, the model receives the set of technologies that qualify as implemented AI, structured positive and negative exemplars, and explicit decision rules that prioritise verifiable functional evidence and impose a conservative interpretation in ambiguous cases. The prompt was iteratively tested across sectors, firm sizes, and types of AI use to verify definitional clarity and minimise interpretive variance. This configuration supports consistent application of the criteria, reproducibility across executions, and comparability across firms, sectors, and years.

Following these definitions, AI in processes captures cases where AI is used to enhance or automate internal operations, typically through predictive maintenance systems, fraud detection models, demand forecasting tools, intelligent document processing, or internal chatbots. AI in products refers to situations in which AI forms part of the functionality offered to clients or end-users, such as AI-enabled software, embedded AI in physical devices, AI-as-a-service platforms, or online systems relying on machine learning, computer vision, or natural language processing. A firm is considered an adopter when at least one fragment provides explicit evidence of implemented AI in processes, products, or both.

At the firm level, companies receive a positive label only when the criteria described above are met in at least one evaluated fragment. These binary indicators—AI adoption overall, AI in processes, and AI in products—are subsequently aggregated by province (NUTS-3), two-digit NACE sector, and firm size category. The classification output is binary (0/1) rather than probabilistic, and no weighted probability scores are used in the construction of the dataset indicators. For each province–sector–size cell, the dataset reports (i) the number of firms with valid website content and (ii) the share of firms adopting AI in any form, in processes, and in products.

Model Evaluation: Reliability and Validity

To ensure that the web-derived indicators released in this dataset are both valid and consistent, the classification pipeline was evaluated using established practices in LLM-based annotation and computational social science, following the metrics reported in Pastor-Merino et al. [18]. Validity was assessed by comparing GPT-4o-mini classifications with a stratified sample of manually coded website fragments covering different sectors, firm sizes, and types of AI use. This procedure evaluates the extent to which the model replicates expert judgement.

The classification pipeline is transparent and reproducible, operating with fixed (closed) model weights across executions. GPT-4o-mini is used as the underlying foundation model within this structured framework. The rubric, operational definitions, fragment-selection criteria, and decision rules are explicitly specified, ensuring procedural transparency. Model outputs are systematically benchmarked against manually coded samples using multiple performance metrics, and robustness is assessed through repeated executions under identical conditions. The dataset does not involve predictive forecasting; rather, it captures contemporaneous evidence of website content.Model validity was quantified using standard performance metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), which is particularly informative under class imbalance. Across validation rounds, the model exhibited high overall performance, with accuracy close to 0.95, precision around 0.79, recall near 0.69 —reflecting conservative decision criteria—, and an F1-score of approximately 0.73. The MCC value, around 0.62, further indicates that the model effectively distinguishes true AI adopters from firms whose websites mention AI only in a superficial or generic manner.

Consistency was evaluated by re-running the full classification pipeline ten times with the same prompt, model, and fragment-selection procedure, and measuring agreement across repetitions using statistics such as Cohen’s Kappa and the intra-class correlation coefficient. The results show high stability for the core AI detection variable (AI1), with ICC = 0.89 and Cohen’s Kappa = 0.88. Both metrics confirm the robustness and reproducibility of the model’s decisions when applied repeatedly to the same input data.

Taken together, these results indicate that the classification pipeline is closely aligned with expert judgement and yields stable outcomes across executions. Because the workflow is fully reproducible, the resulting indicators are comparable across the 2023 and 2025 data collection waves and provide a solid basis for territorial, comparative, and longitudinal analyses of AI adoption in Spain. A detailed account of the full methodological framework and validation procedures is provided in Pastor-Merino et al. [17].

Ethics Statement

The dataset combines publicly accessible corporate website content with non-personal, firm-level information obtained from the SABI database. All SABI variables used (sector, size, and province) refer exclusively to companies as legal entities and contain no personal data. The dataset includes no confidential information and involves no human or animal subjects.
