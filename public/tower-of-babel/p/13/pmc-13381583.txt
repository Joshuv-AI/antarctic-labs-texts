Three in one fluorescent sensing and imaging of chemical warfare agents
Authors: Junhong Liu, Yin Gong, Linwen Tan, Jinfeng Cen, Mo Li, Chuyu Wang, Xuan Zhao, Mingxue Sun, Qitao Tan, Wen-Qi Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemical warfare agents (CWAs) pose severe global threats due to their historical role in mass casualties and ongoing misuse, particularly blister, nerve, and blood agents. To address the urgent need for field-deployable detection tools, we develop TrioAlert, which, to the best of our knowledge, is the first fluorescent sensor capable of simultaneously detecting the blister agent sulfur mustard, the G-type nerve agent sarin, and the blood agent cyanide. The sensor employs an imidazole moiety for the recognition of sulfur mustard and sarin, while cyanide is detected through a reaction at the 4-position of the coumarin fluorophore. TrioAlert demonstrates a rapid response (within 90 s), high sensitivity (limits of detection: 58.5 nM for sulfur mustard, 46.2 nM for sarin, and 53.4 nM for cyanide), and distinct fluorescence and color changes. The detection performance of TrioAlert for the three chemical warfare agents remains stable in natural environmental matrices and across various temperatures. Furthermore, TrioAlert-loaded test strips enable smartphone-based qualitative and quantitative on-site analysis, with visual detection limits of 0.05 ppm for sulfur mustard, 0.01 ppm for sarin, and 5 ppm for cyanide. The sensor also enables the visualization of all three CWAs in live cells and mouse models. Notably, in vivo fluorescence changes precede pathological damage, with distinct fluorescence triggered at sub-pathological doses. This provides an early visual indication for trace CWA exposure. Furthermore, these fluorescence changes correlate with subsequent alterations in pathological biomarkers (P < 0.01), confirming the sensor’s potential for clinical diagnosis. Our study demonstrates the on-site rapid quantitative detection of high-risk chemical warfare agents, thereby advancing the capability to prevent chemical threats and safeguard public health and security.

Introduction

Chemical warfare agents (CWAs) are toxic chemical compounds capable of causing mass casualties on the battlefield1,2. These agents can be categorized into five main types: blister agents, nerve agents, blood agents, choking agents, and incapacitating agents3. Among these CWAs, sulfur mustard (SM) is the typical blister agent, often called the “king of CWAs”4. Sulfur mustard caused at least 100,000 casualties during the Iran-Iraq War, and many victims have continued to suffer from its long-term effects5. Recently, the Organization for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons (OPCW) has confirmed the use of sulfur mustard in the Syrian civil war6. Open-source investigations have also reported the alleged use of chemical weapons, including blister agents, in the Russia-Ukraine conflict. Beyond blister agents, nerve and blood agents also pose significant threats to public safety7,8. Nerve agents and blood agents are the only two categories of lethal CWAs capable of causing mass casualties even at low concentrations1,6. The use of nerve agents in assassinations in Malaysia (2018) and assaults with Novichok agents in London (2019) and Tomsk (2020) underscores their persistent danger to both military personnel and civilians9,10. Similarly, cyanide poses a significant threat through diverse scenarios, including terrorism, accidental ingestion from foods, smoke inhalation, and industrial exposure11. In light of these diverse and persistent threats, developing convenient, reliable, and accurate detection methods for blister, nerve, and blood agents is of critical importance.

Current analytical methods for detecting blister agents, nerve agents, and blood agents include ion mobility spectrometry (Chempro 100/HT, CAM-2, and APD 2000), infrared spectrometry (M21, JSLSCAD, and HazMat ID), Raman spectroscopy (FirstDefender and JCSD), surface acoustic wave (SAW) sensors (SAW MiniCAD mk II and HAZMATCAD), photoionization photometry (AP4C and MiniCAMS), and flame photometry (ppbRAE, MiniRAE Classic, and MiniRAE 3000)12–18. Although instrumental analytical techniques are indispensable for CWA detection, they suffer from several inherent limitations, including false-positive results, high cost, vulnerability to environmental interferences and cross-contamination, and relatively cumbersome sample pretreatment and operational procedures (Table S1)12–14,17. In contrast, colorimetric chemical detection methods, which rely on specific reactions between CWAs and reagents to produce a visible color change, offer compelling advantages for field use, including operational simplicity, low cost, rapid visual readout, and often good selectivity19. Building on this principle, small-molecule fluorescent sensors represent a powerful evolution of chemical detection. They have attracted considerable attention due to their superior sensitivity, spatiotemporal resolution capability, and potential for real-time, in situ monitoring, demonstrating great promise for biological and environmental sensing20–24. We have recently conducted a systematic review of all reported fluorescent sensors for CWAs1. While significant progress has been made in the development of sensors for individual classes of CWAs, only a limited number of systems are capable of detecting more than one class25–33. To the best of our knowledge, no existing fluorescent sensor has been reported that can simultaneously detect three distinct classes of CWAs.

To address this critical gap, we designed and synthesized a series of novel coumarin-based fluorescent sensors. We employed an imidazole group as the recognition unit for sulfur mustard and sarin. For cyanide sensing, the C-4 position of the coumarin scaffold was selected as the recognition site. The C-4 position of 3-substituted coumarins is known to be an electrophilic site susceptible to nucleophilic attack34. In our design, an electron-withdrawing α,β-unsaturated ketone linker was introduced at the 3-position. Through resonance effects, this electron-deficient substituent activates the C-4 position, endowing it with a significant partial positive charge and thereby facilitating nucleophilic attack by cyanide ions. Upon reaction with blister and nerve agents, the fluorescence emission of TrioAlert at 560 nm was quenched within 10 s, accompanied by a color change from yellow to red. In contrast, blood agents caused a shift in the fluorescence emission wavelength from 560 nm to 442 nm, with the solution color fading from yellow to colorless. The sensor exhibited a high sensitivity (limits of detection: 58.5 nM for sulfur mustard, 46.2 nM for sarin, and 53.4 nM for cyanide) and rapid response (within 60 s for sulfur mustard, 10 s for sarin and 90 s for cyanide). Comparative analysis with recently reported fluorescent CWA probes (Table S2) indicates that TrioAlert exhibits sensitivity and response time comparable to or better than existing sensors, with the added capability of simultaneous triple-target detection-a feature not reported for any single sensor to date26,28,29,35–43. By using a smartphone with a red-green-blue (RGB) color application, the test strip loaded with TrioAlert is capable of detecting sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide qualitatively and quantitatively. Importantly, the biomedical applicability of TrioAlert was demonstrated through its use in imaging sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide in live cells and mouse models. These capabilities establish TrioAlert as a practical platform for the rapid, on-site early warning of multi-class CWA exposure, contributing to environmental and chemical security.

Results and discussion

Design and synthesis of the sensors

To develop practical and reliable fluorescent sensors for blister agent sulfur mustard, G-type nerve agent sarin, and blood agent cyanide, the vital step is to construct a stable recognition unit specifically for sulfur mustard. Compared to the numerous recognition units available for nerve and blood agents, thione and thiol groups represent the only two commonly used recognition units for sulfur mustard25,26,44. However, both of these recognition units are prone to oxidation in air, particularly under UV irradiation45. Although we previously developed several thione/thiol-based fluorescent sensors for sulfur mustard detection, subsequent practical application revealed that this susceptibility to oxidation significantly limits their utility26,38,46. Due to the weak electrophilicity and the absence of hydrogen-bond donor or acceptor groups in sulfur mustard, designing a suitable recognition unit for it remains challenging1. Consequently, we sought inspiration for designing a sulfur mustard recognition unit by examining how this agent binds to biomacromolecules in organisms. It is now widely accepted that blister agents can form covalent bonds with proteins and DNA47,48. The N7 position of guanine is the primary binding site for sulfur mustard on DNA47,48. Cysteine residues have long been considered the main binding sites for sulfur mustard on proteins48. However, recent studies indicate that histidine residues also exhibit strong binding affinity towards blister agents49,50. The covalent binding of sulfur mustard to both guanine and histidine residues occurs specifically at their imidazole moieties. Thus, we propose that the imidazole moiety itself could serve as a potential recognition unit for sulfur mustard. We also synthesized two additional sensors, ONP-1 and ONP-2, to investigate the detection capabilities of a similar recognition unit oxazole toward sulfur mustard and sarin. As shown in Fig. 2C, the fluorescent emission of all four sensors was quenched upon contact with sarin. Similar to nerve agents, a variety of strategies for cyanide detection have been reported in the literature. Inspired by these approaches, the C-4 position of the coumarin scaffold was selected as the recognition unit for cyanide in this study. An electron-withdrawing α,β-unsaturated ketone was introduced at the 3-position of coumarin, which not only served as a linker between the coumarin and imidazole moieties but also further activates the C-4 position of the coumarin ring. As a result, the C-4 site became a highly electrophilic center that can be selectively attacked by cyanide ions via a Michael-type nucleophilic addition reaction. Overall, the imidazole moiety was employed as the recognition unit for sulfur mustard and sarin, while the coumarin scaffold functioned both as the recognition unit for cyanide and as the fluorophore of the probe. Meanwhile, the α,β-unsaturated ketone acted as a dual-purpose structural element, serving as both a molecular bridge and an activation group to enhance the reactivity of the coumarin C-4 position. As shown in Fig. 2B, two sensors, TrioAlert and TrioAlert-B were synthesized in a few steps. The synthesis details are provided in the Methods and Materials section.

Spectral responses toward sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide

Having synthesized the sensors, we first evaluated the spectral responses of TrioAlert toward sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide. As shown in Fig. 2D, upon addition of sulfur mustard (100 μM) or sarin (100 μM), significant quenching of the fluorescence emission at 560 nm was observed in acetonitrile-PBS (1:9). Concurrently, the color of the reaction solution changed from yellow to red (Fig. 2G). The fluorescence quenching efficiency of sarin was slightly higher than that of sulfur mustard. The fluorescence quenching of TrioAlert exhibited an linear relationship with the concentrations of sulfur mustard and sarin (Fig. 2E). In contrast to sulfur mustard and sarin, the blood agent cyanide (200 μM) caused a blue shift in the fluorescence emission of TrioAlert from 560 nm to 442 nm (Fig. 2D). These spectral changes were accompanied by a solution color change from yellow to colorless (Fig. 2G). As the cyanide concentration increased, the fluorescence intensity at 442 nm progressively intensified. Cyanide (200 μM) increased the fluorescence intensity at 442 nm from 700 to ~5500 a.u. We also explored the reaction of TrioAlert-B with sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide. TrioAlert-B itself exhibited a stronger fluorescence emission at 576 nm than TrioAlert in acetonitrile-PBS (1:9). However, the addition of high concentrations (200 μM) of sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide all resulted in no significant fluorescence or color change in the TrioAlert-B solution (Fig. 2F, H). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations provided a clear electronic rationale for this stark contrast in reactivity (Fig. S2). In TrioAlert-B, the direct conjugation of the carbonyl with the benzimidazole ring reduced the nucleophilicity of the key nitrogen atom (nucleophilicity index: −0.398), rendering it inert toward both sulfur mustard and sarin. Furthermore, the extended π-system in TrioAlert-B increased the distance between the electron-withdrawing carbonyl and the coumarin core, thereby diminishing its ability to activate the C-4 position (electrophilicity index: 0.191 for TrioAlert vs. a lower value for TrioAlert-B). This insufficient electrophilicity explains its lack of reactivity toward cyanide. Taken together, TrioAlert reacts with three high-threat chemical warfare agents, sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide, and produces distinct color and fluorescence changes.

Sensing mechanism and theoretical calculations of TrioAlert

To elucidate the distinct sensing mechanisms, we performed systematic 1H NMR titration studies complemented by DFT calculations. For the electrophilic agents (sulfur mustard and sarin), definitive assignment of the reaction site relied on the emergence of new diagnostic signals and characteristic deshielding of the benzimidazole scaffold, as the labile N-H proton signal was broadened beyond detection in DMF-d7. Upon addition of sulfur mustard, a notable feature was the emergence of a broad, exchangeable singlet at δ 9.5 ppm (Fig. S3A). This signal was attributed to the acidic proton solvated during the release of HCl, a specific byproduct of the substitution reaction. Concurrently, the aromatic protons of the benzimidazole ring exhibited a pronounced downfield shift. This deshielding effect was a direct consequence of nitrogen alkylation, which generates a cationic center and reduces the electron density on the imidazole ring. Crucially, the characteristic vinylic resonance at δ 8.5 ppm, assigned to the C4-H proton of the coumarin moiety, was retained throughout the titration, confirming that the α,β-unsaturated coumarin framework remained intact during the N-alkylation process. The preservation of this signal ruled out any nucleophilic addition at the coumarin backbone and indicated that sulfur mustard selectively reacted at the imidazole nitrogen without perturbing the π-conjugated donor system.

The reaction with sarin proceeded via a similar nitrogen-centered pathway but displayed distinct spectral signatures. The acidic proton signal appeared further downfield at δ 11.0 ppm compared to the sulfur mustard adduct (δ 9.5 ppm), reflecting the altered chemical environment and increased acidity associated with the phosphorylation byproduct. Crucially, the vinylic resonance at δ 8.5 ppm, assigned to the C4-H proton of the coumarin moiety was retained, confirming that the α,β-unsaturated coumarin framework remained intact during the reaction. Although the overall chromophore skeleton was preserved, the aromatic region exhibited pronounced changes in both splitting patterns and chemical shifts relative to the sulfur mustard spectrum (Fig. S3B), consistent with a distinct electronic perturbation induced by N-phosphorylation at the benzimidazole nitrogen.

In contrast to the nitrogen-centered pathways observed with sulfur mustard and sarin, the interaction with cyanide triggered a site-specific Michael addition mechanism on the coumarin scaffold. Key evidence for this regioselective attack was the complete disappearance of the sharp singlet at δ 8.7 ppm, a resonance assigned to the C4-H proton of the coumarin moiety, and the appearance of a singlet peak at δ 5.17 ppm, indicating the change in hybridization of the C4 center from sp² to sp³ upon nucleophilic addition of CN⁻ (Figure. S3C). Furthermore, the characteristic signals for the trans double bond (J = 15.7 Hz) persisted with negligible shift, further supporting the conclusion that the Michael addition took place at the double bond within the coumarin moiety. Importantly, the diagnostic resonances of the imidazole ring and distal aromatic signals persisted throughout the titration-undergoing only systematic shifts rather than disappearing, which effectively excludes competitive reactions at the nitrogen site or other aromatic positions.

The mechanistic pathways deduced from the NMR titrations were consistent with known reactivity profiles of these analytes. For sulfur mustard and sarin, their reactions with nucleophilic nitrogen centers, such as those in histidine, are well-documented in biological systems50,52,53. Similarly, in TrioAlert, the N1 and N3 atoms of the benzimidazole ring acted as potent nucleophiles. They attacked the electrophilic sulfonium center of sulfur mustard to form the alkylated adduct (TrioAlert-SM), which quenches fluorescence (Fig. 2D). Likewise, the electrophilic phosphonyl group of sarin was susceptible to nucleophilic attack by the benzimidazole nitrogen, leading to the phosphorylated adduct (TrioAlert-NA). In contrast, the detection of cyanide proceeded via a fundamentally different pathway targeting the fluorophore core. The nucleophilic CN- ion underwent a specific addition to the C-4 position of the coumarin pyrone ring, which is highly activated by the electron-withdrawing substituent at the 3-position. This nucleophilic addition transformed the hybridization of the C-4 carbon from sp2 to sp3, effectively interrupting the electronic conjugation of the coumarin lactone system. This structural disruption weakened the intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) process, which was responsible for the blue shift in the emission wavelength (Fig. 2D). To gain deeper insight into how these structural transformations dictate the distinct colorimetric and fluorometric responses, comprehensive density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations were performed on TrioAlert and its corresponding adducts. To ensure the structural reliability of the computational models, a systematic conformational search was first carried out using the Molclus program. All accessible conformers were optimized at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-SVP level with the IEFPCM solvation model (acetonitrile), and their relative Gibbs free energies (ΔGsolv) were evaluated. The results confirm that the selected conformations correspond to the global minima, thereby providing thermodynamically justified structures for subsequent excited-state calculations (Table S3). For the parent TrioAlert, the optimized structure adopts a nearly planar conformation, supporting efficient electronic delocalization across the conjugated framework. TD-DFT calculations reveal that the S0-S1 transition is dominated by the HOMO-LUMO excitation (83.5% contribution). Canonical molecular orbital (CMO) analysis indicates that the HOMO is primarily composed of the lone-pair (n) orbital of the nitrogen atom bridging the coumarin core, while the LUMO exhibits typical π* character. This identifies the excitation as an n-π* transition, with a calculated energy gap of 5.202 eV and a simulated absorption maximum at 402.69 nm. Upon reaction with sulfur mustard and sarin, the electrophilic attack occurred at the benzimidazole nitrogen, as was confirmed by NMR and HRMS analyses, while the conjugated coumarin backbone remained structurally intact. As a result, the electronic structures of TrioAlert-SM and TrioAlert-NA remained largely similar to that of the parent probe. Their S0-S1 transitions are still dominated by HOMO-LUMO excitations (83.2% and 82.2%, respectively), preserving the n-π* excitation character. The simulated absorption maxima (432.75 nm for TrioAlert-SM and 402.42 nm for TrioAlert-NA) indicated that the extended π-conjugation of the chromophore was retained. This preservation of the n-π* transition pathway, which typically involves weak orbital overlap and low radiative efficiency, provides a reasonable explanation for the experimentally observed fluorescence quenching behavior upon interaction with sulfur mustard and sarin. In these systems, the introduction of electron-withdrawing substituents perturbed the electronic distribution without fundamentally altering the conjugated framework, thereby favoring non-radiative decay pathways.

In contrast, the interaction with cyanide induced a fundamentally different electronic response. As established by the NMR results, the nucleophilic Michael addition of CN- at the C4 position of the coumarin moiety converted the carbon hybridization from sp² to sp³, effectively disrupting the extended π-conjugation of the fluorophore. This structural transformation was reflected in the TD-DFT results. The dominant excitation of TrioAlert-CN switched from HOMO-LUMO to a HOMO-1-LUMO transition (90.7% contribution). CMO analysis further revealed that the HOMO-1 is characterized by a delocalized π orbital distributed over the modified coumarin fragment, while the LUMO retains π* character. Consequently, the excitation was reassigned as a typical π-π* transition, in contrast to the n-π* transitions observed in the other systems. Consistently, the disruption of the conjugation led to a substantial increase in the energy gap from 5.202 eV (TrioAlert) to 6.165 eV (TrioAlert-CN). This widening of the energy gap was consistent with the simulated blue shift in the absorption wavelength (from 402.69 nm to 337.70 nm). The transition from a weakly allowed n-π* excitation to a strongly allowed π-π* excitation, together with the interruption of intramolecular electronic communication, accounted for the distinct optical response of the cyanide adduct.

Notably, the orbital isosurface plots (Fig. 3C) clearly visualize this transition behavior: while the HOMO in TrioAlert, TrioAlert-SM, and TrioAlert-NA is localized in the nitrogen-centered lone-pair region, the HOMO-1 in TrioAlert-CN is redistributed over the modified coumarin framework, providing direct visual evidence of the electronic reorganization induced by cyanide addition. The combined experimental (NMR and HRMS) and theoretical (conformational analysis, TD-DFT spectra, and orbital decomposition) results establish a coherent mechanistic picture: the multi-analyte discrimination capability of TrioAlert originates from site-specific chemical transformations that modulate the fundamental nature of the electronic transitions from n-π* to π-π*-as well as the extent of molecular conjugation.

Response time, sensitivity, and selectivity of TrioAlert toward sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide

Next, the response time and limit of detection (LOD) of TrioAlert for sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide were measured. As shown in Figs. 4A and S1B, the fluorescence intensity of TrioAlert solution remained constant over time while all three CWAs elicited obvious fluorescence and color changes within 30 s in different solvents. Among these three CWAs, sarin exhibited the fastest response rate with TrioAlert, achieving a complete response within 10 s (Fig. 4A). The response time of sulfur mustard and cyanide with TrioAlert was 60 s and 90 s, respectively (Figs. 4A and S1B). Meanwhile, the real-time fluorescence change of the TrioAlert solution upon reagent addition was recorded. After addition of sulfur mustard (5 μL, 100 μM) or sarin (5 μL, 100 μM) to the sensor solution, the fluorescence intensity of TrioAlert rapidly decreased under the UV radiation, which reached its minimum within 10 s. Upon contact with cyanide (5 μL, 200 μM) the orange fluorescence gradually decreased within 10 s, while blue fluorescence simultaneously increased. As shown in Fig. 4C, the fluorescence emission intensity of TrioAlert at 560 nm/442 nm displayed linear relationships with the concentrations of sarin, sulfur mustard, and cyanide. According to LOD = 3σ/κ, the LODs of TrioAlert were 0.28 μM for sarin, 0.32 μM for sulfur mustard, and 0.76 μM for cyanide, respectively. We further tested the detection limits of TrioAlert in DMF for sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide, which were determined to be 58.5 nM, 46.2 nM, and 53.4 nM, respectively (Fig. S5). These results demonstrate that TrioAlert exhibits a rapid response rate and low LOD for sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide.

To evaluate the effect of temperature on the stability of the probe, the fluorescence response of TrioAlert toward three classes of chemical warfare agents was examined at different temperatures. As shown in Fig. S6, the responses of TrioAlert to sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide remained stable over a temperature range of 4–80 °C. When the temperature was increased to 85 °C, the fluorescence intensity of the probe after reaction with sulfur mustard and sarin was slightly higher than that observed at room temperature, whereas the response toward cyanide remained stable up to 90 °C. Subsequently, the response time and sensitivity of TrioAlert were investigated at both low (5 °C) and high (50 °C) temperatures. The response kinetics and sensitivity under these conditions were comparable to those at room temperature, with a slightly shortened response time observed at 50 °C. These results indicate that TrioAlert exhibits thermal stability and maintains robust detection performance under both low- and high-temperature conditions.

The influence of pH on the fluorescence response of TrioAlert was then examined to further assess its environmental tolerance. As shown in Fig. S6F, G, TrioAlert displayed stable response behavior toward sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide over a pH range of 4–10. Although the intrinsic fluorescence of the free probe decreased slightly at extreme pH values (pH < 5 or pH > 9), the fluorescence signals generated after reaction with the target agents remained clearly distinguishable. Moreover, the fluorescence intensities corresponding to all three classes of agents were relatively stable throughout the examined pH range, demonstrating that TrioAlert maintains reliable sensing performance even under acidic or alkaline conditions.

To evaluate the selectivity of TrioAlert for sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide, the probe was further treated with other chemical warfare agents and potential interferents. As shown in Fig. 4D, sulfur mustard and sarin efficiently quenched the fluorescence emission of TrioAlert at 560 nm. In contrast, no significant fluorescence quenching was observed upon exposure to other CWAs, including Lewisite, Soman, VX, and BZ, or to common alkylating agents and solvents such as chloroethane, ethylene oxide, formaldehyde, diethylamine, and dimethyl sulfate. For cyanide sensing, TrioAlert was challenged with other relevant anions (SCN⁻, OCN⁻, S₂O₃²⁻, HPO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, HSO₃⁻) as well as neutral species such as glutathione (GSH) and triethylamine. None of these interferents induced a noticeable fluorescence enhancement at 442 nm, whereas cyanide triggered a pronounced fluorescence increase. These results confirm the high selectivity of TrioAlert toward sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide over the tested interferents.

Environmental sample testing and spike recovery experiments

TrioAlert demonstrated reliable performance in complex environmental matrices, as evidenced by spike recovery experiments in tap water, river water, and soil extract. In these environmental matrix samples, the probe exhibited distinct color and fluorescence changes upon reaction with the three chemical warfare agents, which were consistent with those observed in standard solutions (acetonitrile-PBS) (Fig. S7).

As shown in Table S9, TrioAlert displayed good analytical performance for all tested concentrations of the chemical warfare agents in the three environmental matrices. In relatively clean tap water, the mean recoveries for all analytes ranged from 96.5% to 104.8%, with relative standard deviations (RSDs) below 4.9%, indicating accuracy and precision of the probe in matrices with minimal interference. For the more complex river water and soil extract matrices, recoveries were in the range of 91.4-109.7%, and RSDs were slightly higher. The relatively broader recovery fluctuations observed in the soil extract are largely attributed to optical interference, such as light refraction and reflection, caused by turbid particulate matter and heterogeneous impurities in the matrix. In principle, such matrix effects could be further mitigated by optimizing sample pre-treatment protocols, such as implementing finer filtration or enhanced centrifugation steps. Crucially, all mean recovery values fell within the widely accepted 90–110% range for quantitative analysis, and all RSDs remained below 8%54,55. These results confirm that TrioAlert maintains robust and quantifiable detection performance even in challenging environmental samples.

Fabrication and evaluation of portable test strips for TrioAlert

To facilitate straightforward and rapid field detection, portable test strips were fabricated by immobilizing TrioAlert on filter paper (Fig. 5A). The TrioAlert-loaded test strips exhibited orange fluorescence under UV radiation. Exposure of the strips to varying concentrations of sulfur mustard, sarin, or cyanide resulted in concentration-dependent fluorescence and color changes visible to the naked eye (Fig. 5B–D). The visual detection limit (VDL), defined as the minimum concentration required to induce a discernible color change, was determined for each agent. Although the fluorescence responses of TrioAlert toward the three classes of agents were readily observable under controlled conditions, the quenching signals induced by sulfur mustard or sarin may be difficult to distinguish in complex field environments. To address this issue, a ratiometric strategy was introduced by employing TrioAlert-B as a reference probe. TrioAlert-B exhibited similar emission characteristics to TrioAlert but showed no response toward chemical warfare agents. By using TrioAlert-B as an internal reference, the agent-induced quenching signal of TrioAlert could be identified against the constant background fluorescence of TrioAlert-B. Using this ratiometric approach, the VDLs were determined to be 0.05 ppm for sulfur mustard, 0.01 ppm for sarin, and 5 ppm for cyanide. Crucially, all VDLs were well below the respective Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health (IDLH) and Acute Exposure Guideline Level 2 (AEGL-2) values, confirming the system’s capability to provide an early warning before hazardous exposure levels are reached56–58. Imaging of sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide in living cells

Motivated by its promising in vitro performance, we next investigated the capability of TrioAlert to image CWAs in living cells. First, the cytotoxicity of TrioAlert was tested with CCK-8 assays. As shown in Fig. S9, the sensor had almost no effect on cell viability. Next, we used TrioAlert to image sulfur mustard in living HaCaT cells. After incubation with TrioAlert (20 μM), the cells exhibited fluorescence in the green channel and almost no fluorescence in the blue channel (Fig. 6A1). Upon addition of sulfur mustard (100 μM), the green fluorescence of the cells was quenched while the fluorescence in the blue channel remained unchanged. The sulfur mustard-induced reduction of the green fluorescence in cells was completed within 4 min (Fig. 6A2–A4). Furthermore, the cells treated with lower concentrations of sulfur mustard (25 and 50 μM) exhibited higher residual green fluorescence after 4 min (Fig. 6A5, A6) compared to cells treated with a higher concentration (100 μM). Potassium 2,3-butanedione monoxime is the main active ingredient of RSDL that can neutralise sulfur mustard and nerve agents60. Pretreatment with RSDL largely abolished the sulfur mustard-induced fluorescence quenching (Fig. 6A7). Preliminary histopathological and biochemical evaluations conducted at the 10-min post-exposure stage revealed minimal tissue damage and no significant alterations in pathological markers across the high-dose groups. In contrast, the in vivo fluorescence intensities already exhibited statistically significant changes (Fig. 8B), demonstrating that TrioAlert provides a rapid optical warning that precedes the onset of agent-induced morphological or physiological deterioration. However, histopathological analyses of skin and lung tissues harvested 24 hours post-exposure revealed varying degrees of substantial morphological damage, accompanied by significant alterations in agent-specific biochemical markers (Fig. 8C, D). These physiological manifestations align with the established toxicological profiles of sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide. More importantly, a comparative analysis between the early in vivo fluorescence variations and the pathological marker fluctuations revealed a robust correlation(Fig. 8D). This indicates that TrioAlert can reliably predict the severity of CWA-induced physiological damage, thereby offering substantial potential for assisted clinical diagnosis.

Limitations of this study

Although TrioAlert represents the first fluorescent sensor capable of simultaneously detecting three distinct classes of high-threat CWAs, its scope of detectable agents is inherently limited. Certain high-threat CWAs, such as V-series nerve agents and the blister agent Lewisite, cannot be detected. While TrioAlert should theoretically be capable of detecting A-series nerve agents like Novichok, experimental confirmation was not conducted due to the unavailability of Novichok agent sources. Regarding selectivity, although the fluorescence response patterns of sulfur mustard and sarin exhibit a certain degree of similarity, TrioAlert can independently differentiate them through distinct colorimetric changes under ambient light, utilizing the ONP probe as a complementary validation tool. More importantly, strong acids can also decrease the fluorescence of TrioAlert. Despite the limitations of TrioAlert in terms of detection spectrum and selectivity, its development nonetheless represents a meaningful step forward. As highlighted in the OPCW’s Practical Guide for Medical Management of Chemical Warfare Casualties, all portable detection and identification devices, regardless of the technology used, sometimes yield false positives and false negatives due to their sensitivity and selectivity. From the perspective of applying fluorescent sensors to CWA detection, the immediate priority is to design and synthesize a diverse array of fluorescent sensors targeting different CWAs-especially those with relatively low reactivity, such as sulfur mustard. Only by establishing a sufficiently broad sensor library can we, in the future, leverage artificial intelligence to assemble these sensors into arrays capable of achieving full-spectrum monitoring of all CWAs and even enabling rapid on-site identification of unknown toxic agents.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed TrioAlert, the first fluorescent sensor capable of simultaneously detecting three high-threat chemical warfare agents with high sensitivity and rapidity. Its innovative design synergistically combines an imidazole group for dual recognition of sulfur mustard and sarin, with an activated coumarin C-4 site for cyanide detection. This integration endows TrioAlert with low nanomolar to sub-micromolar detection limits, rapid response (within seconds to minutes), and distinct fluorescence and color signals. TrioAlert-loaded test strips enable qualitative and quantitative detection of sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide via a smartphone-based colorimetric analysis program. With low visual detection limits of 0.05 ppm, 0.01 ppm, and 5 ppm, respectively, these strips demonstrate robust early warning capability. Furthermore, the TrioAlert probe was applied to imaging of sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide in living cells and mice. Owing to its rapid response and high sensitivity, TrioAlert enabled detection of these agents before the onset of pathological damage. The fluorescence imaging results correlated significantly with those of standard pathophysiological biomarkers, demonstrating its potential for clinical diagnosis. This study addresses an urgent need for field-deployable tools in chemical threat management and medical applications. Ultimately, the development of an increasing number of multi-target fluorescent sensors for chemical warfare agents, e.g., TrioAlert and NNP-1, is expected to accelerate the advancement of AI-driven sensor arrays, enabling broad-spectrum detection of all existing CWAs and even field detection of uncharacterized chemical threats.

Methods

Safety statement

Sulfur mustard, sarin, soman, potassium cyanide, VX, Lewisite, and BZ are highly toxic chemical warfare agents and present an extreme hazard. All experimental procedures involving these substances must be conducted under strictly controlled safety conditions. Operators are required to wear full-face respirators and other necessary personal protective equipment. All work must be performed in a certified fume hood or glovebox.

Chemicals and measurements

Chemical warfare agents including sulfur mustard, soman, sarin, potassium cyanide, VX, Lewisite, and BZ were obtained from the Institute of Chemical Defense (China). Unless otherwise specified, all reagents were of analytical grade and used directly without further purification. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired on a Bruker AV spectrometer (400/101 MHz) in DMSO-d₆, DMF-d7, or CDCl₃. Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard.

High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) data for TrioAlert and its reaction products with chemical warfare agents were obtained using an Agilent 6520 Q-TOF LC-MS spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. For sample preparation, the products were dissolved in acetonitrile. MS acquisition was performed in positive ion mode with a capillary voltage of 3500 V, a drying gas temperature of 350 °C, a drying gas flow of 10 L/min, and a nebulizer pressure of 35 psi. A total of n = 3 independent replicates were analyzed for each reaction sample, alongside vehicle controls. Mass spectrometry data annotation, exact mass calculation, and molecular formula validation were conducted using Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis software (version B.07.00, Agilent Technologies).

Fluorometric measurements in solutions

All fluorescence spectra were measured at room temperature using a Hitachi F-2710 fluorescence spectrophotometer. The excitation and emission slit widths were set to 5/10 nm and the photomultiplier tube voltage was 400/700 V for NNP-1, NNP-2, ONP-1, ONP-2, TrioAlert, and TrioAlert-B. These sensors were dissolved in acetonitrile to prepare a 1 mM stock solution, which was then diluted with PBS buffer to a working concentration of 20 μM. Sulfur mustard, sarin, and cyanide were added to the solution. The resulting solution (500 μL) was transferred to a quartz cuvette, and the fluorescence spectra were recorded by a fluorescence spectrophotometer. For sulfur mustard and sarin, the excitation wavelength was 480 nm, and the emission spectrum was scanned from 510 to 700 nm. For cyanide, the excitation wavelength was 380 nm, and the emission spectrum was scanned from 410 to 700 nm.

Environmental sample testing and spike recovery

The performance of TrioAlert was evaluated in three representative environmental matrices: municipal tap water, river water, and soil, using spike recovery experiments.

Collection and pretreatment of environmental samples

Tap Water: Tap water samples were collected from the local municipal supply and left to settle for 2 h at room temperature before use.

River Water: Surface water samples were collected from a local river at a depth of 5 cm and stored at 4 °C. Within 6 h of collection, the samples were subjected to high-speed centrifugation (10,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C) to precipitate suspended solids. The supernatant was then sequentially filtered through 0.45 μm and 0.22 μm hydrophilic polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) syringe filters to effectively remove colloidal particles and microorganisms that could induce severe Tyndall scattering.

Soil Extract: Topsoil (0-5 cm depth) was collected from an agricultural field, air-dried, finely ground, and sieved through a 100-mesh screen. A homogenized aliquot (10.0 g) was dispersed in 20 mL of PBS. The suspension was vigorously vortexed for 5 min and sonicated for 30 min to ensure exhaustive extraction. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected and sequentially filtered through 0.45 μm and 0.22 μm PTFE membranes.

Spike recovery assay

The processed environmental samples were placed in transparent centrifuge tubes. A specific volume of each chemical warfare agent was added to the samples to achieve final concentrations (Sulfur mustard and sarin: 10, 20, and 40 μM, cyanide: 25, 50, and 100 μM). TrioAlert stock solution (final concentration 20 μM) was then added, mixed by vortexing, and the fluorescence intensity was measured. The fluorescence testing conditions were consistent with those used in Fluorometric Measurements in Solutions. The measured fluorescence intensities were compared against those from standard calibration curves (prepared in acetonitrile-PBS) to calculate the spike recovery rates for each agent in each matrix.

Sensing mechanism and theoretical calculations of TrioAlert

All theoretical calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 (Revision C.01) software package. To account for the conformational flexibility of the molecules, a systematic conformational search was initially conducted to identify the lowest-energy conformer at room temperature, which was then used as the starting structure for all subsequent calculations. Ground-state geometry optimizations and harmonic vibrational frequency analyses were carried out at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-SVP level of theory. The integral equation formalism variant of the polarizable continuum model (IEFPCM) was employed to simulate the acetonitrile solvent environment. The absence of imaginary frequencies confirmed that all optimized structures represent true local minima on the potential energy surface.

Excited-state properties and vertical transition energies were computed based on time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) at the CAM-B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP level, also incorporating the IEFPCM model for acetonitrile. Canonical molecular orbital (CMO) analysis was conducted using the NBO 6.0 program at the CAM-B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP level in the IEFPCM-acetonitrile environment. Finally, the wavefunction files were processed using the Multiwfn 3.8 (dev) program to generate the corresponding orbital cube files, which were subsequently visualized and rendered using the VMD 1.9.3 software.

Cell culture and fluorescence imaging in living cells

HaCaT cells (catalog no. SCSP-5091), A549 cells (catalog no. SCSP-503), and BV2 cells (catalog no. SCSP-5208) were purchased from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. All cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. For cellular experiments, the cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 10⁵ cells/mL. All operations were performed using aseptic techniques. After cell attachment, fresh medium containing TrioAlert (20 μM) was added, and the cells were incubated for 10 min. Cells were washed three times with PBS to remove excess TrioAlert. Then, different concentrations of sulfur mustard, cyanide, and sarin were added to the cell culture medium and incubated for various durations. Fluorescence imaging was performed using an Agilent BioTek Cytation 5 cell imaging multimode reader equipped with a ×20 objective lens. Images and data were analyzed using BioTek Gen5 software (Excitation wavelength Ex = 380/469 nm). All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) (n = 5).

Animal administration

Six-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from the Animal Center of Naval Medical University and acclimatized for one week prior to experiments under standard housing conditions at a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and 12 h light/dark cycle with free access to standard laboratory chow and purified water. Since sex was not expected to influence the outcomes of this study, it was not considered a biological variable, and only male mice were used. For acute toxicity experiments, mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 3 per group). The control group received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of acetonitrile-PBS, while the TrioAlert group received an i.p. injection of TrioAlert (100 mg/kg) in acetonitrile-PBS. After 24 h, all mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (0.2 mL/10 g). Then mice were euthanized, and the tissues were collected immediately for H&E and Nissl staining, including brain, lung, intestine, spleen, liver, and kidney.

Fluorescent imaging in living mice

For abdominal skin imaging, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 2,2,2-tribromoethanol, and the abdominal hair was removed. Skin exposure concentrations were set at 100 μM for sulfur mustard and sarin, and 200 μM for KCN, as these concentrations elicited in vitro fluorescence responses without causing observable pathological damage over the short experimental duration61–63. The mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5 per group). In the exposure experiments, mice were first topically treated with the specific chemical warfare agent, followed by the topical application of TrioAlert. Specifically, the groups included a control group receiving only acetonitrile-PBS (5 μL), a TrioAlert-only group (5 μL, 50 μM), and three agent exposure groups that were pretreated with sulfur mustard (5 μL, 100 μM), sarin (5 μL, 100 μM), or cyanide (5 μL, 200 μM), respectively, prior to the application of TrioAlert (5 μL, 50 μM). After fluorescence imaging, the abdominal skin was harvested for subsequent analysis.

To evaluate the dose-dependent in vivo fluorescence responses and perform subsequent toxicological assessments, we subjected mice to agent-specific exposure protocols and sampling timelines (n = 5 per subgroup). For sulfur mustard, mice were exposed via the abdominal skin at specific dose gradients of 10, 20, and 50 μg/cm², and were assigned to a single 24-hour observation endpoint. For sarin and hydrogen cyanide (HCN), pulmonary exposures were conducted utilizing a nebulized inhalation model at gradient concentrations of 0.05, 0.1, and 1 ppm (sarin) or 10, 20, and 50 ppm (HCN). To prevent sampling stress or terminal tissue collection from confounding downstream analyses, the animals in the inhalation groups (sarin and HCN) were randomly divided into two independent parallel cohorts corresponding to 10-minute and 24-hour endpoints. Following the designated exposures and in vivo fluorescence imaging, the 10-minute cohorts for sarin and HCN were sacrificed at 10 minutes post-exposure to collect peripheral blood and relevant tissues for the quantification of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and cytochrome c oxidase (COX) activities. Conversely, the 24-hour cohorts across all three agents (including the sulfur mustard group) were continuously monitored for 24 hours post-exposure. Subsequently, peripheral blood was sampled to determine white blood cell (WBC) counts, and the targeted exposed tissues (abdominal skin for sulfur mustard, and lung tissues for sarin and HCN) were harvested and subjected to H&E staining for histopathological evaluation.

Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Tanon Prime In Vivo Imaging System. Images and data were analyzed using Tanon Prime In Vivo Imaging System software. All data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 5).

Statistics & reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on standard practices in the field of chemical sensing and imaging to ensure adequate power for statistical analysis. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.

All experiments were performed using independent biological replicates (at least n = 3 or n = 5, as specified in the figure legends), and all attempts at replication were successful. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5) and Origin (version 2024). For comparisons between two groups, a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, an ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was applied. Linear regression analysis was used to assess the relationship between fluorescence intensity and analyte concentration. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Exact P values are provided in the Source Data file.
