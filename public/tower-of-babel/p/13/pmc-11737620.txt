Trim the sail: A rare cause of newborn hypoxia
Authors: Khalid A. Katranji, Max Jacob Spiro, Cecilia A. Albaro, Harma K. Turbendian, Gretchen L. Kopec
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe right venous valve is critical to the proper development of the fetal heart. As the right venous valve degenerates later in fetal development, residual structures can remain, such as the terminal crest, Eustachian valve, Thebesian valve, and a prominent Chiari network, with a sail-like appearance resembling a spinnaker. While these structures are often incidentally found on adult echocardiograms, we present a case of a term neonate with hypoxia secondary to a persistent right valve of the sinus venosus.

INTRODUCTION

The right venous valve in the right atrium is an essential embryonic component during fetal heart development, crucial for circulation in early gestation. While these valves regress as the heart matures, remnants such as the Eustachian valve and terminal crest may persist, may be observed in adult echocardiograms, and are regarded as benign.[1] Failure of regression leads to structures with potential clinical implications. We describe a rare occurrence of a term infant with hypoxia due to shunting secondary to a persistent right valve of the sinus venosus.

CLINICAL SUMMARY

A healthy, full-term male was delivered at 39 weeks of gestation, with a weight of 3.3 kg, after an unremarkable pregnancy. An echocardiogram was obtained due to persistent hypoxia on his critical congenital heart screening. The initial echocardiogram demonstrated an aneurysmal atrial septum with a right-to-left shunt, a prominent Chiari network, and an abnormal tricuspid valve without stenosis. This prominent Chiari network formed a spinnaker sail-like valve, which led to right-to-left shunting across the patent foramen ovale (PFO) [Videos 1 and 2]. The patient remained mildly hypoxic with oxygen saturations of 85%–92% on room air. A repeat echocardiogram demonstrated a large, persistent right valve of the sinus venosus and PFO [Figure 1]. He was observed, weaned off supplemental oxygen, and discharged home on the day of life 14.

Image of the partial occlusion of the tricuspid valve with the spinnaker, leading to the right-to-left shunting across the patent foramen ovale, labeled

At his 12-month visit, he remained mildly hypoxic with an oxygen saturation of 90%. His echocardiogram was unchanged, with the persistence of the right venous valve and intermittent prolapse through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, thus partially occluding the tricuspid orifice [Figure 2]. As shown in the echocardiogram, the spinnaker formation appears to prolapse into the right ventricle, rendering the blood flow easier to pass from right to left across the PFO. The tricuspid valve, while still unobstructed, has a smaller volume of inflow due to the spinnaker formation; much of the blood is directed from the right atrium to the left atrium across the PFO. The patient was discussed at a multidisciplinary conference and, due to his mild hypoxia and intermittent tricuspid valve occlusion, was recommended for elective surgical resection.

Tricuspid valve with the right valve of the sinus venosus prolapsing toward RV like a spinnaker partially occluding the tricuspid orifice

At 2 years of age, the patient was taken to the operating room for elective resection of the persistent right valve of the sinus venosus through median sternotomy. A decision was made to have surgery before school age. Cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated, the heart was arrested, and intraoperative examination through a right atriotomy demonstrated a partially circumferential, sail-like membrane, which extended from the orifice of the inferior vena cava and inserted at the cranial aspect of the coronary sinus [Figure 3]. This was removed entirely. The patient did well and was discharged on postoperative day 2.

Intraoperative image of Chiari network. The sail-like spinnaker membrane of the Chiari network is demonstrated within the right atrium during resection

His postdischarge course was unremarkable. A follow-up echocardiogram 1 week after discharge showed no residual tissue and no pericardial effusion. A 24-h Holter monitoring revealed normal sinus rhythm, and pulse oximetry revealed normoxia.

DISCUSSION

The right and left valves encircle the orifice of the sinus venosus, which protrudes into the atrial cavity and merges dorsocranially to create a ridge termed the septum spurium.[2] During fetal development, both the right and left venous valves play crucial roles in ensuring oxygenated blood passes from the umbilical vein into the left atrium. By the 9–15 weeks of gestation, the right sinus horn integrates into the smooth portion of the right atrium, while the left sinus horn regresses. Following birth, the closure of the foramen ovale occurs with the regression of the venous valves. The left venous valve vanishes entirely, whereas the right venous valve persists as the terminal crest, Eustachian valve, and Thebesian valve. The arrest of this regression at various stages results in a spectrum of pathologies and echo findings, each carrying possible clinical significance.[13]

Incomplete regression of the right valve of the sinus venosus results in a thin, fenestrated, and mobile lesion that attaches across the right atrium, which has been described by several terms, including Chiari network, cor triatriatum dexter, and spinnaker formation of the sinus venosus valve (due to its sail-like appearance).[4] While its prevalence in adults ranges from 2% to 15%, based on imaging and autopsy studies, its neonatal incidence remains unknown.[25] Within an adult cohort, patients exhibited a high prevalence of arterial embolism (83%), atrial septal aneurysm (21%), and PFO.[6] Although considered of minimal clinical significance when diagnosed during echocardiography, severe cases have been reported, with resultant fetal hydrops and even sudden cardiac death.[5]

This case highlights a rare instance of neonatal hypoxia in an otherwise healthy term infant. Initial supplemental oxygen support was presumably discontinued as pulmonary vascular resistance dropped and the proportion of right-to-left shunting through the PFO decreased. Our patient demonstrated persistent mild hypoxia and an echocardiogram that was concerning for the potential for complete inflow occlusion of the tricuspid valve, and so surgical resection was performed.[6]
