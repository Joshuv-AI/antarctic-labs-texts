Gradient transformer Self-Attention U-Net for enhanced crack detection in concrete bridges
Authors: Bubryur Kim, M Shyamala Devi, Yuvaraj Natarajan, Sri Preethaa K R, C S Yazhini, Jin-Young Park, Chan-Jeong Park, Chang-Yong Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcrete bridge maintenance is crucial for infrastructure sustainability and public safety. Accurate identification of cracks in bridge components is a critical task, yet traditional inspection methods often fall short due to their limitations in accuracy and efficiency. This paper introduces the Gradient Transformer Attention U-Net (GTAU-Net) model, a novel deep learning approach that significantly advances crack detection in bridge components. Unlike conventional attention-based U-Nets, GTAU-Net introduces a Quantum Fused Filter (QFF) to pre-process images by integrating multiple edge and gradient patterns through a quantum-inspired hybrid filtering strategy. It further computes Gradient Saliency Scores (GSS) to dynamically guide the self-attention mechanism, enabling more precise localization and feature extraction. Through this dual enhancement, GTAU-Net effectively handles varying crack sizes, shapes, orientations, and environmental conditions. Experimental results demonstrate that GTAU-Net achieves an impressive 99.42% accuracy significantly outperforming existing models and measures crack lengths, highlighting sustainability. This research contributes to the advancement of automated crack detection technology, offering a promising solution for enhancing infrastructure safety and durability. Introduction

Bridges are essential components of infrastructure and must remain functional even after being subjected to extreme dynamic forces, such as earthquakes, floods, or tsunamis. Catastrophic failures pose serious threats to public safety and can produce devastating outcomes. For example, the collapse of the Morandi Polcevera viaduct in Italy resulted in 43 fatalities and 9 injuries, with direct economic losses estimated at 100 million yuan1. The collapse of the Silver Bridge in 1976 resulted in the tragic loss of 46 lives2. The collapse of the Tuojiang Bridge led to 64 fatalities and 22 injuries, with financial drop projected at approximately 39.747 million yuan3. Design flaws, construction errors, hydraulic forces, collisions, cracks in components, and overloading are the primary causes of bridge failures, accounting for over 70% of all such incidents4. The United States has around 617,000 bridges, with 42% being older than 50 years and 46,154 identified as structurally deficient due to cracks, which potentially affects millions of people5. In Canada, 40% of the roads and bridges are rated to be in poor condition, requiring replacement due to cracks and various other types of damage6. In contrast, Germany has notably ramped up its investment in bridge construction and rehabilitation7. In 1980, the Eureka earthquake in California severely damaged the components of the Tompkins Hill Road overpass, a concrete bridge. The lack of appropriate maintenance led to six injuries due to the failure of supporting columns8. On March 15, 2018, a concrete-truss pedestrian bridge in Miami, Florida, collapsed during its construction phase, causing multiple fatalities and injuries and raising serious concerns about the integrity of its structural elements9. The rehabilitation of reinforced-concrete bridges represents an efficient method to mitigate their environmental impact, especially considering the current emphasis on sustainable development. Moreover, the adoption of modern monitoring systems could enable the early detection of structural issues, allowing for swift and focused repair work10.

Numerous studies have been conducted globally to evaluate the performance of bridges during natural hazard events. The failure of bridge components can lead to several types of catastrophic damage11. Concrete bridges are susceptible to ageing, climatic stresses, and motion wear; hence, their maintenance constitutes a crucial aspect of infrastructure governance. One of the most prevalent indicators of concrete structure deterioration is cracking, which can severely compromise the structural health of bridges12. Increasing traffic volumes and frequent overloading lead to the formation of fatigue cracks in both the reinforcement and concrete, significantly reducing the bridge’s structural strength and requiring timely reinforcement13. Overloading may also deform the bridge deck, leading to joint damage and material separation14. Early crack detection and classification are crucial for prompt intervention to avert catastrophic failures. Because cracks in concrete bridge components are intricate, widespread, and often subtle, they are difficult to detect using traditional inspection techniques involving manual visual examination or traditional image-processing methods15. These approaches lack the accuracy necessary for precise crack classification and severity evaluation, in addition to being laborious and prone to being affected by human error16. However, the progress of deep learning (DL) methods in this domain has sparked interest in the automation of crack identification and classification, which can be achieved through the development of DL-based algorithms17. For instance, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated a remarkable ability to recognize and classify cracks from structural health data. Nevertheless, certain problems remain unresolved, particularly with images depicting cracks of different dimensions, along different directions, and having visibility levels, because of occlusions, noise, and illumination18. DL models with more complex designs incorporating several cutting-edge methods, such as attention mechanisms and encoder–decoder structures for pixel-wise classification, are being developed.

Semantic segmentation classifies each pixel of an image into a specific class but does not differentiate between individual objects of the same class, whereas instance segmentation performs both classification and object delineation, assigning separate masks to each object instance. The advantage of instance segmentation using YOLOv7 WMF19 with connected feature fusion (CFF) for pavement cracks shows that instance level approaches are more effective for detecting discontinuous and overlapping cracks. CFF may struggle with fine-grained pixel-level classification in dense or degraded environments. CFF improves feature reuse but may lack the ability to capture long-range dependencies between spatial features19. The adaptive feature expansion and fusion for component segmentation shows the importance of localized and instance specific feature handling. The adaptive feature expansion method20 improves robustness in standardized patterns but may be less effective in noisy crack environments. These studies emphasize that while semantic segmentation provides dense predictions, instance segmentation methods offer finer granularity and improved detection of complex structures and are directly relevant to the motivation for the proposed GTAU Net framework. Recent advances in computer vision have significantly impacted automated crack analysis in concrete and pavement structures. Early approaches focused on crack classification, where models were trained to identify the presence or absence of cracks in images. This evolved into object detection, where algorithms such as YOLO variants detect and localize cracks within complex scenes, enabling more precise identification of crack regions21. More recently, segmentation methods have become central to crack analysis, providing pixel-level delineation of crack structures. Segmentation approaches can be categorized into instance segmentation, which distinguishes individual crack instances22, and semantic segmentation, which classifies each pixel as crack or background. Semantic segmentation approaches, including transformer-based methods23, have shown challenge in capturing complex crack patterns and enhancing the accuracy of automated structural inspections. By positioning the proposed GTAU-Net within this progression, this research aim to leverage the benefits of semantic segmentation by integrating the adaptive attention with feature fusion to achieve more precise and robust crack detection.

The integration of transformer architectures with CNNs represents a fascinating area of computer vision research, the goal of which is to develop models that can learn global connections and long-range dependencies by leveraging the self-attention mechanisms in transformers, as well as detect local patterns through the convolutional operations of CNNs. The transformer attention mechanism can also be integrated with U-Net (a CNN) to combine the benefits of both architectures; thus, the local spatial patterns in an image can be acquired using U-Net’s convolutional layers while contextual dependencies can be captured by the attention module. Accordingly, this study introduces the gradient transformer attention U-Net (GTAU-Net) model, which generates quantum fused filter (QFF) images to compute gradient saliency scores (GSSs) before performing significance embedding within the feature maps (FMs). Related work

Cracks in concrete structures have been identified using U-Net11 and Otsu threshold segmentation12 with built-in datasets. Crack detection models can be refined by employing histograms and other filtering methods. The context-augmented capsule feature pyramid network13 detects cracks by utilizing a context-augmented module to rapidly expand the neurons. The DFT (Discrete Fourier Transform)-CNN explainable artificial intelligence model14 can be used to detect cracks via knowledge transfer within the hidden layers of the model. VGG167,15–17,20 can detect concrete surface cracks and can be refined by varying the learning rate using different optimizers. Cosine KNN (k-nearest neighbors)18 and other machine learning (ML) classifiers24 have been used to classify cracked and normal regions on concrete surfaces; these models can be enhanced by incorporating advanced feature extraction methods. AlexNet25 can detect cracks from images of concrete underwater dams by employing feature extraction methods to identify the critical areas of the images. The Otsu algorithm26 facilitates crack detection by removing background noise from images of concrete surfaces using morphological noise reduction techniques. NASNetMobile27 can detect cracks in concrete surface images when pre-trained on suitable datasets. A lightweight pavement crack segmentation model28 and the FedCrack29 model was designed to detect concrete cracks; these models must be refined using filtering methods to identify cracks with various shapes. YOLOv5-IDS30 and a deformable transformer–based model31 were designed to detect cracks from concrete surface images; these models can be refined by incorporating crack measurements.

A bilayer crack detection model32 was designed to classify crack severity levels by connecting discontinuous cracks in images. The first layer of this model detects cracks with different textures via adaptive thresholding, while the second layer removes residual noise to classify cracks based on images from a concrete defect dataset. CrackFormer33 is a transformer-based high-resolution network featuring a self-attention module that extracts semantic features from images, along with ML algorithms34 that detect cracks with various patterns on different types of pavement surfaces. SVM (Support vector machine) multi-classifiers35 and the light gradient-boosting machine36 have been used to detect concrete cracks by calculating crack pixel values and determining crack shapes. Adaptive thresholding37 based on median absolute deviation has been utilized to detect cracks on concrete surfaces. The GhostNet al.gorithm38, which performs bilateral filtering and denoising, has been used to detect concrete surface cracks. A dual-encoding fusion network model39 incorporating attention mechanisms was designed to detect cracks on concrete pavement. A model combining groupwise normalization attention with padding–shifting–cutting convolution40 was designed to detect concrete surface cracks. YOLOv8-CDD41 uses a bot-transformer module to detect concrete surface cracks when trained on a concrete defect detection dataset. The aforementioned module accurately extracts the global features, while a triple attention module strengthens the data between the feature channel and spatial feature. A combination of SVM with the Otsu threshold42 has been used to detect concrete cracks. The S2TNet43 model was trained on self-created datasets to detect concrete pavement cracks by enhancing the contrast between the foreground and background features of the images. Cascade CATransUNet44 uses a joint cascade loss function to enhance the segmentation quality integrated with the transformer.

Crack pixel segmentation45 and ResNet46 have been utilized to detect cracks based on pixel representations of concrete matrices. A sequential CNN47 and the SparseInst-CDSM algorithm48 were designed to detect pavement cracks using a central axis skeleton method. The analysis of CNN for detecting the crack with SDNET dataset was done and inferenced with Xception to show high accuracy49. Mask-RCNN50 was integrated with an optimization algorithm to fine-tune geometric features due to temperature effect analysis. A semi-supervised semantic segmentation model51 has been used to detect cracks in bridge pavement. YOLOV752, InceptionV353, and a two-branched CNN model54 can detect cracks in concrete structures when trained on the SDNET2018 and CrackForest datasets. Enhanced Image Classification using Edge CNN (E-CNN)66 demonstrates that explicitly incorporating edge features within the CNN architecture significantly elevates image classification performance. By integrating edge-aware modules, E-CNN is able to enhance feature extraction around object contours, thereby improving the discrimination between object and background, especially in challenging scenarios with complex textures or weak structural cues. BG-Net67 for semantic segmentation highlights that semantic segmentation models benefit markedly from boundary-guidance strategies. By designing a dual-branch network where one branch specializes in semantic content and the other in boundary detection, BG-Net is able to maintain object consistency and produce segmentation maps with more precise and continuous object boundaries. The enhanced crack segmentation method68 exemplifies a hybrid strategy: leveraging the strengths of implicit, deep learning–based feature classification and explicit, rule-based post-processing to deliver reliable, interpretable, and domain-aware crack detection. This hybrid approach not only improves technical performance but also enhances trust and practical applicability in real-world structural health monitoring scenarios, where transparency and reliability are essential.

The image anomaly detection framework69 that leverages a transformer architecture enriched with mutual attention mechanisms. The key advancement lies in the ability of mutual attention to model bidirectional interactions between local and global representations within visual data. By dynamically relating global semantic context to localized image patches and vice versa, the model significantly improves the detection of subtle, spatially dispersed, or context-dependent anomalies. By incorporating an attention pyramid, EAPT efficiently abstracts multi-scale semantic features, reducing computational complexity and memory requirements. By combining local and global context through efficient attention modules, EAPT enhances the model’s ability to capture fine-grained details and broad semantic information that effectively distinguish the defect irregularities. Materials and methods

Figure 1 illustrates the model’s workflow. GTAU-Net was designed to classify six types of crack and non-crack patterns for bridge walls, bridge decks, and bridge pavement. It was trained on the SDNET2018 dataset50, which contains 36,000 images. In Stage 1, crack and clean images of bridge walls, bridge decks, and bridge pavement are collected. The edges and textures of the image pixels are separately processed by hybrid filters. Then, quantum amplitude amplification is applied to the extracted edges and textures, followed by a nonlinear fusion of the pixels to create the QFF images. Stage 2 focuses on image augmentation via geometric transformations, which yields 72,000 images. Stage 3 involves designing the GTAU-Net framework, which processes the augmented images. Augmented images are divided in the form 80:10:10 and trained with GTAU-Net model. The images are processed using three encoder blocks, each featuring a single convolutional layer. The FM of the final encoder block is linearly projected onto the GTSA and global self-attention (GSA) modules to create the output mask. Stage 4 represents the crack measurement post-processing operation, which involves identifying the most probable crack pixels and measuring the crack lengths.

Generation of QFF images

The primary contribution of this work is the creation of QFF images based on the concept of quantum computing, specifically quantum physics principles such as superposition and probability amplitudes. The input crack image is first converted to grayscale and then processed to increase its sharpness. Quantum superposition is leveraged to analyze the multiple aspects of the sharpened image simultaneously, such as the edges and textures. Thereafter, edge enhancement using the Canny edge detector and texture enhancement through Laplacian transformation are performed to obtain an edge-enhanced and texture-enhanced image. Subsequently, based on the principle of quantum probability amplitudes71,73, the probability of each pixel belonging to a crack or non-crack region in the image is determined. Grover’s search sin2 based boosting74,75 was performed to boost the crack pixels in the image. Finally, adaptive filtering was performed to amplify the pixels belonging to cracks and suppress noise pixels. So, the feature vectors of the QFF image were obtained in a nonlinear fashion. The term quantum-inspired is used to describe a classical simulation of principles from quantum computing to improve feature extraction that does not refer to actual quantum computation. Specifically, the creation of QFF images is guided by the concepts of superposition and probability amplitudes in a metaphorical manner. QFF is implemented as a fusion framework that enhances edge and texture information simultaneously and uses an amplitude boosting technique adapted from Grover’s search principles to emphasize crack pixels. GTAU-Net framework design

The augmented QFF images were split into training, validation, and testing subsets in an 80:10:10 ratio and fitted using the GTAU-Net model, as shown in Fig. 4.

GTAU-Net framework.

With GTAU-Net, the QFF images are first processed via convolution in the three-encoder block by the successive max pooling layers in GTAU-Net (Fig. 4) to create the FM. The FM contains both local and broader features and is sent to the GTSA and GSA modules. The novelty of the proposed design lies in the GTSA block’s ability to dynamically identify and focus on the key probable crack pixels of the image. The FM is linearly projected to generate the token vector. The GTSA and GSA modules (Fig. 4) learn the non-local interactions among the features in the FM. The GTSA module receives the token vector and performs non-overlapping patch embedding, converting each patch into a vector. All the patches are embedded to create the feature vector f, with position encodings used to capture spatial relationships. Then, f is divided into query (q), key (k), and value (v) sequence vectors, which are used to compute the GSSs using a self-attention matrix. The scalar target (Fig. 4) is defined for the vector, and the gradient is calculated for each q, k, and v. Subsequently, the GSSs are computed through L2 normalization of the gradients, before being normalized to perform significance embedding. The GSS is an empirical measure derived from the gradient response of the GTSA maps, which highlights the regions most influential for crack prediction. It builds on the concept of saliency maps and integrates them into the transformer gradient flow to weight features adaptively during training.

The contextual attention map (CAM) is then created from the significance embedding. Thereafter, the CAM vector (Fig. 4) is obtained by performing a scaled dot-product operation between q and the transpose of k via softmax normalization. The matrix of the CAM is then multiplied by v to obtain the aggregated weighted attention map, which is used to create the optimized semantic FM. The TSA module (Fig. 4) in GTAU-Net improves feature learning by dynamically focusing on key portions of the bridge crack image, which helps the model discriminate between crack and non-crack areas. The GSA module accurately learns the non-local interactions among the features in the encoder FM. Accordingly, it selectively integrates the universal context of the optimized FM and encodes the contextual data into local features to create the optimized contextual FM. Additionally, a multiscale skip-connection is included between the encoder and decoder blocks to integrate the up-sampled FM with various semantic levels, which facilitates crack segmentation. This skip-connection aggregate semantic features of various scales through operations such as up-sampling, convolution, and concatenation. The final layer contains six neurons, which classify the crack and non-crack patterns in bridge walls, decks, and pavement. The final classified image is then subjected to a crack measurement operation to identify the probable crack pixels and measure the crack lengths. Quality analysis of QFF images

The proposed model was implemented in Python using the Keras library. From the implementation, it is evident that canny edges were observed to have high accuracy. To improve the edge extraction, quantum computing principles were incorporated into the image-filtering process. The obtained QFF images are shown in Fig. 5.

QFF images.

As depicted in Fig. 5, the images were converted to grayscale before being sharpened. The sharpened images were then processed to extract the edges and textures through quantum super-positioning. Then, each pixel was assigned a crack probability based on quantum probability amplitude. Subsequently, Grover’s search sin2 based boosting was performed to boost the crack pixels. Finally, through quantum-inspired adaptive filtering, the QFF images were obtained. While the edge-enhanced images in Fig. 5 display binary-like outputs with strong edges, they largely lack texture, with the edge maps appearing fragmented. Similarly, the texture-enhanced images preserve surface variations better but lack sharp structural boundaries. By comparison, the quantum edge-amplified, quantum texture-amplified, and quantum fused images exhibit finer structural features and edge details than the corresponding edge-enhanced or texture-enhanced images while also highlighting important boundaries. The quality of the obtained QFF images was measured in terms of peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), structural similarity index (SSI), and edge preservation index (EPI), which are defined in Table 6. Figure 6 illustrates the quality of the QFF images based on these metrics. As seen in Fig. 6, the QFF images significantly outperformed the Sobel-, Otsu-, and Canny-filtered images. The PSNR values highlight the superiority of the QFF images in terms of noise level. Similarly, the SSI values (Figure. 6) show that the QFF images maintained high structural integrity and visual consistency with respect to the original versions. Furthermore, the EPI of the QFF images was the highest, indicating exceptional edge retention, which is critical for accurate crack detection. Overall, the proposed QFF filtering technique contributed the most to enhancing the image analysis process, offering high-quality, structurally accurate, and edge-preserving outputs.

Analysis of GTAU-Net FM and post-process crack measurement

Figure 9 presents the FMs constructed by the encoder and decoder blocks in GTAU-Net. These FMs reveal how the model progressively learned and transformed feature representations across different levels of abstraction, guided by both convolutional operations and self-attention mechanisms. In the first encoder block (Fig. 9(a)), the FMs captured low-level features, such as edges, textures, and local intensity variations. The maps exhibited relatively high spatial resolutions with finer textures, which helped in identifying minute crack boundaries and sharp intensity differences in the image. In the second encoder block (Fig. 9(b)), the resolution became more compressed, and the FMs captured more abstract and localized structural patterns. These FMs emphasize more significant areas of interest that likely align with crack regions, while minimizing the influence of background noise. At this stage, the attention mechanism began enhancing important features and suppressing less relevant ones. In the third encoder block (Fig. 9(c)), the FMs became even more focused and compact. The GTSA module contributed to enhancing contextual awareness, allowing the network to correlate distant spatial regions; this represents a key advantage when detecting long or disconnected crack paths. Regarding the decoder blocks, the first block (Fig. 9(d)) started upsampling the encoded features to recover spatial resolution.

FMs produced by encoder and decoder blocks in GTAU-Net.

The FMs here appeared noisier and rougher, indicating the model’s effort to blend fine details from earlier skip-connections with high-level abstract features. The second decoder block (Fig. 9(e)) produced FMs with clearer and more structured crack patterns, benefiting from the guidance provided by the attention-refined encoder features. Finally, in the third decoder block (Fig. 9(f)), the FMs recovered a large portion of the original spatial resolution, displaying more continuous and coherent crack structures. The skip-connections enhanced by the GTSA module played a crucial role in this reconstruction, allowing for spatial details to be retained effectively. Conclusion and future research scope

This study introduced GTAU-Net, a DL model that preprocesses concrete structure images via QFF filtering and integrates a GTSA module into the TAU-Net architecture to achieve crack detection from images of concrete bridges. The proposed model classifies crack and non-crack patterns in bridge decks, bridge walls, and bridge pavement by delineating only the essential image pixels from crack images. The three key contributions of this research are as follows: The first is the concept of generating QFF images based on the quantum physics principles of super-positioning, quantum probability amplitude, and quantum adaptive filtering. The second is the design of the GTSA module in the GTAU-Net framework, which computes the GSSs for the FMs before significance embedding. The third is the post-processing of the GTAU-Net predictions to measure the crack lengths and identify the crack severity in bridges. Based on the implementation results, the GTAU-Net model exhibited an accuracy of 99.42% in crack classification for concrete bridge components.

Extensive evaluation on the SDNET2018 dataset demonstrates that GTAU-Net achieves a mean accuracy of 99.42% across 5-fold cross-validation, with minimal variance and extremely narrow 95% confidence intervals, indicating highly stable and reliable performance across independent data splits. Fold-wise analysis of precision, recall, and F1-score further confirms the model’s consistency, with values exceeding 99.4% in all performance metrics. This demonstrates the superior performance of GTAU-Net is not due to random variation. These results collectively establish GTAU-Net as a robust, generalizable, and reliable model for crack detection, effectively mitigating overfitting and highlighting its potential for practical deployment in infrastructure monitoring. While GTAU-Net demonstrates strong performance on SDNET2018, further evaluation on other datasets and in real-world deployment scenarios would help validate generalizability across diverse structural conditions. To enhance the performance of the model, the existing convolution layer in the encoder block can be replaced with a switchable atrous convolution layer. The cross-attention mechanism can also be integrated into the TSA and GSA modules. To simplify the attention mechanism, a swim transformer can be integrated with the GTAU-Net model for enhanced prediction accuracy.
