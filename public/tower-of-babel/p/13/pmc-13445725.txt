Correlates of diet quality among sedentary white-collar workers in Türkiye: associations with physical activity and lifestyle-related factors
Authors: Büşra Meltem Ecertaş, Nihal Zekiye Erdem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sedentary work environments may contribute to unhealthy dietary behaviors and increase the risk of non-communicable diseases among white-collar employees.This cross-sectional study examined the diet quality and its associations with physical activity while exploratorily examining health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among 208 white-collar workers in Istanbul.Data were collected using an online survey including self-reported anthropometric measures, a three-day dietary record analyzed with the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015), the SF-36 questionnaire, and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ).Mean daily energy, protein, and fiber intakes were below recommended levels, and 65% of participants had poor diet quality (HEI < 51). Higher HEI-2015 scores were correlated with greater fiber, vitamin C, potassium, and iron intake. In multiple regression analysis, older age (β = 0.19, p = 0.007) and high physical activity level (β = 0.17, p = 0.015) were significantly associated with higher HEI-2015 scores, however, the model explained a limited proportion of variance (Adjusted R² = 0.041).Age and physical activity were modestly associated with diet quality, highlighting the potential relevance of workplace health promotion strategies in sedentary occupational settings.

Introduction

Globally, non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including obesity, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers, remain a major public health challenge and an increasing occupational health concern. Sedentary work patterns, prolonged sitting, and limited opportunities for movement have been recognized as workplace-related exposures that may contribute to metabolic risk, unhealthy dietary behaviors, and reduced well-being [1, 2].

Nutrition is an important component of workers’ health, productivity, and occupational well-being. As work environments have shifted from physically demanding to predominantly desk-based settings, attention has increasingly turned toward lifestyle-related chronic conditions and their behavioral risk factors among employees [3, 4].

White-collar employees, whose work is predominantly cognitive and desk-based, are particularly vulnerable to musculoskeletal disorders, obesity, and other lifestyle-related chronic conditions due to prolonged sedentary behavior and unhealthy dietary habits [1–5]. Workplace interventions promoting healthy eating and physical activity have demonstrated positive effects on health outcomes and lifestyle behaviors among office workers [6–9].

Diet quality is commonly assessed using standardized indices such as the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015), a validated measure of adherence to dietary recommendations. Lower HEI scores have been linked to higher mortality from chronic conditions and poorer perceived health status. Accordingly, diet quality may be associated with health outcomes but may also reflect the broader occupational and environmental context in which dietary choices are made [10, 11].

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL), reflecting physical, mental, and social well-being, may also be influenced by lifestyle behaviors and workplace exposures. Sedentary work environments have been associated with reduced HRQoL, although evidence linking HRQoL directly with diet quality in office-based populations remains inconsistent. Nevertheless, evaluating HRQoL alongside diet quality may provide broader contextual insight into lifestyle-related health behaviors in sedentary occupational settings [12, 13].

Despite growing evidence on diet quality, limited research has specifically examined diet quality among sedentary white-collar workers, particularly in middle-income countries such as Türkiye. This population may be at increased risk due to prolonged sedentary behavior, workplace-related constraints, and lifestyle factors that may influence dietary habits [14].

Therefore, this study aims primarily to evaluate diet quality and its associations with physical activity and health-related quality of life in a relatively understudied occupational group.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted among 208 white-collar workers employed at a private company operating in the energy sector in Istanbul, Türkiye, between August 1 and October 1, 2019. The total number of employees in the company was approximately 400. All eligible employees were invited to participate via a company-wide e-mail, and participation was voluntary. A total of 208 employees completed the study, corresponding to a response rate of approximately 52%.

The minimum sample size was estimated using G*Power for multiple linear regression analysis based on a medium effect size, 95% power (1–β = 0.95), and α = 0.05.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Istanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (No: 10840098-604.01.01-E.15023, 03.05.2019).

Participants were classified as white-collar workers if they were employed in a workplace setting where their duties primarily consisted of desk-based activities such as computer-related tasks, attending meetings, giving presentations, reading documents, and making phone calls [15]. Inclusion criteria were: (i) age 18–65 years, (ii) full-time employment in a sedentary desk-based position, and (iii) voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria were: (i) pregnancy, (ii) chronic diseases requiring medical nutrition therapy, (iii) incomplete dietary records.

All participants who completed the study met the inclusion criteria, and no participant reported conditions meeting the predefined exclusion criteria.

Data collection

Data were collected through a structured data collection form administered to all participants. This form included questions on socio-demographic characteristics, occupational and lifestyle factors, and general health-related information. In addition to the researcher-prepared form, standardized and previously validated instruments were used for outcome assessment. Dietary intake was assessed using a 3-day food record, and diet quality was evaluated according to the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015) [16]. Health-related quality of life was evaluated using the Short Form-36 (SF-36) [17] while physical activity level was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [18]. The researcher-prepared form was developed solely for descriptive data collection in accordance with the aims of the study and did not constitute a novel psychometric questionnaire.

Anthropometric measurements

Height and weight were self‑reported by participants and used to calculate body mass index (BMI, kg/m²). Classification of BMI followed World Health Organization (WHO) standards [19]. Although self‑reported anthropometric measures are widely used in epidemiological studies, they may subject to reporting bias.

Dietary assessment

Participants completed a three-day dietary record covering two weekdays and one weekend day. Participants were instructed, prior to completing the dietary intake records, to report foods using their explicit names and detailed contents, and to indicate portion sizes using common household measures such as teaspoons, tablespoons, and cups, or directly in grams when known. When necessary, dietary records were reviewed and clarified with participants to reduce recording errors. Nutrient intakes were analyzed using the BEBIS software program [20] and compared with the Turkish Dietary Guidelines (TÜBER)-2022 recommendations [21]. Special dietary practices such as vegetarian/vegan diets or intentional dieting behaviors were not specifically assessed.

Healthy Eating Index 2015 (HEI-2015)

Diet quality was assessed using the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015), a validated index developed to measure adherence to the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. The HEI-2015 includes 13 components reflecting adequacy (e.g., fruits, vegetables, whole grains) and moderation (e.g., sodium, added sugars, saturated fats), with a total score ranging from 0 to 100, where higher scores indicate better diet quality. Component scores were calculated using a density-based approach (per 1000 kcal) according to standard scoring procedures, and summed to obtain the total HEI-2015 score. Based on total score, diet quality was categorized as poor (≤ 50), needs improvement (51–80), or good (> 80) [16].

Although HEI-2015 was originally developed for the U.S. population, it has been widely applied in diverse populations, including studies conducted in Türkiye. Its components are broadly consistent with general healthy eating principles also reflected in the Turkish Dietary Guidelines (TÜBER) [21].

Health-related quality of life

Health-related quality of life was assessed using the Short Form-36 (SF-36), which evaluates eight subdomains: physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health. Scores for each subscale range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better perceived health status [17]. The Turkish validity and reliability of the SF-36 have been previously established [22].

Physical activity

Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Participants reported walking, moderate, and vigorous physical activities performed for at least 10 min over the previous seven days. Total physical activity was calculated as MET-minutes/week using standard IPAQ scoring procedures (walking: 3.3 METs; moderate: 4.0 METs; vigorous: 8.0 METs). Participants were categorized as having low (< 600), moderate (600–3000), or high (> 3000) physical activity levels [18]. The Turkish validity and reliability of the IPAQ have been established previously [23].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using histograms, Q–Q plots, and skewness/kurtosis values. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. For continuous variables, independent-samples t-tests or one-way ANOVA were used when normality assumptions were met, whereas Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for non-normally distributed variables [24, 25].

Exploratory partial correlation analyses controlling for age and sex were conducted to examine associations between nutrient intake, BMI, HEI-2015, and SF-36 subdomains. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed with HEI-2015 score as the dependent variable and age, BMI, and physical activity level as independent variables. Variables included in the regression model (age, BMI, and physical activity) were selected based on prior literature demonstrating their association with diet quality. Additional variables such as sex and socioeconomic indicators were considered; however, to maintain model parsimony given the sample size, the final model was restricted to key variables. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied [24, 25].

Regression assumptions were evaluated using the Durbin–Watson statistic, variance inflation factor (VIF), tolerance values, residual plots, and Cook’s distance. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 [24, 25].

Results

A total of 208 white-collar workers were included in the study, of whom 138 (66.3%) were female and 70 (33.7%) were male. Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. Male participants had significantly higher mean weight, height, and BMI values than female participants (all p < 0.05).

Diet quality findings are presented in Table 2. The mean HEI-2015 score of the total sample was 44.9 ± 13.1, indicating generally poor diet quality. Overall, 64.9% of participants were classified as having poor diet quality and 35.1% as needing improvement, whereas no participant achieved a “good diet” classification (HEI ≥ 80). Mean HEI-2015 scores did not differ significantly by sex, although female participants had numerically higher scores than male participants. However, HEI classification did not significantly differ across BMI categories (p > 0.05).

Regarding physical activity, 54.3% of participants were classified as having low, 37.0% moderate, and 8.7% high activity levels. Physical activity level did not differ significantly by sex (p > 0.05). In bivariate analyses, physical activity level was not significantly associated with BMI, HEI-2015 classification, or SF-36 scores (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study examined diet quality and its associations with physical activity and HRQoL among sedentary white-collar workers. The main findings were that overall diet quality was poor, most participants did not meet recommended energy and nutrient intakes, and higher HEI-2015 scores were more consistently associated with older age and higher physical activity than with BMI or HRQoL. These findings suggest that diet quality in sedentary occupational settings may be more closely related to behavioral patterns than to anthropometric or perceived well-being measures alone.

In the present study, mean daily intakes of energy, protein, and fiber were below dietary reference values, and the overall HEI-2015 score indicated poor diet quality. These findings suggest that many participants were not meeting basic nutritional recommendations despite being employed in a structured workplace setting. Given that a substantial proportion of adults’ daily food intake occurs during working hours, workplace environments may play an important role in shaping dietary behavior [26]. Previous studies have similarly shown that office workers often skip or inadequately consume meals and may face barriers to accessing healthier food options during the workday. Additional factors such as time constraints, work-related stress, and cultural dietary habits may also influence dietary behaviors among office workers [27–30].

Although HEI-2015 was developed based on the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, it has been widely applied in diverse populations, and its use in this study should be interpreted with consideration of cultural dietary differences [16]. In line with previous studies conducted in Turkish populations, relatively moderate HEI scores have been reported. For instance, in the study by Uyar and Yücecan [31], the mean HEI scores were 60.9 ± 9.7 for females and 61.6 ± 10.9 for males, while another study reported mean HEI scores of 57.1 ± 6.7 and 54.0 ± 6.3, respectively. Such findings suggest that sedentary work patterns may be associated with inadequate intake of essential nutrients and lower diet quality, consistent with the low HEI-2015 scores observed in the present study.

Higher HEI-2015 scores were positively associated with fiber, vitamin C, potassium, and iron intake, indicating that participants with better diet quality also consumed more nutrient-dense foods. This pattern is consistent with healthier dietary profiles characterized by higher consumption of fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains. These findings may indirectly support the applicability of HEI-2015 in this sample and suggest that even within a generally low-scoring population, better diet quality reflects more favorable nutrient intake patterns.

BMI was not significantly associated with HEI-2015 scores in the present study. Although higher diet quality is often linked to lower BMI and reduced obesity risk, this relationship is not consistently observed in cross-sectional research [32–34]. Short-term dietary assessment may not fully capture long-term eating patterns or cumulative lifestyle factors that influence body weight. In addition, the relatively homogeneous occupational setting of the sample may have limited variability in some explanatory factors related to body weight and diet quality.

Regarding physical activity, most participants in this study reported low to moderate activity levels, reflecting the sedentary nature of white-collar occupations. Previous studies have shown that sedentary work patterns are associated with reduced physical activity and increased metabolic risk among office workers. In the current study, individuals with high physical activity levels demonstrated significantly higher HEI-2015 scores than those with low activity levels. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that healthy lifestyle behaviors tend to cluster, with physically active individuals often demonstrating healthier dietary patterns [12].

No significant association was observed between HEI-2015 and overall HRQoL in this study. This may reflect the multidimensional nature of HRQoL, which is influenced by psychosocial, occupational, and environmental factors beyond dietary behavior alone. Previous studies have also reported inconsistent findings regarding the relationship between diet quality and HRQoL [35, 36]. In the present study, HRQoL was included as an exploratory contextual variable rather than a primary explanatory outcome, and the findings should be interpreted accordingly.

The regression analysis identified age and physical activity as significantly associated with diet quality. Older participants showed higher HEI-2015 scores, which may reflect increased health awareness, dietary knowledge, and health-related behavioral changes that develop with age. Similarly, higher levels of physical activity were associated with better diet quality, suggesting that individuals who engage in regular physical activity may also adopt healthier dietary habits. Karavelioğlu et al. [37], also reported a positive association between physical activity and HEI-2015 scores among white-collar employees. Tsuzaki et al. [38], found that higher-quality diets are associated with lower BMI and reduced risk of overweight and obesity, with this association becoming more pronounced with age.

However, the regression model explained only a small proportion of the variance in HEI-2015 scores (Adjusted R² = 0.041). The relatively low explanatory power of the regression model suggests that diet quality is influenced by multiple unmeasured factors, including socioeconomic, environmental, and behavioral variables that were not captured in the present study [26–30]. Future studies should therefore incorporate these additional factors to better explain variations in diet quality.

Taken together, these findings suggest that sedentary office environments may provide a relevant context for workplace health promotion efforts targeting diet quality and lifestyle behaviors. Even in relatively young and employed populations, poor diet quality may coexist with low physical activity and suboptimal nutrient intake. Workplace-based strategies that improve access to healthier food choices and encourage regular movement throughout the workday may therefore offer practical opportunities to support healthier dietary patterns among white-collar employees.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationships between diet quality, physical activity, and health-related quality of life. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as statistical relationships rather than evidence of causal mechanisms. Additionally, the use of a 3-day dietary record may not fully reflect habitual dietary intake and may be influenced by short-term variability. Potential underreporting or misreporting of dietary intake may also have affected estimated energy and nutrient intake values.

Second, dietary intake, physical activity, and anthropometric measures were obtained through self-report, which may have introduced recall and social desirability bias. Third, the sample consisted of relatively homogeneous white-collar workers from a single workplace, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Fourth, although the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015) has been widely used in international research, it was originally developed based on the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, and cultural differences in dietary patterns should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fifth, the relatively low explanatory power of the regression model suggests that additional unmeasured factors may have influenced diet quality. In addition, the large number of correlation analyses may have increased the risk of type I error.

Reliance on self-reported screening information may have limited the identification of all exclusion criteria.

Despite these limitations, this study provides relevant insights to the limited literature by examining diet quality, physical activity, and health-related quality of life within the same framework in sedentary white-collar workers. The use of standardized instruments such as IPAQ and SF-36 strengthens methodological consistency and the sample size provided sufficient statistical power. Future research should employ longitudinal and experimental designs and incorporate broader factors, such as workplace food environment and socioeconomic factors, to better understand diet quality in occupational settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, diet quality was generally poor among sedentary white-collar workers in this study, and higher HEI-2015 scores were more consistently associated with older age and higher physical activity levels. These findings suggest that workplace settings may help inform future workplace-based health promotion strategies aimed at improving dietary behavior among sedentary office employees, particularly through strategies aimed at supporting healthier food choices and regular physical activity.
