Performance evaluation of large language models on Korean medical licensing examination: a three-year comparative analysis
Authors: Hyun Jin Kim, Kiwook Jung, Sunghwan Shin, Wookeun Lee, Jun Hyung Lee, Hee Sue Park, Qute Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Performance evaluation of large language models (LLMs) in non-English medical contexts remains limited, particularly for medical licensing examinations including both text- and image-based questions. Therefore, the performance and reliability of three LLMs—GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro was evaluated using the Korean Medical Licensing Examination (KMLE) questions from 2022 to 2024. We analyzed 942 KMLE questions encompassing text-only and image-based formats across various medical specialties. Reproducibility was evaluated through repeated testing, and inter-model agreement was analyzed using pairwise comparisons. GPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy (83.2%), followed by Claude 3.5 Sonnet (79.5%) and Gemini 1.5 Pro (76.6%). While GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet outperformed text-only questions, Gemini 1.5 Pro consistently performed across both question formats. LLMs demonstrated the strongest performance in internal medicine, pediatrics, and psychiatry, with relatively weak results in medical law. Reproducibility was outstanding, with Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and GPT-4o showing 99.9%, 99.5%, and 97.7%, respectively. Strong inter-model agreement was observed, particularly between GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet. LLMs demonstrate competent performance in medical knowledge assessments, even in non-English contexts, although challenges persist in processing image-based questions and specialized domains. This study provides valuable insights that may inform the future development and application of LLMs in medical education and assessment, although further validation in real-world educational settings is necessary to establish their practical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20066-x.

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI), a subfield of computer science that focuses on developing systems that replicate human cognitive abilities, has experienced significant advancements in recent years, driving progress across numerous domains1. In particular, AI has demonstrated considerable potential within the medical field, particularly in areas such as diagnosis and treatment2–4.

Recently, AI, particularly large language models (LLMs), have been increasingly applied in the healthcare sector. Trained on vast amounts of textual data to understand complex linguistic patterns and contextual nuances, LLMs hold considerable promise for applications such as diagnostic support, medical education, and patient consultation5. Advances in model architecture, the expansion of training datasets, and improvements in computational power have further strengthened the applicability of LLMs in healthcare6. Notably, state-of-the-art models such as GPT-4, GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Claude 3 Opus have recently demonstrated promising capabilities in responding to complex medical inquiries and analyzing intricate clinical cases by refining their understanding through medical documents and clinical data7.

However, for these models to be practically applicable to clinical diagnosis and medical education, their accuracy must be rigorously validated. Prior research suggests that AI-generated responses must consistently exceed 95% accuracy to be deemed reliable in medical contexts, underscoring the need for further development in this area7,8. Several evaluations of LLMs using medical licensing examinations have been conducted across different countries to assess their accuracy. For instance, in the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE), GPT-4 scores ranged between 85.7% and 92.5%9. Moreover, recent studies evaluating the Japanese Medical Licensing Examination reported that GPT-4o achieved an accuracy rate of approximately 90%, thereby highlighting its potential effectiveness, even in non-English-speaking countries7. These findings reflect the ongoing progress of LLMs toward greater accuracy and reliability, bringing them closer to widespread adoption in diagnostic support, medical education, and patient interaction.

However, a recent study revealed performance disparities across languages10, with GPT-4o achieving over 90% accuracy on the English-based USMLE but showing a decrease in performance (84.9%) on the Chinese National Medical Licensing Examination (NMLE). This discrepancy seems to stem from the approach in which LLMs process multiple languages. Recent studies have provided insights into how language models handle multilingualism11,12. The multilingual capabilities of LLMs are analogous to subordinate multilingualism, implying that the model first translates non-English questions into English, processes the information internally in English, and then translates the answers back into the original language. This framework suggests that semantic discrepancies during translation contribute to reduced performance in non-English languages13. Additionally, language confusion can arise during this process14. Consequently, evaluations of LLMs must be conducted within a specific linguistic context of each target language. For Korean users, several benchmarks exist for evaluating LLMs15, with the use of the Korean Medical Licensing Examination (KMLE) being one of the most notable methods for assessing medical domain knowledge16.

In this study, the KMLE, a standardized national exam required to obtain a medical license in Korea, was evaluated to determine its suitability for assessing LLM capabilities. The KMLE comprehensively evaluates foundational medical knowledge and clinical skills across various clinical domains, including medical ethics, providing a robust measure of professional competence17. Owing to its standardized difficulty and question format, it is a suitable dataset for evaluating LLMs in the context of the Korean medical standards. Previous evaluations of LLM performance on Korean healthcare professional licensing examinations have been conducted. A study assessing GPT-4, GPT-3.5, and open-source models such as LLAMA2 on Korean medical exams found that GPT-4 achieved the highest accuracy at 83.06%, although pretraining on English medical contexts did not confer significant advantages for Korean medical datasets16. Another study found that GPT-3.5 scored 69.0% accuracy for English-based questions styled after the Korean dermatology specialist exam, surpassing the passing criterion. In contrast, its performance dropped to 57.0% for Korean-language questions, revealing substantial disparities between the two languages18. These studies highlight the current performance of LLMs on Korean medical exams and suggest the need for further development to enhance their understanding of the Korean language and specific medical contexts. However, these evaluations have been limited to text-only questions and have not incorporated image-based questions, thus restricting assessments to non-multimodal data.

This study aims to conduct a comparative analysis of the latest LLM models—GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro—using both text-only and image-based KMLE questions to evaluate whether these models can achieve high accuracy in answering Korean-language medical questions. Through this research, we aimed to determine the feasibility of applying LLMs in the Korean healthcare sector and identify the limitations of current LLMs that must be addressed to enhance their practical utility in medical applications.

Results

Accuracy analysis of three LLMs on KMLE questions

The accuracy of three LLMs—GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro—was evaluated on KMLE questions from 2022, 2023, and 2024. The dataset included 317 questions from 2022, 314 questions from 2023, and 311 questions from 2024, totaling 942 questions. In terms of accuracy, GPT-4o achieved scores of 77.9%, 85.0%, and 86.8% for 2022, 2023, and 2024, respectively, with an average of 83.2%. Claude 3.5 Sonnet scored 74.4%, 82.2%, and 82.0%, with an average of 79.5%, while Gemini 1.5 Pro scored 71.3%, 80.6%, and 78.1%, with an average of 76.6% (Table 1). GPT-4o demonstrated the highest performance across all years, although the statistical significance varied by year (Supplementary Table 1). In 2022, GPT-4o (77.9%) significantly outperformed Gemini 1.5 Pro (71.3%, P = 0.015); however, the difference with Claude 3.5 Sonnet (74.4%, P > 0.05) was not statistically significant. For 2023, GPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy (85.0%), followed by Claude 3.5 Sonnet (82.2%) and Gemini 1.5 Pro (80.6%). However, these differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). In 2024, GPT-4o (86.8%) significantly outperformed Gemini 1.5 Pro (78.1%, P < 0.001), even though no statistically significant difference was observed compared to Claude 3.5 Sonnet (82.0%, P = 0.025) after Bonferroni correction. When comparing the accuracy of each model to the average performance of the KMLE examinees, all three models demonstrated competitive performance, with GPT-4o consistently achieving the highest scores. All models exceeded the passing threshold of 60% across all years. Moreover, in the overall analysis combining all three years, GPT-4o outperformed both Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Gemini 1.5 Pro, achieving statistically significant accuracy rates (P = 0.003 and P < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 1).

Analysis of specialty-specific performance patterns is shown in Fig. 2C, and detailed accuracy rates provided in Table 1. In cross-model comparisons, GPT-4o demonstrated particularly strong performance in internal medicine (86.1%), significantly outperforming both Claude 3.5 Sonnet (79.4%, P = 0.001) and Gemini 1.5 Pro (76.4%, P < 0.001). In psychiatry, GPT-4o (89.1%, P = 0.013) significantly outperformed Gemini 1.5 Pro (74.5%), while Claude 3.5 Sonnet (89.1%, P = 0.027) did not show a significant difference. Performance differences were observed for other specialties, such as surgery, pediatrics, obstetrics and gynecology, others, and medical law, but they were not significant (all P > 0.05). The within-model analysis revealed varying performance patterns for each model compared to their overall accuracy. GPT-4o demonstrated higher performance in pediatrics (91.1%), psychiatry (89.1%), others (87.2%), and internal medicine (86.1%) while showing relatively low accuracy in surgery (75.8%) and obstetrics and gynecology (70.5%). Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieved notable results in psychiatry (89.1%), others (85.1%), and pediatrics (83.7%). Gemini 1.5 Pro showed higher accuracy in pediatrics (84.6%), surgery (80.2%), and others (78.7%). All models showed relatively lower performance in medical law (GPT-4o: 70.0%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet: 76.7%, Gemini 1.5 Pro: 65.0%) than in other specialties.

To evaluate potential performance variations between deployment methods, we compared the accuracy of web-based and API-based GPT-4o implementations (Supplementary Table 2). Statistical analysis revealed comparable performance between the two versions, with the web-based model achieving an overall accuracy of 83.5% (787/942) compared to the API-based model accuracy of 79.8%, 84.1%, and 86.8% for 2022, 2023, and 2024, respectively. McNemar tests showed no significant differences in accuracy between the two implementations across all years and in overall performance. The response consistency rate between the two implementations was 87.5% (824/942), indicating a substantial agreement in their decision-making patterns.

Reproducibility analysis of model responses

To evaluate the reproducibility of the LLM models, their ability to provide consistent answers when solving the same questions three times was assessed. The evaluation excluded 32 R-type questions from 2022 and included 910 A-type questions over three years (285, 314, and 311 questions from 2022, 2023, and 2024, respectively) (Table 2). Reproducibility, defined as the proportion of questions with identical answers across all three responses, was highest for Claude 3.5 Sonnet at 99.9%, followed by Gemini 1.5 Pro at 99.5%, while GPT-4o also maintained high reproducibility at 97.7%. In terms of response consistency, GPT-4o showed partial agreement (two identical answers out of three) in 20 cases (2.2%) and only one case of complete disagreement (three different answers). This case was a text-only question from the hematology subspecialty regarding the appropriate treatment for aplastic anemia. Claude 3.5 Sonnet demonstrated exceptional consistency, with only one case of partial agreement and no instances of complete disagreement across all 910 questions. Similarly, Gemini 1.5 Pro showed high consistency, with only five cases of partial agreement (0.5%) and no instances of complete disagreement. The degree of agreement across three responses, measured by Fleiss’ ĸ statistic, demonstrated exceptionally high reliability across all models. Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieved the highest agreement with a ĸ value of 0.9991, followed closely by Gemini 1.5 Pro at 0.9954 and GPT-4o at 0.9803, all indicating almost perfect agreement in their responses. All three models demonstrated particularly higher consistency in image-based questions than text-only questions, with both Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Gemini 1.5 Pro achieving perfect consistency (100%) and GPT-4o showing 98.1% consistency.

Reproducibility/Consistency.

Analysis of response concordance among the three LLM models was conducted exclusively on A-type questions, excluding R-type questions from the dataset (Fig. 3). The complete inter-model agreement rates exhibited temporal variations across the three-year period. These rates were observed at 64.0% (203/317) in 2022, 75.5% (237/314) in 2023, and 73.0% (227/311) in 2024. Pairwise comparisons revealed that GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet had the highest agreement rates, maintained between 74.1% and 85.2% across all years. When comparing model pairs involving Gemini 1.5 Pro, the agreement rates were also relatively high, with Claude 3.5 Sonnet showing agreement rates between 68.8% and 80.6% and GPT-4o showing agreement rates between 73.2% and 83.4%. Notably, when all three models reached consensus, instances in which their collective answers were incorrect were present. The rates of incorrect consensus showed a decreasing trend: 10.8% in 2022 (22/203), 6.3% in 2023 (15/237), and 4.8% in 2024 (11/227), indicating an improvement in the reliability of model agreement over the years.

Resource consumption

The computational resource usage for solving questions, including token counts and average task time for each model, is summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Claude 3.5 Sonnet used the highest average number of input tokens at 934, followed by GPT-4o with 818 tokens and Gemini 1.5 Pro with 631 tokens. For output tokens, Claude 3.5 Sonnet used 362 tokens, closely followed by Gemini 1.5 Pro at 357 tokens, while GPT-4o used the fewest with 137 tokens. The average processing time required to solve one year set of questions was the longest for Claude 3.5 Sonnet at 73.9 min. In comparison, GPT-4o completed the tasks in less than one hour, demonstrating faster processing speed than the other models.

Discussion

This study evaluated the medical performance of three LLM models on Korean-language KMLE questions. The analysis revealed that GPT-4o achieved the highest overall accuracy (83.2%), followed by Claude 3.5 Sonnet (79.5%) and Gemini 1.5 Pro (76.6%). GPT-4o consistently demonstrated higher accuracy across all years. In terms of question type, all three models showed a higher accuracy on text-only questions (86.1%, 83.9%, and 76.7%, respectively) than on image-based questions (80.6%, 75.6%, and 76.6%, respectively). Analysis by medical specialties revealed stronger performance in internal medicine, pediatrics, and psychiatry for all models. Regarding reproducibility, Claude 3.5 Sonnet demonstrated exceptional consistency (99.9%), followed by GPT-4o (97.7%), while Gemini 1.5 Pro showed considerably lower reproducibility (73.8%). Inter-model agreement analysis revealed high concordance between the LLMs, with GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet showing the highest pairwise agreement rates (74.1–85.2%), followed by relatively high agreements (73.2–83.4%) when paired with Gemini 1.5 Pro.

Previous studies have evaluated LLM performance in various medical licensing examinations. Kung et al. demonstrated that ChatGPT performed at or near the passing threshold of the USMLE, achieving between 61.5% and 75.0% accuracy across different exam formats without specialized training1. Similarly, Lai et al. evaluated the performance of ChatGPT-4 on the United Kingdom Medical Licensing Assessment (UKMLA), showing an average score of 76.3% across three attempts, with notably poorer performance in specialties such as clinical hematology (28.6%) and gastrointestinal medicine (60.0%)19. In comparison to these studies, our evaluation of the latest LLMs on the KMLE demonstrated a substantially higher performance, with GPT-4o achieving 83.2% accuracy and even the lowest-performing model, Gemini 1.5 Pro, reaching 76.6%, suggesting significant improvements in the medical knowledge capabilities of recent LLM models.

A notable pattern observed in the results was the relatively lower performance across all models in 2022 compared to subsequent years. This trend closely mirrors the performance of medical students who took the same examinations. According to an analysis by the Korea Health Personnel Licensing Examination Institute20–22, the proportion of difficult questions—defined as those with a correct response rate below 80%—was significantly higher in 2022 (40%) than in 2023 and 2024 (34.7% each). This difference in exam difficulty provides important context for interpreting the variation in model performance across years.

When analyzed by question type, both GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet demonstrated higher accuracy on text-only questions compared to image-based questions (86.1% vs. 80.6% for GPT-4o, 83.9% vs. 75.6% for Claude 3.5 Sonnet). This pattern aligns with previous studies on medical licensing examinations. Liu et al.7 reported that all tested LLMs showed approximately a 10% accuracy gap between text-only and image-based questions on the Japanese Medical Licensing Examination, with GPT-4o achieving 92.2% for text-only questions but dropping to 80.4% for image-based questions. Similarly, Nakao et al.23 found that the performance of GPT-4 V decreased when images were included, showing 72% accuracy for text-only questions compared to 68% with images, although this difference was not statistically significant. The decreased performance with image inclusion can be attributed to several factors. First, the limited availability of medical images for training, primarily owing to privacy concerns, may have resulted in insufficient exposure to medical image data during the training phase of the model. Second, incorrect image interpretation might interfere with the decision-making process of the model. Even in cases where text-only information could lead to correct answers, misinterpretation of images could potentially mislead the final judgment of the model. Finally, architectural limitations might exist in current LLMs, as they are primarily optimized for text processing with image handling capabilities added later, potentially resulting in suboptimal performance when processing text and images simultaneously. Interestingly, Gemini 1.5 Pro showed uniquely consistent performance across both question types (76.7% vs. 76.6%), contrasting with the performance patterns of the other two models. This distinct pattern can be attributed to two key technical factors. First, Gemini 1.5 Pro’s built-in content-filtering system blocked the processing of 21 questions containing potentially sensitive medical images, which may have artificially narrowed the performance gap between image-based and text-only questions. Second, differences in image processing methodology could have played a crucial role. While GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet process images through Base64 encoding within the message context, Gemini 1.5 Pro utilizes a different approach, loading images through Python Imaging Library (PIL) before model processing. This fundamental difference in image-handling architecture might contribute to the distinctive performance pattern observed with Gemini 1.5 Pro.

Analysis of specialty-specific performance revealed interesting patterns across LLMs. While all models demonstrated competence across various specialties, notable differences were observed in their strengths. GPT-4o excelled in internal medicine, significantly outperforming the other models (86.1% vs. 79.4% and 76.4%, P < 0.001). This superior performance may reflect the extensive medical literature available in this field, which likely facilitated the training of the GPT-4o model. In psychiatry, both GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieved equally strong performance (89.1%), significantly outperforming Gemini 1.5 Pro (74.5%, P < 0.05). This suggests that GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet may possess more sophisticated processing capabilities for psychiatric concepts.

The performance of the models in medical law is particularly noteworthy. Despite achieving passing scores, all models showed relatively lower performance in this specialty(GPT-4o: 70.0%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet: 76.7%, Gemini 1.5 Pro: 65.0%) than in in other specialties. This trend likely reflects the limited availability of Korean medical law data in the training datasets of these models. While general medical knowledge is extensively documented in international literature, Korean medical regulations and healthcare laws are primarily documented in Korean-language sources, which may be underrepresented in the training data of these predominantly English-trained LLMs. This highlights a limitation of the current models and suggests a potential area for improvement through the increased inclusion of region-specific medical legal content in future model training.

Analysis of reproducibility demonstrated high consistency across all models, with Claude 3.5 Sonnet exhibiting the highest reproducibility (99.9%), followed by Gemini 1.5 Pro (99.5%) and GPT-4o (97.7%). Although small differences in reproducibility were observed among the models, all three demonstrated near-perfect agreement, indicating robust response consistency. These reproducibility rates are notably higher than those reported by Krishna et al.24, who observed lower agreement rates in their evaluation of GPT-3.5 (66.0%–82.7%) and GPT-4 (82.0%–87.3%) using the chat interface. The enhanced reproducibility observed in this study can be attributed to the use of an API implementation with a zero-temperature setting, which minimizes stochastic variations compared to higher temperature parameters commonly used in chat interfaces. The consistency of LLM outputs can be modulated through hyperparameters, particularly the temperature parameter, which influences the probability distribution of the token selection via the softmax function25. Lowering the temperature parameter enhances the deterministic nature of the model outputs, as a zero-temperature setting implements a greedy decoding strategy to select the most probable token deterministically. However, even under a zero-temperature setting, LLMs retain some inherent nondeterministic characteristics. This residual variability can be attributed to floating-point precision limits and nonassociative arithmetic operations in hardware implementations—factors that align with the minimal variability observed in our results26,27. The slightly higher variability of GPT-4o compared to the other models may stem from hardware-specific implementation factors. Schlögl et al.27 demonstrated that platform-specific optimizations and variations in aggregation order can introduce subtle but measurable inconsistencies in neural network inferences. Given the substantially larger computational demand of GPT-4o, it is reasonable to hypothesize that our experimental queries were more frequently distributed across multiple servers than the others. These findings have important implications for the implementation of LLMs in medical education and clinical settings, where consistent and reliable outputs are crucial. They also underscored the need to carefully consider both the implementation method and parameter settings when deploying LLMs in practice.

A distinguishing finding of this study is that the responses of GPT-4o varied depending on whether it was operated via an API or through a web-based chat interface, even when provided with the same query. While the accuracy remained nearly identical, the consistency of the responses was lower, with an agreement rate of 87.5% between the API and web-based responses. This was notably more variable than the reproducibility rate observed when evaluating API-based responses three times. The primary reason for this discrepancy appears to be the difference in the temperature settings. For our experiments, we used a zero-temperature setting for API-based tests, while the web-based interface did not allow temperature adjustment and used default settings (approximately 0.7–1.0). This higher temperature in the web-based interface likely resulted in more diverse responses compared to the consistent outputs from the API with a zero-temperature setting, leading to the observed lower agreement rate between the two methods. Interestingly, our findings align with those of a recent study by Renze and Guven28, which demonstrated that changes in temperature from 0.0 to 1.0 do not have a statistically significant impact on problem-solving performance across various LLMs. This is consistent with our observation that, despite the different temperature settings between the API and web-based interfaces, the accuracy remained nearly identical between the two methods.

The competent performance of LLMs on the KMLE suggests their significant potential as supplementary tools in medical education, moving beyond simple knowledge assessment. For instance, they could function as personalized tutors adapting to student weaknesses, interactive tools for KMLE self-preparation, or even as platforms for clinical reasoning simulations. However, before such applications can be deployed, rigorous validation is essential to address the limitations identified in this study. This includes bridging the accuracy gap from the observed 83.2% for the best-performing model to clinically reliable levels, improving performance in specialized domains like medical law where all models performed relatively poorly, and enhancing the models’ interpretation of image-based questions where accuracy was notably lower for two of the models. In addition, consistency should be ensured by using API-based controlled environments and 0-temperature settings to reduce the risk of hallucinations. Strict security measures are required when processing patient data to protect privacy. Therefore, future development should prioritize domain-specific fine-tuning, validation by medical experts, and the establishment of clear user guidelines to ensure these models are used as effective and safe educational aids.

Conclusion

This study analyzed the accuracy and reproducibility of three recent LLM models using the KMLE questions from 2022 to 2024. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate LLMs on KMLE by incorporating image-based questions. Importantly, the study evaluated Korean, a low-resource language in LLM training, and the models demonstrated strong performance. The results demonstrated that GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieved high levels of accuracy and reproducibility, highlighting their potential as supportive tools for medical education. Our study stands out for several reasons: it utilized a large dataset that included image-based questions, analyzed accuracy by question type (image-based vs. text-only) and department category classification, and uniquely assessed reproducibility by repeating responses of each model three times. In conclusion, this study implemented a multifaceted approach to evaluate the medical performance of LLMs and that of previous studies. Consequently, the findings offer important preliminary insights that could support the future optimization and application of LLMs in academic settings. However, real-world implementation, particularly in educational and assessment environments, requires further empirical validation and careful consideration of contextual factors.

Methods

Study design

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the performance of three state-of-the-art LLMs on the KMLE questions from 2022 to 2024. The conceptual flow of the study design is illustrated in Fig. 4. The primary objective was to assess the capabilities of these models to understand and accurately respond to medical questions presented in Korean, thereby evaluating their proficiency in processing non-English medical content. To ensure a robust assessment, the primary testing method involved automated API-based testing, supplemented by manual validation through chat interfaces to verify consistency and explore any differences between the two methods.

Evaluation dataset

The evaluation dataset comprised KMLE questions from three consecutive years (2022, 2023, and 2024), with 320 questions per year. The KMLE is a national licensing examination for which approximately 3,000 medical school graduates or graduating students (4th-year students who have completed their academic curriculum) apply each year. The exam is conducted over two days, with a total of 320 questions to be solved in 420 min (four sessions, each with 80 questions in 105 min). Given that the pass rate for the KMLE between 2022 and 2024 was high (94.2–95.7%) and that examinees obtain a medical license upon passing, the average performance of the examinees can be considered reflective of the average competency of first-year doctors in Korea. The 2022 dataset included both standard multiple-choice questions (A-type) and multiple-response questions (R-type), while the 2023 and 2024 datasets consisted exclusively of A-type questions. The examination consisted of two types of items: A type and R type. A type (single-best-answer format) required the examinee to choose one correct option out of five. R type (extended-matching format) required selection of a specific number of correct options from a larger list. R type questions comprised 32 items in the 2022 examination and were discontinued starting in 2023. Questions containing multimedia elements, such as videos or audio, were excluded. The questions were preserved in their original Korean format to maintain the integrity of the medical terminology, clinical scenarios, and diagnostic criteria specific to Korean medical practice. The KMLE questions were sourced from publicly available materials released by the Korea Health Personnel Licensing Examination Institute (https://www.kuksiwon.or.kr), which only provided text-based content.

Since the Korea Health Personnel Licensing Examination Institute does not release official images, two licensed physicians independently reviewed the questions and answer choices to identify the clinical context and sourced appropriate images from open-access medical education resources and publicly available datasets. All selected images were reviewed for clinical relevance and ethical compliance, ensuring they were free of identifiable patient information and permitted for non-commercial academic use.

To further enrich the analysis, the questions were classified into seven medical specialties: Internal Medicine, Surgery (general surgery, thoracic surgery, and neurosurgery), Obstetrics and Gynecology, Pediatrics, Psychiatry, Medical Law, and other specialties (including Ophthalmology, Dermatology, Emergency Medicine, Neurology, Otolaryngology, Urology, and Preventive Medicine). Two board-certified specialists in laboratory medicine, each with over 10 years of experience as medical doctors independently classified each question, and in cases of discrepancies, a third medical doctor reviewed the questions to determine the final category. The initial inter-annotator agreement between the two specialists was very high, with a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.938. This classification process ensured that the performance of the model could be accurately assessed across distinct areas of medical expertise.

Model configuration

Three advanced language models were evaluated: GPT-4o (version: GPT-4o-2024-08-06), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (version: Claude-3-5-sonnet-20240620), and Gemini 1.5 Pro (version: Gemini-1.5-pro). For consistency and reproducibility of results, all API interactions were configured with standardized parameters. A comparative overview of the technical specifications for the three models, including their multimodal architecture, image processing capability, context window size, and content filtering policies, is presented in Supplementary Table 411,12,27. The maximum token length was set to 1,000 to allow for comprehensive responses while maintaining computational efficiency. The temperature parameter was set to zero to eliminate randomness in the outputs and ensure reproducibility. All other parameters were maintained at their default values throughout the testing period to maintain the methodological consistency. Given that LLMs retain previous content, a new session was initiated for each problem to prevent inter-problem influence.

Prompt engineering

Prompt engineering was rigorously executed to optimize model performance while ensuring the integrity of the evaluation process. For API-based testing, structured prompts were developed to instruct the models to analyze the question content and select the most appropriate answers from the provided choices. The R-type questions from 2022 required multiple correct answers. Consequently, a two-stage prompting strategy was employed. First, the model was prompted to determine the number of correct answers, followed by prompting to select specific answers. The exact prompts used for each LLM model, including the format for both single-answer and multiple-answer (R-type) questions, are provided in Supplementary Table 5 for transparency and reproducibility5,7. To validate the API performance, three independent medical doctors conducted parallel testing using the GPT-4o chat interface. They employed a zero-shot prompting approach by entering the question and answer choices directly into the chat, recording the model’s responses, and comparing them against the API outputs. This ensured a thorough performance comparison between the two methods. In cases where the models produced incomplete responses or incorrect numbers of answers, standardized clarification prompts were used to ensure response completeness and consistency across all test cases.

Performance assessment

The performance of each model was evaluated by comparing its responses to the official KMLE answers. Several metrics, including accuracy, reproducibility, and inter-model agreement, were employed to provide a comprehensive assessment of model performance. Accuracy was evaluated through an overall comparison of the three models, a comparison between image-based and text-only questions, and a category-based comparison across different medical specialties. Additionally, for GPT-4o, a comparison was made between API-based testing and web-based chat interface testing to assess the interface-dependent performance differences. Each model solved each question three times to assess reproducibility. If all three answers were identical, the result was classified as ‘Complete agreement.’ If two answers matched and one differed, it was classified as ‘Partial agreement.’ If all answers differed, it was classified as ‘Complete disagreement.’ The inter-model agreement was analyzed by comparing the correctness of the answers provided by the three models. R-type questions were excluded from reproducibility and inter-model agreement analyses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to calculate accuracy, reproducibility, and inter-model agreement. Cochran’s Q test with Bonferroni correction, followed by McNemar’s test for post-hoc analyses, was performed to evaluate accuracy differences between LLMs. The chi-square test was used to assess accuracy variations between text-only and image-based questions. In the reproducibility and inter-model agreement analysis, the agreement among the three answers was also evaluated using Fleiss’ κ statistic. The criteria for interpreting κ statistics were as follows: κ values less than 0.2 indicate poor agreement, 0.21 to 0.40 fair agreement, 0.41 to 0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61 to 0.80 indicate substantial agreement, and 0.81 to 1.0 indicate nearly perfect agreement. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1).
