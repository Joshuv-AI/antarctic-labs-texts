Variation of leaf photosynthetic traits is mostly driven by changes in source-sink relationship rather than irradiance in pear tree canopies
Authors: Ningyi Zhang, Ziyi Yang, Elias Kaiser, Kaijie Qi, Chao Tang, Peng Wang, Oluwafemi James Caleb, Alemayehu Ambaw Tsige, Shutian Tao, Shaoling Zhang, Juyou Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the variation of leaf photosynthetic traits and their regulation is essential for accurately predicting canopy photosynthesis and gross primary production of fruit trees and for modelling carbon balance in agroforestry systems. However, this is underexplored in fruit trees in which management practices strongly impact plant morphology and sink: source ratios. We conducted a field experiment in pear with three levels of fruit loads (0%, 10%, and 100%) on two cultivars (Cuiyu and Hosui) with a Y-trellis trained architecture. Irradiance, leaf photosynthesis, and several leaf physiological traits were measured at different canopy heights and developmental stages. Both cultivars exhibited a very shallow vertical gradient of leaf photosynthetic capacity (Amax), despite a significant irradiance gradient. Considering the canopy Amax profile only had a marginal impact on modelling canopy photosynthesis in Y-trellis trained pear trees. In both cultivars, Amax was affected by source-sink variations, albeit in opposite directions: as fruit load increased, Amax increased in Hosui but decreased in Cuiyu. Increases of Amax in Hosui were related to lower carbon accumulation in the leaf and higher stomatal conductance, whereas decreases of Amax in Cuiyu correlated with reductions in leaf nitrogen. We conclude that under the conditions of this study and in Y-trellis trained pear trees, leaf photosynthetic variation was more strongly associated with internal factors than with irradiance gradients, and that the regulation of Amax is cultivar dependent. Introduction

The orchard system accounts for an important part of agricultural plant production and comprises a substantial proportion of the global agroforestry system. Photosynthesis (A) is the major process that governs water and carbon (C) exchange between fruit trees and their environments, and accurately predicting A is thus key to modelling orchard gross primary production and C balance of agroforestry ecosystems. Photosynthetic traits are highly variable and can be affected by many external and internal factors including environmental conditions and genetic characteristics [1, 2]. The variation of photosynthetic traits in fruit tree crops, however, is much less well-studied compared to that in the field and greenhouse crops.

The irradiance (I) profile typically declines vertically within canopies due to self-shading, providing the major driving force for variation of photosynthetic traits within canopies. The I gradient from the top to the bottom can be > 50-fold in dense canopies and 10- to 20-fold in relatively open canopies [3, 4]. This heterogeneous distribution of I causes local acclimation of leaf physiological traits at different canopy heights, typically causing a vertical distribution pattern of leaf photosynthetic capacity (Amax) in the canopy [5]. Light acclimation of Amax in plant canopies can be subdivided further into three types of acclimation: structural, biochemical, and diffusional acclimation. First, leaves developed under high I are characterized by having a higher leaf mass per area (LMA) with thicker mesophyll cell layers than leaves developed under low I, which normally corresponds with a higher Amax given the thicker photosynthetic tissue in the leaf [6, 7]. Second, in fast-growing herbaceous canopies, new leaves are continuously developed, and fast leaf turnover leads to an increase of leaf age from canopy top to bottom. The I gradient and leaf age gradient together lead to a vertical distribution of leaf nitrogen (N) content in the canopy, due to leaf physiological acclimation to high I and N translocation from old to new leaves. Leaf N is strongly positively corelated with leaf A, given that the proteins of the Calvin cycle (especially Rubisco) and thylakoids represent the majority of leaf N [8]. Thus, the vertical leaf N distribution further drives the vertical gradient of Amax in the canopy [9]. Third, leaves with large Amax tend to have high stomatal conductance (gs) and high transpiration rate, which means a high water demand at the top of the canopy. Thus, in some tall tree canopies, hydraulic conductance may also constrain Amax distribution, as the top leaves may need to reduce gs to prevent water loss [10, 11]. Variation of Amax and its underlying traits has mostly been studied in canopies of field and horticultural crops, as well as forest trees [12], while fruit tree crops have been mostly overlooked.

Apart from external factors such as I, many internal factors may influence the variation of photosynthetic traits in fruit tree canopies. Many fruit tree management practices – among which fruit thinning is the most often applied strategy – change the source-sink balance of carbohydrates in the tree, which can affect leaf A. On the one hand, fruit thinning decreases sink demand for C, resulting in carbohydrate accumulation in the leaf that potentially causes feedback inhibition on A [13–15]. On the other hand, a high fruit load may compete for N with leaves, thereby decreasing Amax [16, 17]. However, the effect of source-sink variations on leaf A mostly refers to C balance, while the role of N limitation has been overlooked. Furthermore, the internal regulation of leaf photosynthetic traits by source-sink variation may be affected by plant developmental stages and genotypes. For example in tomato, plant growth was found to be sink-limited at early developmental stages and source-limited at later developmental stage, with different genotypes showing different levels of limitations [18]; in barley, sink limitation on A is less pronounced in perennial genotypes than that in annual genotypes [19]. Understanding the source-sink regulation of A and its control by genotypic variations and development stages in fruit trees is essential for modelling leaf photosynthetic traits in orchard systems.

Canopy A models of fruit trees typically make one of three assumptions: they assume that (1) Amax is identical across the canopy, (2) there are two leaf types (sunlit versus shaded) with two values for Amax, or (3) Amax is directly proportional to I [20–23]. In grapevine, a detailed Amax profile was simulated using linear correlations between leaf N and leaf photosynthetic parameters, thereby including the effects of I and leaf age on leaf N [24]. Nevertheless, there is a dearth of experimental evidence showing to what extent leaf photosynthetic traits vary in fruit tree canopies, to justify the assumptions in modelling canopy A. Despite the fact that the source-sink regulation of A has been explored in numerous fruit tree crops such as apple, peach, and orange, its interactions with plant developmental stages and genotypes, as well as canopy I variations, have received much less attention [13, 14, 25].

The objective of this study was to clarify the variation of leaf photosynthetic traits and its regulation by external (canopy I distribution) and internal (source-sink variation, developmental stage, and genotype) factors in pear – an important fruit tree crop – to provide essential knowledge for making reasonable assumptions in modeling orchard canopy A. To this end, we conducted a field experiment with three levels of fruit thinning treatments on two pear cultivars with different maturation dates. Canopy I distribution was quantified, and A and several traits underlying it were measured at different canopy heights during three developmental stages.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

The field experiment was conducted between March and August 2023 in the campus orchard of Nanjing Agricultural University located at Nanjing, China (32°N, 118°E). Two pear cultivars – Pyrus pyrifolia Nakai cv. Cuiyu (maturation date: mid-July) and Pyrus pyrifolia Nakai cv. Hosui (maturation date: end of August) – trained to Y-trellis were used (height: ~ 2.3 m; crown diameter: ~ 2.4 m; Fig. 1). The pear seedlings were obtained from the nursery of the university and were grown in the campus orchard for six years. Each cultivar was grown in one row with a row distance of 4.5 m and an intra-row plant distance of 2.5 m. During the experimental period, only natural precipitation was used as irrigation. Fertilization was applied before reaching the shoot sprouting stage (28 February), flowering stage (25 March), and fruit expansion stage (15 June), with the first stage only using nitrogen fertilizer (urea) and the following two stages using compound fertilizer (N:P:K = 1:1:1). Acaricides, pesticides, and germicides were sprayed once per month. In the campus orchard, vetch (Vicia sativa) was grown between rows to improve soil fertility, and was harvested and returned to the soil until May (Fig. 1). Therefore, weeding was not necessary before May, and from June to September, weeding was applied three times per month using weeding machines.

Representative images of trees used in the experiment. Y-trellis-trained pear trees in parallel (A) and in perpendicular to the row direction (B: cv. Cuiyu; C: cv. Hosui). The legume vetch (Vicia sativa, white arrow in A) was grown to improve soil fertility

Three fruit thinning treatments were applied: no thinning (100% fruit load), standard thinning often used in commercial pear orchards (10% fruit load), and full thinning (0% fruit load). Fruit thinning was performed when fruits were small (diameter: ~ 1 cm) and was finished within two days (21–22 April 2023). For the treatment with 10% fruit load, fruit thinning was done by first counting all fruits of each fruit cluster and then retaining 10% of the fruits; these were normally at the center of the cluster.

Measurements

Photosynthesis light response curves

Gas exchange measurements were conducted on leaves attached on the tree at three developmental stages – i.e. young fruit, fruit expansion, and maturation (dates in Table S1) – using a portable gas exchange system (LI-6800, Li-Cor Bioscience, Lincoln, NE, USA) equipped with a 2 cm2 leaf chamber fluorometer. Light response curves were measured on a fully mature leaf – which was fully expanded, sun-exposed, and physiologically mature, without visible damage or senescence – at the top, middle, and bottom levels of the canopy (1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 m height from the ground, respectively; Figure S1). All measurements were performed at a relative humidity of 60%, ambient CO2 concentration of 400 ppm, and a flow rate of air through the system of 500 µmol s−1. For measurements at the young fruit stage, air temperature was kept at 25 °C, and for measurements at fruit expansion and maturation stages, air temperature was kept at 28 °C. Comparisons of the gas exchange measurements were made between fruit thinning treatments within the same developmental stage. I was provided by a mixture of red (90%) and blue (10%) LEDs in the fluorometer. Before measurement, leaves were light acclimated at 1800 µmol m−2 s−1 for at least 10 min to allow for both A and gs to stabilize. Then, light response curves were measured by decreasing I in the following steps: 1800, 1500, 1200, 1000, 800, 600, 400, 200, 150, 100, 75, 50, 25, and 0 µmol m−2 s−1, with each step taking 2–3 min. Infra-red gas analyzers were matched at each step.

Nitrogen, carbon, and carbohydrates

Leaves used for gas exchange measurements were sampled. Photos of freshly sampled leaves were analyzed by ImageJ (National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, United States) to obtain leaf area. Then, leaves were dried in the oven at 80 °C for at least 48 h to measure leaf dry weight. Dry samples were ground and kept for the following measurements.

Leaf C and N

Leaf total C and N contents were measured using a N/C analyzer (multi EA S 5000, Analytik Jena, Germany). First, standard curves for N and C were made using phenylalanine as standard substrate. Then, 14–20 mg per leaf sample were measured, and leaf total N and C were determined.

Leaf soluble sugar and starch

Dry leaf material (0.1 g) was put in deionized water to extract soluble sugars by the ultrasound method, at 45 °C [26]. Leaf soluble sugar contents including fructose, glucose, sucrose, and sorbitol were measured using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (Acquity H-class, Waters, United States). For leaf starch measurement, dry leaf material (0.05 g) was dissolved in 80% ethanol. Substances were collected, extracted in concentrated hydrochloric acid, and stained using anthrone sulfuric acid solution. Starch content was measured using a microplate reader (SPARK, TECAN, Switzerland) [26].

Canopy light distribution and leaf area index

Canopy I distribution was measured at all three developmental stages. Cloudy days were chosen to minimize the effects of natural fluctuations in sunlight intensity. Measurements were conducted on three trees per treatment. I was measured using a line quantum sensor (ACCUPAR LP-80, METER Group, Inc, United States) at the same three heights where leaf gas exchange measurements were conducted, and at each height, five horizontal positions were chosen for measurement (Figure S1).

Leaf area index (LAI) was measured following I distribution measurements. First, at least 10 individual leaves respectively from the top, middle, and bottom of the tree canopy were collected to measure leaf area and to calculate average leaf area per canopy layer. Then, the number of leaves was counted above the top, middle, and bottom level of the canopy where I measurements were taken (Figure S1). LAI above different heights of the canopy was then calculated from the above information.

Statistical analysis

The experiment adopted a completely randomized design, and five trees were used per treatment and cultivar (Figure S2). Trees were considered the experimental units (n = 5 per fruit-load treatment per cultivar). For each tree, one leaf per canopy height (top, middle, or bottom) was measured; canopy height was treated as a within-tree factor. First, we tested for interactions between the effects of canopy height and fruit load on each parameter, using two-way ANOVA. Given that no significant interaction was detected (Table S2), data were pooled for the remaining analyses. Normality and homogeneity of variances were tested using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s test, respectively. To account for non-independence of leaves nested within trees, data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means with Tukey adjustment (P < 0.05). Correlation matrices were made based on the results of Pearson correlation analysis (P < 0.05). Statistical analyses were conducted using R (http://www.r-project.org/).

Results

Irradiance variation in pear tree canopies and its influence on leaf and canopy photosynthesis

I exhibited a significant variation from the top to the bottom of the tree canopy in both cultivars. Cuiyu had a slightly higher kL (0.24; Fig. 2 A) than Hosui (0.18; Fig. 2 B). The vertical gradient of leaf N in the canopy was much shallower than that of I. kN of the two cultivars were very close (kN of Cuiyu: 0.02; kN of Hosui: 0.03; Fig. 2 C, D). Leaf photosynthetic parameters were hardly affected by canopy height: Amax was relatively evenly distributed at different canopy heights, regardless of whether this was shown as cumulative LAI, I, or absolute height (Fig. 2 E–H; S3 A-F). There was a significant linear correlation (P = 0.01) between Amax and I/I0 in Hosui for the 100% fruit load treatment, but not in any other case (Figure S4). Similarly to Amax, ΦCO2 and Rd did generally not vary with height, except that Rd in Cuiyu showed a significant correlation with cumulative LAI (Figures S3 G-R; S5). The relationship between gs,1800 and I was nonsignificant in either cultivar, and neither was the relationship between LMA and I (Fig. 2 I-L), suggesting that neither gs,1800 nor LMA varied with height in the canopy.

Effects of source-sink variation and developmental stage on leaf photosynthetic and physiological parameters

Source-sink variation, as induced by different fruit loads, had a more pronounced impact on leaf photosynthetic parameters compared to I. Amax of Cuiyu was generally higher than in Hosui across fruit loads (Fig. 4 A-F). Despite the fact that air temperatures during gas exchange measurements differed between developmental stages – i.e. 25 °C during the young fruit stage and 28 °C during fruit expansion and maturation stages – comparisons of photosynthetic parameters were made between different fruit loads within the same developmental stage where leaf temperatures were not significantly differed (Figure S6). At the young fruit stage, fruit loads did not seem to affect Amax (Fig. 4 A, D). With further development, Amax generally declined and, intriguingly, the two cultivars tended to show opposite Amax responses to fruit loads (Fig. 4 C, F): in Cuiyu, Amax decreased as fruit load increased, whereas in Hosui, Amax increased with fruit load, especially at the fruit maturation stage (Fig. 4 C, F). Both ΦCO2 (range: 0.05–0.06) and Rd (range: 0.9–2.7) were relatively stable under different fruit loads in both cultivars (Figure S7). In Cuiyu, ΦCO2 and Rd were also stable across developmental stages, while in Hosui, ΦCO2 decreased significantly and Rd tended to increase at fruit maturation compared to the young fruit stage (Figure S7). Responses of gs to fruit loads and developmental stages were cultivar-dependent: In Cuiyu, gs hardly responded to fruit loads and was rather stable across developmental stages, whereas in Hosui, gs increased with fruit load and strongly decreased at fruit maturation (Fig. 4 G-L). Both cultivars exhibited a reduction in WUE with increasing fruit loads (Fig. 4 M-R). In Cuiyu, WUE further decreased with fruit development, whereas in Hosui, WUE was less affected by developmental stages (Fig. 4 M-R).

Correlations between leaf photosynthetic parameters and related physiological traits

The correlations between leaf photosynthetic and related physiological parameters were different in the two cultivars (Figure S10). In Cuiyu, Amax showed a strong positive linear relationship with leaf N, but hardly correlated with leaf C (Fig. 6 A, C). In contrast in Hosui, Amax strongly and negatively correlated with leaf C, but hardly correlated with leaf N (Fig. 6 B, D). Interestingly, Amax of Hosui was strongly positively correlated with gs, whereas in Cuiyu, Amax hardly correlated with gs (Fig. 6 E, F). In Cuiyu, the ratio between intercellular and ambient CO2 (Ci/Ca) decreased as Amax increased, and Ci/Ca decreased with lowering the fruit loads (Figs. 6 G; S11), suggesting that stomatal limitation might play a role in regulating Amax changes caused by source-sink variation, whereas this was not the case in Hosui, where Ci/Ca did not correlate with Amax (Fig. 6 G). Generally, Amax showed stronger correlations with different types of sugars in Hosui than in Cuiyu, but their trends were different: in Cuiyu, Amax significantly and positively correlated with contents of fructose, glucose, sorbitol, and total soluble sugar (Fig. 7 A, C, G, I); in Hosui, Amax significantly and positively correlated with contents of glucose and sucrose (Fig. 7 D, F), whereas it significantly and negatively correlated with contents of sorbitol, total soluble sugar, and starch (Fig. 7 H, J, L).

Discussion

The variation of leaf photosynthetic traits in plant canopies has mostly been studied in field crops and forest trees, where Amax distribution in the canopy often correlates with a steep gradient in I. Here, we showed that in pear tree canopies with artificial architectural manipulations – a commonly applied Y-trellis architecture in pear orchards (Fig. 1) – Amax exhibited a negligible distribution profile, and its correlations with I were mostly nonsignificant (Fig. 8). Instead, variation of Amax was related to internal factors, such as source-sink variation induced by fruit thinning as well as the developmental stage, and the direction of such responses differed between the two cultivars (Fig. 8).

A concept map illustrating the relative contributions of environmental (irradiance) versus internal (source–sink balance and developmental stage) factors of the maximum leaf photosynthetic rate (Amax ) variations in Y-trellis pear canopies

Variations of leaf photosynthetic traits in pear canopies were hardly affected by the irradiance profile

Understanding the mechanisms of canopy Amax distribution has long been an important objective for optimizing plant resource allocation for designing high-yielding cultivars, as well as for accurately modelling Acanopy. Generally, the gradient of I is a major determinant of the canopy Amax profile, driving distribution of leaf N and structural traits [7, 12]. In some cases, variations of leaf age, far-red intensity, and hydraulic conductance at different canopy heights were also shown to influence the Amax gradient [10, 32]. However, very few studies have examined the Amax distribution in fruit tree canopies (e.g. [13] in apple trees), and none has been done in pear which is an important fruit crop worldwide. Here, we showed that in pear, Amax did not differ much throughout the canopy (Figs. 2 E–H; S3 A-F; S4). This is in stark contrast to observations from e.g. Populus, Tilia, Corylus, walnut, and wheat [33–35], all of which showed strong Amax gradients. Additionally, kN was rather low for both cultivars (0.02 for Cuiyu and 0.03 for Hosui; Fig. 2 C, D), indicating a very shallow gradient of leaf N in the canopy. Such a low kN was found previously in some rice genotypes (0.01–0.02) [36], but most crops – e.g. soybean, sunflower, and wheat – normally exhibit a larger kN [9].

In orchard production, fruit tree architecture is often manipulated heavily. Special fruit tree architectures – e.g. Slender Spindle Axe, Y-trellis, Vertical Axis, or Solaxe – are designed to improve light distribution in the canopy, improve mechanical management operation, and create tree architectures that are easier to manage for manual labor [37, 38]. Obviously, such manipulations of architecture affect the I gradient, resulting in smaller kL compared to dense crop canopies. The two pear cultivars showed kL of only 0.18–0.24 (Fig. 2 A, B). These kL values correspond with lower values recorded in apple orchards, but were considerably smaller than most values found in field crops – e.g. rice (0.45–0.48) and wheat (0.30–1.31) – and forest trees – e.g. Fagus crenata (0.30–0.70) and Betula platyphylla (0.51–0.71, [9, 39]). Hence, using artificial canopy architectural manipulation such as Y-trellis seems to largely reduce kL. We found that about 30% of incident I penetrated through the canopy (Fig. 2 A, B). A recent meta-analysis suggested that the relationship between Amax and I usually levels off at 30–50% of above-canopy I [12], possibly explaining the nonsignificant vertical distribution patterns of Amax in pear tree canopies seen here.

Fruit tree growth models normally assume an identical Amax for leaves at different canopy heights when modelling Acanopy [22, 40]. Although this assumption tends to overestimate Acanopy in many crops [41, 42], our results suggest that using the same Amax might be sufficiently accurate for modeling Acanopy in fruit trees with artificially manipulated architecture. Simulations of Acanopy in Cuiyu showed that using an identical value for Amax throughout the canopy generally resulted in similar Acanopy compared to Acanopy calculated by considering a vertical Amax profile (Fig. 3A), suggesting that in canopies with shallow I gradients, assuming uniform Amax may be both sufficiently accurate and computationally simpler, particularly for cultivars with very small kA values. In contrast, assuming uniform Amax in Hosui led to an overestimation of Acanopy by of up to 20% at high I, due to the relatively large kA value (0.46) used for Hosui (Figs. 2 H; 3B). However, Amax of Hosui did not vary significantly between different heights in the canopy, and the significant linear relationship was mostly caused by the datapoints from the 100% fruit load (Figures S3 D-F; S4 F). Therefore, whether the Amax distribution profile should be considered when simulating Acanopy of Hosui is still debatable. Potentially, in some cultivars and in some extreme conditions (such as 100% fruit load), Amax distribution should be considered for accurately modelling Acanopy in pear trees. In such cases, the source–sink balance also modified Amax by more than 20%, and therefore represents a key parameter for improving photosynthesis prediction models. Moreover, it is worth noting that these findings are specific to the cultivars, architectural system, and environmental conditions in this study and should be validated in other fruit tree species and orchard systems before broader generalization. Thus, further comparisons with measurements and simulations under different conditions are needed to explore the assumptions that balance the simplicity and accuracy for modelling Acanopy in fruit trees.

Differences in leaf photosynthetic traits were mostly associated with source-sink variations and developmental stages

In contrast to the mild effect of an I gradient on leaf photosynthetic traits in pear tree canopies, our results suggested much stronger impacts of internal factors related to source-sink variation and developmental stage. Amax significantly decreased with tree development and was affected by source-sink variation in both cultivars (Fig. 4 A-F). Leaf temperature gradually increased with developmental stage (Figure S6), by a total 3 °C. However, this is unlikely to have affected the outcomes of this study, as, comparisons among fruit-load treatments were always conducted within the same stage. Moreover, the magnitude of Amax variation across developmental stages was much larger than would be expected from this small temperature difference alone, suggesting that other developmental regulations might play a role.

The different source-sink relationships, on the other hand, may have driven changes in leaf N balance (e.g. Figure 5 H, I), given the increasing sink demand for leaf N, which potentially stimulated N translocation from leaves to sink organs (e.g. fruits). The observed Amax variations were associated with the change of leaf N balance (Fig. 6 C, D), since leaf N generally determines the amount of photosynthetic proteins. Despite the many studies on source-sink regulation of photosynthesis in leaves [43–45], very few have examined the impact of source-sink imbalance on leaf photosynthetic traits at the canopy level. One example was in apple canopies, where the effect of I on Amax was nonsignificant when all fruits were removed from the tree, suggesting that the increase in sink limitation not only decreased leaf-level A but also diminished the distribution of Amax in the canopy [13]. We found a similar trend in Hosui, where the relationship between Amax and I became significant when fruit load increased to 100% (Figure S4 F). Nevertheless, in most cases, a clear vertical distribution pattern of Amax was not shown in both pear cultivars, nor a significant effect of fruit loads on the Amax profile (Figs. 2 E–H; S4). The pear trees in our study exhibited much higher leaf N (3–6 g m−2; Fig. 5 G-L) compared to apple trees (1–3 g m−2) in Ngao et al. [13]. Thus, the N translocation between leaves at different canopy heights might not be a limiting factor that imposes Amax differences in pear trees.

The fact that Amax responded differently to source-sink variations between cultivars (Fig. 4 A-F) suggests potential genotypic variations in sink limitation of Amax in pear trees. The response shown by Hosui corresponded with many other crops (Fig. 4 F), in which leaf A increases with a higher sink demand and decreases due to sink limitation [13, 14, 43, 45]. In Hosui, Amax strongly correlated with leaf carbohydrate concentrations, and significantly negative correlations were found between Amax versus contents of leaf C, sorbitol, total soluble sugar, and starch (Figs. 6 B; 7 H, J, L), suggesting that the accumulation of carbohydrates in the leaf was associated with the feedback inhibition on Amax in Hosui. Additionally, we found a positive correlation between Amax and gs in Hosui (Fig. 6 F). Further breakdown of this correlation showed that the Amax versus gs relationships across fruit loads were only significant during fruit maturation stage, while the Amax versus gs relationships across developmental stages were significant under all fruit loads (Figures S12 B, D, F; S13 B, D, F). This suggests that the change of gs was mostly associated with the decrease of Amax with plant development, and only at the late developmental stage – which was also when the significant effect of fruit load on Amax was found (Fig. 4 F) – gs might affect Amax responses to source-sink variations. Previously, similar coupled responses of leaf A and gs under source-sink variations have been found in many other fruit trees such as apple, peach, mango, and coffee [25, 43, 46–48]. A common explanation may be that the low sink demand decreases gs, further inducing the downregulation of A due to increased non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), owing to the fact that a lower gs reduced leaf transpiration rate and thus increased leaf temperature, causing photochemical impairments [25, 46, 47]. Occasionally, gs was found to independently regulate leaf A under source-sink variations, without increasing NPQ, and the feedback inhibition of A by C accumulation was not involved either [43]. In our case, the regulation of Amax by source-sink variations at the late developmental stage in Hosui might be a combined result of C-related feedback inhibition and changes in gs. Furthermore, the change of Amax with developmental stage might be related to the change of gs, regardless of source-sink conditions.

Interestingly, the strong correlation between Amax and gs was not present in Cuiyu, in which Amax decreased with increasing sink demand (Figs. 4 C; 6 E). Similar results have been found in navel orange: two cultivars exhibited contrasting leaf photosynthetic responses to increasing sink demand [14, 17]. Changes of Amax in Cuiyu were strongly correlated with changes in leaf N (Fig. 6 C), suggesting that N balance might be the major reason explaining changes of Amax under source-sink variations. Albeit against to the general impression – i.e. Amax increases with increasing sink demand, as has been found in Hosui – few previous studies (in orange and almond) also found decreases of leaf A by high sink demand, mostly related to reductions of leaf N [16, 17]. The decrease of Amax by high sink demand in Cuiyu seems like a passive response, resulting from the N competition between leaves and fruits. We conclude that the contrasting photosynthetic responses to source-sink variations in the two pear cultivars indicate substantial genotypic variation: in Hosui, increased fruit load was associated with reduced carbohydrate accumulation in leaves and higher gs, which was consistent with alleviation of sink limitation and enhanced photosynthetic capacity; in contrast, in Cuiyu, increasing fruit load led to a decline in leaf N content, suggesting potential N competition between fruits and leaves, which in turn reduced Amax.

Conclusions

The variation of leaf photosynthetic traits in pear canopies was mostly associated with internal rather than external factors – i.e. irradiance. I gradient had a relatively mild effect on Amax distribution, likely due to lots of I penetrating through the entire canopy in Y-trellis trained pear tree canopy. Instead, the source-sink relationship, developmental stage, and genotypic variation had relatively stronger impacts on leaf photosynthetic variations. In both cultivars, the effect of source-sink variation on Amax was more pronounced at the late developmental stage, but had opposite directions: as fruit load increased, Amax in Hosui increased whereas Amax in Cuiyu decreased. The Amax response in Hosui was related to the accumulation of carbohydrates in the leaf and changes in gs, whereas in Cuiyu, this response was related to reductions in leaf N. We conclude that genetic variation exists in the response of leaf photosynthetic traits to changes in the source-sink relationship, and considering the effect of source-sink balance might be more relevant than considering the I variation for accurately modeling Acanopy in pear trees in which artificial architectural management is used.
