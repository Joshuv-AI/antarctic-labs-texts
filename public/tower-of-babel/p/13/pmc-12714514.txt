Telemetry is a valuable tool for close clinical monitoring in real time. However, when used in nonindicated settings, it contributes to alarm fatigue, increased healthcare costs, and reduced patient satisfaction, without improving outcomes.1, 2, 3 Prior studies have shown that up to one-third of monitored days lack clinical indication.1 Even when appropriate on admission, telemetry is often not discontinued when the indication has resolved.1

Project Rationale

Overuse and inappropriate continuation of telemetry are pertinent problems across many hospital systems. We hypothesized that a quality-improvement initiative focused on reducing unnecessary telemetry usage would improve telemetry-ordering practices. The goals of this project included educating admitting hospitalists and internal medicine residents about American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines for telemetry in order to reduce unnecessary usage upon admission and at 48 hours.

Project Description

We designed a quality-improvement study using a pre-post analysis to measure the effect of education on appropriate telemetry ordering. Telemetry orders upon admission to a tertiary care center were analyzed by 2 physicians based on the 2017 AHA Best Practice guidelines. The same orders were then re-evaluated at 48 hours to assess whether telemetry remained indicated or was appropriately discontinued.

Intervention included an educational lecture given to the teaching hospitalists and internal medicine residents regarding the AHA Best Practice guidelines. Cardiology staff were not targeted in educational interventions. In addition, physical reminders were posted near telemetry stations in the hospital. After the educational intervention, telemetry orders were analyzed in the same fashion over a 2-week period. Results were analyzed for significance with a chi-square test using a P value of significance set to 0.05. Charts of patients who did not have a telemetry order on admission or had telemetry appropriately discontinued were not re-visited during the study.

Project Deliverables

Deliverables associated with this project include a hospital-wide educational session for internal medicine residents and admitting hospitalists regarding AHA telemetry best-practice guidelines along with quick reference reminders pasted in work areas.

Project Outcome, Impact, and Future Directions

Prior to the educational intervention, 156 admissions were analyzed. Following the intervention, 110 admissions were analyzed. The proportion of appropriate telemetry orders on admission significantly increased from 60.9% to 73.6% (P = 0.03; 95% CI: 1.5%-23.9%) (Figure 1). No significant change was observed in appropriateness at 48 hours with 37.9% and 39% of orders deemed appropriate pre- and post-education, respectively (P = 1.00; 95% CI: 17.0%-19.2%) (Figure 2).

Our educational intervention improved initial ordering practices, reducing nonindicated telemetry orders on admission by 12.7%. However, it did not significantly impact ongoing telemetry reassessment or timely discontinuation. Planned future interventions include electronic-based medical record prompts for re-assessment of telemetry need, automatic discontinuation orders, and default monitoring durations. In addition, repeated educational sessions will be held along with physical reminders in order to continue improved usage. Future work should investigate the frequency of repeated education required to maintain high-level ordering practices.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that a resident-led educational initiative can significantly improve the appropriateness of telemetry orders at the time of admission. This is important given the established safety of discontinuing unnecessary telemetry in nonintensive care settings and the burden that continued use places on both patients and healthcare staff.

Prior to the educational intervention, almost 40% of telemetry orders had no true clinical indication, with this number decreasing to 26% after intervention. Similar to our findings, in a multicenter analysis of nonintensive care patients, Benjamin et al found that telemetry use had no true clinical indication in 35% of telemetry days.1 The incidence of arrhythmia was 3.1 per 100 days of nonindicated monitoring, and the arrhythmias were determined to be clinically insignificant.1 Another study of 562 patients admitted to the telemetry floor divided patients into groups based on whether telemetry was truly indicated. Arrhythmia events were significantly more common in patients with a guideline-based indication for telemetry than in those without (34% vs 11%, P = 0.001).2 Importantly, no patients in the “telemetry not indicated” group experienced a clinically significant arrhythmia, whereas 36% of arrhythmic events in the “telemetry indicated” group were deemed clinically significant (P < 0.05).2 Providers should feel comfortable with the safety data of not ordering telemetry in low-risk patients.

Our educational initiative alone was not able to significantly reduce the burden of unnecessary telemetry orders at 48 hours. This is likely due to the additional effort it takes to discontinue telemetry, along with the perceived low risk and low burden of maintaining telemetry. Beyond an educational initiative, it will likely take further electronic medical record interventions such as automatic discontinuation prompts, default monitoring durations, and periodic reassessment alerts in order to tackle this burden.

The need for such measures becomes clear when considering the broader impact of alarm fatigue and cost of telemetry overuse. Multiple studies, including a statement from the Joint Commission, have deemed that between 85% and 99% of alarms are nonactionable.3,4 On average, each nonindicated day of telemetry monitoring incurs a minimum cost of $53 to the institution, driven by increased nursing time and the cost of materials.1 Supporting this, a study by Chaudhary et al5 examined 695 adult patients admitted to general medical floors with telemetry orders and found that 22.3% of initial telemetry assignments were inappropriate, while 56% of patients remained on telemetry longer than clinically necessary. The authors estimated that inefficient utilization of telemetry in nonintensive care settings resulted in an annualized cost of approximately $500,000.5 These findings underscore the significant financial burden associated with overuse of telemetry monitoring in hospital settings.

Conclusions

Our resident-led educational initiative achieved a meaningful improvement in the appropriateness of initial telemetry orders at admission, increasing adherence to guideline-based criteria by 12.7%. However, the intervention alone did not improve appropriateness at 48 hours, likely reflecting the additional cognitive and logistical effort required to discontinue telemetry, as well as a persistent perception among providers that prolonged monitoring carries minimal risk or burden. In reality, the cumulative evidence shows that unnecessary telemetry contributes to alarm fatigue, workflow interruptions, and substantial financial cost without improving patient safety. This is particularly true in low-risk patients who rarely experience clinically significant arrhythmias.

Future work should examine how frequently educational interventions must be repeated to maintain improved ordering behavior over time, as knowledge gains from a single campaign may diminish without reinforcement. In addition, targeted electronic medical record strategies should be evaluated as complementary tools to address the persistent gap in appropriateness at 48 hours. By combining sustained education with system-level safeguards, institutions can optimize telemetry use, enhance patient care, and meaningfully reduce both staff burden and avoidable expenditures.
