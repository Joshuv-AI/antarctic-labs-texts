Multi-omics exploration of hypoxia in gastric cancer
Authors: Yingjie Zhu, Xiaochang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoxia created an intratumoral oxygen gradient, promoting a more aggressive tumoral phenotype. Nevertheless, there was a lack of hypoxic panorama in gastric cancer (GC).MethodsA total of 715 GC samples covering two independent cohorts were used for non-negative matrix factorization clustering and subtype exploration based on a tailored hypoxic signature. The prognosis, molecular markers, pathways, infiltrating of lymphocytes and stromal cells, and response of PD-1 therapy were compared between the subtypes.ResultsGC patients were divided into two groups, one indicted as normoxia, while the other defined as hypoxia. Hallmarks of aggressive tumor features such as EMT, angiogenesis, and KRAS signaling up were significantly enriched under hypoxic conditions and undoubtedly, worse outcome. Additionally, hypoxia confers GC with higher immune score, CAF infiltration, and resistant to pembrolizumab therapy in patients with metastatic GC (mGC).ConclusionsHypoxia-targeted or CAF-oriented therapy may overcome current chemoradiotherapy-resistant advanced GC but warrants further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04321-7.Keywords: Cancer-associated fibroblasts, Gastric cancer, Hypoxia, Immune score

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common malignant tumor in the world. Although the incidence and mortality have declined globally, GC is still the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. With the advancement of surgical techniques and the implementation of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and neoadjuvant therapy, the five-year survival rate of early GC has exceeded over 90%. Nevertheless, the advanced GC usually presents a poor prognosis, with an average overall survival (OS) often less than one year [2].

Hypoxia, as a typical microenvironmental feature in the majority of solid tumors, is associated with strong aggressiveness, poor prognosis, and drug resistance [3, 4]. The rapid proliferation of tumor generally exceeded the growth of surrounding blood vessels, resulting in a 2–9% drop compared to normal oxygen level, with the oxygen level less than 2%. Under the condition of hypoxic microenvironment, hypoxia inducible factors (HIFs) mediated the transcriptional adaptation of cells to hypoxia and play a central role in the progression of hypoxia-induced malignant tumors [5]. HIFs can stabilize and regulate the expression of a variety of genes, including genes related to cell survival, angiogenesis, glycolysis, invasion and metastasis [6]. Considering the close relationship between hypoxia and tumor progression and metastasis, to better reflect the complex cellular response to hypoxia, hypoxic signature has been established in a variety of solid tumor types such as endometrial cancer, pancreatic cancer, and breast cancer, and lung cancer [7–9]. Nevertheless, the genetic characteristics associated with hypoxia in GC are poorly understood.

GC exhibits unique pathophysiological features, such as its association with Helicobacter pylori infection, specific metabolic reprogramming, and a distinctive stromal composition rich in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) and immune cells [10]. These factors can create a GC-specific hypoxic landscape that may differentially regulate key pathways like angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune evasion compared to other solid tumors [11]. Therefore, the rationale for directly adopting other tumor-derived hypoxia gene sets for molecular subtyping in GC is unsuitable and requires explicit justification.

Herein, we used the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, 415 samples) cohort as a discovery set to identify a custom-curated hypoxia-driven signature to explore the potential clustering of GC samples. Crucially, this signature will be rigorously validated for its prognostic and predictive power in an independent, large-scale retrospective cohort GSE62254 (300 samples). This cross-cohort validation is paramount for ensuring the robustness, generalizability, and clinical relevance of our findings, directly addressing the reproducibility crisis in biomarker development.. We found that GC fell into two subtypes according to our core hypoxia-related signature, with the hypoxic tumors exhibited more invasive phenotype and poor prognosis. Nevertheless, the high immune score of the hypoxic group may make it benefit from immune-checkpoint blockade (ICB) treatment independent of high tumor mutation burden (TMB). By focusing on the potential specificity of hypoxic regulation in GC, this work seeks to provide a more precise tool for risk stratification and to uncover novel microenvironment-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Materials and methods

Somatic mutation collection

The clinical information and somatic mutation profiles for 415 GC patients were downloaded from TCGA data portal (Research Resource Identifier- RRID:SCR_003193, 11/09/2018). Then, any mutations including silent mutations, RNA mutations and introns, flanking sequences, 5' untranslated regions (UTR) and 3' UTR were excluded.

Construction of hyopxia-related gene signature for GC

To establish a reliable hypoxic signature, we collected a well-annotated list of gene expression models that are widely cited for various cancer types, including hypoxic 15 gene of pancancer [12], 26-gene hypoxia signature in laryngeal cancer [13], 24-hypoxia gene signature in high-risk bladder cancer [14], 20 genes in MCF7 cells after hypoxia exposure [15], hypoxia-related 28 gene prognostic signature for prostate cancer [16], hypoxic gene expression classifier for head and neck cancer [17, 18], nine genes from Caco-2 CRC cells responded to hypoxia [19], 27-genes induced by hypoxia, pH was not affected in human squamous cell carcinoma [20], and 200 genes under the hypoxia marker in the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB, RRID:SCR_016863) [21]. Then, hypoxia signatures consisting of a total of deduplicated 356 genes derived from above signatures, including canonical hypoxia-responsive ADM, ANGPTL4, CA9, and VEGFA, were used for subsequent non-negative matrix factorization (NMF, RRID:SCR_024284) clustering [22]. To select the best factorization rank r, a critical parameter in NMF, two to six in ascending order, was successively calculated. Then, the best r value was chosen according to certain quality factors, as described in previous work [23].

Gene expression data processing and normalization

All level 3 mRNA RNASeqV2 datasets of GC were downloaded from TCGA (09/12/2017). We excluded genes that have expression levels < 1 (RSEM-normalized) in more than 50% samples. Raw affymetrix HG U133 Plus 2.0 arrays CEL files of GSE62254 were downloaded from GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, RRID:SCR_005012). Then, MAS5 algorithm was used for calculation of the gene expression levels as described in our previous work [23]. As for PRJEB25780, the raw FASTQ sequencing files of 45 GC patients and corresponding clinical data were downloaded from European Nucleotide Archive (ENA, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena, RRID:SCR_006515). Then, the paired-end reads were mapped to human protein coding genes (Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.84) by using HISAT2 (version 2.2.0, RRID:SCR_015530) [24] and counted with featureCounts [25] (RRID:SCR_012919) with the default parameters. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed by prcomp function in R. Significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was identified by the DEGSeq package (RRID:SCR_008480) for R/Bioconductor [26] and selected according to a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P value < 0.05 and fold change > 2 conditions. All heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package (RRID:SCR_016418) in R (64-bit, version 3.0.2, RRID:SCR_001905).

Immune and stromal cell infiltration

Infiltration of stromal and immune cells in tumor tissues was calculated by estimate package (RRID:SCR_026090) and xCell (RRID:SCR_026446) method in R [27]. In GC, studies have validated the efficacy of xCell in delineating prognostically relevant microenvironmental features. For instance, research has shown that high xCell-based scores for CAFs and endothelial cells are strongly associated with worse overall survival and resistance to chemotherapy in GC cohorts [28–30]. These findings underscore the model’s applicability and biological relevance in this malignancy.

DNA methylation data processing

HumanMethylation450 DNA methylation data of GC was downloaded from TCGA (11/13/2016). Probes with “NA” ratio in > 10% of the GC samples were discarded. Next, significant differentially methylated sites (DMSs) were identified by the limma package (RRID:SCR_010943) [31] and selected according to a FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05 and beta-value (ranging from 0–1) change > 0.2.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To explore whether any signature was associated with hypoxia, GSEA was performed by using the hallmark gene sets obtained from the MSigDB [32]. Significantly enriched hallmarks were selected according to a FDR q-value < 0.05.

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis

GO function and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed by the clusterProfiler package (RRID:SCR_016884) in Bioconductor [33]. Significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways in specific subtypes were choosed according to a FDR-adjusted P value < 0.01.

Survival analysis

The survival differences between the hypoxic and normoxic groups were analyzed by the log-rank test with survfit and survdiff functions in survival package (RRID:SCR_021137) for R [34]. Univariate cox model was performed to identify independent risk factors for GC survival. GC patients were then divided into the high- and low-risk groups based on the median prognostic index (PI) as described in our previous work [35].

Results

Identification of GC subtypes based on hypoxic gene expression signature

To determine whether any hypoxia-related subtypes were present in GC, NMF consensus clustering was performed on the 415 GC samples from TCGA based on the established 356-gene hypoxia signature (Table S1). Then, according to the first value of r at which the correlation coefficient began to fall, the first value at which the residual sum of squares (RSS) curve presented an inflection point, and through the direct visual inspection of the consistency matrix, the best r value was selected. Notably, r = 2 fit all of the required quality criteria for NMF cluster determination; in other words, the 415 GC tumors fell into two separate subgroups (Fig. 1A and Fig. S1). Of the 415 GC samples, 127 were classified into hypoxia group and 288 were classified into normoxia group. To validate the two hypoxia-related subtypes identified in the TCGA samples, GSE62254 cohort harboring 300 samples was subject to NMF clustering as well. As expected, two subtypes with the ratio 1:3 (hypoxia vs. normoxia) were observed in the GSE62254 cohort as well (Fig. 1B). In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) was further used to verify the two subgroups identified by NMF. Results showed that the two groups could be well separated in both TCGA and GSE62254 cohorts (Fig. 1C and D).

It has been reported that hypoxia was an adverse prognostic factor for some tumors, and whether hypoxia was related to survival in GC has aroused our curiosity. Notably, we found that hypoxia was significantly correlated with unfavorable OS in TCGA (five-year survival, 0.23 vs. 0.43, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.11–0.45 vs. 0.34–0.56; Fig. 1E) and GSE62254 (0.31 vs. 0.59, 95% CI 0.22–0.44 vs. 0.53–0.66; Fig. 1F). Furthermore, hypoxia led to significantly shorter disease free survival (DFS) (Log rank P = 2.09e−5; Fig. 1G). And undoubtedly, the normoxic group had a lower risk than the hypoxic group with the hazard ratio of 0.69 (95% CI 0.5–0.95, P = 0.02) in TCGA and 0.45 (95% CI 0.32–0.63, P = 2.86e−6) in GSE62254 cohorts, respectively.

Clinicopathological characteristics between the two hypoxic subtypes

Given the two groups could be well distinguished from each other based on hypoxic gene expression signature, we sought to determine the hypoxia-derived clinicopathological characteristics in GC. Intriguingly, hypoxia had a tendency to younger patients, advanced AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) stages, diffuse histology, and resistant to MSI-H in GC (Fig. S2 and Table 1). However, there were no statistically significant differences in gender, HP (helicobacter pylori) and EBV (Epstein-Barr virus) infection between these two groups (Table 1).

Mutation profiles in hypoxic GC

Driver gene mutation is commonly recognized as the major cause of tumorigenesis, we thus want to decipher the hypoxia-driven mutation profiles in GC. Though there was no significant difference between the two groups in variant classification, variant types and SNV class, two-fold higher mutation burden was observed in the normoxic group (Fig. 2A and B). It is tempting to believe that this distinction was related to higher frequency of MSI-H in the normoxic group (Table 1).

Mutational profiles difference between the hypoxic and normoxic groups in GC. A Overall mutational landscape of the hypoxic samples. B Overall mutational landscape of the normoxic samples. C The top 20 driver mutation genes in the hypoxic samples. D The subtype-derived highly mutated genes between the hypoxic and normoxic groups. E The mutation profile of CDH1 in hypoxic samples

With regard to the potential driver mutation genes, we found TP53, ARID1A, and FAT4 were ranked the top common mutated genes in both hypoxic (Fig. 2A and C) and normoxic groups (Fig. 2B). Nevertheless, plenty of subtype-specific driver mutation genes may explain the unfavorable outcomes under hypoxic conditions. The gene CACNA1D had a 10% mutation frequency in the normoxic group, but no mutated samples were found in the hypoxic group. Meanwhile, KRAS (12%), APC (15%), NEB (17%), FAT3 (21%), and ZFHX4 (24%) all had higher mutation frequency in the normoxic group than in the hypoxic group (< 10%, Fig. 2D). In sharp contrast with the abovementioned normoxia-related driver mutation genes, CDH1 (E-cadherin), the gene closely associated with cell adhesion and hereditary diffuse GC syndrome, had a much higher non-silent mutation frequency in the hypoxic group (19%) than in the normoxic group (5%).

Hypoxia-associated molecular signatures in GC

To better clarify the potential biological processes and mechanisms related to hypoixa in GC, GSEA was performed to investigate enriched molecular hallmarks. The results revealed that the hallmarks of aggressive tumor features, such as myogenesis (NES = 2.19, FDR q-value = 0), EMT (NES = 2.17, FDR q-value = 3.1e−4), apical junction (NES = 2.13, FDR q-value = 7.5e−4), Hedgehog signaling (NES = 2.06, FDR q-value = 0.002), angiogenesis (NES = 1.95, FDR q-value = 0.012), coagulation (NES = 1.93, FDR q-value = 0.011), and KRAS signaling up (NES = 1.87, FDR q-value = 0.02) were the most significantly enriched hallmarks under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 3A and B). In contrast, oxidative phosphorylation (NES = −2.02, FDR q-value = 0.003), the opposite biological process versus hypoxia, was one of the most significant hallmarks in the normoxic group. These trends were held well in the GSE62245 dataset (Fig. 3C and D).

To better elucidate the potential mechanism of tumor adaption to hypoxia, aberrant expression genes were identified between these two subtypes. Interestingly, majority of DEGs were upregulated by hypoxia in both TCGA (83.6%, 1,753 out of 2,097 DEGs were upregulated by hypoxia) and GSE62254 (88.5%, 1,112 out of 1,256 DEGs were upregulated by hypoxia) datasets. GO enrichment interrogation based on the hypoxia-upregulated DEGs revealed that hypoxia was closely associated with extracellular matrix/structure organization and cell-substrate adhesion. KEGG pathway enrichment confirmed the significant relevance to ECM-receptor interaction, cell adhesion molecules, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 3E-H). It should be noted that PI3K-Akt signaling pathway was the top hypoxia-driven pathway in both TCGA and GSE62254 datasets (Fig. 3F and H).

Risk stratification of GC rely on hypoxia-related methylation

As mentioned earlier, hypoxia had a close association with mutation and transcriptome, and whether DNA methylation derives a similar effect caught our attention. Notable, 515 differentiated methylated sites (DMSs) were identified between the hypoxic and normoxic group, with two-fold more hypermethylated sites in the hypoxic group (Fig. 4A). Of note, the DMSs remained basically unchanged even after adjusting age in view of DNA methylation is related to changes in age. The globally hypermethylation under hypoxic conditions may be associated with downregulation of methyltransferase DNMT1 and DNMT3B (Fig. S3). We next want to determine a methylation model that could separate the GC patients into two groups with either a high or low prognostic index (PI). Notably, GC patients could be separated into two groups with either high or low PI based on four hypoxia-related methylation sites (cg00650640, cg05295494, cg11256387, and cg08684639) shrunk by LASSO (Fig. 4B). The risk score formula was as follows: Risk score = −0.1389 × beta-value of cg00650640 (Epidermal Growth Factor Like Domain Multiple 6, EGFL6)−0.018 × beta-value of cg05295494 (Adhesion G Protein-Coupled Receptor B3, ADGRB3)−0.2618 × beta-value of cg11256387 (Collagen Type XXIII Alpha 1 Chain, COL23A1)−0.1468 × beta-value of cg08684639 (WD Repeat Domain 17, WDR17). Kaplan–Meier plot and Cox regression showed that the high-risk group had a poor OS (Log rank P = 0.028) and much higher risk than the low-risk group (HR = 12.12, 95% CI = 3.2 − 45.5, P = 0.000217; Fig. 4C).

Hypoxia was associated with higher immune score and CAF infiltration

To explore the impact of hypoxia on the tumor microenvironment (TME) since cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction was one of the most significant activated pathways under hypoxic conditions as mentioned earlier, we first compared the deconvolution of cell components within tumor tissue. Notably, the hypoxic group had obviously higher immune and stromal scores than those in the normoxic group (Fig. 5A and B), especially for the stromal score. We thus further examined the diverse stromal cell fraction by using xCell method. Intriguingly, the largest stromal differences between the hypoxic and normoxic group lie in CAF infiltration. Apparently, enhanced expression of CAF-related genes such as FAP, ACTA2 (also known as α-SMA), and PDGFRB were observed in the hypoxic group (Fig. 6A and B). However, CDH1, the hypoxia-related driver mutation gene, had a significant lower expression level in the hypoxic group than in the normoxic group, which was consistent with the enhanced EMT hallmark observed in the hypoxic group (Fig. 3B and D). There is no doubt that four-fold and eight-fold higher CAF fraction was observed in TCGA (mean: hypoxia 24.1% vs. normoxia 5.9%) and GSE62254 (mean 15.6% vs. 1.9%), respectively (Fig. 6C).

To explore the potential association of CAF infiltration and higher risk score in the hypoxic group, pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate this relevance. Not unexpectedly, a significant positive correlation was observed between GC risk score and CAF infiltration (r = 0.42, P < 2.2e−16; Fig. 6D), indicating that CAF infiltration was an adverse factor for GC.

Hypoxia conferred tumor resistant to pembrolizumab therapy in patients with metastatic gastric cancer (mGC)

Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) such as programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) therapy is efficacious in a subset of mGC, which prompt us to look into the association of hypoxia and ICI response since hypoxia had a tendency to advanced AJCC stages in TCGA (P = 0.006) and GSE62254 (P = 1.581e-06) cohorts (Table 1). PRJEB25780 with 45 mGC who received pembrolizumab therapy showed that two-fold lower responders (Patients who achieved partial response [PR] and complete response [CR] evaluated using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST)) in hypoxia group than the normoxia group (14% VS. 32%, Fig. 6E). In addition, we also found that six out of 14 samples (43%) classified as mesenchymal in the hypoxia group, while no mesenchymal sample was observed in the normoxic group, which was consistent with the aforementioned observations of enriched EMT marker under hypoxia (Fig. 3B and D). Additionally, we also found that the hypoxic group had obviously higher stromal score than those in the normoxic group (Fig. S4). Notably, the hypoxic group had significantly higher endothelial cell, pericytes, and immature dendritic cell (iDC) infiltration than the normoxic group.

Discussion

It has been widely accepted that tumor hypoxia was associated with poor prognosis and resistance to chemoradiotherapy [36]. Hypoxia enhances the aggressiveness and metastasis potential of tumor and leads to immune escape [37]. In this study, we found that GC fell into hypoxic and normoxic groups according to tailored hypoxia-associated signature. Compared with the normoxic group, hypoxic GC exhibited higher invasiveness as characterized by angiogenesis, KRAS signaling up, and EMT hallmarks and definitely, a worse outcome. In addition, the PI3K-Akt pathway ranked the top significantly activated pathway in the hypoxic group, this pathway has been shown to play an important role in the occurrence and development of GC [38]. Aberrant activation of PI3K signaling can not only helps the initiation of GC, but also promotes the progression of GC to a highly malignant type characterized by metastasis and chemotherapy resistance [39]. Meanwhile, inhibition of PI3K/Akt pathway improves the therapeutic effect of paclitaxel on GC cells under hypoxic condition [40]. Thus, the combination of PI3K inhibitor and hypoxia-targeted inhibitor may provide a promising therapy regimen for drug-resistant GC.

Targeting hypoxia has evolved from a conceptual challenge to a clinical reality, though with mixed success. Strategies can be broadly categorized into hypoxia-activated prodrugs, HIF pathway inhibitors, and methods to improve oxygen delivery. Hypoxia-Activated Prodrugs (HAPs) such as Evofosfamide (TH-302), a prodrug activated under severe hypoxia, showed preclinical promise in some cancers [41]. HIF Pathway Inhibitors: Direct small-molecule inhibition of HIF-1α has been challenging due to its complex regulation. However, indirect approaches show clinical traction. For instance, Belzutifan, a potent HIF-2α inhibitor approved for von Hippel-Lindau disease-associated tumors, demonstrates the druggability of the HIF pathway. Its exploration in clear cell renal cell carcinoma provides a paradigm that could be tested in HIF-2α-driven GC subsets. Additionally, drugs like Digoxin (a cardiac glycoside) and Acriflavine, which disrupt HIF-1α dimerization, have shown antitumor effects in preclinical GC models but lack robust clinical data [42]. As for improve oxygen delivery, Qiao et al. [43] use engineered live microalgae can be delivered to hypoxic tumor regions to increase local oxygen levels and resensitize resistant cancer cells to both radio- and phototherapies. Nevertheless, hypoxia is often transient and spatially variable, making a moving target difficult to hit consistently. Furthermore, inhibiting HIF can have on-target off-tumor effects, given its role in normal physiology.

CDH1, as a crucial gene related to epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), has a significantly higher mutation frequency and lower expression level in the hypoxic group than in the normoxic group (Fig. 2D). CDH1 is the most common germline mutation detected in GC and is the basis of hereditary diffuse GC syndrome [44]. In this study, we also found that hypoxia had a tendency to diffuse GC, suggesting hypoxia-oriented therapy is suitable for diffuse GC. In addition, it has been reported that the wild-type APC mediated the repression of HIF-1α, which means the loss-of-function mutations in APC result in the engagement of hypoxia response [45]. This evidence is consistent with the lower mutation frequency of APC in the hypoxic group (Fig. 2D).

Hypoxia is the canonical feature of TME and will increase the expression of inhibitory co-stimulatory ligand PD-L1 on tumor cells through the accumulation of HIF-1α and mediating immune escape [37]. Indeed, a higher immune score that independent of high TMB or MSI-H was observed in the hypoxic group. Additionally, we confirmed the unfavorable effect (Patients who achieved progressive disease [PD] and stable disease [SD] evaluated using the RECIST) of hypoxia in a Korea cohort containing 45 mGC who received pembrolizumab therapy (Fig. 6E). The observation of high immune scores coupled with poor anti-PD-1 response in GC highlights the complexity of tumor-immune interactions. This paradox underscores that the immune contexture of cancer is multidimensional. A simple “hot” versus “cold” dichotomy is inadequate for predicting ICI response in GC. Several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms can explain poor ICI response despite high immune score. 1) The mere presence of CD8⁺ T cells does not equate to anti-tumor functionality [46]. In GC, a chronically antigen-rich TME can lead to T-cell exhaustion, characterized by upregulation of multiple inhibitory receptors (e.g., TIM-3, LAG-3, TIGIT) beyond PD-1. Thus, dual checkpoint blockade such as combining anti-PD-1 with anti-LAG-3 or anti-TIGIT antibodies to overcome co-inhibitory receptor-mediated exhaustion. 2) A high immune score may be driven not only by effector cells but also by immunosuppressive populations. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) often co-infiltrate tumors with cytotoxic lymphocytes (Fig. S4). A TME rich in both CD8⁺ T cells and Tregs may represent an “immune-balanced” or suppressed state resistant to single-agent PD-1 inhibition. Therefore, targeting myeloid cells such as adding agents that deplete MDSCs or repolarize TAMs (e.g., CSF-1R inhibitors) may alleviate immunosuppression [47]. 3) The spatial relationship between immune cells and cancer cells is crucial. High immune scores that arise from lymphocytes trapped in the stroma or clustered in tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) at the invasive margin, without direct contact with tumor cells (immune exclusion), may not translate to effective tumor cell killing [48]. Evaluating the geographic distribution of lymphocytes relative to tumor cells through multiplex immunohistochemistry or spatial omics may partially explain non-responders show a compartmentalized pattern that resistant to immunotherapy. From a therapeutic standpoint, this analysis argues for combination strategies in GC patients although with high immune scores.

Another interesting finding in this study was enhanced infiltration of CAFs under hypoxic conditions. There is a lot of recent evidence that IL-6 plays a key role in orchestrating the crosstalk between CAF and tumor cells through the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway [49–51]. Thus, hypoxic GC patients may also benefit from tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor (IL-6R)-blocking antibody) because CAF has been demonstrated to be associated with drug resistance in GC [52], which has been approved for treatment of rheumatoid arthritis and giant-cell arteritis [53, 54]. However, emerging evidence indicates that CAFs are not a uniform population but consist of distinct subtypes, each with unique origins, molecular profiles, and functional roles. These subtypes, which may include myofibroblastic (myCAF), inflammatory (iCAF), and antigen-presenting (apCAF) phenotypes, can exert opposing influences on tumor progression, metastasis, immune evasion, and response to therapy [55]. The analysis treated CAFs as a single, homogeneous entity, primarily through bulk tissue analysis and non-specific markers (e.g., α-SMA, FAP). This approach fails to capture the functional diversity within the CAF population. Consequently, the associations observed between CAF abundance and clinical outcomes (such as overall survival or response to chemotherapy) may be conflated. Future studies in GC should incorporate methods such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to comprehensively map CAF subtypes or even spatial transcriptomics to locate specific CAF subtypes within the GC TME capable of resolving CAF heterogeneity. Additionally, targeting the inhibition of CAF activation or CAF reprogramming and depletion and CAF-related pathways such as TGF-β, FAK, LIF, JAK/STAT, Hedgehog and IL6 may aid cancer prevention [56]. In general, more flexible combination treatments that target intratumoral hypoxia need to be explored to prolong the survival of GC patients.

It must be pointed out that some limitations of our study require further research. Firstly, many conclusions of our study were derived from bioinformatic analysis, so future work may employ in vitro cell coculture or even organoid to avoid analysis bias. Second, the molecular mechanism of hypoxia conferred tumor resistant to pembrolizumab therapy needs further investigation. Additionally, the mechanism of CAF infiltration and hypoxia level should also be clarified in future work.

Conclusions

GC fell into two subtypes according to our custom-built hypoxia-related signature, with the hypoxic tumors exhibited a more invasive phenotype and worse prognosis. Nevertheless, the higher immune score of the hypoxic group may make it benefit from ICB treatment independent of high TMB. In addition, hypoxia-targeted or CAF-oriented therapy may overcome current chemoradiotherapy-resistant advanced GC but warrants further investigation.
