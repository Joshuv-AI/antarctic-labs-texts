Parkinson's disease, herpes simplex virus type 1, and nanopore sequencing: A case analysis
Authors: Zehua Wang, Hangxuan Wang, Xinming Su, Jiasheng Mao, Shuhan Zhou, Yuhua Zhang, Xiaowei Ying, Chaojie Wei, Shiwei Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson's disease (PD) is a chronic neurodegenerative disorder that impacts the central nervous system, primarily the motor nervous system. Patients frequently experience respiratory muscle dysfunction, which can lead to swallowing disorders, coughing, ultimately inducing aspiration pneumonia. PD patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) are at risk of being infected with drug-resistant pathogens. This case report presents a PD patient with aspiration pneumonia. As the patient did not show improvement after empirical treatments such as antibiotics, nanopore targeted sequencing (NTS) was employed to detect pathogens in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid in real time, enabling timely detection of HSV-1 infection and adjustment of the treatment strategy. Additionally, whole-exome sequencing (WES) was utilized to explore the genetic background of the patient and identify key mutation genes related to both PD and HSV-1. An attempt was made to explore the drug selection and mechanism connection between the two diseases from the perspectives of protein structure changes and lactylation modification, with the hope of providing new clues for precision medicine. Moreover, it was found that Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores and neutrophils were related to pneumonia pathogen detection results. It is speculated that NTS can not only detect pathogen infection but also evaluate the treatment effect of pneumonia. Overall, this study proposed a novel method for the diagnosis and treatment of PD complicated by aspiration pneumonia by combining clinical indicators and genetic analysis, providing a new idea for the dynamic monitoring and precise treatment of PD, a disease with complex pathogenesis and complications.

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor symptoms such as rigidity, bradykinesia, and tremor, along with non-motor symptoms including cognitive impairment. Its core pathology involves neuronal degeneration and demyelination, which progress over time. Many patients develop impaired muscle control and respiratory dysfunction, leading to dysphagia and aspiration of food or liquid into the trachea. This frequently results in aspiration pneumonia, a major cause of mortality in PD. The underlying pathogenesis involves activation of proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α and CXC chemokines, which recruit neutrophils that release free radicals and proteases. These damage the pulmonary microvascular endothelium, causing exudation, cough, and pulmonary congestion [1]. Clinical studies indicate that the risk of aspiration pneumonia in PD patients is three times higher than in the general population, with mortality rates reaching 23.9 % at one month, 65.2 % at one year, and 91.8 % at five years after the first episode. Diagnosis requires integration of clinical presentation, imaging, and microbiological tests, while treatment centers on physical therapy, pharmacologic management, and careful monitoring of swallowing function.

Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), a neurotropic virus capable of establishing persistent or latent infection in neurons, may also influence the onset and progression of PD. Evidence suggests an enhanced humoral immune response to HSV-1 in PD patients [2], though definitive causality remains unproven. Genetic factors, such as PILRA polymorphisms [3], may interact with HSV-1 infection to increase disease susceptibility. Mechanistically, HSV-1 may contribute to neurodegeneration through autophagy dysregulation and altered immune responses, although these processes are not fully understood.

Nanopore targeted sequencing (NTS), a third-generation sequencing technology, enables real-time determination of nucleic acid sequences by monitoring electrical current changes as individual molecules pass through nanopores [4]. Motor proteins regulate molecular passage, while nanopore proteins detect current disruptions, which are decoded computationally to determine sequence [5]. Compared with earlier sequencing methods, NTS offers advantages including minimal preprocessing, ultra-long read lengths, real-time output, portability, low cost, and clinical adaptability. Its rapid and precise detection capabilities make it particularly suitable for intensive care unit (ICU) settings, where resistant pathogens, nosocomial infections, and empirical treatments complicate management. Bedside application further minimizes transport-related delays and cross-infection risks.

In the context of PD, NTS shows promise not only for pathogen detection but also for studying disease-related proteins. Advances in engineered nanopores allow detection of post-translational modifications of Tau protein [6], analysis of α-synuclein membrane interactions and aggregation [7], and conformational changes of PD-related proteins [8]. Strategies such as 3D blocking mapping and genetic code expansion further enhance detection precision, suggesting potential applications in both aspiration pneumonia diagnosis and PD biomarker discovery.

Here we report a case of PD complicated by aspiration pneumonia. After empirical antibiotic therapy failed, NTS identified HSV-1, guiding the initiation of targeted antiviral therapy with favorable outcomes. NTS was further used to dynamically monitor HSV-1 levels, confirming treatment efficacy. Integration of whole-exome sequencing (WES) data with literature analysis led us to hypothesize that HSV-1 infection may interact with this patient's genetic background to shape disease progression. This case highlights the potential of NTS for dynamic monitoring of viral infection in PD-associated aspiration pneumonia and provides preliminary evidence supporting its application in personalized antiviral therapy. Larger-scale studies are needed to confirm these findings and assess clinical utility.

NTS-based disease progression monitoring

A chest X-ray revealed multiple patchy shadows in both lungs, suggesting a lung infection. The patient underwent bronchoalveolar lavage under bronchoscopy, and the lavage fluid was submitted to both the hospital laboratory and a third-party sequencing facility for pathogen detection using NTS. As shown in Fig. 1C, the NTS process involves screening for pathogen-specific nucleic acid regions, designing targeted primers, establishing a multiplex PCR reaction system, constructing sequencing libraries, performing NTS sequencing, and completing data analysis and result interpretation. Both hospital and third-party sequencing reports demonstrated high-confidence identification of Klebsiella pneumoniae, supporting it as the initial cause of pneumonia and guiding the initiation of empirical antimicrobial therapy. Following elevation of infection markers (Table 3), treatment was escalated to a broader and more potent antibiotic regimen. However, the patient's respiratory symptoms worsened and infection markers failed to improve, suggesting the possibility of another pathogen. The third-party sequencing facility, with broader data coverage, identified HSV-1 with significant read counts and confidence (Table 4). Given HSV-1's neurotropic nature and its association with neuroinflammation and infectious exacerbations [9,10], we hypothesize a synergistic interaction between HSV-1 and K. pneumoniae. HSV-1 infection may disrupt the respiratory mucosal barrier, facilitating bacterial adhesion and colonization, while virus-induced neuroinflammation may aggravate swallowing dysfunction in PD, increasing aspiration risk and perpetuating infection. After pathogen identification, clinicians adjusted treatment to target both pathogens based on susceptibility testing and clinical guidelines. Six days later, repeat NTS testing of the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid showed a marked reduction in HSV-1 sequences to four, and the patient's pulmonary infection had resolved, indicating effective antiviral therapy (Table 4). Two months later, NTS detected recurrence of nosocomial infection, but this was controlled with antimicrobial regimen adjustment without further respiratory deterioration. Six days later, repeat NTS testing of the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid showed a marked reduction in HSV-1 sequences to four, and the patient's pulmonary infection had resolved, indicating effective antiviral therapy (Table 4).

Data cleaning of whole-exome sequencing (WES)

Initially, the exome DNA of the subjects was captured and enriched, followed by high-throughput sequencing. Post-sequencing, the obtained data was meticulously processed and analyzed. The specific steps are as follows: Firstly, the reads from sequencing were compared with the human reference genome provided by NCBI. Subsequently, three types of mutations, namely copy number variation (CNV), insertion and deletion (INDEL), and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), were detected. These mutations were annotated and screened using the tools. Among them, CNV detection was employed by Control-FREEC software. INDEL detection was carried out using bcftools software, and functional annotation was performed using the ANNOVAR tool (https://annovar). SNP detection was employed by bcftools software, and the ANNOVAR tool was further employed to functionally annotate SNPs. Eventually, the sequencing results were successfully obtained (Table 5).

All criteria were based on internationally recognized standards for genetic analysis and the predefined data analysis workflow of this study. Three core aspects guided variant selection. First, the allele frequency threshold was designed to exclude high-frequency benign polymorphisms. Variants were jointly filtered across multiple population databases, with a minor allele frequency (MAF) cutoff set at <1 % (0.01). Population-wide resources (1000 Genomes Project, gnomAD exome, ExAC) and East Asian–specific databases (1000g_EAS, gnomAD_exome_AF_eas, ExAC_EAS) were used in combination. Only variants with a MAF <0.01 in all databases were retained. Variants with MAF ≥0.01 in any database and without clinical evidence of pathogenicity (e.g., annotated “Pathogenic” in ClinVar) were excluded to avoid false associations while preserving rare, population-specific variants. Second, pathogenicity scoring was performed using a multi-tool consensus approach. Variants predicted as “harmful” or “probably harmful” by at least one validated tool were retained, including SIFT (≤0.05, deleterious), PolyPhen-2_HDIV (≥0.453, possibly/probably damaging), MutationTaster (A/D, pathogenic), CADD (Phred ≥10, top 10 % harmful, with ≥15 prioritized), and dbscSNV (ADA/RF ≥ 0.6, possible splicing impact). This integrative strategy minimized bias from any single tool. Third, clinical annotation focused on variants relevant to the study phenotype (PD with HSV-1 pneumonia). ANNOVAR was used to integrate OMIM, ClinVar, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) Catalog databases to identify variants associated with PD, viral infection, pneumonia, or mitochondrial/immune pathways. False positives from repetitive genomic regions were removed, and variants expressed in brain (PD-related) and lung (pneumonia-related) tissues were prioritized using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA). This three-step process ensured relevance to both the clinical phenotype and underlying biology.

Functional enrichment analysis of mutant genes

The clusterProfiler helped in identifying the biological processes and the functions of different Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways that were involved. A comprehensive analysis was conducted on the genes harboring CNV, INDEL, and SNP mutations. Subsequently, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to further elucidate their biological functions.

The hypergeometric test was used to calculate raw P values, and the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR).

Molecular docking

To explore the impact of gene mutations on currently administered drugs, we conducted molecular docking studies. Disease-associated mutant genes identified through KEGG enrichment analysis were selected, and the molecular structures of the currently used drug compounds were obtained from PubChem for docking studies. The three-dimensional structures of the target proteins were retrieved from the AlphaFold3 protein database. PyMOL software was utilized to remove initial ligands and water molecules from the protein structures. Subsequently, AutoDock Tools 1.5.7 was employed to add hydrogen atoms, assign charges, and merge nonpolar hydrogen atoms to the proteins. The interactions between these proteins and the drugs were analyzed using AutoDock Vina software version 1.1.2. Finally, PyMOL software was used to visualize the molecular docking structures.

Result

Core clinical findings: diagnostic and therapeutic evaluation of Parkinson's disease with aspiration pneumonia using NTS monitoring

This study reports a case of PD complicated by aspiration pneumonia. The initial next-generation sequencing (NTS) analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid revealed high sequencing read counts and strong identification confidence for Klebsiella pneumoniae, confirming it as a likely bacterial pathogen underlying the pulmonary infection. HSV-1 was also detected with significant sequence counts and confidence levels (Table 4), suggesting that viral infection may have contributed to the inflammatory process. These findings provided critical evidence for pathogen identification, enabling tailored therapeutic strategies.

A follow-up NTS analysis performed six days later showed a marked reduction in HSV-1 sequence counts compared with the initial test. Concurrently, clinical assessment indicated notable improvement in the patient's pulmonary symptoms, including cough, sputum production, and dyspnea. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of PD with aspiration pneumonia in which NTS was successfully applied to dynamically monitor treatment response. This observation provides preliminary clinical evidence supporting the feasibility of NTS-guided pathogen surveillance in PD-related pneumonia and highlights its potential to optimize therapeutic decisions in viral co-infections. However, these findings are limited to a single case and cannot be directly generalized. Broader validation in multicenter, large-cohort studies is warranted.

Ancillary genomic analyses: whole-exome sequencing (WES) and functional association exploration

Following informed consent from the patient's family, peripheral blood was collected for WES. Raw sequencing quality control was performed by Wuhan Bena Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), and subsequent data cleaning, functional analysis, and mechanistic interpretation were conducted by our team. These analyses are exploratory in nature and intended to provide hypotheses for future mechanistic research linking PD and HSV-1 infection, rather than immediate clinical guidance.

WES data cleaning results

During WES data processing, priority was given to variants located in functional regions such as exons and splice sites, while variants in intergenic regions were excluded due to their limited direct relevance to PD pathology or HSV-1 infection. Secondary filtering focused on variants producing amino acid changes and those with potential clinical relevance. Copy number variant (CNV) analysis identified 123 mutations (91 deletions, 32 duplications), refined to 76 events (56 deletions, 20 duplications) involving 75 coding regions and one intronic region. Genes identified through CNV, INDEL, and SNP screening are hypothesized to contribute to processes such as neuronal mitochondrial maintenance and innate immune regulation—both of which are critical to PD neurodegeneration and antiviral defense. However, these associations remain speculative and require functional validation.

Potential associations between lactylation-related genes, Parkinson's disease, and HSV-1 infection

Emerging evidence indicates that lactate-driven histone modification (lactylation) is closely linked to PD pathology. Early PD is marked by dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra, manifesting as motor and non-motor symptoms [11]. Lactylation can influence dopaminergic neuron survival by regulating genes involved in neuroinflammation and mitochondrial function [12]. Elevated lactate levels have been reported in the cerebrospinal fluid of late-onset PD patients, and in mouse PD models, increased glycolysis-induced lactate accumulation has been shown to exacerbate neuroinflammation and dopaminergic injury [13].

Guided by this knowledge, WES data were screened for lactylation-related mutations. Fourteen lactylation-associated genes, two histone-encoding genes, one PD-related gene, and 13 HSV-1–associated genes were identified. These were imported into the STRING database for protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis, supplemented with SNP variation data (Fig. S1). Protein phosphatase 1 catalytic subunit gamma isomer (PPP1CC) emerged as a potential hub gene bridging lactylation, PD, and HSV-1 infection.

PPP1CC encodes the catalytic subunit of protein phosphatase 1 (PP1), which complexes with PPP1R15A (GADD34) to regulate eIF2α (EIF2S1) dephosphorylation, restoring protein synthesis. HSV-1 US11 protein can disrupt EIF2S1-ATF4 signaling and inhibit PRKN (Parkin) expression, thereby impairing Parkin-mediated mitophagy—a key PD pathway involving α-synuclein, PINK1, and DJ-1 ([14]). Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, orchestrates mitochondrial quality control [15], assisted by PINK1-mediated localization to mitochondria, ubiquitination of voltage - dependent anion selective channel (VDAC), and recruitment of p62 to initiate mitophagy [16]. DJ-1 protects mitochondrial function under oxidative stress [17].

PPP1CC has also been linked to glycogen synthase 1 (GYS1) and circadian clock protein periodic clock protein 2 (PER2). GYS1 regulation by glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) phosphorylation is relevant, as GSK3β overexpression induces mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in dopaminergic neurons [18]. PER2, a transcriptional repressor phosphorylated by PP1, may underlie sleep disturbances in PD ([19]). Additionally, ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunit S1 (NDUFS1) expression is reduced in the cerebellum of daytime-restricted mice [20], linking metabolic regulation to PD pathophysiology. Together, these findings suggest PPP1CC as a potential central mediator of PD, HSV-1 infection, and lactylation, meriting further investigation.

Disease-associated gene screening via KEGG enrichment analysis

KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on CNV-, INDEL-, and SNP-associated genes (workflow shown in Fig. 1B). One PD-associated gene (NDUFS1) and 13 HSV-1–associated genes (ZNF91, ZNF155, ZNF225, ZNF615, TSC1, ZNF195, HLA-DQA2, IFNA6, TRAF6, PPP1CC, ZNF850, ZNF530, and ZNF551) were identified.

NDUFS1, encoding a mitochondrial complex I subunit, plays a central role in electron transfer. Mutations cause complex I deficiency, disrupting neuronal energy metabolism and accelerating PD progression [21,22]. ZNF family proteins regulate transcriptional responses and may influence HSV-1 immune defense. TSC1 mutations can derepress rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), promoting α-synuclein aggregation and autophagy inhibition after HSV-1 infection [23]. HLA-DQA2 mutations may impair MHC II–mediated antigen presentation [24]. IFNA6 mutations could disrupt type I interferon responses to HSV-1 ([25]). TRAF6 mutations may alter NF-κB signaling and viral clearance [26]. PPP1CC mutations could diminish immune response effectiveness [27,28].

Integrating these findings with prior evidence—such as elevated HSV-1 antibody titers in PD patients [3] and possible cross-reactivity between HSV-1 antigens and α-synuclein [29]—we propose a hypothesis: HSV-1 infection may interact with patient-specific PD genetic backgrounds (e.g., NDUFS1 mutations) to exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation, thereby promoting disease progression. This hypothesis requires functional experimental validation. The regulatory pathways of five core genes (TSC1, HLA-DQA2, IFNA6, TRAF6, and PPP1CC) are illustrated in Fig. 4.

Statistical analysis indicated that both raw P values and FDR-adjusted values for the core pathways exceeded 0.05, suggesting no significant pathway enrichment. This limitation reflects the single-case study design and small sample size, which constrain statistical power. Nonetheless, when considered alongside the patient's clinical phenotype (antiviral response) and variant pathogenicity scores (e.g., HLA-DQA2 CADD score of 22.5), the results provide exploratory insights (Table 6). Validation through larger cohorts and mechanistic experiments is required (see Table 7).

Molecular docking analysis

To evaluate the potential impact of gene mutations on drug efficacy, we investigated a missense mutation in HLA-DQA2 (NM_020,056:exon3:c.A463C:p.T155P), a gene closely associated with HSV-1 infection and immune regulation in Parkinson's disease (PD). Molecular docking analysis was performed between tigecycline and this variant. Pathogenicity prediction using MutationAssessor and FATHMM indicated a high pathogenicity score, suggesting that the mutation may alter protein function.

Docking analysis (Fig. 3) suggested that the HLA-DQA2 p. T155P mutation weakens hydrogen bonding interactions between tigecycline and the protein. Hydrogen bonding, a weak electrostatic interaction essential for biomolecular recognition, strongly influences the affinity and specificity of protein–ligand binding [30,31]. The mutation may reduce the number or strength of hydrogen bonds by decreasing polar complementarity and spatial matching within the binding site. Weakened hydrogen bonding could, in theory, lower tigecycline binding affinity for HLA-DQA2, potentially requiring higher drug dosages to maintain therapeutic efficacy, thereby increasing the risk of adverse effects and nonspecific interactions. In addition, this altered binding may affect tigecycline's immunoregulatory targeting efficiency, further compromising the normal immune function of HLA-DQA2.

Discussion

This study, integrating clinical observations with molecular analyses in a patient with concurrent PD and HSV-1 infection, reveals potential mechanistic links among PD, HSV-1 infection, and genetic background. Importantly, NTS revealed a temporal correlation between HSV-1 load and pneumonia severity: viral replication decreased after antiviral treatment, followed by resolution of lung inflammation. Based on this observation, we hypothesize that HSV-1 infection may interact with PD-related genetic susceptibility (e.g., variants in mitochondrial function genes), amplifying immune-inflammatory responses and contributing to PD-associated aspiration pneumonia. However, factors such as immune status and bacterial co-infection may also influence outcomes, and the bidirectional interactions between herpesvirus infection and neurodegeneration remain unresolved.

At the molecular level, WES identified key mutations clustered within two core pathways: mitochondrial dysfunction and immune dysregulation [32,33]. These pathways form a mechanistic bridge linking PD, HSV-1 infection, and genetic susceptibility. Regarding mitochondrial dysfunction, mutations in the PD-related gene NDUFS1 impair nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) dehydrogenase activity, destabilize mitochondrial complex I, disrupt NADH homeostasis, and increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress in dopaminergic neurons [34], a hallmark of PD. Concurrent viral replication further burdens mitochondrial function, reducing cellular resistance to HSV-1 infection [35]. In particular, NDUFS1 mutations may create a “metabolic vulnerability” that heightens cellular susceptibility to HSV-1–mediated injury in the context of PD.

In the context of immune dysregulation, mutations in immune-related genes weaken antiviral defenses and amplify neuroinflammation, creating an environment conducive to persistent HSV-1 infection and accelerated PD pathology. HLA-DQA2 mutations impair MHC class II-mediated antigen presentation, reducing T-cell recognition and clearance of HSV-1 ([36]), while dysregulated expression disrupts WNT/β-catenin signaling via astrocyte–microglia interactions, exacerbating neuronal loss and motor/cognitive decline in PD ([37]). TRAF6 mutations inhibit NF-κB-mediated antiviral responses, enabling viral immune evasion [38], while simultaneously promoting α-synuclein ubiquitination and activating pro-inflammatory pathways, aggravating PD-related neuroinflammation [39,40]. TSC1 mutations relieve mTORC1 inhibition, impairing autophagic clearance of HSV-1 and α-synuclein aggregates, linking persistent infection to accelerated PD pathology [41]. IFNA6 mutations reduce type I interferon production, weakening innate antiviral defenses and promoting HSV-1 replication while exacerbating systemic inflammation in PD. Additionally, molecular docking analyses revealed that HLA-DQA2 mutations reduce hydrogen bonding with tigecycline, suggesting that genetic background may influence drug efficacy.

This study provides a framework for understanding PD–HSV-1 interactions, yet further validation is required. Future research should employ cellular and animal models to elucidate how NDUFS1, HLA-DQA2, TRAF6, TSC1, and IFNA6 mutations synergistically regulate mitochondrial function, antiviral immunity, and neuroinflammation in PD. Given the potential of NTS in disease detection and dynamic monitoring, optimizing this technology for precise real-time tracking of PD-related infections will aid in guiding treatment adjustments. Additionally, exploring how gene mutations alter drug binding could inform drug development strategies, while expanding cohort studies will clarify whether these mutations increase the risk of PD–HSV-1 comorbidity.

In conclusion, this study suggests that PD, HSV-1 infection, and genetic variants may converge on pathways of mitochondrial dysfunction and immune dysregulation. While targeting these pathways may provide new opportunities for managing PD-related complications, further studies with larger cohorts are required to validate these findings and refine therapeutic strategies.
