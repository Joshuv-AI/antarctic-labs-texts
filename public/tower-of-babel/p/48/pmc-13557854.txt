Using an artificial intelligence tool to improve the recognition of pseudoprogression in pleural mesothelioma patients treated with immunotherapy: a case series
Authors: Illaa Smesseim, Kalina Chupetlovska, Valerio Pugliese, Jacobus A. Burgers, Kevin B. W. Groot Lipman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPseudoprogression, defined as initial radiological tumor growth followed by treatment response, can complicate treatment response assessment in patients receiving immunotherapy. In pleural mesothelioma (PM) patients treated with immunotherapy reliable identification of pseudoprogression remains challenging with the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) v1.1 criteria. Automated volumetric assessment using an artificial intelligence (AI) tool (ARTIMES) may provide additional insight into tumor response patterns. We aim to describe cases of pseudoprogression identified using the ARTIMES criteria in PM patients treated with immunotherapy.Case DescriptionWe performed a retrospective observational study of adult PM patients treated with immunotherapy at a tertiary center between January 1, 2013 and July 1, 2022. Tumor volumes were automatically segmented and quantified using the ARTIMES model. Patients demonstrating disease progression according to ARTIMES criteria followed by subsequent tumor volume decrease were evaluated for pseudoprogression. The results were presented in graphs depicting tumor volume, treatment duration, and the start and stop dates of immunotherapy. Among 368 PM patients treated with immunotherapy, 8 patients met inclusion criteria for confirmed pseudoprogression. All patients were male, with a median age of 67.5 years. Most patients had epithelioid histology (75.0%). All patients showed initial volumetric progression on early follow-up imaging, followed by objective tumor volume reduction either during continued immunotherapy or after treatment discontinuation. Volumetric tumor tracking enabled visualization of delayed responses that would have been difficult to interpret using standard imaging comparisons alone.ConclusionsPseudoprogression occurs in a subset of PM patients treated with immunotherapy. Automated volumetric tumor assessment can help identify pseudoprogression patterns. Integrating volumetric analysis with clinical evaluation may reduce premature discontinuation of potentially effective immunotherapy.

Key findings

• Pseudoprogression was identified in 8/368 (2.2%) pleural mesothelioma patients treated with immunotherapy.

• All showed initial tumor volume increase followed by subsequent reduction.

What is known and what is new?

• Pseudoprogression can complicate response assessment during immunotherapy, and distinguishing it from true progression remains challenging.

• ARTIMES automated volumetric assessment can identify and visualize delayed tumor responses in pleural mesothelioma.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• ARTIMES volumetric assessment may complement conventional imaging and help avoid premature discontinuation of effective immunotherapy. Prospective validation of this method is needed.

Introduction

With the introduction of immunotherapy, a new phenomenon regarding tumor response evaluation was introduced, namely pseudoprogression (1). This is defined as the growth of lesions or the appearance of new lesions followed by a response (1,2). This phenomenon was first observed in immunotherapy trials, where patients who initially showed disease progression according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) ultimately achieved a durable response when treatment was continued (3-7). This new phenomenon led to difficulties in assessing tumor response with RECIST for patients treated with immunotherapy. And with the skyrocketing use of immunotherapy for various tumor types, there was a growing need for newer criteria to reliably determine disease response. Wolchok et al. (1) developed new criteria, the immune RECIST (iRECIST), to be used as response criteria in trials testing immunotherapy, see Table 1. The main difference with the RECIST 1.1 is that a repeat scan at least four weeks after the initial scan showing radiological signs of progression is now required for a reliable tumor assessment.

Pleural mesothelioma (PM) is one tumor type in which treatment has shifted more toward immunotherapy, particularly in the non-epithelioid subtype, where a significant overall survival benefit has been demonstrated (8).

Pseudoprogression in patients with PM treated with immunotherapy has been previously described (9-12) and its incidence is estimated to be 6.9% (10). Barnet et al. (12) described a case series of two patients who were treated with pembrolizumab [programmed death-1 (PD-1) receptor inhibitor] as part of a phase I study (KEYNOTE-028). The first patient had progressive disease (PD) after five cycles of pembrolizumab (2 mg/kg every 3 weeks) according to the modified RECIST (mRECIST) criteria. Treatment was discontinued, but 33 weeks after discontinuation of pembrolizumab, a near-complete response was observed. In the second patient, treatment with pembrolizumab was discontinued after ten cycles due to radiological progression according to RECIST. However, 16 weeks after treatment discontinuation, follow-up scans showed partial response (PR).

An artificial intelligence (AI) model for automatic total tumor volume measurement, ARTIMES, was previously developed (13). Compared with mRECIST, ARTIMES provides a volumetric rather than unidimensional assessment, which may allow for more accurate detection of tumor response or progression. Its potential for improved interobserver agreement is currently being investigated in the ongoing COMET trial, a prospective, blinded, single-center study comparing mRECIST v1.1 and ARTIMES in patients with PM (14). This volumetric approach may be particularly useful for tumors with irregular shape, offering a more comprehensive assessment of treatment response. Using the ARTIMES model, novel volumetric response evaluation criteria were established to improve upon the current standard, mRECIST (13). The criteria for volumetric PD and PR according to the ARTIMES criteria were used. PD was defined as an increase in tumor volume with +35 mL and +40% of the baseline volume or an absolute growth of 100 mL, and PR as a decrease with −35 mL (13). The baseline scan is defined as the last scan performed before the start of immunotherapy and must have been conducted within 4 weeks prior to treatment initiation.

We aimed to describe multiple cases of pseudoprogression as measured by the ARTIMES criteria (15) in PM patients treated with immunotherapy to emphasize that clinicians should consider pseudoprogression when there is radiological progression according to the mRECIST 1.1 and volume based tumor measurement criteria. We present this article in accordance with the AME Case Series reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-1-0207/rc).

Case presentation

We conducted a retrospective observational study at a tertiary center in the Netherlands. All adult patients with PM treated with dual immunotherapy from January 1, 2013 to July 1, 2022, were screened from the local PM patient database for the following inclusion criteria: Adult patients with histologically proven PM who have been treated with immunotherapy for at least one cycle and have at least one follow-up scan after radiological progression. The tumors on the CT-scans were automatically segmented and their volume was determined (13).

Patients were selected from the patients’ database based on disease progression according to the ARTIMES criteria (13). Since ARTIMES facilitates large scale tumor volume tracking automatically and more accurately, we performed volumetric analyses on all CT scans of patients to look for patterns of pseudoprogression. The results will be presented in a graph where tumor volume, treatment time, and the start and stop dates of immunotherapy will be depicted. One thoracic radiologist (K.C.) assessed tumor response using mRECIST criteria version 1.1.Demographic and clinical variables, including age, gender, histology type were collected from patient records. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1.

From our database of 368 PM patients treated with immunotherapy, a total of 20 patients with potential pseudoprogression were identified according to the ARTIMES criteria. After aligning the treatment start- and end dates with the tumor volume response per scan, eight patients met the inclusion criteria, see Figure 1. All patients were male and the median age was 67.5 years (interquartile range, 65.5–78.0 years). Six patients (75.0%) had an epithelioid subtype, one patient sarcomatoid and one biphasic.

Study flowchart (13).

Patient A (see Figure 2) was a 66-year-old male with PM (epithelioid subtype) who had received four cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy at a different hospital. Four months later, disease progression was observed, and he was subsequently treated with nivolumab and ipilimumab for 8 months. The first scan after initiating immunotherapy showed PD according to RECIST 1.1. However, because of clinical improvement, including reduced dyspnea and suspicion of pseudoprogression, treatment was continued. Subsequent scans confirmed an objective response, supporting the presence of pseudoprogression. Due to clinical deterioration, treatment was paused for 3 months. When the following scan showed disease progression, best supportive care was initiated.

Treatment and tumor volume overview in patient A during immunotherapy.

Patient B (see Figure 3) was an 80-year-old diagnosed with right-sided PM (epithelioid subtype). He was treated with four cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy. Ten months later, disease progression was observed, and he was subsequently treated with nivolumab for 2 months. Because disease progression was documented on two subsequent CT-scans, immunotherapy was discontinued. The patient continued to be monitored and received regular CT-scans to assess tumor growth. After the initial increase in tumor volume, subsequent scans showed a decrease, indicating pseudoprogression.

Treatment and tumor volume overview in patient B during immunotherapy.

Patient C (see Figure 4) was a 65-year-old male diagnosed with right-sided PM (epithelioid subtype). He was initially treated with four cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy before being referred to our hospital (Netherlands Cancer Institute) due to disease progression. At our hospital, he received nivolumab and ipilimumab for 2 years. The first two scans after starting dual immunotherapy showed PD. Because the patient’s performance status was good, treatment was continued. Subsequent scans demonstrated an impressive objective response, suggesting that the initial progression might have represented pseudoprogression. Disease remained stable until treatment was discontinued due to immune-related colitis. A year and a half after stopping immunotherapy, pleural disease progression was observed again, along with a pancreatic metastasis. An application to restart immunotherapy was approved, and initial scans showed a PR. However, immunotherapy was discontinued once more due to a flare-up of the immune-related colitis.

Treatment and tumor volume overview in patient C.

Patient D (see Figure 5) was a 77-year-old man diagnosed with left-sided PM (sarcomatoid subtype). He underwent four cycles of carboplatin-pemetrexed chemotherapy, achieving stable disease (SD). A few months after chemotherapy, PD was observed on a CT-scan. Nivolumab was subsequently started. The follow-up scan 6 weeks after the initiating immunotherapy demonstrated disease progression. Even though the patient had no worsening respiratory symptoms, the second scan at 12 weeks confirmed PD, leading to discontinuation of immunotherapy. Interestingly, a delayed response to immunotherapy was observed after its discontinuation, but immunotherapy was not restarted. Several months later, the patient experienced disease progression and ultimately died due to respiratory failure.

Treatment and tumor volume overview in patient D during immunotherapy.

Patient E (see Figure 6) was a 79-year-old male with left-sided PM (epithelioid subtype) who was initially treated with platinum-based chemotherapy, achieving PR. A few months later, PD was observed, and the patient was treated with nivolumab-ipilimumab. The first CT scan showed PD, but due to suspicion of pseudoprogression and the patient’s good performance status, treatment was continued. The subsequent scan demonstrated a PR. After several months, immunotherapy was discontinued due to PD, and a needle-tract metastasis was treated with palliative radiotherapy.

Treatment and tumor volume overview in patient E during immunotherapy.

Patient F (see Figure 7) was a 60-year-old patient with right-sided PM (biphasic subtype) who had previously received platinum-based chemotherapy. He was referred to our hospital due to disease progression 1 year after treatment. The patient was subsequently treated with nivolumab, but after one cycle, disease progression was observed on the CT-scan. Treatment was discontinued because of this progression and a deteriorating performance status caused by nausea, likely related to immunotherapy-toxicity. After tapering morphine, the nausea improved, but immunotherapy was not restarted.

Treatment and tumor volume overview in patient F during immunotherapy.

Six months after discontinuing immunotherapy, the patient presented with confusion, and brain metastases were detected on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Dexamethasone was initiated to manage symptoms related to the brain metastases, and a best supportive care approach was chosen. The patient passed away 3 months after the diagnosis of brain metastases.

Patient G (see Figure 8) was a 66-year-old man with left-sided PM (epithelioid subtype) who was treated with three cycles of carboplatin-pemetrexed, resulting in a PR. One and a half years after treatment, disease progression was observed, and the patient was subsequently treated with nivolumab. The first CT scan showed PD according to mRECIST 1.1. However, because of clinical improvement, treatment was continued. Subsequent scans demonstrated evidence of treatment response. After 8 months of therapy, the patient experienced clinical deterioration and disease progression according to mRECIST 1.1, leading to discontinuation of therapy.

Treatment and tumor volume overview in patient G during immunotherapy.

Patient H (see Figure 9) was a 69-year-old male with left-sided PM (epithelioid subtype) and was initially treated with cisplatin-pemetrexed-axitinib and pleurectomy. Five years after this treatment, disease progression was observed on the CT-scan, and the patient was subsequently treated with nivolumab. Despite progression on the first response evaluation, treatment was continued due to clinical improvement. Follow-up scans showed evidence of treatment response, but immunotherapy was discontinued approximately 6 months after initiation due to severe fatigue attributed to treatment-related toxicity.

Treatment and tumor volume overview in patient H during immunotherapy.

For all patients, treatment response of the first scans after treatment initiation was also evaluated for our study according to mRECIST 1.1 by one thoracic radiologist (K.C.). Only one patient (patient F) exhibited pseudoprogression following mRECIST, with initial PD followed by a PR. Among the remaining patients, A, B, G, and H demonstrated SD, patient C had PD and remained PD on subsequent assessment and patients D, and E achieved PR, see Table S1.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the occurrence of pseudoprogression in PM patients treated with immunotherapy. From a database of 368 patients, 8 had pseudoprogression according to the ARTIMES criteria. The cases show that volume-based tumor measurement can give an accurate insight in pseudoprogression volume patterns. Because in current practice radiologists typically compare the current scan with the previous one, it can be difficult to assess the overall course of tumor size using CT scans alone.

Currently, the diagnosis pseudoprogression might be suspected at the first response evaluation and can only be confirmed retrospectively. The AI-based volume measurement tool used in this study may support this process by providing a rapid and intuitive visualization of tumor volume over time, allowing proper integration of imaging with the clinical status irrespective whether immunotherapy was stopped or not. At subsequent response evaluations, the AI tool identified pseudoprogression more frequently and efficiently that mRECIST, which is inherently hampered by a high interobserver variability. The discrepancy between mRECIST assessments performed by treating physicians in routine clinical practice and those by the thoracic radiologist highlights the interobserver variability inherent to mRECIST v1.1.

The study is limited by its retrospective design and small sample size. Also, identification of pseudoprogression relied on ARTIMES criteria and not mRECIST v1.1. criteria which is different from clinical practice. Additionally, the heterogeneity of prior treatments, differences in immunotherapy regimens, and variable follow-up intervals may affect interpretation of treatment response.

These findings suggest that a subset of PM patients treated with immunotherapy may experience pseudoprogression, but the exact size of this subset remains unclear because, in clinical practice there is a lack of a clear overview of tumor size changes based on CT-scan images. Continuation of therapy in clinically stable patients, despite initial radiologic progression, may allow for eventual tumor response. Another limitation is that, as this is a retrospective study, the timing of CT-scans varied across PM patients. Consequently, even in patients classified as having no pseudoprogression, the evaluation may not be fully accurate due to differences in imaging timing, which could affect the classification of tumor responses. Based on our results, we recommend interpreting radiological findings in the context of the patient’s clinical status and optionally continuing treatment in clinically improving patients to assess for possible pseudoprogression.

The exact molecular mechanism of pseudoprogression is still not fully understood (16). Previous studies have described that the major mechanisms of pseudoprogression consist of infiltration of immune cells, such as CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and macrophages, as well as tumor necrosis, hemorrhage, and edema (9,17-20). However, it remains unclear to what extent pseudoprogression can be explained by these mechanisms, and in which cases it should be considered a delayed response to immunotherapy.

Conclusions

Pseudoprogression is an uncommon or may be underdiagnosed but important phenomenon in PM patients treated with immunotherapy. Careful monitoring of both clinical signs and imaging is essential to guide treatment and avoid stopping therapy prematurely.
