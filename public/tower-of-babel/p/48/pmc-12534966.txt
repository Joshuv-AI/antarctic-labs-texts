Bioimaging application and growth-promoting behavior of Auxin-derived carbon dots on cancer cell and seed performance
Authors: Ghasem Noorkhajavi, Meysam Najaflou, Hana Farsinezhad, Afagh Yavari, Sonia Fathi-karkan, Mehdi Shahgolzari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAuxin-derived carbon dots (Aux-CDs) were synthesized and evaluated for their dual functionality in biomedical and agricultural contexts. One-step hydrothermal synthesis was employed for the first time to produce eco-friendly and cost-effective Aux-CDs. The approach involves introducing a mixture of Auxin and water into the reactor. The fluorescence in the Aux-CDs was initially confirmed using a UV transilluminator. Physicochemical characterizations were evaluated via UV–Vis spectrophotometry (absorbance at 470 nm), zeta potential measurement (-37.8 mV), photoluminescence, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR). The total quantum yield exhibited was 43%, and the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed a size range of approximately 10 nm. In cancer cells, the MTT assay revealed that Aux-CDs demonstrated low cytotoxicity in 4T1 cells up to 10 µg/mL. The fluorescence accumulation indicates the internalization of the Aux-CDs by 4T1 cells. In seed performance, we examined the effects of Aux-CDs on through nanopriming. The wheat seeds (the Drum and Ehsan varieties) were incubated with CDs to check the absorption of Aux-CDs by them. The fluorescence was observed under a UV-transilluminator in the growing parts of seeds, indicating the absorption of Aux-CDs during wheat growth. Cancer cell and wheat imaging showed that fluorescent Aux-CDs could be used as a bioimaging agent. Additionally, Aux-CDs significantly improved seed germination percentage and the performance index in the Drum and Ehsan varieties in low concentration (1 µg/mL) compared to high concentration (10 µg/mL). Consequently, Aux-CDs synthesized via a one-step hydrothermal method exhibit high quantum yield and biocompatibility, and can act as multifunctional nanomaterials for bioimaging and plant growth enhancement in biomedical and agricultural research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-025-00563-3.Keywords: Carbon dots (CDs), Bioimaging, Cancer, Nanopriming, Wheat seed

Introduction

Hydrothermal synthesis is a thermal engineering technique that optimizes temperature and pressure to facilitate desired chemical reactions and material properties. In this process, solid materials are combined with an aqueous solution in a high-temperature, high-pressure reaction vessel to enable controlled material formation [1]. Hydrothermal synthesis has emerged as the most suitable method for fabricating various materials due to its simplicity, eco-friendliness, and ability to precisely control key physicochemical characteristics [2–4]. Optimizing synthetic and post-synthetic parameters like solvent, temperature, pressure, and time is essential for practical application in material science [5, 6]. Researchers are refining these parameters to control material properties, which can ultimately influence their physicochemical and biological behavior in various applications [5]. Hydrothermal synthesis is applied as a versatile and cost-effective method for producing nanoparticles with highly tunable properties and a high degree of purity and uniformity by precisely controlling synthetic and post-synthetic parameters [7]. Hydrothermal synthesis has also been used for various nanoparticle syntheses, such as metallic nanoparticles [8](e.g., silver [9], TiO2 [7, 10]), and carbon nanoparticles [8]. Recently, hydrothermal synthesis has gained prominence for producing carbon dots (CDs) due to its high quantum efficiency, cost-effectiveness, eco-friendly characteristics, and non-toxic nature compared to other fabrication methods [5, 9].

CDs are a novel type of carbon-based fluorescent nanoparticles (size < 10 nm) with quantum dot-like behavior that exhibit strong photoluminescence [10]. They are increasingly regarded as viable alternatives to traditional fluorescent dyes due to their diverse optical properties and high resistance to photobleaching, making them promising for various scientific and industrial applications such as bioimaging and sensing [11]. The fluorescence of CDs is closely linked to their surface conditions, with a core that can be crystalline or amorphous and a surface shell containing polar or nonpolar groups. Functional groups enhance fluorescence via surface passivation/functionalization, significantly affecting their optical performance [12]. Additionally, synthetic and post-synthetic parameters can impact CDs’ fluorescence intensity, quantum yield, surface performance, and particle size distribution in a controlled manner [13, 14]. Typically, increasing temperature enhances the carbonization process and boosts crystallinity, while moderate temperatures help maintain functional groups on the surface and improve biocompatibility for biomedical applications. CDs are incredibly versatile, finding applications in numerous scientific fields, including sensing, bioimaging, antibacterial agents, fluorescent patterning inks, photocatalysis, nanomedicines, and nanofillers due to their multifunctional properties [10, 12]. The application of CDs in biomedical imaging and plant sciences has garnered significant attention, primarily due to their non-toxic nature and highly tunable optical properties. These characteristics enable precise modulation of their photoluminescence, facilitating enhanced imaging resolution and sensitivity in biological systems. Additionally, their biocompatibility ensures minimal cytotoxicity, making them ideal candidates for in vivo imaging and biological investigations. As a result, carbon dots are emerging as powerful tools for advancing diagnostic methodologies and improving analytical techniques in both medical and agricultural research with broad potential impact [15]. The preparation of fluorescent CDs can be achieved through A wide array of natural and synthetic precursors, including small molecules, polysaccharides, proteins, and polymers [3, 16–19]. These precursors possess positive, negative, neutral, hydrophilic, hydrophobic, or zwitterionic characteristics, which influence their physicochemical behavior [18, 20]. Plant-derived organic molecules have recently garnered attention as sustainable precursors for CD synthesis due to their availability and eco-friendly nature [6, 19].

Nowadays, CDs synthesized from bio-resources have attracted significant attention. Bio-resources are rich in C, H, and O elements and functional groups on their surface for enhancing bio-functionality [19, 21]. Thus, using these bio-resources for CD synthesis can significantly enhance their luminescent properties and selectivity and increase solubility [19]. A bio-resource with an intriguing carbon source is Auxin, a phytohormone that regulates growth and development [22, 23]. Furthermore, Auxin formulations, such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and indole-3-butyric acid (IBA), contain nitrogen-rich aromatic structures that can facilitate heteroatom doping. The formation of graphitic and heteroatom-doped a strategy towards improving the functionality of CDs [19, 24–26]. Thus, the inclusion of Auxin as a precursor can offer distinct advantages beyond conventional CDs. Aux-CDs can display residual Auxin-like functional groups with inherent bioactivity on their surface, facilitating interactions with biological systems generally not seen with conventional CDs [27]. Auxin functional groups enable receptor-mediated absorption and localization; thus, their integration into Aux-CDs can improve cellular absorption, and, similar to other CDs [3, 28], it enhances imaging efficiency and specificity in bioimaging. However, the effective utilization of Auxins in CD synthesis necessitates a thorough understanding of the thermal transformation mechanisms that occur during hydrothermal processing to ensure reproducibility and efficiency [29, 30].

This study represents the first investigation into synthesizing CDs using Auxin as a carbon source. We optimized Aux-CDs synthesis for dual applications in cancer cell photoimaging and wheat seed growth through a one-step hydrothermal method. While bioimaging and plant growth promotion may initially appear as distinct domains, we strategically integrated these roles due to Aux-CDs’ unique surface chemistry and optical properties. The decision to explore both functions stems from the inherent multifunctionality imparted by Auxin: its nitrogen-rich structure enhances photoluminescence and cellular interaction for imaging, while its biological origin and preserved Auxin-like activity support plant development. Thus, the dual evaluation reflects a single mechanistic innovation using a bioactive plant hormone as a nanomaterial precursor to achieve synergistic capabilities across biomedical and agricultural systems.

Experimental section

Synthesis of Aux-CDs

Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) (10 mg, ≥ 98% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Catalog No. I2886) was solubilized in 20 mL of deionized water under magnetic stirring at ambient temperature. The solution was placed in a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave (Parr Instrument Co.) and heated to 200 °C for 3 h under autogenous pressure. The temperature was meticulously controlled with an external digital thermocouple. Subsequent to the process, the autoclave was permitted to cool spontaneously to ambient temperature (about 25 °C).

Heating IAA to 200 °C results in the breakdown of its original molecular structure, a phenomenon anticipated in carbonization processes. IAA functions efficiently as a nitrogen-dense, aromatic carbon precursor. As the parent Auxin undergoes structural decomposition, nitrogen-containing fragments and functional groups are likely incorporated into the carbon dot structure, enhancing the reported bioactivity. Prior research has demonstrated that carbon dots originating from bioactive substances can preserve or even augment biological characteristics via surface functionalization or heteroatom doping. Our findings indicate that the growth-promoting effects observed during seed priming imply that the produced Aux-CDs demonstrate Auxin-like action, despite the lack of complete IAA molecules.

The IAA utilized was not derived from biological sources but was employed as supplied, without additional purification or alteration. The product was preserved in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended specifications to ensure chemical stability and consistency throughout the study.

Following manufacturing, the solution was purified by dialysis using a 1 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) membrane (Spectra/Por®) against deionized water for 24 h at room temperature, with water exchanges performed every 4 h. Subsequent to dialysis, the solution was subjected to filtration using a 0.22 μm syringe filter to eliminate residual particles, followed by lyophilization to yield dry Aux-CDs powder. The powder was preserved in amber vials at 4 °C in darkness. The mass of the desiccated Aux-CDs was quantified gravimetrically to provide standardized concentrations for subsequent applications, including cytotoxicity and seed priming experiments. All glassware was meticulously cleansed with deionized water and subjected to autoclaving to avert contamination.

Quantum yield (QY) measurement

The QY of Aux-CDs was estimated using the equation Q = Qs (As/Is) (I/A) (n/ns)2 [31]. Quinnin sulfate (QY = 0.546) was used as the standard. Here, A stands for (absorbance of Auxin = 0.043), As for (absorbance of quinine sulfate (as reference) 0.035), I for (integral of photoluminescence intensity of Auxin = 104361), Is for (integral of photoluminescence intensity of quinine sulfate = 107442), n for (the refractive index = 1).

Analytical methods

In the study, a transmission electron microscope (TEM, ZEISS EM900) and dynamic light scattering (DLS, Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments, and Malvern, UK) were used to determine the size of the synthesized Aux-CDs. The morphology of the particles was analyzed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Tescan MIRA3, Czech Republic). Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Oxford, United Kingdom) was used to analyze elemental composition and mapping. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to study the crystallinity of the carbon dots. The pattern of the particles was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (Tongda, China). The data were collected within the 2θ range from 10° to 50° with a step size of 0.02° and a step time of 0.5 s. The functional groups of the synthesized particles were identified using Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR, Bruker Tensor 27, Germany) in the frequency range of 500–4000 cm-1 and a resolution of 1 cm− 1.

Cancer cell imaging and cytotoxicity

Tumor cells (4T1 cells) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium, incubated, and then treated with Aux-CDs. The 4T1 cell line is a murine breast cancer model commonly used in biomedical research for evaluating imaging and therapeutic agents due to its aggressive tumorigenicity and ability to closely mimic human metastatic breast cancer. Cells were washed three times with PBS to remove non-associated compounds. Cells were analyzed with fluorescence imaging, and competition experiments were performed to confirm receptor-mediated uptake. The cells were then used for fluorescence imaging. Cytotoxicity of Aux-CDs was assessed on 4T1 cells using a standard MTT assay (expanded MTT). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and then spiked with different concentrations of Aux-CDs (0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 10 µg/mL). The 1–10 µg/mL concentration range was selected based on prior studies indicating that Aux-CDs typically remain biocompatible and exhibit minimal cytotoxicity below 100 µg/mL. This range was intentionally chosen to avoid aggregation-related toxicity or oxidative damage commonly observed at higher (≥ mg/mL) concentrations. The formazan was released, and the viability of the cells was calculated. The MTT assay was performed following the established protocol (Mosmann, 1983) [32]. Subsequently, upon treatment, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was introduced to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the medium was meticulously discarded, and 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was introduced to solubilize the formazan crystals. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated using the formula.

Cell Viability (%) = (Absorbance of treated cells / Absorbance of control cells) × 100.

Seed nanopriming with Aux-CDs

Aux-CDs growth-inducing activities were investigated using a nanopriming approach. Wheat seeds of Drum and Ehsan varieties were surface-sterilized using 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 10 min and then washed three times with sterile distilled water to eliminate any remaining disinfectant. Sterilized seeds were inoculated in Aux-CDs solutions at 1 µg/mL and 10 µg/mL concentration for 12 h in the dark at 25 °C. For the free Auxin (IAA) comparator treatment, seeds were primed with an aqueous IAA solution at 285 µM, which corresponds to an estimated 5% of the theoretical maximum Auxin content in the 1 µg/mL Aux-CDs treatment, based on precursor loading during synthesis. This concentration was selected to approximate equivalence under the assumption that only a small fraction of the original Auxin mass remains functionally available after carbonization. Exact molar equivalence between treatments was not analytically confirmed.

It was adapted from standard practice by Mahakham et al. (2016; 2017) [33, 34], and it serves as the foundation for nanoparticle-assisted seed priming. Seeds were subsequently transferred to 90 mm sterilized Petri dishes with Whatman No. 1 filter paper that had been wetted by 5 mL of sterilized deionized water after priming. The plates were incubated in a 25 ± 1 °C growth chamber with a light/dark cycle of 16:8 h, 60–70% relative humidity, and cool white fluorescent lamp lighting (~ 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹).

Three replicates of each treatment were performed, each with 25 seeds. Biological replicates consisted of single batches of seeds and separate treatment solutions, while technical replicates relied on three duplicate measurements per condition. The growth chamber, where this study was conducted at Tabriz University of Medical Sciences in northwestern Iran, had regular environmental conditions typical of a cold semi-arid region with average summer temperatures between 20 and 30 °C and low precipitation. The conditions were rigorously observed and adjusted daily.

Seedlings were analyzed under ultraviolet light (λ = 365 nm) using a portable transilluminator for fluorescence imaging. The images were captured with a Nikon D3500 DSLR camera affixed to a tripod and subsequently processed with ImageJ software to enhance contrast and quantify fluorescence intensity. Samples were irrigated on a daily basis during the experiment. Day 8 was allocated for evaluating morphological characteristics, encompassing seedling, shoot, and root lengths, germination rate, and fluorescence emission.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were assessed and given as mean ± standard deviation (SD) derived from three separate biological replicates (n = 3) for each treatment group. Statistical comparisons were performed utilizing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), succeeded by Tukey’s post hoc test to ascertain significant differences among means. Prior to ANOVA, data were assessed for normal distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) to ensure validity of parametric tests. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was used for all analyses. Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

The present study developed an eco-friendly, simple, cost-efficient, and one-step hydrothermal method to synthesize carbon dots using Auxin (Aux-CDs). Auxin was selected above other phytohormones due to its nitrogen-containing aromatic structure, which facilitates carbonization and doping in carbon dot production, along with its recognized role in regulating plant growth and development. This dual function as an effective carbon source and a bioactive compound makes Auxin an exceptionally suitable precursor for multifunctional carbon dots. The resulting carbon dots were characterized based on their properties. The TEM and SEM images with DLs (expanded DL) and the zeta potential results of the carbon dots are shown in Fig. 1. The results presented in this study illustrate the proficient synthesis of Aux-CDs with a diameter of less than 10 nm and a zeta potential of about − 37 mV by a low-temperature synthesis technique. According to the TEM and SEM analyses, the carbon dots exhibit excellent dispersion and remarkable stability, making them potential candidates for various applications, including bioimaging, sensing, and optoelectronics.

The general structure of the synthesized Aux-CDs exhibits an almost amorphous nature, as shown by XRD analysis (Fig. 2c), with a series of distinct peaks and a hump near 24 degrees, corresponding to a lattice spacing of 0.36 nm. Notably, this particular lattice spacing has been frequently observed in XRD patterns of CDs [35]. Moreover, the XRD pattern obtained closely resembles the known diffraction pattern [Ref. Most of the FTIR peaks in the Aux-CDs shifted, or their intensity changed, in agreement with the Auxin FTIR result due to structural changes and the successful formation of Aux-CDs (Figs. 2a and b). The FTIR peaks observed at 3388 cm− 1 and 3000 cm− 1 in Auxin or Aux-CDs are typically associated with the stretching vibration of the C-H and O-H bonds, indicating the presence of aliphatic hydrocarbon groups (-CH) and the hydroxyl group in the carboxylic acid; however, the peak associated with O-H almost disappeared in the FTIR result of the Aux-CDs. Also, peaks 1337, 1300, and 739 cm− 1 associated with C-N, C-O, and N-H bonds in Auxin, respectively, were removed or shifted after synthesizing Aux-CDs, demonstrating the role of these groups in Aux-CDs formation. The disappearance or restructuring of these peaks can be attributed to the decomposition and carbonization processes involved in hydrothermal synthesis. At elevated temperatures (~ 200 °C), Auxin undergoes degradation by dehydration, deamination, and decarboxylation processes, resulting in the alteration or degradation of functional groups including N–H, C–N, and O–H. These chemical changes create new bonding configurations in the carbon dot framework, and the initial vibrational modes for Auxin are no longer detectable or show up at different wave numbers. Thus, the vanishing of these peaks signals successful conversion of Auxin into a new carbon-based nanostructure. The FTIR peak of Auxin at approximately 1698 cm− 1 is typically associated with the stretching vibration of the aromatic C = O bond in the carboxyl group. In the Aux-CDs, this peak is shifted to the lower levels (1588 cm− 1). Also, the peaks at 1212 and 928 cm− 1 are typically associated with the bending vibrations of the C-O and O-H bonds in the carboxyl group in Auxin compounds. The carboxyl group plays a vital role in the formation of carbon points. The peaks at 1212 and 928 (here, writing units) have spectacularly decreased in intensity and also shifted in carbon points, showing that this group is mainly involved in forming carbon dots.

The peaks around 850, 786, and 600 cm− 1, which are associated with C-H bonds in aromatic and alkyne groups, remain unshifted in the Aux-CDs, but their intensity increased after synthesis. In contrast, the peaks at 1091 and 1032 cm− 1, which are associated with C-O bonds in the ester group, remain unchanged in the Aux-CDs. In addition, the peaks at 1454 and 1412 cm− 1, which indicate stretching vibrations in C-C and C-H bonds and are typically aligned with bending vibrations in methyl and aromatic rings, show consistent patterns in the Aux-CDs results. The peak at 517 cm− 1, which is associated with C-Cl bonds, also exhibits the same features in both sets of results.

Cancer cell imaging and cytotoxicity

The results of the fluorescence microscopy experiments show the potential for applying Aux-CDs for bioimaging. 4T1 cancer cells were treated with Aux-CDs at concentrations up to 10 µg/mL for 4 h (Fig. 4a). The fluorescence accumulation indicates the internalization of the Aux-CDs by 4T1 cells. Imaging techniques enable real-time monitoring of tumor morphology, allowing physicians to track the development of tumor tissue. They can also adjust the drug dosage to avoid overtreatment with harmful side effects and, at the same time, rule out undertreatment and the associated possibility of incomplete tumor remission [49, 50]. Several model systems for targeted imaging have been developed by biodistribution and accumulation of imaging agents in tumor areas [50, 51]. Nevertheless, there are several drawbacks associated with imaging agents, including their inability to cross biological barriers, low biocompatibility, and instability. In recent years, exemplary successes have been achieved in applying nanotheranostics against various types of cancer, enabling the developed drugs or imaging agents to overcome the specific disadvantages of some tumors [52, 53]. To date, many nanotheranostic approaches have been developed through targeted, precision bioimaging and oncotherapy to improve treatment [53, 54].

(a) Fluorescence microscopy image of fixed 4T1 cells incubated with Aux-CDs (10 µg/mL) for 4 h (20× magnification). (b) 4T1 cells viability in the Aux-CDs concentrations (0, 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 µg/mL) for 24 h (n = 3)

Since single-walled carbon nanotubes were identified by electrophoretic purification, CDs have emerged as a new category of carbon nanomaterials due to their outstanding physicochemical properties and diverse applicability [55]. These CDs exhibit various structures, sizes, physicochemical properties, and surface functional groups [56, 57]. CDs have gained much recognition over the past decade for their capabilities as light-emitting diodes, photocatalysts, and bio-imaging agents. In addition, they can promote plant growth due to their favorable optical, mechanical, thermal, electronic, and biocompatible properties [58]. The MTT assay revealed that Aux-CDs demonstrated low cytotoxicity in 4T1 cells up to 10 µg/mL (Fig. 4b), which aligns with existing literature reporting high biocompatibility of CDs at sub-100 µg/mL concentrations. All imaging and cytotoxicity experiments were conducted at biologically realistic µg/mL doses. Numerous cell lines, concentrations, and surface coatings have been tested for the cytotoxic effects of CDs (viability, mortality, and proliferation) [59, 60]. While the MTT assay supports low toxicity of Aux-CDs at concentrations up to 10 µg/mL, further validation of these findings is needed. Specifically, complementary assays such as intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, mitochondrial membrane potential changes, and apoptosis-related markers (e.g., Annexin V/PI staining) would offer mechanistic insights into the nature of the observed biocompatibility. Such assays will be prioritized in future work to fully elucidate Aux-CDs’ interaction with mammalian cells [61, 62]. The low toxicity is specific to CDs, and they typically exhibit minimal toxicity at concentrations below 200 µg mL− 1, with a threshold of 80% cell viability observed over 24 h [63].

Growth-promoting behavior

Aux-CDs have demonstrated considerable potential in boosting plant growth. Since agricultural outputs depend on the plants’ ability to perform photosynthesis, CDs, with their photoluminescence properties, can enhance photosynthesis and efficiently harness solar energy, leading to increased crop yields. Besides their photoluminescence, the hydrophilic nature of CDs promotes plant growth by speeding up seed germination and improving water absorption. Additionally, their antimicrobial and antioxidant characteristics help alleviate both biotic and abiotic stresses, thereby enhancing plant health [64]. Consequently, the use of CDs can significantly improve plant growth. CDs can enhance plant growth and development through many mechanisms. Their photoluminescent characteristics improve photosynthetic efficiency, while their antioxidant qualities reduce oxidative stress during germination [65]. Moreover, CDs can augment water absorption and nutrient dispersion, hence enhancing seedling vigor [66]. Li et al. (2018) discovered that carbon dots derived from glucose and graphite rods enhanced seed germination, root elongation, and enzymatic activity in rice plants [67]. Dall Agnol et al. (2021) similarly shown that carbon dots produced from Spirulina promoted lentil seedling development at low concentrations [68]. These findings highlight the promise of CDs as sustainable nanomaterials in agriculture. Furthermore, priming is a process that enhances plants by using biological or chemical agents, boosting growth indicators, and improving stress resistance for successful plant growth from germination to the seedling stage [69]. Treating seeds with nanoparticles and then drying them can enhance stress resistance during germination by creating nanopores, increasing the expression of aquaporin genes, changing seed metabolism, and boosting oxidative respiration [69, 70]. Nanopriming facilitates quick water absorption by seeds and improves endosperm metabolism [71]. Additionally, nanopriming boosts oxidative respiration, leading to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that initiate metabolic processes linked to germination. Superoxide dismutase catalyzes the conversion of O2 to H2O2, allowing interaction with the phytohormone gibberellic acid (GA), which promotes embryo growth and seed germination [69, 72]. Our study used Aux-CDs as priming agents to improve wheat seed performance. Our results indicated that the effect of CDs varied depending on dose, variety, and trait measured. In the Drum variety, 1 µg/mL Aux-CDs significantly increased root length and germination percentage compared with the control, whereas other parameters, including seedling length and shoot length, showed no significant differences. In the Ehsan variety, no statistically significant changes were observed for any measured parameter. At 10 µg/mL, both varieties exhibited numerical decreases in growth parameters, with substantial reductions in some traits for Drum (Table 2 Supplementary, Fig. 5a–d). However, not all observed differences at 1 µg/mL were statistically significant when compared with the control group. The length of 1 µg/mL Aux-CDs-treated Drum wheat seedlings was not significantly different from the free Auxin (285 µM IAA) treatment (p = 0.09). Although the IAA concentration was chosen to approximate the estimated Auxin content in the Aux-CDs, the actual Auxin content of the nanoparticles was not chemically quantified; therefore, precise molar equivalence between the two treatments cannot be confirmed.

Bars indicated with the same letter are not significantly different (P < 0.05, Tukey test). Values are the mean ± SD. (e) Fluorescence image of seedlings under UV light (365 nm)

Previous investigations have demonstrated analogous concentration-dependent effects. Dall Agnol et al. (2021) revealed that modest concentrations of Spirulina-derived carbon dots (0.05–0.1 mg/mL) markedly enhanced lentil seedling growth, whereas elevated quantities exhibited reduced efficacy [68]. Similarly, Li et al. (2018) indicated that carbon dots produced from glucose and graphite rods improved seed germination and enzymatic activity in rice; however, elevated concentrations resulted in inhibited growth due to oxidative stress [67]. These findings partially align with our data, where 1 µg/mL Aux-CDs produced statistically significant root length and germination percentage improvements in the Drum and Ehsan varieties. Furthermore, the seed performance index in the higher concentration (10 µg/mL) compared to the low concentration (1 µg/mL) may be due to nanoparticle agglomeration at higher doses, which is able to inhibit water imbibition in seeds and create a physical barrier over the seed surface. High concentrations of CDs are also known to induce oxidative stress through the generation of ROS, which causes damage to cellular homeostasis and inhibits normal germination and growth processes. Dose-dependent such a type of phytotoxicity has also been found in previous nanomaterials and plant system research. Collectively, our results suggest that Aux-CDs nanopriming may have an optimum concentration range for specific wheat genotypes, beyond which adverse effects can occur.

The roots of the plants showed remarkable uptake of the Aux-CDs under UV-Vis light. In particular, the roots emitted an intense blue light, indicating a significant uptake of these nanoparticles (Fig. 5e). Pete et al. confirmed that CD-treated seeds exhibit blue-green fluorescence in seeds and growing parts, confirming their absorption without functionalization [73]. Nanoparticles support seed absorption, elimination of reactive oxygen species, biosynthesis of gibberellic acid, and conversion of starch, which facilitates embryo growth. The presence of multiple layers of parenchyma cells may lead to an increased accumulation of nanoparticles in the seed coat, which could impair embryo growth. The concentration in the seed coat could hinder this process [71]. In a study by Lucas Dall Agnol et al., carbon dots were synthesized from spirulina, a plant biomass source, without a surface passivating agent. The final result exhibited a wide range of dissolution properties and was also effective for the growth of lentil seeds at concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 0.1 mg/mL. The reported CDs can potentially be used as a hopeful, sustainable, bio-derived substance for plant species in the realm of agriculture that endures [68]. In another study, Hao Li et al. synthesized CDs from various sources, such as glucose and graphite rods, and studied their effects on the growth of rice plants. The synthesized CDs could infiltrate various components of rice plants, resulting in a remarkable increase in root elongation, seed germination, enzyme activity, seedling length, and carbohydrate production. In addition, CDs can potentially enhance the expression of the thionin gene (Os06g32600), ultimately improving disease resistance in rice plants [67]. It has been shown that CD priming can promote root and shoot growth in a concentration-dependent manner. CD priming can alter Auxin activities and its spatial distribution in roots, suggesting that CD priming may be associated with changes in Auxin activity and its spatial distribution in roots, potentially leading to transcriptional changes of some genes related to Auxin biogenesis and transport, which could affect root growth and development [74]. These possible mechanisms require further validation through targeted molecular and physiological studies. Further research on the effects of CD priming on the germination and growth of wheat under unfavorable conditions such as cold, salt, and drought could confirm its sustainable improvement. These observations could shed light on the potential environmental impacts associated with using such nanoparticles.

Based on the presence of Auxin functional groups on the surface of Aux-CDs, we propose that Aux-CDs have the ability to modulate plant hormone pathways such as natural Auxins. These characteristics make Aux-CDs promising candidates for propelling sustainable agriculture. Our study demonstrates their dual function to promote plant growth and enable bioimaging, while the molecular uptake mechanisms and signaling pathways must be more clearly defined. The Auxin-like behavior exhibited and fluorescence profiles are suggestive of potential Auxin receptor-mediated signaling and nanoparticle uptake. Future research must encompass transcriptome profiling, hormone signaling tests, and uptake pathway blockage to ascertain if Aux-CDs operate through traditional Auxin pathways or elicit novel nanomaterial-specific responses. Molecular dynamics simulations, gene expression analyses analogous to cellular proliferation, and proteome profiling will be essential for elucidating the underlying mechanisms and enhancing the utilization of Auxin-derived carbon dots in agricultural and industrial contexts.

A methodological consideration in this study is that the free IAA treatment, included as a comparator, was prepared at 285 µM, which was calculated from precursor loading to represent approximately 5% of the theoretical maximum Auxin content in the 1 µg/mL Aux-CDs treatment. This value reflects an assumption that most of the Auxin undergoes structural transformation during hydrothermal synthesis, leaving only a small proportion of Auxin-like functional groups on the nanoparticle surface. Because the Auxin content of the Aux-CDs was not directly measured, molar equivalence between free Auxin and Aux-CDs cannot be guaranteed. Therefore, differences observed between these treatments should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect both formulation and dosage effects.

Conclusion

Using Auxin, a plant hormone, to synthesize Aux-CDs represents a significant nanotechnology advancement. Using Auxin, a plant hormone, to synthesize Aux-CDs represents a significant nanotechnology advancement. Can be synthesized using a simple, environmentally friendly, cost-effective hydrothermal method, resulting in nanoparticles with excellent water solubility, small sizes, and high quantum yields. The novelty of this work lies in using Auxin as a carbon source, marking the first time a plant hormone has been utilized to synthesize CDs. Nanoparticles derived from this method are environmentally friendly and possess unique properties due to their biologically relevant precursor. The dual functionality of Aux-CDs as bioimaging agents and plant growth enhancers underscores their potential to bridge the gap between biomedical research and sustainable agriculture. In bioimaging, Aux-CDs exhibit low cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 10 µg/mL and efficient cellular uptake, supporting their promise as potential imaging agents in cancer research. Future studies are needed to confirm these findings with additional assays evaluating oxidative stress, apoptosis, and long-term cellular responses to Aux-CDs exposure. Furthermore, Aux-CDs significantly improved seed germination percentage and the performance index in the Drum and Ehsan varieties in low concentration (1 µg/mL) compared to low concentration (10 µg/mL). These findings highlight the need for genotype-specific evaluations before generalizing Aux-CDs’ potential as nanopriming agents. By optimizing the synthesis process and exploring the application of Aux-CDs in other crops and cancer models, the full potential of these nanoparticles will be unlocked. Ultimately, this work contributes to the growing knowledge on sustainable nanomaterials and their applications, offering a promising avenue for advancing biotechnology and agricultural science.
