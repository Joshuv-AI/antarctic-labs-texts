Transient pacing pulse on the T-wave: What is the mechanism?
Authors: Yuta Sudo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A patient with a dual-chamber pacemaker for sick sinus syndrome was hospitalized for heart failure. The electrocardiography (ECG) during hospitalization displayed seemingly abnormal pacing artifacts. This report focuses on the problem-solving of an abnormal-looking paced ECG and identifies the pacemaker's operational behavior as the underlying reason.

Case

An 82-year-old woman, with an Ingenio™ (Boston Scientific, Natick, MA, USA) dual-chamber pacemaker implanted 9 years prior to presentation for sick sinus syndrome, was hospitalized for heart failure. The waveform of the monitored electrocardiography (ECG) during hospitalization is shown in Fig. 1. The patient was asymptomatic during the study period. Device interrogation performed on the day of hospitalization showed no problems with the lead parameters; however, ventricular-atrial (VA) conduction of approximately 430 ms was observed during ventricular pacing (VP). Discussion

The monitored ECG in Fig. 1 can be divided into two types, when classified based on pacemaker behavior. Only VP pulses were observed in the first five and the last three beats. It was challenging to identify the P-wave; this is likely the behavior of atrial sensing (AS)-VP. In addition to the VP pulse, another pacing pulse, designated as pulse X, which was found to overlap with the T-wave of the previous beat (Fig. 2A), was observed in the intervening seven beats. The issue in this case pertained to the identity of pulse X and understanding the mechanism through which it was generated. The interval between pulse X and the VP pulse was 400 ms. If pulse X were assumed to be a VP, the ventricular pacing rate would be 150 bpm, which is impossible because it exceeds the maximum tracking rate of 120 bpm. Moreover, the possibility of pulse X being atrial pacing (AP) seemed unlikely due to the atrioventricular (AV) delay exceeding the programmed value of a maximum of 300 ms. The long-time recording of the monitored ECG was crucial (Fig. 2B). A closer look revealed that this phenomenon occurred at regular intervals and time periods. In other words, it was highly unlikely to be random or temporary, but in fact an outcome of the preprogrammed function of the device.Fig. 2Regular (A) and prolonged (B) monitored electrocardiography (ECG) (reprint, numbers are in ms). As seen in Fig. 2A, the interventricular interval was approximately 700 ms until VP (4) but changed to 800 ms from VP (5) to VP (12) and then returned to approximately 700 ms. From VP (5) to VP (12), a special function called “AV Search+” was in operation. This function, available in Boston Scientific pacemakers, promotes intrinsic conduction. When this function is turned on, the AV delay is periodically extended to 400 ms for every set of search intervals (32 intervals in this case). If ventricular sensing is not detected within the 8-cycle search period, the AV delay returns to the programmed length. Based on this, Fig. 3 rearranges the marks in Fig. 2A. Up to VP (32), the device behaved as AS-VP with a programmed AV delay. “AV Search+” was triggered by the next atrial event (in this case, the P-wave was presumed to exist near the end of the preceding T-wave), and AV delay was extended up to 400 ms. At this point, according to the programmed lower-rate setting of 75 bpm, VP #1 was inserted at the 800-ms interventricular interval, without the AV delay being extended to 400 ms. From the next beat, the AP timing was therefore 400 ms earlier than the lower-rate interval of 800 ms, allowing the AV delay to be extended to 400 ms. Since no ventricular sensing events occurred within 400 ms of the AP, VP occurred at that time. This was the device's behavior pattern from #2 to #8. AV Search+ then disengaged, and the AV delay returned to the programmed length. Since AV Search+ is repeated every 32 beats, the “band,” as shown in Fig. 2B, is observed in the long-time recording. Naturally, the events observed in this ECG were not abnormal device behaviors. However, in the absence of intrinsic ventricular beats, as in this case, this function should be turned off.Fig. 3Annotated monitored electrocardiography (ECG) (reprint, numbers are in ms). The purpose of promoting intrinsic conduction is to prevent heart failure hospitalization or atrial fibrillation [[1], [2], [3]]. This function is offered by many pacemaker manufacturers, although the names and algorithm details can vary. The primary factor complicating the interpretation of ECG changes caused by this function in this case is the relatively high lower-rate setting of 75 bpm. If the lower rate were set to 60 bpm, this phenomenon would be easier to understand because the AV delay would only be prolonged while the atrial event remained AS. However, in practice, this phenomenon seems complicated because the high lower-rate value causes the atrial event to abruptly change to an AP, and its pacing pulse also overlaps with the preceding T-wave. Other factors that may interfere with the interpretation include difficulty in identifying the P-wave and the variability of the programmed AV delay. To correctly interpret a seemingly complex ECG, it is essential to carefully check the programmed parameters and analyze the waveforms.
