Vitreoretinal Myeloid Cell Heterogeneity: Diverse Roles in Homeostasis, Immune Surveillance, and Pathophysiology
Authors: Colin A Lemire, Jeremy A Lavine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe retina is a central nervous system tissue with immune-privileged status, protected by the blood-retina barrier and maintained by specialized resident tissue macrophages: microglia, hyalocytes, and perivascular macrophages. These cells exhibit specific ontogeny, spatial localization, and immunologic functions. Each population contributes to homeostasis, phagocytosis, and immunoregulation in their local microenvironment. Microglia are the most abundant immune cells in the retina, and their key functions include vascular and synaptic regulation. Hyalocytes are abundant in the vitreous and at the vitreoretinal interface with key functions including vasoregression and maintenance of a clear visual axis. Perivascular macrophages are present along major venules in the superficial retina and may regulate immune cell transendothelial migration. This review summarizes key vitreoretinal myeloid cell functions, compares/contrasts the relative abundances of antigen presentation machinery and T cell costimulatory molecules, and places these expression levels into the context of immune surveillance in the experimental autoimmune uveitis model.Keywords: retina, macrophage, inflammation, antigen presentation, MHCII

Introduction

The eye is a specialized sensory organ that contains unique microenvironments. The compartments of the eye include the cornea, ciliary body, iris, vitreous, retina, and choroid. The cornea is optically clear with organized collagen fibrils and is avascular in homeostatic conditions. The ciliary body anteriorly and the choroid posteriorly are, similar to the cornea, outside the specialized intraocular immune privileged microenvironment. The iris, vitreous, and retina are inside the eye, immune privileged, and protected by the blood-retina barrier (BRB) and/or blood-ocular barrier. As a result of these unique compartments, each of these microenvironments has adapted specific cell populations and mechanisms for managing the inflammatory response.1 The retina is unique among the tissues of the eye because it is a component of the central nervous system and exists behind the specialized BRB. The BRB consists of an outer blood-ocular barrier formed by tight junctions between retinal pigment epithelium cells, and an inner BRB made of tight junctions between vascular endothelial cells with contributions from pericytes, astrocytes, Müller cells, and perivascular macrophages. The BRB excludes most immune cells and is vital for maintaining homeostasis of the neurosensory retina. In this review, we focus on vitreoretinal immune cells including microglia, hyalocytes, and perivascular macrophages and discuss what is known about their roles in homeostasis, physiology, immune surveillance, and pathology.

Vitreoretinal immune cell classification and origin

Within the vitreoretinal compartment of the eye, resident tissue macrophages (RTMs) comprise the most important members of the immune surveillance machinery. These include microglia, which exist within the plexiform layers of the retina parenchyma; hyalocytes, the resident macrophages of the vitreous and vitreoretinal interface; and perivascular macrophages, which reside within the BRB in the superficial vascular plexus.2–4 It is important to discuss the unique properties of these cell types and how to distinguish them.

The RTMs of the retina can be differentiated by expression of characteristic markers (Table 1). Microglia, hyalocytes, and perivascular macrophages all express IBA1, making this a poor choice for discerning macrophage subtypes.5 Microglia can be distinguished by their expression of Tmem119 and P2ry12, and are highly similar to brain microglia in their expression profile.6 Importantly, the expression of these highly specific homeostatic microglia markers can be reduced during disease states.7 Hyalocytes can be identified by their unique expression of CD169.8 Perivascular macrophages and hyalocytes both express Mrc1/CD206 and Pf4, but they can be further discriminated based on their location and morphology.4,5 Hyalocytes generally have an amoeboid morphology and are located on the retinal surface near the inner limiting membrane. Alternatively, perivascular macrophages reside within the vascular sheath of the large venules of the superficial retina and have an elongated appearance at steady state.4

The ontogeny of macrophages is important for understanding how they adapt to their niche and respond to stimuli.9 Historically, determining the origin of retinal macrophages was complicated by experiments that used irradiation to deplete resident populations to study their repopulation. These studies critically did not account for the consequential damage to the BRB from irradiation, and led to the false conclusion that microglia and perivascular macrophages originate from bone marrow progenitors.10 More recent studies using parabiosis and transgenic reporter mice have allowed for more clarity on the question of origin and dynamics. It is established that bone marrow–derived cells do not enter the retina at steady state and microglia are solely derived from the yolk sac and exhibit self-renewal.11–13 Similarly, hyalocytes are long-living products of yolk-sac hematopoiesis and likewise do not rely on circulating monocytes.14 This makes vitreoretinal macrophages unique compared with ocular macrophages in the choroid, cornea, and ciliary body, which can have contributions from fetal progenitors and monocyte-derived cells.1,13,15 The origin and dynamics of perivascular macrophages in the retina has not been firmly established. However, clues do exist in the literature to their origin. Because perivascular macrophages are the major histocompatibility complex class II (MHCII)–expressing cell in the retina by immunofluorescence imaging, and MHCII-expressing cells can be detected by flow cytometry, we can infer the origin of perivascular macrophages from fate mapping based upon retinal MHCII expression.4,16,17 Fate mapping of MHCII+ cells by the Saban group17 found that they are long-lived over a 3-mo period. In agreement, fate mapping vitreoretinal interface macrophages, which include microglia, vitreal hyalocytes, and perivascular macrophages, demonstrates that vitreoretinal surface macrophages are also long-lived over a 1-mo period.5 Although definitive experiments are lacking, it is likely that perivascular macrophages are long-lived, self-replenishing cells like microglia and vitreal hyalocytes, which would agree with known brain perivascular macrophages.18 Lack of bone marrow replenishment during homeostasis may make the retina and vitreous more prone to pathology after BRB breakdown. For example, local injury and infiltration/engraftment of bone marrow–derived macrophages can displace fetal-derived microglia after recovery and lead to an altered immunologic response to a subsequent stimulus.19 Alternatively, even without displacement of localized macrophages, peripheral immune responses can influence yolk sac–derived macrophages. For example, peripheral infectious can epigenetically reprogram long-lived Cx3cr1+ retinal and/or choroidal macrophages, resulting in more proangiogenic responses in the laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (CNV) model of neovascular age-related macular degeneration.20 Thus, the origin and lifespan of perivascular macrophages remains an important unanswered question.

T cell activation and experimental autoimmune uveitis

Immune surveillance in the retina is a tightly regulated process, balancing defense against pathogens with the need to protect delicate neural tissue from immune-mediated damage.21 Under homeostatic conditions, retinal RTMs—including microglia, hyalocytes, and perivascular macrophages—monitor their environment for debris, cellular stress, or signs of infection. Upon encountering foreign antigens or danger signals, these sentinel cells can initiate an innate immune response or potentially activate adaptive immunity by presenting antigen to T cells. In an immune-privileged environment like the retina; however, such responses are actively dampened to prevent excessive inflammation that could wreak havoc on the sensitive tissue and threaten vision. Disruption of this balance can lead to pathological inflammation and irreversible retinal damage, as observed in diseases such as uveitis, diabetic retinopathy, and age-related macular degeneration.22

The ability to mount a strong T cell response depends on multiple stages of signaling, which may be provided by RTMs in the retina. The first signal involves recognition of antigen presented on MHC molecules. The second costimulatory signal is delivered via ligands on antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as CD80 and CD86, that interact with T cell receptors and can have either an activating or inhibitory effect.23 For example, APC CD80 interacting with T cell CD28 will lead to T cell activation and production of proinflammatory cytokines, activating the T cell. However, CD80 interaction with PD-L1 will result in cell cycle arrest, inhibiting the T cell. The third stage of signaling includes cytokines that influence T cell differentiation toward effector phenotypes such as T helper 1 (Th1), Th17, or T regulatory subsets.23 Macrophages in the CNS can upregulate both MHC and costimulatory molecules in response to inflammatory cues; therefore, expression levels may be underestimated in studies analyzing steady state tissues.24

Experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) is a well-established, T cell–mediated model of human autoimmune uveitis. In canonical models, mice are immunized peripherally with interphotoreceptor retinoid-binding protein, leading to T cell priming in lymphoid organs such as the spleen and lymph nodes.25,26 These primed retina-specific T cells then migrate to the eye, where they again encounter antigen, become reactivated, cross the BRB, and trigger intraocular inflammation. Studies have demonstrated that either Th1 or Th17 effector subsets are capable of driving disease, though Th17 cells are generally dominant in the induction of acute EAU.27,28 The heterogeneity of these T cells is notable, with subsets coexpressing IFN-γ and IL-17 exhibiting enhanced migratory capacity across retinal endothelium.29

Despite the importance of T cells in driving retinal inflammation, the precise mechanisms of antigen presentation within the retina remain under investigation. Okunuki et al.30 demonstrated that depletion of all retinal resident macrophages via CSF1R inhibition prevented the development of EAU, implicating these cells in early disease initiation. More recently, Shirahama et al.31 showed that depletion of MHCII from P2ry12- or Tmem119-expressing microglia impaired the amplification phase of disease but not its initiation, suggesting that other resident APCs–such as potentially perivascular macrophages–may be responsible for the local presentation of antigen and reactivation of T cells. An uninvestigated question is whether retinal APCs need to costimulate primed T cells for either disease induction or amplification.

As EAU progresses from acute to chronic phases, the composition and behavior of infiltrating T cells shifts. While Th17 cells dominate early responses, chronic EAU is characterized by the presence of long-lived CD4+ and CD8+ memory T cells in the retina.28,32 Effector memory CD8+ T cells expressing CD69 and CD103 as well as inhibitory receptors such as PD1, indicating that they may act to limit long-term inflammation.32 Notably, adoptively transferred uveitogenic CD4+ memory T cells can home to the retina, breach the BRB, and stimulate inflammation, features that may explain why anti-IL-17 therapies often fail to prevent disease recurrence: they target acute effector cells but not persisting memory subsets.28 The mechanisms by which tissue-resident memory T cells sustain retinal inflammation remain under investigation. They likely include local reactivation by resident APCs, and release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17 and IFN-γ that further recruit and activate innate immune cells.

The EAU model provides a valuable system for evaluating immune surveillance and antigen presentation in the retina. It allows for the study of how T cells are primed and reactivated locally, how the BRB is breached, and how memory T cells are maintained in chronic disease. EAU also provides the means to study the populations of potential APCs in the retina and how they are engaged at different stage of disease. Critical questions that remain to be investigated using EAU include elucidating which cells function as APCs, how cells are involved in costimulation, and whether retinal APCs have the ability to activate naïve T cells or only to reactivate previously primed T cells.

Microglia

In mice, microglia first appear in the retina around embryonal day 16, emerging near the developing blood vessels on the vitreoretinal surface.33 As the retina continues to develop postnatally, microglia migrate, eventually occupying 3 niches in the retinal nerve fiber layer/ganglion cell layer, inner plexiform layer and outer plexiform layer (Fig. 1).5,34 Migration into the retina is also associated with a change in morphology as the early amoeboid microglia change into their mature ramified appearance with many processes.35 Even within the retina, microglia are a heterogeneous population. In the inner plexiform layer, maintenance of microglia is dependent on IL34 binding to the CSF1R.13 This is not true for microglia in the outer plexiform layer, and has not been investigated in the retinal nerve fiber layer/ganglion cell layer, which suggests that these cells have adapted varying functions to suit their specific niches. With aging, microglia migrate toward the outer retina, eventually entering the subretinal space.34 Concurrently, microglia become less ramified with age and the area that their processes can survey becomes smaller.34 These changes may contribute to the decrease in immune privilege in the retina that is seen with aging.

Microglia are highly involved in retinal vasculature formation and regulation of blood vessel dynamics. Retinal blood vessels and microglia are confined to the same layers of the retina, indicative of their close functional relationship. In the developing eye, microglia are vital for adequate vascularization of the retina (Fig. 1).36 Their role in angiogenesis is confirmed in models of oxygen-induced retinopathy. Single cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) has identified glycolytic Spp1+ microglia associated with retinal neovascularization.37 Further, knockout of glycolytic enzymes from myeloid cells decreases retinal neovascularization, demonstrating that glycolytic microglia are associated with and necessary for retinal neovascularization in the oxygen-induced retinopathy model.37,38 Microglia may also have a role in the development of CNV.39,40 Murine studies show that CX3CR1+ cells accumulate in laser-induced CNV lesions and that inhibition of NF-κB and VEGF signaling on these cells reduces neovascularization, demonstrating the importance of macrophages in general because CX3CR1 is a pan-macrophage marker.41,42 Repopulation of retinal microglia following depletion with a CSF1R inhibitor showed that repopulating cells had a less activated phenotype and attenuated CNV,43 suggesting that macrophage origin and age is important. Although these studies provide insight into the role of macrophages in general, targeting cells expressing CX3CR1 or CSF1R encompasses all retinal macrophages, not just microglia. Experiments using mice that can target microglia through Tmem119 or P2ry12 would be necessary to clarify their role in this pathology. Mature microglia extend their processes to interact with blood vessels, creating the neurovascular unit.44 At these microglia-vessel contact points, CX3CR1 signaling mediates vasomodulation and blood flow to the retina.44 Given the importance of disruptions in vascular integrity in many inflammatory conditions, the dynamic interactions between microglia and retinal vasculature point to a key role for these cells in maintaining the integrity of the BRB.

Phagocytosis is another major function of microglia that plays various roles in development and homeostasis. During development, Itgam/CD11b/CR3 expressing microglia near the vitreoretinal surface interact with immature synapses that bind C1q and C3, activating the classical complement pathway to assist in synaptic pruning (Fig. 1).45–48 With aging, subretinal microglia support the RPE through phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments and lipids.49 Subretinal microglia express a unique transcription profile including genes like Lgals3/galectin-3, Trem2, Fabp5, Gpnmb, and Spp1, among others.13 This subretinal microglia transcriptome signature is relevant across disease models, including light damage, sodium iodate, genetic retinal degeneration, and aging.50 Further, TREM2 signaling orchestrates migration of microglia to the subretinal space and induces galectin-3 expression, which are essential for microglia phagocytosis and neuroprotection. Neuroprotective, subretinal microglia are found in patients with age-related macular degeneration both by scRNA-seq, flow cytometry, and immunofluorescence.50 In neovascular age-related macular degeneration, activation of the alternative complement pathway drives CNV and deposition of C3 accompanies microglia migration to the outer retina, indicating a potential role in synapse remodeling after tissue damage.47,51 In diabetic retinopathy, activated microglia colocalize with C3, suggesting that phagocytosis may be occurring.52 Conversely, increased production of Complement factor H by retinal microglia may act to block alternative pathway activation and protect the retina.52 However, these studies did not use microglia-specific markers to differentiate microglia from monocyte-derived macrophages during disease models. Nevertheless, the complex interactions between microglia and the complement pathway warrant further investigation.

At rest, microglia contribute to immune homeostasis by taking on an anti-inflammatory phenotype. The CD200:CD200R axis is particularly important for limiting microglial activation (Fig. 1). Expression of CD200 by retinal axons and endothelial cells reduces NOS2 expression by microglia, suppressing their inflammatory function.53 Homeostatic neuronal signaling via CX3CL1 also suppresses microglia activation (Fig. 1).54 Cx3cr1−/− mice have hyperactivated microglia, impaired vascular integrity, and accelerated retinal degeneration.54,55 However, these studies are limited because other retina-resident macrophages express Cx3cr1. Additionally, CX3CR1 is necessary for microglial movement, and the level of surveillance may be modulated by signaling from neurons and endothelial cells as well as injury status.56 With aging, microglia become less branched and their movements are slower, which may limit their ability to quickly detect and respond to signals.34 Further, with age, expression of CD200R and CX3CR1 by microglia decreases, resulting in heightened activation.57 In addition, microglia begin to express higher levels of C3 and CFB, promoters of the alternative complement pathway.58 Collectively, these age-related changes may cause microglia to take on a more activated phenotype while also influencing the broader retinal microenvironment to be more proinflammatory.

The ability of microglia to function as APCs or transport antigens outside of the retina remains a topic of great interest. In the healthy retina, microglia do not express high levels of MHCII.4,13 RPE-derived TGFβ suppresses microglia MHCII expression and the expression of T cell costimulation molecules such as CD80 and CD86.59 Populations of MHCII+ cells near the optic nerve and periphery that were once thought to be microglia are now recognized to be perivascular macrophages similar to those present at other CNS interfaces.4,22 In agreement, our scRNA-seq data from CD45+ cells of the resting mouse retina and choroid shows that microglia express low levels of MHCII (H2-Ab1, H2-Eb1) compared with other APCs like dendritic cell and macrophage (MacA-E) populations of the choroid (Fig. 2).60 As discussed previously, microglia can costimulate T cells via CD80, CD86, TIM4, CD40, and CD70.23 Steady-state microglia express all of these ligands at very low levels, except CD86 which is moderately expressed, suggesting that microglia may be poor T cell costimulators (Fig. 2). Furthermore, after systemic endotoxin treatment, microglia remain poor expressers of MHCII and CD80, and the low amounts of CD86 reduce even further. In agreement, retinal microglia isolated from endotoxin-exposed mice are poor activators of T cell proliferation compared with splenic macrophages.61 Despite these low levels of MHCII, CD80, and CD86, comparison with brain microglia highlights some important differences. Retinal microglia express higher levels of MHCI (H2-D1, H2-K1), Cd83, Cd74, and Ifi30, which are all important for antigen processing, compared with brain microglia.62 Importantly, after systemic infection with a neurotropic variant of lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, more CD8+ T cell infiltration is observed in the retina than the brain, suggesting functional MHCI antigen cross presentation than is greater in the retina than brain.62 However, retinal microglia express equivalent levels of MHCII, Cd80, Cd86, Timd4, Cd40, and Cd70 to brain microglia, suggesting that MHCII antigen presentation may be equivalent between tissues.62 In the brain, electrical signals from neurons suppresses microglial MHCI and MHCII expression to preserve an anti-inflammatory microenvironment.24 These data suggest that, at baseline, retina microglia are more proinflammatory than the brain, are capable of more antigen cross presentation via MHCI to CD8+ T cells, but they still have diminished antigen presenting and T cell costimulating capabilities compared with professional APCs.

The expression profiles of microglia change significantly during activation, resulting in altered functionality. As discussed previously, elimination of all retinal macrophages with a CSF1R antagonist prevented initiation of EAU, suggesting the importance of a local retinal APC for T cell reactivation.30 Further, depletion of MHCII from P2ry12- or Tmem119-expressing microglia impaired the amplification phase of EAU, but not initiation,31 suggesting that microglia are not the initiators. Overall, these data suggest that microglia must be activated to increase their expression of MHCII and even potentially other T cell costimulatory molecules to further amplify the inflammatory response during EAU. In support, in the brain, primed T cells can cross the blood-brain barrier, can release proinflammatory signals such as IFNγ and TNF, and are capable of inducing expression of MHCI and MHCII as well as costimulatory signals such as CD80 and CD86 expression on resident microglia.24

Hyalocytes

Moving into the vitreous, hyalocytes are the resident macrophages and key players in immune regulation. While their functions are specifically adapted to the environment of the vitreous, given their location at the vitreoretinal interface and their role in immune surveillance, their contribution to vitreoretinal inflammation cannot be overlooked.

Hyalocytes take on multiple functions that contribute to the establishment and maintenance of a clear visual axis, vital for ensuring visual acuity. Hyalocytes are present early in the developing eye, where they are first involved in regression of the hyaloidal vasculature. In human fetal eyes, involution of the hyaloid is evident at 11 wk gestational age, with hyalocytes localized to sites of regression.63 Although the process by which hyalocytes coordinate this event is not completely understood, murine hyalocytes rely on WNT signaling to induce cell death.64 Hyalocyte expression of Wnt7b acts via short range paracrine signaling to induce vascular endothelial cell regression (Fig. 1).64 The function of human and murine hyalocytes in development is also modulated by signals such as Ang2, TGF-β2, HIF-1⍺, and VEGF; however, the exact mechanisms by which they contribute to hyalocyte function have not been fully elucidated.65 Throughout aging, hyalocytes continuously synthesize proteins that make up the clear vitreous body, including collagen and versican.66 Although they do not synthesize hyaluronan, a major component of the extracellular matrix of the vitreous, they do express HYAL2, an enzyme involved in degradation of hyaluronan.67 In clinical samples from patients with proliferative vitreoretinal conditions, IBA1+ myeloid cells that may be hyalocytes have been found to coexpress ⍺-SMA, suggesting a potential role in fibrosis.68

The phagocytosis function of hyalocytes also contributes to the maintenance of a transparent vitreous. Hyalocytes express genes important for phagocytosis including MERTK and CD74 (Fig. 1).66 Similarly, Pf4+ populations in mice express high levels of Cd68, an important gene for phagocytosis function (Fig. 2). This function allows them to engage in erythrophagocytosis and clear vitreous hemorrhage.69

The expression profile of hyalocytes is closely related to microglia, likely reflecting their similar yolk sac ontogenies and potentially functions related to immune surveillance.67 Like microglia, hyalocyte motility relies on KCNK13, a potassium channel, and CX3CR1 as they surveil their environment for debris and pathogens (Fig. 1).3,66 Once they have phagocytosed material, hyalocytes can present antigens to T cells due to their strong expression of MHCII in human samples.3,4 In mice, hyalocytes are ∼5% MHCII+, which is more than microglia but less than macrophages in the choroid.4,70 Hyalocytes could be key contributors to systemic immune tolerance, potentially via MHCII. Inoculation of antigen into the vitreous results in systemic immune tolerance to that antigen, a finding termed vitreous cavity–associated immune deviation.71 Further, injection of GFP+ peritoneal macrophages into the vitreous cavity leads to the presence of GFP+ macrophages in the spleen, and splenic natural killer T cells were necessary for vitreous cavity–associated immune deviation. The recent discovery of lymphatic drainage between the vitreous chamber and the deep cervical lymph nodes via the optic nerve sheath may explain how hyalocytes or antigen can traffic outside of the eye.72

The expression profile of hyalocytes also sheds light on how they contribute to immune privilege in the eye. They express factors including POMC, TGFB2, CD46, and CD86, which prevent T cell and complement activation (Fig. 1).66 POMC (proopiomelanocortin), which is a precursor to MSH (melanocyte-stimulating hormone), and TGFβ2 can induce T regulatory cells; in agreement, POMC, MSH, and TGFβ2 have been found in the aqueous humor, potentially produced by hyalocytes.73 CD46 is found in the vitreous and has a role in suppression of the alternative complement pathway.74 Our own scRNA-seq data in mice show that Pf4+ populations likely containing hyalocytes express low levels of T cell costimulation factors such as Cd80 and Cd70 but equivalent levels of Cd86, Timd4, and CD40 to both choroidal macrophages and dendritic cells (Fig. 2). Importantly, this expression profile agrees with multiparameter flow cytometry of choroidal macrophages which shows low CD80 and high CD86.61 This expression profile demonstrates that hyalocytes at baseline can directly contribute to a microenvironment in the vitreous that is protective against inflammation but also are capable of antigen presentation and T cell activation at intermediate levels between poor activators like microglia and choroidal macrophages, which express equivalent CD80 and CD86 to hyalocytes but higher MHCII. However, these are inferences based on messenger RNA and flow cytometry data; direct comparisons of T cell activation comparing choroidal macrophages and hyalocytes would be necessary to validate these expression profiles.

The balance between immune suppression and activation can be altered during disease, when inflammation in the vitreous is destructive. Similar to changes in microglia with aging, hyalocyte density increases significantly in aged mice and may contribute to chronic low level inflammation in the eye.8 Experimental models of uveitis also show evidence of hyalocyte activation and proliferation.8 Despite the potential role of hyalocyte dysfunction in ocular pathology, our understanding of their role in inflammation remains incomplete. Continued exploration of hyalocytes role in surveillance of the vitreous and how they contribute to immunoregulation in the retina will provide a clearer picture of the complex neuroimmune interactions of the eye.

Perivascular macrophages

Perivascular macrophages represent a third population of RTMs in the vitreoretinal space. In the central nervous system, perivascular macrophages take on many functions in health and disease, serving as sentinels at the blood-tissue barrier, maintaining homeostasis through their involvement in immune privilege, and recruiting leukocytes during inflammation. Perivascular macrophages in the brain have been implicated in the pathogenesis of stroke, Parkinson’s disease, insulin resistance, Alzheimer’s disease, and hypertension, making them a prime therapeutic target and the subject of extensive ongoing research.75 In the eye, perivascular macrophages have been less well characterized, but given the similarities between the brain and retina, their role in homeostasis and dysfunction deserves careful inquiry.

Perivascular macrophages in the retina were first described by Mendes-Jorge et al.76 as a unique, nonmicroglia population of macrophages that moved along blood vessels and phagocytosed blood-borne tracers without compromising the integrity of the BRB. Importantly, perivascular macrophages differ from microglia in that they continuously express Msr1/CD204/SR-A (scavenger receptor A) rather than only after activation, suggesting that they are better at detecting some antigens in the healthy retina (Fig. 1).76 Perivascular macrophages are also found to exist in thinner areas of the basement membrane, potentially allowing them greater access to circulating ligands.76 During damage to the BRB or photoreceptor degeneration, autofluorescent macrophages that could be either perivascular macrophages or monocyte-derived macrophages migrate to the site of injury.76 Their function in these situations remains unclear, although it has been suggested that they may be exercising an immunosuppressive role to limit inflammatory activation.

The niche of retinal perivascular macrophages is mostly contained to the vascular sheath around the large venules of the inner retina.4,5 As another distinction from microglia, perivascular macrophages are ∼15% MHCII+ and enriched for the expression of genes involved in immune cell chemotaxis.4 In a model of retinal inflammation with intravitreal CCL2 injections, perivascular macrophages are associated with and necessary for extravasation of Ly6C+ monocytes into the retina.4 This suggests that perivascular macrophages have a role in regulating transendothelial migration of immune cells, in agreement with their location near thinner areas of the vascular sheath basement membrane (Fig. 1).

Based on their scavenger function, expression of MHCII, and location at the BRB, perivascular macrophages are a prime candidate to be a prominent APC in the retina. Like hyalocytes that express a transcriptome consistent with intermediate T cell activation, Pf4+ perivascular macrophages in mice express low levels of Cd80 and Cd70, but equivalent levels of Cd86, Timd4, and CD40 to choroidal macrophages and dendritic cells (Fig. 2). Because perivascular macrophages express more MHCII than hyalocytes but less MHCII than choroidal macrophages, and express equivalent levels of T cell costimulatory molecules to hyalocytes and choroidal macrophages, it is likely that perivascular macrophages are more capable of antigen presentation than hyalocytes but less than choroidal macrophages. Specifically in the context of EAU, perivascular macrophages may represent the population of RTMs that is necessary to reactivate circulating primed retina-specific T cells.31 Further study of the role perivascular macrophages have in regulating initiation of inflammation and direct T cell activation are warranted.

Insight into the function of retinal perivascular macrophages may be gleaned from studying their analogous counterparts in the brain. As components of the blood-brain barrier, perivascular macrophages provide immune surveillance and present antigen to T cells in a variety of CNS conditions.77,78 They have also been shown to possess key roles in regulating innate and adaptive immune responses. In stroke, perivascular macrophages increase vascular permeability and immune cell recruitment.79 In experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, a model of multiple sclerosis in the brain that is analogous to EAU, perivascular macrophages have a supportive role in early development of disease. They have been shown to reactivate encephalitogenic T cells that enter the CNS, initiating local disease.80 These findings suggest that retinal perivascular macrophages may similarly have a key role in reactivating primed T cells during EAU and other inflammatory conditions.

Conclusions

Immune surveillance in the retina represents a delicate balance between vigilance and restraint. As a tissue with high functional specialization and limited regenerative capacity, the retina relies on a unique immune environment that is equipped to detect threats while minimizing bystander damage. RTMs, including microglia, hyalocytes, and perivascular macrophages, are central to this surveillance, continuously monitoring the retinal microenvironment for signs of infection, injury, or dysregulated homeostasis.

The ability of these sentinels to remain quiescent or become activated in a context-dependent manner is a defining feature of immune privilege. However, this equilibrium is disrupted in an array of pathologies that result in tissue damaging and sight threatening inflammation. EAU serves as a critical model for studying how immune surveillance mechanisms can trigger chronic inflammatory activation. Recent studies have underscored the importance of specific macrophage subsets in shaping the local immune response and suggest that nonmicroglial resident macrophages may initiate the earliest stages of T cell activation within the retina.

Despite these advances, major questions remain. How are autoantigens presented under homeostatic versus inflammatory conditions? Which retinal cells provide the full battery of signals required for pathogenic T cell activation? How is surveillance altered with age or in chronic autoimmune conditions? A deeper understanding of how immune surveillance operates in the retina will be essential for designing interventions that preserve immune protection without provoking vision-threatening inflammation. As we uncover the molecular and cellular circuits of retinal immune surveillance, we move closer to therapies that can restore or reinforce immune balance in both autoimmune and degenerative eye disease.
