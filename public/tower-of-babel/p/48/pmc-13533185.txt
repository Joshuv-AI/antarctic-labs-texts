Ulnar variance in youth sport
Authors: Noah M Scigliano, Ignacio Garcia Fleury, Terry L Hayes, Joseph A Buckwalter V
Published: 2026

Introduction

Trends toward early specialization in youth sports have resulted in higher volumes of activity with higher rates of injury [1], particularly for overuse injury [2]. Prevalence of injury is cited as high as 40%–65% [3, 4], a majority related to overuse rather than acute trauma. Repetitive microtrauma is of particular concern in skeletally immature athletes as the growing skeleton is susceptible to epiphyseal stress injury, which may result in growth disturbance, deformity, and premature physeal arrest requiring surgical intervention [5]. Prevalence of epiphyseal stress injury at the distal radius ranges anywhere from 10% to 83% [6–16] in youth athletes competing in a variety of youth sports [15], whether symptomatic [8, 9, 11] or not [10, 12].

Among sports that use the upper extremity as weight-bearing limbs, such as gymnastics and platform diving, repetitive axial loading across the wrist during skeletal immaturity places the distal radial-epiphyseal-physeal-metaphyseal complex at risk for stress-related injury. In this population, wrist pain is common, with reported pooled prevalence reaching 53% among young gymnasts, and acute vs chronic wrist injuries reported in 34% and 36% of athletes, respectively [17]. These repetitive compressive forces during periods of active growth can disrupt normal physeal chondrocyte organization and endochondral ossification, leading to stress-related changes of the distal radial physis.

This injury pattern is commonly referred to as “Gymnast wrist” (Fig. 1), and it represents a spectrum of pathology often described in three stages: (i) wrist pain without radiographic abnormality, (ii) physeal injury indicating stress injury, and (iii) resultant positive ulnar variance (PUV) or other growth deformity [17, 18]. This resultant ulnar-positive alignment is hypothesized to increase load transmission across the ulnocarpal joint and predispose athletes to ulnar-sided wrist pathology, including ulnar impaction syndrome and triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) injury (Fig. 2), which may require surgery in severe cases [6, 19].

Ulnar variance (UV) is a radiographic measurement between the distal articular surfaces of the radius and ulna on standard posteroanterior radiographs. When the relative heights of the distal radius and ulna are equal or within 1 mm of each other, this is termed neutral UV. When the distal articular surface of the ulna is longer than the radius by greater than 1 mm, this is termed PUV (Fig. 3), and when the radius is equally longer, negative UV (Fig. 4) [20]. Although the exact values are dependent on measurement modality [20]. As this bony relationship changes, so do the transmission of forces through the radius and ulna in cadaveric studies. For example, in ulnar-negative wrists, the radius and ulna bear 94% and 6% of weight, compared with 69% and 31% in ulnar-positive wrists [21].

Considering the potential biomechanical impact of PUV and its association with overuse injury in young athletes, several studies [6–9,22–24] have sought to evaluate relationships between UV and biological profiles, training characteristics, skeletal maturity, and wrist pain/injury. This systematic review aims to clarify the prevalence and significance of UV in youth athletes, identify proposed mechanisms for its development, and examine factors associated with its magnitude.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review was performed in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines (Fig. 5) [25]. Comprehensive search strategies were developed by the authors and a health sciences librarian. Index and keyword methods were utilized to develop search strategies for the following databases: PubMed, CINAHL, EMBASE, and SPORTDiscus. No pre-established database filters were used other than an English language filter. Searches were finalized in November 2025, including all literature prior to 23 October 2025. The full PubMed search strategy (Table 1) was adapted for other databases. References of included studies were analyzed in addition to database searches for further article review.

Eligibility criteria

Articles were pooled from each database search and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were reviewed by two authors (N.M.S., T.L.H.), and a pooled list was created for full-text review. Authors reviewed full texts for qualitative review dependent on article record of UV in a youth population that participates in recreational or competitive sport. Any disagreements for study inclusion were discussed with and resolved by a senior author (J.A.B.V.). Case reports, case series, commentaries, letters, reviews, editorials, abstracts, and non-English studies were excluded. Additional grounds for exclusion were studies that recorded ulnar variance in non-athlete or adult populations. If age was not specified for specific patients/participants in relevant literature, the paper was excluded.

Data extraction and analysis

Extracted data from each article included study design, patient number, sport, average/range chronological age, average/range skeletal age, average training hours per week, image capture, UV measurement style, definition of UV, results, and conclusions. Descriptive statistics were performed using Microsoft Excel.

Quality assessment

To assess the quality of studies and reduce any potential risk of bias, the Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) [26] tool was used for any cross-sectional and retrospective observational studies. The Newcastle-Ottawa scale [27] was implemented for the single prospective cohort study included in the review. Study quality was assessed by two authors (N.M.S., T.L.H.).

Results

Study selection

A total of 432 articles were extracted for review after combining database searches and excluding duplicates. After title/abstract screening, 368 articles were excluded. Upon full-text review, 10 articles were identified that met full criteria for inclusion into the study. An additional article was identified during full text review that existed outside of search parameters, yielding a total of 11 articles for inclusion into this systematic review.

Methodological quality assessment

All studies that were appraised demonstrated adequate quality for inclusion into this review. AXIS scores, for cross-sectional and retrospective observational studies, ranged from low to moderate risk of bias. The most common reasons that introduced risk for bias in these studies were a lack of power size calculation and convenience sampling involved in the study design. The single prospective study evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale scored 7/9 stars, which would indicate moderate/high quality. Stars were deducted in this study for absence of a non-exposed cohort and incomplete adjustment for confounders.

Study characteristics

Eight observational, cross-sectional studies and one prospective study were identified for analytic review. Two retrospective studies were included for descriptive review, yielding a total of eleven studies in this systematic review. Included studies and relevant extracted data can be found in Table 2.

Generalities and main results of included studies

Imaging and measurement

Posteroanterior radiographs were utilized for image capture in all studies, and UV was measured primarily using Hafner’s method [28] for immature wrists and Palmer’s method (method of concentric circles) [29] for mature wrists. The method of perpendiculars [30] was utilized in two studies [6, 31], one of which [31] cited for its “high inter-rater reliability” as rationale for implementation. However, neither study specifically justified selection of this technique vs Hafner’s or Palmer’s method.

Age-related

The number of patients in each study ranged from 23 to 261, of which all included gymnasts. Across included studies, excluding duplicate patient databases, the average chronological age was 13.6 years, and average training regimen was 16.1 hours per week (n = 601) weighted by sample size.

Cross-sectional vs. longitudinal research

Of included studies, all were cross-sectional or retrospective observational in design except for one prospective cohort study [32]. Two retrospective case studies [31, 33] were excluded from analytic review due to limited overlap with participant and training variables analyzed in other studies, however, they were retained within the overall review to support clinical relevance and treatment of symptomatic PUV.

Anthropometric comparisons (height, weight, sex, bone age)

Anthropometric and maturational variables evaluated across studies included chronological age, skeletal age, height, weight, body composition, lean body mass, and skeletal maturity [8, 9, 23, 24, 32, 34].

Other comparisons (e.g. wrist pain, grip strength)

Additional comparisons with UV values included presence of wrist pain, handgrip strength, hand dominance, and side-to-side value differences [6, 8, 9, 22, 24, 32]. Associations between UV and wrist pain and/or training characteristics were evaluated in multiple studies [6, 8, 9, 22, 24, 32], while grip strength comparisons were specifically analyzed by Amaral et al. [22, 24] and DiFiori et al. [9]

Synthesis of results

Frequency of excessive PUV was inconsistently defined across studies due to heterogeneous measurement techniques and thresholds. Given this, we defined excessive PUV based on values greater than +2 mm to extrapolate prevalence of excessive PUV in these studies. Even then, frequency of excessive PUV could not be extrapolated in two studies [31, 33]. Although the specific percentage of subjects with PUV >2 mm could not be identified in an additional two studies [6, 32], 8.2% of wrists were found to have PUV >3 mm in the unfused physis group of Chang et al. (1995) [6], and the range for PRPR (the distance between the most proximal point of the radial and ulnar metaphyses) and DIDI (distance between the most distal point of the radial metaphysis and ulnar metaphysis) at second measurement in Amaral et al. (2014) [32] did exceed 2 mm. Zero percent of subjects exhibited excessive PUV at first measurement in Amaral et al. (2014) [32], as well as in four additional studies [8, 9, 22, 24]. The rest of included studies found prevalence of PUV to be between 0% and 50% of their sample, which is further highlighted in Table 2.

Five cross-sectional studies [7–9, 22, 23] reported UV values that were more positive compared to age-predicted norms [28]. For example, DiFiori et al. (1997) [8] found gymnasts in their group to have a mean UV of −1.1 mm compared to −2.3 mm in nongymnasts, and DeSmet et al. (1994) [7] found significant differences in both adult (−0.43 mm nongymnasts vs +1.9 mm gymnasts) and 14–15 year old subjects (see Table 2). One study [24] found the opposite, where UV values were more negative (PRPR-left-2.4 mm, PRPR-right −2.8 mm) than age-predicted norms (−2.2 mm). Considering chronological age, two studies [22, 32] found a positive correlation with UV; meanwhile, three studies [7–9] reported no significant associations between the two variables. Similarly, three studies [23, 32, 34] found positive correlations between UV and skeletal age.

Many articles included this review investigated UV and its relationship with biological/training characteristics, wrist pain, and other anthropometric variables. Although hypothesized by many that training characteristics would be associated with UV, only one study [6] found a tendency for increasing PUV with more years of training. Seven studies [7–9,22–24, 32] found no significant differences between UV and training characteristics/volume. Although Amaral et al. (2014) [32] did find a shift towards less negative UV over an 18-month period in their longitudinal cohort, this change in UV was not consistently associated with training volume. Four studies found associations between anthropometric variables and UV. Amaral et al. (2015) [24] saw an inverse relationship between total body fat percentage and UV, Claessens et al. (1996) [23] reported increased risk for ulnar positivity in gymnasts who are relatively tall with a high lean body mass, De Smet et al. (1994) [7] found positive correlations between UV and height/weight, and Amaral et al. (2014) [32] saw more significant UV change with higher weight and initial BMI.

No studies included in this review found significant associations between UV and wrist pain [6, 8, 9, 24]. However, DiFiori et al. (2002) [9] demonstrated that distal radial physeal stress injury, rather than UV, was independently associated with wrist pain. Lastly, three studies [8, 9, 22] reported differences in UV between intra-individual wrists, none of which associated with hand dominance.

Two retrospective case series evaluated management of symptomatic PUV utilizing different surgical techniques: ulnar epiphysiodesis [31] vs ulnar shortening osteotomy [33].

Discussion

Stress on the wrist through repetitive weight-bearing activity can lead to stress injury of the distal radial growth plate complex, which may lead to localized growth arrest and subsequent PUV [6]. Whether it is radial epiphyseal growth termination, relative outpacing of ulnar epiphyseal growth, or genetic predisposition for select anatomical alignment, the development of PUV in youth athletes is likely multifactorial, influenced by both mechanical and biologic pathways. While the mechanism is still up for debate, literature in this review demonstrates a significant difference in UV values for those of youth athletes and age-matched nonathlete cohorts.

Amaral et al. (2012) [22] investigated UV in skeletally immature female gymnasts and its relation to biological and training characteristics. After grouping gymnasts by skill level, they found a trend towards more PUV in elite gymnasts, competition level as defined by the Portuguese Federation of Gymnastics, compared to both nonelite gymnast subgroups. The UV measures in these groups also became more positive with increasing age, and on average were significantly more positive than nonathlete matched cohorts. Despite this, they found no significant differences in UV and training characteristics of the gymnasts. They did, however, find significant, but low correlations between DIDI-L (distance between the most distal point of the radial metaphysis and ulnar metaphysis in the left wrist) and skeletal age, stature, and body fat mass percentage, supporting the hypothesis that gymnasts who are taller, heavier, with greater training regimens have more PUV [22]. Lastly, they found significant intra-personal differences in UV and selected hand dominance by the first hand to reach contact with the ground during a cartwheel maneuver, supported in later biomechanical investigations as a gymnastics dominant hand due to sports-specific loading behaviors [35]. Although significant differences were not found when comparing hours of training with UV, evidence of intra-personal differences in UV on a dominantly utilized wrist for gymnastics training further supports the idea of repetitive compression resulting in potential physeal arrest and altered biomechanics about the wrist.

On the other hand, Amaral et al. (2015) [24] found more negative UV values in their cohort of 23 Portuguese skeletally immature male gymnasts compared to Hafner reference populations [28]. No significant differences were identified between UV and biological characteristics, training regimen, and wrist pain. Hand grip strength was surprisingly higher in painful wrists compared with painless wrists. This is to say that UV alone, without coexisting ulnar-sided pathology, is insufficient to explain wrist pain in this population.

Using data from the 1987 Artistic World Gymnastics Championships, Claessens et al. (1996) [23] and De Smet et al. (1994) [7] also found no significant association between UV and training duration in elite female gymnasts. Ulnar variance values were significantly more positive in 14–15-year-old gymnasts and at the upper limit of normal for younger ages compared with nonathlete populations. A significant, positive relationship existed between UV and skeletal maturity. While training exposure was not associated with UV, taller stature and physique-related characteristics consistent with greater lean body mass were associated with more PUV. These findings parallel those of Amaral et al. (2015) [24], who reported an inverse relationship between fat percentage and ulnar variance. Collectively, suggesting that intrinsic anthropometric and body composition factors may influence ulnar variance during skeletal maturation. While body composition was not assessed by De Smet et al. (1994) [7], they did find that UV correlated significantly with height and weight, indicating body size contributes to ulnar variance.

Buenen et al. (1999) [34] further explored the relationship between UV and skeletal maturation in this specific cohort. Gymnasts with PUV greater than 2 mm demonstrated significantly greater skeletal maturity and advanced ulnar maturation compared with those with UV less than 2 mm. However, no significant differences were observed in radial skeletal maturity, suggesting that PUV may occur independently of radial maturation. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, a causal relationship between repetitive compression, physeal arrest, and PUV cannot be inferred by this data alone, especially without controlling for individual training regimen/loadbearing frequency.

No significant associations between UV and wrist pain, chronological age, and training regimen were highlighted in nonelite gymnast cohorts studied by Difiori et al. (1997, 2002) [8, 9]. Wrist pain was highly prevalent and associated with distal radial physeal injury in a non-elite youth gymnast cohort [9]. Suggesting that distal radial physeal stress, and not UV positivity, could be a primary driver for ulnar-sided wrist pain in athletes with regular, severe upper extremity loading patterns. Furthermore, Chang et al. (1995) [6] found similar nonsignificant associations between the two variables, suggesting that PUV alone does not induce wrist pain. However, this study did demonstrate increasing ulnar positivity with years of training (23.6% of wrists in 1st year, 81% of wrists in 8th year), which may reflect the specific loading patterns experienced in this cohort.

In the only prospective study identified for review, Amaral et al. (2014) [32] found that both PRPR (the distance between the most proximal point of the radial and ulnar metaphyses) and DIDI became more positive in a 25 Portuguese gymnast cohort over an 18-month training period. Ulnar variance also demonstrated correlations with anthropometric values (weight, height, BMI, handgrip strength), skeletal maturity, and years of practice, but not weekly training volume. Thus, even prospective measurement of UV could not identify a dose–response relationship with loading behavior in young gymnasts.

A consistent theme emerges from these investigations. Youth athletes, specifically gymnasts, exhibit more PUV than nonathlete peers; however, this anatomic difference lacks a clear relationship with wrist pain or training exposure. Thus, implying a multifactorial framework in which genetic predisposition and repetitive loading behaviors interact to influence UV, rather than a simplistic, single causative model.

Notably, UV values in all of the included studies were measured under static, resting conditions on posteroanterior radiographs. However, UV is dynamic and has been shown to become more positive under both partial [36] and full axial loading conditions [35, 37], with increased degree of change in dominant extremities [37], pronated gripping [37], and patients with wrist pain [36]. A biomechanic observational study [35] also found that for every 1 mm increase in ulnar positivity, a 2.8% increase in maximum force was applied in the hypothenar region, which could explain ulnar-sided wrist pain during active vs resting conditions. Together, this suggests that static conditions may underestimate UV findings and their associations with training intensity and wrist pain. It also underscores the importance of using dynamic image captures, such as load-bearing computed tomography, to study unique pathology affected by load-bearing.

While often asymptomatic, PUV may contribute to a variety of pathologic syndromes about the wrist joint, including ulnar impaction syndrome, TFCC injury, and gymnast’s wrist. Regardless of the underlying etiology of PUV, Campbell et al. (2022) [31] demonstrated that ulnar epiphysiodesis successfully terminates ulnar growth in pediatric patients with symptomatic PUV secondary to premature distal radial physeal closure. In their cohort of 30 patients, epiphysiodesis was successful in 93.5% of cases, with 90.3% of patients reporting complete resolution of symptoms and an average UV correction of 3.1 mm at final follow-up. An alternate surgical treatment for symptomatic UV is ulnar shortening osteotomy. In a study by Fishman et al. (2018) [33], seven patients with TFCC injury and coexisting PUV were successfully treated with ulnar shortening osteotomy and arthroscopic TFCC repair, allowing for quick return to sport at preoperative levels. While studied in a limited patient population, these studies suggest that when PUV becomes symptomatic, often in the setting of neighboring structural pathology, joint leveling procedures or growth modulation can improve symptoms and get youth athletes back to competition in reasonable timeframes. However, PUV noticed incidentally in the absence of symptoms or related structural pathology should not necessitate surgical intervention in these athletes. Furthermore, conservative, nonoperative management is often performed in youth athletes discontinuing participation in competitive sport or those without pain [33, 38].

Limitations

This review has several strengths. The first is the comprehensive multi-database search strategy performed by the authors in collaboration with a references services librarian, to ensure capture of relevant literature on a relatively niche concept. This review not only looks at PUV in youth athletes, but also its relationship with several different variables that may impact its development. Inclusion of several different study types also allows for broadened understanding of PUV prevalence, suggested mechanisms, and potential treatment strategies.

Several limitations exist within this literature review. It is limited by available literature on UV in youth athletes, in particular PUV in athletes who participate in sports outside of gymnastics. Besides one prospective cohort identified [32], all studies were observational, cross-sectional designs. Thus, providing only a “snapshot” of injury prevalence. This precludes the ability to infer causation between repetitive loading/training behavior, UV, and wrist pain. Also, individual risk profiles were not able to be determined; for example, Chang et al. (1995) [6] state that 8.2% of wrists had >3 mm PUV, although the specific individual values are not given. Measurement techniques for UV differed, and strict definitions for positive vs negative UV were not always stated [8, 9,31–34]. Three studies utilized the same cohort data [7, 23, 34], and many included small sample sizes [8, 9, 22, 24,31–33]. Finally, as highlighted, UV was measured in static conditions, which may not completely reflect structural anatomy and resultant biomechanic load transfer about the wrist joint under load.

Future research

Future investigations in youth athletes should utilize both static and resting conditions to measure UV with standardized measurement techniques. Additional longitudinal, prospective studies may help further delineate the relationship between PUV and age, wrist pain, and multiple anthropometric variables. In these future studies, we suggest inclusion of individual participant data for better understanding of individual PUV risk profiles.

In conclusion, youth elite athletes have more PUV values on average than non-elite athletes, whom both have more PUV values compared to age-matched nonathlete cohorts; however, there is not a significant relationship between PUV and wrist pain or training volume. Ulnar variance becomes more positive over time in youth athletes due to a multifactorial framework of genetic predisposition and repetitive loading behaviors, and is associated with several pathologic wrist conditions. Chronological age, skeletal age, and anthropometric variables such as body mass show inconsistent correlations with UV values [8, 9, 24]. Newer evidence suggests the role of dynamic measurement modalities to measure UV, which may better reflect wrist anatomy under weight-bearing conditions and help prevent ulnar-sided pathology in young athletes subject to repetitive upper extremity loading in their sport [35, 37].
