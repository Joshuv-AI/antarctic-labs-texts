Mid-Term Experience With 1,196 Ozaki Procedures
Authors: Shigeyuki Ozaki, Yasuhiro Hoshino, Shinya Unai, Serge C Harb, William C Frankel, Hiromasa Hayama, Mikio Takatoo, Nagaki Kiyohara, Masami Goda, Shinichiro Shimura, Lars G Svensson, Jeevanantham Rajeswaran, Eugene H Blackstone, Gösta B Pettersson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntroduced in 2007, the Ozaki procedure-templated cusp replacement with autologous pericardium—is a new option for treating aortic valve disease. Mid-term outcomes and valve performance have not yet been reported.ObjectivesThis study assesses longitudinal hemodynamics, left ventricular reverse remodeling, reoperation, and survival in the original Ozaki cohort.Methods1,196 patients underwent Ozaki procedures (60% male, age 11-90 years) at Toho University Ohashi Medical Center from April 2007 to May 2021: 2.4% had infective endocarditis, 27% had bicuspid valves, 54% had aortic stenosis, 24% had aortic regurgitation, 7.3% had mixed lesions, and 46% underwent concomitant procedures. Clinical outcomes, echocardiograms, and follow-up data (50% followed >3.2 years, 10% >9 years) were analyzed using nonlinear mixed-effects regression for valve performance and time-to-event analyses for reoperation and mortality.ResultsMean cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic clamp times for isolated Ozaki procedures were 151 ± 37 and 105 ± 29 minutes, respectively. Thirty-day mortality was 1.7%, new stroke occurred in 2.6%, new dialysis in 4.0%, and permanent pacemakers were implanted in 1.5%. At 6 months, 5 years, and 10 years, mean aortic valve gradient was 7.4, 8.0, and 8.2 mm Hg, and ≥2+ regurgitation was 0.30%, 2.9%, and 6.6%, respectively. Left ventricular mass index decreased from 141 ± 52 g/m2 preoperatively to 90 ± 51 g/m2 at 10 years. Survival and freedom from reoperation at 10 years was 78% and 92%, respectively.ConclusionsThe Ozaki procedure creates a good aortic valve with stable low gradients and left ventricular reverse remodeling. A low but increasing rate of regurgitation is observed over time, with low reoperation risk, supporting its continued use.Key words: aortic valve replacement, autologous pericardium, effective orifice area, left ventricular mass regression, valve performance

In 2007, Shigeyuki Ozaki and colleagues introduced a new technique to create aortic valve cusps from autologous pericardium, obviating the need for prosthetic aortic valve replacement (AVR). Reproducibility was ensured by detailed description and developed tools and templates. A stitch-by-stitch description of the technical details and early results of the first 404 patients were reported in 2014 (Central Illustration).1 In 2017, the series with 850 patients demonstrated excellent hemodynamics and outcomes with a mean follow-up of 4 years.2 Since then, other centers have published smaller series confirming safety, reproducibility, and good early valve performance.3, 4, 5, 6 Attractive features of this procedure include single-valve surgery preserving the native aortic root, use of autologous tissue, large cusp coaptation height that minimizes stress,1 preservation of anulus mobility, and reproducibility. This results in valves with large effective orifice area (EOA),7 laminar flow, and kinetics similar to those of native aortic valves,8 with no need for anticoagulation, low risk of pacemaker implant,7 and no prosthetic valve cost.

Longer-term outcomes, including valve performance and durability, remain unknown, and at this stage only Ozaki’s pioneering series can provide additional information. The present study, conducted by Cleveland Clinic in collaboration with Ozaki’s team, aimed to analyze outcomes to date in the original Ozaki procedure cohort with focus on valve performance, left ventricular (LV) reverse remodeling, need for reintervention, and survival.

Patients and methods

Patients

From April 2007 to May 2021, 1,196 consecutive Ozaki procedures, 646 isolated and 550 with concomitant procedures, were performed at Toho University Ohashi Medical Center (Supplemental Table 1). During that period, essentially no prosthetic aortic valve implants were performed there. All patients were Japanese, with a mean age of 68 ± 15 years (range: 11-90 years); 484 (40%) were female. One hundred fifty-five (13%) were on dialysis; 322 (27%) had a bicuspid aortic valve; and 29 (2.4%) had infective endocarditis. Fifty (4.2%) had previous cardiac surgery (Table 1). Indications for surgery included aortic stenosis (AS) in 651 (54%), aortic regurgitation (AR) in 289 (24%), and mixed AS/AR in 87 (7.3%). Operative details are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Ozaki procedure

Autologous pericardium was used in 1,134 cases (95%), but bovine in 48 (4%) and equine in 14 (1%) for reoperation or to facilitate minimally invasive surgery. Excised autologous pericardium is stretched on a plate and tanned for 10 minutes with 0.6% glutaraldehyde solution, then rinsed in normal saline 3 times for 6 minutes. Size of the new cusps is determined by the intercommissural distances measured using Ozaki sizers (Tokyo Research Center for Advanced Surgical Technology Co, Ltd). Using an Ozaki template designed to create appropriate shape for generous coaptation, 3 cusps are drawn and cut from the treated and dried pericardium. Cusps are individually sutured to the anulus with 4-0 monofilament running suture. Aspirin is prescribed for 6 months after discharge.

Endpoints

Endpoints were postoperative in-hospital morbidity and 30-day mortality, follow-up valve performance, including aortic mean gradient, AR grade, LV mass index, time-related aortic valve reintervention, and mid-term mortality.

Mean aortic valve gradient, AR grade (none, mild, moderate, severe), and LV mass index were assessed on all serial postoperative echocardiograms, performed at approximately 1 week, 1 month, and every 6 months thereafter. In total, 5,543 echocardiograms were available for 1,048 patients (88% of cohort). Median echocardiographic follow-up was 2.7 years, and 80 patients (6.7% of the cohort) have follow-up echocardiograms beyond 10 years. Longitudinal data were censored at aortic valve reoperation.

Reoperative events and vital status were obtained by systematic anniversary follow-up. Median follow-up was 3.2 years, with 25% of patients followed >6.7 years, 10% >9.0 years, and 5% >10.5 years (Supplemental Figure 2).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute) and R v3.6.0 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, https://www.R-project.org). Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± SD or as equivalent median and 15th and 85th percentiles for skewed distributions. Categorical data are summarized by frequencies and percentages. CIs for longitudinal estimates used a bootstrap percentile method and time-to-event estimates used a delta method to obtain 68% confidence bands equivalent to ±1 standard error.

Longitudinal analyses

A multiphase, nonlinear, mixed-effects longitudinal regression model was used to estimate temporal trends of postoperative mean gradient and LV mass index.9 To assess temporal trend of individual postoperative AR ordinal grades, longitudinal analysis used a nonlinear, multiphase, mixed-effects cumulative logit regression model.10 Because only 15 follow-up echocardiograms showed severe AR, these were combined with moderate AR. Models were implemented using SAS PROC NLMIXED.

To identify variables modulating these outcomes, we used Boostmtree in R11 considering variables in Supplemental Appendix 1. Missing covariate data were imputed “on-the-fly” (Supplemental Appendix 2).12 Variable importance was used to hierarchically order variables in relation to degree of reduction of prediction error,13 separated into an overall effect and covariate–time interaction effects. Partial dependency plots were used to visualize the resulting relationships.14

Time-to-event analysis

Freedom from events was estimated nonparametrically by the Kaplan-Meier method and parametrically using a multiphase hazard model.15 We used random forests for survival (time-related events) to identify factors associated with time-related reoperation using randomForestSRC.16 A forest of 5,000 regression trees was grown using variables listed in Supplemental Appendix 1. Each branch (node) was split using a subset of 11 randomly selected candidate variables.

To identify risk factors for death, multivariable analysis was performed in the multiphase hazard function domain using variables listed in Supplemental Appendix 1.15 Before multivariable analysis, 5-fold multiple imputation using a multivariate imputation with chained equations was employed to impute missing predictor data (SAS PROC MI).17 Variable selection, with a P value criterion for retention of variables in the model of 0.05, used bagging (bootstrap aggregation).18 All variables with bootstrap reliability of ≥50% were retained as risk factors in the analysis. Following Rubin,17 we then combined estimates from the 5 models using these identified risk factors (SAS PROC MIANALYZE) to yield final regression coefficient estimates, the variance–covariance matrix, and P values.

To estimate intrinsic risk of valve reoperation without the competing risk of mortality, a conditional probability was calculated from cumulative incidence functions.19 Instantaneous risk (hazard function) for reoperation and death were estimated by a parametric method.15 Simultaneous operative transition probabilities (cumulative incidence functions) from the followed living patients without reoperation into either death or reoperation were calculated by integrating the parametric equations.

Results

In-hospital morbidity and mortality

Thirty-day mortality was 1.7% (20 patients) (Supplemental Table 2). Thirty-one patients (2.6%) had a stroke, and 41 (4.0%) developed acute renal failure requiring dialysis. Overall, 18 (1.5%) developed complete heart block requiring a pacemaker, 3 of 643 patients (0.47%) undergoing isolated Ozaki procedures vs 15 of 553 (2.7%) having concomitant procedures.

Postoperative valve hemodynamic performance

Mean aortic valve gradient was 7.4 mm Hg at 6 months, 8.1 mm Hg at 3 years, and 8.2 mm Hg at 10 years (Figure 1A). Use of nonautologous pericardium to create the cusps, smaller cusp size, smaller anulus size, and higher creatinine were associated with higher postoperative mean gradients, but younger age was not (Supplemental Figure 3).

The percentage of patients with moderate or severe AR gradually increased from 0.59% at 1 year to 6.6% at 10 years (Figure 1B). LV mass index decreased from 141 g/m2 preoperatively to 100 g/m2 at 6 months and 90 g/m2 by 10 years (Figure 1C).

Aortic valve reoperation

Thirty-eight reoperations occurred during follow-up: 17 for infective endocarditis, 18 for structural valve deterioration (SVD; AR in 15 and AS in 3), 2 for suture tear, and 1 for anular dilatation.

Seven reoperations for infective endocarditis followed equal tricuspidization. Among these, 3 patients had invasive disease requiring extensive debridement and anulus reconstruction.

Actuarial occurrence (Kaplan-Meier) of aortic valve re-replacement for SVD was 0% at 1 year, 1.1% at 5 years, and 4.7% at 10 years (Supplemental Figure 4), and for all indications, 0.4%, 3.2%, and 7.9% at the same time points (Figure 4A). When stratified by age, actuarial occurrence of reoperation for SVD at 10 years was 9.1% for patients <55 years of age, 4.1% for patients 55 to 80 years of age, and 1.2% for patients >80 years of age (Figure 4B).

Mortality

During follow-up, 167 deaths occurred, 19 cardiac, 125 noncardiac, and 23 unknown reasons. An early high-risk phase was followed by a late risk of death of 2%/patient-year after 1 year (Figure 6A). Survival at 6 months, and 1, 3, 5, and 10 years after Ozaki procedure was 95%, 93%, 90%, 86%, and 78%, respectively, comparable to, or in older patients better than, age-sex–matched Japanese population norms. Survival without reoperation was 72% at 10 years (Supplemental Figure 5). Older age (Figure 6B), kidney failure, and LV dysfunction were associated with increased risk of death (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

Principal findings

Ozaki valves have low gradients that remain stable for at least 10 years, associated with continued LV reverse remodeling for at least that time. However, they have a low but slowly increasing risk of AR as time progresses, but the risk of reoperation is low, with half being for infective endocarditis. Younger patients had a higher risk of reoperation due to SVD. Survival was comparable to that of the Japanese population.

Valve hemodynamics: large effective orifice area

Early results of the Ozaki procedure presented by multiple centers have shown excellent hemodynamic performance. Rosseykin et al20 reported lower gradients and larger valve areas than with Hancock II and PERIMOUNT bioprostheses. Our recent study, comparing Ozaki with PERIMOUNT bioprostheses, showed a lower gradient but more long-term AR.21 Stented valve prostheses not only restrict the EOA, but they are also rigid, restricting natural aortic anulus motion, which normally increases EOA by 10% to 15% in systole.22 Although stentless bioprostheses have better hemodynamics than stented ones, the extra tissue required to support them limits EOA, and durability is similar to or less than for stented bioprostheses.23, 24, 25 A meta-analysis by Mylonas and colleagues, including 22 studies of 1,891 patients who underwent Ozaki procedures, reported a postoperative mean gradient of 7.7 mm Hg and mean EOA index of 2.1 ± 0.5 cm2/m2 at discharge,7 larger than both the Magna Ease (1.2 ± 0.25 cm2/m2)26 and the Freestyle stentless valve (0.9-1.0 cm2/m2).27 Iida and colleagues highlighted the effectiveness of Ozaki procedure for the small aortic anulus.28 Saisho and colleagues demonstrated, in an exvivo study, that the Ozaki valve EOA was similar to that of native aortic valves and substantially larger than that of bioprosthetic or mechanical valves. Furthermore, the Ozaki valve EOA increases with flow similar to the native valve, unlike prosthetic valves.29 A four-dimensional computed tomography analysis of Ozaki valves showed laminar flow, similar to native valves.8

Aortic regurgitation

Over time, AR increased after the Ozaki procedure. We previously demonstrated that AR risk was associated with a learning curve over the first 300 cases, the 5-year risk then decreased, but around the time the wing extension and equal tricuspidization modifications were introduced, risk of AR increased.21 This latest increase may represent another learning phase for these new techniques and surgeons in training to learn the procedure.

Left ventricular reverse remodeling

LV mass reduction of 30% by 6 months, with continued reduction beyond 10 years after the Ozaki procedure, is better than reported after stented bioprostheses or transcatheter AVR.30,31 The larger EOA and anulus expansion with increased output are likely explanations. LV mass reduction occurred despite the increase in AR over time.

Reoperation

Younger age was associated with higher risk of reoperation, with a linear trend from age 20 years to age 70, although in the mid-term, the numerical increase in risk is still low. Thus, in patients aged 55 or younger, 12-year freedom from all-cause reoperation was 90% and reoperation for SVD 92%. This is comparable to the German Dutch Ross Registry’s reported 12-year freedom from reoperation after Ross procedures for both the autograft and allograft of 88% with the subcoronary technique, 77% with unsupported root replacement, and 87% with supported root replacement.32 Ozaki valve kinetics mimic those of the native valve, as the valve opens before the onset of blood flow through it, a result of expansion of the aortic root, a mechanism thought to reduce cusp stress and deterioration.33

It remains a concern that almost half of the reoperations were due to infective endocarditis, similar to previous reports of AVR using autologous pericardium.34,35 In 2012, Ozaki hypothesized that uneven opening of the cusps might increase risk of infective endocarditis and introduced equal tricuspidization. However, there was little difference in occurrence of infective endocarditis after this technique was used. Renal failure was a risk factor for reoperation, but few patients on dialysis developed calcific degeneration requiring reintervention.36

Use of alternative material

Although use of autologous pericardium is a major attraction of the Ozaki procedure, xenopericardium enables a minimally invasive approach, can be used in reoperations, saves operative time spent harvesting, preparing, tanning, and rinsing autologous pericardium and performs similarly in the short term. The number of xenopericardium cases now exceeds 100, some of which are more than 15 years of age, and we look forward to further investigations.

Study limitations

In addition to the limitations inherent in observational studies, the main limitation of this study is that nearly all procedures were performed or supervised by one surgeon (Ozaki). Mean age of our cohort is 68 years, which may render our results less applicable for younger patients, who we anticipate may benefit most from this procedure given its excellent hemodynamics and avoidance of lifelong anticoagulation. Although the experience in young patients is limited, the medium-term risk of reoperation compared with that after a Ross procedure is similar. Information about mechanical properties of pericardium and remodeling over time is also limited.

Conclusions

This study confirms the safety and effectiveness of the Ozaki procedure to justify its expanded use. The procedure creates a good aortic valve with low stable gradient, excellent LV remodeling, an acceptable risk of AR, and an overall low risk of reoperation. Risk of infective endocarditis remains a concern. Specific criteria for patient selection have yet to be refined. Further studies of modifications and long-term performance are needed.

COMPETENCE IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Introduced in 2007, the Ozaki procedure is an attractive option for AVR using autologous tissue and preserving native anulus motion. Although a learning curve exists, the Ozaki procedure creates good aortic valves with stable low gradients, regurgitation increases over time, but medium-term risk of reoperation is low, supporting continued use.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Additional research studying mechanisms and risk factors for failure and long-term valve function is warranted to expand its use and to establish its role within the armamentarium of aortic valve procedures.
