Effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up programme on motor performance in U-15 female soccer players: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Tahir Volkan Aslan, Sakir Tufekci, Yalin Aygun, Burak Canpolat, Fatma Hilal Yagin, Sacide Tufekci, Huseyin Gurer, Nouf H Alkhamees, Zizi M Ibrahim, Sameer Badri AL-Mhanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study is to investigate the effect of the FIFA 11 + warm-up programme on the agility, jumping and sprint performance of U-15 female soccer players.MethodsA twenty-four female U-15 football players (≥ 3 years of playing experience; regular training) randomly allocated to either the FIFA 11 + experimental or a control group in a parallel pretest–posttest design. The experimental group performed the FIFA 11 + during training sessions, three times per week for six weeks. The control group continued their regular warm-up. Performance outcomes were assessed at baseline and post-intervention using the Illinois agility test, countermovement vertical jump and 30-m sprint.ResultsA significant group × time interaction was found for the Illinois Agility (IA) test (F = 23.056, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.512), with a greater improvement in the experimental group (ΔExp–ΔCon = − 0.74 s; 95% CI: − 1.00 to − 0.48). Vertical jump performance also showed a significant interaction (F = 6.749, p = 0.016, ηp² = 0.235), favoring the experimental group (ΔExp–ΔCon = + 1.79 cm; 95% CI: +0.42 to + 3.15). In contrast, the 30 m sprint test revealed no significant interaction (F = 1.126, p = 0.300, ηp² = 0.049), with minimal between-group differences (ΔExp–ΔCon = − 0.05 s; 95% CI: − 0.17 to + 0.07). Overall, the intervention enhanced agility and jump performance but not sprint speed.ConclusionsThese results show that the FIFA 11 + warm-up program can be effective in improving agility and vertical jump performance in under-15 female soccer players, but it did not create a significant difference between groups in 30-meter sprint performance. These findings suggest that the program effectively supports neuromuscular control and lower extremity strength development, but more complex speed components such as sprinting may require additional or different training stimuli.Keywords: Warm-up, FIFA 11+, Female soccer players, Motor performance

Introduction

Football is a highly demanding sport that requires players to frequently perform accelerations and decelerations, change of direction (COD), engage in physical contact, and execute ball-handling actions, all of which necessitate superior athletic performance at a competitive level [1, 2]. Achieving and sustaining such performance depends on comprehensive strategies implemented throughout the season that support athlete health, optimize training routines, enhance efficiency, and minimize the risk of injury [3–5]. Among these strategies, the implementation of an effective warm-up routine is particularly crucial. Warm-up exercises represent a vital preparatory phase prior to physical activity, enabling athletes to become both physically and mentally prepared for the demands of the sport and to better tolerate the intensity of training and competition [6]. As in other sports, well-designed warm-up protocols in football that enhance neuromuscular activation and dynamic stability play a key role in reducing injury risk while simultaneously improving performance [7]. Programs such as FIFA 11 + specifically target neuromuscular coordination, contributing to the optimization of posture, movement alignment, and muscular responsiveness. Previous research has demonstrated that the regular integration of such preventive exercise programs into training sessions can significantly reduce injury incidence particularly among youth players while also promoting improved athletic performance and overall player safety [8].

The FIFA 11 + program is a structured warm-up protocol specifically designed to enhance movement efficiency and overall performance in soccer players. Regarded as a significant advancement in athletic conditioning, this program aims not only to improve physical capacities but also to reduce the risk of injury [9, 10]. Developed by FIFA, the program places strong emphasis on neuromuscular control, balance, and strength, which are essential components for both performance optimization and injury prevention [11, 12]. Evidence from the literature indicates that implementing the FIFA 11 + program at least twice per week for approximately ten weeks supports athlete health, enhances motor control, and leads to a reduction in injury incidence [13–16]. To maximize its effectiveness, FIFA recommends performing the FIFA 11 + warm-up protocol a minimum of two times per week. It has been reported that consistent application of the program can reduce training-related injuries by 37% and match-related injuries by 29% [17]. Similarly, a study conducted on elite basketball players reported a decrease in injury rates following the implementation of this protocol [18].

Performance differences between male and female athletes are multifaceted and cannot be attributed solely to biological distinctions [19]. Factors such as muscle mass, hormonal profiles, and cardiovascular capacity contribute to the development of specific adaptive responses [19–21]. Consequently, while sex-related differences in motor performance may confer certain advantages, biomechanical characteristics (such as joint flexibility and movement patterns) should be understood not as fixed traits, but as dynamic outcomes of the ongoing interaction between biological, environmental, and cultural influences. Despite the widespread use of the FIFA 11 + program, its effects on the motor performance of female soccer players remain insufficiently investigated, and the existing evidence is limited, mixed, and at times contradictory.

Pardos-Mainer et al. [22] demonstrated in a 10-week randomized trial involving 36 adolescent female soccer players that the FIFA 11 + program led to improvements in unilateral countermovement and drop jump performance, enhanced dynamic balance across multiple directions of the Y-Balance Test, and reduced lower-extremity asymmetries. Similarly, Parsons et al. [23] reported improvements in trunk muscle endurance, movement control, and agility following a five-month cluster-randomized study conducted with 43 prepubescent female players in the U10–U11 age categories. Stepinski et al. [24] also observed significant increases in countermovement jump performance and Functional Movement Screen (FMS) scores among 18 elite Polish women’s national team players over the course of a competitive season. In contrast, sprint and agility outcomes remained unchanged; however, correlational analyses indicated that superior functional fitness was associated with better sprint performance. In a longitudinal study, Reyes Laredo et al. [25] monitored 27 female soccer players at four assessment points throughout a competitive season and identified significant improvements in COD ability and lower-extremity strength from mid-season to the end of the season. Likewise, a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Kambitta Valappil et al. [26] concluded that the FIFA 11 + program produced small to moderate improvements in dynamic balance, COD speed, and vertical jump height when compared with traditional warm-up routines.

This study, which examined the FIFA 11 + warm-up program implemented in U15 female soccer players, addresses a notable gap in the literature by focusing on a sample that is unique with respect to both age and sex. Despite the growing popularity of women’s football and increased participation in sport at younger ages, scientific evidence regarding warm-up protocols specifically designed for female athletes within this age group remains limited. As the FIFA 11 + program has predominantly been evaluated in adult or male populations, findings related to its effects on adolescent female soccer players contribute to the development of age- and sex-specific evidence-based practices. Moreover, the outcomes of such structured interventions, which take into account the physical and biomechanical demands of athletes during growth and maturation, provide context-specific and practically relevant insights for coaches and sport scientists. In light of the existing literature, the purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up program on COD ability, jumping performance, and sprint performance in female soccer players under the age of 15.

This study was structured around the following clearly stated hypotheses in order to examine the effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up program on motor performance outcomes in U-15 female soccer players.

H1: A six-week FIFA 11+ warm-up protocol improves agility performance in U-15 female soccer players.

H2: A six-week FIFA 11+ warm-up protocol improves jumping performance in U-15 female soccer players.

H3: A six-week FIFA 11+ warm-up protocol improves sprint performance in U-15 female soccer players.

Methods

Participants

A total of 24 female soccer players (2 goalkeepers and 22 players) competing in the U-15 age category voluntarily participated in the study, female soccer players, all of whom had more than three years of regular training experience in an amateur football team.

The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (a) a minimum of three years of football training experience; (b) no history of musculoskeletal injuries or disorders that could influence performance outcomes; and (c) a commitment to attend all training sessions and assessment procedures. Participants were excluded from the study if they sustained an injury during the intervention period, failed to attend pretest or posttest assessments, did not comply with the intervention protocol, or engaged in intense physical activity within 24 h prior to the testing sessions.

Prior to participant recruitment, the required sample size was calculated using GPower software [27]. The calculation was based on an expected effect size of 0.25, an alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.80, indicating that a minimum of ten participants per group would be sufficient [28]. The study initially began with 29 athletes; however, two participants were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, and three were excluded due to nonattendance at training sessions or testing procedures. Consequently, the study was completed with 24 participants, comprising 12 players in the experimental group (age: 14.58 ± 0.51 years; height: 161.33 ± 4.88 cm; body mass: 54.22 ± 5.96 kg; body mass index: 21.28 ± 2.67 kg/m²; training experience: 4.25 ± 1.05 years) and 12 players in the control group (age: 14.50 ± 0.52 years; height: 161.16 ± 4.83 cm; body mass: 56.15 ± 6.58 kg; body mass index: 21.65 ± 2.45 kg/m²; training experience: 4.08 ± 0.79 years) (Table 1). All remaining participants successfully completed the intervention and testing protocols and were included in the subsequent statistical analyses. In this study, participants were assigned to two groups (experimental and control) using a simple randomization method by an independent, blinded researcher. The purpose of randomisation was to obtain equal numbers in the experimental and control groups and to minimise bias by ensuring that variables other than the one being examined were distributed as evenly as possible. To assign athletes to the intervention or control conditions, a sealed-envelope technique was employed. Athletes who satisfied the inclusion criteria and consented to participate were provided with 24 opaque white envelopes and instructed to choose one. Inside these envelopes, twelve contained a card labelled “1” and the remaining twelve contained a card labelled “2”. Once an athlete selected an envelope, the researcher opened it to disclose the group allocation (1 = experimental; 2 = control). The athlete was then placed into the corresponding group based on the number drawn. After completing the randomisation procedure, athletes were divided into two groups comprising 12 participants each—one experimental and one control. Subsequently, individuals in the experimental group were assigned identification numbers from 1 to 12, and the same numbering system was applied to those in the control group (Fig. 1).

Study design

A pretest–posttest design with a control group was employed in this study. Athletes were assigned to two groups using a simple randomization method by an independent blind investigator. Within this randomized controlled pretest–posttest experimental framework with parallel groups, participants were allocated either to the FIFA 11 + experimental group or to a control group that continued with their usual warm-up routines consisting of running drills, 5 × 2 small-sided games, and stretching exercises. The intervention was conducted during the competitive season and was implemented during the warm-up phase of training sessions three times per week on nonconsecutive days over a six-week period.

Prior to the start of the study, three familiarization sessions were administered to allow participants to become accustomed to the exercise protocols. At baseline, both the experimental and control groups underwent initial assessments that included agility performance, vertical jump height, and 30 m sprint time measurements. To minimize circadian effects, all assessments were performed during the athletes’ regular training hours between 17:00 and 19:00, on the same artificial turf field where they routinely trained, under comparable environmental conditions with temperatures ranging from 17 to 19 °C and relative humidity between %50–60, while wearing football boots.

Before the testing sessions, participants completed two practice trials to ensure the reliability of performance outcomes. Athletes were instructed to refrain from vigorous physical activity for 24 h prior to testing, to go to bed before midnight on the night preceding the assessments, and to avoid consuming caffeinated beverages for at least four hours before testing. After six weeks, both groups repeated the agility, vertical jump, and 30-m sprint tests, completing the data collection process (Fig. 2).

Research study protocol

Training protocol

The training volume was planned to be identical for both groups and each group performed warm-up protocols with different content, each lasting a total of 20 min. The experimental group implemented the FIFA 11 + program, which is designed to address modifiable injury risk factors such as neuromuscular control, static and dynamic balance, and the hamstring to quadriceps strength ratio [29]. The FIFA 11 + protocol consisted of a systematically organized three-part structure comprising 15 exercises, as described in detail by Soligard et al. [29]. The first section, lasting eight minutes, included low-intensity running exercises integrated with dynamic stretching and light partner contact. The second section, lasting ten minutes, consisted of six sets of exercises, each with three progressive levels, focusing on core stability, balance, lower-extremity strength, and plyometric activities. The exercises were performed for two weeks at each level of difficulty. The final section, lasting two minutes, emphasized moderate- to high-intensity running combined with cutting and COD tasks (Table 2).

In contrast, the control group continued their routine warm-up protocol throughout the six-week intervention period. This program consisted of running exercises lasting five minutes, 5 × 2 small-sided games lasting ten minutes, and general stretching exercises lasting five minutes, resulting in a total duration of 20 min. This traditional warm-up routine was selected as the control condition because it reflected the standard pre-training practice routinely experienced by the players, thereby allowing for a realistic comparison with the FIFA 11 + program. All training sessions were supervised by coaches experienced in the FIFA 11 + protocol to ensure the correct execution of all exercises.

Measurements

Anthropometric measurements

Participants’ height was measured to the nearest 0.01 m using a stadiometer (SECA, Germany), while body mass was assessed to the nearest 0.1 kg using an electronic scale (Tanita BC-418 MA, Japan). During the assessment procedures, participants wore light clothing and refrained from using any accessories that could influence body weight measurements. All body mass values were recorded in kilograms, and particular care was taken to ensure standardized conditions for accurate measurement of height and body mass across all participants.

Performance tests

The effects of the warm-up protocols on athletic performance were evaluated using the Illinois agility test, vertical jump test, and 30 m sprint test. Prior to testing, the test procedures were explained to the athletes and two familiarization trials were conducted. Each participant completed two trials separated by a three-minute passive recovery period, and the best performance was recorded for analysis.

The Illinois agility test [30]

was performed on a 10 × 5 m rectangular course with four cones placed centrally at 3.3 m intervals. To ensure precise measurements, timing gates with a sensitivity of 0.01 s (Smart Speed, Fusion Sport, Australia) were positioned at the start and finish lines. Following a familiarization run, players completed two timed trials. Starting from the prone position, they sprinted to the 10 m mark, returned to the starting line, ran in a zigzag pattern between the cones, and finished with a straight sprint to the end. To ensure test reliability, each athlete performed two trials, and the fastest time (in seconds) was considered in the analysis. Participants were given a standard four-minute rest period between consecutive trials, allowing sufficient recovery to achieve peak performance in the second trial.

Vertical jump performance

was assessed using a digital measurement system (Fusion Sport, Australia) by performing the countermovement jump test [31]. Participants were instructed to flex their knees to approximately 90° before jumping and to keep their hands on their hips throughout the movement. When ready, the athlete squatted to roughly a 90° knee angle and then immediately jumped vertically as high as possible, landing simultaneously on both feet on the mat. Each athlete performed two maximum jumps with a 4-minute rest period between jumps, and the highest jump was recorded for analysis.

Sprint ability

was assessed using a 30-meter sprint protocol. Before the test, athletes were informed about the test protocol and asked to perform a trial run. Running times were measured in seconds using a double-door electronic timing device (Smart Speed, Fusion Sport, Australia) placed at the start and finish lines. Each athlete performed two sprint trials with a 4-minute rest period between runs, and the fastest time achieved in the two trials was taken as the criterion performance [32].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all variables were found to be normally distributed. The homogeneity of variances was tested using the Levene test. Data meeting these assumptions were analyzed using two-factor repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with group (experimental-control) × time (pre-test-post-test) factors to control for Type I error probability. When a significant group × time interaction was detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc tests.

Due to the two-level nature of the time factor, the assumption of sphericity was automatically satisfied, and Mauchly’s sphericity test was not required. Additionally, for each test, within-group changes (Δ) and between-group differences (ΔExp–ΔCon) were calculated, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were provided to assess the reliability of the group differences. Effect sizes for the ANOVA results were reported using partial eta-squared (ηp²) values (small: 0.01; medium: 0.06; large: 0.14) [33]. The significance level was set at p < 0.05, and the data were presented as mean (M) ± standard deviation (SD).

Research ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences at Inonu University (Approval date: 30 July 2024; decision number: 2024/6288). Participation in this study was entirely voluntary.

The group × time interaction was found to be significant in the IA test (F = 23.056, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.512). Discussion

The aim of this study is to investigate the effect of the FIFA 11 + warm-up programme on the agility, jumping and sprint performance of U-15 female soccer players. The findings of this study reveal the effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up program on different physical performance components in U-15 female soccer players. The analyses showed significant improvements in favor of the experimental group, particularly in agility (IA) and vertical jump (VJ) performance. Indeed, the marked decrease observed in the IA test (Δ = − 0.91 s) was considerably higher than the change in the control group (Δ = − 0.17 s), and the difference between the groups was found to be statistically significant (ΔExp–ΔCon = − 0.74 s; 95% CI: − 1.00 to − 0.48). Similarly, the increase in VJ performance was higher in the experimental group (Δ = +2.18 cm), providing a significant advantage compared to the limited change in the control group (ΔExp–ΔCon = + 1.79 cm; 95% CI: +0.42 to + 3.15). In contrast, no significant effect was observed between groups in 30 m sprint performance, and the largely similar changes (ΔExp–ΔCon = − 0.05 s; 95% CI: − 0.17 to + 0.07) indicated that the intervention did not provide a significant contribution to speed performance. These findings provide important clues that the program is effective in improving agility and lower extremity explosive power, but does not provide the same level of improvement in more complex speed components such as sprint performance.

The results of the study indicate that the intervention had a highly significant and substantial effect on agility performance; the high level of significance of the group × time interaction (p < 0.001) and large effect size (ηp² = 0.512) clearly demonstrate the intervention-specific effect of the marked reduction in IA duration observed in the experimental group (F = 23.056, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.512). This finding indicates that structured warm-up and training protocols, such as the FIFA 11 + program, can effectively enhance athletes’ agility performance. Previous studies have similarly reported that the FIFA 11 + program contributes to improvements in agility across various sports disciplines. For instance, Foqha et al. [34] observed notable improvements in football players, while Hosseini et al. [35] reported comparable findings in volleyball players following the implementation of the FIFA 11 + program, highlighting its broad performance-enhancing benefits. In a study conducted by Trajković et al. [36] on young football players, the FIFA 11 + program was found to produce significant improvements in Illinois agility test scores. Another study involving youth football players also reported similar enhancements in agility parameters after the application of the FIFA 11 + program [37]. Additionally, a study examining the effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up program on agility concluded that the program could improve agility performance in young football players [38]. Moeini et al. [39] conducted a systematic review investigating the effects of the FIFA 11 + program on the performance of young football players and concluded that this warm-up routine led to substantial improvements in key physical attributes, including sprint speed and agility. Similarly, Banduni et al. [40] reported through a systematic review and meta-analysis that the FIFA 11 + injury prevention protocol not only reduced overall injury incidence but also positively influenced performance indicators such as agility and 20 m sprint ability. In addition, Asgari et al. [41] confirmed significant improvements in agility following the regular implementation of the FIFA 11 + protocol.

Collectively, these studies highlight the effectiveness of the program in enhancing both injury prevention and athletic performance among young football players. The findings of the present study are largely consistent with the existing literature and further strengthen the evidence supporting the effectiveness of the FIFA 11 + warm-up protocol. The significant improvement observed between pre-test and post-test agility scores in the experimental group, favoring post-test outcomes, indicates that the applied program effectively enhanced agility performance. The significant reduction in Illinois agility test time observed in the experimental group may be explained by the structure of the FIFA 11 + program, which promotes improvements in neuromuscular control and dynamic balance. The inclusion of single-leg balance exercises, running variations focused on COD tasks, and low to moderate intensity plyometric exercises may have enhanced coordinated activation of the lower-extremity musculature and joint stability, thereby improving braking and re-acceleration processes. In particular, eccentric and concentric loading patterns targeting the hip and knee musculature are thought to improve postural control and force transmission, which are key components of COD performance. These findings suggest that, beyond its injury prevention benefits, the FIFA 11 + program offers a functional training approach for enhancing agility performance in young football players.

This study found that the intervention had a significant and strong effect on vertical jump performance; the significant group × time interaction (p = 0.016) and large effect size (ηp² = 0.235) support that the marked performance increase observed in the experimental group was a development specific to the intervention (F = 6.749, p = 0.016, ηp² = 0.235). Similar findings have been reported in previous research. For instance, Sar et al. [42] compared different warm-up routines and concluded that the FIFA 11 + neuromuscular warm-up program produced superior effects on athletes’ vertical jump performance when compared with alternative protocols. Likewise, Silva et al. [43] observed marked improvements in vertical jump ability among twelve male football players following the implementation of a nine-week FIFA 11 + program. Supporting these findings, Pomares-Noguera et al. [37] documented significant gains in both agility and vertical jump parameters after a four-week FIFA 11 + training intervention. In addition, Soligard et al. [8] emphasized that the application of the FIFA 11 + protocol in young football players not only reduced injury risk but also led to meaningful improvements in performance indicators, particularly vertical jump ability. Similarly, in a study examining the effects of different warm-up protocols, Zobeydi et al. reported that the FIFA 11 + group had significantly higher vertical jump height performance compared to the dynamic stretching group [44]. These results collectively confirm the effectiveness of the program in enhancing explosive power and overall athletic performance.

The observed improvements, which are consistent with the findings of the present study, may be explained by the positive effects of the FIFA 11 + program on lower-extremity muscle strength, plyometric capacity, and neuromuscular coordination. The jump, balance, and strength-oriented exercises included in the program contribute to the development of explosive power by improving the elastic properties of the muscle–tendon unit and increasing the capacity for rapid force production. In addition, movements aimed at enhancing core stability and postural control facilitate more efficient force transfer, which directly influences vertical jump height. Therefore, the significant improvement observed in vertical jump performance can be interpreted as an indicator of the FIFA 11 + program’s impact on lower-body performance through plyometric loading and functional strength adaptations.

In this study, no significant interaction effect of group × time was detected on 30-meter sprint performance (F = 1.126, p = 0.300, ηp² = 0.049; 95% CI = − 0.17 to + 0.07). The small improvement observed in the experimental group (Δ = − 0.10 s) was quite similar to the change in the control group, and the low statistical power of the difference between the groups also supports this result. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting that general warm-up-based or low-intensity neuromuscular interventions have a limited effect on sprint performance. The current literature also reports that the effect of the FIFA 11 + warm-up programme on sprinting is relatively limited or mixed. A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis reported that FIFA 11 + applications significantly improved dynamic balance and agility; small positive effects were reported in explosive power indicators such as vertical jump, but no statistically significant improvements were observed in sprint performance. These review findings indicate that the programme does not provide consistent improvement in sprint times [26]. Similarly, the FIFA 11 + warm-up protocol has been shown to have a limited effect on acute sprint performance. In a study comparing different warm-up protocols, small improvements were observed in anaerobic performance parameters such as post-FIFA 11 + sprints, but these changes did not reach statistical significance; this suggests that more targeted or long-term training approaches are needed to improve sprint performance [45]. Neto et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis study which showed that athletes in the FIFA 11 group had a non-significant (p = 0.17) improvement in running sprints [46]. Steffen et al. [47] reported no significant differences between groups in sprint times between pre- and post-tests in their study of the FIFA11 + programme.

The lack of significant change in sprint performance can largely be attributed to the neuromuscular activation profile and exercise content of the FIFA 11 + programme. As the programme is primarily aimed at improving postural control, balance, basic strength and movement quality, it does not sufficiently stimulate performance determinants that determine sprint speed, such as high-intensity eccentric force production, rapid force development, ground reaction force production and type II fibre activation. The development of sprint speed is also dependent on highly specific motor skills such as high-speed running mechanics and the optimal stride frequency-length relationship; FIFA 11 + does not include technical or high-intensity sprint drills targeting these specific components. Therefore, the lack of meaningful improvement in sprint performance may stem from the programme’s neuromuscular objectives not directly aligning with the biomechanical requirements of sprinting.

Limitations

Although the findings of this study provide valuable contributions, several limitations should be considered to inform future research. Firstly, the study sample consisted solely of female footballers under the age of 15, recruited on a voluntary basis from a single football academy. This sampling approach limits the generalisability of the findings to male athletes, players in different age groups, or those competing at different performance levels or in different training environments. Therefore, the use of broader and more heterogeneous samples in future studies, including athletes of different genders, age groups, competitive levels and training backgrounds, will enhance the generalisability of the findings. In addition, the six-week duration of the intervention restricts the conclusions that can be drawn regarding the long-term effects of the program. Accordingly, future research may benefit from longer intervention periods to evaluate the sustainability of performance improvements over time. Finally, menstrual cycle status was not monitored in this study, and future investigations incorporating precise control of hormonal phases may enhance methodological rigor, particularly in research involving female athletes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the FIFA 11 + warm-up programme may contribute to improvements in agility and vertical jump performance in U-15 female soccer players. The findings suggest that the programme was particularly effective in enhancing change-of-direction ability and lower-extremity explosive power, whereas no meaningful improvement was observed in 30 m sprint performance. From a practical perspective, the FIFA 11 + programme may be considered a useful warm-up strategy for coaches aiming to improve neuromuscular performance characteristics such as agility and jumping ability in young female soccer players.
