Verifier-initiated quantum message-authentication via quantum zero-knowledge proofs
Authors: Wusheng Wang, Masahito Hayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
On-demand authentication is critical for scalable quantum systems, yet many existing quantum signature and message-authentication schemes are signer-initiated, requiring advance distribution of authentication material even when no verification occurs. We introduce verifier-initiated quantum digital signatures (VIQDS), in which the verifier requests authentication only when needed and the signer responds once; after issuance, verification proceeds without further interaction. Practically, shifting authentication to a verifier-driven, on-demand workflow reduces avoidable communication and storage overhead and aligns with deployments where verification is sporadic, such as distributed services and audit-oriented infrastructures. Our approach leverages quantum zero-knowledge techniques so that verification reveals nothing about the signer’s secret key beyond the fact that the signature is valid. We present a general conversion principle from suitable quantum proof protocols to VIQDS, together with a concrete realization based on elementary qubit platforms. Here, we show information-theoretic security against forgery and privacy against curious verifiers without computational hardness assumptions.

Introduction

Digital signatures are a cornerstone of modern cybersecurity, providing guarantees of integrity, authenticity, and non-repudiation across scientific, governmental, and industrial systems. As quantum technologies emerge, researchers have proposed quantum versions of authentication and signing. Recent experimental progress has shown a strong feasibility of real quantum digital signature applications: H. Yin et al. demonstrated a quantum digital signature for signing a document with at most 264-length1. Chapman et al. implemented an entanglement-based quantum digital signature over a campus network with sufficiently low qubit error rates (<5%)2. J. Xu et al. obtained a semiconductor-based quantum network combining quantum Byzantine agreement and quantum digital signature3. Y. Du et al. achieved a chip-integrated quantum signature network over 200 km4. X. Cao et al. demonstrated a quantum e-commerce scheme based on quantum digital signature that achieves a signing rate of 0.82 times per second for an agreement size of 0.428 megabit5. However, most existing quantum designs assume a signer-initiated model, where the signer proactively distributes authentication data before it is needed. In many real-world scenarios, such as decentralized networks or large-scale administrative workflows, this approach is inefficient. To address this, we introduce the concept of a verifier-initiated quantum digital signature (VIQDS), a protocol where the verifier requests authentication only when needed. This on-demand pattern reduces unnecessary pre-distribution and aligns with scalable verification in blockchain systems and other high-throughput environments6–8. Classical cryptography has long shown that a verifier-initiated approach to signing and authentication can be implemented efficiently using zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs). A zero-knowledge proof is a protocol that allows a prover to convince a verifier that a statement is true without revealing any additional information beyond its validity9–14. Building on these successes, we argue that the same paradigm is equally important in quantum settings. In this work, we extend the concept to quantum zero-knowledge proofs (QZKPs), which serve as the natural foundation for constructing secure verifier-initiated protocols in the quantum domain.

From a security perspective, the classical reliance of expressive zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) on computational assumptions raises serious concerns in the presence of quantum adversaries. Foundational results show that one-way functions are necessary and sufficient for computational zero knowledge for all of NP12,14,15; yet many classical one-way functions (e.g., factoring-based) become vulnerable under quantum algorithms. This tension has led to two complementary directions. The post-quantum approach seeks to preserve classical ZKP constructions by replacing fragile assumptions with ones believed to be quantum-resistant, such as transparent and hash-based designs or lattice-based hardness assumptions16–19, often using techniques like re-randomization for module-LWE to enable efficient proofs20. In contrast, the quantum-native approach abandons computational assumptions entirely and employs quantum zero-knowledge proofs (QZKPs) that, in some cases, achieve information-theoretic security grounded in physical principles21. This makes quantum-native methods particularly appealing for long-term security, as guarantees based on the laws of physics remain robust even against future breakthroughs in cryptanalysis.

Despite these advances, a critical gap remains: no existing work provides a fully specified VIQDS protocol while existing studies22,23 discussed verifier-initiated quantum identification protocols. Current QZKP-based authentication frameworks-including those formalized in ref. 24, Definition4.2,25, Definition 4.1, and26, Definition 9-implicitly assume signer-initiated workflows. While suitable for certain contexts, signer initiation imposes persistent communication and computational overhead, as the signer must proactively perform authentication steps for documents that may never be examined. By contrast, a verifier-initiated approach-while conceptually beneficial even in quantum settings-has so far only been proposed in classical cryptographic systems, where it enables on-demand retrieval, reducing unnecessary pre-distribution and improving scalability in high-throughput or decentralized environments6–8. Table 1 highlights three limitations of existing approaches. First, most quantum message-authentication/signature schemes are signer-initiated, which incurs avoidable pre-distribution overhead when verification is on-demand. Second, existing verifier-initiated quantum proposals mainly focus on identity authentication rather than message authentication. Third, current QZKP-based authentication frameworks for messages implicitly follow signer-initiated workflows, leaving the verifier-initiated message-authentication setting unaddressed.

A second, more subtle gap concerns how QZKP security is modeled when used as the core of an authentication protocol. The quantum interactive proof (QIP) framework and its complexity-theoretic foundations provide powerful abstractions for properties such as completeness and soundness27,28. Building on these foundations, several QZKP paradigms have emerged: schemes based on the indistinguishability of quantum states29, hybrid approaches combining Hamiltonian measurements with zero-knowledge techniques for QMA30,31, and constructions secured through certified deletion32–34, alongside seminal complexity results for quantum zero knowledge35. However, these models often overlook practical adversarial behaviors relevant to authentication, such as dishonest provers or curious verifiers, leaving a critical gap between theoretical guarantees and real-world security requirements.

Prevailing definitions of soundness often fail to capture dishonest provers who deviate from prescribed interactions in ways unrelated to any valid witness. In authentication, such deviations create concrete opportunities for forgery if left unmodeled. On the verifier side, the traditional dichotomy between honest and fully dishonest is also too coarse. Inspired by the notion of a specious adversary in quantum private information retrieval-an indistinguishable-but-curious entity-we argue that QZKPs must address verifiers who attempt to extract secret information while maintaining transcripts that appear honest36–38. Because an honest prover cannot distinguish a specious verifier from a truly honest one, zero-knowledge guarantees must be robust against this subtle yet realistic threat.

As our goals and contributions, we address both gaps identified above to enable verifier initiation for quantum-secure signatures and ensure practical implementability. First, we refine the QZKP framework to explicitly incorporate adversarial models that account for both dishonest provers and specious verifiers, thereby ensuring robustness to these realistic threats. Second, we develop a general concatenation method for the refined QZKP framework by which the soundness can be exponentially enhanced. Third, we introduce a general compiler that systematically transforms any QZKP satisfying these properties into a verifier-initiated quantum digital signature (VIQDS) protocol as in Table 2. Under this transformation, completeness of the underlying QZKP guarantees completeness of VIQDS, while security against dishonest provers and specious verifiers is preserved. Fourth, we present a concrete, highly efficient QZKP protocol that leverages the interplay between measurement-induced disturbance and the eigenstructure of observables. Importantly, this protocol is not only theoretically sound but also experimentally feasible: it can be implemented using current qubit technologies such as photonic or trapped-ion platforms, without requiring exotic hardware or unproven primitives. Integrating this QZKP into our generic conversion yields a practical VIQDS protocol with the desired interaction pattern and end-to-end security. Results

Formulation of VIQDS

In classical cryptography, verifier-initiated authentication has long been realized through zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs). Examples include Fiat-Shamir, Schnorr, and zk-SNARKs. In these protocols, the verifier requests a proof while the prover responds only when prompted. Such an on-demand model is efficient and scalable and is widely used in systems such as secure login and anonymous credentials.

In contrast, existing quantum digital signature schemes - including those based on quantum zero-knowledge proofs24–26 - mostly follow the signer-initiated model where the signer generates and distributes signatures in advance. This is inefficient in large-scale or decentralized systems that take on-demand verification as the norm.

To close this gap, we introduce the verifier-initiated quantum digital signature (VIQDS). Here, the verifier - not the signer - initiates authentication. The signer generates a signature only when requested. This avoids precomputation and pre-distribution. In addition, it has many merits, including lower communication overhead, greater scalability, and resource-conscious authentication.

The above structure gives the verifier control over when and for which message authentication occurs, which is critical for real-world systems where efficiency and scalability matter. In the one-copy setting considered here, each participant, including the verifier, stores exactly one copy of the quantum public-key state pk.

Our one-copy, one-time setting is defined by public-key consumption and refresh as follows. In our security model, each participant (including the verifier) stores exactly one copy of the quantum public key state pk. Verification is specified as a binary POVM acting on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(pk,m,{{\rm{sgn}}})$$\end{document}(pk,m,sgn), and therefore, the stored pk is treated as a one-time token: a single authentication/verification attempt consumes the public key and we do not assume reusability. Hence, one stored key copy supports exactly one authentication. To authenticate further messages, the signer refreshes the public key by rerunning the key-generation phase and redistributing a fresh pk state when needed.

This one-copy regime is deliberate: it captures on-demand authentication settings where public-key states are distributed as physical tokens and are consumed by verification measurements. In this sense, VIQDS can be viewed as a publicly verifiable signing/authentication primitive with explicit key rotation, akin to one-time signature usage where each verification consumes the corresponding public-key token. Our security definitions and proofs are stated for this regime; extending to multi-copy public keys requires separate modeling and analysis.

The reason that a one-copy quantum public key is essential in our setting is as follows. The role of the one-copy assumption is best understood by contrasting the quantum and classical settings. If the public key were a classical object (a fully readable classical description), then the verifier could directly obtain the identification information of the public key from that description and mount the corresponding attack; in this sense, the one-copy restriction would be vacuous in the classical setting. In our construction, however, the public key is an unknown quantum state held in a single copy. The most direct attack route is to identify the public-key state and use the resulting classical description to mount an attack, but such identification cannot be achieved from a single copy: full state reconstruction (tomography) fundamentally requires many identical copies. Therefore, restricting the verifier to one copy rules out this direct identification-based route, which is precisely where the quantum nature of pk becomes essential in our verifier-initiated setting.

Next, we discuss the security against curious but transcript-indistinguishable adversaries. Each participant receives a copy of the public key during key generation. They may try to extract secret information while appearing honest. To capture this threat, we introduce the notion of a specious participant. Speaking informally, a specious participant behaves indistinguishably from an honest participant in observable transcripts, while they may try to learn the signer’s private key. When a participant plays the role of the verifier in VIQDS, we refer to it as a (quantum/classical) specious verifier in the underlying VIS/QZKP formulation. These definitions reflect realistic adversarial behaviors. The adversary does not break the protocol’s observable behavior. However, they try to extract secret information. Security against such adversaries ensures that the signer’s private key remains protected - even if a participant is curious or malicious.

The reasons why specious participants are a realistic threat are as follows. In many real-world systems, participants are not assumed to be fully honest. On the other hand, they are also not assumed to be fully malicious. Instead, they may behave speciously - appearing honest while secretly trying to gain an advantage. This behavior is plausible for two reasons:If a participant’s dishonest behavior is not detected, they face no penalty. However, if they remain undetected, they gain secret information without cost.

To rigorously evaluate VIQDS, we introduce the following key notions as formal security properties. A protocol is said to satisfy α-completeness if, whenever Alice and Bob act honestly, Bob accepts the signature with probability at least α. A forging attack is defined as follows. An adversary intercepts communication during the pre-signing and signing phases. The verifier requests a signature on m. The protocol is said to be information-theoretically unforgeable with quantum specious participants if any adversary succeeds in a forging attack with probability at most ϵ when all colluding participants are quantum specious and both the signer and the verifier are honest. Similarly, the protocol is said to be information-theoretically unforgeable with classical specious participants if any adversary succeeds in a forging attack with probability at most ϵ when all colluding participants are classical specious and both the signer and the verifier are honest.

The reason why this security model matters, and how it mitigates dishonest behavior, can be explained as follows. Traditional models typically treat adversaries as either fully honest or fully malicious. Our notion of (quantum) specious participants captures an intermediate and realistic threat: an adversary may actively interact with the signer while keeping the interaction indistinguishable from honest behavior from the signer’s perspective. Concretely, a specious adversary is constrained so that the signer’s quantum state (and, in particular, its classical memory distribution) at the signing phase remains the same as in an honest execution, even though the adversary may measure or manipulate its own public-key copy to extract information. By proving security against such adversaries—namely, that any forging attack succeeds with probability at most ϵ—we obtain robustness beyond conventional honest/dishonest dichotomies, supporting practical deployment in settings where participants may be curious yet risk-averse.

A further concern in multi-participant deployments is the unauthorized transfer of a public-key copy to another party who later acts as a verifier. Accumulating additional copies could, in principle, increase the recipient’s ability to extract information and thereby raise the risk of key compromise or forgery attempts. To discourage such collusion, we allow an optional auditing mechanism in which the signer (as the prover in the underlying VIS/QZKP view) can randomly designate certain participants to act as verifiers. While this option is not essential to the core verifier-initiated workflow, it provides a practical deterrent against public-key sharing in distributed environments.

Importantly, our VIQDS formulation itself is primitive-agnostic: it is specified purely in terms of roles, phases, and security goals. We next connect this formulation to quantum zero-knowledge proofs via VIS protocols, which yields a concrete and secure VIQDS construction.

QIP: Foundations for Zero-Knowledge in VIQDS

The core idea behind VIQDS is to repurpose the roles in a quantum zero-knowledge proof (QZKP): the prover becomes the signer, and the verifier takes the role of the verifier in VIQDS. To make this conversion secure and meaningful, we first need to establish a quantum interactive proof (QIP) framework that reflects real-world adversarial behavior - not just honest or passive parties, but also dishonest provers and curious verifiers. This is essential for VIQDS. In applications like blockchain or decentralized systems, the verifier initiates authentication on-demand. It must be protected against both forged signatures and information leakage.

We now describe the basic structure of a quantum interactive proof game. It involves two parties: a prover (P) and a verifier (V). The verifier wants the prover to prove a statement r. If the proof succeeds, the verifier outputs 1; otherwise, 0. The game proceeds over m rounds. Both parties hold quantum registers: P has register Pj, and V has register Vj. In round 1, the verifier prepares a quantum state using the quantum operation Z1, and sends a quantum message X0 to the prover. In round 2, the prover applies the quantum operation Z2 to its register, and sends back a message Y0 (quantum or classical). In round 3, the verifier applies the quantum operation Z3, sends message X1, and so on. After m − 1 rounds, the verifier applies the final quantum operation Zm and outputs a binary decision - 1 for acceptance, 0 for rejection. Here, each quantum operation Zj is given as a trace-preserving completely positive (TP-CP) map.

We now discuss the security of this 3-round structure. This structure is not arbitrary; rather, it strikes an effective balance between efficiency and security: The quantum challenge in round 0 prevents precomputation. To ensure security, we require two fundamental properties: completeness and soundness.

These two forms of soundness are not redundant. They address fundamentally different threats: one is an honest-but-mistaken signer, the other is an actively malicious forger. Both are common in real-world authentication systems. We say a quantum interactive proof system satisfies β-soundness if it satisfies both type 1 and type 2. This dual soundness is essential for VIQDS. Formally, we denote an quantum interactive proof game as QIPαβ if it has α-completeness and β-soundness.

However, in authentication applications, completeness and soundness alone are not enough. The proof relies on secret knowledge (the witness). If the verifier is dishonest or curious, he may try to learn this secret. To prevent this risk, we must add the property of zero-knowledge. This is particularly important for VIQDS: the verifier should learn nothing about the signer’s private key - even if he is curious or specious.

QZKP: Protecting against specious verifiers in VIQDS

Building on the quantum interactive proof (QIP) framework established in the previous section, we now focus on a critical extension: ensuring that the verifier learns nothing about the prover’s secret - even when the verifier behaves speciously. While QIP provides the structural foundation for interaction and security properties such as completeness and soundness, it does not inherently guarantee privacy of the witness. To address this, we introduce the concept of quantum zero-knowledge proofs (QZKPs), which augment QIP protocols with privacy guarantees essential for secure authentication in VIQDS.

In particular, we refine the adversarial model introduced in QIP - including specious verifiers - and define corresponding zero-knowledge properties that ensure the signer’s private key remains protected even under subtle and realistic threats. This refinement leads naturally to the concept of quantum zero-knowledge proofs (QZKPs), which extend QIP by guaranteeing that the verifier learns nothing beyond the validity of the statement.

To capture realistic adversarial behavior in quantum authentication, we introduce three types of verifiers - honest, specious, and dishonest - and define corresponding zero-knowledge properties. A verifier is said to be honest if it always follows the protocol exactly. In VIQDS, this corresponds to a standard non-adversarial verifier.

A verifier is said to be quantum specious if, at each step, it interacts with the prover in a way that leaves the prover’s quantum state unchanged. More precisely, when the prover is honest, the joint quantum state on the prover’s local system, including its classical memory, is identical at each verifier step i to what it would be under an honest verifier. In VIQDS, this models a verifier that attempts to learn the private key without altering the observable transcript.

A verifier is said to be classical specious if, at each prover step, it interacts with the prover in a way that leaves the prover’s classical memory unchanged. More precisely, when the prover is honest, the classical variables stored in the prover’s memory follow the same statistical distribution at each prover step i as they would under an honest verifier. In VIQDS, this models a verifier that may attempt to learn the witness through classical side channels without changing the prover’s classical view of the interaction.

Note that any quantum specious verifier is also a classical specious verifier. Importantly, because the messages sent by a specious verifier are identical to those sent by an honest verifier, the prover cannot distinguish between them - making this a subtle but realistic threat in quantum authentication. We call a verifier dishonest if it may perform arbitrary deviations from the protocol, for example, by measuring quantum states in unintended bases or by deviating from the protocol entirely. In VIQDS, this corresponds to a fully adversarial verifier.

Based on these verifier types, we introduce four levels of quantum zero-knowledge. A protocol is said to be honest-verifier QZKP (HVQZKP) if, when both parties are honest, the verifier’s final state is independent of the witness Wt. This is the weakest form and is suitable only for non-adversarial settings. A protocol is said to be quantum-specious-verifier QZKP (QSVQZKP) if, when the prover is honest, and the verifier is quantum specious, the verifier’s state at each step is independent of the witness Wt. This notion is crucial for VIQDS because it ensures that even a curious but transcript-indistinguishable verifier cannot learn the private key. A protocol is said to be classical-specious-verifier QZKP (CSVQZKP) if, when the prover is honest and the verifier is classical specious, the verifier’s state at each step is independent of the witness Wt. Here, classical specious means that at each prover step the prover’s classical memory has the same distribution as in the honest-verifier interaction, while no constraint is imposed on the prover’s quantum state. A protocol is said to be dishonest-verifier QZKP (DVQZKP) if, for a dishonest verifier, the verifier’s state at each step depends only on public information and not on the witness. This is the strongest form and is suitable for fully adversarial settings.

These notions induce two related hierarchies, and it is important to distinguish them. At the level of verifier models, a quantum-specious verifier is, by definition, also classical-specious (i.e., it satisfies the classical-specious requirement as well). However, when we speak of QZKP—namely, protocol classes defined by zero-knowledge against a given verifier model—the inclusion goes in the opposite direction: zero-knowledge against classical-specious verifiers implies zero-knowledge against quantum-specious verifiers. Accordingly, among protocol classes, HVQZKP is the weakest and DVQZKP is the strongest, and in particular CSVQZKP ⊆ QSVQZKP. Thus, in our one-copy setting, CSV-verifier zero-knowledge implies QSV-verifier zero-knowledge.

Discussion

In this work, we have introduced a high-efficiency quantum zero-knowledge proof (QZKP) protocol that satisfies the standard properties of completeness and zero-knowledge. Importantly, it also provides two novel security guarantees: - soundness against dishonest provers, and - zero-knowledge even in the presence of specious verifiers.

These features address critical gaps in existing QZKP frameworks, which often assume either a passive prover or an honest verifier - assumptions that do not hold in real-world authentication systems.

Our protocol relies on simple, experimentally feasible operations: random number generation, eigenstate preparation and measurement, and classical comparison. By using an observable’s eigenstates as witnesses, we exploit fundamental quantum mechanics to achieve unconditional security - without relying on unproven computational hardness assumptions. This makes our approach particularly valuable for long-term security, where guarantees based on physical principles remain robust against future advances in cryptanalysis.

We have also presented a general compiler: any QZKP satisfying our security properties can be systematically transformed into a verifier-initiated quantum digital signature (VIQDS) protocol. This compiler allows us to directly translate the strong security of our QZKP - especially its soundness against dishonest provers - into a VIQDS protocol. The resulting VIQDS inherits robust unforgeability and protection against specious verifiers. These properties are formally proven in Methods, ensuring the protocol’s security is not just intuitive but mathematically grounded.

A key innovation of our work is the use of qubit systems with dimension d = 2l (a prime power). This choice is not merely technical - it is essential for achieving our tight security bound of ϵ = 2−l. By leveraging the algebraic structure of finite fields, we ensure that the forging probability is exactly 2−l, and can be made exponentially small by increasing l - for example, from l = 10 to l = 20 - without altering the protocol’s structure or the number of quantum systems per message.

This makes our protocol not only secure, but also scalable: even modest qubit systems - realizable with current photonic or trapped-ion platforms - can provide security levels comparable to classical cryptographic schemes, but with unconditional guarantees based on quantum mechanics.

This approach enables practical, scalable quantum authentication - ideal for applications like blockchain, where on-demand verification is essential. Looking ahead, several directions for future work emerge. These include extending the framework to multipartite settings to enable authentication among multiple parties, which would be important for decentralized systems and multi-signature schemes; optimizing resource overheads, particularly for longer messages, by reducing the number of required quantum systems; and exploring applications to quantum authentication and delegated quantum computation, where verification without revealing secret information is essential.

One limitation of our current protocol is its assumption of noise-free quantum systems and perfect quantum gates. In real-world implementations, noise and imperfections may affect performance. Future work will investigate error-tolerant variants, potentially using quantum error correction or fault-tolerant techniques to maintain security under realistic conditions.

In conclusion, our results pave the way for robust, practical zero-knowledge proofs in the quantum era. By bridging the gap between theoretical security and practical efficiency, we provide a foundation for the next generation of quantum-secure authentication protocols that are secure, scalable, and aligned with the operational needs of real-world systems.

Finally, although our main focus is the ideal setting, we also provide a robustness analysis against finite-coherence memory, operational imperfections, and detection loss in Supplementary Notes 4 and 5. There we derive conservative effective completeness/soundness parameters under these non-idealities, together with explicit sufficient feasibility thresholds. Moreover, as long as the resulting verification gap remains positive, repetition with thresholding continues to yield exponential suppression of honest-reject and forger-accept probabilities (see Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Theorem VI.1).

Security analysis of the VIQDS protocol

To show Theorem 2, we now provide the security analysis of the verifier-initiated quantum digital signature (VIQDS) protocol introduced in Results. This protocol is constructed by converting a quantum VIS protocol - a type of quantum zero-knowledge proof (QZKP) - using our general compiler. The security of the VIQDS protocol directly follows from the security properties of the underlying VIS protocol. Theorem 1 of the Results is shown by the combination of the following theorems.

Theorem 3

If the original quantum VIS protocol satisfies α-completeness, then the resulting VIQDS protocol also satisfies α-completeness.

Proof

Suppose the original VIS protocol satisfies α-completeness. In the VIQDS protocol, Alice (the signer) acts as the prover, and Bob (the verifier) acts as the verifier. When both parties follow the protocol - using the correct private key sk and message m - the underlying VIS protocol accepts with probability at least α. Therefore, the VIQDS protocol also accepts with probability at least α, satisfying α-completeness.

Experimental feasibility under current technologies

Our proposed protocol is fundamentally constructed by the concatenation of a Quantum Zero-Knowledge Proof (QZKP) protocol based on p-dimensional qudit systems. The base QZKP protocol requires only the unitary operations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathsf{Z}}(t)$$\end{document}Z(t), projective measurements for the basis \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\{\left|\phi (t,{t}^{{\prime} })\right\rangle \}}_{{t}^{{\prime} }}$$\end{document}{ϕ(t,t′)}t′, and quantum memory. While the qudit-based protocol provides the general framework, it is sufficient to combine several qubit-based QZKP protocols to implement a robust security parameter through concatenation. In the case of qubits, the base protocol simplifies, requiring only the unitary operation σz, and projective measurements in the σx and σy bases, in addition to quantum memory. These operations can be realized by the combination of the measurement of σx and the Z-axis π/2 rotation Rz(π/2).

Experimentally, implementing these required operations, measurements, and practical quantum memories is increasingly feasible, although platform-specific trade-offs persist. Photonic systems naturally support both qudit and qubit encodings-utilizing degrees of freedom such as time-bins43, orbital angular momentum44, or combined polarization-spatial modes45. Recent integrated-photonics work has demonstrated high-fidelity, high-dimensional state preparation and efficient analyzers, making rich qudit gates (as well as qubit gates) and measurements practical on chip-scale hardware46. Both qubit and qudit measurements are realized by adopting multiport beamsplitters built with multicore optical fibers47. Photonics’ main memory challenge is that flying photons do not self-store; thus, progress focuses on hybrid approaches (e.g., solid-state emitters48 coupled to nanophotonic cavities and atomic gases49) and engineered delay techniques50 that trade complexity for longer-lived photonic storage.

Superconducting platforms, by contrast, can implement qudits (multilevel transmon51 and cavity modes52) directly, as well as qubits. These platforms have demonstrated dynamical-decoupling and gate protocols that meaningfully suppress decoherence for multilevel operations53, while cavity memories (3D54 or planar high-Q resonators55) provide some of the longest on-chip storage times available today. Furthermore, superconducting generalized measurement is implemented by a hybrid method called the Naimark-terminated binary tree56. However, this method has so far been realized only for superconducting qubits, highlighting the imperative to develop a generalized, experimentally viable measurement protocol for superconducting qudits.

Although our main focus is the ideal setting, we also consider three standard implementation effects when assessing feasibility: (i) finite-coherence memory, where the verifier’s one-copy public key undergoes a storage channel Λmem(τ) over dwell time τ; (ii) imperfect operations/measurements, where each intended CPTP map Zj is implemented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{Z}}_{j}={{{\mathcal{N}}}}_{j}\circ {Z}_{j}$$\end{document}Z~j=Nj∘Zj and the verification POVM effect is perturbed within an operator-norm budget; and (iii) detection loss, where the decisive measurement may output an erasure symbol ⊥ with probability λloss under abort-on-erasure or reject-on-erasure conventions. The resulting renormalization of the completeness/soundness parameters and the corresponding (approximate) zero-knowledge guarantee are stated and proved in the Supplementary Note 4 (Robustness Theorem), together with explicit sufficient feasibility thresholds in Supplementary Note 5.
