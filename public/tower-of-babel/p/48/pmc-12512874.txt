Hepatitis B vaccination status, knowledge and associated factors among healthcare professionals in Dongola, Sudan, 2024: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mogahid Mohamed Awad, Sami Altalib, Kamil M. Shaaban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a significant global health concern, with 254 million people chronically infected worldwide and 1.1 million annual deaths from related complications. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) face an elevated risk of HBV infection due to occupational exposure, with a global prevalence of 2.3% among HCPs, increasing to 6.8% in Africa. Vaccination is the most effective preventive measure against HBV, yet coverage remains suboptimal, particularly in developing countries, including Sudan.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess HBV vaccination status, knowledge and associated factors among HCPs in Dongola, Sudan.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among HCPs in two hospitals in Dongola, Sudan, from May to July 2024. The sample size was calculated as 206 using OpenEpi, based on a 50% estimated vaccination proportion, 95% confidence level, and 5% margin of error, and adjusted by 10% for nonresponse. Participants were recruited via stratified random sampling. Data were collected through a structured and pre-tested questionnaire assessing socio-demographic characteristics, HBV knowledge, and vaccination status. HBV knowledge was evaluated via 21 items, with scores ≥ 13 classified as “good knowledge”. Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics, vaccination status and HBV knowledge. Factors associated with HBV vaccination status were assessed via logistic regression analyses.ResultsAmong the 227 HCPs, 211 participated in the study. The majority were under 30 years old (84.83%), female (70.62%), and had less than 5 years of work experience (65.40%). A total of 52.13% reported being vaccinated against HBV, with 65.45% of those receiving all three recommended doses. Notably, vaccine unavailability was reported by 93.84% of the participants. In terms of HBV knowledge, 62.09% were classified as having good knowledge about HBV. In the adjusted analysis, factors significantly associated with higher vaccination rates included more work experience (5–10 years: aOR 2.60, 95% CI 1.24–5.43; >10 years: aOR 4.16, 95% CI 1.08–16.05) and greater HBV knowledge (aOR 2.72, 95% CI 1.48–5.01). However, laboratory technologists had lower odds of vaccination than doctors (aOR 0.19, 95% CI 0.07–0.56).ConclusionThis study revealed suboptimal HBV vaccination coverage among HCPs, with only 34.12% being fully vaccinated. Key barriers included vaccine unavailability, whereas longer work experience and greater HBV knowledge were positively associated with vaccination status. These findings emphasise the need for comprehensive interventions to improve vaccine accessibility, enhance HBV education, and implement mandatory vaccination policies in healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11703-x.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a virus that causes liver damage. When it is chronic, HBV infection is associated with an increased risk of developing liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which can eventually lead to death. According to recent World Health Organisation (WHO) reports, 254 million people are infected with chronic hepatitis B globally, with approximately 1.2 million new infections each year. Additionally, in 2022, 1.1 million people died as a result of hepatitis B, mostly from cirrhosis and HCC [1]. HBV can be transmitted through contact with infected blood or other body fluids, including during sexual activity, unsafe injections, exposure to sharp objects, and transmission from mother to child during birth [2]. Furthermore, the prevalence of HBV is highest in the WHO African region (7.5%), with 82.3 million people infected [3]. The Eastern Mediterranean region also has a high prevalence, with 4.3 million people infected with the virus [4]. Similarly, the prevalence of HBV among the general population in Khartoum, the capital of Sudan, is over 8% [5].

Owing to the nature of their work, HCPs are more prone to acquire HBV because of accidental contact with blood and body fluids [6]. The prevalence of HBV among HCPs is as high as 2.3% globally, 6.8% in Africa, and 4.9% in Sudan [7–9]. The incidence of needlestick injuries among HCPs around the world is approximately 3 million per year, with each HCP sustaining up to four injuries per year [10]. Additionally, the risk of contracting hepatitis B by HCPs is four times greater than that of the general adult population [11]. Among the blood-borne transmitted viruses, HBV has the highest risk of transmission (30%), followed by HCV (1–3%) and HIV (0.3%). Therefore, HBV represents an important healthcare occupational hazard [12, 13].

Since there is currently no cure for HBV, the most effective method for protection against hepatitis B is immunisation. The HBV vaccine is safe and provides lifelong immunity against the virus [1]. The vaccine is typically administered intramuscularly in three doses at 0, 1, and 6 months [14]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and infection prevention guidelines of the WHO recommend that all HCPs be vaccinated against HBV before they begin clinical attachments during their time in medical school as part of occupational safety measures [15]. However, vaccine coverage remains low among medical staff, especially in developing countries [16]. Many factors can influence vaccine uptake by HCPs, such as the unavailability and cost of the vaccine, lack of time, and fear of vaccine side effects. Additionally, a lack of knowledge of HCPs about HBV can prevent the provision of preventive health education and infection control [17].

Globally, there are varying levels of HBV knowledge and vaccination coverage among HCPs. While knowledge levels range from 47% in Cameroon to 86% in Afghanistan [18, 19], vaccination coverage shows significant regional disparities, with full vaccination rates varying from as low as 9.74% in South Sudan to 69.44% in Pakistan [20, 21]. In some regions, vaccination rates are less than 50% [22, 23], demonstrating the potential for achieving better coverage. Healthcare institutions play a crucial role in HBV prevention through vaccine provision and the implementation of vaccination programmes; however, vaccine availability remains a significant challenge in many healthcare settings, particularly in resource-limited regions [24]. Additionally, professional experience has emerged as an important factor influencing HBV vaccination uptake, with more experienced HCPs typically demonstrating higher vaccination rates [18, 19].

Since limited data exist on HBV vaccination coverage and influencing factors among HCPs in Sudan, particularly in Dongola, the capital of the Northern State, this study aimed to address this gap by evaluating HBV vaccination status, knowledge and associated factors in HCPs.

Methods

Study design

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted to assess HBV vaccination status and associated factors among HCPs in Dongola Specialised and Dongola Police Hospitals in Sudan, between 1 May and 31 July 2024.

Inclusion criteria

The study included HCPs currently employed at Dongola Specialised Hospital and Dongola Police Hospital during the study period. Eligible participants were doctors (including dentists), nurses and laboratory technologists actively performing their duties. By focusing on HCPs actively working during the study, the criteria aimed to gather relevant and up-to-date insights from those directly involved in patient care and hospital operations.

Exclusion criteria

HCPs on sick leave, maternity leave, or long-term administrative leave during the study period were excluded. This ensured that only active participants contributed to the study. Additionally, HCPs who declined to provide written informed consent were excluded.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated via OpenEpi version 3, a statistical software for epidemiology and public health, on the basis of the known population size of HCPs at Dongola Specialised and Dongola Police Hospitals. With a total HCP population size of 444, a 50% estimated proportion of vaccinated HCPs (due to the lack of data concerning the proportion of vaccinated HCPs in Sudan), a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a minimum required sample size of 206 were calculated. To address potential nonresponse, we incorporated an anticipated 10% nonresponse rate. This adjustment accounts for the potential impact of workload on participation. Therefore, the total sample size was 227.

Sampling procedure

A stratified random sampling technique was employed to select the study participants from Dongola Specialised and Dongola Police Hospitals, proportionate to the number of HCPs in each institution. Accordingly, 63.4% of participants were from Dongola Specialised Hospital, while 36.6% were from Dongola Police Hospital. Within each hospital, the number of healthcare professionals from each role—doctors (including dentists), nurses, and laboratory technologists—was selected proportionally based on the total number of HCPs in that role at the hospital. Shift schedules obtained from the hospitals’ administrations and the heads of the departments were used as the sampling frame.

Data collection

A structured and pre-tested questionnaire, adopted from a study by Tsega-Ab Abebaw et al. [25], was used for data collection. This questionnaire had been previously administered to HCPs, ensuring its reliability and appropriateness for the target population. Therefore, a pilot study was not conducted for this study, as the questionnaire had already been validated in a previous study involving a similar targeted population. The questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews. In hospitals, one of the authors (MM) and two medical students specifically trained for data collection conducted interviews during participants’ working hours. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before starting the data collection. The questionnaire contained 34 questions assessing the socio-demographic characteristics of HCPs, their knowledge of HBV, their vaccination status, and the factors influencing vaccination status. To evaluate knowledge about HBV, 21 items were administered to HCPs. The questionnaire comprised 10 questions on transmission routes, 7 on natural history and diagnosis, and 4 on prevention methods. The participants were asked to respond to each statement with ‘true’, ‘false’, or ‘I do not know’. Correct answers were given a score of 1, whereas incorrect answers and ‘I do not know’ responses were scored 0. The total possible score ranged from 0 to 21. The median knowledge score was used as the cut-off point. Therefore, HCPs were categorised on the basis of their total scores as having “good knowledge” (score ≥ 13) or “inadequate knowledge” (score < 13). This classification allowed for a binary assessment of HBV knowledge among the study participants [25].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, occupation, marital status, and work experience), vaccination status, and knowledge levels of the participants. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables, whereas medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were used for continuous variables that were not normally distributed. For the knowledge assessment, participants were classified as having “good knowledge” or “inadequate knowledge” on the basis of their total knowledge score. The median knowledge score and its IQR were calculated to describe the central tendency and spread of the knowledge scores.

To assess factors associated with HBV vaccination status, both unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression analyses were performed. The dependent variable was the binary outcome vaccination status (vaccinated, partially vaccinated or fully vaccinated vs. unvaccinated), while the independent variables included age, gender, marital status, occupation, work experience, vaccine availability, and knowledge about HBV. In the unadjusted analysis, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each variable. Subsequently, an adjusted logistic regression model was constructed, including age, work experience, knowledge about HBV, and vaccine availability as covariates. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs were computed from this model.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. Data analysis was performed via STATA, version 17 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). The results were presented in tables and figures, including a bar chart for vaccination status and a forest plot illustrating the odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals from the logistic regression analyses. The figures were created via R Statistical Software version 4.4.0.

Results

Participant characteristics

Among the 227 HCPs, 211 were included in this study (response rate = 92.95%), while the remaining participants were unreachable. The majority of the participants were young, with 84.83% under 30 years of age, and predominantly female (70.62%). Most respondents were unmarried (77.73%) and had less than 5 years of work experience (65.40%). The sample comprised mainly doctors (including dentists) (45.50%), followed by nurses (37.91%) and laboratory technologists (16.59%) (Table 1).

HBV vaccination status

In terms of HBV vaccination status, 52.13% of the participants reported being vaccinated, with 65.45% of those vaccinated receiving all three recommended doses (34.12% of the total participants) (Fig. 1). Among the unvaccinated individuals, the most common reasons were vaccine unavailability in nearby institutions (32.68%) and at their workplace (15.84%). Notably, 93.84% of the participants reported that the vaccine was not available at their institution (Table 1).

Factors associated with vaccination status

According to the unadjusted logistic regression analysis, several factors were significantly associated with HBV vaccination status among HCPs. Those aged 30 years and above (OR: 3.95, 95% CI: 1.63–9.60, p = 0.002), married individuals (OR: 3.02, 95% CI: 1.49–6.15, p = 0.002), those with 5–10 years of work experience (OR: 2.76, 95% CI: 1.41–5.41, p = 0.003), and those with more than 10 years of experience (OR: 6.21, 95% CI: 1.99–19.31, p = 0.002) were more likely to be vaccinated. Compared with doctors, laboratory technologists had lower odds of vaccination (OR: 0.26, 95% CI: 0.11–0.61; p = 0.002). HCPs with greater knowledge about HBV also demonstrated an increased likelihood of vaccination (OR: 2.61, 95% CI: 1.47–4.64, p = 0.001).

After adjusting for age, work experience, knowledge, and vaccine availability, we found that occupation, work experience, and knowledge about HBV remained significantly associated with vaccination status. Compared with doctors, laboratory technologists continued to have lower odds of vaccination (aOR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.07–0.56, p = 0.002). The association with work experience persisted for both those with 5–10 years (aOR: 2.60, 95% CI: 1.24–5.43, p = 0.011) and those with more than 10 years of experience (aOR: 4.16, 95% CI: 1.08–16.05, p = 0.039). Therefore, more work experience is associated with a greater likelihood of being vaccinated. HCPs with good knowledge maintained higher odds of vaccination. (aOR: 2.72, 95% CI: 1.48–5.01, p = 0.001). Notably, age and marital status lost their significance in the adjusted model, whereas gender and vaccine availability showed no significant associations in either analysis (Fig. 2 and Table 2).

Discussion

Principal results

This study assessed HBV vaccination status, knowledge, and associated factors among healthcare professionals (HCPs) in two tertiary care hospitals in Dongola, Sudan. Among the key findings, we found that vaccination coverage was moderate, with 52.13% of HCPs reporting being vaccinated against HBV. Among those who were vaccinated, 65.45% had received all three recommended doses.

In terms of knowledge about HBV, 62.09% of the participants were classified as having “good knowledge”, with a median knowledge score of 13 (IQR 12–14). However, a significant challenge identified in the study was vaccine availability; an alarming 93.84% of participants reported that the HBV vaccine was not available at their institution. Several factors were significantly associated with vaccination status. Notably, HCPs with 5–10 years of work experience were more likely to be vaccinated, and those with more than 10 years of experience were even more likely. Additionally, HCPs with greater knowledge about HBV were more likely to be vaccinated. Conversely, laboratory technologists had significantly lower odds of vaccination than did doctors. Finally, age, marital status, gender, and vaccine availability were not significantly associated with vaccination status in the adjusted analysis.

Comparison with literature

Knowledge of HBV

While our findings align with those of some studies, they differ from others, highlighting the variability in HBV knowledge among HCPs across different regions. In our study, 62% of the participants were classified as having “good knowledge” about HBV, which is comparable to the results reported in Ethiopia and Cambodia. Specifically, Abebaw et al. reported that 63% of 410 participants in Ethiopia were knowledgeable about HBV, whereas Sok et al. reported that 63% of 259 participants in Cambodia were knowledgeable about HBV [25, 26]. However, our results are low compared with those of many other studies. For example, in Khartoum, Sudan, 70% of HCPs had good knowledge of the virus [27]. Additionally, a study in Afghanistan demonstrated that 86% of 502 HCPs were knowledgeable [18]. In our study, the notable difference in HCPs’ knowledge about HBV might be because most of our participants had few years of service, resulting in limited exposure to health education, training sessions, and workshops that could improve their knowledge about HBV. On the other hand, our results are higher than those reported in Cameroon by Jean Jacques N. Noubiap et al., where only 47% of the participants were knowledgeable about HBV [19]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the differing educational profiles of the study populations. In our study, a larger proportion of participants had attained higher educational levels, whereas in the Cameroonian study, 53% of participants had only secondary education.

HBV vaccination status

Despite the safety and efficacy of the hepatitis B vaccine, vaccination coverage among HCPs in this study remains significantly low. Our study revealed that only 34.12% of HCPs were fully vaccinated against HBV, indicating a significant gap in vaccination coverage among this high-risk population. A previous study conducted among HCPs in Omdurman Teaching Hospital in Sudan reported that only 26.19% were fully vaccinated against HBV [24]. Additionally, another study carried out in South Sudan reported that, of 154 HCPs at Juba Teaching Hospital, only 9.74% had received all three doses of the vaccine [21]. Moreover, a study conducted in Somalia reported a 16.40% full vaccination rate among HCPs [22]. In Ethiopia, only 12.90% of HCPs at Shashemene Zonal Town were fully vaccinated [25]. The higher vaccination rates in our study than in other studies may be because HCPs in many parts of Sudan often receive the HBV vaccine during medical school. Medical student associations provide the HBV vaccine to students for free before they begin clinical training in hospitals. On the other hand, the results of vaccination status in our study were low compared with those of other studies. For example, a study conducted in Cambodia reported that 59.80% of the participants had been vaccinated, with 49.39% fully vaccinated [26]. Additionally, among 252 participants at two secondary care hospitals in Pakistan, 69.44% were completely vaccinated [20]. In Nigeria, research conducted among 857 healthcare workers estimated that 42.00% of the participants were fully vaccinated [28]. Furthermore, a study performed in Bangladesh reported that, of 2046 participants, 66.60% were vaccinated against HBV [29]. Another study conducted in Sudan by Mahgoub and colleagues reported that 56.60% of 106 participants completed three doses of the vaccine [30]. The lower vaccination rates among HCPs in our study than in the abovementioned studies might be due to the lack of job entry requirements, limited vaccine availability in institutions, and the gap in national immunisation programs in our setting. Additionally, the hepatitis B vaccine is not free in every center, which further limits access for HCPs.

Reasons for non-vaccination

In our study, nearly half of the HCPs (47.80%) were unvaccinated. The primary reason for non-vaccination was " the vaccine was not found in nearby institutions " (32.68%), followed by " the vaccine was not delivered in the institution " and " always forget " (15.84% for each). These findings suggest that the unavailability of the vaccine could be a significant reason for remaining unvaccinated. A similar conclusion was reported in Somalia in 2020, where 42% of the unvaccinated participants mentioned vaccine unavailability as the primary reason [22]. On the other hand, our results differ from those of other studies conducted in Nigeria [31], Ethiopia [23], Pakistan [32], India [33] and Korea [34], with the main reason being the cost of the vaccine. Furthermore, 11.88% of the participants in our study explained " lack of time “, which is similar to the findings of a study carried out in Cameroon [19]. Moreover, in our study, 14.85% of the respondents mentioned " never thought about the vaccine “, which is lower than the percentage reported in a study carried out in Pakistan (21.70%) [35]. Additionally, approximately 5% of the HCPs in our study mentioned " I do not know there is a vaccine “, which is close to the 4.70% reported in Tanzania [36] and higher than the 1.90% reported in Cameroon [37].

Factors influencing HBV vaccination

The likelihood of receiving the HBV vaccine increases with years of experience. In this study, HCPs with more than 10 years of work experience were significantly more likely to be vaccinated than those with less than 5 years of work experience. A similar conclusion was reached in another study conducted in Ethiopia [25].

The reason behind this finding might be that experienced HCPs are more aware of the risks of HBV and the importance of vaccination due to their longer exposure to health education and vaccination programs. Additionally, our study revealed that knowledge about HBV is an important factor affecting HBV vaccine status. HCPs with good knowledge were more likely to be vaccinated than those with inadequate knowledge about the virus. This conclusion was also reported in a previously mentioned study [26]. Therefore, increasing the level of knowledge about HBV and the importance of vaccination can increase vaccine uptake in this high-risk population.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the use of a stratified random sampling technique ensured a representative sample of HCPs from different occupations across two hospitals in Dongola, Sudan. This sampling method enhances the generalisability of our findings to similar healthcare settings in the region. Second, the study employed a comprehensive questionnaire that assessed not only vaccination status but also knowledge about HBV and factors influencing vaccination, providing a holistic view of the issue. Third, the high response rate (211 out of 227 HCPs) further strengthens the validity of our results.

However, there are limitations to consider when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the factors examined and vaccination status. Second, the reliance on self-reported vaccination status without verification through medical records or serological testing may have introduced recall bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of the vaccination coverage estimates. Third, the study was conducted in two hospitals in Dongola, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other regions of Sudan or different healthcare settings. Fourth, while the knowledge assessment tool was comprehensive, it was not validated specifically for the Sudanese context, which could impact its cultural appropriateness and accuracy in assessing HBV knowledge in this population. Finally, while we adjusted for several demographic factors in our analyses, the possibility of unmeasured confounding cannot be ruled out, which may influence the observed associations.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into HBV vaccination status and associated factors among HCPs in Dongola, Sudan, and serves as a foundation for future research and interventions aimed at improving vaccination coverage in this high-risk population.

Implications for research

On the basis of our findings, several implications for future research can be drawn. First, there is a need for comprehensive seroprevalence studies of HBV among HCPs across different regions of Sudan. These studies should include tests for HBsAg, anti-HBc, and anti-HBs to provide a complete picture of HBV infection and immune status. Additionally, investigating the antibody response to HBV vaccination among HCPs, including factors that may influence seroconversion rates and long-term immunity, is crucial [38]. Qualitative research involving in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with HCPs could further explore their knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding HBV prevention and vaccination, providing valuable insights into barriers to vaccination and adherence to safety protocols. Furthermore, engaging hospital administrators and health policymakers in qualitative studies would help identify systemic barriers to effective HBV prevention and vaccination programmes in healthcare settings, informing the development of more effective policies and interventions. Longitudinal cohort studies are also recommended to track HBV infection rates, vaccination coverage, and occupational exposures among HCPs over time, which could aid in identifying trends and evaluating the effectiveness of prevention strategies. Finally, comparative studies examining HBV seroprevalence and risk factors among HCPs in various healthcare settings—such as urban versus rural or public versus private—could help identify disparities and tailor interventions accordingly. Collectively, these research directions could enhance our understanding of HBV epidemiology among HCPs in Sudan and inform evidence-based policies for prevention and control.

Implications for practice

Healthcare institutions in Dongola and across Sudan should implement comprehensive HBV education and training programmes for all HCPs, with a focus on improving knowledge about transmission routes, prevention methods, and the management of HBV-infected patients. The Northern State Ministry of Health and hospital administrations in Dongola should prioritise ensuring the availability and affordability of HBV vaccines and establish policies for the mandatory vaccination of healthcare workers, especially given the low vaccination rates and reported vaccine unavailability rates. Regular assessments of HCPs’ knowledge and practices regarding HBV should be conducted in healthcare facilities in Dongola to identify gaps and tailor educational interventions accordingly. Moreover, healthcare institutions need to promote a supportive environment that encourages HCPs to adhere to proper infection control practices and report occupational exposures, considering the high-risk nature of HBV in the region. Finally, medical and nursing curricula in Sudanese educational institutions should be updated to include comprehensive education on HBV prevention and management from the early years of training, addressing the knowledge gaps identified in our study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the HBV vaccination status and associated factors among HCPs in Dongola, Sudan. The findings revealed a moderate level of HBV knowledge and a suboptimal vaccination rate among HCPs, with only 34% being fully vaccinated. The study identified significant barriers to vaccination, primarily the unavailability of vaccines in healthcare institutions. The factors positively associated with vaccination status included longer work experience and higher levels of HBV knowledge, highlighting the importance of continuous education and training. The study also revealed disparities in vaccination rates among different healthcare professions, with laboratory technologists having significantly lower odds of being vaccinated than do doctors. These findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive interventions in Dongola and Sudan to improve HBV knowledge, increase vaccine availability, and increase vaccination coverage among HCPs. Recommendations include implementing mandatory vaccination policies, conducting regular vaccination campaigns, incorporating HBV education into medical and nursing curricula, and establishing occupational health checks for vaccination status. By addressing these issues, healthcare institutions can significantly reduce the risk of HBV transmission among HCPs and patients, ultimately improving overall health outcomes in Dongola and Sudan. Future research should focus on seroprevalence studies, antibody response to vaccination, and qualitative investigations to further understand and address the challenges in HBV prevention and control among HCPs in Sudan.
