Barley powdery mildew effector CSEP0162 interacts with the endosome membrane-binding domain of barley MON1

Powdery mildew fungi cause disease in many different plants. As obligate biotrophic pathogens, they are entirely dependent on living leaf epidermal cells, which they invade using specialized structures, called haustoria, for nutrient uptake. Liao et al. (2023) addresses the three forms of immunity that plants manifest against powdery mildew fungi. Initially, papillae are deposited as cell wall fortifications at attack sites. Secondly, encasements are built as extensions to the extracellular papilla structure around the invading haustoria. Both are pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) responses. Meanwhile, pathogens suppress immunity with the help of effectors (He et al., 2020; Yuen et al., 2023), which in turn may be detected, and subsequently activate effector-triggered immunity (ETI). This involves the invaded cell and perhaps neighbouring cells undergoing programmed cell death, known as the hypersensitive reaction (HR). While PTI is activated by plasma membrane-localized receptor kinases, ETI is activated by Nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) cytosolic receptors (Jones et al., 2024).

Detailed molecular insight into how effectors suppress immunity is essential for designing disease-resilient plants. Thus, it is interesting that plant immunity to a large extent is entwined with basic plant cellular mechanisms, such as membrane trafficking processes, making these cellular pathways prime targets for the pathogen. For example, the plant late endomembrane pathway was found to be required for ETI mediated by coiled-coil NLR (CNL) intracellular receptors in Arabidopsis, Nicotiana benthamiana, and barley (Schultz-Larsen et al., 2018; Sabelleck et al., 2025). Therefore, the available rather detailed understanding of membrane trafficking becomes useful for the advancement of plant–pathogen studies.

In the study by Liao et al. (2023), an effector (CSEP0162) from the barley powdery mildew fungus (Blumeria hordei) was found to interact with an important component of the plant late endomembrane pathway, barley MONENSIN SENSITIVITY1 (HvMON1), which is involved in the fusion of endosomes to their target membrane. It was noteworthy that overexpression of CSEP0162 and silencing of HvMON1 inhibited formation of encasements around haustoria, and that silencing of HvMON1 hampered barley resistance, activated by the CNL Mla3.

MON1 dimerizes with CALCIUM CAFFEINE ZINC SENSITIVITY1 (CCZ1) to serve as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for Rab7 GTPases while associated with the outer membrane of the multivesicular body (MVB) (Aniento et al., 2022; Herrmann et al., 2023). Here the MON1–CCZ1 complex is recruited to the membrane by GTP-bound Rab5 GTPase, after which it mediates exchange of GDP to GTP in Rab7. Subsequently, this activated Rab7 interacts with the homotypic fusion and protein-sorting (HOPS) complex to tether the MVB to the tonoplast for membrane fusion and delivery of cargo for degradation.

Liao et al. (2023) found HvMON1 to be a target of CSEP0162 in yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) analysis and, in the initial library screen, the 60 amino acid C-terminus of HvMON1 was identified. Subsequently, ‘full-length’ (amino acids 22–598) HvMON1–CSEP0162 interaction was observed in planta using bifluorescence complementation (BiFC). However, in subsequent studies, we have regrettably been unable to replicate that ‘full-length’ HvMON1 interacts with CSEP0162 in Y2H, as was described in Liao et al. (2023). The new study confirms interaction between CSEP0162 and the C-terminal 60 amino acids of HvMON1 in the Y2H system, but not the ‘full-length’ HvMON1 (Fig. 1). This failure of interaction is not due to lack of ‘full-length’ HvMON1 expression, as interaction with HvCCZ1 is found in the Y2H system (Fig. 1). As before, the effector CSEP0105 was used as a negative control. [The previous annotation of HvMON1, referred to in Liao et al. (2023), lacked a 21 amino acid unstructured N-terminal domain relative to the current annotation, HORVU.MOREX.r3.3HG0330240.2. Therefore, amino acids 22–598 is referred to here as ‘full-length’ HvMON1].

We have furthermore used the ColabFold version (v1.5.5) of AlphaFold2-Multimer (Evans et al., 2021, Preprint; Jumper et al., 2021; Mirdita et al., 2022) for predicting the structures of the protein complexes between HvMON1 and CSEP0162 (Fig. 2A), and between full-length HvMON1, HvCCZ1, and CSEP0162 (Fig. 2B). Interestingly, interaction between CSEP0162 and HvMON1 was only predicted to occur when CCZ1 was included in the complex, and in this case, CSEP0162 binds to the C-terminal 60 amino acid region of HvMON1. This agrees with the results from the Y2H analysis indicating that this 60 amino acid region is responsible for the interaction (Fig. 1). Moreover, this provides a possible explanation for the failed interaction between CSEP0162 and the full-length HvMON1 in our GAL4-based Y2H assay, as this takes place in the nucleus in the probable absence of the large cytoplasmic yeast CCZ1 (∼80 kDa). We therefore propose that interaction with CCZ1 is needed for a conformational change of HvMON1, making the C-terminus accessible for CSEP0162. This requirement would be met in the in planta BiFC assay in Liao et al. (2023), where N. benthamiana CCZ1 may form a complex with HvMON1 and enable interaction with CSEP0162.

The new results do not affect the final conclusion of the work, as the studies pertaining to the role of CSEP0162 and HvMON1 in immunity have been carried out in planta, in which case a functional HvMON1–CCZ1 complex is expected to be targeted by CSEP0162.

Meanwhile, a novel crystal-supported structural prediction of the MON1–CCZ1 complex in the yeast species Chaetomium thermophilum has been presented (Herrmann et al., 2023). The predicted barley complex (Fig. 2B) very much resembles this. Herrmann et al. (2023) studied how the CtMON1–CCZ1 complex interacts with endocytic membranes and found that several positively charged residues in the CtMON1 C-terminal α-helix are essential for this interaction by binding to negatively charged phosphatidylinositol phosphate (PIP) membrane lipid head groups. Interestingly, these positively charged residues are conserved in the C-terminal (amino acids 539–598) region of barley HvMON1 (Fig. 3).

Alignment of MON1 C-termini from yeast (Chaetomium thermophilum) and barley (Hordeum vulgare) shows high conservation of positively charged residues (underlined), mediating PIP binding. Bar, α-helix.

Having identified this C-terminal domain of HvMON1 by structural prediction and Y2H to interact with CSEP0162 (Figs 1, 2B), we suggest that this effector interferes with Rab7 activation by preventing HvMON1 from binding to endocytic membranes. Future studies will therefore address whether CSEP0162 indeed inhibits immunity by blocking traffic to the vacuole in barley and thereby inhibits immunity.
