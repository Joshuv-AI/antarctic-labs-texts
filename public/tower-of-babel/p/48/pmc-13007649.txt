Targeted repair of a refractory ventral cerebrospinal fluid leak identified by dynamic computed tomography myelography: illustrative case

Spontaneous spinal CSF leaks present both diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to their variable symptomatology and imaging features.1 While positional headache is the most common symptom, other manifestations may include nausea, tinnitus, dizziness, visual disturbances, fatigue, and neck pain.2 Many cases of spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) are due to spinal CSF leaks, and early localization of the site of leakage is critical to prevent worsening symptoms and ensure the proper diagnosis is made.3,4 Dynamic CT myelography (dCTM) addresses limitations to conventional CT myelography (CTM) by enabling real-time contrast tracking to improve localization and guide targeted intervention.5

Illustrative Case

Clinical History and Examination

A 36-year-old female with a history of Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome initially presented with positional occipital headaches, visual blurring, muffled hearing, dysphagia, and bilateral upper extremity paresthesias. Her first spontaneous CSF leak was diagnosed 7 years earlier and successfully treated with a nontargeted lumbar epidural blood patch. She denied any recent trauma or triggers for decreased intracranial pressure. Notably, she had undergone spinal anesthesia for two prior cesarean deliveries, performed 3 and 6 years earlier.

Initial noncontrast MRI of the brain demonstrated imaging findings consistent with intracranial hypotension (Fig. 1). Given that the patient was 34 weeks pregnant, CTM was deferred, and the patient underwent nontargeted lumbar epidural blood patch placement without imaging guidance by the anesthesia service with complete resolution of symptoms. Six months later, she returned with recurrent symptoms. Repeat brain MRI performed with intravenous contrast showed similar findings of intracranial hypotension with additional pachymeningeal enhancement (Fig. 2). Total spine MRI demonstrated a ventral extradural CSF intensity collection raising concern for a type 1 CSF leak (Fig. 3). CTM confirmed a ventral extradural contrast collection from T4 to T11 but did not identify the site of leakage. At our institution, a preliminary noncontrast CT is not routinely obtained prior to dCTM. Although some centers perform this step, there is no consensus on its necessity; therefore, we elected to avoid the additional radiation exposure for this patient. She then underwent dCTM to better localize the site of CSF leakage. Initial caudal-to-cranial imaging showed no immediate contrast extravasation, suggesting the absence of a high-flow leak. Subsequent cranial-to-caudal imaging, however, revealed a subtle ventral contrast collection just right of midline at T10–11 (Fig. 4), suggesting a dural defect and site of CSF leakage. Delayed supine imaging confirmed a well-defined ventral epidural collection extending from T6–7 to T10–11, consistent with prior studies. Given persistent symptoms and localization of the defect, open surgical repair was pursued. Surgical Procedure

The surgical procedure was performed under general anesthesia in a prone position with continuous neurophysiological monitoring. A T10 and T11 laminectomy was performed with a right-sided T11 transpedicular approach, which provided direct visualization of the ventrolateral dural surface while minimizing manipulation of the spinal cord. This approach allowed identification of the exiting right T10 and T11 nerve roots. After identifying the nerve roots, the lateral and ventral aspects of the dura were inspected, where there was a clear ventral dural defect. There was no clear CSF egress even with Valsalva maneuvers. Primary closure of this dural defect would have required either intradural exploration or nerve root sacrifice. Given the risks of these maneuvers and the lack of CSF egress indicating a low-flow CSF leak, we elected to reinforce the defect as opposed to perform primary closure. An oversized patch of TachoSil was wrapped around the lateral and ventral aspects of the dura, and DuraSeal6 was used to hold the TachoSil in place. The incision was closed in the usual watertight fashion. There were no neurophysiological changes noted during the procedure.

Postoperative Course

At the 2-week follow-up for suture removal, the incision was well healed, and the patient had no recurrence of symptoms. At the 1-month follow-up, she showed significant clinical improvement without signs of ongoing CSF leakage. At the 6-month follow-up, she continued to have complete resolution of her symptoms and was able to increase her activity tolerance without the return of her symptoms.

Discussion

Observations

Accurate localization of spontaneous spinal CSF leaks remains one of the most challenging aspects of evaluating patients with SIH. CTM often confirms the presence of an extradural fluid collection but may fail to pinpoint the exact site of leakage, particularly in cases with high-flow dynamics, large collections, or multiple dural tears. The delay between intrathecal contrast injection and image acquisition allows contrast to equilibrate between the intradural and extradural compartments, obscuring the leak site. Ventral leaks are especially prone to underdiagnosis because of subtle imaging features and reduced visibility in the prone position.

To address these limitations, many centers incorporate dCTM or digital subtraction myelography (DSM) early in the workup of refractory leaks.7,8 At our institution, patients with suspected SIH undergo brain and total spine MRI as the initial step. If an extradural spinal fluid collection is seen, noncontrast spine CT may be performed to evaluate for osseous pathology such as a bony spicule. Dynamic CTM is then performed to identify the dural defect and site of CSF leakage. If no extradural fluid is present on the total spine MRI, evaluation shifts toward localization of a CSF-venous fistula using decubitus CTM or DSM.

Within the broader diagnostic algorithm for spontaneous spinal CSF leaks, dCTM should be considered a targeted adjunct within a multimodal framework. Brain and total spine MRI remain first-line for detecting indirect signs of CSF leakage, followed by conventional CTM for patients with definite extradural collections or low-flow lateral leaks.9 Dynamic CTM is often most useful for high-flow ventral and lateral leaks or when a CSF-venous fistula is suspected.10,11 It is important to note that patients with known connective tissue disorders warrant a lower threshold for CT-based imaging given their predisposition to dural fragility and spontaneous leaks, which may present with subtle or multiple defects that are less apparent on MRI. Current consensus guidelines recommend selecting the myelographic technique according to the presence or absence of spinal longitudinal epidural collection. CTM, DSM, or ultrafast CTM is preferred when a collection is present, whereas lateral decubitus CTM or DSM is indicated for suspected CSF-venous fistula in the absence of collection.12 This stepwise approach emphasizes that these techniques are complementary and should be selected according to leak type, flow dynamics, and institutional expertise.

Although high-resolution thin-cut T2-weighted MRI can be sensitive for detecting spinal CSF leaks in some cases,13 it did not demonstrate a dural defect in our patient. This may reflect the subtle imaging features of ventral high-flow tears and the difficulty of visualizing small defects without dynamic contrast assessment. Full spine thin-cut imaging is also time-intensive, easily motion degraded, and often challenging to interpret, which can limit its utility in urgent or complex cases.

Dynamic CTM is most effective when a type 1 (ventral dural tear) or type 2 (lateral dural tear from the nerve root sleeve) leak is suspected.14 Mamlouk et al.14 described a modified protocol for these leaks, emphasizing rapid table positioning, immediate scanning postinjection, and the importance of patient orientation to minimize contrast equilibration. Dillon15 adds practical guidance for maximizing diagnostic yield by highlighting common pitfalls, including inadequate patient positioning, suboptimal injection technique, and insufficient coverage of the suspected spinal level. Dillon15 emphasizes that dCTM can often achieve definitive localization in centers without access to biplane angiography for DSM, provided the technique is executed precisely. Thielen et al.16 demonstrated that ultrafast dCTM can identify high-flow leaks caused by spiculated osteophytes. In their series, this technique localized leaks in cases in which CTM and MRI were inconclusive, particularly for subtle ventral dural tears.

A caveat of dCTM is its higher radiation exposure compared with single-phase CTM, given that the dynamic technique requires multiple sequential acquisitions over the same region. Although there are limited articles examining the radiation dose of dCTM studies in patients with SIH, there are ongoing efforts to reduce radiation exposure.17 Additionally, this modality requires specialized equipment and multidisciplinary coordination that are not universally available across institutions, therefore typically limiting access to high-volume or tertiary centers. These factors should be considered when selecting imaging modalities, particularly in patients who may require repeated studies.

In the current case, neither MRI nor CTM localized the leak despite persistent symptoms. Dynamic CTM definitively identified a ventral dural defect at T10–11, consistent with prior reports in which this technique revealed occult leaks missed by conventional modalities. This case underscores the value of incorporating dCTM early in the diagnostic algorithm when a type 1 or type 2 leak is suspected. Early, targeted localization not only aids in definitive surgical management but also reduces the need for repeated nontargeted epidural blood patches, minimizes treatment delays, and improves patient outcomes.12,18

Additionally, recurrent or refractory CSF leaks should prompt consideration of underlying connective tissue disorders.19 This patient had a diagnosis of Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome, a genetic condition typically characterized by dermatologic, pulmonary, and renal findings, but may also predispose to dural fragility.20 Identification of SIH in such patients should raise clinical suspicion for spontaneous spinal CSF leakage.

Surgical management of ventral spinal CSF leaks also presents unique challenges due to accessibility and difficulty of primary dural closure.21 Biological sealants were used in this case and have become valuable alternatives when sutured closure carries a high risk of persistent leakage or neurological injury.22 Gazzeri et al.23 reported durable closure and prevention of postoperative CSF leaks following repair of ventral cervical dural tears using TachoSil. Similarly, Carretta et al.24 found that TachoSil reduced rates of postoperative CSF leaks compared to sutured dural closures alone. A nonrandomized, multicenter study by Kim et al.6 demonstrated that the DuraSeal Exact Spine Sealant System (DESS) was safe and yielded similar rates of CSF leakage, infection, and neurosurgical adverse events compared to controls across a broad patient population. The low-swell formulation of DESS minimizes postoperative expansion, reducing the risk of neural compression in spine surgery and supporting its use in carefully selected patients.25

Lessons

Dynamic CTM is a valuable diagnostic tool for localizing high-flow type 1 and type 2 spinal CSF leaks, particularly when conventional imaging is inconclusive. Incorporating a preliminary noncontrast spine CT study to identify osseous pathology before dCTM can enhance leak localization and guide targeted repair. In patients in whom primary dural closure is not feasible, biological sealants such as TachoSil, reinforced with low-swell DuraSeal, may offer a safe and effective alternative. Early use of this imaging and management strategy in recurrent or refractory leaks may improve surgical outcomes and minimize unnecessary procedures or treatment delays.
