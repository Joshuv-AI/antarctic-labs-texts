Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 with MEN1 c.652C>T (p.Arg218Trp): variant reclassified to likely pathogenic
Authors: Muhammed Kizilgul, Alyssa Gardner, Raul Herrera, Giovanna F Klimovitsky, Shalamar D Sibley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) syndrome is the most common inherited cause of familial primary hyperparathyroidism. Diagnosis can be clinical, with at least 2 MEN1-associated tumors, or with 1 tumor plus a family history, or through genetic testing. A 31-year-old woman presented with hypercalcemia due to primary hyperparathyroidism. A 4-dimensional computed tomography (4D CT) and ultrasonography (US) of neck revealed 1 right-sided and 1 left-sided lesion consistent with parathyroid adenomas. Given her young age and a maternal history of primary hyperparathyroidism genetic testing was pursued and identified an MEN1 (NM_130799.2): c.652C>T (p.Arg218Trp) variant, reported as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) with conflicting interpretations across laboratories. This variant had conflicting interpretations of VUS or pathogenic among several genetic testing laboratories. Further evaluation revealed a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor and a pituitary cyst, with otherwise normal hormonal testing. Genetic testing in the patient's mother identified the same MEN1 variant. The presence of a clinical MEN1 diagnosis and familial segregation in 2 affected individuals provides additional evidence supporting reclassification of this variant from VUS to likely pathogenic.Keywords: multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, c.652C>T (p.Arg218Trp), pathogenic variant, primary hyperparathyroidism

Introduction

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) is a rare endocrine tumor predisposition syndrome caused by pathogenic variants in the MEN1 gene on chromosome 11q13 [1]. The MEN1 gene encodes menin, a tumor suppressor protein that plays a crucial role in gene transcription regulation. With penetrance exceeding 95%, nearly all carriers develop tumors by age 50. In patients with primary hyperparathyroidism (pHPT), the most prevalent hereditary syndrome is MEN1 [2]. A clinical diagnosis of MEN1 syndrome can be established in patients who exhibit at least 2 out of the 3 characteristic tumor types—parathyroid, pituitary, or gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors—or in individuals with one of these tumors and a first-degree relative diagnosed with MEN1. Alternatively, diagnosis may be confirmed by identifying a germline MEN1 pathogenic variant [1]. More than 1500 MEN1 pathogenic variants have been reported, most resulting in protein truncation, though no clear genotype–phenotype correlation has been identified [3, 4].

Here, we present a case in which genetic testing identified a heterozygous MEN1 variant (c.652C>T, p.Arg218Trp) classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) by the performing laboratory, with 3 other laboratories at the time also classifying it as a VUS and 1 laboratory as pathogenic. However, the presence of bilateral parathyroid adenomas, a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor (pNET), and a family history of pHPT provided clinical evidence to support the pathogenic nature of this variant.

However, the presence of bilateral parathyroid adenomas, a pNET, and the identification of the same MEN1 variant in her mother with a history of primary hyperparathyroidism provided clinical evidence supporting the pathogenicity of this variant.

Case presentation

Here, we describe a 31-year-old female evaluated for a 4-year history of asymptomatic hypercalcemia. Physical examination was unremarkable. Family history was notable for pHPT in her mother, who had undergone parathyroidectomy. Laboratory examinations showed serum parathyroid hormone (PTH) of 74.8 pg/mL (International System of Units [SI]: 7.9 pmol/L) (reference range, 8.7-77.1 pg/mL [SI: 0.92-8.17 pmol/L]) with an elevated calcium level of 10.5 mg/dL (SI: 2.6 mmol/L) (reference range: 8.4-10.2 mg/dL [SI: 2.10-2.55 mmol/L]). A 4D-CT scan of the neck showed right and left lesions consistent with parathyroid adenomas. Neck ultrasonography (US) revealed 2 hypoechoic lesions, measuring 16 mm on the right and 12 mm in the left, compatible with parathyroid adenomas (Fig. 1). Given her young age, family history, and bilateral adenomas on imaging, germline genetic testing through a commercial laboratory was pursued. The multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) panel identified her to have a heterozygous variant in the MEN1 gene (NM_130799.2): c.652C>T (p.Arg218Trp). This variant had conflicting interpretations among commercial genetic testing laboratories in ClinVar, classified as VUS by 3 other laboratories at the time and as pathogenic by 1 laboratory. Given the high clinical suspicion for this variant to be indeed pathogenic, further evaluation was pursued. Pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a 3-mm cyst, and anterior pituitary hormonal evaluation was within normal limits (Table 1). An abdominal MRI was notable for a 1-cm low gradient echo, T2 hyperintense, progressively peripherally enhancing lesion within the anterior superior pancreatic body. Further imaging with a Gallium-68 DOTA-(Tyr³)-octreotate positron emission tomography (68Ga DOTATATE PET) scan revealed one small focus of tracer uptake that appeared to involve the proximal tail of the pancreas, along with another small focus of tracer uptake, potentially secondary to a small peripancreatic lymph node, with no abnormal uptake in the liver to suggest liver metastases (Fig. 2).

Diagnostic assessment

The patient has a biochemical diagnosis of pHPT, with a baseline PTH of 74.8 pg/mL (7.9 pmol/L) and serum calcium of 10.5 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L). A 24-hour urinary calcium of 219.2 mg/24 hours (5.5 mmol/24 hours) effectively excludes familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia, while a vitamin D level of 44 ng/mL (110 nmol/L) and preserved renal function (creatinine 0.7 mg/dL [62 µmol/L]) exclude secondary hyperparathyroidism. Imaging identified 2 parathyroid adenomas. Additionally, abdominal MRI revealed a pancreatic lesion, confirmed by fine-needle aspiration (FNA) as a well-differentiated pNET. The MEN panel identified a heterozygous VUS: MEN1 (NM_130799.2): c.652C>T (p.Arg218Trp). These findings collectively support the diagnosis of MEN1-related multigland endocrine involvement.

Treatment

After removal of both parathyroid adenomas, PTH and serum calcium levels normalized.

Outcome and follow-up

Regarding the well-differentiated pNET, given the size, lack of metastatic features, and absence of biochemical evidence of it being functional, our tumor board panel opted for a conservative approach and ongoing surveillance with periodic imaging. Follow-up abdominal imaging is planned 1 year after initial imaging. Serum gastrin and chromogranin A levels remained within normal limits.

Discussion

In this case report, we describe MEN1 syndrome diagnosed clinically and supported by genetic testing in the patient and her mother, demonstrating familial segregation of the identified variant in 2 affected individuals. Our findings provide additional evidence to support reclassification of this variant from a VUS to likely pathogenic, as conflicting interpretations of its pathogenicity currently exist. Although strong genotype–phenotype correlations are generally limited in MEN1 syndrome, characterizing the type of MEN1 variant may provide insight into its functional impact and clinical consequences. The patient's variant (c.652C>T, p.Arg218Trp) is a missense change in exon 3 of the MEN1 gene, reported to result in aberrant splicing and an absent or altered protein.

Primary hyperparathyroidism is often earliest manifestation of MEN1, though symptomatic disease typically emerges in the third decade of life. MEN1-associated pHPT is usually multiglandular, with asymmetric and asynchronous gland involvement [5]. Serum calcium and PTH elevations are often milder than in sporadic pHPT [6, 7]. Current guidelines recommend genetic evaluation in patients with pHPT diagnosed before age 30, multigland disease, or a suggestive family history [8].

Menin is a tumor suppressor regulating transcription, genome stability, and cell proliferation. Tumorigenesis typically follows a “2-hit” model, with a germline pathogenic variant and somatic loss of the wild-type allele. De novo MEN1 variants account for ∼10% of cases, and pathogenic variants are not identified in 5-25% of clinically diagnosed patients [9]. Such cases may represent phenocopies, partial deletions, or noncoding mutations [10]. Population-level studies suggest evolutionary constraint within the MEN1 coding region [11]. American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines classify genetic variants as benign, likely benign, pathogenic, or likely pathogenic, but some variants—especially rare missense changes—remain as “VUS” due to limited or conflicting evidence. Incorporating patient phenotype can improve interpretation, and MEN1-specific modifications to ACMG criteria have been proposed to enhance accuracy [11-14].

MEN1 diagnosis can be established through clinical, familial, and genetic criteria. Clinically, the diagnosis is made when a patient presents with 2 or more MEN1-associated tumor types. In familial MEN1 syndrome, the development of just one MEN1-associated tumor in a family member is sufficient to meet the diagnostic criteria [1]. This case provides strong clinical evidence supporting the pathogenicity of the MEN1 (NM_130799.2): c.652C>T (p.Arg218Trp) variant, which has conflicting classifications in ClinVar. Although 4 laboratories, including the performing laboratory, classified it as a VUS, the presence of multiple MEN1-related endocrine neoplasms, including bilateral parathyroid adenomas and a pNET, along with a family history of parathyroid disease, strongly supports its causal role in MEN1 syndrome. Currently, 7 laboratories have reported this variant, excluding our evaluation: 4 classify it as pathogenic or likely pathogenic, and 3 as a VUS. Missense pathogenic variants affecting critical residues have been previously implicated in MEN1-related tumorigenesis [15]. Given this patient's clinical findings, this variant likely disrupts menin function, leading to early-onset, multiglandular endocrine involvement consistent with MEN1 syndrome. While gene and variant-specific data are essential in classification of genetic variants, clinical and phenotypic data can provide further valuable data for appropriate genotypic correlation and variant classification. Although genetic testing was not performed in other family members, testing could provide further insight into segregation of this variant and help guide clinical management for at-risk relatives.

Most experts recommend bilateral cervical exploration with subtotal parathyroidectomy and cervical thymectomy as the preferred initial surgical approach in MEN1 [5]. In this case, focused resection of 2 dominant adenomas normalized calcium and PTH levels. The high recurrence rate of pHPT in MEN1 distinguishes it from sporadic disease and necessitates lifelong surveillance [1].

More than 80% of MEN1 patients develop duodenopancreatic neuroendocrine tumors by age 80. Advances in imaging have increased detection of nonfunctional pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (NF-PanNETs), which now comprise most cases; gastrinomas account for ∼30% and insulinomas for 10-15% [16, 17]. Surgery is recommended for NF-PanNETs larger than 2 cm or those demonstrating progression [18]. In our patient, a 1-cm well-differentiated PanNET was identified and confirmed by FNA. Given its size, lack of metastases, and absence of hormonal activity, conservative management with surveillance was appropriate.

MEN1-related pituitary adenomas (PAs) exhibit a slight predominance among females, and the average patient's age at diagnosis falls within the fourth decade. The lifetime incidence of PAs in patients with MEN1 syndrome is ∼50%. The most common type of PAs among patients with MEN1 syndrome is prolactinomas, accounting for 28-45% of cases, followed closely by nonfunctioning PAs [19, 20]. In this case, pituitary MRI revealed a small cyst with normal hormone levels. The significance of pituitary cysts in MEN1 is unclear, and given the absence of functional abnormalities, surveillance was chosen.

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of integrating clinical phenotype when interpreting VUS results in MEN1. Clinical evidence can support reclassification to likely pathogenic, reducing uncertainty in genetic counseling and guiding management. The case also emphasizes the value of genetic testing in young patients with multiple endocrine tumors and relevant family history. Further studies on genotype–phenotype correlations and the functional impact of MEN1 variants in MEN1-associated endocrine tumors will be essential to improve risk stratification and management strategies.
