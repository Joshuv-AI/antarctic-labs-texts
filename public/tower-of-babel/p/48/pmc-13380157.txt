Updating the Medical History for the Age of Medical Devices
Authors: Derek T. O’Keeffe
Published: 2026

A patient presented with presyncope and palpitations several days after pacemaker generator replacement, raising concern that his prior individualized programming had not been restored. The surface electrocardiogram confirmed ventricular capture, but it could not reconstruct the patient’s previous pacing mode, rate-response settings, or detection thresholds or explain why those settings had been selected. None of that prior programming was recorded in our electronic health record (EHR). The cardiology fellow spent much of the day telephoning the device clinic and manufacturer, reconstructing fragments of a history that should have taken seconds to retrieve. Discharge was delayed by 24 hours.

Some months earlier, a different patient arrived in diabetic ketoacidosis with an insulin pump whose basal rates, correction factors, and recent occlusion alarms were nowhere in the record. We could measure her glucose and treat her ketoacidosis, but we could not see the therapy that had been working for her before she became unwell. These were not isolated lapses. They reflect a structural gap: programmable devices now sustain and shape care for millions of patients, yet the medical history has not evolved a reliable way to capture and surface their configuration at the point of care.

What Is Actually Missing

It is worth being precise about the gap because it is easily misunderstood. Modern EHRs are reasonably good at capturing what a device does, interrogation summaries, pump delivery totals, and ventilator waveforms. They are poor at capturing how a device is configured: the pacing thresholds, detection zones, basal schedules, and alarm settings that determine its behavior for an individual patient. In an acute presentation, a clinician can often infer device function from its effect, an electrocardiogram shows whether a pacemaker is capturing, and a glucose level shows whether insulin is reaching the patient. But the effect cannot be run backward. An electrocardiogram cannot reconstruct the arrhythmia detection settings erased by a generator change, and a glucose value cannot reveal the basal profile that achieved stable control for that patient over the preceding year. It is this longitudinal configuration history, not real-time function, that is lost across transitions of care, and it cannot be recovered by measurement at the bedside.

Availability, Not Interpretation

A reasonable objection is that configuration parameters are specialist territory: a generalist will not, and should not, reinterpret a detection zone or retune a basal schedule. That is correct, and it is not what this argument requires. The failure is not that nonspecialists cannot act on configuration data; it is that the data are absent at the moment and place where the relevant specialist is asked to act. When a device is reset, or a patient crosses an institutional boundary, the prior configuration is not merely inaccessible to the generalist, it is unrecorded for everyone, including the cardiologist or endocrinologist subsequently consulted, who is then left to reconstruct it by telephone. The nonspecialist’s task is recognition and escalation: knowing that a programmable device is present, that its history may be relevant, and that the appropriate service should be involved. The specialist’s task is unchanged except that it becomes possible at the point of care rather than after a day of archaeology. What is proposed is therefore structured availability for the right clinician, not the redistribution of clinical action to the wrong one.

An Old Problem in a New Form

Medicine has expanded the history before. A century ago, Henry Plummer unified fragmented patient information at the Mayo Clinic, creating the foundation of the modern medical record.1 Later generations added allergies, family history, and the social determinants of health as their relevance to outcomes became clear. Each expansion was a pragmatic response to a new source of clinical complexity. Programmable, data-generating devices are the current instance, and the one the history has yet to absorb; standard teaching texts on history-taking still do not list medical devices among the core components of a complete history.2

The scale is not marginal. Approximately 3 million people worldwide live with a permanent pacemaker, with roughly 600,000 implanted each year.3 More than 350,000 people in the United States use an insulin pump, up from approximately 7000 in 1990.4 Continuous glucose monitors, neurostimulators, home continuous positive airway pressure units, and consumer wearables add further configurable, data-generating layers, most siloed in proprietary applications outside the record.

Beyond Identification: The Unaddressed Gap

This problem is recognized, and important groundwork exists. Maisel et al5 documented how device recalls and safety alerts translate into patient harm, establishing why knowing exactly which device a patient carries matters. Wilson et al6 subsequently set out a health-system roadmap for capturing unique device identifiers (UDIs) at the point of care, showing that reliable device identification is both necessary and operationally feasible. Both treat the device primarily as a discrete unit to be identified and tracked. What neither addresses and what remains unsolved is the longitudinal configuration history of programmable devices: the settings that change over time, are tuned to the individual, and are routinely lost when a patient crosses a care boundary. Identification answers which device; it does not answer how it was set, by whom, and why. Closing the first gap does not close the second, and the second requires a different solution.

Why Current Systems Fall Short

The shortfall is not primarily technical. HL7 Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources model device data,7 and the Food and Drug Administration’s UDI rule has been in force for over a decade.8 Yet uptake is inconsistent: even well-resourced health systems frequently fail to capture UDIs in catheterization laboratories and operating theaters despite the technical means to do so.6 Three barriers recur. Incentives are misaligned, as comprehensive device documentation is rarely tied to reimbursement, so the investment case is weak. Interoperability is fragmented, so that where standards are used, fields are populated inconsistently, producing noise rather than a usable record.9 And workflows define no point at which device configuration is captured and reconciled, so the task defaults to the patient.

A Safety Gap That Is Also an Equity Gap

That default carries an equity cost. When the record does not hold device information, patients are made the integrators of their own technical data—expected to recall parameters, retain wallet cards, and operate manufacturer portals. This burden falls most heavily on those least able to carry it: older patients managing several devices, patients with limited health literacy, and patients with limited English proficiency for whom manufacturer resources may be unusable. The likely result is a familiar inversion, in which structured device documentation is least available precisely where the clinical stakes are highest, an instance of what might be termed an inverse digital care law. A safety gap distributed unequally is not only a safety problem but an equity one, and neither dimension is addressed by device identification alone.

A Tractable Proposal: Static vs Dynamic Devices

The clinical stakes are not confined to programmable devices. A radiologist cannot safely proceed with magnetic resonance imaging without knowing whether an in situ device is magnetic resonance-conditional, and a surgeon cannot plan a revision without the specifications of the hardware already implanted—information that, when missing, delays care for reasons unrelated to any device malfunction, and that no bedside measurement can supply in advance. These are real and common harms. But they are largely problems of device identification and specification: knowing which device is present and what it is. That is the territory Maisel et al5 and Wilson et al6 already address, and it is, in principle, solvable by reliable identification. The harder and still-unaddressed problem lies with devices whose clinically relevant state is not fixed at implantation but evolves—which motivates a distinction the record does not currently make: between static and dynamic devices.

Static devices such as coronary stents, prosthetic joints, and intraocular lenses are adequately defined by their identity and specifications, which do not change after implantation. Dynamic devices such as pacemakers, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators, insulin pumps, continuous glucose monitors, neurostimulators, and home respiratory devices are defined as much by their evolving configuration as by their identity, and it is their configuration history that is clinically consequential and currently lost. The 2 categories have different documentation needs, and conflating them is part of why progress has stalled.

Four Focused Priorities

First, device-aware history-taking should be taught explicitly: students and trainees should learn to ask what devices a patient uses, when each was last adjusted or interrogated, and whether any alerts or malfunctions have occurred—recognizing when device context is relevant rather than memorizing specifications. Second, structured UDI capture for implanted devices should be required and, wherever possible, automated through supply-chain integration, building directly on the roadmap Wilson et al describe.6 Third, initial effort should concentrate on high-risk dynamic devices, where missing configuration history poses the clearest and most immediate safety risk. Fourth, payer- or government-supported pilots focused on this subset could test feasibility, clinician burden, and outcomes before any broader mandate.

The Broader Opportunity

Structured device data would also unlock value beyond safety. Explainable machine-learning models already use continuous glucose monitoring streams to forecast hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia an hour ahead with useful accuracy.10 Cardiac device streams could surface early signals of malfunction or disease progression. None of this is reachable at scale while the foundational step, structured, accessible device documentation remains unmet.

The medical history has always adapted to advances in care; it can adapt again. The question is whether the profession leads that change deliberately or absorbs it reactively, after further preventable harm. The technology exists and the clinical need is clear. What is required is the alignment of incentives, workflows, and education around the purpose record-keeping has always served: better decisions for the patient in front of us.
