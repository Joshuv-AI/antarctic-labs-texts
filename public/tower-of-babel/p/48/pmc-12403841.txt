The effect of debate-based nursing ethics education on the moral sensitivity and judgment of nursing students
Authors: Hamedeh Heydari Sharifabad, Sara Heydari, Shirin Ranjbar, Khadijeh Nasiriani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMoral sensitivity and the ability to make ethical judgments are foundational competencies for ethical performance among nursing students. These two moral capabilities are not innate; rather, they must be cultivated through education. The effectiveness of teaching ethical concepts and employing suitable educational methods remains a topic of debate. This study aimed to assess the impact of debate-based nursing ethics training on the moral sensitivity and moral judgment of nursing students.MethodsThis study was conducted as a clinical trial involving 72 undergraduate nursing students. The participants were assigned to two groups: 37 students in the debate-based group and 35 in the lecture group. Demographic information, Lutzen moral sensitivity, and Rest moral judgment questionnaires were administered before the intervention and one month afterward. In the discussion group, students were divided into two subgroups of eighteen, with each subgroup participating in two 3-hour discussion sessions that addressed eleven scenarios focused on ethical challenges in nursing. In the lecture group, the session was conducted as a single four-hour lecture. The data were analyzed using SPSS version 16 software, employing paired and independent t-tests.ResultsThe two groups did not differ significantly in terms of demographic variables (P<0.05).Prior to the study two groups, the mean of moral sensitivity were (52.89±19.33) and (54.87±13.32). In addition, the first and third stories of moral judgment did not reveal a statistically significant difference (P<0.05). After the study, the moral sensitivity scores in the lecture group were (73.16±14.23), compared to (86.13±11.48) in the debate-based group. A statistically significant difference was observed in the mean moral judgment scores between the lecture and debate-based groups (P<0.001)ConclusionTeaching nursing ethics through both debate-based and lecture-based methods can enhance the moral sensitivity and judgment of nursing students. However, the debate-based method appears to be more effective. Given the significance of learning the Iranian Code of Nursing Ethics, both methods—particularly the debate-based approach—should be employed based on the educational context and available resources.Clinical trial numberThis study was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir) on 2024-06-04, with the IRCT ID: IRCT20240215061016N2. (https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/76848).Keywords: Debate-based, Lecture, Moral judgment, Moral sensitivity, Nursing ethics, Nursing codes of ethics

Introduction

Ethics encompasses the principles and values that govern individual and collective behavior within the framework of right and wrong [1]. Ethic represents a set of behavioral characteristics derived from personal beliefs, values, commitment, significantly influencing individuals’ attitudes, perspectives, and actions [2, 3]. The concept of professional ethics has emerged as one of the most critical topics, in recent years [4]. Professional ethics is regarded as a fundamental element across all health professions, including nursing [5]. Nursing, both historically and in contemporary practice, involves numerous ethical considerations, and over time, nursing ethics have evolved in response to advancements in the field [6, 7]. In particular, today’s healthcare systems demand nurses who not only possess strong therapeutic skills but also prioritize ethical caregiving [8]. Any failure to adhere to nursing ethics can adversely affect even the most scientifically sound and optimal practices [9].

This study prospectively registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials on June 21, 2024; registration code: IRCT20240215061016N2.

One of the key dimensions of nursing ethics is moral sensitivity, which plays a crucial role in fulfilling professional responsibilities and making ethical decisions [10]. Moral sensitivity is defined as the ability to recognize ethical dilemmas [11]. It encompasses responsibility, awareness, and the significance of ethical values in challenging situations, enabling nurses to effectively perform their duties [12, 13]. The presence of moral sensitivity enhancing the likelihood of promptly identifying patient issues and making appropriate decisions [2]. Furthermore, acknowledging the importance of moral sensitivity helps prevent irreparable harm that may arise from failing to adhere to the principles of professional nursing ethics, which can adversely affect both patients and the healthcare system [14], a decline or absence of moral sensitivity can result in morally incompatible care and such as job distress [12, 15], promoting and enhancing moral sensitivity, as a vital mission of nursing education, and necessitates the implementation of appropriate and contemporary educational methods [16]. About this, Hakbilen et al. (2023) reported that the moral sensitivity of Turkish nursing students was moderate [17]. Additionally, Mohammadi et al. demonstrated that a lack of moral awareness among nursing students is one of the most significant barriers to developing moral sensitivity [18].

On the other hand, moral judgment is regarded as a crucial criterion for the professional competence of nurses. Judgment based on ethical standards significantly enhances nurses’ performance in delivering quality and desirable care, making this issue vital within the nursing profession, which consists of essential members of the medical team [19]. Ethical judgment involves an objective and fair evaluation of others’ performance, as well as an individual’s perspective on the regulations, customs, traditions, and norms that govern interpersonal interactions [20]. A sufficient level of ethical judgment contributes to the provision of higher-quality care for patients and enhances job satisfaction among nurses [21]. Furthermore, to make sound decisions in the nursing profession, it is essential to possess adequate judgment regarding ethical issues. The absence of such judgment may result in the neglect of ethical considerations, leading to actions that are inappropriate for the situation. This can have serious consequences for the patient, the patient’s family, and even the nurse [22]. Recent studies indicate that without interventions focused on ethical education, the development of ethical judgment progresses very slowly [23].

Teaching ethical issues to nursing students is fundamental [24]. Nursing students represent one of the most critical groups in need of ethical education [25]. Although ethics education in undergraduate nursing can be enhanced through various educational and learning strategies, there remains a lack of consensus regarding the content and structure of these ethics courses [26]. The ethics education in undergraduate nursing education in Iran is usually taught through lectures [27]. Ethics education through lectures may be associated with a lack of active engagement, reduced focus and motivation, lack of opportunity to practice ethical skills, and reduced effectiveness in changing attitudes and values [28].

The most effective methods for delivering ethics education in nursing are still under debate, and it appears necessary to revise both the content and instructional approaches [29]. Debate-based learning approach, reflection, group discussions, and real-world scenario discussions are considered some of the most effective strategies for teaching and training in ethics [29]. Debate-based is a powerful educational approach where group dynamics—the mutual influence among members—can lead to changes in behavior and lifestyle. This method fosters an active process of interaction between instructors and students, allowing participants to share their opinions, ideas, and experiences on specific topics [30]. It aims to promote active learning by providing opportunities for critical thinking. Participants should engage actively in the problem-solving process through discussion, which enhances their learning experience. Rather than merely receiving theoretical training in ethical principles, individuals develop a nuanced understanding of clinical ethical challenges and learn to apply ethical principles and guidelines in practical situations [31, 32]. Studies on the use of debate-based ethics education in nursing ethics education are limited. Kim and Park (2019) showed the debate-based ethics education for undergraduate nursing students is effective in promoting moral judgment. However, no statistically significant difference was found for moral sensitivity between the two groups. Therefore, more evidence is needed [28].Although debate based method requires specific skills, choosing debate based learning can help develop reasoning, dialogue, and critical thinking skills, increase levels of engagement and motivation, and increase generalizability and applicability to diverse situations, which may be preferable to case-based learning in some cases. Therefore, considering the significance of this method in the realm of health service provision and the scarcity of research in this area, along with other pertinent issues raised, this study was designed to assess the impact of debate-based nursing ethics education on the sensitivity and moral judgment of nursing students.

Method

Study design

The research was a parallel, Nonblinding clinical trial registered under the number IRCT20240215061016N2. The clinical trial data was collected from June 21, 2024, until September 22, 2024. In this study, due to the contact between students in two classes, it was not possible to implement blinding.

Participants and recruitment

The sample consisted of nursing students enrolled in the fifth semester at Shahid Sadoughi Nursing and Midwifery School in Yazd, Iran. The research population comprised 85 individuals, and the sample was selected with a 95% confidence level. 76 individuals were assigned to two groups 38, based on an estimated population of 70 individuals and a 10% dropout rate, as indicated in the Morgan table. Initially, a list of students was compiled for sampling. Eligible students were then selected based on specific entry and exit criteria. The assignment of study groups was conducted through a lottery based on Class A and Class B. So, students living in the dormitory were assigned to the lecture group, while students living at home were placed in the debate-based group. The inclusion criteria for the study required participants to be nursing students in their fifth semester who consented to participate in the research. The exclusion criteria included students who were on leave or sick leave, as well as those who did not participate in the course or failed to complete the questionnaire in its entirety. Attrition was considered from the study.

Measurement instruments

In this study, participants completed a personal profile form along with a short-form ethical sensitivity and moral judgment test. The ethical sensitivity questionnaire, developed by Lutzen et al. consisted of 25 items [33]. Respondents indicated their level of agreement with each statement using a 5-point Likert scale (5 = strongly agree, 4 = somewhat agree, 3 = neither agree nor disagree, 2 = somewhat disagree, 1 = strongly disagree). The validity and reliability of this questionnaire have been confirmed in Iran by Hajibabaee [34]. Additionally, the reliability of the questionnaire was reaffirmed in the research by Jalali et al., which reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.82 [35]. The minimum and maximum scores for all questions were 0 and 100, respectively. Consequently, if score on moral sensitivity falls between 0 and 50, their level of moral sensitivity is considered low; between 51 and 75, moderate; and between 76 and 100, high [29].

Short form of the Moral Judgment Test was utilized in this study. This test developed by Rest and the Minnesota Group. The moral judgment test comprises three hypothetical scenarios, each followed by 12 questions rated on a Likert scale, where test takers must indicate their responses from least important to most important. Rest et al. reported the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this test, which ranged from 0.76 to 0.80 [36]. Nejat & Hatami established the test’s reliability at 0.79 and its validity at 0.68 in Persian version [37]. The questionnaire items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (5 = very much, 4 = quite a bit, 3 = somewhat, 2 = a little, 1 = not at all). Scores between 12 and 24 indicate a weak level of moral reasoning, scores between 24 and 36 reflect an average level, and scores above 36 signify a very good level of moral reasoning [38].

Subsequently, in the debate-Based group, the students were further divided into two smaller groups, and two 3-hour discussion sessions were conducted for each group, focusing on eleven different scenarios.

At this stage, given that each group consists of 18 people, an effort was made to divide them into two factions: those in favor and those against. The educator was present at the meeting as a facilitator and observer. It should be noted that the educator was a principal researcher and expert in nursing ethics.

Academic Debate: Based on the proposed rules— time allotted for each team, the requirement to articulate arguments for both agreements and disagreements, and the necessity to document these arguments—the debate was conducted under the supervision of the educator. The implementation method was as follows: First, the educator presented the scenario, which was also provided in writing to both the pro and con teams. Each group was then given five minutes to discuss the scenario collaboratively and to record their reasons for agreement or disagreement. To foster the development of students’ reasoning skills, they were permitted to utilize both physical and online resources during this stage. The debate commenced with the first team, determined by a coin toss conducted by the educator. Each round was expected to last five minutes, with the educator overseeing adherence to the time limits, ensuring respectful conduct towards the opposing team, enforcing the requirement to express arguments, and issuing warnings as necessary.

The educator would lead the meeting and, while refraining from expressing a personal opinion for or against any topic, would provide the necessary framework for presenting various viewpoints. This approach would ensure that the discussion remained focused on the main topics of the meeting.

Data analyses

The data for this study were analyzed using SPSS version 16 software. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, and frequencies, were employed, along with inferential statistics using the independent t-test and paired t test. A significance level of 0.05 was established, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was conducted to assess the normal distribution of the data. 2.143.

Results

According to the findings, the average age of students in the lecture group was 21.34 ± 1.96 years, while in the Debate-Based group, it was 21.25 ± 1.38 years. The average grade point average (GPA) of students in the lecture group was 15.74 ± 0.88, compared to 16.35 ± 1.39 in the Debate-Based group. An independent t-test revealed no significant difference in age (P = 0.81) or GPA (P = 0.07) between the two groups. In the lecture group, 40.6% of the students were male and 59.4% were female, while in the Debate-Based group, 47.2% were male and 52.8% were female. Additionally, 96.9% of the students in the lecture group were single, while 1.3% were married; conversely, 100% of the students in the Debate-Based group were single. This difference did not reach statistical significance, as determined by Fisher’s exact test (P = 0.471 and P = 0.631).

At the beginning of the study, using an independent t-test, we compared the mean scores of moral sensitivity and its dimensions among students in two groups: lecture-based and Debate-Based, there was no significant difference between the lecture group (mean = 16.33, SD = 52.89) and the Debate-Based group (mean = 13.33, SD = 54.87) (P-value > 0.05). After the study, results to compare the mean scores of moral sensitivity in both groups between/within groups indicated that, at a significance level of 0.05, there was a significant difference in all assessed moral areas before and after the intervention (P < 0.05) (see Table 1).

Discussion

Based on the findings, there was no significant difference between the two groups—lecture-based and Debate-Based—in terms of age, gender, and marital status. Therefore, it can be concluded that the two groups were comparable regarding these demographic variables.

The scores for moral sensitivity and its dimensions before the study in the two groups—lecture-based and debate-based indicate that the areas of respect for modifying autonomy, interpersonal orientation, experiencing conflict, and managing conflicts were above the median in both groups. Conversely, the areas concerning adherence to ‘rules,’ structuring moral meaning, and expressing benevolence were below the median in both groups. Additionally, based on the questionnaire, which defines a total score between 50 and 75 as average, it can be concluded that both groups were at the average level, albeit at the lower end of that range, and this difference was not statistically significant. After the study, the comparison of moral sensitivity scores and their dimensions between the lecture-based and debate-based groups revealed that both groups scored above the median in all ethical domains. However, the increase in scores for the debate-based group was statistically significant, resulting in a high overall level of moral sensitivity. In contrast, although the lecture group also showed an increase, their total score remained in the medium range, highlighting a significant difference between the two groups. Therefore, it can be concluded that both teaching methods can enhance moral sensitivity in students, but the improvement was more pronounced in the debate-based group. In accordance with the findings of the study, Fard et al. (2022) in the narrative method [1]; and Nasiriani et al. (2020) in virtual storytelling [29]; as well as Heidari et al. (2024) in distance education by mobile phones [16]; found that when teaching ethical principles to students or nurses, there was no significant difference in moral sensitivity between the test and control groups before the study. However, after the study, a statistically significant difference was observed in the mean scores of moral sensitivity between the groups. In contrast, Kim and Park (2019) in debate-based and lecture-based groups [26]; and Bagheri et al. (2022) in reflective learning through clinical scenarios [41]; reported that teaching ethics to nursing students did not yield a statistically significant difference in moral sensitivity following the intervention. The lack of improvement in moral sensitivity in these study may be due to limitations in the study design, type and duration of training, assessment tools, student background and experience level, environmental and cultural factors, and sample size and diversity of participants.

According to the findings, moral sensitivity and its associated domains increased significantly in the lecture group following the intervention; however, the total score remained at an average level despite this increase. In contrast, the Debate-Based group demonstrated a significantly greater increase in moral sensitivity after the intervention compared to before, with the total score rising from an average level to a high level. Thus, while both methods enhanced the moral sensitivity of students, the increase was more pronounced in the Debate-Based group. In accordance with the findings of the present study, a single-group experimental study conducted by Kaya & Boz (2023) in Turkey using the case-based learning approach [42]; Keshmiri and Fazljoo (2024) in case-based learning [12]; Azizi and Sasanpour (2018) in lecture [14]; Jahandide et al. (2022) in educational intervention [43]; and Heidari et al. (2024) in distance education [16]; indicated that the mean post-test scores were significantly higher than the pre-test scores in nursing students or nurses, which aligns with the results of the present study. Therefore, based on the results of this study and other research, ethics education for nursing students and nurses—regardless of the method employed—can enhance ethical sensitivity. However, the effectiveness of these educational approaches varies, with Debate-Based methods and scenario presentations yielding greater improvements. In contrast to the findings of the present study, Ertugrul et al. (2022) [44]; and Yeom et al. (2017) [45]; reported that the mean moral sensitivity of students in the intervention group, following an ethics workshop conducted in a lecture format, did not differ statistically from that of the control group. To explain the differences in results, several factors can be considered, including the educational content, the time allocated for presenting the material, the methods employed, the number of participants, and the various study tools used.

Other findings indicated that the comparison of the mean scores of moral judgment between the lecture-based and debate-based groups prior to the intervention revealed that both groups were at an average level for the first and third stories. However, they demonstrated a very good level of moral judgment in the second story, where a statistically significant difference between the two groups was observed. It is important to note that the moral judgment questionnaire used in this study was the short form of the Rest questionnaire. The second scenario focused on the school environment and the behaviors of the principal, parents, and students, which may have been considered without careful reflection, as it was not directly related to the nursing field. However, when comparing the average scores of students’ moral judgment in the two groups—lecture-based and debate-based—after the intervention, both groups demonstrated commendable performance across all three scenarios. The average scores of students in the debate-based group were higher than those in the lecture group, and there was a significant difference between the two groups in all three scenarios. Therefore, it can be concluded that both educational methods can enhance students’ moral judgment; however, the improvement in moral judgment through the debate-based method is more pronounced. A study by Fard et al. (2022) [1] ; and Namadi et al. (2017) [46]; corroborates these findings, indicating that there was no significant difference in moral reasoning between the two groups. However, following the intervention, a significant difference in moral reasoning emerged between the two groups. Additionally, Kim and Park (2019) demonstrated in Korea that moral judgment among nursing students in the debate group significantly improved compared to those in the lecture group, aligning with the findings of this study [26]. Therefore, based on the aforementioned studies and additional research, it is anticipated that teaching nursing ethics through various methods, particularly debate-based approaches, can serve as an effective educational method.

Other findings indicated that in both the debate-based and lecture groups, the mean scores for all three stories increased, demonstrating a significant difference before and after the intervention. The first and third stories improved from an average to a very good level of moral judgment, while the second story consistently remained at a very good level. Consequently, both methods contributed to enhancing students’ moral judgment. Supporting these results, Azizi et al. (2014)in lecture [47] and Wong & Low (2024) with their online debate method [48] found that after implementing ethics education, there was a significant increase in the mean scores of nurses’ moral judgment or critical thinking skills, and improved their understanding of ethical dilemmas. However, Khatiban et al. (2018) revealed that the mean score of moral reasoning in the lecture group did not differ significantly after the intervention and one month later. In contrast, the moral reasoning of the problem-based group showed significant improvement immediately after the intervention and one month later [49]. Based on the findings of this study and others, it is recommended to utilize both lecture and debate-based educational methods to enhance moral judgment, although the effectiveness of the debate-based method appears to be more pronounced.

This study encountered several limitations. The questionnaire used to assess moral judgment was the short form of the Rest et al. Moral Judgment questionnaire. This tool may not have been entirely suitable for measuring moral judgment, as some of the scenarios are not directly related to the fields of health and treatment. Nevertheless, this questionnaire has been used in similar studies, and no alternative instruments were identified. In this research, two standard educational scenarios were either designed or selected for the eleven axes derived from the Iranian Nursing Ethics Code, which were consistent across the groups being taught. This consistency could potentially influence the results due to the students’ interactions with one another. Therefore, there is a need to develop a broader range of educational scenarios, and the limitations of the extracurricular class should be taken into account. Other limitations of the study imbalance in intervention duration and the single-center design, short follow-up (1 month) and not constitute true randomization.

Conclusion

Based on the findings, moral sensitivity and moral judgment among nursing students were significantly higher in the debate-based education method compared to the lecture method. Given the importance of understanding the principles of nursing ethics for students, particularly in enhancing moral sensitivity and judgment, it is recommended that students be continuously assessed and monitored throughout the academic year. The study findings indicate that both lecture and debate-based methods contributed to improved moral sensitivity and judgment in students; however, the effectiveness was notably greater in the debate-based group. Therefore, nursing schools can use both lecture and debate-based methods for education. It seems that whenever more time for training, conditions and educational facilities are more conducive, the debate-based method should be prioritized for teaching nursing ethics. Additionally, educational planners and nursing managers may advocate for both methods in the ongoing training and professional development of nurses in the field of nursing ethics, considering their demonstrated effectiveness.
