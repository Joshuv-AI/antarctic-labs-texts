Elevated gamma glutamyl transferase levels as screening marker of stage 3–4 hepatic fibrosis in youth-onset type 2 diabetes mellitus: a retrospective chart review
Authors: Aditi Dasgupta, Panteha Hayati Rezvan, Tania Mitsinikos, Nancy Chang, Juan C. Espinoza, Lily C. Chao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a comorbidity of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) that can lead to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) with liver fibrosis. With rising incidence of youth-onset T2D (YOT2D), there is a need for noninvasive screening tools for MASH with fibrosis. Although multiple screening guidelines recommend measuring alanine transaminase (ALT) at YOT2D diagnosis and at regular intervals, ALT has low sensitivity in identifying MASH with fibrosis. The objective of this study is to identify other noninvasive biomarkers in assessing liver fibrosis severity in YOT2D with MASLD.MethodsA single-center retrospective chart review was conducted on 51 patients with elevated liver transaminases, liver biopsy from 2010 to 2023, and diagnosis of T2D. Clinical, histopathological, imaging, and biochemical data were extracted from electronic medical records. Hepatic fibrosis prediction scores were calculated. Associations between these factors and stages of fibrosis were examined.Results59% of patients had no or stage 1–2 fibrosis and 41% had stage 3–4 fibrosis. Compared to those with no or stage 1–2 fibrosis, patients with stage 3–4 fibrosis were more likely to be male (p = 0.021), had a higher median gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT) (p = 0.026), and higher median liver stiffness on MRE (p = 0.011). For every 10 U/L increase in GGT, the odds of having stage 3–4 fibrosis increased by 20% (odds ratio: 1.20, 95% confidence interval: 1.05, 1.38; p = 0.007), adjusting for age, sex, hemoglobin A1C, and BMI z-score.ConclusionsUsing biopsy proven outcomes, we identified a positive association between GGT levels and hepatic fibrosis stages in YOT2D. Background

The incidence of youth-onset type 2 diabetes (YOT2D) is rising [1, 2]. Children with YOT2D are at risk for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). MASLD is a disease spectrum ranging from excess fat in the liver, or steatosis, to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) with or without fibrosis. MASH with fibrosis can progress to cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma [3, 4]. MASLD is now the most common form of chronic liver disease in children in the United States [5]. The growing prevalence of MASLD and MASH in children follows the rise in youth-onset obesity and YOT2D. This increase poses major, long-lasting risks, including hepatic and extra-hepatic morbidity and mortality, with extra-hepatic morbidity primarily being related to cardiovascular disease [6].

Screening for MASLD is recommended with serum alanine transaminase (ALT) levels. This widely available, cost-effective biomarker is easy to obtain and has sex-specific ranges. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) obesity management guidelines recommend using sex-specific 95th % upper limit of normal (ULN) values for ALT screening (> 22 U/L for girls and > 26 U/L for boys). These thresholds have good sensitivity (85%-92%) and specificity (80%) for detecting MASLD in US children with obesity [7]. For youth with T2D, the International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes recommends screening for MASLD at diagnosis and annually thereafter by measuring ALT and aspartate transaminase (AST) levels [2]. Referral to pediatric gastroenterology should be considered for persistently elevated or worsening transaminases [2, 8]. The prevalence of elevated ALT in YOT2D is unknown but was estimated to be up to 71% of Hispanic and 88% of non-Hispanic children in one study [9].

Although ALT is the recommended test to screen for MASLD, there are several challenges with this screening tool. First, higher ALT levels increase specificity but decrease sensitivity for detecting chronic liver disease [7]. Second, ALT primarily correlates with steatosis but poorly with fibrosis [10]. In a study of adults with asymptomatic T2D and normal transaminases, the prevalence of biopsy proven MASH with fibrosis was 20% [11]. Thus, ALT is not a good predictor of fibrosis. Additional serum markers such as hyaluronic acid, plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1) have been shown to correlate with pediatric MASLD but have not been externally validated and therefore are not routinely used in clinical practice [10].

In addition to single serum markers, hepatic fibrosis prediction scores have been explored as a noninvasive tool. These scores combine clinical and biochemical markers to create a prediction score for MASH with fibrosis. Scores like the NAFLD fibrosis score (NFS), AST to Platelet Ratio Index (APRI), and Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) have been validated in adult populations; however, they have not shown the same level of performance in pediatric populations [3, 10, 12, 13]. The reason for the poor performance of adult hepatic fibrosis prediction scores in pediatric populations is not clear but could relate to the use of age in the calculation of the risk score. A pediatric-specific hepatic fibrosis prediction score, the pediatric NAFLD fibrosis index (PNFI) was developed and showed promise. However, PNFI includes waist circumference as a measurement, which is not routinely obtained in clinical practice, limiting the feasibility of validating and adopting its use [14].

Another noninvasive tool for detecting MASH with fibrosis is imaging. Vibration-controlled transient elastography is a cost-effective, ultrasound-based modality that has been shown to have a strong correlation with pediatric liver fibrosis. Limitations include inter-operator variability and body mass index (BMI) above 30 kg/m2 [15]. Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) is another form of imaging that can be performed independently of abdominal wall fat deposition while also assessing the entire liver. MRE use is limited by cost and availability, however, and therefore is not widely used. Thus, while imaging is a helpful adjunct in determining MASLD disease severity, it is not used alone for diagnosis [16].

With the rising incidence of YOT2D, there is a growing need to identify noninvasive screening tools for MASH with fibrosis. While studies have examined the role of noninvasive methods for predicting MASH with fibrosis in the pediatric population, no study has specifically investigated YOT2D patients. Additionally, liver biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosing MASH with fibrosis. Due to cost and safety, however, the use of liver biopsy is limited in pediatrics. Many studies use elevations in serum ALT or ultrasound findings as surrogate indicators of MASH with fibrosis. Our study is the first to look at YOT2D with confirmed fibrosis on percutaneous liver biopsy. We aim to assist primary care providers and pediatric endocrinologists by identifying an accessible biomarker to assess liver fibrosis severity in youth with T2D.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective chart review at a single pediatric hospital. We created a query using procedure codes for liver biopsy (any type) and ICD-9/10 codes for T2D or a prescription for metformin between 2010 and 2023. We defined youth-onset T2D as age of T2D diagnosis below 20 years. Our query yielded 110 total patients aged less than 20 years at the time of liver biopsy. Patient charts were then reviewed to ensure they were eligible for inclusion. Inclusion criteria included [1] age 10–20 years [2] liver biopsy and [3] T2D (defined as A1C ≥ 6.5% at any point prior to biopsy and clinician determination of diagnosis). Exclusion criteria included [1] other liver diseases (Wilson’s syndrome, liver transplantation, viral hepatitis, alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency, autoimmune hepatitis, metabolic disorder) [2], history of cancer [3], history of congenital heart disease, or [4] non-T2D (type 1 diabetes, secondary diabetes, mature onset diabetes of youth). Criteria for liver biopsy at Children’s Hospital Los Angeles includes patients with persistent elevation in transaminases despite lifestyle changes without definitive diagnosis. The Institutional Review Board of Children’s Hospital Los Angeles approved the protocol and determined that written informed consent for participation was not required.

Histopathological examination

All patients received percutaneous liver biopsies. Histopathologic samples were reviewed by institutional pathologists and were graded using the METAVIR NAFLD scoring system. Stages of fibrosis include stage 0: no fibrosis; stage 1: portal tract fibrosis without septa; stage 2: portal tract fibrosis with rare septa formation; stage 3: numerous septa/bridging fibrosis; and stage 4: cirrhosis [17].

Laboratory tests and clinical data

Laboratory tests including serum AST (U/L), ALT (U/L), total alkaline phosphatase activity (Alk phos)(U/L), GGT (U/L), total bilirubin (mg/dL), platelets (K/uL), albumin (g/dL), total protein (g/dL), and hemoglobin A1C (HgbA1C) (%). BMI (kg/m2) was calculated for all patients. All parameters (laboratory values and BMI) were the most proximal in time to liver biopsy, with the majority obtained one-week preceding biopsy.

Imaging

MRE data was collected for patients if the study was completed prior to biopsy. An MRE value of 2.70 kPa was the threshold for detection of stage 2 fibrosis or greater based on existing pediatric literature [16].

Noninvasive hepatic fibrosis prediction scores

The following prediction scores were calculated for each patient. NFS was calculated as -1.675 + (0.037 x age [years]) + (0.094 x BMI [kg/m2]) + (1.13 x impaired fasting glucose or diabetes [yes = 1, no = 0]) + (0.99 x AST/ALT ratio) - (0.013 x platelet [109/L]) - (0.66 x albumin [g/dL]) [17]. Race and ethnicity

Race and ethnicity were initially recorded as two separate variables in the electronic health record, but we combined them into a single variable, “race/ethnicity”, to better reflect Southern California’s large Latino population and to align with the updates to the Statistical Policy Directive No. 15: Standards for Maintaining, Collecting, and Presenting Federal Data on Race and Ethnicity [20, 21]. The recoding followed these rules: 1) if ethnicity was recorded as Hispanic and race as Other, Unknown, or White, we reclassified race/ethnicity as Latino”; 2) if ethnicity was Hispanic and race as Asian, Black, or More than one race, we reclassified as “Multiple races”; 3) if both race and ethnicity were unknown, we categorized them as “Unknown”.

Statistical analysis

We initially compared patients’ demographics, clinical, and laboratory characteristics across different stages of fibrosis using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables. We then explored associations between fibrosis staging and potential clinical factors via univariate ordinal logistic regression models. Upon identifying significant associations, we systematically incorporated additional factors into the models that were expected to play a role in predicting stages of fibrosis. Covariates were entered one at a time and their impact was assessed individually before considering their collective effect. Statistical significance was determined a priori at 𝛼 = 0.05 (2-sided). Analyses were conducted in Stata MP version-18.0 [22].

Results

Sample characteristics

Out of the initial sample of 110 patients, 53 met the eligibility criteria. Twenty-four patients had other liver diseases, 16 patients had history of cancer, 1 had history of congenital heart disease, 16 had non-T2D diagnosis, and 2 patients were excluded for availability of data. This yielded a total of 51 total patients. Table 1 summarizes the demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of the patients. The median age was 16 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 14, 18) and more than half of the patients were male (n = 29, 56.9%). The majority identified as Latino (n = 43, 72.3%). Roughly 86% of the patients had public health insurance (n = 44). The median BMI was 34.4 kg/m2 [32.4, 39.7] and the median HgbA1C was 6.9% [6.3, 8.7]. Transaminases were elevated with median AST of 93 U/L [59, 126] and median ALT of 152 U/L [92, 244]. Median value of NFS was − 2.6 [-3.1, -1.9], FIB-4 was 0.4 [0.3, 0.6], and APRI was 0.8 [0.5, 1.1].

Comparison of characteristics across different fibrosis stages in YOT2D patients

The prevalence of fibrosis was as follows: stage 0 fibrosis, 4% (n = 2); stage 1 fibrosis, 27.4% (n = 14); stage 2 fibrosis, 27.4% (n = 14); stage 3 fibrosis, 37.3% (n = 19); stage 4 fibrosis, 4% (n = 2). Due to the limited sample size, we grouped fibrosis stages into three categories: none or stage 1 (n = 16, 31.4%); stage 2 (n = 14, 27.4%); and stage 3 or 4 (n = 21, 41.2%). As shown in Table 2, compared to patients with no (stage 0 or 1) or mild fibrosis (stage 2), patients with stage 3–4 fibrosis were more likely to be male (p = 0.021) and had a higher median GGT (p = 0.026). By contrast, ALT showed no statistical significance across stages of fibrosis (p = 0.9688). The median liver stiffness on MRE was higher among patients diagnosed with stage 3–4 fibrosis compared to those with no or early-stage fibrosis (p = 0.011). Among the patients with stage 2–4 fibrosis by biopsy and MRE data, 10 out of 19 patients were classified as having no fibrosis by MRE, using a threshold of 2.7 kPa (Table 2). There were no additional statistically significant associations between other demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables with the stages of fibrosis.

We further explored the odds ratio (OR) of stage 3–4 fibrosis based on GGT in Table 3. Specifically, for every 10-unit (U/L) increase in GGT, the odds of having stage 3–4 fibrosis increased by 17% (odds ratio [OR]: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.33; p = 0.019). No evidence of univariate associations between fibrosis stages and other clinical factors, including the noninvasive fibrosis scores NFS, FIB-4, or APRI, was found (Table S1).

Discussion

In this study of 51 children with biopsy-proven liver fibrosis and YOT2D with elevated transaminases, we observed an association between GGT levels and hepatic fibrosis stages. There was no statistically significant association between ALT level or any other clinical or biochemical markers with fibrosis stages. Similarly, our study found no associations between the stage of fibrosis and three noninvasive hepatic fibrosis prediction scores used in adults. ALT is an imperfect screen for MASH with fibrosis, and imaging modalities alone are not used to diagnose fibrosis. Our study highlights the potential utility of GGT levels as a screening tool for MASH with fibrosis in YOT2D, especially in the context of elevated transaminases. Future studies with larger sample size are needed to determine if GGT is an appropriate screening tool for hepatic fibrosis in YOT2D. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the relationship between GGT and biopsy proven MASH with fibrosis in pediatric patients with T2D.

GGT levels have been shown to correlate with glycemia in adults. Several studies have demonstrated a correlation between GGT and HbA1c in newly diagnosed adults with T2D or first-degree relatives of those with T2D, although the GGT ranges are mostly within the normal limits [23, 24]. Gohel and Chacko also reported a correlation between HbA1c and GGT, with GGT ranges extending into the 70s U/L range [25]. However, this cross-sectional study had no imaging or biopsy evaluation, it is thus unknown if HbA1c or GGT levels correlate with underlying fibrosis. In an adult cohort with T2D who underwent liver biopsy, Salmela et al. demonstrated an association between GGT and glycemic control and BMI, but not severity of liver biopsy stages [26]. In contrast, we did not observe differences in HbA1C levels across fibrosis stages or association between HbA1c and GGT. Of note, the Salmela study used a different histopathological staging for liver fibrosis than our study, which may contribute to the differences in outcomes [26]. The difference may also be attributable to our population of adolescents compared to older adults with T2D, highlighting the importance of conducting studies in pediatric population rather than making inferences from adult studies.

Our study also illustrates the limitations of MRE in detecting MASH with fibrosis. Although higher MRE readings were associated with stage of fibrosis, using the current cut-off of 2.7 kPa, MRE misclassified 10 out of 19 (53%) patients with biopsy-proven fibrosis as not having fibrosis. This may suggest that kPA cutoffs in this population need to be lowered. Our small sample size limits the applicability of this interpretation. Furthermore, due to high costs and limited availability, MRE is currently confined to use within academic centers.

Current treatment modalities for pediatric MASLD are limited and primarily consist of lifestyle interventions, however novel therapies are under investigation and could significantly impact youth with MASLD and T2D. Newer therapies such as GLP-1 agonists have shown positive results in phase 2 adult studies, but studies in adolescents are needed [27, 28]. In 2024, resmetirom became the first medication to receive FDA approval for the treatment of moderate to advanced liver fibrosis due to MASLD in adults aged 18 and older [29]. The development of a treatment for this chronic condition further underscores the urgent need to develop noninvasive tools to predict fibrosis in our younger populations.

The major strength of our study is the use of the gold standard of liver biopsy in staging fibrosis in YOT2D. Our study also includes a racially diverse and primarily publicly insured sample. Our results support several findings in the YOT2D population that have been shown in non-diabetes cohorts of pediatric populations. First, ALT level is not associated with severity of MASH with fibrosis in YOT2D with persistent transaminase elevation. Second, hepatic fibrosis predictions scores are not associated with severity of pediatric liver fibrosis in YOT2D (2, 9, 14–15).

One limitation of our study was a relatively small sample size, due to limitations in pediatric liver biopsy. Second, our population of YOT2D patients had persistent elevations in transaminases despite lifestyle changes, prompting liver biopsy. Not all YOT2D patients have persistence in transaminase elevation, potentially limiting the applicability of our results to the broader population. Third, there was a notable skew towards more advanced stages of liver disease in our cohort, resulting in paucity of patients with stage 0 fibrosis, while a significant proportion, 41%, exhibited stage 3 fibrosis or higher. This pattern may reflect the clinical decision of conducting liver biopsy in patients at the highest risk for liver fibrosis, as determined by the pediatric gastroenterologist’s assessment. Our study was conducted at a large tertiary care center. Children referred to tertiary care centers often present with more advanced disease, potentially skewing our findings towards such cases. Therefore, caution should be exercised when applying our results to the general population.

In conclusion, our study identified a connection between GGT levels and hepatic fibrosis stages in YOT2D patients with biopsy-proven MASH with fibrosis. While most non-invasive biomarkers and hepatic fibrosis prediction scores did not perform well, patients with stage 3–4 fibrosis were more likely to have higher median GGT levels. Further studies are needed to validate our findings and to help determine if there is a threshold for GGT value at which YOT2D should be referred to pediatric gastroenterology. Nevertheless, our results suggest that primary care providers caring for youth with T2D may consider incorporating GGT levels into the screening tool for patients with elevated transaminases, helping prioritization of referrals to pediatric gastroenterology.
