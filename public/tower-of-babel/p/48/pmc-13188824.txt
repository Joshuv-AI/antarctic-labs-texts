Asymptomatic multisegmental epidural hematoma following unilateral biportal endoscopic lumbar decompression: a case report
Authors: Chun-li Zeng, Jian-cai Zhang, Jin-song Yu, Meng-jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis case is noteworthy as it presents an asymptomatic multisegmental epidural hematoma spanning five spinal segments (T12–L4) following unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) decompression, with the patient achieving favorable recovery without surgical intervention. This phenomenon has rarely been documented in existing literature and may serve as a reference for the management of epidural hematoma after UBE procedures.Case presentationAn 82-year-old female patient (Chinese, the Han nationality) presenting with refractory left L5 radiculopathy and neurogenic claudication was diagnosed with degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (DLSS) at L4–L5. Following unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) decompression surgery, postoperative computed tomography (CT) scans revealed extensive epidural hematomas spanning multiple spinal levels from T12 to L4. The epidural hematoma resolved with conservative treatment. At the 3-month postoperative assessment, the patient reported minimal lower limb pain and exhibited no bladder or bowel dysfunction. A follow-up magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at 50 days postoperatively confirmed complete resolution of the epidural hematoma. Background

Unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) decompression has emerged as a paradigm-shifting minimally invasive technique for lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS), celebrated for its reduced tissue trauma and favorable clinical outcomes [1]. While perioperative complications are generally low, with an overall incidence of 6.27% (including dural tears and nerve root irritation), epidural hematoma (EDH) remains a critical concern (0.27%) [2]. Notably, existing literature predominantly describes single-segment or adjacent-segment EDH, with only anecdotal reports of multisegmental involvement (≥ 3 segments), which failed to document any instance of ultra-multisegmental EDH spanning five or more spinal levels [3–5]. Against this backdrop, our case presents a unique clinical scenario: an asymptomatic EDH extending from T12 to L4 (five segments) following UBE decompression, with complete resolution via conservative management. This observation challenges current understanding of EDH pathophysiology in UBE, particularly regarding the mechanisms underlying longitudinal spread across non-adjacent segments. Furthermore, the absence of neurological deficits despite extensive hematoma volume offers critical insights into the natural history of asymptomatic EDH, which may inform risk stratification and avoid unnecessary reoperations [5, 6].

Given the paucity of data on ultra-multisegmental EDH in UBE, this case not only expands the spectrum of complications of this innovative technique but also provides actionable guidance for clinicians navigating similar scenarios.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old female (Chinese, the Han nationality) presented with 12-week refractory left L5 radiculopathy and neurogenic claudication (Visual Analog Scale [VAS] 8/10), characterized by activity-exacerbated distending pain and incomplete rest relief. Prior to the diagnosis of L4–L5 degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (Schizas grade C on MRI, Fig. 1), the patient underwent unsuccessful multimodal conservative management, including structured physiotherapy and pharmacotherapy, while preoperative computed tomography excluded intraspinal hematoma (Fig. 2). The patient maintained well-controlled hypertension for 10 years under continuous antihypertensive therapy. Hematologic screening before surgery revealed no history of hemorrhagic diathesis or exposure to anticoagulants, with normal coagulation profiles. Physical examination demonstrated preserved spinal sagittal alignment, localized tenderness (Grade 1/3), and percussion pain over the L4–L5 spinous processes, along with full active range of motion (lumbar flexion 70°, extension 25°). Radicular pain in the left L5 dermatome (buttock, posterior thigh, lateral calf) was associated with hypesthesia (Grade 1/2) over the lateral calf and lateral malleolus. The patient exhibited a positive left straight leg raise test at 70° (Lasègue's sign) with concomitant dorsiflexion exacerbation, consistent with L5 radiculopathy, while motor strength (L2–S1 myotomes) and sensation were intact in the contralateral limb. Peripheral pulses (dorsalis pedis/posterior tibial) were palpable without trophic changes. Deep tendon reflexes demonstrated 2 + bilaterally at patellar and Achilles tendons, with absent pathological reflexes (Babinski, Clonus). The diagnosis of L4–L5 canal stenosis (Schizas Grade C) with neurogenic claudication was confirmed. A multidisciplinary preoperative assessment confirmed the patient's surgical eligibility, with comprehensive risk stratification excluding major cardiopulmonary compromise Physical Status Classification System (ASA class II), uncorrected coagulopathy, or active spinal infection. Under general anesthesia, UBE decompression was performed via bilateral working ports established 3 cm off midline at L4–L5, utilizing a 30° arthroscope (4 mm) and radiofrequency probe under continuous saline irrigation (pressure maintained at 30 mmHg). Intraoperatively, adequate decompression of L4–L5 was confirmed through endoscopic visualization, as demonstrated by dural sac reexpansion and free nerve root mobility. No epidural vessel injury was documented, with total blood loss of 25 mL. The principal operative time, from skin incision to closure, was 88 minutes. Following attainment of an Aldrete recovery score of ≥ 9 in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), quantitative assessment confirmed resolution of left lower limb radicular pain, with the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) decreasing from 8/10 preoperatively to 2/10, and improvement in the positive straight leg raise (SLR) test to 75°. At 24-hour postoperative assessment, the patient reported complete resolution of left lower limb radicular pain, with no neurological deficits or surgical site complications observed. Early functional recovery was evidenced by independent ambulation > 50 m without support. Drain management followed the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocol, with the surgical drain being removed on postoperative day 3 after confirming a 24-hour output < 5 mL. Persistent capillary oozing from the superficial subcutaneous plexus necessitated targeted suture ligation after failed compression therapy, achieving immediate hemostasis without hematoma formation. A postoperative day 3 computed tomography (CT) with intravenous contrast demonstrated multicompartmental epidural hematomas extending from T12 to L4 (Fig. 3). The UBE technique allows for effective bilateral neural element decompression through a single portal on the symptomatic side, minimizing tissue trauma while addressing central and foraminal stenosis while preserving > 50% of the facet joint complex, as confirmed by postoperative CT imaging. Sequential neurological evaluations showed preserved motor-sensory function in both lower extremities (maintaining ASIA Impairment Scale grade E), with intact bladder/bowel control confirmed by standardized voiding diaries and anorectal manometry. On the fifth day after surgery, the patient strongly insisted on discharge despite unsuccessful attempts to dissuade them. Subsequent weekly phone follow-ups were conducted, advising the patient to promptly return to the hospital if they experienced any lower limb symptoms or abnormalities in urination or defecation. Over the 6-month follow-up period, serial patient-reported outcome measures indicated minimal symptom burden, with Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores maintaining ≤ 8/100 and Numeric Rating Scales (NRS) pain averaging 1.2 ± 0.3 at all scheduled intervals. At the structured 3-month postoperative evaluation, a comprehensive assessment documented absence of clinically significant radiculopathy and preserved sacral function. Sequential MRI surveillance at the 50-day follow-up confirmed complete resolution of the postoperative hematoma, as evidenced by absence of T1 hyperintensity and normalized spinal cord decompression (Fig. 4). A structured telemedicine assessment at postoperative 24 weeks documented the absence of clinically significant lower motor neuron dysfunction and preserved cauda equina function.

Discussion

Minimally invasive endoscopic decompression demonstrates significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss, accelerated functional recovery, and enhanced perioperative safety in comparison to open fusion, making it as the preferred intervention for high-risk elderly patients (ASA ≥ III) with contraindications to instrumented procedures. Symptomatic postoperative spinal epidural hematoma (SEH) represents a critical neurosurgical emergency, with the majority of cases presenting within the critical ≤ 48-hour timeframe, highlighting the importance of evidence-based vigilance protocols in modern spine practice [7]. While asymptomatic SEH may be managed with protocolized nonoperative surveillance, immediate surgical decompression within ≤ 6 hours is imperative for cases manifesting lower extremity motor deficits or evolving cauda equina syndrome. Diagnostic delays exceeding the critical ≤ 6-hour window significantly elevate the risk of permanent neurological deficits [8].

Multisegmental spinal epidural hematoma (mSEH) is a rare yet critical complication associated with endoscopic spine procedures. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying mSEH are not fully understood and may involve specific epidural pressure dynamics unique to minimally invasive techniques. Inadequate intraoperative hemostasis represents a primary etiological factor, wherein ongoing bleeding from epidural venous plexuses and cancellous bone surfaces can adversely impact surgical outcomes. Iatrogenic injury to radiculomedullary vessels during spinal procedures may initiate hematogenous dissemination through the subarachnoid space, facilitated by cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, leading to multidirectional hematoma expansion spanning exceeding 3 spinal segments [7]. Suboptimal drainage management, such as early tube removal (< 24 hours) with low drainage volume (< 30 mL/24 hours), along with improper application of flowable hemostatic agents (for example, gelatin-based materials covering the dura), may impede hematoma evacuation, increasing reoperation risk. Premature drainage tube removal (< 48 hours postoperatively) combined with mechanical compression of the catheter system significantly increased reoperation rates. The drainage catheter was electively discontinued on postoperative day 3 upon meeting standardized extraction criteria (drainage output < 30 mL/24 hours for consecutive 8 hours). Following minor bleeding from small blood vessels at the incision site, initial management with pressure dressing was ineffective, subsequently necessitating suturing for control. There is a possibility that blood may have retrograded into the spinal canal, potentially resulting in hematoma formation. Early ambulation within 24 hours postoperatively significantly heightens risks of recurrent hemorrhage. Inherent coagulopathies coupled with perioperative anticoagulant mismanagement significantly elevate hemorrhagic risk [9]. Elderly patients may have abnormal blood clotting function or increased vascular fragility, predisposing them to an increased risk of hematoma formation [10]. In spinal endoscopic surgery, hypotensive anesthesia is commonly employed during the procedure to minimize surgical field bleeding. Nevertheless, a rapid rebound in blood pressure before the conclusion of the surgery may increase vascular pressure in the operative area, potentially compromising intraoperative hemostasis or causing new vascular trauma. Research indicates that a rapid blood pressure recovery may significantly increase the risk of postoperative bleeding. To avoid postoperative hemorrhage, it is advisable to gradually normalize blood pressure to preoperative levels before concluding the surgery, thus avoiding abrupt fluctuations in blood pressure [11]. Furthermore, during surgery, it is crucial to minimize excessive traction and trauma to the paravertebral veins and extramedullary vessels whenever possible.

To reduce the risk of spinal hematoma, the following preventive measures are recommended: Promptly address bleeding from the bone surface and muscles using electrocautery, and apply bone wax as needed to seal and arrest bleeding on the bone surface. It is recognized that the extent of surgical trauma correlates with the likelihood of hematoma occurrence [3]. During surgery, meticulous management is crucial: control bleeding meticulously, avoid excessive traction, and minimize damage to paraspinal vessels. For more extensive endoscopic procedures, the placement of a drainage tube and ensuring effective drainage are essential. In UBE procedures, standardized subfascial drainage is routinely established. Proper maintenance of the drainage system—ensuring patency, preventing displacement, and securing fixation—is critical to minimize ongoing mechanical trauma that may predispose to recurrent hemorrhage within the spinal canal and paraspinal regions. Premature removal significantly elevates reoperation risk. Structured early mobilization protocols mandate activity restriction within the initial 24–48 postoperative hours to reduce the risk of recurrent microvascular hemorrhage by managing hemodynamic stress and biomechanical loading. Protocol-driven anticoagulant stewardship requires stratified risk assessment, particularly in advanced-age cohorts (≥ 75 years) or patients with (International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis) ISTH-defined coagulopathies. Precision dosing based on real-time viscoelastic testing and chronopharmacological principles is essential to mitigate hemorrhagic complications. Initiation of anticoagulation therapy demands thorough risk stratification [8, 10]. To mitigate the risk of postoperative intraspinal hemorrhage and hematoma formation in this case, anticoagulant therapy was intentionally avoided. Instead, continuous intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) was implemented as the primary thromboprophylaxis modality during the postoperative care period. Close communication with the anesthesiologist is essential during surgery to effectively return blood pressure to its preoperative level [11]. In this case, we reduced the risk of intraspinal hemorrhage by controlling blood pressure preoperatively and maintaining it at a lower level during the procedure. The neurological sequelae of SEH are linked to the size of the hematoma and its compressive effects. Neurological deficits present as segment-specific radiculomyelopathy, with sensorimotor decline correlating to the degree of spinal canal obstruction and the duration of spinal cord hypoperfusion. Analysis of factors contributing to postoperative intraspinal hematoma formation

CT and MRI are the primary diagnostic tools for intraspinal hematoma assessment. CT imaging rapidly visualizes high-density images within the spinal canal, particularly useful for acute hematomas, but may have reduced sensitivity in detecting chronic hematomas. MRI scans reveal important details [10]: T1-weighted images show isointense or hyperintense signals, while T2-weighted images show hypointense or mixed signals. Enhanced MRI scans reveal ring enhancement (subacute or chronic hematoma). Postoperative surveillance CT demonstrated a multisegmental epidural hematoma extending from T12 to L4, exhibiting critical canal compromise (Fig. 3). Follow-up MRI at 50 days postoperatively confirmed complete hematoma resolution (Fig. 4), evidencing normalized thecal sac morphology and resolution of susceptibility artifacts on Susceptibility Weighted Imaging (SWI) sequences. Continued vigilance for the development of metachronous hematomas at non-contiguous levels remains crucial, especially in patients with coagulopathy profiles [12].

This non-compressive hematoma maintained neurological integrity (ASIA Grade E). Following a conservative management approach guided by protocols and serial neuromonitoring, the hematoma underwent complete radiological resolution without the need for intervention, highlighting the self-remitting nature of such lesions. In contrast, the development of significant neurological deficits (ASIA Grade ≤ C or NASS Grade ≥ 3) due to compressive hematoma represents a surgical emergency, necessitating emergent decompression within the critical 6-hour window post-symptom onset to optimize functional recovery. Postoperative recovery is inseparable from rehabilitation and close observation of the condition. Acute phase of hematoma, strict immobilization and postural management are required to prevent further neural injury. When turning the patient, a rigid lumbar brace should be worn to maintain spinal alignment. Sitting up or standing is prohibited. Administer: mannitol (125 ml q12h for 3–5 days), dexamethasone (10 mg intravenous qd, tapering the dose after 3 days), mecobalamin (0.5 mg tid) to promote nerve repair. Anticoagulant/antiplatelet medications are contraindicated to avoid hematoma expansion. Apply pneumatic compression therapy to prevent deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Assess muscle strength (for example, ankle dorsiflexion, toe flexion), sensation (pain, touch), and sphincter function (indwelling catheterization for dysuria) every 4 hours. If any decrease in muscle strength or worsening of sensory deficits occurs, immediately repeat CT/MRI. On the fifth postoperative day, the patient strongly requested discharge due to the absence of muscle strength or sensory impairment during continuous observation. Post-discharge, functional exercises were instructed, and both the patient and their family members were rehabilitation department medical staff. At home, the patient performed muscle and joint function exercises, as well as straight leg raises to prevent nerve root adhesion, while the muscle strength, sensation, and sphincter function of both lower limbs were continuously monitored. At the follow-up visit 2 weeks later, the patient showed no motor or sensory deficits in the lower limbs. Based on the assessment of deep venous ultrasound findings, the patient was guided to resume activities ranging from bed rest to sitting and walking. During the subsequent 50-day follow-up observation, no signs of neurological impairment were detected. Re-examination by MRI confirmed complete absorption of the hematoma.

Conclusion

In summary, while long-segment epidural hematoma following unilateral biportal endoscopic decompression is uncommon, its potential risks should not be underestimated. In this case, the patient's favorable outcome was achieved through prompt imaging assessment and conservative management, possibly influenced by factors such as inadequate hemostasis during surgery, suboptimal drainage, and abnormal coagulation status. For asymptomatic spinal surgery patients with SEH, vigilant monitoring of lower limb nerve function is paramount. Emergency exploratory decompression surgery should be performed if necessary. In cases of worsening postoperative neurological symptoms, timely evacuation of lumbar epidural hematoma is recommended to facilitate neural recovery. Future research is warranted to better understand the mechanisms of formation and develop preventive strategies to reduce the occurrence of similar complications.
