Venetoclax-Based Regimens in Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukemia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Mohammed Abdulgayoom, Mohammad S. Afana, Leen Haj Saleh, Aadhila Abbas Manthiri, Noor A. Aweer, Mohammad Bakheet, Abdulrahman F. Al-Mashdali, Shehab F. Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) is a biologically heterogeneous myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm with limited disease-modifying treatment options beyond hypomethylating agents (HMAs) and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Venetoclax, a selective BCL-2 inhibitor, is increasingly used off-label in CMML, but its CMML-specific efficacy and safety remain incompletely defined.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with PRISMA 2020. Eligible studies included adult patients with CMML treated with venetoclax-based regimens and reporting extractable CMML-specific outcomes. PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, conference proceedings, and trial registries were searched through August 2025. Random-effects meta-analyses of proportions were performed for complete remission (CR), marrow complete remission (mCR), and overall response rate (ORR).ResultsSeventeen publications representing nine unique studies were included, comprising 145 venetoclax-treated CMML patients. Most regimens combined venetoclax with azacitidine, decitabine, or oral decitabine-cedazuridine, with heterogeneous dosing schedules and frequent CYP3A-guided dose modifications. Responses typically occurred early, within one to two treatment cycles, but durability was generally modest. The pooled CR rate was 19.1% (95% CI: 9.4–34.9; I2 = 55%), the pooled mCR rate was 36.4% (95% CI: 24.7–50.0; I2 = 21%), and the pooled ORR was 71.9% (95% CI: 56.5–83.4; I2 = 56%). Venetoclax-based therapy was associated with substantial myelosuppression, including frequent grade ≥3 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, with clinically relevant infectious complications. Early mortality was low in studies reporting short-term outcomes.ConclusionVenetoclax-based regimens demonstrate measurable but limited activity in CMML, with high overall response rates but low CR rates and modest durability. Prospective CMML-specific trials are needed to define optimal dosing, clarify comparative effectiveness, and identify patients most likely to benefit.

Introduction

Chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) is a clonal hematopoietic malignancy classified by the World Health Organization (WHO 2022) and International Consensus Classification (ICC 2022) as a myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm, reflecting its dual dysplastic and proliferative biology. Clinically, CMML is subtyped into dysplastic and proliferative (pCMML) forms based on a white blood cell count threshold of 13 × 109/L, a distinction that carries biological and prognostic significance. In the 2022 WHO 5th edition and ICC systems, the prior CMML-0 category was removed and CMML is subcategorized using a two-tier blast-based scheme (CMML-1 and CMML-2), where higher blast categories are associated with increased AML transformation risk and inferior overall survival (OS) [1–3].

Prognostication in CMML has evolved substantially with the development of clinical and clinico-molecular scoring systems, including the CMML-specific Prognostic Scoring System (CPSS), its molecularly integrated variant (CPSS-Mol), and other CMML-specific models such as the GFM and Mayo scores. In practice, MDS-derived models such as IPSS-M are also sometimes applied in this overlap neoplasm. These frameworks incorporate clinical variables such as cytopenias, transfusion dependence, and blast percentage, together with cytogenetic risk categories and recurrent mutations – including ASXL1, SRSF2, TET2, RUNX1, and RAS-pathway genes – to stratify patients into meaningful risk groups. High-risk CMML is commonly characterized by proliferative features, excess blasts, ASXL1 or RUNX1 mutations, and RAS-pathway activation, and is associated with shorter survival and higher rates of AML progression [4–8].

Despite the availability of risk-adapted management strategies, therapeutic options for CMML remain limited. Hydroxyurea remains the preferred first-line cytoreductive therapy for pCMML, particularly for the control of leukocytosis, splenomegaly, and symptomatic inflammatory manifestations. For patients with higher risk disease, hypomethylating agents (HMAs) such as azacitidine and decitabine are the most commonly used disease-directed therapies, supported largely by extrapolation from MDS trials and prospective CMML subgroup analyses. Although HMAs can induce hematologic improvement (HI) and delay progression in a subset of patients, their impact on long-term outcomes remains limited. In the randomized phase III DACOTA trial in advanced pCMML, decitabine did not improve event-free survival or OS compared with hydroxyurea, underscoring the absence of clearly disease-modifying benefit with currently available non-transplant approaches. Reported median OS for higher risk CMML generally ranges from 20 to 30 months, highlighting the limitations of existing therapies. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) remains the only potentially curative option, but its use is restricted to a minority of patients because of advanced age, comorbidities, and disease-related morbidity [9–13].

Venetoclax, a selective BCL-2 inhibitor, has shown substantial activity in AML when combined with HMAs, leading to regulatory approval in older or unfit AML patients and expanding interest in its use across related myeloid neoplasms [14]. However, this rationale should be interpreted with caution. In higher risk MDS, the phase III VERONA trial did not demonstrate an OS benefit for azacitidine plus venetoclax compared with azacitidine plus placebo, despite higher response rates in some subgroups, particularly in patients with increased blast burden. These findings underscore that improved response does not necessarily translate into a survival advantage in adjacent myeloid diseases [15]. In addition, preclinical and translational data from monocytic AML suggest relative venetoclax resistance mediated, at least in part, by increased MCL-1 dependence, a mechanism that may be relevant to CMML given its monocytic biology [16]. Nevertheless, the growing off-label use of venetoclax in CMML is supported by a biologically plausible rationale, overlapping therapeutic paradigms with AML and higher risk MDS, and emerging clinical signals of activity from retrospective cohorts and early phase studies [17]. At the same time, the available evidence remains heterogeneous and fragmented, spanning small studies, mixed CMML/MDS/MPN populations, and conference abstracts, with substantial variability in venetoclax dosing, treatment combinations, response criteria, and patient selection.

To date, no comprehensive synthesis has specifically evaluated the use, effectiveness, safety, and limitations of venetoclax-based regimens in CMML. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to consolidate all published and unpublished evidence on venetoclax therapy in CMML, describe the clinical and molecular characteristics of treated populations, summarize treatment patterns and outcomes, and identify knowledge gaps to inform future research and clinical decision-making. Where sufficient data were available, a focused meta-analysis was performed to generate pooled estimates of key response outcomes. Given the position of CMML at the intersection of myelodysplastic and myeloproliferative biology, together with the expanding role of BCL-2 inhibition across hematologic malignancies, defining the therapeutic role of venetoclax in CMML is of direct clinical relevance to the broader hematology-oncology community.

Methods

Study Design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 reporting standards, with the completed checklist provided in online supplementary material S1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551885). The protocol was developed prospectively prior to the literature search. The objective was to synthesize clinical evidence on venetoclax-based therapies in CMML, including response rates, survival outcomes, safety, biomarker associations, and comparative effectiveness versus HMA monotherapy.

Eligibility Criteria

We included studies evaluating adult patients (≥18 years) with CMML (CMML-1/2 per WHO 2022/ICC 2022; dysplastic or proliferative) treated with venetoclax-based regimens in combination with HMAs or other systemic therapies. For legacy reports using the prior CMML-0/1/2 schema, CMML-0 cases were considered within the contemporary CMML-1 category. Eligible studies were required to report at least one clinical efficacy endpoint and to include at least five CMML patients. Mixed MDS/CMML cohorts were included only when CMML-specific outcomes were separately reported.

Studies were excluded if they involved JMML or pediatric populations; comprised preclinical or mechanistic work; were reviews, commentaries, or editorials without original data; were non-English; reported only secondary AML outcomes without extractable CMML-phase data; or consisted of case reports/series with fewer than five CMML patients. Full criteria are summarized in online supplementary Table S1.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane CENTRAL was conducted from database inception through August 2025. Additional sources included gray literature from major hematology conferences (ASH and EHA), clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov), and reference lists of relevant articles and citation networks to identify any missed studies. The full search strategies for all databases are provided in online supplementary material S2.

Study Selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and performed full-text assessments. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or third-reviewer arbitration. Reasons for exclusion were documented. When multiple publications originated from the same patient cohort, only the most complete or most recent publication was included to avoid duplication. Data Extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a piloted template. Extracted variables included study design, setting, CMML sample size, patient characteristics, venetoclax regimen (dose, schedule, combinations, CYP3A4 adjustments), efficacy outcomes, safety, AML transformation, and transplant use. Subgroup and biomarker findings were also captured.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Formal risk-of-bias tools such as ROBINS-I were not applied because the included evidence consisted primarily of small, retrospective cohorts with heterogeneous designs and noncomparable outcome reporting, for which quantitative scoring would be misleading. Instead, sources of bias were assessed qualitatively and integrated into interpretation.

Data Synthesis

Descriptive Synthesis

Given substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, all studies were synthesized descriptively with structured tables summarizing patient characteristics, venetoclax regimens, efficacy outcomes, safety, and transplant use.

Meta-Analysis

A random-effects meta-analysis of proportions was performed for the three outcomes most consistently reported across studies: complete remission (CR), marrow complete remission (mCR), and overall response rate (ORR). Proportions were pooled using a logit transformation with a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model. Heterogeneity was quantified using I2 and τ2 statistics. Studies lacking extractable denominators were excluded from pooling.

Subgroup and Biomarker Analyses

Due to small sample sizes and inconsistent reporting, subgroup and molecular analyses were synthesized narratively. Associations between venetoclax response and mutations (e.g., ASXL1, TET2, RAS-pathway, IDH1/2, NPM1) were extracted qualitatively from individual studies.

Comparative Effectiveness Analyses

Comparative effectiveness data were synthesized descriptively from studies that reported outcomes for venetoclax-based regimens alongside HMA-only comparators, either within the same cohort or using matched external control populations. Reported comparative outcomes, including response rates and survival endpoints, were extracted as presented by the original authors. No independent statistical adjustment, re-analysis, or reconstruction of comparative datasets was undertaken.

Transplant Outcomes

HSCT rates and posttransplant survival outcomes were extracted descriptively. No pooling was performed due to heterogeneity in transplant eligibility, timing, and conditioning regimens.

Results

Study Selection

The systematic search retrieved 453 records from Embase, PubMed, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov. After removing 112 duplicates, 341 unique citations underwent title and abstract screening, of which 281 were excluded. The remaining 60 full-text articles were reviewed in detail, leading to the exclusion of 41 studies for reasons including insufficient sample size, nonrelevant interventions or outcomes, non-English language, or lack of extractable CMML-specific data. In total, 19 records met the inclusion criteria. After consolidating multiple reports originating from the same cohorts, these 17 publications represented 9 unique clinical studies. The study selection process is summarized in Figure 1.

Study Characteristics

The final analysis included nine unique studies [17–25] derived from 17 published reports (Table 1). Publication years ranged from 2021 to 2025, whereas patient enrollment periods spanned a much wider timeframe (2010–2025). Most studies were conducted in the USA, with one originating from China. Regarding study design, the evidence base consisted predominantly of retrospective observational cohorts, supplemented by two phase I/II prospective clinical trials. The publication types included full manuscripts, conference abstracts, and letters with extractable CMML-specific outcomes. Most studies were single-center, although several multicenter cohorts – including Csizmar et al. [21], Saliba et al. [24], and Tremblay et al. [25] – were also represented.

Patient and Disease Characteristics

Across the 9 studies, CMML sample sizes and populations varied, but demographics were broadly consistent with typical CMML, with median ages in the late 60s to mid-70s and a male predominance (Table 2). Clinical CMML subtypes showed considerable variability: several cohorts were enriched for proliferative disease (e.g., Bazinet et al. [20]; Li et al. [22]; Rockwell et al. [23]), whereas others included mainly dysplastic presentations (Bataller et al. [19]; Csizmar et al. [21]). Overall, a slight predominance of pCMML was observed across studies.

WHO/ICC categories, when reported, frequently reflected higher risk disease, with notable representation of CMML-2 in Bazinet et al. [20], Montalban-Bravo et al. [17], and Tremblay et al. In summary, venetoclax-treated cohorts represented a clinically heterogeneous but overall high-risk CMML population, consistent with patterns seen in contemporary observational studies.

Venetoclax Regimens and Dosing

Venetoclax dosing varied considerably across studies, reflecting differences in study design, backbone regimens, and concomitant medications (online suppl. Table S2). Most cohorts used short-course schedules of 14 days per 28-day cycle, consistent with AML-derived protocols, although some employed modified or metronomic approaches. For example, weekly venetoclax administration was used in metronomic decitabine-based regimens, while cycle lengths ranged from 7 to 28 days in institutional practice-based studies, depending on cytopenia recovery and patient tolerance. Backbone therapy was predominantly HMA-based, using azacitidine, decitabine, or oral decitabine-cedazuridine combinations. Combination partners beyond HMAs were uncommon, with low-dose cytarabine, arsenic trioxide, or investigational agents used only in isolated cases.

Across studies, venetoclax dosing was frequently modified based on CYP3A inhibitor use, particularly with triazole antifungal prophylaxis. Reported strategies typically included 50–75% dose reductions for moderate or strong inhibitors, whereas some cohorts did not specify formal adjustment protocols. Overall, venetoclax administration in CMML was characterized by heterogeneous dosing schedules, near-universal integration with HMAs, and routine CYP3A-guided dose modification when antifungal prophylaxis was used.

Efficacy Outcomes

Response Definitions

Response assessment varied across studies. Most cohorts evaluated treatment responses using the IWG 2006 criteria originally developed for MDS, sometimes applied to CMML as modified IWG 2006 definitions. Several studies instead used the MDS/MPN response criteria proposed by Savona et al. [26], while some reports referenced updated HI definitions consistent with the IWG 2018 revision. This variability in response frameworks likely contributes to heterogeneity in reported response outcomes. The response criteria applied in each study are summarized in Table 2, and detailed response definitions are provided in online supplementary Table S3.

Response Rates across Studies

Across the nine venetoclax-treated CMML cohorts (n = 145), response outcomes were heterogeneous but showed consistent clinical activity (Fig. 2). Prior therapy exposure varied substantially across cohorts and represents a major source of heterogeneity. Several studies evaluated clearly HMA-naïve/frontline populations (Bataller et al. [19]; Rockwell et al. [23]; Li et al. [22] HMA + VEN cohort; Tremblay et al. [25]), whereas others included mixed populations comprising both HMA-naïve and HMA-failure patients (Ball et al. [18]; Bazinet et al. [20]; Montalban-Bravo et al. [17]; Saliba et al. [24]; Csizmar et al. [21]) (Table 2). Because most mixed cohorts did not provide CMML-specific outcomes stratified by prior HMA exposure, a formal subgroup meta-analysis comparing HMA-naïve versus HMA-exposed CMML was not feasible. Accordingly, response patterns were interpreted descriptively in the context of treatment status. In addition, because response definitions differed across studies (IWG 2006, modified IWG 2006, and Savona et al. [26] MDS/MPN criteria), and HI capture was inconsistent, pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, particularly for mCR and mCR + HI endpoints.

Complete Remission. CR rates varied widely across studies, ranging from 0% to 62.5%. The highest CR frequencies were observed in treatment-naïve cohorts utilizing metronomic or oral HMA backbones, including Rockwell et al. [23] (62.5%) and Bataller et al. [19] (50%). Moderate CR rates were reported in Bazinet et al. [20] (33%) and Tremblay et al. [25] (22%), whereas minimal CR activity was observed in mixed or heavily pretreated cohorts (e.g., 4–8% in Montalban-Bravo et al. [17]; Csizmar et al. [21]; Ball et al. [18]). No CRs were observed in Saliba et al. [24]. In the random-effects meta-analysis, the pooled CR rate was 19.1% (95% CI: 9.4–34.9%), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 ≈ 55%), reflecting differences in disease risk, prior therapy exposure, backbone regimen, and venetoclax dosing. Notably, higher CR frequencies were predominantly observed in HMA-naïve cohorts, including Tremblay et al. [25], Bataller et al. [19], and Rockwell et al. [23], supporting treatment status as an important contributor to between-study variability.

Marrow CR. mCR was reported inconsistently but showed higher activity than CR. Reported mCR rates ranged from 20% to 48% in retrospective cohorts (e.g., Csizmar et al. [21]; Montalban-Bravo et al. [17]), and 33% in both Bazinet et al. [20] and Bataller et al. [19]. Several studies (Rockwell et al. [23]; Saliba et al. [24]; Tremblay et al. [25]) did not provide mCR data. In some cohorts, mCR occurred without HI, suggesting cytoreduction without full recovery. In a random-effects meta-analysis of proportions, the pooled mCR rate was 36.4% (95% CI: 24.7–50.0%), with low-moderate heterogeneity (I2 ≈ 21%).

Overall Response Rate. ORR was more consistently robust across studies, ranging from 50% to 100%. The highest ORRs were observed in smaller treatment-naïve cohorts (Bataller et al. [19]; Bazinet et al. [20]; Rockwell et al. [23]; all 100%) and in the large prospective study by Tremblay et al. [25] (90%). Lower ORRs were noted in mixed-risk or previously treated populations, including Saliba et al. [24] (50%), Csizmar et al. [21] (53%), and Ball et al. [18] (62%). In the pooled meta-analysis, the ORR was 71.9% (95% CI, 56.5–83.4%), again with moderate heterogeneity (I2 ≈ 56%). Similarly, the highest ORRs were largely concentrated in HMA-naïve cohorts, while mixed or previously treated populations generally demonstrated lower CR depth despite preserved ORRs.

mCR with HI. Reporting of mCR with HI (mCR + HI) was limited across studies, with only five cohorts providing extractable data. Rates ranged from 7% to 33%, generally reflecting modest hematologic recovery even when marrow blast clearance was achieved. The highest mCR+HI rate was observed in the Bazinet et al. [20] cohort (33%), a small CMML-2 cohort partially HMA-naïve. Intermediate rates were reported by Montalban-Bravo et al. [17] (11%) and Bataller et al. [19], 17%), while minimal benefit was seen in Ball et al. [18], 8%) and Csizmar et al. [21], 7%). No mCR + HI data were available for Rockwell et al. [23], Saliba et al. [24], or Tremblay et al. [25].

Response Kinetics and Durability

Across cohorts, responses occurred early, typically after 1–2 cycles. Where evaluated, duration of response (DOR) was variable but generally limited, ranging from 3 months (Csizmar et al. [21]) to ∼9 months in Tremblay et al. [25], with intermediate durability in Bazinet et al. [20] (6.3 months). These findings indicate that venetoclax-based regimens can induce rapid and deep remissions, although response durability remains modest, particularly in relapsed/refractory disease.

OS and Leukemic Transformation

OS was reported heterogeneously across the included studies, with most cohorts providing median OS descriptively rather than hazard ratios or other standardized time-to-event measures. Among cohorts with numerically available median OS, survival generally ranged from 9.0 to 19.1 months, including Montalban-Bravo et al. [17] (relapsed CMML, 9.0 months), Saliba et al. [24] (13.8 months), Csizmar et al. [21] (14.3 months), Bazinet et al. [20] (14.5 months; 95% CI: 9.1–NC), Ball et al. [18] (15.6 months), and Tremblay et al. [25] (HMA + VEN, propensity-score matched cohort, 19.1 months). By contrast, median OS was not reached in the treatment-naïve CMML cohort reported by Montalban-Bravo et al. [17] or in the HMA + VEN arm of Li et al. [22], underscoring the variability in survival according to treatment setting, baseline disease characteristics, and duration of follow-up. Overall, the available data suggest modest survival outcomes in most venetoclax-treated CMML cohorts, with some variability that likely reflects differences in prior therapy exposure, disease risk, cohort composition, and study design. Because OS was typically reported as median values without hazard ratios, variance estimates, or other standardized survival metrics, formal meta-analysis of survival outcomes was not feasible. Accordingly, OS data were summarized descriptively and visualized using a forest-style plot of reported median OS values (Fig. 3) to facilitate cross-study comparison.

Comparative Effectiveness of HMA + VEN versus HMA Alone

Two studies assessed the comparative effectiveness of venetoclax-based therapy versus HMA monotherapy in CMML, although using different methodological approaches (Li et al. [22]; Tremblay et al. [25]). Li et al. [22] was a retrospective cohort study that directly included both HMA-only and HMA + VEN treatment groups. In the CMML subgroup, the ORR was numerically higher with HMA + VEN compared with HMA monotherapy (53.8% vs. 31.6%); however, CR (7.7% vs. 0%) and mCR (7.7% vs. 5.3%) rates remained low across both arms, and no differences reached statistical significance. Survival outcomes were likewise comparable: median OS was not reached with HMA + VEN versus 14.7 months with HMA alone (p = 0.38), and EFS showed no significant difference (17.3 vs. 13.4 months; p = 0.91).

Tremblay et al. [25], in contrast, included only patients treated with upfront HMA + VEN. To allow comparison with HMA monotherapy, the investigators used a propensity score-matched external control cohort of CMML patients who had received HMA alone. In this matched analysis, ORR was markedly higher with HMA + VEN (91% vs. 39%; p < 0.001); however, no OS benefit was observed (median 19.1 vs. 19.1 months; p = 0.85). Collectively, the comparative evidence demonstrates that venetoclax combinations consistently improve response depth relative to HMA monotherapy, but survival benefits remain variable and may be influenced by disease risk, patient selection, and the extent of allo-HSCT utilization across cohorts.

Venetoclax as a Bridge to Allogeneic Stem Cell Transplantation

Multiple studies highlight the use of venetoclax-based regimens as a potential bridge to allo-HSCT. In Ball et al. [18], Csizmar et al. [21], and Montalban-Bravo et al. [17] cohorts, approximately 20–32% of CMML patients proceeded to transplant following VEN-based therapy. Tremblay et al. [25] reported the largest transplant experience, with 43% of CMML patients treated with HMA + VEN proceeding to allo-HSCT; in that study, the median OS after HSCT was 40.1 months.

Safety and Tolerability

Venetoclax-based regimens in CMML were consistently associated with substantial hematologic toxicity, particularly neutropenia and thrombocytopenia (online suppl. Table S4). Across studies, reported grade ≥3 or severe cytopenias were frequent, including neutropenia in 39%–74% and thrombocytopenia in 35%–85% where specifically reported. Ball et al. [18] reported grade 4 neutropenia in 61% and grade 4 thrombocytopenia in 35%, while Bataller et al. [19] reported neutropenia in 74% and thrombocytopenia in 85%. Febrile neutropenia was also clinically relevant, reported in 15% in Ball et al. [18], 17% in Bazinet et al. [20], and 21% in Bataller et al. [19], although denominators were not always CMML-specific.

Infectious complications were common among evaluable cohorts. Bataller et al. [19] reported lung infection in 10%, sepsis in 5%, and SARS-CoV-2 infection in 5%. Bazinet et al. [20] reported lung infection in 30% and sepsis in 22% in the overall cohort. In Li et al. [22], lung infection rates in the HMA + VEN arm reached 30.8%, with additional soft tissue infection (7.7%) and upper respiratory tract infection (7.7%). These findings support that infection risk is a major component of the toxicity profile, likely reflecting both treatment-related myelosuppression and baseline disease-related immune compromise.

Early mortality appeared relatively low in studies reporting short-term outcomes. Ball et al. [18] reported 30-day mortality of 4%, whereas Bataller et al. [19] reported 0% at 4 weeks and 2.6% at 8 weeks. However, early mortality was not uniformly reported across studies. Treatment discontinuation also varied substantially and was driven by heterogeneous reasons including refractory cytopenias, progression, AML transformation, death, and transition to allo-HSCT. Ball et al. [18] reported a 62% discontinuation rate, largely due to refractory cytopenias and progressive disease, while Bataller et al. [19] more commonly reported discontinuation related to allo-HSCT. Rockwell et al. [23] reported discontinuation in 37.5%, largely due to death.

Reported non-hematologic adverse events were variable and less systematically captured, but included fatigue, edema, constipation, nausea, headache, electrolyte disturbances, tumor lysis syndrome, bleeding events, cardiac toxicity, hepatic dysfunction, and hospitalizations. Notable serious toxicities included tumor lysis syndrome in 9% in Csizmar et al. [21], spontaneous bleeding in 9% including one fatal intracranial hemorrhage, and in Saliba et al. [24] grade 3–4 heart failure (10%), grade 3 tumor lysis syndrome (10%), neutropenic enterocolitis, and intracranial/retinal hemorrhage.

Overall, venetoclax-based regimens in CMML appear to have a safety profile dominated by predictable myelosuppression and infection risk, with occasional serious bleeding, cardiac, or metabolic complications. However, these toxicity estimates should be interpreted with caution because most included studies were retrospective and adverse event reporting was heterogeneous and likely incomplete. Compared with prospective trials, retrospective cohorts are less likely to capture lower grade, transient, and non-hematologic toxicities in a standardized manner. This limitation underscores the need for prospective safety reporting in future CMML studies of venetoclax-based therapy.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, venetoclax-based therapy showed modest clinical activity in CMML. CR rates were uniformly low across studies, with a pooled CR rate of 19%, indicating that deep marrow remission is uncommon. mCR occurred more frequently, with a pooled rate of 36%, although its clinical significance varied across cohorts, depending on whether HI was achieved. ORRs were higher, with a pooled estimate of 72%, but most responses reflected partial responses or disease stabilization rather than CR. The durability of the benefit was limited, with responses typically lasting only several months. As observed in AML/MDS, treatment-naïve patients tended to have higher response rates, whereas previously treated or HMA-refractory patients had consistently lower CR and ORR rates [27]. Overall, these findings suggest that venetoclax-based regimens provide limited but measurable activity in CMML. While deep remissions are uncommon, venetoclax may offer short-term disease control in selected patients, particularly in frontline settings or when used as a bridge to allogeneic transplantation.

Treatment options for CMML remain limited and largely non-curative. In pCMML, hydroxyurea continues to be the most commonly used first-line cytoreductive therapy for controlling leukocytosis, splenomegaly, and disease-related symptoms [28]. In higher risk or dysplastic CMML, HMAs such as azacitidine or decitabine represent the preferred disease-modifying treatment, although responses are often partial and transient [29]. Allogeneic HSCT remains the only curative option but is feasible in only a minority of patients [10]. Within this therapeutic landscape, the modest CR and mCR rates observed with venetoclax-based regimens do not support their use as a replacement for HMA monotherapy or hydroxyurea. Although venetoclax combinations appear to produce somewhat deeper responses than historical HMA therapy alone, this has not translated into a consistent survival benefit and is accompanied by substantial myelosuppression. Moreover, mCR without HI has been de-emphasized in more recent response frameworks because of its uncertain correlation with meaningful clinical benefit; therefore, mCR reported in retrospective CMML cohorts should be interpreted cautiously [30]. Taken together, current evidence suggests that venetoclax should be viewed as a selective intensification strategy rather than a universal standard of care, potentially useful in higher risk disease, pCMML requiring rapid cytoreduction, or as a bridge to allogeneic transplantation. In routine practice, treatment decisions should remain individualized, incorporating disease phenotype, risk category, molecular profile, patient fitness, and transplant eligibility.

At the same time, the apparent benefit of venetoclax in transplant-eligible patients must be interpreted cautiously. Observational reports suggesting longer survival among patients who ultimately underwent alloHSCT after venetoclax-based therapy are highly vulnerable to selection bias and immortal-time bias, since only patients who remain alive, responsive, and fit can proceed to transplant. Accordingly, such survival differences should not be taken as evidence that venetoclax itself improves transplant outcomes. This caution is supported by contemporary transplant decision analyses, which have not shown a uniform survival advantage for immediate allo-HSCT across all CMML risk groups and instead emphasize the importance of timing and patient selection [31]. Venetoclax may therefore serve as a useful cytoreductive bridge in selected patients, but its precise role in transplant strategy remains uncertain.

Another important finding of this review is that the molecular landscape of CMML appears to influence response to venetoclax. Across multiple cohorts, RAS-pathway mutations, including NRAS, KRAS, CBL, NF1, and PTPN11, were consistently associated with lower CR rates, early resistance, and inferior survival, making them the clearest adverse predictors of venetoclax activity. These observations align with preclinical data suggesting that RAS-driven disease may escape BCL-2 dependence through altered mitochondrial priming, and they support investigation of future combinations incorporating MAPK-pathway inhibition [32, 33]. By contrast, mutations traditionally associated with adverse prognosis in CMML, particularly ASXL1, did not appear to confer clear resistance to venetoclax in the available studies. This distinction is clinically relevant, as it suggests that poor prognostic markers do not necessarily predict venetoclax refractoriness. Indeed, venetoclax-based therapy may retain activity in myelodysplasia-related disease biology, including epigenetically defined subgroups, a concept supported by translational studies and recent clinico-genomic AML data [34–36]. Several cohorts also described favorable response signals in TET2- and in IDH1/2-mutated disease, consistent with AML experience showing greater apoptotic dependence on BCL-2 in this subgroup [37]. Taken together, the available data suggest that RAS-pathway lesions may identify relative resistance, whereas ASXL1-, TET2-, and possibly IDH-mutated disease may remain compatible with venetoclax sensitivity.

This review is limited by the nature of the available evidence. Most included studies were retrospective, with small CMML subgroups, heterogeneous patient populations, and varied venetoclax dosing schedules and response definitions. Several included reports were derived from broader MDS/CMML or high-risk MDS cohorts in which CMML was a prespecified subgroup; although we only included studies with separately reported CMML outcomes, these CMML-specific estimates may still be influenced by selection and reporting bias. Reporting of key outcomes – such as duration of response, HI, and posttransplant survival – was inconsistent, and molecular data were incomplete in several studies, limiting the ability to perform genotype-specific analyses. Comparative effectiveness findings were based on non-randomized designs, with risk of residual confounding despite matching attempts. Publication bias is also likely, as negative or small CMML experiences may remain unreported. Collectively, these limitations reduce the precision and generalizability of pooled estimates and underscore the need for dedicated prospective CMML trials.

Future research should prioritize prospective, CMML-specific clinical trials evaluating venetoclax-based regimens with standardized dosing, uniform response criteria, and clearly defined endpoints. Randomized studies comparing HMA + venetoclax versus HMA alone in frontline higher risk CMML are needed to determine whether deeper responses translate into meaningful survival benefit. Given the strong resistance signal associated with RAS-pathway mutations, studies incorporating MAPK-pathway inhibitors or other targeted agents represent a rational next step. Molecularly stratified analyses, including MRD assessments and functional profiling, may help refine biomarker-driven patient selection. Additionally, the role of venetoclax as pre-transplant cytoreduction warrants further evaluation, including optimal timing, impact on transplant-related toxicity, and post-transplant outcomes. Large multicenter registries could complement clinical trials by providing real-world data on long-term toxicity, supportive care practices, and survival. Overall, the current evidence base highlights the need for systematic, biologically informed exploration of venetoclax in CMML to clarify its therapeutic niche and define which patients stand to benefit most.

Conclusion

Venetoclax-based regimens demonstrate modest but measurable activity in CMML, with low CR rates, intermediate marrow response rates, and higher ORRs driven largely by partial responses or disease stabilization. Clinical benefit is generally short-lived and appears greatest in treatment-naïve, higher risk, or transplant-intended patients. Prospective CMML-specific trials are needed to define optimal dosing, clarify comparative effectiveness versus standard therapies, and establish molecular predictors of benefit. Until such data are available, venetoclax should be integrated judiciously within individualized, risk-adapted CMML treatment strategies.
