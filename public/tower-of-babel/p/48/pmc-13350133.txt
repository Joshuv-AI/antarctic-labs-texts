The predictive value of contrast-enhanced CT characteristics combined with composite inflammatory markers for lymph node metastasis in T1 colorectal cancer
Authors: Xiaoran Li, Xiaoming Fu, Chuanyang Zhang, Miaomiao Li, Dong Han, Guangdong Fu, Yumei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the predictive value of contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) characteristics of peritumoral lymph node (LN) combined with composite inflammatory markers in lymph node metastasis (LNM) for patients with T1 colorectal cancer (CRC), and evaluated their diagnostic efficacy.MethodsThis retrospective study included 212 patients with T1 CRC (non-LNM: n = 185; LNM: n = 27). The CECT characteristics of the peritumoral LN and inflammatory markers were analyzed. Variables with statistical significance were included in penalized logistic regression (the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, LASSO) for further feature selection and coefficient shrinkage. The diagnostic performance of the prediction models was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic curve. The net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to quantify the incremental predictive value between the models, and the decision curve analysis was plotted to evaluate the clinical practicability.Results27/212 (12.74%) patients had LNM. Six CECT characteristics, three inflammatory markers and six clinical pathological parameters differed significantly between the two groups (all p < 0.05). Eight non-zero coefficient variables were retained. The preoperative prediction model based on CECT characteristics and inflammatory markers had the highest AUC (AUC = 0.830), with NRI and IDI both greater than 0, and had the optimal clinical decision-making value.ConclusionsPreoperative CECT characteristics of LN and composite inflammatory markers were significantly correlated with the occurrence of LNM in T1 CRC patients. The combined model may be useful for the early identification of LNM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-026-05159-x.

Introduction

According to the 2022 Global Cancer Research Center report, colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most common malignant tumor worldwide and is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines (NCCN) indicate that early detection and effective treatment are key to reducing the mortality rate of CRC [2]. Recent minimally invasive therapy &allied technologies (MITAT) initiatives such as the CLASSICA software also reflected the field’s shift toward multicenter, clinically embedded validation of decision-support technologies in CRC oncology [3]. Early-stage (T1) CRC refers to tumor with infiltration limited to the mucosal layer or submucosal layer, typically carrying a favorable prognosis with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate as high as 81.2% [4, 5]. Most early-stage patients can be cured by endoscopic treatment [6]. However, lymph node metastasis (LNM) still occurs in 7%–15% of T1 CRC patients [7], which will lead to the progression in CRC staging and poor prognosis, as well as these patients often require additional radical surgery, lymph node (LN) dissection, and even radiotherapy/chemotherapy [8]. Such interventions result in reduced quality of life and increased financial burden for patients. Accurately predicting the risk of LNM in T1 CRC is of great significance in clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) has been recommended by NCCN as the preferred preoperative examination for CRC [2, 9]. It can help physicians determine the exact location and size of the primary lesion and LNs. Johnson et al. reported that using CT to prospectively observe morphological features of the colon and rectum improved the detection rate of CRC [10]. Chen et al. demonstrated that using CECT to analyze the enhancement degree of CRC and the ratio of low-density within the tumor can predict the prognosis status [11]. Most studies still focus on the characteristics of CRC, while the specific details of the peritumoral LNs, especially small LNs (short-axis diameter < 5 mm), are also worthy exploration. Furthermore, due to the anatomical peculiarities of T1 CRC lesion, it may be difficult to visualize on CT. In contrast, peritumoral LNs exist independently within the adipose background of the abdominal and pelvic cavities, with clear demarcation from surrounding tissues, making them easier to observe.

The occurrence and progression of malignant tumors are not isolated events, but rather a dynamic process that interacts with the complex microenvironment of the body [12]. In recent years, an increasing number of research have shown that the inflammatory microenvironment plays a pivotal role in intestinal tumor [13, 14]. Some studies have reported that platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), Lymphocyte-monocyte ratio (LMR) and other indicators were significantly correlated with pathological T stage, OS rate and prognosis of CRC patients [15–18]. However, the correlation between changes in systemic inflammatory response and the LNM in patients with T1 CRC has not been well explored so far.

Therefore, we primarily investigated the clinical value of combining CECT features of peritumoral LN with inflammatory markers in predicting LNM of T1 CRC in our study. Then, we developed multiple models and evaluated their diagnostic efficacy, aiming to identify the optimal model for prediction of LNM in T1 CRC before surgery.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital. As this was a retrospective study, the ethics committee waived the requirement for informed consent.

A total of 258 patients with T1 CRC from January 2018 to May 2025 were included. All patients underwent standard surgical tumor resection and LN dissection. The inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically confirmed T1 CRC with known LN status; (2) complete inflammatory markers information; (3) available CECT examination within 2 weeks before surgery. Patients were excluded with any of the following criteria: (1) received anti-tumor therapy preoperative such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy; (2) accompanied with severe primary diseases of important organs such as the heart, liver; (3) clinical evidence of infection or other inflammatory diseases; (4) a history of other cancers; (5) taken non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or anticoagulants within the last 3 months.

Finally, 212 cases of T1 CRC were enrolled in this study, among which 27 cases had LNM (20 males and 7 females, median age 66 years), and 185 cases had non-LNM (112 males and 73 females, median age 68 years). Laboratory data collection

Routine blood analysis was performed using a Beckman Coulter automatic CBC analyzer (Beckman Coulter Inc., USA) and electrochemiluminescence immunoassay. Inflammatory markers and oncological indicators were measured, including neutrophil, lymphocyte, platelet, albumin, CRP, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen (CA) 199 and CA724 counts. NLR values were calculated with the neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, PLR values were calculated with the absolute platelet and lymphocyte counts. LMR values were calculated with the lymphocyte and monocyte counts, and CAR values were calculated with the CRP and albumin counts.

CT image acquisition

A 256-row spiral CT scanner (Revolution GE 256, GE Healthcare, USA) was used for abdominal CECT examination. Patients were placed in the supine position with the upper limbs raised over the top of the head. The scanning range covered the entire abdomen. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage of 120 kVp, automatic tube current modulation, rotation time of 0.8 s/r, field of view 40–50 cm, matrix of 512 × 512, pitch of 0.992, noise index of 9.0. After the unenhanced images were acquired, all patients were injected with 1.5 mL/kg intravenous nonionic iodinated contrast agents (Omnipaque 350 mg I/mL, GE Healthcare) at a rate of 3 mL/s (total amount of 80 mL) via a high-pressure syringe (Stellant D-CE, Bayer Drive, Indianola, USA). The arterial phase (AP) scan images were obtained when the trigger threshold for the aorta reached 150 HU. The venous phase (VP) scan images were obtained with a delay of 25–35 s later, and the total timing from injection was about 60–70 s. All acquired imaging data were reconstructed using a soft tissue window algorithm with a slice thickness of 1.25 mm, and three-dimensional reconstructions were also performed.

Image analysis

Two radiologists with 9 years of experience in diagnosing intestinal tumor jointly analyzed the abdominal CT images without knowing the all clinicopathological results, except the location of the tumor provided from endoscopic results. The reviewers observed all LNs over 1 mm in size within 5 cm of the CRC boundary, selected the most morphologically suspicious or the largest LN, and evaluated its CT features, such as the long- and short-axis diameters, L/S ratio (ratio between two average orthogonal long/short-axis diameters), margin, necrosis and heterogeneity enhancement. A circular region of interest (ROI) was delineated on the maximum cross-section of the selected LN, and the ROI was replicated on the same layer in other two CT phases. The CT mean values of the LN in the non-contrast phase (N), AP, and VP were measured, and were recorded as N value mean, AP value mean, and VP value mean, respectively. With the N value, AP value and VP value mean as reference, the attenuation differences of the LN after enhancement (mean AP—N, mean VP—AP) were calculated. The characteristics of the enhancement degree and enhancement pattern of the LN were included. Details of the most morphologically suspicious LN definition and assessment criteria are provided in Table S1. When there was a disagreement in the judgment by the two reviewers, the final result was confirmed and recorded by a senior radiologist with 20 years of experience in diagnosing intestinal tumor. To determine interobserver reproducibility, a reviewer with 10 years of experience repeated the above procedure. The Cohen’s kappa or intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to assess interobserver variability.

Pathological evaluation

Pathological characteristics of T1 CRC were obtained from electronic medical records. All intestinal specimens were processed according to standard pathological procedures. Each patient’s report was reviewed and confirmed by two experienced pathologists. T1a was defined as tumor invasion into the mucosa, but not into the submucosa, and the invasion depth was less than 1000 μm. T1b was defined as tumor invasion into the submucosa with a depth of ≥ 1000 μm. Based on the Ki-67 expression index, Ki-67 ≥ 80% was defined as high expression, and Ki-67 < 80% was defined as low expression.

Statistical analysis

In the comparison between LNM and non-LNM groups, continuous variables were represented by the median with interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed by counts and percentage. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To avoid the potential overfitting associated with two-step modeling, we directly employed penalized logistic regression with an L1 penalty (the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, LASSO) to select and model the variables identified in the univariate analysis. The penalty parameter λ was determined using tenfold cross-validation. The final model retained variables with non-zero coefficients, and their shrunken coefficient estimates were reported. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was employed to evaluate the discriminative ability of models. The net reclassification improvement (NRI) and the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were further calculated to quantify the incremental predictive value. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted by drawing the relationship curve between net benefit and threshold probability to assess the practicality of the model in clinical practice. The interobserver agreement of the CECT features were estimated with the kappa or ICC (0.000–0.200: poor; 0.201–0.400: fair; 0.401–0.600: moderate; 0.601–0.800: good; 0.801–1.000: excellent). All data analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant differences.

Inflammatory markers and CT imaging data

This study further analyzed the differences in inflammatory markers, imaging characteristics and tumor markers between the two groups (Table 2). The results showed that LMR was significantly lower, while the PLR and CAR were both significantly higher than those in the non-LNM group (all p < 0.001). In terms of imaging finding, both the long- and short-axis diameter of LNs in the LNM group were significantly larger than those in the non-LNM group (all p < 0.001), and were frequently accompanied by necrosis, irregular margin and heterogeneous enhancement (all p < 0.001). The L/S ratio was lower in the LNM group (p = 0.004). Representative CT images are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. Among tumor markers, CA724 exhibited a higher elevation rate in the LNM group (p = 0.007). There were no significant differences in other parameters between the two groups. All CECT features showed good to excellent interobserver agreement in the evaluation of T1 CRC. There were 6/7 features with excellent interobserver agreement (kappa/ICC = 0.839–0.963). The margin feature showed good interobserver agreement (kappa = 0.770) (Table S2, Fig. S1).

Variables selection and predictive model construction

LASSO regression was applied to select variables in order to identify important predictors related to the outcome. The optimal regularization parameter was determined through ten-fold cross-validation, with the corresponding Lambda.1se value of 0.0011. At this lambda value, the non-zero coefficient variables ultimately retained include CAR, necrosis, differentiation, T stage, LVI, smoke, L/S ratio and LMR. Among them, the coefficient of CAR had the largest absolute value (|β|= 0.411), indicating that it has the highest predictive importance; followed by differentiation (|β|= 0.382) and necrosis (|β|= 0.176). Additionally, the coefficients of LMR, L/S ratio, etc. were negative, suggesting a negative correlation with the outcome, while CAR, necrosis, etc. showed a positive correlation (Fig. 4). Discussion

In this study, the LNM rate of T1 CRC was 12.74%, consistent with previous reports indicating that the LNM rate of T1 CRC does not exceed 15% [7, 19, 20]. The treatment modality of T1 CRC is closely related to patient prognosis, and the contemporary MITAT evidence on technology-specific oncology benefits emphasizes that adjusting surgical radical resection according to biological risk is a reasonable direction for early CRC treatment [21]. Our research developed prediction models for LNM in T1 CRC patients. The results indicated that the prediction model established by combining the CECT features of LN and composite inflammatory markers had excellent performance (AUC = 0.830).

Most of the literature focuses on patients with multi-stage (T1-T4) CRC, while there are few in-depth studies on specific T1 CRC. Li et al. reported that LNM of T1 CRC was significantly associated with the tumor location, as well as presence of necrosis, irregular outer border, and heterogeneity enhancement [22]. Our results were generally consistent with the above study. Characteristics such as size, necrosis, and irregular margins of LN were high risk factors for LNM [23, 24]. Necrosis mainly resulted from the uncontrolled rapid proliferation of tumor cells in the LNs, leading to central ischemic necrosis due to relatively insufficient blood supply. At the same time, the attack of host immune cells could also aggravate cell death [25]. The irregular margin directly reflected the aggressive growth of the tumor, and the cancer cells broke through the LN capsule to infiltrate the surrounding tissues, and triggered the irregular fibrotic encapsulation reaction [25, 26]. These two features on CT imaging were low density necrotic area without central enhancement and vague boundary of LN contour, respectively, suggesting that the metastatic lesion had the biological characteristics of active proliferation and strong invasion, which were important indicators for clinical evaluation of tumor progression and prognosis [26–28]. Hong et al. found that the short-axis diameter, necrosis, and heterogeneity enhancement of LN are significantly correlated with the status of LN. The AUC of the short-axis diameter of LN was 0.703, which can be used to predict the LNM in CRC patients [29]. Our research results proved that these characteristics are still applicable to T1 CRC. It is worth mentioning that the interobserver consistency of CT features was good to excellent (kappa = 0.770–0.871, ICC = 0.935, 0.963), indicating that the features of LNs extracted from CECT images had good stability in the evaluation of T1 CRC.

Multiple studies had confirmed that inflammatory responses were significantly associated with the invasive behavior and poor clinical prognosis of intestinal cancer. Catal et al. conducted a study on colon cancer patients and found that platelet counts and PLR values were significantly correlated with LNM [30]. The findings of Wen et al. indicated that NLR and LMR were independent risk factors for predicting LNM in CRC patients, with the AUC value of the combined diagnostic model reaching 0.839 [31]. Our study revealed that the PLR and CAR were higher in the LNM group of T1 CRC, while LMR was lower compared to the non-LNM group. Those easily accessible indexes might serve as indicative biomarkers for LNM in T1 CRC, and suggested that the inflammatory response of the body was enhanced. Liao et al. analyzed 6329 CRC patients and confirmed that CAR can serve as a prognostic indicator for CRC patients [32]. Furthermore, some studies had analyzed that lower LMR was associated with adverse mortality outcome in cancer [33]; decreased LMR might serve as a biomarker for early detection and risk stratification of CRC [34]. Inflammatory markers essentially quantified the state between the systemic inflammatory response and anti-tumor immunity in the body. Their abnormal levels reflected specific immune processes in the tumor microenvironment. Lymphocytes could inhibit tumor progression. Lower levels of lymphocytes correlated with poor prognosis in malignant tumors [35]. Meanwhile, the combination of high CRP and low albumin could suppress the function of lymphocytes, leading to the failure of immune surveillance [36]. The increases in monocyte and platelet counts were conducive to the formation of tumor blood vessels/lymphatic vessels. These processes ultimately promoted the invasion of tumor and the occurrence of LNM [13].

The application of the “radiology-immunology” combined model can enhance the value of cross-dimensional integration, and their complementary nature may reduce false-positive phenomena. Therefore, this study employed LASSO analysis to establish models incorporating various factors and combinations. The results showed that Model 6, which combined imaging factors with inflammatory marker factors, had the highest AUC value (AUC = 0.830) compared to the single-category models (Model 1–4) and the multi-categories model without imaging factors (Model 5). Moreover, both the NRI and IDI were > 0, indicating that this model was the optimal model in terms of prediction accuracy, clinical decision value and classification/discrimination ability. Combining multiple indicators could present the results more precise, and integrated model could better predict LNM in T1 CRC. Real-world variability in surgical strategies for T1 CRC further highlights the need for objective, reproducible preoperative tools to standardise escalation to completion surgery when LNM risk is substantial [37].

Although pathological examination was the gold standard for staging of CRC, it still had some disadvantages, such as sampling error, time-consuming and labor-intensive process, the results depended on the subjective experience of operators to a certain extent, and patients had to bear the harm of invasive examination. Our pathological findings were consistent with the risk factors for LNM reported in the guidelines [38, 39]. The AUC value of Model 2 based on pathological factors was 0.756, which was lower than that of preoperative comprehensive Model 6, indicating the increasingly important role of multi-technology fusion. It was notable that we observed that T1 CRC with Ki-67 ≥ 80% was associated with LNM. Luo et al. pointed out that CRC patients with Ki-67 high expression had poor prognosis and a high risk of metastasis [40]. However, there was no unified conclusion on the optimal threshold for Ki-67 high expression. Our results might provide certain reference value for future research.

There were still several limitations in this current study. Firstly, there was some selection bias in this study, all selected were T1 CRC patients who underwent radical surgery and LN dissection, which may increase the high-risk cases. In the future, the sample type and external validation will be expanded to increase the universality of the model. Secondly, the selected LN in CECT might not perfectly match with the location of postoperative pathological specimen, especially in areas with multiple LNs drainage. However, the primary objective of our study was not to achieve 100% precise localization of which LN is definitely likely to be metastatic on the images, but to explore whether the imaging features of the LN that radiologists consider "most suspicious" in the current routine preoperative CT assessment have the potential to predict LNM. Thirdly, minor measurement bias may occur in CT value for some small LNs. Although we adopted a semi-quantitative standard and implemented a strict consensus process to minimize the differences among observers. Future study should integrate new technologies such as radiomics to establish multimodal predictive model.

Conclusions

CECT features of LN and the composite inflammatory markers were significantly correlated with the occurrence of LNM in T1 CRC patients. The model established based on five factors including CAR, necrosis, LMR, etc. had the best clinical value in predicting LNM. The application of this model may be expected to realize early identification of LNM, so as to guide clinicians to formulate more optimized treatment plans before surgery and improve the prognosis of T1 CRC patients.
