Trends in ARDS-related mortality among US adult patients with diabetes, 1999–2023
Authors: Songsong Luo, Leyi Ni, Xiaoyuan Shen, Lujiao Mo, Hongliang Dong, Jiawei Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and diabetes mellitus (DM) constitute a high-risk comorbidity, with diabetes-related immune dysfunction and metabolic dysregulation exacerbating ARDS severity and worsening outcomes. However, long-term national mortality trends and subgroup disparities for this comorbidity remain inadequately characterized. Using US national mortality data from the CDC WONDER database (1999–2023), we analyzed death records listing both ARDS (ICD-10 J80) and all types of DM (ICD-10 E10-E14) as underlying or contributing causes of death. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) per 1,00,000 population were standardized to the 2000 US census. Temporal trends were evaluated using Joinpoint regression to compute average annual percent change (AAPC) and identify inflection points, with stratification by sex, race/ethnicity, census region, urbanization level, and age group. From 1999 to 2023, overall AAMR for ARDS–DM comorbidity declined from 0.66 to 0.40 per 1,00,000 (AAPC: −0.12%, 95% CI: −2.75 to 2.58). A triphasic pattern emerged: pre-pandemic relative stability or gradual decline, a sharp mortality surge during the COVID-19 pandemic (2017–2020/21), followed by steep post-pandemic decline (2020/21–2023). Persistent disparities were evident, with higher 2023 mortality among males, non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic populations, and residents of the South and West census regions. While long-term AAMR for ARDS–DM comorbidity showed an overall declining trend, the COVID-19 pandemic induced significant transient disruption. Preexisting socioeconomic and demographic disparities in mortality persisted throughout the 25-year period, most evident in rural areas, the South, and the Midwest, highlighting the continued need for targeted public health strategies to reduce severe respiratory complications in high-risk diabetic populations.

1. Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and diabetes mellitus (DM) represent critical clinical and public health challenges worldwide. ARDS, a life-threatening form of respiratory failure, is characterized by bilateral pulmonary infiltrates and severe hypoxemia, with mortality rates historically exceeding 40% despite advances in critical care.[1] Recent multicenter observational studies have elucidated distinct pathophysiological phenotypes of ARDS, particularly in the context of COVID-19, revealing heterogeneous inflammatory and respiratory mechanics patterns that influence therapeutic responses.[2] Concurrently, DM, a chronic metabolic disorder that encompasses all subtypes, including type 1, type 2, and other specified forms, and has reached epidemic proportions, has been established as a major risk factor for severe infections and complications. Importantly, hyperglycemia, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction are pathophysiological features shared across diabetes subtypes, suggesting that the adverse interplay with ARDS is not restricted to type 2 diabetes alone. Meta-analyses consistently demonstrate that diabetes significantly increases both the incidence and mortality of ARDS, with diabetic patients exhibiting heightened susceptibility to developing ARDS from various insults such as pneumonia and sepsis.[3,4]

The pathophysiological interplay between DM and ARDS is profound and multidirectional. Chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance create a pro-inflammatory state characterized by endothelial dysfunction, impaired alveolar-capillary barrier integrity, and dysregulated immune responses.[5] Diabetes-associated immune dysfunction manifests as impaired interferon-gamma-dependent antibacterial defense in the lungs, compromising the ability to clear respiratory pathogens and predisposing individuals to severe pulmonary infections.[6] Furthermore, hyperglycemia during acute illness, whether preexisting diabetes or stress-induced, has been linked to increased insulin resistance, adipose tissue infectivity in viral infections, and worse clinical outcomes in ARDS patients.[7] This bidirectional relationship underscores the critical need for integrated management strategies addressing both metabolic control and respiratory support in this vulnerable population.

The COVID-19 pandemic dramatically highlighted this dangerous synergy. SARS-CoV-2 infection emerged as a major cause of severe ARDS, with diabetes rapidly identified as one of the strongest predictors of progression to critical illness and death.[8] The bidirectional relationship between COVID-19 and diabetes, where infection worsens glycemic control and diabetes exacerbates infection severity, created a vicious cycle of metabolic and respiratory decompensation.[9] National epidemiological analyses have revealed substantial geographic and racial/ethnic disparities in diabetes-related mortality, with disproportionate burdens affecting minority populations and specific regions of the United States.[10] These patterns suggest that preexisting health inequities may have been amplified during the pandemic, potentially disrupting long-term mortality trends for conditions like ARDS–DM comorbidity.

Despite advances in the management of both ARDS and DM, there remains a critical gap in population-level studies examining long-term mortality trends specifically related to their comorbidity, particularly across demographic and geographic subgroups. Recent analyses of ARDS mortality have documented persistent racial disparities, with higher death rates among Black populations compared to White populations.[11] Similarly, comprehensive assessments of diabetes-atrial fibrillation comorbidity have revealed complex temporal patterns and demographic variations in mortality.[12] However, systematic evaluation of ARDS–DM comorbidity mortality spanning pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic periods remains limited.

This study addresses this gap by conducting a comprehensive 25-year analysis (1999–2023) of mortality attributable to ARDS–DM comorbidity in the United States. We examine age-adjusted mortality rates and perform stratified analyses by sex, race/ethnicity, census region, urbanization level, and age group. Our objectives are to characterize long-term temporal trends in ARDS–DM comorbidity mortality, identify persistent or emerging demographic and geographic disparities, assess the specific impact of the COVID-19 pandemic era on mortality patterns, and provide evidence to inform public health strategies aimed at mitigating the burden of severe respiratory complications in the expanding diabetic population.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study setting and population

This descriptive study analyzed US national mortality data from the CDC WONDER database for 1999–2023 to examine trends in deaths associated with comorbid ARDS and DM. Data were extracted from the Multiple Cause of Death Public Use file, containing standardized death certificate records from all US registration areas.[13] Causes of death were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10): ARDS by code J80 and DM by codes E10–E14 (encompassing type 1, type 2, and other forms of diabetes).[11,12,14] Death records were included if either condition was listed as the underlying or contributing cause of death. This multi-cause approach comprehensively captures the mortality burden of ARDS–DM comorbidity, consistent with established methodologies in comorbidity mortality research.

The study population was restricted to individuals aged ≥35 years to focus on the demographic in which ARDS–DM comorbidity is more commonly observed and clinically significant, given the relatively lower prevalence and attributable mortality in younger adults.

Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) per 1,00,000 population were calculated and standardized to the 2000 US Census population. Long-term trends were evaluated using Joinpoint regression analysis, estimating average annual percent change (AAPC) and identifying significant inflection points. Stratified analyses were performed by sex, race/ethnicity, census region, urbanization level, and age group. Segmented annual percent change (APC) estimates characterized short-term fluctuations, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic period.

This study used only publicly available, de-identified aggregate data and was therefore exempt from Institutional Review Board approval. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.[15]

2.2. Data abstraction

Mortality data were extracted from the Multiple Cause of Death public-use files within the CDC WONDER database, stratified by key demographic and geographic variables including sex, race and ethnicity, census region, and urbanization level.

Race and ethnicity were categorized as Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and non-Hispanic Other. Census regions were classified as Northeast, Midwest, South, and West. Urbanization status was defined according to the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) classification scheme, distinguishing between metropolitan and nonmetropolitan areas.[16]

All data were derived exclusively from death certificate records within the database’s publicly available files.

It should be noted that in the trend analysis stratified by urbanization level, the AAMR for 2023 was recalculated based on the 2020 urban-rural classification standards, while the 1999 data were based on the classification standards in effect at that time. Therefore, the AAPC for urbanization stratification reflects the combined effect of changes in classification standards and actual mortality changes, and should be interpreted with particular caution and not directly compared with trends in other groups based on fixed standards.

2.3. Statistical analysis

AAMR per 1,00,000 population, standardized to the 2000 US census, was calculated to quantify the mortality burden associated with ARDS–DM from 1999 to 2023. Temporal trends in AAMR were analyzed using Joinpoint Regression (version 5.4.0.0; National Cancer Institute),[17] which applies segmented log-linear modeling to estimate APC and corresponding 95% confidence intervals for each trend segment. The model assumes log-linearity within segments, independence of residuals, and homoscedasticity. To characterize trend changes without overfitting, a maximum of 3 joinpoints were permitted, aligning with recommendations for a 25-year observation period. The optimal model was selected using permutation tests controlling the overall type I error rate at α = 0.05. Statistically significant trend transitions were identified when APC differed significantly from zero (2-tailed t test, α = 0.05).

The long-term trend over the entire study period was summarized as AAPC. Stratified analyses were conducted across demographic and geographic subgroups. Parallelism of trends between subgroups was assessed by comparing respective AAPCs. In addition to AAMR, crude mortality rates with 95% confidence intervals were computed to describe absolute differences within specific subgroups. All analyses were performed using statistical tools and procedures recommended by the National Cancer Institute’s Joinpoint program.

3. Results

3.1. Overall trends in ARDS and DM-related mortality

Between 1999 and 2023, deaths from ARDS with DM among US adults increased from 943 to 1140. During the same period, the overall AAMR declined from 0.66 (95% CI: 0.62–0.70) to 0.40 (95% CI: 0.38–0.43) per 100,000 population. The overall AAPC was –0.12% (95% CI: –2.75 to 2.58), indicating a non-significant long-term downward trend. This overall trend, however, masks substantial heterogeneity across demographic and geographic subgroups. Age- and sex-stratified analyses revealed consistent triphasic patterns (pre-pandemic decline, pandemic surge, and post-pandemic decline), whereas racial/ethnic and regional disparities persisted throughout the study period. Urbanization-stratified results are presented for descriptive purposes only, as changes in NCHS urban-rural classification standards between 1999 and 2023 preclude valid long-term AAPC estimation and cross-temporal comparisons for these strata.

3.2. ARDS and DM-related mortality trends stratified by age groups

Between 1999 and 2023, AAMR for ARDS with DM consistently increased with advancing age (Table 1). Among older age groups, the oldest cohort (85 years and older) showed AAMR decreasing from 1.93 to 1.16 per 1,00,000 population (AAPC: −1.18%; 95% CI: −6.35 to 4.27); the 75 to 84 age group experienced a decline from 2.25 to 1.46 (AAPC: −0.40%; 95% CI: −3.75 to 3.07); and the 65 to 74 age group decreased from 1.40 to 1.04 (AAPC: −1.88%; 95% CI: −8.09 to 4.75). For middle-aged groups, the 55 to 64 age group showed AAMR decreasing from 0.70 to 0.58 (AAPC: −0.99%; 95% CI: −6.89 to 5.29), while younger age groups (35–44 and 45–54 years) demonstrated modest absolute AAMR values throughout the period, with nonsignificant long-term AAPCs of −0.99% and −0.28%, respectively. None of these long-term AAPC estimates reached statistical significance.

3.3. ARDS and DM-related mortality trends stratified by gender

Between 1999 and 2023, sex-based disparities in ARDS–DM mortality were observed (Table 1). The AAMR for males remained consistently higher than for females throughout the study period. Male AAMR decreased from 0.78 (95% CI: 0.71–0.86) to 0.51 (95% CI: 0.46–0.55) per 1,00,000, while female AAMR declined from 0.61 (95% CI: 0.56–0.67) to 0.35 (95% CI: 0.32–0.38). The long-term AAPC for males was 0.25% (95% CI: −2.81 to 3.41), and for females, it was −0.59% (95% CI: −3.61 to 2.51), with neither reaching statistical significance as confidence intervals for both estimates included zero. Overall AAMR for both sexes combined decreased from 0.66 (95% CI: 0.62–0.70) to 0.40 (95% CI: 0.38–0.43), with a corresponding overall AAPC of −0.12% (95% CI: −2.75 to 2.58), which was also nonsignificant.

Joinpoint analyses revealed consistent triphasic temporal patterns for both sexes (Fig. 2). During the pre-pandemic period (1999–2017), both males (APC: −4.40%; 95% CI: −5.17 to −3.62) and females (APC: −4.90%; 95% CI: −5.62 to −4.18) experienced significant declining trends. The pandemic period (2017–2020) was characterized by dramatic mortality surges, with males showing a more pronounced increase (APC: 153.21%; 95% CI: 96.40–226.45) compared to females (APC: 126.39%; 95% CI: 76.14–190.98). Following the pandemic peak (2020–2023), both sexes demonstrated steep, statistically significant declines: males (APC: −47.22%; 95% CI: −50.65 to –43.55) and females (APC: –43.07%; 95% CI: –47.03 to –38.80).

3.4. ARDS and DM-related mortality trends stratified by race and ethnicity

From 1999 to 2023, significant racial and ethnic disparities in ARDS–DM mortality were observed (Table 1). In 1999, Hispanic individuals had the highest AAMR at 1.50 per 1,00,000 (95% CI: 1.22–1.78), followed by non-Hispanic (NH) Other individuals at 1.23 (95% CI: 0.90–1.65), NH Black individuals at 1.19 (95% CI: 0.99–1.38), and NH White individuals at 0.53 (95% CI: 0.49–0.57). By 2023, AAMR declined in all groups: NH White individuals maintained the lowest AAMR at 0.34 (95% CI: 0.31–0.37), followed by NH Black at 0.61, Hispanic at 0.70, and NH Other populations. Despite these declining age-adjusted rates, absolute death counts rose among some groups, most notably among Hispanic (+89.8%) and NH Other (+73.1%) populations, reflecting population growth, demographic aging, and expansion of high-risk groups rather than increasing per-capita risk. Accordingly, temporal risk comparisons should rely on AAMR rather than raw counts. Long-term AAPCs from 1999 to 2023 were negative for all groups but did not reach statistical significance.

Joinpoint analyses revealed a distinct triphasic pattern across all racial and ethnic groups (Fig. 3). During the pre-pandemic period (1999 to approximately 2017), all groups experienced significant declines in AAMR, with Hispanic individuals showing the steepest decline (APC: −7.02%; 95% CI: −8.38 to −5.65), followed by NH Black (APC: −5.95%; 95% CI: −7.27 to −4.61), NH Other (APC: −4.91%; 95% CI: −6.72 to −3.07), and NH White (APC: −3.42%; 95% CI: −5.21 to −1.59). The pandemic period (approximately 2017–2020/2021) was marked by sharp, significant increases, with the magnitude of surge most extreme among Hispanic (APC: 212.79%; 95% CI: 106.44–373.94) and NH Black populations (APC: 174.69%; 95% CI: 75.22–330.63), compared to NH Other (APC: 143.62%; 95% CI: 38.71–327.88) and NH White (APC: 70.49%; 95% CI: 37.62–111.21). During the post-pandemic period (2020/2021–2023), all groups demonstrated steep, significant declines: NH White (APC: −64.09%; 95% CI: −77.47 to −42.76), NH Black (APC: −57.01%; 95% CI: −62.06 to −51.29), Hispanic (APC: −54.43%; 95% CI: −58.75 to −49.66), and NH Other (APC: −50.89%; 95% CI: −57.12 to −43.76).

3.5. ARDS and DM-related mortality trends stratified by census region

From 1999 to 2023, geographic disparities in ARDS–DM mortality were evident across US census regions (Table 1). In 1999, AAMR was similar in the South and West (both 0.60 per 1,00,000), with lower rates in the Midwest (0.52) and Northeast (0.42). By 2023, the West exhibited the highest AAMR at 0.58 (95% CI: 0.51–0.64), followed by the South at 0.48 (95% CI: 0.44–0.52), Midwest at 0.33, and the Northeast, which maintained the lowest AAMR at 0.24 (95% CI: 0.19–0.28). For context, absolute death counts showed diverging regional patterns with the West (+61.5%) and South (+30.8%) recording increases and the Northeast and Midwest seeing reductions (−25.6% and −20.5%, respectively), largely reflecting differential regional population growth rather than changing per-capita risk; AAMR remains the appropriate metric for temporal risk comparisons. Long-term AAPC was negative for all regions, ranging from −0.47% in the West to −2.36% in the Midwest, though none reached statistical significance.

Joinpoint analyses demonstrated similar triphasic patterns across all 4 census regions (Fig. 4). During the pre-pandemic period (1999–2017), all regions experienced significant declining trends: Northeast (APC: −4.94%; 95% CI: −6.10 to −3.77), Midwest (APC: −3.85%; 95% CI: −6.36 to −1.27), South (APC: −3.42%; 95% CI: −5.68 to −1.12), and West (APC: −3.13%; 95% CI: −5.50 to −0.69). The pandemic period (2017 to 2020/2021) was characterized by dramatic mortality surges, with the Northeast showing the most extreme increase (APC: 161.49%; 95% CI: 66.73–310.09), followed by the West (APC: 101.16%; 95% CI: 54.68–161.60), South (APC: 91.36%; 95% CI: 48.20–147.10), and Midwest (APC: 83.68%; 95% CI: 36.63–146.93). Following the pandemic peak (2020/2021 to 2023), all regions demonstrated steep, statistically significant declines: South (APC: −69.63%; 95% CI: −82.08 to −48.51), West (APC: −68.92%; 95% CI: −80.91 to −49.38), Midwest (APC: −68.30%; 95% CI: −83.98 to −37.25), and Northeast (APC: −55.70%; 95% CI: −61.13 to −49.51).

3.6. ARDS and DM-related mortality trends stratified by urbanization level

Given that the 2023 data applied updated (2020) urban-rural classification standards, the calculation basis for urbanization-stratified AAMR differs between 1999 and 2023, making cross-year comparisons within urbanization strata invalid. Due to this incomparability, long-term AAPC estimates for urbanization strata are not reported, as they cannot be distinguished from reclassification artifacts. For descriptive context, the apparent AAMR (based on mixed classification standards) was 0.55 (95% CI: 0.52–0.59) and 0.51 (95% CI: 0.43–0.59) in metropolitan and nonmetropolitan areas in 1999, and 3.12 (95% CI: 3.05–3.19) and 3.08 (95% CI: 2.91–3.26) in 2023, respectively (Table 1); however, this large apparent difference is very likely attributable primarily to the reclassification of standards rather than actual mortality rate growth, and should not be used for temporal trend inference.

Joinpoint analyses for urbanization level must be interpreted with extreme caution due to classification standard changes (Fig. 5). During the pre-pandemic period (1999–2018), both metropolitan (APC: −4.09%; 95% CI: −4.86 to −3.31) and nonmetropolitan areas (APC: −1.85%; 95% CI: −2.36 to −1.32) showed declining trends. The pandemic period (2018–2020) was associated with dramatic apparent increases: metropolitan areas (APC: 254.10%; 95% CI: 184.82–340.23) and nonmetropolitan areas (APC: 187.77%; 95% CI: 152.73–227.67). However, as noted above, the observed patterns in urbanization-stratified data are confounded by changes in classification schemes and should not be interpreted as reflecting true urban-rural mortality trends.

4. Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive assessment of 25-year trends in mortality associated with comorbid ARDS and DM in the United States, with particular attention to variations across demographic and geographic subgroups. While total deaths related to ARDS–DM comorbidity increased from 943 in 1999 to 1140 in 2023, overall AAMR showed a nonsignificant long-term declining trend (AAPC: −0.12%), characterized by relative stability at the national level with a transient but significant surge during the COVID-19 pandemic (2018–2021), followed by rapid decline. Urbanization-stratified analyses were limited by changes in NCHS urban-rural classification standards between study years, which preclude valid long-term AAPC comparisons for metropolitan and nonmetropolitan strata; those apparent trends likely reflect reclassification effects rather than true mortality change. Furthermore, a transient but sharp rise in mortality curves was evident across nearly all subgroups from 2018 to 2021, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic. This pattern mirrors findings from New York City, where substantial geographic variation in COVID-19 hospitalizations and deaths was observed across different boroughs, reflecting differential impacts on vulnerable populations.[18] After 2021, rapid mortality decline was generally observed across subgroups.

While our death certificate data cannot directly verify specific pathophysiological mechanisms, existing laboratory and clinical literature suggests that the long-term patterns observed may reflect counterbalancing effects of therapeutic advances and evolving population health profiles. Advances in critical care medicine have been significant, particularly in understanding ARDS pathophysiology. Recent multicenter prospective studies have elucidated distinct phenotypes of acute respiratory distress syndrome, with implications for targeted therapeutic approaches.[2] Adoption of a new global definition of ARDS in 2024 has further refined diagnostic criteria and may improve consistency in clinical management.[19] Concurrently, diabetes care has evolved substantially, with updated evidence-based standards emphasizing individualized glycemic targets, cardiovascular risk reduction, and comprehensive complication screening.[20] The transient yet dramatic surge in AAMR between 2018 and 2021 warrants particular attention, as multiple risk factors for COVID-19-related mortality, including older age, male sex, obesity, and preexisting comorbidities such as diabetes, were strongly associated with fatal outcomes during this period.[21]

While our aggregate mortality data cannot directly attribute observed fluctuations to specific mechanisms, convergent evidence from the literature points to both direct viral pathogenicity and systemic healthcare disruption. A systematic review documented significant reductions in healthcare utilization across multiple settings, including emergency departments, outpatient clinics, and elective procedures, with patients deferring or avoiding necessary care due to fear of infection or healthcare system restrictions.[22] The intersection of COVID-19 with diabetes is particularly concerning, as people with diabetes demonstrate worse clinical outcomes when infected with SARS-CoV-2. Evidence suggests that hyperglycemia and diabetes-related metabolic dysfunction may impair immune responses, increase viral entry into cells, and promote a pro-inflammatory state.[23] Population-based cohort studies from England demonstrated that both type 1 and type 2 diabetes were associated with significantly increased COVID-19-related mortality, with hazard ratios remaining elevated even after adjustment for multiple confounders.[24] Furthermore, hospitalizations for chronic conditions and acute medical problems declined substantially during pandemic periods, indicating widespread care disruption beyond COVID-19 itself.[25] Potential long-term post-COVID effects such as postinfectious pulmonary dysfunction and cardiometabolic sequelae require cautious interpretation, as our aggregate mortality data lack individual-level infection histories necessary to establish such associations. Moving forward, it is crucial to continuously monitor health trends of this population and develop strategies to strengthen both chronic disease management and the responsiveness of acute healthcare systems to better prepare for potential future public health crises. Global modeling estimates indicate that over 1.7 billion individuals worldwide face an increased risk of severe COVID-19 due to underlying health conditions, highlighting the immense vulnerability of patients with comorbidities during public health emergencies.[26]

Analysis stratified by age group indicated that mortality from ARDS comorbid with DM was markedly higher among older patients. The age groups 75 to 84 years and 85 years and above exhibited the highest AAMRs. This pattern is consistent with data from intensive care units in Lombardy, Italy, where advanced age emerged as one of the strongest independent risk factors for mortality among critically ill patients, with odds ratios increasing progressively with each decade of life.[27] Older adults often have reduced physiological reserve and a higher burden of comorbidities, which may intensify the severity of both ARDS and DM. Although long-term AAPC for older age groups was negative, it did not reach statistical significance, indicating persistent disease burden. The mortality surge during the pandemic was most extreme among the oldest populations. Age-specific mortality analyses reveal exponential increases in infection fatality rates with advancing age, with individuals over 80 years facing more than a 100-fold higher risk compared to those under 40 years.[28] This reflects not only greater clinical frailty and immunosenescence but also age-related differences in inflammatory responses and organ reserve. Furthermore, physical limitations and social isolation may have hindered timely access to medical care for this group, as documented disparities in healthcare engagement affected vulnerable elderly populations disproportionately during lockdown periods.[29]

Significant gender disparities were observed. AAMR was higher for males than females in 2023, and long-term AAPC was positive (albeit nonsignificant) for males compared to negative for females. This disparity aligns with accumulating evidence that biological sex fundamentally impacts immune responses and disease outcomes. Males demonstrate more severe inflammatory responses to viral infections, potentially due to sex differences in innate and adaptive immunity, as well as differential expression of viral entry receptors such as ACE2 (angiotensin-converting enzyme 2).[30] Additionally, sex hormones, particularly androgens and estrogens, play critical roles in modulating immune function, with estrogen generally promoting more robust antiviral responses while testosterone may suppress certain protective immune mechanisms. Behavioral factors such as healthcare-seeking patterns, comorbidity prevalence, and occupational exposures also contribute to observed differences.[31] Health education and management strategies may need to consider gender-specific approaches, recognizing that sex and gender act as fundamental modifiers of health, disease susceptibility, and therapeutic responses across the lifespan.

Pronounced racial and ethnic disparities were evident. The Hispanic population had the highest AAMR in 1999 and despite a subsequent declining age-adjusted rate, experienced the largest increase in absolute death counts by 2023 an increase attributable primarily to population growth and demographic aging rather than rising per-capita risk. Although AAMR showed declining trends across all racial/ethnic groups, long-term AAPCs were not statistically significant. These findings are consistent with extensive documentation of COVID-19’s disproportionate impact on racial and ethnic minorities in the United States. Black, Hispanic, and Native American populations experienced substantially higher rates of infection, hospitalization, and death compared to White populations.[32] A systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that racial and ethnic minority groups faced elevated risks across nearly all COVID-19 outcomes, with effects persisting after adjustment for age, comorbidities, and socioeconomic factors.[33] The reasons behind these disparities are complex and multifaceted, involving biological vulnerabilities, socioeconomic factors, and systemic inequities in healthcare access and quality. Structural determinants including residential segregation, occupational exposures, crowded housing conditions, limited healthcare access, and chronic exposure to discrimination create cumulative disadvantage that manifests as differential disease burden. Although observed within the context of a single country, these disparities reflect broader patterns of health inequality requiring multilevel interventions addressing both individual risk factors and upstream social determinants.

Geographic disparities were also apparent. The West and South census regions saw substantial increases in death counts, and the West had the highest AAMR in 2023. However, the most striking geographic disparity was observed in stratification by urbanization level. Epidemiological analyses of COVID-19 dynamics revealed important differences in transmission patterns, healthcare capacity, and mortality outcomes between urban and rural areas in the United States.[34] While initial outbreaks concentrated in dense metropolitan areas, subsequent waves affected rural communities with limited critical care infrastructure and greater baseline prevalence of chronic conditions. The variation in AAMRs across regions and settings indicates the necessity of formulating region-specific and setting-specific policies that account for local healthcare capacity, population health profiles, and community characteristics. Rural areas face unique challenges, including physician shortages, hospital closures, longer travel distances to specialized care, and higher prevalence of comorbidities, all factors that may contribute to worse outcomes for patients with complex conditions like comorbid ARDS and DM.

This study has several important limitations. First, death certificate data are subject to misclassification and coding inconsistencies over time, particularly for comorbid conditions where the assignment of underlying versus contributing causes may be ambiguous. Systematic changes in coding practices may also affect temporal trend interpretation. Second, race/ethnicity classifications are based on death certificate reporting and may be subject to misclassification, particularly for multiracial individuals and certain minority groups. Third, CDC WONDER data suppression for small-population cells limits precision in certain geographic regions and demographic strata. Fourth, the absence of individual-level clinical information such as ARDS severity, glycemic control, or specific treatment regimens limited our ability to adjust for potential confounders and assess the impact of therapeutic advances. Fifth, although we described mortality fluctuations during the COVID-19 pandemic, the study design cannot directly attribute these changes to specific pandemic-related mechanisms versus other concurrent temporal trends. Sixth, data on diabetes duration at the time of death were unavailable in the CDC WONDER database. This is a clinically meaningful limitation, as longer disease duration is associated with greater microvascular and macrovascular complication burden, which may independently worsen ARDS prognosis. Future studies utilizing individual-level clinical databases should examine whether diabetes duration modifies mortality risk in ARDS patients. Seventh, information on antidiabetic medications was not captured. The impact of specific pharmacological treatments such as insulin, metformin, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors on ARDS outcomes may vary substantially, given their differential effects on glycemic control, inflammation, and end-organ protection. As improvements in diabetes pharmacotherapy represent one of the most plausible explanations for the long-term declining AAMR, future research should evaluate how evolving treatment regimens contribute to mortality trends in this comorbid population. Eighth, the CDC WONDER database does not provide data on additional comorbidities beyond those coded on death certificates, preventing adjustment for the full burden of concurrent conditions. Diabetes frequently coexists with cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and obesity, all of which may independently increase ARDS mortality and are often more prevalent with longer diabetes duration. While our findings reflect population-level trends, individual-level risk is likely shaped substantially by the cumulative comorbidity burden, a dimension that could not be fully addressed in the current study design. Finally, as analysis was restricted to US mortality data, caution is warranted when generalizing findings to other populations with different healthcare systems, demographic compositions, and epidemiological contexts.

This study demonstrates that mortality associated with comorbid ARDS and DM presents a complex picture: long-term relative stability at the national level, concerning and significant increases in both urban and rural settings, and acute vulnerability to systemic shocks such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Persistent disparities were most evident in rural areas, the South, and the Midwest. These findings underscore public health and health systems-level priorities, including strengthening chronic disease management infrastructure, ensuring equitable distribution of critical care resources, and addressing health inequities in both routine care and emergency preparedness. The analysis provides a reproducible framework for monitoring comorbidity-related mortality and highlights the critical importance of health equity considerations in both routine care and emergency preparedness. Future research should focus on elucidating drivers of regional and urban-rural disparities in ARDS–DM mortality using harmonized geographic classification frameworks, identifying specific care gaps exacerbated during pandemics, and evaluating the effectiveness of integrated care models tailored for patients with concurrent respiratory and metabolic diseases. Drawing on successful models of chronic disease management, such as programs that have demonstrated reduced hospital readmissions through coordinated care and patient engagement,[35] targeted interventions may help mitigate overall disease burden and reduce persistent disparities in this vulnerable population.
