Horror Fandom, morbid curiosity, and psychological resilience amid war conflict and economic crisis among university students in Lebanon: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rawad Affan, Marilyn Yammine, Mia Karam, Matthia Abboud, Shafika Assaad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological resilience is crucial during prolonged instability, allowing individuals to adapt to change demands, manage intense stress, and maintain mental health. Engagement with horror media and morbid curiosity may be associated with resilience through simulated threat exposure, evidence remains limited in populations experiencing chronic crises.ObjectiveThis study aims to examine the associations between horror-related film engagement, trait morbid curiosity, and psychological resilience among Lebanese university students living amid ongoing socio-economic and political instability.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 235 university students in Lebanon using a self-administered online survey. Measures included film genre preferences, the Pandemic Psychological Resilience Scale (PPRS), and the Morbid Curiosity Scale (MCS). Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analyses were performed.ResultsParticipants demonstrated moderate to high psychological resilience (mean = 4.26 ± 0.81) alongside notable emotional distress. Morbid curiosity levels were moderate (mean = 3.42 ± 1.25) and were significantly associated with preferences for darker film genres, particularly crime (r = 0.539), horror (r = 0.524), and psychological thriller (r = 0.507) (p < 0.001). However, no significant association was found between psychological resilience and horror media engagement or morbid curiosity. A weak negative association was observed between preference for alien-invasion films and resilience (r = − 0.177, p = 0.006).ConclusionWhile morbid curiosity is strongly linked to engagement with threat-related media, it does not appear to translate into increased psychological resilience in a chronic multi-crisis context. These findings highlight the importance of contextual factors in shaping adaptive psychological mechanisms.

Introduction

Psychological resilience refers to a dynamic capacity to maintain or regain mental health, functioning, and adaptive engagement when facing significant adversity, rather than to the complete absence of distress [1]. Exposure to war, forced displacement, economic collapse, and other mass crises is consistently associated with elevated psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress symptoms, and broader impairment [2–6].

Evidence from conflict settings and mass displacement shows substantial mental health burden [2, 3] the first year of the Russia-Ukraine war was associated with high stress, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms among Ukrainian civilians [4] and economic crises in Spain and Greece were linked to worsening mental health and suicide-related or population health indicators [5, 6]. These findings support the need to identify mechanisms that may explain why some people maintain adaptive functioning despite severe and sustained stress [1].

Comparable concerns are present in the Arab region, where armed conflict, forced displacement, socio-political instability, and economic stressors have been associated with elevated psychological strain and variable resilience outcomes [7, 8]. Work with Syrians affected by armed conflict, including refugees in Jordan and Lebanon, documented substantial trauma exposure and psychosocial distress [7], while regional monitoring has emphasized vulnerability and resilience differences across Arab countries facing overlapping risks [8].

Lebanon represents a relevant chronic crisis context. Since 2019, Lebanon has experienced one of the most severe economic collapses globally, alongside political uncertainty and regional tension [9]. The Beirut port explosion on 4 August 2020 added an acute traumatic event to this chronic background, with reports of widespread destruction and psychological consequences, including depressive and post-traumatic stress symptoms among exposed populations and university students [10–12]. Since October 2023, Lebanon has also faced escalating hostilities, mortality, displacement, attacks affecting health services, and growing mental health needs, with continued social consequences reported into 2026 [13, 14].

The Lebanese context is therefore not only an example of acute exposure to a single event, but also of cumulative and chronic uncertainty. This distinction matters theoretically because mechanisms that may help individuals adapt during a bounded acute crisis may operate differently when real-life threat is prolonged, recurrent, and difficult to control [1, 8–14]. In chronic crisis contexts, resilience may coexist with distress and may depend on lived exposure, social support, intergroup relations, and structural conditions, not only on individual preferences or voluntary exposure to fictional threat [1, 3, 7, 8].

Threat-simulation theory provides one possible, but not deterministic, explanation for why frightening fiction could be associated with adaptive functioning. Fictional narratives allow people to simulate dangerous or uncertain situations in a safe and bounded context [15]. When applied to horror media, this suggests a possible pathway in which voluntary exposure to controllable fictional threat may support cognitive rehearsal, emotional regulation, uncertainty tolerance, and calibration of fear responses; these processes could, in turn, relate to perceived coping and resilience [15–18].

Morbid curiosity is related to, but distinct from, horror fandom. Morbid curiosity refers to a trait-like tendency to seek information about threatening, dangerous, or disturbing phenomena [19], whereas horror fandom or horror-related genre preference reflects voluntary engagement with specific media content [16, 20].

Prior empirical work provides mixed reasons to expect associations among these variables. During the early COVID-19 pandemic, horror fans and morbidly curious individuals reported higher psychological resilience [20], and subsequent work linked interest in suspense-horror with resilience-related outcomes during the pandemic [21]. At the same time, recreational fear studies indicate that fear enjoyment depends on controlled arousal, perceived safety, and individual differences, suggesting that threat-related media engagement is unlikely to have uniform psychological meaning across contexts [17, 18].

Alternative explanations must also be considered. Preferences for horror, crime, psychological thrillers, or other dark genres may reflect broader traits and habits such as sensation seeking, openness or intellect, comfort with disgust, lower avoidance of disturbing content, or general media consumption rather than a resilience-specific mechanism [16, 19, 22].

Conflict and post-conflict social psychology also indicates that adaptation to threat is shaped by more than individual media preferences. Disturbing war-related memories can reduce openness to contact and peace through meta-dehumanization and outgroup dehumanization, and humanizing interventions may be less effective among individuals carrying more disturbing war memories [23]. In addition, humanizing information can improve intergroup behavioral intentions through reduced anxiety and increased empathy, particularly among those with high levels of negative direct contact [24].

Accordingly, the contextual contribution of this study is framed as a specific empirical extension. Existing evidence linking horror fandom, morbid curiosity, and resilience has been based largely on Western samples and acute crisis conditions [20, 21]. It remains unclear whether the same associations appear among university students living in a chronic multi-crisis environment in which simulated threats coexist with ongoing economic, political, and conflict-related stressors [8–14, 23, 24].

One theoretical way to account for this possible contextual boundary is Hobfoll’s Conservation of Resources theory, which offers a scarcity of psychological resources perspective. Hobfoll proposed that stress occurs when valued resources are threatened, lost, or insufficiently replenished [25]. In a sustained multi-crisis environment such as Lebanon, students’ cognitive and emotional resources may already be heavily taxed by immediate survival concerns, economic insecurity, displacement risk, and the continuous management of conflict-related uncertainty [8–14]. Consequently, there may be limited surplus capacity for the proactive coping or threat rehearsal that simulated fear is theorized to facilitate. Voluntary exposure to fictional fear may therefore lose its compensatory value when real-life threat is chronic, recurrent, and difficult to control [18, 25].

The present study aims to examine the associations between horror-related movie genre engagement, trait morbid curiosity, and the psychological resilience among Lebanese university students living amid prolonged socio-economic and political instability. Specifically, this study seeks to: (1) assess the association between engagement with horror-related movie genres and psychological resilience; (2) examine the relationship between trait morbid curiosity and psychological resilience; and (3) explore the associations between film genre preferences and levels of morbid curiosity in a sustained multi-crisis context.

Materials and methods

Participants and study design

This study used a cross-sectional study design and a self-administered online validated questionnaire. A total convenient sample consisting of 235 students from Lebanese university students participated in the study, recruited in the 2025–2026 academic year.

The target population consisted of undergraduate and graduate students at different universities in Lebanon using a convenient sampling method, and the age range fall between 18 and 56 years old. This study targeted students through their social groups and social media sites to ensure a wider and diversified study group, and only university students were allowed to participate. Students who participated in the study were informed about the objectives of the study and assured of their voluntary participation, confidentiality, and anonymity of the study. Informed consent was obtained before the start of the survey.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered fully to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, including respect for persons, beneficence, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty [26].

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee (REC) of the Human Research Protection Program (HRPP) at the Holy Spirit University of Kaslik under reference number HRPP/202610/FT/0101.

Survey structure

The survey used in the present study included four major components that aimed to extensively measure the sociodemographic factors, exposure to different movie genres, psychological resilience, and morbid curiosity among Lebanese university students.

The first section gathered basic sociodemographic information, including age, gender, and year of study, allowing for appropriate characterization of the sample.

The second section measured movie genre preferences using ten items based on a validated scale [20]. The scale used a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The items measured the respondents’ affinity towards different movie genres like horror, zombies, psychological thriller, supernatural, apocalypse, science fiction, crime, comedy, and romance. The scores for this section were computed by adding all the items together, with higher scores indicating more engagement in the corresponding movie genres. Film genre preference was assessed using single-item ratings for each genre. These items reflect participants’ self-reported preference for specific genres, rather than objective or detailed measurement.

The third section of the scale was used to assess psychological resilience, which was done by administering the Pandemic Psychological Resilience Scale (PPRS). This scale has twelve items, and they are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1, which means “strongly disagree,” to 7, which means “strongly agree.” Consistent with the instructions provided in the validation study of the scale, psychological distress-related items, which include increased depression, anxiety, irritability, or sleep problems, were reverse scored. Items that included emotional coping, optimism, one’s ability to cope, meaningfulness, and the ability to find joy, on the other hand, were forward scored. After reverse-scoring the psychological distress-related items, all twelve items were summed up to generate a total resilience score. The higher the total resilience score, the higher the level of psychological resilience [20].

Although the PPRS was originally developed in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, it was selected for the present study because its items capture broad psychological responses to large-scale collective adversity rather than pandemic-specific symptoms alone. Specifically, the scale assesses emotional distress, coping ability, optimism, perceived capacity to endure difficult circumstances, and the ability to experience enjoyment despite crisis. These domains are conceptually relevant to the Lebanese context, which is characterized by prolonged war-related insecurity, economic collapse, political instability, and chronic uncertainty. Therefore, the PPRS was considered appropriate for assessing resilience in a sustained multi-crisis environment. Minor contextual wording was used where necessary to reflect the current crisis context while preserving the original meaning of the items. In the present sample, the PPRS demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α = 0.892.

The fourth section measured the participants’ levels of morbid curiosity with the 24-item Morbid Curiosity Scale (MCS), a reliable tool specifically designed to assess individual differences in curiosity about threatening or dangerous content [19]. The MCS includes four subscales: minds of dangerous people, interpersonal violence, body violation, and paranormal danger. The scale showed good internal reliability and stability in its factor structure. Each item was measured using a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). Higher scores on the scale indicated more trait morbid curiosity. The subscales were calculated as the sum of items measuring the four factors.

Data collection and storage

The study employed an online survey that utilized a secure site to conduct the research. All the collected data was anonymous and did not include any personally identifiable information such as name, phone number, student ID, or email address. The responses to the survey were collected via a secure online site and were only accessible to the research team.

The collected data was not shared with any third party and was used solely for academic research purposes and can be obtained from the corresponding author on reasonable requests.

Data analysis

The data analysis was conducted by using SPSS (Version 24). For instance, descriptive statistical analysis such as means, frequency, and standard deviations was used to describe the participants’ background. On the other hand, inferential statistical analysis, such correlation analysis, was used to examine the relationship between horror media exposure, morbid curiosity, and psychological resilience during war conflict and economic crisis.

The internal consistency of the Psychological Resilience Scale in the present sample was good, with Cronbach’s α = 0.892. This indicates that the scale demonstrated acceptable reliability among Lebanese university students in the current context of war-related insecurity, economic collapse, and chronic instability.

Items reflecting coping ability and optimism showed relatively high mean scores, including belief in the ability to get through difficult situations (M = 5.24, SD = 1.64), ability to get through challenges (M = 5.12, SD = 1.71), and finding things to laugh about (M = 5.34, SD = 1.68). Similarly for feeling more nervous or anxious (M = 5.20, SD = 1.86).

Moderate scores were observed for items such as feeling positively about the future (M = 4.72, SD = 1.86), finding things to enjoy (M = 4.69, SD = 1.75), and feeling life is meaningful (M = 4.33, SD = 1.74), indicating generally positive but variable perceptions of well-being.

Lower mean scores were found for items such as sleep problems (M = 2.94, SD = 2.04) and feeling more irritable (M = 3.36, SD = 2.17).

Overall, the findings suggest that while participants exhibit strong perceived coping abilities and adaptive traits, they also report notable levels of emotional distress (Table 3).

Correlations between film genre preferences, morbid curiosity, and psychological resilience scores

Significant positive correlations were observed between most film genres and morbid curiosity scores (p < 0.001 for most genres), indicating that higher preference for these genres, in particularly crime (r = 0.539), horror (r = 0.524), and psychological thriller (r = 0.507), is associated with increased levels of morbid curiosity. In contrast, romance (r = 0.121, p = 0.065) showed a weak and non-significant association with morbid curiosity.

Regarding psychological resilience, no strong or consistent associations were identified across most genres, as the majority of correlations were weak and statistically non-significant. However, a small but significant negative correlation was found between alien-invasion genre preference and psychological resilience (r = -0.177, p = 0.006), suggesting that higher preference for this genre may be associated with slightly lower resilience levels.

Additionally, no significant correlation was found between total morbid curiosity and psychological resilience scores (r = 0.156, p = 0.17), indicating that levels of morbid curiosity are not significantly associated with resilience among participants.

Overall, these findings suggest that morbid curiosity is strongly linked to preferences for darker and more intense film genres, whereas psychological resilience appears largely independent of genre preferences, with only minimal and inconsistent associations observed (Table 5).

Discussion

This study provides insight into the relationship between morbid curiosity, horror media engagement, and psychological resilience among Lebanese university students living in a context of prolonged socio-economic and political instability. Lebanon has been facing overlapping stressors for several years, including severe economic collapse since 2019 and ongoing armed conflict since 2023, which remains present in 2026 [8, 9, 13, 14]. This chronic exposure to instability is important when interpreting the present findings, as it reflects the context of continuous uncertainty, insecurity, and cumulative stress rather than a single acute crisis.

Our findings suggest that morbid curiosity among Lebanese university students follows a selective pattern, with greater interest in cognitively engaging aspects of threat, such as crime motives and psychological profiling, rather than direct exposure to graphic content. A similar pattern has previously been described in relation to morbid curiosity, where individuals tend to seek explanatory information about dangerous situations rather than purely sensory or visually disturbing experiences [19]. At the same time, some dimensions of morbid curiosity, particularly those related to body violation and interpersonal violence, have also been associated with greater attraction to physically disturbing stimuli, suggesting that morbid curiosity may involve both cognitive and sensory components depending on the individual [19].

This pattern can be further understood through theoretical perspectives on simulated threats. The threat-simulation framework proposes that fictional narratives allow individuals to mentally simulate threatening situations in a safe environment, thereby facilitating emotional and cognitive processing of fear [15]. Similarly, controlled engagement with fear-inducing experiences has been described as a potential form of emotional regulation, in which individuals voluntarily expose themselves to negative but safe stimuli in order to enhance coping flexibility [17]. Research on horror media has also supported this perspective, showing that frightening content may be associated with threat-processing mechanisms and adaptive emotional responses under certain conditions [16, 20].

Regarding media engagement, the relatively lower preference for horror-related genres observed in this study differs from findings reported in other contexts. Previous studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic found that individuals who engaged more with horror media or suspense-horror content demonstrated greater resilience or psychological preparedness during the crisis [20, 21]. These findings suggested that fictional threat may serve as an adaptive coping mechanism by allowing individuals to simulate and process stressful experiences in a controlled environment. However, the present findings do not provide evidence for such an adaptive resilience mechanism among Lebanese university students. In this sample, morbid curiosity was associated with preference for darker film genres, but neither morbid curiosity nor horror engagement was significantly associated with psychological resilience.

One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that the nature and duration of stressors may differ across contexts. While the COVID-19 pandemic represented a more acute and time-limited crisis in prior studies, the Lebanese context is marked by prolonged socio-economic and political instability [8, 9, 13, 14]. However, this interpretation should be considered speculative, as the present study did not directly measure perceived chronic threat, individual exposure to crisis events, or subjective insecurity. In such conditions, continuous exposure to real-life threat may reduce the adaptive value of simulated fear, limiting the potential role of horror media in enhancing psychological resilience [18]. In addition, the relatively low engagement with horror media observed in this study may also contribute to this finding, as only a small proportion of participants strongly preferred horror content, while a larger proportion reported strong disagreement with it.

Moreover, the present study identified an association between morbid curiosity and preference for darker film genres, particularly horror, crime, and psychological thrillers. This indicates that individuals who are more curious about threatening or disturbing phenomena are also more likely to engage with darker and more suspenseful content [19, 20]. This relationship is also supported by the distribution of responses on the morbid curiosity scale, where participants showed relatively higher agreement with cognitively engaging items such as curiosity about crime, supernatural themes, killer motives, and criminal profiling, while lower agreement was seen for more graphic stimuli such as public execution, bodies and funerals, and violent uncensored content. These findings indicate convergent associations between morbid curiosity and preferences for darker or threat-related genres, however, the present study did not measure participants’ motivations for engaging with such content.

In comparison with previous findings, the association between morbid curiosity and horror-related media appears to be relatively consistent across populations [19, 20]. However, unlike findings reported during acute crises, this engagement was not associated with increased resilience in the present study [20, 21]. This may indicate that although morbid curiosity can predict attraction to threatening media, it does not necessarily function as a protective psychological mechanism in all environments. Its possible adaptive value may depend strongly on whether the surrounding stressor is temporary and manageable or chronic and unavoidable.

In terms of psychological resilience, participants in the present study demonstrated a moderate to high overall level of resilience. Notably, many participants expressed strong belief in their ability to get through difficult situations and cope with challenges, reflecting an important sense of perceived coping ability. At the same time, a substantial proportion also reported elevated anxiety and emotional distress. This pattern is consistent with foundational resilience theory, which emphasizes that resilience does not necessarily imply the absence of distress, but rather the capacity to maintain or regain adaptive functioning after adversity [27]. In prolonged crisis settings, individuals may continue to function, adapt, and maintain hope while still experiencing anxiety, emotional exhaustion, or trauma-related symptoms.

The only genre-specific association with psychological resilience was observed for alien-invasion films, which showed a small negative correlation with resilience. One possible explanation is that alien-invasion narratives often emphasize uncontrollable external threat, loss of agency, and disruption of familiar social structures. Such themes may resonate differently in a context marked by chronic uncertainty and instability. However, this interpretation remains exploratory, as the study did not assess participants’ perceptions of specific genre themes.

This coexistence of resilience and distress is especially relevant in the Lebanese context. University students in Lebanon have been living through cumulative exposure to multiple crises, including economic hardship, social instability, the aftermath of the Beirut blast, and ongoing war-related insecurity [9, 14]. These conditions may foster adaptive coping in some domains while simultaneously increasing emotional strain in others. Therefore, resilience in this setting should not be interpreted as the absence of suffering, but rather as the continued capacity to function and endure despite persistent adversity.

Taken together, these findings suggest that while the relationship between morbid curiosity and engagement with horror media appears consistent with previous literature, its translation into psychological resilience may vary depending on contextual factors [19–21]. In the Lebanese context, the relatively low proportion of strong horror engagement, combined with the chronic and ongoing nature of real-life threats, indicates that simulated threat exposure may not play a major adaptive role. In contrast, in contexts characterized by more acute and time-limited stressors, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, higher engagement with horror media appears to be more closely linked to resilience outcomes [20, 21]. Overall, the present findings highlight the importance of considering the broader socio-political environment when interpreting how individuals respond to threatening media and how resilience develops under prolonged crisis conditions.

Strength and limitations

An important strength of this study that is the first one done in Lebanon to examine the relationship between morbid curiosity, horror media engagement, and psychological resilience in a population exposed to prolonged and overlapping crises. The use of validated measurement tools for both psychological resilience and morbid curiosity support the reliability of the findings. The inclusion of university students from different academic levels also provides a broader representation of young adults within this context.

This study is based on a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between variables. All variables were assessed using a self-report online questionnaire at a single time point, which may introduce common-method bias and response bias. In addition, the use of an online survey and convenience sampling may affect the representativeness of the sample. Future research should include more detailed contextual measures, such as perceived chronic threat, economic hardship, and direct conflict exposure. As well as more comprehensive measures of horror engagement and examine whether these associations differ by gender.

Conclusion

This study explored the relationship between horror media engagement, morbid curiosity, and psychological resilience among Lebanese university students living in a context marked by ongoing socio-economic instability and exposure to crisis. Overall, this study found that morbid curiosity was associated with stronger preferences for darker and threat-related film genres among Lebanese university students. However, neither horror-related genre preference nor morbid curiosity was meaningfully associated with psychological resilience in this sample.

This study adds to the existing literature by examining these relationships in a non-Western and chronically unstable context, which has received less attention in previous research. It also highlights the importance of considering contextual differences when interpreting how individuals respond to stress and adapt psychologically.

Future research could build on these findings by using longitudinal approaches and by exploring additional factors, such as coping strategies, more detailed measures of horror engagement, and contextual crisis exposure in order to better understand what contributes to resilience in prolonged crisis settings.
