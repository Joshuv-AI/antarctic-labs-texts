Integrated transcriptomic network analysis reveals shared immunogenomic regulators between glioma and type 2 diabetes, highlighting VCAN as a prominent marker
Authors: Sara Tavallaei, Fariba Dehghanian, Masih Saboori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioma patients with type 2 diabetes show heterogeneous clinical outcomes, yet the underlying immunogenomic connections remain poorly defined. We aimed to identify shared peripheral immune transcriptomic signatures linking glioma (GBM/LGG) and T2D. Using an integrated systems biology framework combining single-cell and bulk RNA-seq, gene co‑expression networks, and transcription factor–regulatory networks, we characterized convergent transcriptomic and regulatory alterations across the two conditions. Functional bioinformatic analyses and qPCR validation further examined key candidate markers. A set of shared immune-related genes and their regulators was identified between glioma subtypes (GBM and LGG) and T2D, among which VCAN, SATB1, and CEBPD emerged as top candidates. These markers were associated with inflammatory processes and monocyte trafficking signatures. Notably, VCAN emerged as a consistently significant marker in both GBM and T2D across multiple bioinformatic analyses and qPCR validation. Overall, this study highlights candidate immunogenomic regulators linking peripheral immune alterations in glioma and T2D and provides a framework for future mechanistic studies on metabolic–immune interactions in cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54758-9.

Introduction

Gliomas, the solid tumors of the central nervous system, are the most prevalent neoplasms in the CNS1. According to the latest WHO classification system, adult primary diffuse gliomas are categorized into astrocytoma (grades 2, 3, and 4), oligodendroglioma, and glioblastoma based on histological and molecular profiles2. Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and deadliest brain tumor in adults, with a 5-year relative survival rate of 5.8%. Approximately 49% of malignant brain tumors are GBM, while 30% are low-grade infiltrating gliomas (grades 2–3)3,4. Current glioma diagnosis integrates molecular profiling with MRI and CT scans5,6. The current standard treatment method is the Stupp protocol, while several promising new therapies are under development, including targeted therapies, immunotherapies, laser interstitial thermal therapy, and tumor-treating fields (TTFields)7. Numerous factors are reported to indirectly influence glioma prognosis and therapeutic response, with metabolic comorbidities such as type 2 diabetes (T2D) exerting a significant impact8–10. The immune profile of gliomas with comorbidities is inadequately studied, given gliomas’ high resistance to immunotherapy and the presence of the blood-brain barrier11.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a prominent comorbidity associated with various forms of cancer. It represents one of the most prevalent metabolic disorders globally, characterized by impaired insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells and tissue insulin resistance (IR). Several epidemiological studies have reported that T2D can accelerate tumor progression in specific malignancies, including colorectal, hepatocellular, and pancreatic cancers. Conversely, T2D comorbidity in other cancer types, such as prostate cancer, has demonstrated either a negative correlation with tumor progression or no significant impact at all12–14. Moreover, four well-established shared risk factors between T2D and cancer are obesity, oxidative stress and inflammation, hyperglycemia, and hyperinsulinemia12.

Specifically pertaining to the glioma-T2D comorbidity, multiple retrospective and pooled studies (utilizing cohort, case-control, meta-analysis, and other statistical methodologies) have been conducted. These studies investigated various facets of the glioma-T2D relationship, including the impacts of hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, T2D, and metformin on tumor progression and patient survival. Regarding the hyperglycemia factor, previous studies have reported both increased15–19 and decreased risks of GBM20,21. Similarly, regarding the mere presence of T2D, some studies have indicated an elevated risk of GBM15,17,22,23, while others have suggested a decreased risk20,24–26; notably, one study found no significant association between diabetes and GBM27. Overall, the findings are highly inconsistent and may reflect underlying systemic variability in immune and metabolic signaling that influences tumor behavior within the brain microenvironment. Three key gaps remain in existing research: (A) a systems biology-based analysis of how T2D genetic predisposition influences gliomas beyond just GBM, (B) the identification of key immune regulators as well as the characterization of peripheral blood-to-TME immune crosstalk in the context of comorbidity, and (C) an examination of the underlying factors contributing to the observed clinical paradoxes. Addressing this knowledge gap is crucial for advancing comorbidity-specific immunotherapeutic strategies.

This study characterizes transcriptomic and regulatory similarities between GBM/LGG and T2D in the peripheral immune system, potentially relevant to their comorbidity. We employed an integrative systems biology approach, combining single-cell/bulk RNA-seq with gene co-expression networks, TF-regulatory networks, and functional analyses. Top candidate markers were validated via qPCR in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of gliomas and T2D patient samples and GBM-relevant cell lines. Methods

Single-cell RNA-seq data collection & processing

This study focused on GBM, LGG, T2D, and healthy individuals. We collected single-cell RNA sequencing datasets of PBMC samples from the GEO database (GBM: GSE224090, LGG: GSE188620, T2D: GSE165816, healthy: GSE174609).

The GBM and LGG datasets were generated by 10X Genomics in FASTQ format. Thus, using CellRanger (v 7.1.0), we aligned the raw sequences to the Gencode.v40 human reference genome and quantified gene expression counts. For all groups (GBM, LGG, T2D, and healthy), gene expression matrices were processed by Seurat (v 4.2.1)28 in R (v 4.3). Primary filtering was based on min.cell = 100 and min.features = 300, except for the LGG group with 700 and 900, respectively. Main filterings were set for each group proportional to the status of the datasets, considering the sum of UMIs below 10,000 to prevent doublets and mitochondrial gene counts below 15% to remove probable dead cells. Batch effect correction was performed using ComBat-seq (in SVA v 3.48.0). Normalization relied on the default Seurat method in the LogNormalize function with a scale.factor of 10,000. Next, we performed imputation to correct for dropouts as much as possible. The ADImpute package (v 1.8.0)29 tests three methods, including Baseline, DrImpute, and Network, for each gene to identify the best imputation method. We found the “Network” method to be the most suitable imputation strategy for most genes in our study. Two thousand variable features were detected using the “vst” method, and all features were scaled and centered by regressing out the mitochondrial genes. PCA analysis was conducted on the variable features, and cell clusters were created using significant PCs with the KNN algorithm. Finally, cell type annotation was performed automatically using the scType package.

DGE analysis on scRNA-seq data

DGE analysis was performed for each status (GBM, LGG, and T2D) in contrast to the healthy controls in each cell type. We utilized the DESeq2 package (v 1.36.0) with a pseudobulk approach, which sums the UMIs for all cells within a sample and then calculates the expression logarithmic fold change. We then filtered DEGs with |log2FC| > 0.58 and p-value < 0.05 to identify the most significant genes.

Although initial DEG detection used nominal p‑values (p < 0.05) to capture broad transcriptomic signatures, we additionally calculated adjusted p‑values (FDR) for all contrasts. Because the primary goal of this step was exploratory gene discovery, nominal thresholds were applied to avoid overlooking genes that could be validated through downstream multi‑layer analyses (scRNA–bulk integration, WGCNA, GRN inference, cell‑type enrichment, and qPCR). Finally, all final candidate genes passed multi-layer analyses—including VCAN—were checked for their FDR values, and were significant (adjusted p < 0.05), demonstrating the robustness of the findings.

Gene co-expression network construction on scRNA-seq data

Due to the sparse nature of scRNA-seq data and the sensitivity of network construction to this sparsity, we employed the hdWGCNA package (v 0.4.00)30 to address the high sparsity level through meta-cell construction. In this approach, a defined number of cells with identical biological origins are grouped to form a meta-cell, and the average gene expression value of the cells within a meta-cell is calculated. Consequently, we obtain a metacell gene expression matrix. In this study, we constructed modules of co-expressed genes from the meta-cell gene expression matrix for each status (GBM, LGG, T2D, and healthy) across each cell type. The K parameter for the meta-cell was set to 25. The thresholding power was determined based on a scale-free topology with a scale-free index greater than 0.8. The network type was a signed hybrid. Module eigengene (ME) was calculated using the SVD method, and module membership (KME) was assessed for each module.

Preservation analysis on scRNA-seq data

The reference dataset was healthy status, while the query was one of the GBM, LGG, or T2D. In this part, each query dataset was projected onto the reference to identify the most non-preserved modules that indicated the most involved genes in each disorder. This process used the ModulePreservation function from the hdWGCNA package, with 250 permutations in each status and each specific cell type. The modules with the highest median rank and Zsummary score of less than two were identified as the most non-preserved in each status.

Selection of glioma and T2D shared genes

To address the project’s questions, we first identified common genes between DEGs of GBM and T2D, as well as between LGG and T2D. Next, we found common genes between the non-preserved modules of GBM and T2D and between LGG and T2D. These common genes illustrate the relationships between glioma grades and type 2 diabetes and potential comorbidity consequences. Both previous steps were carried out using the tidyverse package (v 1.3.2) in R. Finally, we obtained the intersection of selected markers of DGE and network analysis to identify the most significant markers in the GBM-T2D and LGG-T2D associations.

Bulk RNA-seq data collection & processing

The PBMC bulk RNA-seq datasets for the GBM and healthy groups were obtained from Dan Qi et al.‘s study31 in gene expression matrix format. The PBMC data for the T2D group were collected from the SRA database in FASTQ format (SRP274496).

The tissue bulk RNA-seq datasets for the GBM group were sourced from the TCGA, CPTAC-3, and HCMI-CMDC projects available on the GDC portal. Normal brain datasets were retrieved from the GTEx portal.

The raw sequencing files were quality-checked using the FastQC tool (v 0.12.1). Trimming was performed using the HEADCROP:10 method in the Trimmomatic tool (v 0.39). The trimmed reads were aligned to the Hg38 reference genome using HISAT2 (v 2.2.1). Gene expression counts were quantified using the FeatureCounts tool from the Subread package (v 2.0.3). The gene expression matrices for all groups were processed using the DESeq2 and tidyverse packages in R. The processing steps included gene filtering, batch effect correction (implemented via combat-seq from the sva package), normalization, and transformation (using the “vst” method), as well as outlier sample detection based on PCA and hierarchical clustering. The matrix processing was conducted separately for PBMC and tissue data.

DGE analysis on bulk RNA-seq data

We utilized the DESeq2 package to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each condition (GBM and T2D in PBMC data, and GBM in tissue data) compared to healthy controls. DESeq2 models count data using a negative binomial distribution, estimates gene-wise dispersion parameters, and performs differential expression testing using the Wald test. We then filtered DEGs with |log2FC| > 0.58 and p-value < 0.05 to select the most significant genes.

DESeq2-adjusted p‑values (FDR) were also computed for all contrasts. Although the main filtering step used nominal p‑values (p < 0.05), this was intended as an exploratory discovery phase. Finally, the key genes prioritized for downstream analyses were checked, and showed consistent significance across nominal and adjusted p‑values, supporting the robustness of the bulk DEG findings.

Gene co-expression network construction on PBMC bulk RNA-seq data

We used the WGCNA package (v 1.72-1)32 to construct signed hybrid modules of co-expressed genes for each status separately. First, we detected genes and samples with missing values using the goodSampleGenes function. Next, we calculated pairwise gene correlations using the Pearson method. The thresholding power was set based on scale-free topology, with a scale-free index higher than 0.8. After thresholding gene correlations to the selected power, we created the gene-gene adjacency matrix and the topological overlap matrix (TOM). Using the hclust function, we examined the hierarchical clustering of modules and merged close modules. The module eigengene (ME) was calculated for each module.

Module-Trait Analysis on PBMC Bulk RNA-seq Data

This analysis measured the correlation between each module and the biological status (GBM/T2D). First, the modules were sorted based on their module eigengenes. Next, module membership and GeneSignificance were calculated for the selected modules. GeneSignificance is a metric that indicates the degree of connection of a gene with the desired biological characteristic. Lastly, the modules most associated with GBM and T2D were chosen for further investigation.

In silico validation of scRNA-seq data analysis results

We identified common genes between DEGs of GBM and T2D from bulk RNA-seq data. Additionally, we found genes shared between GBM and T2D in significant modules in the module-trait analysis. Consequently, we established the intersection of DGE and the network. We evaluated the results of single-cell data by determining the intersection of GBM-T2D genes selected in the single-cell section and those selected in the bulk section. No open-access bulk RNA-seq data of PBMC were available for LGG patients to validate the LGG-T2D genes.

Gene regulatory network construction on scRNA-seq data

The hdWGCNA package33 connects to multiple external reference databases (JASPAR and Ensemble) to find potential transcription factors (TFs) on each gene’s promoter region. Using XGBoost algorithms, the expression of each gene is modelled by its potential TFs. The predicted gene expression is then compared to the input data’s gene expression. Consequently, TFs with either a positive or negative correlation (activator or repressor) are the most likely candidates for the associated gene in that data. Among these, those with higher gain value (and frequency) have better predictive power for the corresponding gene regulon. Hence, we employed this method to construct gene regulatory networks (GRNs) for each group (GBM, LGG, and T2D) in each cell type separately. The defined TFs were categorized into two groups: Group+ includes TFs of upregulated genes that positively correlate with the gene (and these TFs have exhibited an upregulation pattern). Group– encompasses TFs of downregulated genes that negatively correlate with the gene (and these TFs have demonstrated an upregulation pattern). Thus, we selected activator TFs for upregulated markers and repressor TFs for downregulated ones. In the next step, we looked for common TFs associated with each gene between GBM and T2D and between LGG and T2D (in the same and/or different cell types).

GSEA on scRNA-seq data

We used gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to investigate the biological hallmarks related to the shared genes. It was conducted using the fgsea package (v 1.26.0), which utilizes “stat” values calculated in DGE analysis to determine the NES value for each biological hallmark in a fast calculation mode. The higher the absolute NES value for a hallmark, the greater its impact on the disease under investigation. Here, we performed GSEA separately for each condition (GBM, LGG, and T2D). Next, we sought to identify the selected genes from the previous steps within significant hallmarks.

AUCell

We utilized AUCell analysis (v 1.30.0) to determine which cell clusters were enriched for the shared gene signatures of GBM–T2D and LGG–T2D. The Seurat objects of the PBMC samples in each condition, encompassing all the cell types, served as inputs. The identified cell populations with the enriched signature were biologically annotated.

TIMER immune association

We utilized the ‘Gene’ module of TIMER2.0 Immune Association to compute the correlation between gene expression (of shared markers in GBM-T2D and LGG-T2D) and the level of immune infiltration (via the identified cell type in AUCell analysis) within the microenvironment of GBM and LGG.

Human sample collection

This cross-sectional study included patients with T2D, glioma grades II–III, Glioblastoma, and healthy individuals. Peripheral blood samples of participants were collected from two authorized medical centers over two years (2022–2023). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The ethics committee at the University of Isfahan approved the study protocol (IR.ARI.MUI.REC.1400.108, Approval date: 2022-02-26), and all participants provided written informed consent. Sample selection for the glioma group was based on molecular and pathological reports in accordance with the WHO tumor classification system, and only patients with primary tumors were included. All participants were fasting and had not undergone any chemotherapy or radiotherapy before sample collection. For each case, 2 mL of peripheral blood was drawn, transferred to a screw-cap laboratory tube containing EDTA anticoagulant, and stored at 4 °C. PBMC isolation from the samples commenced within 6 h after sampling.

Cell culture

The human astrocytoma cell line 1321N1 (glial-like morphology) and the human Glioblastoma cell line U-251 MG (pleomorphic/astrocytoid morphology) were used in this study. The 1321N1 and U-251 MG cell lines were obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran and Iran Stem Cell Technology Co., respectively. Both cell lines were mycoplasma-free and authenticated by STR profiling. U-251 MG cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin streptomycin (Gibco), while the 1321N1 cells, as a non-GBM astrocytic control, were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were passaged routinely and harvested at the logarithmic growth phase for RNA extraction.

PBMC isolation, RNA extraction, and cDNA synthesis

PBMCs from peripheral blood were isolated using density gradient centrifugation with Lymphodex (Innotrain Ficoll, Germany). Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Genius.Gene, Iran) and chloroform. Subsequently, the extracted RNA was washed with isopropanol and 75% ethanol. The quality and quantity of the RNA samples were evaluated using the NanoDrop One/OneC spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and gel electrophoresis. Potential genomic DNA contamination was removed from the RNA samples using a DNase I RNase-free kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Finally, following the manufacturer’s protocol, RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the AddScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Addbio, Korea).

Real-time qPCR assay

RT-qPCR was conducted using the RealQ Plus 2X Master Mix Green without ROX (Amplicon, Denmark) on the Rotor-Gene (Q MDx 5plex HRM, USA). Human Beta-Actin was used as the endogenous control. Primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST and verified with Oligo7. The sequences of the primers were as follows: B-ACT_F: 5′-TTCGAGCAAGAGATGGCCA-3′, B-ACT_R: 5′-CACAGGACTCCATGCCCAG-3′, VCAN_F: 5′-TGACTGTGGATGGGGTTGTG-3′, VCAN_R: 5′-GCGTCACACTGCTCAAATCC-3′, PLBD1_F: 5′-GCAAGAGGTGGGCAGACAT-3′, and PLBD1_R: 5′-AGGGCCAATATCCTTTCCGT-3′. The VCAN primers were designed to cover all four variants of this transcript.

Statistical analysis

Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔCT) method. Data distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For each disease group, significance comparison was performed against the healthy control group using unpaired two‑tailed t‑tests. Significance for cell line comparisons was assessed using a one‑sample t‑test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the diagnostic performance of candidate genes, and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals was calculated.

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical tests were done in Prism software (v8).

Results

High-resolution cell type annotation of PBMCs using Seurat enabled transcriptome analysis of immune subpopulations

17,824 cells in the GBM group, 53,825 in LGG, 27,463 in T2D, and 31,851 in the healthy control group passed QC filtering. Batch effects were corrected as much as possible, as shown in Figures S1−S3. The first 15 PCs were the most significant for downstream steps across all groups. Clusters were determined using an optimized resolution. Each group contained a variety of immune cell types. For subsequent analyses, we focused on three major immune cell groups: T cells, B cells, and monocytes. Accordingly, all subtypes belonging to each lineage were aggregated under the general annotation of that group.

43 shared markers with similar transcriptomic dysregulation between GBM and T2D were identified as candidates relevant to their comorbidity

Figures 2−4 present co-expressed modules and their hub genes identified in monocytes (similar results for other cell types are depicted in Figures S4−S9). The preservation status of modules for monocytes is shown in Fig. 5 (similar results for other cell types are indicated in Figures S10−S11). We integrated DGE and co-expression network analysis to identify highly significant markers associated with the development of each condition (Fig. 6). This analysis focused on identifying shared transcriptomic patterns between T2D and GBM through the immune system, which may be relevant to their comorbidity. Such similar dysregulation may reflect potential interplay between the two diseases. Hence, we identified 43 common genes between the markers of GBM and T2D with the same transcriptomic dysregulation pattern (14 upregulated genes and 29 downregulated genes). EEF1G, FABP5, F8A1, TRIM68, IZUMO4, ZNF793, ZNF347, and NBPF9 are genes that exert their effects in B cells in both disorders. SATB1, CDCA7L, NHSL2, F8A1, ZNF395, STAG3, GAS6-AS1, LGMN, IZUMO4, ZNF793, and ZNF347 demonstrate their effects in T cells, whereas GABARAP is present in monocytes of both GBM and T2D patients. Other genes among the 43 shared markers originated from different cell types; for example, a gene could be a hub in T cells of GBM while serving as a hub in monocytes of T2D. Detailed information on all 43 genes, including their DGE characteristics and cell‑type origins, is provided in Table S1.

129 shared markers with similar transcriptomic dysregulation were identified between T2D and LGG

Once again, we combined DGE and co-expression network analysis to identify highly significant markers involved in the development of each condition (Fig. 7). We found 129 common genes between the significant markers of LGG and T2D. These 129 genes may contribute to immune alterations in LGG-T2D interplay through inducing similar expression patterns (56 upregulated genes and 73 downregulated genes). Some shared genes originated from different cell types, whereas others were derived from the same cell type in both disorders. A comprehensive overview of the 129 genes, including their DGE characteristics and cell-type origins, is provided in Table S2.

Notably, overlapping markers were observed between the T2D–GBM and T2D–LGG comparisons. RBP7, VCAN, EEF1G, FOLR3, PLBD1, and GABARAP with upregulation and SATB1, CDCA7L, NHSL2, CEBPD, NBPF9, RAB3IP, and TMEM204 with downregulation pattern were shared genes between T2D and both GBM and LGG.

PBMC Bulk RNA-seq data analysis confirmed the core findings of single-cell data in GBM

We investigated shared markers between GBM and T2D using DGE and Module-Trait network analysis using an integrative approach applied to PBMC bulk RNA‑seq datasets (Figure S12). In this analysis, we identified 86 downregulated genes and 64 upregulated genes as common markers between GBM and T2D. All information regarding these genes is provided in Table S3. Comparison of bulk and single‑cell results revealed some consistent patterns. The most notable was VCAN, which exhibited a similar upregulation pattern in both GBM and T2D across single‑cell and bulk datasets.

GRNs indicate that transcriptional dysregulation of TFs regulating shared genes may enhance the relationship between T2D and glioma

If transcriptional dysregulation of relevant TFs strengthens the associated gene‑expression pattern—particularly the shared pattern observed between T2D and glioma—the gene can be considered a more reliable candidate contributing to their comorbidity. Therefore, we constructed the TF-gene regulatory network on single-cell data of GBM, LGG and T2D to address four questions, which are explained below:

Q1. Among the shared genes, is there any gene whose TFs exhibit an aligned regulatory effect, as described above? Yes, the TFs of 29 genes (out of 43 genes) in the GBM-T2D exhibited aligned effects. In the case of LGG-T2D, the TFs of 94 genes (out of 129 genes) demonstrated the same results. All the information about these genes and their TFs is provided in Tables S4−S5.

Q2. Which genes have common TFs between T2D and glioma? In GBM-T2D, 22 genes had common TFs, while in LGG-T2D, 71 genes had common TFs. (Tables S4−S5).

Q3. Is there any TF that regulates multiple genes? KLF9, ZBTB7A, HMBOX1, FOSB, STAT2, ZBTB18, EGR1, JUND, GFI1, GATA3, MAFF, and FOXP1 are TFs that each regulate several genes in GBM-T2D. NFE2, KLF9, ZBTB7A, KLF6, FOS, FOSB, JUND, JUN, MNT, and JUNB are TFs that regulate several genes in LGG-T2D.

Q4. Are any of the shared genes themselves transcription factor? In GBM-T2D, CEBPD is a transcription factor. In LGG-T2D, FOSL2, NR4A2, CEBPD, TP53, TGIF1, RFX1, NR4A1, and KLF4 are also transcription factors. The targets of these TFs are not among the shared genes but may involve additional genes in the glioma–T2D relationship. Details on the regulatory links of some TFs are displayed in Figure S13.

Some shared genes exhibit similar pathway enrichment between glioma and T2D

Gene set enrichment analysis revealed enrichment of common pathways between glioma and T2D through some shared genes. For example, among the common genes between GBM–T2D and LGG–T2D, SATB1 was implicated in apoptosis and KRAS signaling pathways. VCAN was associated with pathways including apical junction, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, glycolysis, and angiogenesis, while CEBPD was involved in TNFA signaling via NFκB. We also examined whether TFs regulating these genes were enriched in the reported pathways. For CEBPD in the TNFA–NFκB pathway, four TFs—KLF9, FOSB, FOS, and JUNB—were also enriched in the same pathway. Pathway significance based on p‑values is reported in Fig. 8 for monocytes and for each condition, and in Figure S14 for T and B cells.

Classical monocytes show stronger enrichment of the shared genes signature than other peripheral immune cells

AUCell analysis revealed that the 43 shared genes in GBM-T2D are predominantly enriched in classical monocytes, and a similar pattern was observed for the 129 shared genes in LGG-T2D (Fig. 9a–h). Although some shared genes were detected in T cells, B cells, and monocytes, classical monocytes appear to show stronger enrichment for the overall shared genes signature.

Additionally, we assessed the correlation between gene expression and monocyte infiltration using TIMER to identify genes that may influence monocyte infiltration from peripheral blood into the tumor immune microenvironment (Fig. 9i–j). This web tool estimates correlations using multiple algorithms based on TCGA datasets. According to TIMER2.0, the genes VCAN, FOLR3, PLBD1, SATB1, CDCA7L, CEBPD, RAB3IP, and TMEM204 are more likely to influence monocyte infiltration in GBM. Similarly, VCAN, FOLR3, PLBD1, GABARAP, SATB1, CDCA7L, NHSL2, CEBPD, RAB3IP, and TMEM204 are more likely to influence monocyte infiltration in LGG.

qPCR confirms VCAN as a significant molecular marker in the T2D and GBM cohorts

To experimentally validate the bioinformatically identified, similar transcriptional dysregulation between glioma grades and T2D, we selected two candidate genes from the intersection of GBM-T2D and LGG-T2D common gene sets. Candidates were ranked based on their Log2FC and p-value, with VCAN and PLBD1 emerging as the top two. Quantitative real-time PCR was employed to assess the expression patterns of VCAN and PLBD1 across our study cohorts (cross‑sectional).

Our study cohort comprised 20 GBM cases, 20 T2D cases, 9 glioma grade III cases, and 9 glioma grade II cases. A control group of 20 healthy individuals was included for both glioma and T2D assessments. Furthermore, the 1321N1 and U-251 MG cell lines were incorporated into the study. qPCR analysis confirmed the overexpression of VCAN across all tested groups, aligning with our bioinformatics predictions. Notably, VCAN exhibited statistically significant changes within the GBM and T2D groups, with mean expression fold changes of 4.33 (p = 0.0037) and 2.96 (p = 0.0005), respectively. In contrast, the results for PLBD1 did not reach statistical significance. Detailed mean fold change calculations for both genes across all groups are presented in Table S6, and plots illustrating statistical significance are shown in Fig. 10a-d. Based on these findings and supported by diagnostic performance ROC curves (Fig. 10e-f), VCAN is established as a reliable molecular marker associated with GBM (AUC = 0.7579, 95% CI = 0.6030–0.9128, P = 0.0059) and T2D (AUC = 0.8263, 95% CI = 0.6960–0.9566, P = 0.0005).

VCAN overexpression parallels between GBM brain tissue and peripheral blood

VCAN emerged as a significant marker in both single-cell and bulk peripheral blood datasets, and in PBMC samples from GBM and T2D cohorts. It was enriched in some cancer-related pathways and associated with monocyte infiltration within the GBM tumor microenvironment. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate its expression in GBM brain tissue compared with normal brain to gain deeper insights into its level within the GBM TME. Using bulk RNA-seq datasets from GBM and normal brain samples, we performed differential gene expression analysis. Our results showed that VCAN was significantly overexpressed in GBM tissue, with a log fold change of 3.34 (p < 1 × 10⁻⁶, adjusted p < 1 × 10⁻⁶). This expression pattern may reflect consistent VCAN upregulation across the peripheral blood–TME axis.

Discussion

The prevalence of glioma patients with comorbid T2D is increasing. Since T2D affects the immune system and modulates immune profiles34, it may influence tumor immune microenvironment through the peripheral blood-TME axis. In this study, we investigated the interplay between glioma and T2D by identifying shared transcriptomic markers within the peripheral immune system.

Using single-cell RNA-seq data, we constructed co-expression and TF–gene regulatory networks, alongside differential gene expression analysis to identify key genes in GBM, LGG, and T2D with an integrative approach. Given that shared key genes exhibiting similar transcriptomic dysregulation between glioma and T2D offer potential insights into their comorbidity, we identified shared key genes in GBM-T2D and LGG-T2D. Subsequently, bulk RNA-seq data were utilized to validate these findings. Further analyses, including GSEA, AUCELL, and TIMER, were performed to gain deeper mechanistic insights. Finally, real-time qPCR was conducted on two selected genes to confirm their expression patterns in collected human PBMC samples and relevant cell lines.

Our bioinformatic analyses revealed 43 genes in GBM-T2D and 129 genes in LGG-T2D exhibiting concordant expression patterns between glioma and T2D in PBMCs. Considering TF–gene regulatory relationships, TFs of 22 genes (out of 43) in GBM-T2D and 71 genes (out of 129) in LGG-T2D demonstrated aligned expression-regulatory patterns with their corresponding genes, signifying shared regulatory mechanisms between glioma and T2D. These genes (along with their TFs) represent candidates potentially modulating immune system interactions in the context of glioma-T2D interplay. Some candidate genes were shared between glioma and T2D via the same immune cell type, whereas others were shared through distinct cell types. AUCell analysis indicated that the overall effect of all candidate genes was significant in classical monocytes (CD14++ CD16− monocytes). Among the candidates shared between LGG-T2D and GBM-T2D, SATB1, VCAN, and CEBPD (along with their respective TFs) demonstrated significant enrichment in specific biological pathways within monocytes. Glycolysis with VCAN contribution (positive association with the development of GBM and T2D) and Apoptosis with SATB1 contribution (negative association with the development of GBM and T2D) in GBM-T2D, along with TNFα-NFκB with CEBPD involvement (negative association with the development of LGG and T2D) in LGG-T2D, were the most statistically significant. According to TIMER, VCAN, SATB1, and CEBPD are reportedly involved in monocyte infiltration in both GBM and LGG. Additionally, VCAN was found to be overexpressed in GBM brain tissue bulk datasets. Our experimental validation confirmed significant VCAN overexpression in both GBM and T2D groups within our PBMCs sample collection, aligning with the bioinformatic findings. For glioma II and III, the results were insignificant, likely attributable to the limited sample size.

We assessed only the two top log2FC-ranked candidate genes, VCAN and PLBD1, experimentally. Given that VCAN, SATB1, and CEBPD emerged as top candidates in our integrative analyses, and VCAN received both bioinformatic and experimental support, we focus the discussion on these three genes.

The VCAN-encoded protein, Versican, is a major extracellular matrix (ECM) component involved in cell adhesion, proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and inflammation35–37. According to the Human Protein Atlas database, this gene primarily functions within monocytes. VCAN has been recognized as a prognostic marker across various neoplasms, particularly among glycolysis-related markers38–42. Furthermore, VCAN expression positively correlates with high immune cell infiltration in gastric cancer, most notably with monocytes and M2 macrophages39,40. However, the specific molecular mechanisms by which VCAN overexpression influences the glycolysis pathway, particularly within peripheral immune cells, remain underexplored.

Our findings demonstrate VCAN overexpression and increased glycolysis in monocytes and T lymphocytes within individual GBM and T2D groups. Furthermore, we established an association between VCAN overexpression and monocyte infiltration in glioma. Building upon previous research, elevated inflammation and lactate (resulting from increased glycolysis) are known to enhance blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability43–45. Consequently, we hypothesize that VCAN overexpression in PBMCs of GBM cases, by exacerbating glycolysis and inflammation, may facilitate the attachment and subsequent infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the brain.

These infiltrated immune cells, particularly T lymphocytes and monocytes, may further increase local Versican levels, consistent with their transcriptomic profiles observed in both peripheral blood and brain tissue in our study. Prior reports indicate that within a high-Versican TME under hypoxic conditions, cancer cells exhibit enhanced glycolysis, thereby promoting proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Moreover, infiltrated T cells become ineffective due to glioma cells downregulating major histocompatibility complex (MHC) expression while simultaneously overexpressing programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1)46,47. In the context of infiltrated monocytes, CD14 + monocytes (classical monocytes) exposed to GBM cells may acquire MDSC (myeloid-derived suppressor cell)-like characteristics, such as upregulating the production of immunosuppressive factors like B7-H1, IL-10, and TGF-β, and inducing apoptosis in activated lymphocytes47. Additionally, some infiltrated CD14 + monocytes might differentiate into bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM)48. BMDMs exert immunosuppressive effects through tumor cell phagocytosis (and gaining M2-like phenotype), secreting suppressive cytokines, and activating immune checkpoint pathways49–51. Conversely, elevated levels of lactate can promote macrophage polarization towards a pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype45; however, high-grade gliomas typically display an increase in M2 macrophages and a decrease in M1. Recent findings suggest that GBM-derived exosomes can convert anti-tumoral macrophages into a pro-tumoral state46,47. In parallel, in diabetic patients, an increase in M1 macrophage precursor monocytes (CD14 + CD16+ phenotype) has been reported in peripheral blood52,53, and obesity and diabetes are associated with a shift in the macrophage balance towards the M1 phenotype, driven by factors like free fatty acids (FFAs), hyperglycemia, and tissue hypoxia54–56.

While the direct impact of VCAN overexpression in PBMCs on T2D cases has not been extensively investigated, previous reports suggest that VCAN contributes to systemic inflammation and tissue insulin resistance. High systemic inflammation can induce VCAN overexpression, creating a potential feedback loop where increased VCAN levels further promote inflammation57–60. Versican can interact with lymphoid cells and stimulate the production of inflammatory cytokines60.

In conclusion, integrating our findings with prior literature, we predict a heightened inflammatory state in GBM-T2D comorbid cases, driven by both cancerous and diabetic conditions, and further amplified by VCAN overexpression. We hypothesize that elevated VCAN levels may enhance the infiltration of monocytes and T lymphocytes, fostering an immunosuppressive TME in comorbid GBM-T2D cases through mechanisms including cancer cell promotion, T cell dysfunction, and the differentiation of CD14 + monocytes into BMDMs and MDSCs. Concurrently, there is a possibility of M1 macrophage dominance over M2 phenotypes and the formation of an immunosupportive TME in GBM-T2D comorbidities, potentially mediated by lactate-induced macrophage polarization and the transition of M1 precursors to M1 macrophages in the blood, followed by their infiltration into the TME due to the diabetic milieu. This dualistic potential warrants examination through comprehensive bioinformatic and experimental approaches in a comorbid cohort.

Within the individual LGG and T2D groups, our bioinformatic analysis revealed VCAN overexpression in T lymphocytes. In line with its proposed roles in promoting inflammation and immune cell trafficking, VCAN may facilitate increased T-cell entry into the LGG TME. Consistent with previous studies, localized T lymphocytes within the LGG TME exhibit a dual function. On one hand, they can enhance the antitumor immune response, for instance, through cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) inducing tumor apoptosis by recognizing tumor antigens. On the other hand, they may contribute to tumor progression via interactions with microglia and the secretion of suppressive cytokines61–64. Further investigation with a larger sample size for the LGG cohort, and additionally a LGG-T2D cohort, is required to determine the status of T cell duality in LGG-T2D comorbid TMEs.

The SATB1 protein recruits chromatin-remodeling factors to regulate chromatin structure and gene expression. Reduced SATB1 levels lead to the abnormal activation of CD4 + T cells and the infiltration of inflammatory cells into various organs65. Furthermore, reduced SATB1 levels correlate with increased PD-1 expression on T cells. This dysregulation compromises antitumor immunity and accelerates T cell fatigue within inflammatory environments, such as the TME. Consistent with these observations, low SATB1 levels in tumor-infiltrating T cells have been linked to elevated PD-1 and reduced IFN-γ production66,67.

The direct impact of SATB1 reduction in PBMCs on T2D status has not been directly investigated, but its established roles in inflammation suggest a plausible biological interplay.

Our bioinformatic analysis observed SATB1 downregulation in T lymphocytes in individual GBM and T2D groups. Based on the mentioned reports, we postulate that SATB1 downregulation in T lymphocytes may increase the infiltration rate into the GBM TME, consistent with the effects of VCAN on T cells. Within the GBM TME, localized T cells might exhibit distinct phenotypes, with some maintaining an inflammatory profile while others engage in immunosuppressive activities, potentially via T cell fatigue.

Our GSEA indicated a negative correlation between SATB1-involved apoptosis and the progression of GBM and T2D in peripheral immune cells. This suggests that reduced SATB1 in T cells might lead to decreased apoptosis. This finding contrasts with previous studies, which report that SATB1 reduction leads to increased apoptosis in non-malignant T cells67–70. The discrepancy in our results might stem from the analysis of a heterogeneous T cell population, as we examined all subtypes collectively, and further investigations are needed on specific subtypes.

CEBPD plays a crucial role in inducing the expression of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines and has a dual role in macrophage polarization. A decrease in CEBPD inhibits the production of proinflammatory molecules and may disrupt the M1/M2 balance71–73. Previous studies indicate that CEBPD has complex interactions with NF-κB. A reduction in CEBPD appears to disrupt its crosstalk with NF-κB, thereby diminishing the inflammatory response to TNFα. While, CEBPD establishes an autoregulatory loop by inducing FBXW7α, a known negative feedback regulator. Consequently, a reduction in CEBPD leads to decreased FBXW7α levels and potentially uncontrolled activation of NF-κB in response to TNFα72,73.

Our study reveals that CEBPD is downregulated in all peripheral immune cell types examined, within the LGG and T2D groups. This downregulation is associated with the overexpression of four of its repressor TFs (FOSB, FOS, KLF9, and JUNB) via TNFα-NFκB.

FOS and JUNB, as key components of the AP-1 complex, interact physically and functionally with NF-κB. An increase in these factors may consequently enhance the cooperation between AP-1 and NF-κB74. KLF9 directly interacts with NF-κB, and its elevated levels are shown to limit the activity of the TNFα-NFκB pathway through direct inhibition of NF-κB. The combined effect of decreased CEBPD and increased KLF9 is likely to result in a more potent suppression of inflammation. This synergistic effect may be achieved through the dual mechanism of reduced inflammatory cytokine production (due to decreased CEBPD) and inhibited NF-κB transcriptional activity (due to increased KLF9)73,75,76.

The negative correlation between CEBPD-involved TNFα-NFκB pathway and progression of LGG and T2D in peripheral blood immune cells, observed in our GSEA, suggests that reduced CEBPD expression may attenuate inflammatory responses in these conditions. However, the effects of FOS and JUNB, besides the aforementioned NFκB activation through the negative feedback, should not be overlooked. The dual and context-dependent roles of CEBPD in modulating inflammatory responses warrant further investigation, particularly in LGG–T2D comorbid cohorts.

The anti-inflammatory effects of CEBPD may help reduce the tissue damage associated with T2D. Previous reports have shown that reduced CEBPD expression is linked to elevated fasting blood glucose levels77. In line with this, another study demonstrated that T2D patients with good glycemic control exhibit higher CEBPD expression and improved immune function compared with those with poor glycemic control78. These findings suggest that CEBPD can exert dual roles in T2D.

All the interpretations presented above are grounded in evidence from our study in separate GBM/LGG and T2D cohorts and are consistent with previous literature. However, in the context of their comorbidity, our conclusions remain hypothetical. This study has several limitations, including the relatively small experimental sample size, the absence of a comorbid glioma–T2D cohort in both bioinformatic and experimental analyses, and the lack of mechanistic functional assays. Notably, our experimental validation provides stronger support for VCAN compared with the other highlighted genes, and the findings related to LGG appear less robust than those for GBM.

For future research, we recommend experimentally testing the hypotheses proposed in this study and further investigating additional candidate genes implicated in the glioma–T2D interplay. If follow‑up studies demonstrate that this comorbidity is immunosuppressive in some patients while immunosupportive in others, such insights may form a critical foundation for developing personalized immunotherapies tailored specifically to comorbid cases.

Conclusion

Using an integrative transcriptomic framework, this study identified shared molecular markers showing concordant transcriptomic dysregulation, common transcriptional regulators, and overlapping pathway enrichment (in some markers) between glioma (GBM/LGG) and T2D in peripheral immune cells. These findings suggest potential molecular associations between the two conditions through systemic immune and inflammatory mechanisms.

Integrating our functional bioinformatic analyses with previous literature, some of these markers may exert dual roles in the context of promoting inflammation, directing antitumor immune responses, and contributing to T2D pathogenesis. However, the current evidence is not sufficient to confirm these hypotheses. Such dual roles may partly be related to the longstanding epidemiological paradoxes reported in glioma-T2D associations.

Finally, among the investigated markers, VCAN emerged as a consistently significant marker in both GBM and T2D across multiple bioinformatic analyses and experimental validation.
