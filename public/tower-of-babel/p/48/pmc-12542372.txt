Re-examining the association between region-specific pain recurrence and muscle force strategies in patients with patellofemoral pain via OpenSim and artificial intelligence: a prospective cohort study toward targeted rehabilitation
Authors: Zeyi Zhang, Ting Fan, Jin Wu, Youping Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study utilized artificial intelligence (AI)–based machine learning algorithms, alongside the shapley additive explanations (SHAP) framework, to identify lower-limb muscle force patterns associated with recurrent patellofemoral pain (PFP) in the anterior and posterior patellar (APP), medial border of the patella (MBP), and lateral border of the patella (LBP) regions. The goal was to inform region-specific strength training strategies.MethodsA total of 299 patients with prior PFP underwent baseline biomechanical assessments, during which lower-limb and trunk muscle forces were estimated using OpenSim modeling. Participants were then prospectively followed for six months and categorized into pain-free, APP, MBP, or LBP groups according to PFP recurrence and pain location. Machine learning models were subsequently applied in conjunction with the SHAP framework to identify region-specific associations between muscle force patterns and PFP incidence.ResultsAPP recurrence was linked to gracilis force < 0.055 N/kg, adductor longus force > 0.110 N/kg, tibialis anterior force < 0.678 N/kg, tensor fasciae latae force > 0.144 N/kg, and internal oblique force < 0.699 N/kg. MBP recurrence was associated with rectus femoris force > 0.800 N/kg, gracilis force > 0.054 N/kg, gluteus maximus force > 0.379 N/kg, adductor longus force > 0.711 N/kg, and semitendinosus force < 0.037 N/kg. LBP recurrence corresponded to rectus femoris force < 0.530 N/kg, adductor longus force > 0.194 N/kg, tensor fasciae latae force < 0.082 N/kg, gracilis force > 0.040 N/kg, and gluteus maximus force < 0.151 N/kg.ConclusionsMachine learning analyses revealed region-specific muscle force patterns predictive of PFP recurrence, offering a biomechanical foundation for targeted strength interventions in APP, MBP, and LBP cases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-025-01762-1.Keywords: Patellofemoral pain, Pain regions, Gait analysis, Neuromuscular control, Predictive modeling

Introduction

Patellofemoral pain (PFP) is a common musculoskeletal disorder characterized by insidious pain in the anterior, posterior, or peripatellar regions [1]. It predominantly affects individuals under 40 with high levels of physical activity, exhibiting a lifetime prevalence of approximately 25% [2]. Once established, 57% of patients develop abnormal gait patterns that result in excessive loading of the patellofemoral joint during ambulation, leading to recurrent pain [3]. The persistent and repetitive anterior or peripatellar pain during walking severely compromises quality of life and increases the risk of knee osteoarthritis [4].

Given the severe consequences of PFP recurrence, targeted prevention is imperative, and clarifying the mechanisms underlying PFP recurrence is a prerequisite for devising effective prevention strategies. Current medical research has attempted to elucidate these mechanisms, identifying factors such as age, physical activity level [5], acetabular bone marrow lesions, and knee effusion scores as potentially associated with PFP [6]. However, these mechanisms are relatively broad and difficult to modify, rendering them unsuitable for developing specific preventive training protocols. Consequently, an increasing number of researchers are investigating muscle force patterns during ambulation as a more practical explanation for PFP recurrence. Alsaleh et al. [7] conducted a meta-analysis showing that, compared with uninjured individuals (without PFP), patients with patellofemoral pain exhibited lower concentric strength of the quadriceps, biceps femoris, semitendinosus, and semimembranosus during isolated knee-motion tasks, such as seated resisted knee extension/flexion and open kinetic chain (OKC) strength tests. These reduced strength characteristics may be associated with the recurrence of PFP. In contrast, Besier et al. [8] reported that, compared with the control group, patients with PFP exhibited greater co-contraction of the quadriceps and hamstrings during walking, suggesting that such muscle force patterns may be linked to the recurrence of PFP. Moreover, a systematic review by Neal et al. [9], which synthesized prospective studies investigating potential risk factors for PFP, reported that quadriceps weakness and excessive hip abductor strength, when assessed concentrically with an isokinetic dynamometer, were associated with an increased incidence of PFP. Nevertheless, Plastaras et al. [10] found that, during static testing, patients with PFP exhibited significantly reduced isometric hip abductor strength compared with healthy controls.

It is evident that controversy still exists regarding which muscle force characteristics contribute to PFP recurrence. One reason for this disagreement may be that previous studies have not distinguished among different PFP regions. However, earlier research has indicated that although PFP pain is generally diffuse, there are nonetheless certain focal areas—identifiable through palpation and self-report—with differing recurrence rates, specifically 27% in the anterior and posterior patellar (APP) region, 50% in the medial border of the patella (MBP) region, and 73% in the lateral border of the patella (LBP) region [11]. The variation in incidence suggests that the underlying pathogenic mechanisms may differ, and a generalized analysis is likely to yield inaccurate conclusions [11]. Therefore, studies that separately analyze the muscle force patterns leading to pain in the APP, MBP, and LBP regions are required to obtain more robust findings. On the other hand, previous investigations have predominantly employed traditional statistical methods, such as analysis of variance and logistic regression [12], to elucidate the muscle force factors contributing to PFP recurrence. Traditional statistical methods are typically predicated on a set of assumptions—such as linear relationships among variables and independence of observations—while also remaining inherently limited in their capacity to detect and model interaction effects [13]. Consequently, these approaches are ill-suited for analyzing complex, high-dimensional datasets. However, biomechanical data—particularly muscle force profiles—are inherently complex and high-dimensional, which makes traditional statistical methods considerably limited in this context [13]. In addition, conventional techniques often lack precision in identifying the most critical risk factors, which may result in inaccuracies and biased interpretations, and in turn give rise to contradictory findings across studies [13]. In contrast, artificial intelligence (AI) technologies—particularly machine learning algorithms—can capture nonlinear patterns and high-dimensional interactions without relying on these restrictive assumptions, thereby offering broader applicability [13]. When applied to complex clinical datasets—for instance, in analyzing muscle force characteristics associated with PFP recurrence—machine learning may yield higher predictive accuracy and offer more comprehensive insights.

Although machine learning models offer notable advantages in predictive accuracy, their inherent “black-box” nature limits interpretability, which may, to some extent, undermine their value in clinical practice [14]. To address this limitation, recent studies have integrated the shapley additive explanations (SHAP) framework with machine learning models to enhance transparency and credibility of predictive outcomes [15]. SHAP, originally derived from game theory, provides a systematic means of quantifying feature contributions: a SHAP value of 0 indicates no additional effect of a variable on the prediction; a positive SHAP value signifies a feature’s positive contribution, raising the prediction above the mean; whereas a negative SHAP value reflects a negative contribution, lowering the prediction below the mean [15]. By ranking features according to their SHAP values, we can rigorously delineate the key muscle force determinants underlying the region-specific recurrence of PFP, thereby providing more precise guidance for its prevention in each anatomical region. Nevertheless, research in this area remains limited, and patients with PFP continue to face a substantial risk of recurrence.

Therefore, this study employs AI techniques—specifically, machine learning algorithms integrated with the SHAP framework—to analyze the muscle force patterns associated with pain in the APP, MBP, and LBP regions. The findings are expected to inform the development of targeted prevention strategies, particularly in designing strength training protocols, thereby enhancing rehabilitation outcomes and quality of life for PFP patients, and opening new avenues for research on the prevention of related conditions. Building on the study objectives and prior findings, we hypothesize that the recurrence of PFP across different anatomical regions is driven by heterogeneous strategies of muscle force generation.

Methods

Experimental design

This study is a 6-month prospective cohort investigation aimed at elucidating the muscle force patterns associated with PFP in different regions. Participants with a history of PFP were recruited via campus posters. Following an initial screening, eligible subjects underwent a baseline assessment during which lower-limb and trunk muscle forces were computed using an OpenSim model (OpenSim Project, Stanford University, Stanford, California, United States). Over the subsequent 6 months, participants were allocated into groups based on the presence of PFP during walking and the pain location: pain-free, APP pain, MBP pain, and LBP pain groups. To mitigate the potential influence of changes in walking posture on the study outcomes, subjects underwent three follow-up biomechanical assessments between the baseline test and PFP recurrence. Finally, group assignments served as the dependent variable and muscle force indices as feature variables in a four-class machine learning analysis to elucidate the biomechanical mechanisms underlying pain in the APP, MBP, and LBP regions.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 16 and 25 years; (2) a history of pain in the anterior, posterior, or peripatellar region during at least two of the following activities—running, squatting, ascending/descending stairs, prolonged sitting, jumping, or resisted knee extension; (3) pain unrelated to direct trauma and persisting for at least three months; (4) a pain intensity of at least 3 on a 10-cm visual analog scale (VAS), where 0 indicates no pain and 10 the worst pain imaginable; and (5) absence of any pain symptoms at the baseline assessment [16, 17]. Exclusion criteria were: (1) a history of knee surgery; (2) the presence of back, hip, or ankle injuries or pain at the time of study entry; (3) patellar instability; (4) symptoms indicative of meniscal or ligamentous knee injuries; and (5) neurological disorders affecting movement.

During the 6-month study period, investigators evaluated PFP recurrence during ambulation every three days using the VAS. When a patient’s VAS score exceeded 3, an immediate in‐person evaluation was performed. PFP recurrence was defined as the re‐emergence of pain in the anterior, posterior, or peripatellar regions during ambulation—with a VAS score >3—and after exclusion of other traumatic etiologies for PFP [18]. Based on previous studies delineating PFP regional segmentation [19], participants were subsequently classified into the APP, MBP, or LBP pain groups (Fig. 1a). All participants provided written informed consent approved by the ethics committee.

Data acquisition and testing protocol

Baseline and follow-up biomechanical testing were conducted as follows: Participants first completed a 3-minute static stretching protocol (with specific methods, target muscle groups, durations, and repetitions detailed in Supplementary File 1), after which they changed into standardized testing apparel, including footwear, a fitted top, and shorts. Next, in accordance with the Plug-in-Gait full-body protocol, 39 reflective markers (14 mm in diameter) were affixed to key bony landmarks—including C7, T10, bilateral anterior and posterior superior iliac spines, the thigh, lateral knee, shank, ankle, toe, and heel. Following marker placement, static calibration data were collected. During the formal test, participants walked along a 2 × 10 m walkway at a self-selected pace that replicated their habitual gait (Fig. 1c). Walking speed during testing was measured with the Vicon motion-capture system (Vicon Motion Systems Ltd., Yarnton, Oxfordshire, United Kingdom). The mean center-of-mass velocity in the anterior–posterior (X) direction, recorded as participants stepped onto the force plate, was used to quantify walking speed, and trial-to-trial variations were restricted to within ± 3%. Kinematic data were captured using a 12-camera Vicon v2.2 system (Vicon Motion Systems Ltd., Yarnton, Oxfordshire, United Kingdom) at a sampling rate of 200 Hz, while kinetic data were concurrently recorded from two embedded AMTI BMS400600 force plates (Advanced Mechanical Technology, Inc., Watertown, Massachusetts, United States) at 1000 Hz, with a focus on the affected leg’s parameters. Additionally, a 32-channel surface electromyography (EMG) system (Noraxon Ultium; Noraxon USA Inc., Scottsdale, Arizona, United States) with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz was employed to record EMG signals from the following muscles during walking: rectus femoris, biceps femoris, semimembranosus, semitendinosus, tibialis anterior, and lateral gastrocnemius. Electrodes were placed over the belly of each target muscle and carefully aligned with the direction of the underlying fibers to optimize signal acquisition. Placement was performed by a licensed physical therapist with extensive anatomical and clinical expertise, ensuring accurate localization of each target muscle based on anatomical landmarks. In addition, we strictly adhered to the SENIAM guidelines, maintained standardized inter-electrode distances, and conducted pilot testing with isolated muscle contractions to verify signal quality and minimize potential crosstalk [20]. Five valid trials were obtained per session, with a 1-minute rest period between tests.

Data processing

This study employed Vicon Nexus software (Vicon Motion Systems Ltd., Yarnton, Oxfordshire, United Kingdom) for post-processing the collected kinematic and kinetic data, with a particular focus on biomechanical parameters during the stance phase of walking. First, foot contact was defined as the moment when the ground reaction force (GRF) exceeded 20 N, and toe-off was determined when GRF fell below 20 N, thereby delineating the stance phase. Next, both the marker trajectory data (sampled at 200 Hz) and the GRF data (sampled at 1000 Hz) were processed using a fourth-order, zero-lag Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 20 Hz [21]. Subsequently, transformation matrices were established, joint centers computed, and local coordinate systems constructed to ultimately calculate the three-dimensional angles of the trunk, hip, knee, and ankle. Joint moments were then derived using inverse dynamics. Concurrently, EMG data were processed using Noraxon (Noraxon USA Inc., Scottsdale, Arizona, United States) analysis software. The signals were first band-pass filtered at 20–450 Hz [22], then full-wave rectified and smoothed with an RMS filter using a 250-ms moving window [22]. Standardized muscle activation levels were computed as the ratio of muscle activity during the stance phase to the peak activity recorded across five tests (with 0 indicating no activation and 1 full activation) [22]. A muscle was considered to be fully activated when its EMG signal exceeded the resting baseline mean plus three standard deviations (mean + 3 SD) [23]. All processed data were subsequently utilized to validate the OpenSim model.

OpenSim modeling

The acquired kinematic data were converted to TRC format and the kinetic data to MOT format before being imported into the OpenSim (OpenSim Project, Stanford University, Stanford, California, United States) model for musculoskeletal analysis (Fig. 1d). The modeling process comprised model scaling, inverse kinematics, residual reduction analysis (RRA), and computed muscle control (CMC) [24]. Initially, model scaling involved generating a generic model based on the subject’s height, weight, and muscle characteristics, followed by scaling the segment lengths and masses using the experimental marker data [24]. Inverse kinematics was then performed using a weighted least-squares approach to minimize the three-dimensional coordinate errors between the experimental and model markers [24]. The RRA was applied to reduce discrepancies between the force plate measurements and the forces computed via Newton’s second law, ensuring that the residual force and moment errors remained within acceptable limits [24]. Subsequently, the CMC algorithm utilized the model’s generalized coordinates and the experimental data to correct joint acceleration errors through feedback and optimized muscle activation levels via an optimization algorithm, thereby yielding refined muscle force parameters [24]. Finally, the accuracy of the OpenSim model was validated by comparing the simulated kinematics, kinetics, and muscle activation data with their corresponding experimental measurements. The model was deemed valid and reliable if the sagittal-plane angles of the hip, knee, and ankle differed by less than 5°, the residual force and moment at the pelvis were below 20 N and 75 Nm, respectively, and the muscle activation curves exhibited good concordance [25].

Machine learning

Data selection and pre-processing

Initially, missing values were handled according to a 30% threshold: variables with less than 30% missing data were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) method, whereas those exceeding 30% were excluded from the analysis [26]. The rationale for selecting a 30% threshold is that previous studies have shown that high levels of missingness reduce the accuracy of imputation and increase data homogeneity [27]. Accordingly, we adopted 30% as a conservative and widely accepted cutoff for variable exclusion [27]. In our dataset, however, the variable with the highest missingness (Internal oblique) had only 1.05% missing data. Therefore, no variables were excluded due to missingness in this study. The dataset was then partitioned into training and test sets in an 8:2 ratio via stratified random sampling. Subsequently, a multicollinearity assessment was performed on 28 candidate features, with severe collinearity defined as a variance inflation factor (VIF) >10 or a correlation coefficient (R) >0.8 [28]. Based on this analysis, 12 variables were excluded, leaving 16 features for subsequent modeling. Additionally, to mitigate sample imbalance among groups, the SMOTE algorithm was applied to the training set to generate minority class samples and balance the data distribution [29].

Machine learning model construction

In this study, we opted for machine learning rather than neural networks or deep learning models. This decision was motivated by two considerations. First, prior research has shown that deep learning approaches typically realize their advantages only when trained on substantially large datasets, whereas our sample size was more suitable for conventional machine learning methods [30]. Second, machine learning algorithms can be readily integrated with well-established interpretability frameworks, such as SHAP, enabling us to elucidate the specific contributions of muscle force patterns to the recurrence risk of PFP across different anatomical regions.

Accordingly, three machine learning models—extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), random forest (RF), and support vector machine (SVM) with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel—were employed to predict and analyze the muscle force patterns contributing to PFP across different regions. During model construction, lower-limb muscle forces derived from OpenSim were used as feature variables, while the recurrence of PFP across different regions was designated as the target variable. XGBoost and RF, both ensemble methods based on decision trees, are particularly well-suited for handling high-dimensional data [31], whereas SVM leverages kernel functions to effectively process non-linear relationships [32]. All models were implemented using the scikit-learn package in Python 3.8 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, Delaware, United States). To assess predictive performance and reduce the risk of both underfitting and overfitting, five-fold cross-validation was employed [33]. Furthermore, hyperparameters were fine-tuned using a grid search algorithm, with the optimal parameter set defined as that which maximized the average cross-validation score, thereby refining the final model configurations. Importantly, all hyperparameter search and evaluation were conducted exclusively within the training set, while the test set was reserved strictly for final validation.

Model validation and evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, recall, precision, F1 score, as well as the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC). These metrics were calculated using the following formulas:

Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN).

Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN).

Recall = TP / (TP + FN).

Recall = TP / (TP + FN).

Precision = TP / (TP + FP).

Precision = TP / (TP + FP).

F1 score = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall).

F1 score = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall).

Here, TP, FP, TN, and FN denote true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives, respectively. The above data were obtained by inputting the test set into the trained model to generate predictions, which were then compared against the true outcomes. A true positive (TP) was defined as a case in which the true outcome indicated recurrence and the model also predicted recurrence. A false positive (FP) referred to cases in which the true outcome indicated no recurrence, but the model incorrectly predicted recurrence. A true negative (TN) was defined as a case in which the true outcome indicated no recurrence and the model correctly predicted no recurrence. A false negative (FN) referred to cases in which the true outcome indicated recurrence, but the model incorrectly predicted no recurrence. The optimal model was selected based on these performance indices and subsequently employed to analyze the muscle force factors contributing to recurrence across different PFP regions.

Visualization of model results

Additionally, SHAP values were computed for each feature in the four-class machine learning models. The SHAP framework facilitates both local and global interpretability by quantifying the hierarchical importance of potential influencing factors, thereby elucidating the key muscle force factors contributing to PFP across different regions [34].

Results

Participant characteristics

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 299 patients were initially enrolled. During the follow-up period, 4 participants sustained ankle sprains, 2 incurred knee ligament injuries, and 5 were unable to complete the study for personal reasons, resulting in a final cohort of 288 subjects (Fig. 1b). As detailed in Table 1, the 288 patients with PFP had a mean age of 23.45 years, an average height of 1.77 m, an average weight of 70.96 kg, a mean walking speed of 1.38 m/s, and an average of 30.13 trials. Among these, 123 exhibited pain in the APP region, 51 in the MBP region, 44 in the LBP region, while 70 reported no significant pain symptoms. OpenSim model validation

Based on previous studies, six representative lower limb muscles—namely, the rectus femoris, semimembranosus, semitendinosus, biceps femoris, tibialis anterior, and lateral gastrocnemius [35]—were selected to validate the OpenSim model outputs. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the muscle parameters obtained during the stance phase through OpenSim modeling were generally consistent with the experimentally recorded EMG signals. Moreover, the joint angles and moments computed via OpenSim’s inverse kinematics and inverse dynamics algorithms closely matched the data acquired from the motion capture system and force plates (see Supplementary File 1). Taken together, these findings demonstrate the high reliability of the OpenSim model employed in this study.

Collinearity diagnostics of feature variables

As shown in Fig. 3a and b, among the 28 candidate features, the VIF values for the external oblique, gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius, adductor magnus, fibularis longus, fibularis brevis, posterior tibialis, flexor digitorum longus, extensor digitorum brevis, and abductor hallucis exceeded 10, with values of 34.7, 46.1, 228.2, 12.7, 54.2, 1600.4, 57.3, 92.9, 69.5, 15.9, 41.3, and 18.6, respectively, and correlation coefficients greater than 0.8, indicating severe collinearity. Consequently, these features were excluded. The remaining 16 features exhibited VIF values ranging from 1.57 to 6.34 and correlation coefficients below 0.8, confirming that no collinearity existed among the final set of variables.

Model validation

Figure 3d shows the ROC curves for the XGBoost, RF, and SVM models. Initially, model performance was evaluated by calculating the area under the ROC curve (AUC). For predicting APP pain, the AUC values were 0.997 (95% CI 0.993–0.999) for XGBoost, 0.983 (95% CI 0.973–0.991) for RF, and 0.923 (95% CI 0.897–0.947) for SVM; for MBP pain, they were 0.997 (95% CI 0.994–0.999), 0.982 (95% CI 0.969–0.991), and 0.963 (95% CI 0.936–0.984), respectively; and for LBP pain, the corresponding values were 0.997 (95% CI 0.994–0.999), 0.980 (95% CI 0.967–0.991), and 0.952 (95% CI 0.922–0.978). These results indicate that, based solely on AUC, the XGBoost model exhibits superior predictive performance.

In addition, accuracy, recall, precision, and F1 scores were computed to further assess model performance (Fig. 3c). For APP pain prediction, XGBoost achieved accuracy, recall, precision, and F1 scores of 0.959 (95% CI 0.941–0.976), 0.970 (95% CI 0.945–0.991), 0.938 (95% CI 0.904–0.968), and 0.954 (95% CI 0.932–0.973), respectively; RF obtained 0.696 (95% CI 0.655–0.735), 0.950 (95% CI 0.918–0.979), 0.893 (95% CI 0.849–0.930), and 0.920 (95% CI 0.892–0.945); and SVM achieved 0.857 (95% CI 0.824–0.887), 0.886 (95% CI 0.841–0.929), 0.805 (95% CI 0.750–0.854), and 0.844 (95% CI 0.802–0.878). For MBP pain, the corresponding metrics for XGBoost were 0.974 (95% CI 0.959–0.987), 0.906 (95% CI 0.842–0.963), 0.951 (95% CI 0.901–0.989), and 0.928 (95% CI 0.884–0.966); for RF, 0.924 (95% CI 0.898–0.948), 0.765 (95% CI 0.671–0.854), 0.929 (95% CI 0.860–0.985), and 0.839 (95% CI 0.771–0.899); and for SVM, 0.941 (95% CI 0.920–0.961), 0.753 (95% CI 0.659–0.845), 0.914 (95% CI 0.844–0.973), and 0.826 (95% CI 0.754–0.888). For LBP pain, XGBoost yielded values of 0.983 (95% CI 0.970–0.994), 0.931 (95% CI 0.866–0.985), 0.957 (95% CI 0.905–0.999), and 0.944 (95% CI 0.900–0.979); RF, 0.957 (95% CI 0.937–0.974), 0.819 (95% CI 0.728–0.905), 0.894 (95% CI 0.811–0.966), and 0.855 (95% CI 0.788–0.914); and SVM, 0.935 (95% CI 0.911–0.957), 0.667 (95% CI 0.557–0.771), 0.889 (95% CI 0.803–0.966), and 0.762 (95% CI 0.674–0.837). Taken together, these findings confirm that XGBoost outperforms both RF and SVM in predicting pain across the APP, MBP, and LBP regions. Consequently, SHAP values were calculated based on the XGBoost model.

Visualization of feature importance

To intuitively elucidate the contribution of individual features to the model outcomes and their relative importance, SHAP values were computed for each variable (Table 2). The results reveal that reduced strength in the gracilis, tibialis anterior, and internal oblique muscles, along with excessive strength in the adductor longus and tensor fascia latae, are the primary factors precipitating pain in the APP region (Fig. 4a, b). On this basis, SHAP dependency plots were constructed to explicate in detail how each feature influences the model predictions (Fig. 4c–g). Specifically, during ambulation, a gracilis strength below 0.055 N/kg, an adductor longus strength exceeding 0.110 N/kg, a tibialis anterior strength below 0.678 N/kg, a tensor fascia latae strength above 0.144 N/kg, and an internal oblique strength below 0.699 N/kg are associated with a significantly elevated risk of APP pain. Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 4h, SHAP force plots were employed to further analyze personalized risk factors. For instance, subject 189 experienced APP pain during the follow-up period primarily because he consistently exhibited excessive adductor longus strength and insufficient gracilis strength during gait, which were determined to be the causal factors underlying his condition.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated an artificial intelligence (machine learning) model to elucidate the muscle force-production characteristics associated with the recurrence of pain in the APP, MBP, and LBP regions. The findings support our hypothesis, indicating that the muscle force–related factors underlying PFP recurrence differ across anatomical regions. Among the three machine learning classifiers employed, the XGBoost model exhibited the highest accuracy, with predictive performance ranging from 95.9% to 98.3% in forecasting pain across these regions. Consequently, SHAP values were computed based on the XGBoost model to visually represent the importance of each feature, thereby delineating the key muscle force patterns that precipitate PFP in distinct regions.

In addressing overfitting, it is noteworthy that XGBoost incorporates embedded regularization during training, specifically the L1 (lambda) and L2 (alpha) penalties, which effectively constrain model complexity and reduce the likelihood of overfitting to the training set [36]. In addition, we employed five-fold cross-validation within the training data to optimize hyperparameters, thereby avoiding the inappropriate use of the test set for model tuning and further mitigating the risk of overfitting. Nevertheless, given that the AUC achieved by the XGBoost models in this study was relatively high (>0.99), it remains possible that some degree of uncontrollable overfitting occurred. This observation highlights the need for future research to expand both sample size and feature dimensionality, as well as to improve group balance during testing, in order to further safeguard against overfitting.

Muscle force production patterns associated with APP region pain

Insufficient strength in the gracilis, tibialis anterior, and internal oblique muscles, combined with excessive strength in the adductor longus and tensor fascia latae, constitutes the principal precipitating factors for pain in the APP region. Although both the gracilis and adductor longus are located on the medial thigh, their distinct insertion sites result in different contributions to joint kinematics [37, 38]. Specifically, the gracilis inserts on the medial aspect of the proximal tibia, facilitating hip internal rotation, whereas the adductor longus terminates at the linea aspera of the femur and functions to externally rotate the hip [37, 38]. Consequently, insufficient strength of the gracilis, combined with excessive strength of the adductor longus, may result in exaggerated hip external rotation during gait. Cadaveric studies have demonstrated that a hip external rotation of 30° can significantly increase patellofemoral joint contact forces [39]. During ambulation, the contact force generated by hip external rotation is continuously loaded onto the anterior aspect of the patella, peaking at knee flexion angles between 30° and 60°. Over time, this repetitive loading may precipitate the recurrence of pain in the APP region [39]. It is noteworthy that recent studies suggest potential differences in neural innervation and functional roles between the proximal and distal regions of the same muscle [40]. For the gracilis, the proximal fibers, originating from the medial aspect of the ischiopubic ramus, may primarily contribute to hip joint motion. In contrast, the distal fibers extend across the knee joint, where they merge with the tendons of the sartorius and semitendinosus to form the pes anserinus, inserting onto the superior medial tibia [41]. This anatomical arrangement suggests that the distal fibers may be more directly involved in knee movement and tibial internal rotation. Considering that current evidence generally supports the association between gracilis insufficiency and the recurrence of PFP, primarily through alterations in hip joint mechanics as previously discussed [37–39], clinical practice should place particular emphasis on targeted activation and rehabilitation of the proximal gracilis. Such interventions may help prevent recurrent pain in the APP region.

Our findings indicate that insufficient tibialis anterior strength is a primary precipitant of pain in the APP region. Notably, previous cohort studies have not clearly delineated the relationship between tibialis anterior strength and PFP. The present study’s ability to capture this association may be attributable to the employment of a machine learning model, which can discern more subtle inter-variable relationships that may be pivotal for PFP prevention. Inadequate tibialis anterior strength disrupts the balance between plantarflexor and dorsiflexor forces at the ankle, thereby impairing sagittal plane stability [42]. As a consequence, the ankle’s capacity for sagittal attenuation is markedly diminished, allowing unbuffered ground reaction forces to be transmitted proximally to the knee and patellofemoral joints, which leads to excessive localized loading on the patellar surface and ultimately precipitates APP pain [43]. Furthermore, the machine learning model revealed that diminished internal oblique strength is associated with APP pain, corroborating findings from previous cross-sectional studies [44]. Insufficient internal oblique strength increases trunk inclination and rotation during ambulation. In particular, when the internal oblique of the contralateral side is weak, the trunk tends to rotate and tilt toward the affected side. Given that the trunk accounts for over 50% of body weight, such a shift in mass distribution results in an increased overall load on the affected limb, thereby elevating patellofemoral joint stress [44]. This heightened joint stress exacerbates anterior patellar wear during knee flexion-extension cycles, ultimately culminating in APP pain [45].

Excessive strength in the tensor fascia latae is also a major predictor of APP region pain. Previous studies have further reported that such overexertion of the tensor fasciae latae can induce hip flexion [46]. However, during gait, excessive hip flexion results in forward tilting of the trunk, thereby shifting the center of mass anteriorly and increasing the load on the anterior aspect of the knee, ultimately precipitating recurrent APP pain [47]. Moreover, excessive force of the tensor fasciae latae may disrupt the strength balance between the hip flexors and extensors [48], thereby undermining sagittal-plane core stability and consequently amplifying anterior–posterior displacement of the center of mass [49]. In response, the lower limb joints often adopt a “stiff” movement pattern to stabilize the center of mass and prevent falls. Nevertheless, in such a “stiff” gait pattern, energy absorption by tissues distal to the knee may be reduced, thereby increasing the mechanical load on the knee joint. This load may be repeatedly transmitted to the anterior patellar region, ultimately resulting in patellar cartilage damage and the recurrence of APP [47].

Muscle force production patterns associated with MBP region pain

The machine learning model identified that excessive strength in the rectus femoris, gracilis, gluteus maximus, and adductor longus muscles, coupled with insufficient strength in the semitendinosus, constitutes the primary mechanism underlying MBP region pain. Notably, the rectus femoris serves as a knee extensor, whereas the semitendinosus—being part of the hamstring group—functions as a knee flexor. Excessive rectus femoris force, combined with insufficient semitendinosus strength, may lead to an increased knee extension moment during gait. Prior investigations have demonstrated that when the knee extension moment reaches 240 Nm, the patellofemoral joint stress escalates by 3.9 MPa [50]. Furthermore, inadequate semitendinosus strength may compromise the dynamic control of the knee’s frontal plane, resulting in augmented lateral displacement of the patella, exacerbation of medial patellar wear, and ultimately the manifestation of MBP region pain in patients with PFP [51]. Recent studies have reported that the proximal and distal compartments of the semitendinosus exhibit distinct discharge rates and differences in the variability of neural drive [40]. It is speculated that the region of the semitendinosus located closer to the knee joint may be more directly involved in knee and patellofemoral joint loading and movement, and thus more strongly associated with the recurrence of MBP. Consequently, targeted stimulation and rehabilitation of this knee-adjacent region of the semitendinosus during clinical training may yield greater therapeutic benefits and improve the effectiveness of preventing MBP recurrence. However, this assumption remains speculative and requires confirmation through future cohort studies.

During ambulation, excessive force generated by the gracilis and adductor longus muscles is a key precipitant of MBP region pain. In addition to mediating hip internal and external rotation, both muscles are part of the hip adductor group; their combined force production may lead to pronounced hip adduction [52]. Studies by McKenzie et al. [53] and Willson et al. [54] have shown that patients with PFP exhibit significantly greater hip adduction during functional activities—such as running, jump landing, and squatting—compared with healthy controls, thereby further validating these findings. Ultrasonographic data indicate that when hip adduction reaches 20°, lateral displacement of the patella increases by approximately 7.3 mm [55]. Repeated lateral displacement may exacerbate friction at the medial femoral condyle and medial patellar region, ultimately precipitating pain in the MBP region [55].

Results from the machine learning model indicate that excessive gluteus maximus strength is one of the contributing factors to pain in the MBP region. Previous studies have demonstrated that excessive force production by the gluteus maximus during gait leads to heightened frontal-plane stiffness at the hip joint [56]. Given that the hip and knee joints serve as the primary sites for frontal-plane energy absorption during ground contact—accounting for 59.1% and 38.5% of energy absorption, respectively [57]—excessive hip stiffness markedly reduces its capacity to absorb energy. Consequently, unbuffered ground reaction forces are transferred to the knee joint [57], increasing the frontal-plane load and elevating medial patellar pressure [57]. Moreover, excessive force production by the gluteus maximus during gait may promote an overly upright trunk posture, thereby elevating the center of mass and increasing lateral trunk sway. The lateral forces generated by this trunk movement are repeatedly imposed on the knee joint, which is already burdened by high energy absorption demands [58], ultimately leading to repeated compression of the medial patellar margin and significant pain over time.

Muscle force production patterns associated with LBP region pain

Our explainable machine learning model revealed that insufficient strength in the rectus femoris, tensor fascia latae, and gluteus maximus, coupled with excessive force generated by the adductor longus and gracilis, constitutes the primary mechanism underlying pain in the LBP region. Among these, the rectus femoris—an integral component of the quadriceps—primarily generates the knee extension moment during gait [59]. Powers et al. [60] reported that PFP patients exhibit a knee extension moment of 23.6 N·cm/kg, compared to 30.4 N·cm/kg in healthy individuals; the inability of the rectus femoris to produce an adequate extension moment may therefore serve as a precipitating factor for PFP. Moreover, the quadriceps play a critical role in controlling patellar tracking during knee flexion-extension. Weakness in this muscle group can result in aberrant patellar kinematics, including increased frontal plane displacement of the patella and consequent excessive compression of its lateral edge, ultimately leading to pain [59]. It should be noted that previous studies have reported regional heterogeneity in the activation of the rectus femoris under different contraction tasks. Specifically, hip flexion predominantly activates the proximal region of the muscle, whereas knee extension primarily recruits its central and distal regions [61]. This task-dependent distribution is thought to arise from the relatively segregated organization of motor neuron pools within the spinal cord, where neurons innervating the proximal region are mainly located in the rostral part of the motor nucleus, while those serving the distal region are positioned more caudally [61]. Such topographical differentiation provides a mechanistic explanation for the distinct activation patterns of the proximal versus distal regions of the rectus femoris [61]. In the context of the present study, the distal region may be a critical contributor to LBP-related pain, as it is more directly associated with knee and patellofemoral joint function, suggesting that this region should receive particular attention during clinical interventions [61]. However, we did not directly examine the relationship between regional rectus femoris activation and LBP pain in this study. Accordingly, this interpretation remains speculative and requires systematic investigation in future research.

Based on our findings, insufficient gluteus maximus strength predisposes individuals to LBP region pain. The gluteus maximus not only facilitates hip extension but also functions as a hip external rotator; when its strength is diminished, the hip and femur tend to exhibit excessive internal rotation. Powers et al. [62] demonstrated via magnetic resonance imaging that an 8° increase in femoral internal rotation can induce a 13° patellar external rotation, during which the lateral edge of the patella is repeatedly subjected to friction and compressive forces against the lateral femoral condyle. Moreover, owing to the inherent anatomy of the patellofemoral joint, the lateral force on the patella during gait (0.54 BW) far exceeds that on the medial side (0.15 BW) [63]. Collectively, this loading environment markedly increases the risk of LBP region pain. Furthermore, our machine learning results indicate that insufficient tensor fascia latae strength, combined with excessive force generated by the adductor longus and gracilis, further predisposes to LBP region pain. The tensor fascia latae, a principal component of the hip abductor group [64], contrasts with the adductor longus and gracilis, which are key elements of the hip adductor group. Reduced strength of the tensor fascia latae, combined with excessive force production by the adductor longus and gracilis, leads to pronounced hip adduction during ambulation [52, 65]. Ultrasonographic evidence indicates that, compared with a neutral position, 20° of hip adduction results in a 0.24-cm increase in lateral patellar displacement. Repeated lateral displacement of the patella may exacerbate wear and local compressive loading on its lateral margin, ultimately precipitating LBP region pain [66].

Implications for preventive training

Targeted neuromuscular training may alleviate regional PFP by correcting specific deficits in force-generation patterns. For the prevention of APP region pain, it is imperative to enhance the strength of the hip internal rotator group to counteract the excessive force generated by the adductor longus, which predisposes the hip to external rotation. In addition, incorporating resistance exercises for the tibialis anterior, alongside relaxation protocols for the tensor fascia latae to prevent its overcontraction during the stance phase, is recommended. Furthermore, targeted training of the contralateral internal oblique may help correct trunk lean during gait, thereby reducing unilateral knee loading.

In the context of MBP region pain, emphasis should be placed on augmenting hamstring strength to restore a balanced knee flexion–extension moment and improve knee joint stability. Concurrently, targeted strengthening of the hip abductor group is recommended to counteract the aberrant force production by the gracilis and adductor longus during gait. Furthermore, relaxation training for the gluteus maximus may increase the hip joint’s frontal-plane mobility and its capacity for energy absorption, thereby reducing the overall load on the knee joint.

For LBP region pain, priority should be given to reinforcing the rectus femoris and gluteus maximus. Concurrently, targeted strengthening of the hip abductor group (e.g., tensor fascia latae) should be implemented, while relaxation training for the hip adductor group (e.g., adductor longus and gracilis) is recommended to correct imbalances between hip adduction and abduction. This integrated approach is expected to enhance proximal joint stability and ultimately reduce lateral displacement and loading of the patellofemoral joint.

It should be emphasized that although the SHAP dependence plots identified apparent “thresholds” for these risk factors, these values do not represent clinically meaningful cut-off points. Rather, they indicate the points at which the relationship between a given feature and the risk of PFP recurrence begins to change within the model. Accordingly, these thresholds cannot be directly applied in clinical practice and should instead be viewed as preliminary reference values that require further verification and refinement through systematic clinical studies.

Looking ahead, our model could be integrated into clinical decision-support tools, such as OpenSim-based musculoskeletal modeling platforms or gait analysis systems, to enable direct identification of region-specific risks of PFP recurrence and the muscle strength deficits most in need of improvement. Such integration would not only facilitate the development of individualized intervention strategies but also provide more robust evidence to inform clinical decision making. Furthermore, review studies have indicated that different regions within the same muscle exhibit distinct levels of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) phosphorylation during eccentric and concentric training [67]. For instance, following eccentric training of the vastus lateralis, pY397-FAK levels in the distal region were approximately four times higher than those observed after concentric training, contributing to distal hypertrophy of the quadriceps [67]. Given that different training modalities induce region-specific muscle hypertrophy, future strength interventions for patients with PFP should first identify the specific muscle regions implicated in the condition and then select training strategies that effectively target these regions. This study provides only a preliminary intervention framework by treating the muscle as a single functional unit. Future research should build on these findings to investigate more refined, region-specific approaches.

Limitations

Although this study provides preliminary evidence of distinct muscle force strategies associated with PFP across different anatomical regions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although SHAP was used to preliminarily identify the points at which the relationship between muscle strength features and the risk of PFP recurrence begins to shift within the machine-learning model, their generalizability may be limited by individual variability and environmental context. To translate these thresholds into clinical practice, further clinical studies are needed for validation and refinement. Furthermore, although a six-month follow-up in a cohort of 299 participants yielded important initial findings, a longer observation window and a larger, more heterogeneous sample would likely offer a more comprehensive understanding of the temporal dynamics and recurrence patterns of PFP. Finally, recent studies have suggested that neural innervation and activation levels may vary across different regions (proximal versus distal) of the same muscle [40]. In the present study, however, the OpenSim modeling approach treated each muscle as a single unit, which made it difficult to examine how region-specific muscle activation might be related to the recurrence of PFP. This limitation underscores the need for future cohort studies to further investigate these region-dependent mechanisms underlying PFP recurrence. Finally, recent studies have demonstrated that neural innervation and activation levels can differ across distinct regions of the same muscle, such as proximal versus distal segments [40, 61]. This phenomenon is likely attributable to the differential spatial organization of motor neurons within the spinal cord that control the proximal and distal portions of the muscle [61]. However, in the present study, the OpenSim modeling approach treated each muscle as a single unit, limiting our ability to investigate the relationship between region-specific muscle activation and PFP recurrence. This limitation underscores the need for future cohort studies to examine the region-dependent mechanisms underlying PFP recurrence. In addition, evidence from training studies indicates that different muscle regions exhibit distinct molecular responses during concentric and eccentric exercises, which can lead to region-specific hypertrophy [67]. Because the present study considered muscles as whole units, it provides limited guidance for interventions targeting specific muscle regions, representing a key limitation of this work.

Conclusion

Harnessing artificial intelligence through the integration of machine learning and the SHAP framework, we identified distinct muscle force-production patterns associated with region-specific recurrence of PFP. APP pain was primarily linked to reduced strength of the gracilis and tibialis anterior, alongside excessive force production by the adductor longus. MBP pain was characterized by increased force production of the rectus femoris, gracilis, and gluteus maximus. In contrast, LBP pain was associated with reduced strength in the rectus femoris and tensor fasciae latae, coupled with excessive force production by the adductor longus. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for the development of region-tailored strength interventions, offering a promising direction for AI-informed strategies to mitigate PFP recurrence.
