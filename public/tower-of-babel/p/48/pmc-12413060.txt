ChatGPT’s role in the rapidly evolving hematologic cancer landscape
Authors: Tiffany Nong, Sean Britton, Viralkumar Bhanderi, Justin Taylor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims: Many patients seek accurate, understandable information about their disease and treatment, turning to the internet or messaging providers. This study aims to validate chatbots’ ability to deliver accurate information, contributing to the literature on AI’s role in cancer care and helping to improve these tools for patients and caregivers. Methods: A set of questions about hematologic malignancies was created with input from oncologists and reputable websites and then submitted to ChatGPT 3.5. Each response was rated by hematology-oncology physicians from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5) regarding its accuracy and usefulness for patients, with multiple reviewers ensuring consistency. Results: The general queries category received a higher average score of 3.38 compared to 3.06 in the novel therapies category, indicating relatively better satisfaction. However, no question achieved scores greater than 4 (agree) or 5 (strongly agree), with most scores ranging from 3.0 to 3.8, reflecting a neutral stance, suggesting room for improvement. Conclusions: ChatGPT struggled with providing current and specific information for patient-specific queries and novel therapies, especially in rapidly advancing fields like acute myeloid leukemia. These deficiencies are likely due to AI’s reliance on large data sets, leading to less influence from novel therapies.Keywords: AI, ChatGPT, hematology, AI assisted, cancer, healthcare technology, AI chatbots, artificial intelligence

Introduction

Many cancer patients and their caregivers attempt to find accurate, understandable information about their disease, treatment options, and supportive care, which may lead them to search the internet or message providers [1,2]. Post-pandemic, there has been a 1.6-fold increase in the number of patient messages through the electronic medical systems [3]. AI chatbots have the potential to provide personalized, on-demand cancer information to patients, empowering them to be more actively involved in their care and decision-making. According to the National Cancer Institute Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS), patients are increasing their health literacy by turning to online resources [4]. However, the role of AI-powered chatbots in addressing this need has not been completely established to provide reliable cancer information [5]. Studies have highlighted both the potential and limitations of using ChatGPT and other large language models (LLMs) as medical chatbots, representing a disruptive technology that offers convenient healthcare information access, however, with potentially misleading information [6–8].

A recent systematic review that covered 31 studies showed a generally positive user sentiment. 27/30 of those studies showed users positively rating conversational agents’ usability, and 26/31 of those studies showed positive user satisfaction [9]. Recent work has shown that 585 evaluations by evaluators in an online forum preferred chatbot responses to physician responses in 78.6% (95% CI, 75.0%–81.8%) of the cases. The chatbot responses were longer than physician responses (average: 211 words vs. 52 words), 3.6 times greater prevalence of good or very good quality responses, and had 9.8 times greater prevalence of empathetic or very empathetic responses [10] This makes chatbots like ChatGPT, an attractive option for patients, but may pose an issue for their physicians if the information being presented is not accurate.

This study aims to build on the limited existing literature that validates the ability of current chatbots to consistently provide accurate cancer information that can be readily available for patient use. By addressing these critical aspects, this research project has the potential to contribute to the growing body of literature on the use of AI technologies in cancer care, informing the development and implementation of these tools to better serve the needs of cancer patients and their caregivers.

Methods

Study design

This study obtained an IRB-exempt determination as it did not qualify for Human Subjects Research requiring IRB approval. A set of 10 questions (Table 1) regarding topics in hematologic malignancies was formulated in conjunction with a hematology oncologist and websites such as the National Cancer Institute and the American Cancer Society.

The selection of 10 questions was based on the clinical observation that cancer patients’ information needs evolve throughout their treatment journey. Early in diagnosis, patients typically ask broad, general questions about their disease, while later in their treatment course, they seek more specific information about their particular mutations, prognosis, and novel therapeutic options. This progression informed our stratification of questions into two categories: general questions about hematologic cancer (n = 5) and highly specific questions regarding more novel therapies and mutations (n = 5).

Each question was submitted in July 2024 through ChatGPT version 3.5 (San Francisco, CA, U.S.). We chose to use ChatGPT 3.5 over GPT 4.0 because it was freely accessible to all users at the time of our study, while GPT-4 was only available to paid subscribers with limited free access for the general public.

The identical set of questions was input into the online AI model on separate occasions for every individual survey sent to verify consistency. The queries were done in privacy mode, as well as starting a new chat to ensure there was no bias in the responses of subsequent queries.

Four attending hematology oncology physicians rated each response using this scale.

There were 3 male and 1 female reviewers, all of whom sub-specialize in treating leukemia specifically. Multiple reviewers allowed for the assessment of consistency across the various AI-generated responses.

Survey scale

A survey containing the question followed by the AI-generated responses, and the following options: 1-strongly disagree: This information is potentially harmful, the response is incorrect or misleading, 2-disagree: I would not recommend this to a patient as the response contains some inaccuracies or lacks clarity, 3-neutral: The response is neither accurate nor inaccurate; it is ambiguous or incomplete, 4-agree: I would recommend this information to a patient, and the response is generally accurate but may lack some detail, 5-strongly agree: I would recommend this information to a patient, and the response is accurate, clear, and comprehensive.

When comparing the two assessed categories, the general queries category received a higher average score of 3.38, indicating relatively better satisfaction or agreement among reviewers (Figure 1). In contrast, the novel therapies category achieved a lower mean score of 3.06, highlighting comparatively weaker responses for those queries. Inter-rater reliability among the four physicians was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa (κ = 0.164), indicating slight agreement beyond chance. This level of agreement suggests considerable variability in how the physicians evaluated ChatGPT’s performance, which is quite common in subjective rating studies, especially in medical contexts where clinical judgment can vary significantly between practitioners.

The physicians rated ChatGPT’s responses to general queries higher than those related to novel therapies.

An analysis of the scores for all queries reveals that none of the questions achieved a score of 5 (strongly agree), emphasizing that no topic met a strong consensus as accurate, clear, and comprehensive for the recommendation of the information to a patient. For the majority of the queries – 9 out of 10 – the mean scores fell within the range of 3.0–3.8, reflecting a generally neutral stance among reviewers. This suggests that while some information was satisfactory, it lacked the clarity, depth, or completeness required for higher levels of agreement. However, one query stood out as a clear outlier, receiving a significantly lower mean score of 2.3 (disagree). This particularly low score signals a critical weakness in the response for this specific topic, suggesting that it was especially deficient in accuracy, detail, or relevance.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that ChatGPT was given lower evaluation scores by physicians when asked specific questions regarding more novel therapies and mutations in comparison to general questions regarding hematologic cancer. Responses regarding novel therapies and genetic mutations were often found to lack relevant details or were not up to date, reflecting either insufficient knowledge, outdated resources, or limited access to current advancements in these areas [11]. This suggests a potential gap in ChatGPT’s extraction of information regarding rapidly evolving therapeutic options and emerging molecular markers. For example, the outlier query that received a score of 2.3 (disagree) was “What is my prognosis with a FLT3 mutation and how will it affect my treatment?” ChatGPT 3.5’s knowledge cutoff of September 2021 likely contributed to outdated information on recent therapeutic advances, such as FLT3 inhibitors like midostaurin (2017) and quizartinib (2023) [12,13]. Machine learning models rely on training data, and when ChatGPT only has a small number of sources available, it may pull information from less reliable sources [14].

One study evaluating the ability of ChatGPT to answer questions about cervical cancer found that it was able to present factual information like survivorship and prevention, but was unable to provide accurate information about treatment or questions specific to the patient’s situation [15,16]. Another study input 26 cancer diagnosis descriptions into ChatGPT 3.5 and found that it was unable to provide accurate treatment recommendations [17], and another found that they often do not follow diagnostic guidelines [18].

Similar studies have shown mostly accurate responses by ChatGPT when evaluated by oncologists, to more generalized questions regarding cancer risk factors, prevention, and diagnosis [19]. However, when patients are past the point of diagnosis and well within their course of treatment, they may have more specific questions regarding their prognosis. This is relevant, especially in diseases like acute myeloid leukemia, where precision medicine is a rapidly progressing field with the pathogenic roles of mutations and novel therapies.

While our study revealed ChatGPT’s limitations in answering mutation-specific and therapy-specific questions, these challenges mirror those faced by clinicians in practice. These limitations mirror challenges faced by clinicians, who also struggle with rapidly evolving precision oncology information and must balance technical accuracy with patient comprehension as well as time constraints during visits [20,21]. Communication variability exists among clinicians, with some providing detailed technical explanations while others offer simplified summaries.

In 2023, cancer therapies accounted for 24% of all FDA approvals, highlighting the continuous discovery of novel therapies in cancer treatment [22]. This poses an issue for the use of AI in this context, as these findings suggest that AI was unable to provide the most current information, and cancer patients should not rely on AI for questions specific to their prognosis or therapy. ChatGPT 3.5 is limited to the training provided by its developer, in which it only pulls data up to a certain date, or if there was a recent clinical trial leading to FDA approval of a therapy, the source output of one paper is not sufficient for ChatGPT to produce that as a response. Other AI platforms such as Claude and Med-PaLM may have higher performance in terms of relevance and completeness when given medical information [23–26], but ChatGPT was chosen to be used in this study as it was more well known at the time and was more likely to be used amongst the general population with over 100 million daily users, and over 800 million users weekly [27,28]. Of note, there are now other LLM-based models such as Claude, Gemini, Copilot and even trials on chatbot conversational agents are being investigated [29].

Past studies have shown that evaluators preferred chatbot responses over physician responses in terms of empathy and quality, which may suggest the potential that AI has to assist in drafting responses to patient questions [10]. This would save the provider time in developing a long response, in which they would only have to check the response for quality and approve it. One study indicated that patients’ feedback about a chatbot indicated a desire for personalized responses and therapy recommendations suited to their specific situations [30]. This is particularly relevant in the realm of hematologic malignancies, where precision and personalized medicine play a role in the course of disease.

Study limitations

First, a limitation of this study is that the ten questions represent a small sample of the vast number of potential oncology queries. The absence of a formal power analysis and the limited sample size of four reviewers restricts statistical significance and generalizability. However, expanding the study scope was not feasible given the practical challenges of recruiting busy attending hematology-oncology physicians, as a longer survey would likely have resulted in reduced participation rates and potentially compromised data quality.

Second, the study only evaluated ChatGPT 3.5, limiting generalizability to other AI chatbots (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, Copilot) that patients might encounter. Third, ChatGPT responses were collected at a single time point in July 2024, and AI model performance can change with updates or retraining, as seen with the development of the ChatGPT-4 model. Fourth, the study used researcher-generated questions rather than actual patient-generated queries, which may differ in phrasing, complexity, or emotional context.

Conclusions and future directions

ChatGPT may lack the latest information and accurate answers on specialized topics due to its reliance on training data. Due to the fact that AI is malleable and these studies have shown that AI does not present 100% accurate or updated information needed to effectively and safely educate patients, a physician will always be needed, at least at this time to approve AI information [31]. As chatbots become more prevalent in cancer care, they may help triage routine patient questions and concerns, allowing oncologists to allocate more time or be more efficient during their visits [32–34]. However, successful implementation requires protocols for physicians to effectively vet and approve chatbot-generated responses before they reach the patient [35]
