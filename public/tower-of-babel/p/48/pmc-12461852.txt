Calcium Intake and Bone Mineral Density in People With HIV and/or Chronic Hepatitis C
Authors: Zoey B Temesgen, Zelalem Temesgen, Roger J Bedimo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreased prevalence of reduced bone mineral density (BMD) is well documented in persons infected with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), or both. This study's purpose was to determine if an association exists between dietary calcium intake and BMD in these patients.MethodsParticipants were individuals with virologically suppressed HIV or untreated HCV monoinfections, those with HIV/HCV coinfection, and noninfected controls. The Hertzler-Frary dietary calcium questionnaire documented calcium intake with calcium amounts based on the US Department of Agriculture nutrient database. BMD was measured at baseline by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry, and χ2 tests were used to assess the relationship between BMD and infection status. One-way analysis of covariance was used to compare calcium intake data with the prevalence of normal BMD and osteoporosis by infection group. Adjustments were made for race, age, body mass index, and smoking status. Spearman correlation was used to identify relationships between calcium intake from foods/beverages and BMD.ResultsA total of 452 men were recruited into the study. Osteoporosis and osteopenia were prevalent, but there was no association between infection group and BMD result (χ2(6) = 6.813; P = .34). Only HCV-infected patients with osteoporosis had lower calcium intake than patients with normal BMD (mean difference, 409 mg/d [95% confidence interval, 35–784 mg/d]; P = .03).ConclusionsIn this study population, dietary calcium intake was correlated with reduced BMD only in patients with HCV monoinfection.Keywords: bone mineral density, calcium, hepatitis C, HIV, osteoporosis

With the increased survival of people with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), the burden of comorbidity is significantly increasing, with 45% of people with HIV predicted to have ≥2 physical comorbid conditions by 2030 [1]. These include increased fracture risk [2–5]. Reduced bone mineral density (BMD) in patients with HIV infection, hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, or HIV/HCV coinfection has been observed for many years. Brown [6] estimated that 67% of the monoinfected HIV population had low BMD with 15% having osteoporosis. In patients with HCV monoinfection, the prevalence of osteoporosis ranges from 14% to 28% [6–8], HCV-associated reduced BMD and increased fracture risk might be even higher in postmenopausal women [9].

Although all of these infection states are associated with increased prevalence of reduced BMD, the exact cause is unclear; there are possibly many contributing factors. For patients living with HIV, these include the HIV infection itself, the effects of antiretroviral therapy (ART), and HIV-related inflammation [10]. For patients with HCV infection, contributing factors include chronic inflammation and liver dysfunction [11]. A study by Maalouf et al [12] found that osteopenia or osteoporosis can occur in earlier stages of HCV, without advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis developing. HIV/HCV coinfection appears to have an added negative effect on bone health compared to monoinfection of either virus [13]; Dong et al [14] estimated that 22% of patients with HIV/HCV coinfection have osteoporosis. Another contributing factor might be that HIV/HCV-coinfected patients have a higher rate of fibrosis progression than HCV-monoinfected patients [15, 16].

Bedimo et al described an “over-representation of traditional risk factors” for reduced BMD in these patients [17]. Some risk factors, such as sex, race, and age, are not modifiable. Others are, including hypogonadism, steroid use, nutritional deficiencies, low body mass index (BMI), physical inactivity, alcohol consumption, and tobacco use [18]. Among nutritional deficiencies, calcium has been cited as a nutrient of concern in the setting of HIV infection [19].

Bone growth is influenced by dietary intake of calcium; adequate amounts are essential to prevent bone loss in the aging [20]. When calcium intake is inadequate, bone tissue is reabsorbed from bones to keep serum calcium levels constant, leading to reduced BMD [21]. Most Americans do not consume enough calcium [22], and there is limited published research on the calcium intake in patients HIV, HCV, or HIV/HCV infection, and its relationship with BMD in these groups.

The current study's primary purpose was to determine whether there is an association between dietary calcium intake and BMD in patients with HIV, HCV, or HIV/HCV coinfection. Its secondary purposes were to identify the prevalence of osteopenia and osteoporosis in each infection group and to examine differences in calcium intake between infection groups and their correlation with BMD.

METHODS

Participants

The Bone Health Study was a cross-sectional study conducted at the VA North Texas Health Care System (VANTHCS) to evaluate rates and determinants of decreased bone health among HIV- and HCV-infected patients [23]. HIV-monoinfected, HCV-monoinfected, HIV/HCV-coinfected, and uninfected veterans (controls) receiving care at VANTHCS in Dallas, Texas were enrolled in the study. The VANTHCS Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol, and all patients provided written informed consent.

Treatment History

HIV-infected and HIV/HCV-coinfected patients were enrolled if HIV viremia had been suppressed with ART for ≥6 months. HCV-infected and HIV/HCV-coinfected patients were enrolled if their HCV was not previously or currently treated.

Assessment of Dietary Calcium Intake

Dietary intake of calcium was captured at study enrollment using a Hertzler-Frary dietary calcium rapid assessment questionnaire, which has been tested and validated [24]. Calcium amounts for each item on the calcium questionnaire were taken from the US Department of Agriculture nutrient database. The number of servings of each food/beverage item consumed by the participants was converted to milligrams. If a patient indicated that he took a calcium supplement, the dosage was assumed to be 1000 mg/d.

Assessment of BMD

Dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) was performed at baseline; BMD at the lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip was recorded. The World Health Organization defined osteopenia for postmenopausal women and older men as a T score between −1.0 and −2.49. A T score of −2.5 or less at any of the skeletal sites recorded represented osteoporosis [25].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were expressed as means with SDs. Data were visually inspected for normality using box plots and histograms. Unadjusted calcium intake results were expressed as medians, and χ2 tests were used to assess the relationship between BMD and infection status. One-way analysis of covariance was used to compare the transformed calcium intake data with the prevalence of normal BMD and osteoporosis by infection group. Relationships between calcium intake amounts from foods or beverages and BMD were assessed by means of Spearman rank correlation. Differences were considered significant at P < .05. All analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 23.0 (2014; IBM Corporation).

A univariate linear regression model was constructed with dietary calcium intake amounts without supplements as continuous dependent variables. To assess whether BMD and infection state were independently associated with calcium intake, the model was adjusted for recognized bone health risk factors potentially confounding the association: age, BMI, race (black or nonblack), and smoking status.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Of 452 participants recruited into the study, 16 were excluded after being identified as outliers using 1.5 times the interquartile range for calcium intake. Analyses were performed with and without the outliers for comparison, and the removal of the outliers did not change the results.

Patient characteristics are presented in Table 1. The patient cohort was all male, and the majority were African American (63%). The mean age was 55 years, the mean BMI was 28.72 (calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared), 54% (n = 234) were smokers, and 15% (n = 64) reported taking a calcium supplement. The large majority of study participants with HIV were currently receiving or had previously received tenofovir disoproxil fumarate at baseline (94% of HIV/HCV and 92% of HIV participants; Table 1).

Prevalence of Osteopenia and Osteoporosis in All Groups

The prevalence of osteopenia and osteoporosis in each group is presented in Table 1. Numerically, the HIV/HCV group had the highest prevalence of osteopenia and osteoporosis, while prevalences in the HIV and HCV monoinfection groups and the control group were similar. However, using a χ2 test for homogeneity, there was no association between infection group and the BMD results (χ2(6) = 6.813; P = .34).

Association With Infection Group

A Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the calcium intake data, confirming that it lacked normal distribution. Therefore, a square root transformation was applied to the calcium data before analysis. Table 1 reports the percentage of patients in each infection group who were taking a calcium supplement as well as the median calcium intake by infection group, with and without supplements. After adjustment for BMI, race, age, and smoking status, there was no significant difference in median dietary calcium intake among infection and control groups (F (3426) = 0.966; P = .41). There was also no significant difference in median calcium intake including supplements between infection and control groups (F (3426) = 0.886; P = .45).

Association of Calcium Intake With BMD

Controlling for age, BMI, race, and smoking status, there were no significant differences in median calcium intake between patients with normal BMD and those with osteoporosis in the HIV-infected, HIV/HCV-coinfected, and control groups. As shown in Figure 1, patients with osteoporosis showed a trend toward lower calcium intake, which was not significant. However, there was a significant difference in median dietary calcium intake between HCV-infected patients by BMD status (F (2, 92) = 3.955; P = .02). Post hoc analysis showed that the median calcium intake was significantly lower in HCV-infected patients with osteoporosis than in those with normal BMD (mean difference, 409 mg/d [95% confidence interval, 35–784 mg/d]; P = .03).

DISCUSSION

In this all-male, predominantly African American study cohort, no significant differences in BMD were demonstrated among HIV infection, HCV infection, HIV/HCV coinfection, and control groups. There were no significant differences in median calcium intake between patients with normal BMD and osteoporosis in the HIV, HIV/HCV, or control groups. However, calcium intake was significantly lower in HCV-infected patients with osteoporosis compared to those with normal BMD. The estimated median dietary calcium intake in this study (606–788 mg/d) was well below the recommended dietary allowance of calcium (1000 mg/d) for men aged 51–70 years [26].

Previous research documented the higher prevalence of osteoporosis and osteopenia in HIV, HCV, and HIV/HCV infection [14]. In HIV/HCV-coinfected patients, a meta-analysis confirmed that low BMD was more prevalent than with HIV monoinfection (pooled odds ratio, 1.98 [95% confidence interval, 1.18–3.31]) [27]. We have shown that HIV and HCV were independently associated with reduced BMD [23] but they might do so via different mechanism(s). Unlike HIV, HCV did not seem to increase bone turnover [23].

The prevalence of osteoporosis and osteopenia in this study, including among controls, was higher than what has been reported for the United States generation population. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) in 2005–2008 [28] found that 38% of American men >50 years old had osteopenia and 4% had osteoporosis. A study by Wright et al [29] estimated the prevalence of osteoporosis and osteopenia using the 2010 US Census population and applied prevalence estimates for men aged ≥50 years from NHANES 2005–2010. It determined that 4.3% of men in the United States in 2010 had osteoporosis, and 35.3% had osteopenia.

Several studies have examined calcium intake and prevalence of osteoporosis. A 12-year follow-up in the Framingham Offspring Study examined dairy product consumption and BMD and found that consumption of milk and yogurt but no other dairy products was positively associated with BMD [30]. Another study of patients with HIV did not find a relationship between BMD and daily calcium intake, but among food items rich in calcium, yogurt consumption was positively correlated with BMD in the lumbar spine in the HIV group (P < .05) [19].

Meeting daily requirements with nondairy sources of calcium—such as beans, leafy green vegetables, and almonds—is difficult [31]. In the present study, HCV-monoinfected patients with osteoporosis were the only group identified as having a significantly lower calcium intake than HCV-monoinfected patients with normal BMD. Since increased bone resorption has not been observed with HCV infection [23], a diet lacking calcium is, at least in part, a possible explanation for reduced BMD. While it is well established that HCV is a possible risk factor for osteoporosis, other research has found that the virus may be less influential than other risk factors, such as poor nutrition and comorbid conditions like hypertension, in contributing to the development of osteoporosis [32].

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study has several strengths. Foremost is its use of a large cohort of patients with HIV- and HCV monoinfection, as well as those coinfected with both viruses and uninfected controls. Moreover, to our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between calcium intake and DXA T scores within these infection groups.

The current study is subject to several notable limitations that warrant consideration. First, the exclusively male and predominantly African American patient population limits the generalizability of the findings to broader demographics. Second, the method used to assess dietary calcium intake presented inherent flaws, notably omission in quantifying calcium supplement dosages, noninclusion of certain calcium-rich foods like pizza, and lack of precision in capturing the amount of calcium in blended coffee drinks and calcium-fortified energy bars. Together with the absence of information on intake of other nutrients that play an integral role in bone health (vitamin D, magnesium, phosphorus, and protein), this undermines the accuracy and comprehensiveness of the dietary data collected.

Another limitation is the reliability of DXA as the sole metric for assessing fracture risk in patients living with HIV or HCV. While DXA is considered the reference standard for measuring BMD, it was originally developed and validated for assessing fracture risk in postmenopausal women [33]. Its utility in assessing fracture risk in persons living with HIV may not be fully extrapolated. The risk of fracture in this population is complex, involving an interplay between traditional risk factors and risk factors specific to HIV infection. Our previous analyses suggest that HIV increases bone turnover, while HCV does not. Treated HIV infection was associated with significant elevations in the bone resorption marker CTX, and the bone formation marker osteocalcin (Figure 2), a consistent finding in HIV-infected patients receiving ART [33, 34]. Therefore, apart from likely overrepresentation of “traditional” fracture risk factors (Figure 2), the mechanisms by which HCV increases fracture risk remain largely unexplained and seem to be independent of BMD.

In conclusion, healthcare providers should be aware that reduced BMD is a major health concern among patients monoinfected with HIV or HCV or coinfected with both. Low intake of dietary calcium does not appear to be related to reduced BMD in patients with HIV, but it could be one possible explanation in those with HCV monoinfection Moreover, some dairy products, like milk and yogurt, might be more protective of bone health in these patient populations. Finally, primary prevention of bone disease in patients with HIV or HCV infection should include the promotion of behavioral changes in food intake to improve diet quality.

More nutritional studies are needed to evaluate a broader range of nutrients concerning bone health, such as vitamin D and protein, and to better understand if and how an overall well-balanced diet can better protect bone health in these patient populations. In addition, future research should focus on fracture risk since it is a meaningful outcome measurement.
