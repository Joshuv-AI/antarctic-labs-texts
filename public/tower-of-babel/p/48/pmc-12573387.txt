Stability of intravenous medicines – evidence of maximum temperature reached in both summer and winter within soft shell elastomeric pumps
Authors: Susanna Maria van der Merwe, Nicholas Boyd, Simba Mavhunga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveElastomeric devices or pumps are a valuable tool to deliver outpatient parenteral therapy and have been used for administration of chemotherapy, antibiotics and pain medication. A key determinant of effective treatment is to consider the stability of medicines within these devices. It is widely known that an increase in temperature positively correlates to an increase in drug degradation. The objective of our work was to measure the temperature within soft shell elastomeric devices, under simulated outpatient treatment conditions in summer and winter months, and to determine the maximum temperature reached within these periods of use.MethodsThermocouples were inserted within soft shell Easypump II (B Braun Medical, Sheffield, UK) elastomeric pumps and the temperature was monitored under simulated outpatient conditions during cold and warm weather with different fill volumes. Temperature monitoring was also conducted with varying levels of insulation around the devices.ResultsOur results show that internal temperatures remained below 32°C±1°C in winter and summer months, including during times defined as a heatwave. Fill volume and ambient temperature were shown to be significant factors affecting the internal temperatures reached.ConclusionA soft shell Easypump II elastomeric pump, if used within its carry pouch, will maintain the internal solution below a temperature of 32°C±1°C if patients correctly adhere to handling guidance. Our results show that further improvements to the insulation material used in carry pouches can significantly restrict the rate of temperature rise within the pumps and will give more assurance in relation to preventing degradation especially considering the increases in extreme weather conditions observed in recent years due to global warming.

Introduction

The decision to allow patients to receive treatment at home is driven by a number of factors including patient preference, cost effectiveness and the increase in hospital acquired infections.1 2 Furthermore, it is increasingly being used by overburdened public healthcare providers as a tool to reduce costs, including by optimising hospital bed capacity and managing patient flow,3 while not compromising patient care.4

One way of delivering parenteral treatment at home is through the use of an elastomeric infusion device commonly known as an elastomeric pump. These devices are suitable for the delivery of ambulatory parenteral therapy to a patient in a controlled manner, including agents used for chemotherapy, analgesics and antibiotics, the latter administration approach commonly known as outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT).4 5

Elastomeric pumps comprise of an elastic balloon reservoir inside a durable outer shell, made from either a tough plastic or an adaptable elastomer the latter being described as soft shell devices. The pumps work through the deflation of the fluid-filled balloon reservoir creating mechanical pressure which pushes the fluid through the tubing and into the catheter attached to the patient. The rate of flow can be decided by the chosen lumen size and length of the tubing. Hence, flow control tubing is usually made from plastics which maintain their form even in changing temperatures.6

Despite the benefits, there is a general lack of data regarding drug stability in these devices, due to the fluctuating and higher internal temperatures that are encountered. This has led to concerns over their safety and viability for use in ambulatory treatment. An increase in temperature positively correlates to an increase in drug degradation due to heat being able to influence common degradation mechanisms such as hydrolysis and oxidation.7 In the United Kingdom, evidence based guidance8 was issued in 2018 by the National Health Service (NHS) Pharmaceutical Quality Assurance Committee (PQAC) in relation to pharmaceutical issues concerning OPAT and other ambulatory intravenous (IV) therapies.9 The guidance concluded that if devices are handled appropriately it is likely that the temperature of the solutions within them will remain below 32°C. Correct handling includes not covering the device with bedclothes at night and also not exposing the device to direct sunlight, even if it is stored within a carry pouch. Therefore, the temperature to perform stability testing was specified as 32°C±1°C, however, importantly, only if data are available to support this limit not being exceeded under a worst case scenario.9

Building on our previous work,10 in this study we present new and robust evidence that the soft shell device we tested complies with the stability guidance provided by the PQAC with internal temperatures remaining below 32°C±1°C if used as advised. In addition, we present for the first time data that demonstrate the significant effect that changing the insulation material around the device can have on keeping the internal fluid temperature below this limit for longer, therefore reducing the risk of degradation linked to increased temperature.

Materials and methods

Materials

Easyump II disposable soft shell elastomeric infusion pump systems, with a fill volume of 270 mL, insulated carry pouches, normal sterile saline and fill syringes were supplied by B Braun Medical Limited, Sheffield, UK. Inconel 600 insulated type K Miniature 0.75 mm diameter 150 mm long thermocouples, with miniature flat pin plugs and miniature thermocouple connectors, were obtained from Thermocouple Direct, Uxbridge, UK. An 88 598 AZ four channel K Type Temperature Sensor SD card data logger (AZ Instrument Corporation, Taiwan, ROC) was used to record the temperature data.

Preparation of the soft shell elastomeric pumps

A thermocouple was inserted into the device through the infusion tube which was shortened to ensure the tip was within the centre of the device. The infusion tube was sealed with a purposely designed and manufactured single-element compression seal fitting and the pump was filled to the maximum fill volume of 270 mL (n=3) or half the fill volume (n=3) with normal saline, the most common diluent used in IV administration, under aseptic conditions. Pumps were left observed for a period of 24 hours to ensure there was no leakage before any work was started.

Temperature monitoring during simulated in-use conditions

The temperatures inside the elastomeric pumps were monitored during simulated conditions as advised by the PQAC9 with the aim of determining the maximum temperature reached within the period. Before starting any measurement, devices (n=3) were cooled to 5°C±3°C at which point the three devices were connected to the data logger (the start of a 25-hour period of recording at 1 minute intervals) and allowed to acclimatise for 1 hour as advised for an in-use clinical situation.

Following this acclimatisation period as advised by B. Braun, the manufacturer, the devices were placed in their insulated carry pouches. They were then worn around the waist by a healthy volunteer who was one of the main investigators. The temperature was recorded for a further 24 hours to complete the 25-hour period while the investigator went about their daily routine. This included time at home, work and travel to and from work, while ensuring that the devices were never exposed to direct sunlight. At night time, before bed, the carry pouches, with the soft shell elastomeric devices within, were detached and placed away from the investigator, ensuring they were not under the bed clothes as recommended by the PQAC8 and B. Braun. The guidance was followed at all times during the course of the investigation.

Each soft shell elastomeric pump was subjected to 3 days of testing in mild to warm weather (25, 27 and 30 August 2020, summer) with external temperatures in Portsmouth, UK area ranging between approximately 12°C and 21°C. Testing in cold weather (21, 24 and 26 February 2020, winter), with external temperatures ranging between approximately 3°C and 12°C, was also carried out for 3 days in each soft shell elastomeric pump, with periods of time under a thick coat, simulating conditions which may be encountered by a patient during clinical use. Three pumps were worn on each measurement day to determine intra-day variation.

In addition to this a further 2 days of measurements, 1 day each with pumps half and full-filled respectively, were taken during a period of weather described as a heatwave.11 Temperature records in the Portsmouth, UK area reported external temperatures ranging between a low of approximately 17°C (early morning) and high of approximately 33°C (in the afternoon) on 10 and 11 August 2020.

The temperatures reached inside the pumps under the conditions described above were recorded alongside the ambient temperature, for each 25-hour period using a free thermocouple placed external to the pumps.

Temperature monitoring under static conditions with varying levels of insulation

Pumps (n=3), either filled to full volume or to half volume, were cooled to 5°C before the start of the experiment. One pump was placed in a modified vacuum flask to represent a high level of insulation, another in the insulated carry pouch supplied by B. Braun (current insulation scheme) and one had no insulation and was therefore bare. The pumps were placed in an incubator (chamber) at 37°C with a free thermocouple. Data were recorded at 1 minute intervals until 25°C was reached.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the significance of the effect of ambient temperature and fill volume on the internal diluent temperatures we performed a statistical analysis of the data. A two-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) (p<0.05) with a post-hoc Tukey test were performed, using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA USA, www.graphpad.com (accessed 31 July 2021). The two independent variables were ambient temperature (in summer or winter weather) and fill volume used, either fully-filled or half-filled, with maximum temperature the dependent variable.

The decrease in internal diluent temperature, is as expected more pronounced in the winter than summer months and when the pumps are half-filled. High ambient temperatures above 35°C were registered for short periods but did not significantly impact on the internal diluent temperature. Temperatures measured under a winter coat at times measured between 30 and 35°C (figure 2B), however due to the delay in heat transfer the increased ambient temperature had little impact on the internal diluent temperatures.

During the study period the United Kingdom experienced a period of weather over a week where outdoor temperatures exceeded 30°C during the day in the local area. This provided an opportunity to study the effects of extreme weather. In-use conditions were similar to those described but due to the high temperatures outside the investigator remained mostly indoors, which was government advice at the time.

During this period the maximum ambient temperature reached was 36.1°C and temperatures remained higher for most of the measurement period with ambient temperatures in excess of 25°C. The combination of the higher ambient temperatures during this period and wearing the devices close to the body resulted in internal diluent temperatures exceeding 32°C for a short period of time, approximately 50 minutes. As expected with the higher ambient temperatures the decrease in internal diluent temperatures at night time, when the devices were detached from the investigator’s body, was less pronounced compared with the winter months (figure 3).

Effectiveness of insulation in preventing excessive increase of internal diluent temperature

Figure 5 shows the effectiveness of two different types of insulation on preventing the excessive rise of the internal diluent temperature with (A) pumps filled to half volume and (B) pumps fully-filled.

Internal temperature change in individual pumps surrounded by different levels of insulation. (A) Pumps filled to half volume with diluent; (B) Pumps filled to full volume with diluent. Modified vacuum flask represents highest level of insulation, followed by insulation provided by carry pouch supplied by pump manufacturer, and bare pumps without any insulation within the incubator chamber.

When the soft shell elastomeric pumps are bare (no insulation surrounding them) the internal temperature rapidly increases under constant exposure to heat at 37°C, a temperature purposely selected to see how the material will perform in a hot and dry climate, and the internal temperature reaches 32°C at around 20 minutes. The current carry pouch supplied by the manufacturer to use with the soft shell elastomeric pumps goes some way in providing protection and delays the temperature rise to 32°C by approximately 240 minutes, 4 hours, for both fill volumes when exposed to constant heat at 37°C. The upper limit of 32°C was reached between 320 minutes and 390 minutes across the two diluent volumes. The modified vacuum container on the other hand maintained the temperature below 32°C for approximately 1040 minutes, around 17 hours, for the half-filled diluent volume and more significant for the entire 24 hour period for the fully filled pumps. Considering that most pumps when supplied to patients are filled to full capacity, to allow for a 24 hour infusion period, this data indicates that improved insulation can potentially maintain temperatures below the current specified level of 32°C, in countries experiencing very hot weather, since mankind will try to avoid exposure to excessively high temperatures especially if unwell.

Discussion

Despite the benefits, there remains a lack of ‘in-use’ data demonstrating real-world temperature fluctuations within soft shell elastomeric pumps. The internal solution (consisting of active ingredients and diluent) temperature will increase during use due to the ambient temperature and body heat that they are exposed to. Heat transfer mainly occurs through conduction and convection. From the statistical analysis it is evident that both the diluent fill volume and the external ambient temperature has a significant effect on the maximum internal temperature reached within a specified time period and the rate of temperature increase. It is therefore essential that patients and healthcare professionals receive training in the correct use of the pumps.

Maximum temperatures reported in this work correlate with a small number of previous studies carried out in other elastomeric pumps.10 12 Our findings provide further evidence that the upper temperature limit for stability studies of 32°C±1°C is sensible in countries normally not experiencing extreme heat, although in recent years extreme weather has become more prevalent due to global warming. In countries that do regularly have average temperatures above 30°C, during the day in summer months, the 32°C limit may be exceeded and the protection provided by the current supplied insulated carry pouch may not be sufficient. However, a change in insulation material can provide assurance, as shown, or alternatively a method of active cooling can be used, both approaches currently being considered by the authors. It is important though that the cost-effectiveness of these systems remains preserved to ensure that these systems provide a realistic alternative to inpatient IV administration.

Conclusion

Our study provides the first evidence that these soft shell devices, if used correctly within a manufacturer supplied carry pouch, will maintain the internal solution below a temperature of 32°C in both summer and winter months if patients correctly follow and adhere to guidance.
