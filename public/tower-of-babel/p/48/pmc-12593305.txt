Talking with adolescent and young adult cancer survivors about health after cancer: A review and communication guide for clinicians
Authors: Stephanie M Smith, Lauren C Heathcote, Jennifer N John, Jasan Zimmerman, Catherine Benedict, Abby R Rosenberg, Lidia Schapira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescent and young adult (AYA) cancer survivors represent a vulnerable population in cancer care and survivorship. AYA survivors are a heterogeneous group that includes people between the ages of 15 and 39 years who were treated for cancer during their childhood or AYA years, at which time they had variable agency and may have received cancer care in pediatric or adult settings. AYA survivors experience one or multiple health care transitions, moving from active oncology to posttreatment survivorship and/or from pediatric to adult care. Clinician communication that centers the needs and preferences of the AYA and their family (parent, partner, other support person) is a therapeutic tool that can support AYAs in these health care transitions and promote AYA engagement in their care. In this article, the authors review clinician communication practices through the lens of AYAs' and families' lived experiences with a focus on the initial diagnosis and treatment phase, completion of treatment, and throughout posttreatment survivorship care. Specific communication topics relevant to survivorship encompass managing uncertainty and fear of cancer recurrence, discussing treatment‐related future health risks, and supporting self‐management and engagement in care. Best practices for clinician communication include maintaining openness, compassion, and flexibility to re‐assess and adapt communication styles as an AYA cancer survivors' needs, concerns, and preferences change over time.Keywords: adolescent and young adult, cancer survivorship, health communication, patient–clinician relationship

Adolescents and young adults (AYAs), defined by the National Cancer Institute as individuals between ages 15 and 39 years, are recognized as a vulnerable population in cancer care and survivorship. 1 , 2 , 3 AYAs have unique developmental and life‐stage considerations and, compared with same‐age peers, are at increased risk of late effects: health conditions such as cardiovascular disease, infertility, and subsequent malignancies that are caused by and develop after cancer treatment. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Greater than 80% of AYAs diagnosed with cancer in the United States today will become long‐term (≥5‐year) cancer survivors. 12 The population of AYA cancer survivors is heterogeneous and includes people diagnosed between ages 15 and 39 years as well as those diagnosed before age 15 years who age into young adulthood after a childhood cancer diagnosis. AYA survivors have diverse experiences during cancer treatment, affected by their age at diagnosis and treatment setting (e.g., pediatric vs. adult settings, academic vs. community settings). 13

AYA cancer survivors should receive lifelong specialized medical care tailored to their risk of treatment‐related late effects. 14 , 15 , 16 They face many transitions in care—from active treatment to posttreatment monitoring, from oncology to long‐term follow‐up/survivorship, and, for some, from pediatric to adult care settings. 17 Each transition places AYA survivors at risk for loss to follow‐up, discontinuity in care, and nonadherence to follow‐up recommendations. 14 Effective communication among the triad of the AYA, their family, and their clinicians throughout these transitions is critically important to ensure that the AYA not only learns what they need to know about their past cancer history, health risks, and survivorship care needs but also develops self‐management skills. 18 Self‐management refers to cancer survivors' ability to navigate the health care system and take responsibility for their well‐being and future health needs. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Communication that supports AYA self‐management promotes engagement in their care. 23

The science of patient–physician communication supports open and frank information sharing. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Patient narratives and qualitative research on the lived experience of cancer survivors and families add to this by illustrating the intense emotions and disorientation that accompany care transitions, such as reaching the end of cancer treatment. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 Throughout this article, we define family broadly to include parents, caregivers, partners, spouses, or other support people involved with an AYA's care. Clinician communication research suggests that remaining curious, humble, and open to the patient and family's experience is the best way to establish trust, that relationships matter and evolve over the illness trajectory, and that addressing the information needs of patients and families promotes empowerment and self‐management. 18 , 24 , 25 , 27 , 35 , 36 Effective clinician communication helps build a therapeutic alliance with families throughout the illness experience by centering human connection, empathy, and presence and by partnering with inclusivity, humor, and honesty. 35 , 36

In this review, we approach clinician communication through the lens of the patient and family's lived experience, starting with the lead‐up to the diagnosis, including communication about a cancer diagnosis and treatment plan, discussions during cancer treatment and at completion of treatment, support and expectation setting during transitions of care, and communication about follow‐up/survivorship care (Figure 1). Although not the focus of this article, there is a robust literature supporting communication best practices in the setting of advanced and incurable pediatric/AYA cancer, relapsed or refractory cancer, and end‐of‐life care settings. 26 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 Where applicable, we apply key principles from these studies to conversations about cancer survivorship. At the end of each section, we include practice points to guide clinicians with the goal of translating research into practice. We acknowledge there is no one‐size‐fits‐all communication approach that will work well for all AYA cancer survivors or their families. The challenge for clinicians is to meet each patient and family with openness, compassion, and a willingness to adapt their approach to fit the situation.

IT MAY BE CANCER

There are many ways in which patients and families first learn of their cancer diagnosis. Children and younger AYAs who are seen in pediatric settings are often referred for workup in an emergency department, hospital, or outpatient oncology clinic and may receive the definitive diagnosis from their pediatrician or an oncologist. 43 AYAs who are evaluated in adult settings more often learn of their diagnosis from nononcology clinicians; in one survey of >400 adults with cancer (including AYAs aged 19 years and older), only 22% were told of their diagnosis by an oncologist. 44 Oncologists or primary care physicians were more likely than other physicians (e.g., surgeons, emergency medicine, other specialists) to share the diagnosis in a personal setting and include treatment options in the discussion, both of which were associated with greater patient satisfaction. 44

One qualitative study explored families' and predominantly nononcology physicians' perspectives on communication leading up to a childhood cancer diagnosis. 45 Physicians often used vague language (mass, tumor) or medical jargon (hematology/oncology, malignancy, neoplasm) before a confirmed diagnosis. Families with high health literacy reported clearly understanding their physicians and knowing there was concern for cancer, whereas those with lower health literacy did not understand this and later reported shock at the cancer diagnosis. As such, medical jargon should be minimized to enable understanding. Families have expressed different preferences regarding their physicians' use of the term cancer when the diagnosis had not been confirmed, suggesting the importance of tailoring for each family. 45

For families facing a possible cancer diagnosis, supportive relationships and anticipatory guidance provide benefits. 46 , 47 Many children, AYAs, and families are first told by well meaning physicians that their symptoms are “probably nothing to worry about” and could be caused by infection, anxiety, or another common condition. Although it is always appropriate to adjust communication based on the level of suspicion for cancer versus another cause, it is preferable to prioritize honesty and the family's right to know any and all information that pertains to their health and well‐being. 26 , 48 There is strong evidence from palliative care communication that, even when the news is difficult, truth‐telling by physicians does not cause distress or undermine hope. 26 , 42 , 49 , 50

Many families may not know what questions to ask or how to navigate their first oncology consultation. Advocacy and philanthropic societies webpages (Table 1) contain useful information geared toward preparing the patient and family to get the most from their meetings with the cancer team by asking questions. 51 , 52 , 53 Often pressed for time, clinicians may need to remember to take the time to allow an AYA or family member to share their story because it shows interest in the person and a willingness to enter into a mutually respectful, trusting partnership among the patient, family, and clinician.

WE HAVE A DIAGNOSIS

There is limited literature to guide new cancer diagnosis conversations specifically for AYAs treated in adult settings, thus we draw from literature pertaining to AYAs treated in pediatric settings. More than 20 years ago, Mack and Grier published “The Day One Talk”, an article describing their process for disclosing a new cancer diagnosis to children and AYAs in pediatric settings, and their families. 24 The article was based on the authors' experience, resonated with pediatric oncologists, and influenced the curriculum for trainees in the field. The authors emphasized conveying three key points to families: (1) the diagnosis includes the word cancer (some families may not recognize that lymphoma, leukemia, or sarcoma are types of cancers), (2) providing an overview of treatment and its goals (cure, palliation, prognosis), and (3) that the cancer was not caused by something the child or parents did or did not do. 24 Importantly, the overarching aim of the conversation is to create a connection between physician and family. Key recommendations for clinician communication include assessing what families already know about their child's condition, listening actively, responding to emotions, setting expectations in terms of clinician honesty even if the news is difficult, using simple, clear language, and pausing often to give space and time for processing. 24

A team of investigators more recently examined how the day‐1 talk is implemented in clinical practice by interviewing 20 pediatric oncologists and fellows and surveying 70 pediatric oncology fellows and 38 fellowship program directors. 38 Data support that the content of the day‐1 talk is typically covered in a series of conversations delivered in smaller chunks or units over days and weeks. Nearly all fellows (96%) learn how to lead these conversations through shadowing, with about 50% reporting didactic training and <25% simulation‐based training. 38 The reliance on shadowing rather than formal curricula or simulations suggests that many pediatric oncology training programs in the United States lack standardized communication skills training, leaving trainees to learn through observation without receiving personalized feedback or coaching. 54 , 55

As recommended in “The Day One Talk,” oncologists and parents/families typically come to a shared decision about whether to involve the child in the initial conversation based on family preference, the child's age, and their developmental readiness. 24 , 38 In one survey of 360 parents of children with cancer (25% of whom were adolescents aged 13–18 years), most but not all adolescents were present for the new diagnosis conversation (67% of those aged 13–15 years, 84% of those aged 6–18 years). 56 The child's presence or absence did not correlate with whether the parent received prognostic information or their perception of information quality or communication quality, supporting the practice of individualizing the information based on family and child preferences. 56 Even if the child is not present at the initial discussion, partnering with families to communicate clearly with the child about their diagnosis in an age‐appropriate and developmentally appropriate way is recommended.

Starting at the moment there is a diagnosis, clinicians should engage parents and families in discussions about how to best communicate, including when and how to share information with each family member. Some parents hesitate to share the cancer diagnosis with their child or adolescent, seeking to protect them from potentially upsetting information. 57 Instead of protecting, this can generate distrust and disengagement. 23 Regardless of chronologic age or developmental stage, AYAs have individual preferences about how much information they want and from whom, and this often changes over time. At diagnosis, clinicians should directly ask AYAs whether they want all the details, none of the details (delegating to a parent or family member instead), or somewhere in between. Taking the time to understand an AYA's and family's communication preferences upfront helps support a therapeutic relationship. It is also helpful to periodically reassess their preferences for receiving information and the extent to which they want to be involved in clinical decision‐making. 23

Studies have indicated that the vast majority of AYAs and parents want to know about their likelihood of responding to treatment and risk of recurrence. 26 , 37 , 50 , 56 , 58 In the real world, however, the optimism bias—a human tendency to overestimate the likelihood of positive events and underestimate the likelihood of negative events—affects how oncologists communicate prognosis and how patients and families process it. 41 , 59 Importantly, honest prognostic disclosure by clinicians can support hope even when the prognosis is poor. 41 , 50 , 60

In one study of >350 parents of children and AYAs with cancer (31% adolescents aged 13–18 years), high‐quality physician communication style (rated by parents reporting how often the physician gave understandable answers, took enough time to answer questions, and conveyed information sensitively) and high‐quality prognosis information were associated with greater trust in the oncologist, peace of mind, communication‐related hope, and less anxiety. 60 However, non‐White and Hispanic parents and parents of children with a poor prognosis were less likely to have an accurate understanding of prognosis, despite their physicians disclosing it. 60 This work draws a critical distinction between prognostic disclosure and prognostic understanding because disclosure was not associated with an improved parental understanding of prognosis. 60 A related study demonstrated that, although prognostic information was highly valued by parents regardless of their race and ethnicity, physicians underestimated how interested Black and Hispanic parents were in receiving prognostic information, which may lead them to share less with these families and negatively affect quality of care. 58 To address this implicit bias, it is critically important to directly ask families how much they want to know and how they prefer to receive the information and to tailor communication accordingly. 25

The original day‐1 talk article recommended participation of a multidisciplinary care team in new diagnosis conversations, including the oncologist, nurse, social worker, and interpreter, should one be needed. 24 Mack and Grier wrote that the presence of a team did not distract the oncologist or the parent and served to bring together the various professionals who would assume responsibility for some aspect of the child's care. 24 In practice, how often other team members are included varies because conversations cannot always wait until standard working hours when psychosocial teams are more readily available. 38 Even if not present for the initial day‐1 talk, social workers, psychologists, and child life specialists (for AYAs treated in pediatric settings) are essential members of the cancer team and help communicate with and support families as they experience the effect of cancer on school, work, family routines, relationships, mental health, and quality of life. 61 , 62 , 63 , 64

A systematic review of communication preferences at the time of a pediatric/AYA cancer diagnosis suggested that families prefer a team‐based approach in which physicians, nurses, and other health professionals provide multiple types of support. 63 In one multisite survey study, 129 children and AYAs (74% adolescents aged 13–18 years) and their parents reflected on their experiences with clinician communication after a cancer diagnosis. 65 Approximately 20% of children and AYAs recalled having no discussion about the emotional effect of cancer treatment before starting treatment, and 40% wanted greater discussion of the effect of treatment on their daily activities. 65 Clinicians can help by always welcoming questions warmly and providing answers that are honest; normalizing emotions such as anxiety, anger, and sadness; and modeling adaptive coping behaviors.

Understanding the prognosis and implications of treatment, including potential side effects and late effects, influences AYAs' and families' choices for therapy. The question arises, however, about how much information can be conveyed and assimilated because these conversations are often overwhelming. 66 , 67 If information about late effects is included in the initial consultation(s), it is usually calibrated with statements that support the expected clinical benefit of such treatment. Managing the volume and intensity of information that needs to be conveyed in the days, weeks, and months after a cancer diagnosis requires clear, consistent communication and tailoring for each AYA's and family's preferences to prevent information overload and overwhelm.

FAMILIES WITH NON‐ENGLISH–LANGUAGE PREFERENCE

In‐person professional medical interpreters are essential for high‐quality communication yet are not always available for families with non‐English–language preference, based on resource limitations and clinician choice. 68 , 69 , 70 Despite strong evidence that professional medical interpreters improve the quality of care for patients with limited English proficiency, 71 , 72 clinicians may continue to rely on communication shorts, such as their own language skills even if limited, asking bilingual staff or family members to help interpret. 69 , 70 Interpreter modality also affects the quality of communication, with video‐based and phone‐based interpretation ranking significantly worse than in‐person interpretation for family meetings or establishing rapport between patients and clinicians, from the perspective of medical interpreters. 73

One qualitative study of 32 Spanish‐speaking parents/caregivers of children/AYAs with cancer highlighted several linguistic and cultural factors that contributed to poor experiences at the time of a new diagnosis consultation. 68 Some families equated any type of cancer with death based on cultural beliefs and wished clinicians had told them more specifically about their child’s prognosis and potential benefits of treatment. Families recalled clinicians bluntly describing their child’s cancer diagnosis without attending to emotions. 68 Others described limited availability of in‐person interpreters, especially after hours, and some felt uncomfortable voicing certain concerns or emotions through an interpreter. 68 These findings are consistent with themes identified in larger reviews of non‐English–language preferred patient experiences in other clinical settings 74 , 75 and are increasingly common in multicultural societies with immigrant and refugee populations.

Aiming to improve the communication experiences of Spanish‐language preferred families, one pediatric cancer center in the United States Midwest developed a novel model for delivering culturally and linguistically tailored care. 76 The limited English‐proficiency patient–family advocate role served three functions: professional Spanish interpreter with expertise in childhood cancer, cultural liaison, and health care systems advocate/case manager for Spanish‐language preferred families. 76 Parents and health care professionals were satisfied with this role and felt it positively influenced patient–family–clinician communication and trust. 76

Although not the primary focus of the current article, language‐related barriers to pediatric/AYA cancer clinical trial participation and informed consent have been well described. 77 , 78 , 79 Non‐English–language preferred families are underrepresented in Children’s Oncology Group clinical trials in part because of limited consent form translation and in‐person interpretation resources. 79 Language preference and health literacy (which is distinct from, but often conflated with, language) both affect the effectiveness of clinician communication about clinical trials. 77 , 78

If communication is viewed as an important tool for fostering healing relationships and responding to emotions, 27 , 35 discordance in clinician–patient and clinician–family language preference risks the formation of a therapeutic alliance. A single‐site study in adult oncology patients (including AYAs aged 26–39 years) compared audio recordings of four groups of patient–oncologist dyads during 34 initial oncology clinic visits: language‐discordant/professionally interpreted in Spanish, partly language‐concordant (oncologist communicated partly in Spanish and/or ad hoc staff or family interpreted), English language‐concordant, and Spanish language‐concordant. 80 An analysis of the recordings demonstrate that Spanish‐concordant dyads expressed the most emotion, English‐concordant and Spanish‐concordant dyads expressed more partnership/facilitation statements (e.g., assessing understanding, asking for opinions or permission to proceed) than language‐discordant dyads, and interpreters communicated a low percentage of both patients’ and clinicians’ partnership/facilitation statements. 80 Although that study examined patient–clinician dyads, the findings may also apply to the more complicated patient–family–clinician triad that often occurs in AYA cancer care. Clinicians building a therapeutic alliance in language‐discordant settings may benefit from placing special emphasis on communication related to partnership building and responding to emotions. Taking the time to establish rapport separately with the interpreter before entering the patient’s room or consultation room may help clinicians and interpreters communicate more easily, and this too may promote understanding and lower the stress for all parties involved.

EVOLVING RELATIONSHIPS AND WORRIES DURING CANCER TREATMENT

Recognizing the sheer volume of information that is involved in new diagnosis conversations, along with the emotional intensity of this time, there is a limit to how much patients and families can absorb and retain from these early conversations. 66 During the early treatment period, AYAs and their parents report receiving insufficient anticipatory guidance (e.g., response to treatment, side effects) and may also be ambivalent about discussing late effects when embarking on a course of life‐saving treatment. 67 , 81 During treatment, clinic visits are devoted to immediate medical issues, such as reviewing laboratory results and managing symptoms, and may feel rushed; therefore, it this not a good time to ask questions about the bigger picture.

Guided by the perceived need of AYAs and their families to revisit information related to the diagnosis, prognosis and treatment, Feraco and colleagues tested a communication intervention called the day‐100 talk. 67 , 82 This involved a structured family–clinician conference designed to capture family concerns and preferences for communication within the first several months of a cancer diagnosis. Families received a family conversation worksheet before the conference to elicit their worries, hopes, and questions regarding the cancer diagnosis and treatment and to describe their family context and communication preferences. 67 , 82 Oncology and psychosocial clinicians participated in a communication skills training that included the structured day‐100 talk communication guide and subsequently met face‐to‐face with each family an average of 1–2 months after study enrollment. 82 Families were highly satisfied with the day‐100 talk intervention. However, the authors note it was resource‐intensive and not likely scalable in its current form. 82 Clinicians can take this knowledge forward and ask patients and families at regular intervals whether they would like to revisit earlier conversations at a time that is not rushed and can be set aside for this purpose.

A survey of approximately 200 AYAs with cancer who were within 12 weeks of diagnosis provides some insight into the types of information that AYAs seek during the early cancer treatment period. 83 Although >90% reported receiving good/excellent information about their diagnosis, fewer than two thirds reported receiving good/excellent information about their risk of late effects or infertility. 83 AYAs highly valued information about late effects, including infertility, and many also found these topics distressing. 83 That study highlights the importance of age‐appropriate decision aids, empathic communication, and supportive interventions.

FINISHING CANCER TREATMENT

The completion of cancer treatment is often marked by celebrations in pediatric cancer treatment settings, as >85% of children/AYAs diagnosed with cancer will become long‐term survivors. Clinics may mark this milestone with an end‐of‐treatment party, complete with certificates signed by care team members, cake, ringing the end‐of‐treatment bell, songs, and cheers. Amid this excitement, many AYAs and families feel nervous, scared, and hesitant about what is to come. 34 , 84 , 85 There is an abrupt change in routine that may be accompanied by heightened fear of cancer recurrence as AYAs and families adjust to no longer having weekly or monthly oncology visits to reassure them that they are responding well to treatment. 33 , 34 , 86 , 87 , 88 Many AYAs and families experience a loss of social support in the setting of having much less frequent contact with their nurses, physicians, social workers, and other oncology care team members. 34 , 84 , 86 , 87 When completing treatment, AYAs may experience forced positivity and optimism from family and friends who mistakenly assume that the end of treatment heralds the end of the cancer experience. Clinicians can help by normalizing that the process of psychosocial adjustment to cancer survivorship takes time, as does adaptation to their new normal posttreatment. 29 , 89 AYA cancer survivors may experience feelings of guilt, having become close with peers whose cancer did not respond to treatment or recurred or worries about the burden they placed on loved ones. 28 , 32 , 33 , 90

Oncology clinicians can help their patients by recognizing, naming, and normalizing the wide range of emotions that often coexist when nearing or reaching the end of cancer treatment. Clear communication and expectation setting with anticipatory guidance about this care transition may help prepare AYAs and their families. 28 AYAs may feel relief when clinicians normalize the challenge of adjusting to posttreatment routines and acknowledge that emotional healing takes time. Dedicated end‐of‐treatment discussions also provide the opportunity for clinicians to connect AYAs with peer support, screen for psychosocial distress, and refer to specialized mental health care when appropriate. 28

Twelve years after publication of “The Day One Talk,” Wilejto and colleagues published their article “Completion of Therapy Talk,” which has received much less attention in pediatric/AYA oncology training and practice. 91 The authors posit that much of oncology care takes place through conversations and that the end‐of‐treatment conversation requires structure to assist with upcoming transitions in care. 91 Although these conversations typically unfold over multiple visits leading up to and after the actual completion of cancer treatment, 32 , 91 there are common themes: (1) to provide an opportunity to revisit the cancer experience and process the effect of cancer on the patient and their family, (2) to provide anticipatory guidance and give specific information about monitoring and surveillance to minimize future health risks, and (3) to provide referrals needed for rehabilitation.

Clinicians should begin the conversation with open‐ended questions exploring how the AYA and family are feeling at this moment. 91 Probing may elicit what they are most looking forward to and what they are most worried about, questions that require the clinician to be prepared to talk about possible long‐term treatment‐related toxicities. Families may have experienced unwanted complications, such as intensive care unit stays or unexpected early toxicities from treatment, and may welcome an opportunity to talk and process their experiences and give feedback to the care team. 81 At times, AYAs and families may feel like they are hearing about late effects for the first time, even if the information was given earlier in the course of the disease. The conversation can then move to discussing the new normal, a term coined by cancer survivors to describe the unique adjustments they experience after cancer treatment amid late/long‐term effects, changes in lifestyle, and shifts in social relationships. 89 Talking about life after cancer provides an opportunity for clinicians to recommend age‐appropriate information and resources, including support groups and online peer communities (e.g., Stupid Cancer). 28 , 92 Many survivors are at risk for late effects throughout the posttreatment period, and they need a roadmap for off‐therapy monitoring, including scans, blood tests, and clinic visits. 32 , 91 Finally, it is important for the oncologist to remind families to re‐establish a therapeutic relationship with their primary care physician. AYAs and families have identified a need for greater physician communication with their primary care physician and school posttreatment. 93 Caring for AYAs in their transition out of cancer treatment involves efforts to coordinate care; through these simple tasks, clinicians can convey support for their patient’s future well‐being.

MANAGING FEAR, WORRY, AND UNCERTAINTY

All cancer survivors experience uncertainty about the future, which can persist for decades after completion of cancer treatment. 94 For AYA cancer survivors, health‐related uncertainty extends beyond the risk of recurrence to include concerns about late effects—long‐term health conditions caused by prior cancer treatments. Although managing uncertainty is a cornerstone of patient‐centered communication, there is limited empirical guidance on how clinicians can best support survivors in this regard. 95 , 96 Fewer than one half of nearly 1800 adult cancer survivors surveyed in the Health Information National Trends Survey felt they received adequate communication from clinicians about managing uncertainty. 97

Fear of cancer recurrence and anxiety related to surveillance scans are particularly pressing unmet needs among AYA survivors. 98 , 99 , 100 , 101 , 102 , 103 , 104 , 105 These fears are frequently under‐addressed in survivorship care because of several factors, including patients’ reluctance to raise such concerns (prioritizing other issues, fear of receiving bad news) and clinicians feeling unequipped to address these psychological challenges. Even when clinicians aim to provide support for fear of recurrence, misconceptions about what constitutes effective communication can hinder progress. Honest, open discussions about short‐term and long‐term health risks, including recurrence and late effects, are essential for fostering trust and shared decision making. Research demonstrates that fear of cancer recurrence is most strongly correlated with younger age at diagnosis, female gender, and emotional symptoms at baseline and is rarely correlated with a patient’s actual risk of recurrence. 98 Survivors with low recurrence risk may still experience debilitating fears that impair their quality of life, suggesting that this should be discussed with all survivors regardless of empirical risk.

Although evidence for effective communication strategies addressing fear of recurrence in AYA cancer survivors is limited, insights can be drawn from a promising intervention developed in the breast cancer setting: CIFeR (Clinician Intervention to Reduce Fear of Recurrence). 106 This brief intervention, lasting 7–9 minutes on average, is delivered by medical oncologists during routine follow‐up appointments. It includes three core components: (1) validating concerns and reframing them as natural responses to the cancer experience, (2) providing personalized prognostic information tailored to the survivor’s clinical context, and (3) offering practical advice on managing worry. 106

Although normalizing fears of recurrence is important, clinicians must also acknowledge that these fears can be among the most distressing and disruptive aspects of survivorship. Simply stating that such fears are normal may unintentionally devalue the patient’s experience. Instead, empathic and direct communication is crucial. For example, a clinician might say, “It’s understandable to feel this way—fears of recurrence are very real and can be overwhelming at times. Let’s talk about how we can manage them together.” This approach validates the survivor’s feelings while opening the door to collaborative problem‐solving. Clinicians can also reframe fear of recurrence as a sign of the survivor’s commitment to their health and well‐being, which can encourage proactive and positive behaviors.

Helping patients distinguish between productive and unproductive worry is critical. For instance, a clinician might say, “It’s okay to acknowledge your fears, but let’s focus on what’s within your control, like maintaining regular check‐ups and adopting a healthy lifestyle.” Such guidance helps survivors redirect their energy toward actionable steps, reducing the paralyzing effect of unproductive worry. Fostering an open and ongoing dialogue about fears of recurrence and late effects is essential because these concerns may not emerge immediately after treatment but can escalate years later. This underscores the importance of addressing these fears over the long term rather than as a one‐time discussion. Proactively exploring and validating these concerns during follow‐up care also enables clinicians to identify patients who may benefit from specialized psycho‐oncology support, ensuring timely referral and comprehensive care.

Many AYA cancer survivors worry about bodily symptoms as a sign of cancer recurrence, which can negatively affect health‐related quality of life. 107 Survivors may respond to these concerns in one of two ways: becoming hypervigilant and over‐monitoring symptoms or ignoring them entirely because of fear. 108 Hypervigilance to bodily symptoms is recognized as one of four central characteristics of clinically severe fear of cancer recurrence, as identified by an international survey of 65 experts on the fear of recurrence. 109 Effective communication in the oncology clinic should balance encouraging appropriate symptom self‐monitoring with avoiding unnecessary anxiety that impairs quality of life. An international survey of 196 pediatric oncologists suggested that greater than 90% believed it important for off‐therapy patients to monitor for symptoms of recurrence or late effects. 110 However, whereas two thirds of clinicians often or always communicated symptoms that might indicate recurrence or late effects, only 40% regularly communicated which symptoms were unlikely to indicate recurrence. More than one half expressed interest in communication training to improve this aspect of care. 110

Clinicians’ responses to patients’ uncertainty are often influenced by their own tolerance for uncertainty and anxiety about relapse or late effects. 108 Some clinicians may lean toward immediate reassurance through scans or interventions, whereas others may adopt a more measured approach. Notably, oncologists with greater than 15 years of experience are more likely to communicate about symptoms unlikely to indicate recurrence, suggesting that confidence in addressing uncertainty develops over time. 110 Normalizing and validating fears is an essential first step in addressing symptom‐related concerns. Acknowledging that these worries are a common and understandable part of survivorship can help patients feel seen and supported. Many survivors may not volunteer their worries unless prompted, so clinicians may need to actively elicit these concerns.

Providing clear and specific guidance about symptoms is also helpful when tailored for the information needs and preferences of the AYA and their family. Clinicians can discuss which symptoms are more likely to indicate recurrence, which are less concerning, and the appropriate timelines for seeking medical attention. Vague messages like, “You know your body—come back if something feels wrong” may be distressing for survivors, particularly those who feel disconnected from their posttreatment bodies. Instead, clear and direct communication about what symptoms mean and how to respond to them can be more reassuring and empowering for AYAs. A clinician may state, “Many people experience swelling or pain when increasing their activities after the type of surgery you had, and it is unlikely to indicate recurrence.”

Another strategy is helping AYA survivors distinguish between symptoms of recurrence and manageable late effects. For example, explaining that fatigue or pain might be long‐term side effects of treatment rather than signs of recurrence can provide significant relief. This differentiation can also encourage survivors to seek earlier support for treatable late effects rather than avoiding these discussions out of fear that the symptoms indicate a recurrence. Simple, reassuring messages, such as “Cancer survivors get colds too,” can further normalize health concerns and reduce the tendency to overinterpret everyday bodily changes. Finally, clinicians can use discussions about symptoms as opportunities to address the psychological impact of fear. For instance, when considering a workup for a reported symptom, they might explore how often the AYA worries about similar issues and how these fears affect their daily life. These conversations can help clinicians identify AYAs who may benefit from additional psycho‐oncology support and ensure that fears are comprehensively addressed.

Although fear of recurrence has been studied extensively, the acute anxiety associated with upcoming surveillance scans and tests—termed scanxiety—has received less attention. 111 Historically, scanxiety was considered an inevitable aspect of cancer care, something beyond the scope of intervention. However, research indicates that scanxiety is not merely a fleeting discomfort; it can feel overwhelming and serve as a traumatic reminder of the cancer experience, perpetuating fear and anxiety over time. 112 Survivors often describe living in the shadow of a volcano, where the looming possibility of recurrence is ever‐present. 108 There is growing recognition that clinical communication can play a vital role in mitigating scanxiety. 112 Simple, proactive messages during follow‐up appointments can provide significant reassurance. For example, clinicians can preemptively address common worries by explaining that delays in clinic start times or extended waiting room periods do not typically indicate issues with scan results. Direct release of results to patients using electronic patient portals adds more complexity because asynchronously viewing results may contribute to anxiety, especially when findings are not clearly normal. Clinicians can engage patients in proactively planning how to manage communication (e.g., setting a predetermined date/time to review results by phone, telehealth, or in‐person even if they will be available in a patient portal ahead of this). AYA survivors may also benefit from receiving scan‐related information as soon as they enter the consultation room. The waiting room can be a particularly stressful environment, and delaying the provision of reassuring information—by starting the consultation with questions about recent health issues or performing clinical examinations—can exacerbate anxiety and hinder AYAs’ ability to fully engage in discussions about their health or concerns.

COMMUNICATION ABOUT CANCER SURVIVORSHIP CARE

All AYA cancer survivors will experience at least one health care transition from active oncology care to either primary care or specialized survivorship programs. 14 AYAs who were diagnosed and treated in pediatric settings also transition from pediatric to adult health care systems, which may coincide with or occur at a different time from the transition out of active oncology follow‐up. 14 International guidelines recommend that cancer survivors who were diagnosed as children or AYAs receive lifelong, specialized, long‐term follow‐up care (survivorship care) tailored to their risk of late effects. 16 , 113 AYA survivors who were treated in adult settings have even more variable recommendations for survivorship care and heterogeneous models of care. 15 Survivorship care implementation differs across care settings in the United States and internationally. 15 , 113 , 114 Regardless of the survivorship care model, communication should be guided by developmentally appropriate language and adapted to the needs of each AYA and family. However, the variability in AYA survivorship care implementation means communication about survivorship care is also highly variable.

Despite the importance of healthy lifestyles (e.g., control of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors) in mitigating risk of treatment‐related late effects, 115 , 116 there is limited research on communication strategies for discussing health risks and health behaviors among AYA survivors of childhood cancer. Communicating with AYAs about hypothetical future health risks is especially complex because AYAs may be more prone to risk‐taking behaviors and more focused on the present than the future. 17 , 18 Investing the time and effort to develop trust and emotional safety with AYAs helps promote open communication and may allow clinicians to engage AYAs in identifying the most salient health risks for them. 117

Although much attention has been placed on understanding what information needs to be given to childhood/AYA cancer survivors after treatment, very little has been written about how and how often clinicians should discuss this information with childhood/AYA cancer survivors and families. The only study to date that analyzed actual communication practices in AYA cancer survivorship video‐recorded 66 routine long‐term follow‐up visits at a large pediatric oncology clinic in Norway over a 2‐year period. 118 Those investigators observed that 71% of discussions about potential late effects were initiated by pediatric oncologists, and only 15% and 14% were initiated by AYA cancer survivors and their parents, respectively; whereas AYAs were more likely than clinicians to initiate discussions about the psychosocial effects of treatment. 118 This suggests a potential disconnect between clinicians’ and families’ priorities in these survivorship visits. The authors also identified wide variability between pediatric oncologists in terms of the type and volume of information provided to AYAs in their clinics, suggesting that physician communication style determined late effects–related communication practices rather than tailoring for patient/family communication preferences. 118 This leaves AYA cancer survivors and families vulnerable to receiving a one‐size‐fits‐all approach to survivorship communication that may be more reflective of their clinician’s preferences than their own.

There is an opportunity to discuss the risk of late effects as part of informed consent before starting cancer treatment. However, pediatric oncologists have expressed discomfort talking about future long‐term health risks when needing to urgently begin treatment, not wanting to overwhelm families or undermine hope. 119 This leads to variations in the extent to which patients and families are introduced to the concept of late effects before or during treatment. 83 , 120 Even when late effects are introduced early on, this information may not be retained given the volume of information conveyed at the same time. 66 Introducing the concept of survivorship care is essential to prepare AYA cancer survivors to take ownership of their care in the future, 17 and many AYAs wish they had received more information about late effects earlier on in their care. 121 Research is underway to develop communication tools to support these discussions earlier during cancer treatment rather than waiting for the advent of dedicated, long‐term follow‐up/survivorship clinics. 66 , 119

AYA survivors report high rates of unmet informational and supportive care needs related to sexual health and fertility. 122 , 123 Barriers to such discussions include patient and/or clinician discomfort with the topics, clinicians’ lack of knowledge and training, lack of time during clinic visits, and the presence of accompanying parents or partners who may overtly or inadvertently prevent discussions of sensitive or embarrassing topics. 124 Published guidelines exist for assessing sexual health and fertility concerns and to help guide clinical discussions and interventions, including for AYA cancer survivors. 28 , 125 , 126 All conversations should be developmentally appropriate, depending on survivors’ age, maturity, and readiness to talk about topics surrounding sexual health, fertility, and family building. For adolescents, it may be necessary to navigate issues surrounding confidentiality and parental consent. Conversations should be held periodically as a routine part of survivorship care to assess whether patients’ interests or concerns have changed. Finally, it is important to individualize communication and to be mindful of diverse cultural backgrounds, norms, and beliefs as well as AYAs’ self‐identified gender and sexual identities. Communication should be inclusive and respectful, and clinicians should be aware of the language they use, preconceived assumptions, and overall openness to and acceptance of diverse gender and sexual orientations. 126

AYA cancer survivors and parents have recognized the need for more supportive communication at transitions between phases of treatment, e.g., from active treatment to off‐treatment follow‐up, and to long‐term follow‐up/survivorship. 66 , 127 In focus groups with AYA cancer survivors, some AYAs felt that follow‐up care prevented them from moving forward with their cancer‐free adult lives, that medical screening tests were unnecessary if they were asymptomatic, and preferred to steer clear of care to avoid potentially distressing news about their health. 128 Other AYAs have suggested reframing survivorship care from cancer and risk prevention to normalcy, wellness, and health promotion to mitigate anxiety associated with future health risks. 127 Balancing AYAs’ preferences for wellness‐focused communication with the need to share difficult and potentially distressing news about future health risks is one reason why having a repertoire of communication skills allowing the clinician to be fully present and nimble in their approach is essential in this field.

Practice points

Acknowledge that past cancer treatments can affect future health. Frame survivorship care as focusing on what people need to know to stay healthy (e.g. “Some of the treatments you had can have effects that show up later on, so staying in touch with your care team helps us keep an eye on your health and catch anything early—this way, we can support you in staying as healthy as possible.”).

HOW MIGHT CLINICIAN COMMUNICATION AFFECT AYA CANCER SURVIVORS’ ENGAGEMENT IN CARE?

Several studies have proposed a link between effective patient/family‐centered communication and greater engagement in AYA cancer survivorship care, 23 , 129 , 130 making communication an appealing target for intervention. A qualitative analysis of parents of childhood cancer survivors suggested that trust in clinicians mediates the association between communication and engagement in follow‐up care. 130 Based on interviews with parents, authors theorized that positive communication experiences increase parental trust in clinicians, which, in turn, promotes adherence to recommendations and understanding of the importance of long‐term follow‐up care. 130 Another study in adult cancer survivors (including some AYAs treated in adult settings) showed that greater patient‐centered communication was associated with lower cancer information avoidance, which was also mediated by trust in clinicians. 131

There is a critical need for more culturally and linguistically diverse participants in cancer‐related communication research 132 and an opportunity to leverage community partnerships to involve underrepresented populations in the development of communication interventions to improve survivorship care. 133 One community‐engaged qualitative study of 27 Hispanic/Latino/a/e AYA childhood cancer survivors and 21 parents/caregivers suggested that involving the nuclear family in survivorship care could promote AYA engagement in their care. 134 Another community‐engaged qualitative study highlighted several factors that impede communication about cancer survivorship topics both within Hispanic/Latino AYA–parent dyads and between AYAs or parents and clinicians. 133 AYAs felt overwhelmed by medical information, parents were hesitant to ask questions of clinicians, and some AYAs found speaking with their parents about their past cancer challenging because of language barriers between the bilingual AYA (who was more comfortable with medical terms in English) and monolingual Spanish‐speaking parent. 133 Other studies suggest that health‐related communication within families (between AYA cancer survivors and parents) may support AYA engagement in follow‐up care. 135 Clinicians may facilitate conversations between AYAs and parents in the survivorship clinic setting to help transfer cancer‐related information from parent to AYA and improve AYA readiness to engage with care.

COMMUNICATION AS A THERAPEUTIC TOOL

As clinicians, the people we care for teach us to be present and accompany them through cancer treatment and survivorship. 136 Listening to and learning from the AYAs and families in our clinic allows us to personalize our care and communication. How we engage AYAs and their families as partners in discussions about cancer and how we relate to them as individuals with unique and changeable communication preferences affect the relationships we form and the care they receive.

Communication is a powerful therapeutic tool. Words can normalize and reassure, contribute to (or undermine) trust, and empower actions. Communication training helps teach clinicians how to harness the power of verbal and nonverbal communication to strengthen patient–family–clinician relationships. There is also a need for communication tools to be designed with and for AYAs and families to help them identify and express their needs, concerns, and questions. This is especially important for individuals with lower health literacy and non‐English–language preference, who encounter even more language and communication barriers in health care settings.

In writings and testimonials, AYA cancer survivors remind us that the transitions between the before cancer, during cancer, and after cancer versions of themselves are fluid and complicated. 30 , 31 Similarly, the phases of cancer treatment, posttreatment surveillance, and long‐term survivorship care are not discrete moments in time, nor are they experienced in the same way by each person. Given this complexity, we recommend a flexible communication approach that centers the needs and preferences of each AYA and family and a willingness to adapt in the months and years after cancer treatment.
