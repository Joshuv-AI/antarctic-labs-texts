Alginate oligosaccharides in the food industry: emerging functional roles, health benefits, and technological applications – Review
Authors: Eric Biney, Derrick Asare, Kit-Leong Cheong, Hai-Jing Zhong, Saiyi Zhong, Malairaj Sathuvan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) are low-molecular-weight fragments produced by depolymerizing alginate from brown seaweeds. Rich in guluronic and mannuronic residues, AOS show broad bioactivity, including antioxidant, prebiotic, immunomodulatory, and antimicrobial effects, supporting their inclusion in functional foods. In food systems, they act as stabilizers, thickeners, fat replacers, and encapsulation matrices, and they form edible films and coatings that enhance safety and extend shelf life, matching clean-label and sustainability goals. Despite strong potential, obstacles remain in scalable production, cost efficiency, structural characterization, and regulatory acceptance. This review synthesizes current evidence on links between structure and function, evaluates health benefits, and maps applications across food categories. It also outlines priorities for future work, including standardized manufacturing, robust clinical validation, and integrated process design, to realize AOS as bioactive and multifunctional ingredients in modern food systems.

Introduction

Polysaccharides called alginates are naturally occurring biopolymers predominantly derived from the cell walls of brown seaweeds such as Laminaria, Ascophyllum, Macrocystis, and Sargassum (Flores-Contreras et al., 2023; Fonseca-Barahona et al., 2025). These marine macroalgae represent renewable and sustainable resources, making alginates widely available, environmentally friendly, and suitable for large-scale utilization (Rahman et al., 2024). Structurally, alginate is composed of linear chains of β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and α-L-guluronic acid (G), which are arranged in homopolymeric (MM, GG) and heteropolymeric (MG) block structures (Román-guerrero et al., 2025; Van Rooyen et al., 2024). The ratio and sequence of M and G residues significantly influence the physicochemical properties of alginate, including gel strength, viscosity, and metal-ion binding capacity (Hurtado et al., 2022).

Low-molecular-weight derivatives of alginate, termed alginate oligosaccharides (AOS), can be produced via controlled enzymatic hydrolysis using alginate lyases or by acid hydrolysis (Kaur et al., 2024). Enzymatic methods are increasingly preferred, as they generate structurally defined AOS with preserved functional groups and enhanced bioactivity while reducing undesirable by-products (Chelliah et al., 2024). AOS have attracted growing attention due to their unique biological functions, such as antimicrobial, antioxidant, prebiotic, and immunomodulatory effects, as well as their technological potential in food formulations.

Low-molecular-weight carbohydrates known as alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) are created when alginate, a naturally occurring anionic polymer that is mostly isolated from brown seaweeds like Laminaria, Ascophyllum, and Macrocystis species, is carefully broken down. The linear chains of β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and α-L-guluronic acid (G) residues, organised in different block sequences, make up alginate. Alginate can be hydrolysed by enzymes or acids to produce AOS with unique structural characteristics and biological activity. These are primarily determined by the molecules' size and M/G ratio.

AOS are water soluble and absorb quickly, which makes them useful for a lot of biological and industrial uses (Pisani et al., 2024). AOS have received growing interest in recent years due to its multifuctional properties, which include antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and particularly prebiotic advantages (Pereira et al., 2025; Santacroce et al., 2024). These qualities make them good candidates for use in functional foods and nutraceuticals (Arshad et al., 2025; Pereira et al., 2025; Santacroce et al., 2024). Additionally, AOS display advantageous physicochemical properties that enable their application as food stabilizers, thickeners, and bioactives carriers in current food systems (Jönsson et al., 2020). As stated by (Świder et al., 2023; Zlatanović et al., 2019), the possisibilities for AOS to be used as long lasting, health-promoting food ingredients keep growing.

Significance of alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) in the food industry

Alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) are very promising for the food sector because they can be used as both bioactive ingredients and technological agents (Mrudulakumari Vasudevan et al., 2021).

Derived from the enzymatic or chemical breakdown of alginate, AOS combine health-enhancing properties with functional attributes that meet the evolving demands for clean-label, sustainable, and health-oriented food products (J. Li et al., 2018; J. Wan et al., 2020). One of the most valuable contributions of AOS lies in their biological activity (M. Liu et al., 2021). By encouraging the development of advantageous gut bacteria including Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, which improves immunological response, metabolic balance, and digestive health, AOS exhibits prebiotic benefits (H. Y. Li et al., 2021). Also, because they are antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant, they are good candidates for functional foods and nutraceuticals that are meant to prevent chronic diseases and promote overall health (Ghosh et al., 2022). From technological point of view, AOS help keep foods products stable and of high quality (Hassoun, Anusha Siddiqui, et al., 2024). As thickeners, stabilizers, emulsifiers, and encapsulating agents, they improve the mouthfeel, texture, and shelf life of many foods, including dairy products, drinks, baked good, and meat replacement (Alam, Majid, et al., 2025). AOS are also utilized in edible coatings and films, which can preserved fresh fruits and vegetables and ready-to-eat foods against moisture loss and microbiology contamination (Kocira et al., 2021). Also, using AOS help the business move towards using natural, plant-based, and long-lasting substance (Domokos-Szabolcsy et al., 2023). AOS are biopolymers that comes from the ocean. They are a renewable alternative to artificial additives that fits with environmental aims and people's desire for eco-friendly products (Sasounian et al., 2024). AOS is a useful instrument for innovation in current food systems because it can do many things (Shin et al., 2025).

Rationale for reviewing alginate oligosaccharides (AOS)

Alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) have attracted increases scientific and industrial interest in recent years due their varied biological activities and versatile functional roles in food systems (Domokos-Szabolcsy et al., 2023). AOS are type of bioactive chemical that comes from marine brown algae. They could help solve important problems in the food business, such as the need for health-promoting ingredients, clean-label formulations, and environmentally friendly food processing methods (Bürck et al., 2024; Menaa et al., 2021; Remya et al., 2022). Although there is more and more research on AOS, there is not enough of it that looks at their biological consequences, how they work, and how they might be used in technology (Hoonjan et al., 2018). Current work predominantly emphasises descrete elements, such as prebiotic benefits or antixiodant activity, without encompassing the comprehensive range of AOS functions in food science (Shin et al., 2025).

Preparation of AOS depolymerization of alginate

Physical and chemical methods

One traditional technique for depolymerising polysaccharides is chemical degradation (Cheng et al., 2024). Chemical methods for producing AOS have been thoroughly investigated. For alginate modification, the most widely employed chemical regents are hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and hydrochloric acid (HCl). Alginic acid and sodium alginate were depolymerised using aqueous HCl solutions with pH values between 2.8 and 4.5 and 0.1 and 1.0 M (Ariyo et al., 1998). AOS DP is significantly impacted by the hydrolytic reaction's parameters as well as the alginate's source composition. Alginate from Lessonia vadosa, which was collected in Punta Arenas in southern Chile, had different M/G ratios (0.21 to 1.69). A partial hydrolysis of the different alginates produced oligomer products, such as oligoMG, MAOS, and GAOS (Chand'ıa et al., 2016; Chandia et al., 2001). The intramolecular glycosidic cleavage of polyMG alginate at C-4 is dominated by the undissociated carboxyl groups at C-5 in weak acid, which act directly as proton donors (Holtan et al., 2006; Pawar & Edgar, 2012). Since there are no other contaminants and just H2O is produced as a byproduct, H2O2 depolymerisation of alginate is a more environmentally friendly method of producing AOS than HCl (Mhlongo et al., 2023). It is yet unknown how precisely H2O2 depolymerises alginate. It was believed that the C-1 protons from M or G residues were initially abstracted by hydroxyl radicals produced by H2O2, causing structural rearrangement and breakdown linkages in glycosidic alginate (J. Liu et al., 2019). AOS generation during the oxidative depolymerisation of alginate is influenced by reaction circumstances (Zimoch-Korzycka et al., 2021). For the first hour of the process, alginate degraded more quickly at temperatures above 40 °C when H2O2 was present (X. Li et al., 2010; Prodanovic et al., 2015). The alginate polymer's MW declined linearly over time at pH 5 to 7, and the initial alginate concentration had minimal impact (Madsen et al., 2021a; X. Wang et al., 2022).

Enzymatic method

An alternative to chemical hydrolysis for the same goal is enzymatic hydrolysis, which yields AOS. Unlike chemical hydrolysis, which produces large amounts of monosaccharides and unwanted harmful byproducts, this process doesn't require specialised equipment. α-agarase (EC 3.2.1.158) is one of the enzymes that hydrolyse red seaweed polysaccharide specifically to create AOS and COS (Ariga et al., 1987), β-agarase (EC 3.2.1.81) (Kazłowski et al., 2015), β-porphyranase (EC 3.2.1.178)(Ropartz et al., 2015), β-galactosidase (EC 3.2.1.23) (C. H. Lee et al., 2015), κ-carrageenase (EC 3.2.1.83) (Y. Sun et al., 2014), ι-carrageenase (EC 3.2.1.157)(Shen et al., 2017), λ-carrageenase (EC 3.2.1.162) (J. Li et al., 2014), cellulase (EC 3.2.1.4)(Duan et al., 2016), and pectinase (EC 3.2.1.15)(Kwon et al., 2016). Microorganisms from a variety of environments, such as soil, seawater, marine algae, marine molluscs, and the human gut, produce these enzymes. Polysaccharides serve as a carbon source for microorganisms that feed on marine algae in energy production. Consequently, maritime habitats are home to the majority of the microorganisms that produce specific enzymes needed for the hydrolysis of red seaweed polysaccharides. It is possible to isolate just a few number of agarolytic bacteria from terrestrial habitats. In earlier papers, the procedures for separating these microbes, purifying the enzymes, and assessing the enzymatic activity are compiled by (Chi et al., 2012). Enzymatic production of AOS has been described in numerous reports. Escherichia coli's recombinant-agarase (Aga 50 A) hydrolyzes Gracilaria spp. agar to produce more AOS than commercial agarose (N. Kim et al., 2018). The immobilized enzyme technique has been used in industrial applications to obtain oligosaccharides in batches in a continuous process (Nguyen et al., 2018; Pottratz et al., 2022; Urrutia et al., 2013). The advantages of immobilized enzymes include the ease of enzyme recovery from the reaction mixture, ease of controlling the de-polymerization product, rapid termination of the enzyme reaction, and feasibility for application in various engineering designs (Liese & Hilterhaus, 2013). The optimum method for creating AOS for the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetics industries is thought to be enzymatic hydrolysis (Cheong, Chen, et al., 2025). The need for hydrolytic enzymes is therefore growing quickly, which is propelling research into low-cost, highly selective, and high-activity enzymes (Abdel-Mageed, 2025; Mao et al., 2024). When AOS is produced using either of the above stated techniques, impurities or unwanted substances such monosaccharides, protein, acid, and furfural may be present (Kashyap et al., 2024). To produce food- or pharmaceutical-grade oligosaccharides and guarantee the maximum purity of the AOS, the reaction mixture must be purified (Zeng et al., 2023). The various applications of oligosaccharides in various fields determine the desired purity of oligosaccharides (X. Chen et al., 2021). The AOS purification procedures include ethanol precipitation (da Silva et al., 2012; Q. Yang et al., 2025), activated carbon adsorption (Y. Wang & Wang, 2021; Y. Wang & Wu, 2022), membrane separation (R. Ma et al., 2024), and gel filtration chromatography (Deng et al., 2021).

Low ethanol concentrations induce the precipitation of the enzyme in the reaction mixture, whereas the membrane separation process removes salts and other minute molecules (Moreno-González & Ottens, 2021). Nonetheless, the oligosaccharides remain inadequately purified after membrane separation, activated carbon adsorption, and ethanol precipitation. Gel filtration chromatography, however, produces pure oligosaccharides of differing molecular weights (Fan et al., 2020; Zimmermann et al., 2023). A series of AOS derived from Porphyra umbilicalis polysaccharides were purified through three successive Superdex 30 (26/60) gel filtration columns (Correc et al., 2011; C. H. Huang et al., 2021). To make very pure AOS, other size-exclusion columns, including Superdex peptide 10/300 GL, are used (Abe & Časar, 2025), Toyopearl HW-40C (Aoyama et al., 2018; M. Li et al., 2021), Bio-gel P-6 (Velasco Abadia et al., 2023), Bio-gel P-2 (Cheong et al., 2022), and Sephadex LH-20 (Tian et al., 2018), have also been successfully utilized.

A key quality factor for oligosaccharides that enhances their biological effects and facilitates commercial production is their purity (Asbury & Saville, 2025; Hao et al., 2024). High-purity oligosaccharides with equivalent degrees of polymerization are generated using gel filtration chromatography (Krishna Perumal et al., 2023; L. Zhang et al., 2022). But this method has some problems, such as being expensive and hard to copy on a wide scale (Miric et al., 2023). Membrane separation and precipitation can facilitate the large-scale production of oligosaccharide products; nevertheless, the resultant mixtures of oligosaccharides with varying molecular weights lack optimal purity (X. Ji et al., 2024; Lomartire & Gonçalves, 2023; Suzuki et al., 2021).

AOS modulates intestinal homeostasis via the regulation of gut microbiota

The gut microbiota comprises harmful and helpful microorganisms, and the equilibrium between them is essential for maintaining gut health. We investigated the impact of AOS on gut probiotics and pathogenic bacteria. AOS enhance gastrointestinal health through probiotic metabolites (Bidell et al., 2022). Probiotics make a lot of postbiotic metabolites, which are very crucial for keeping people healthy (C. Liu et al., 2023; Pelton, 2020). Some people think that vitamin K is an underappreciated factor that affects how gut microbial communities work (Ellis et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2022). B vitamins help in metabolism, signalling, and important microbial bioactivities. Vitamins C, E, and B2 are well-known antioxidants that affect the luminal redox balance (K. S. Hossain et al., 2022; Joshi et al., 2023). Most gut bacteria are capable of synthesising several B vitamins, including biotin, cobalamin, folates, nicotinic acid, pantothenic acid, pyridoxine, riboflavin, and thiamine (H. Wang et al., 2022; Y. Yang et al., 2024). Lactobacillus salivarius can make GSH, a strong antioxidant that may fix damage done to most cells by ROS (Yuan et al., 2022). A family of small peptides referred to as antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) is crucial to the innate immune system of gut health (Zong et al., 2020).

Bacteriocins (Fig. 1A) are small cationic peptides synthesised by specific probiotic lactic acid bacteria that induce pore formation to eliminate pathogenic cells (Anjana & Tiwari, 2022). Defensins are a type of cationic antimicrobial peptide that stops bacterial infections (Mhlongo et al., 2023; Pero et al., 2019; J. Wu et al., 2022) and are critical elements of innate immunity in gut health (Chung & Raffatellu, 2019; Erny et al., 2021). The principal bioactive metabolite of Saccharomyces boulardii is phenyllactic acid, a result of phenylalanine catabolism (Fu et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024). Phenyl lactic acid mitigates colitis induced by Salmonella Typhimurium through the modification and regulation of gut microbiota components, short-chain fatty acid production, and inflammatory processes (M. Lee et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2021). A prominent indicator of gastrointestinal problems and nutritional condition is volatile organic compounds (VOCs) (Lueno et al., 2022). Bacillus amyloliquefaciens and certain yeasts produce elevated concentrations of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) (Ngo et al., 2021). AOS has been reported to impact the most relevant lactic acid bacteria (Yudiati et al., 2021; Zhuge et al., 2020), yeasts (Chávez-Falcón et al., 2022), and other probiotics (Guleria et al., 2016) under simulated gastrointestinal conditions. Applications of alginate in the food industry

Alginate and its various derivatives have been utilized in the food industry for their unique thickening, gelling, film-forming, stabilising, and other colloidal properties (Parreidt et al., 2018). Gelling is essential for instant milk confections, jellies, pastry cream fillings, fruit pies, animal feed, and restructured fruit; thickening is advantageous for sauces, syrups, ice cream toppings, and similar applications (Palamthodi et al., 2021). The colloidal qualities are essential when incorporating sodium alginate into ice creamor employing propylene glycol alginate (PGA) to stabilise beer foam or suspend particulates in fruit beverages (Kumar et al., 2022). The incorporation of alginate enhances icings, rendering them non-sticky, and facilitates the effortless covering of baked items with plastic wrap (Chadha et al., 2022). According to Chadha et al. (2022), Alginate is beneficial for water-in-oil emulsions such as mayonnaise and salad dressing. The use of alginate to thicken them reduces the likelihood of their separation into distinct water and oil phases (Galogahi et al., 2025).

Alginate is added to ice cream to prevent ice crystals from forming during freezing, resulting in a smoother final product (Zagorska et al., 2022). This is particularly crucial when ice cream softens between the store and the freezer at home (Farooq et al., 2023; Huwaidi & Al-Tameemi, 2025). When huge ice crystals form in ice cream, it gives off an unwanted gritty feeling because there is not enough alginate or a similar stabiliser (Pawar & Edgar, 2012). A minimal amount of PGA used can result in stable, long-lasting beer froth (Jørgensen & Heick, 2021). As stated by (Al-Hatamleh et al., 2022) and (Castillo-Rodríguez et al., 2025), alginate was initially used as a gelling agent ingredient in the manufacturing of artificial cherries. Large drops of a flavored and coloured sodium alginate solution were allowed to fall into a calcium salt solution throughout this procedure (Malvano et al., 2022; Soteyome & Thedkwanchai, 2024). Because the cherry-flavored gels did not melt, they gained much popularity in baked goods (Soteyome & Thedkwanchai, 2024). Thanks to automated, continuous procedures based on similar principles, fruit substitutes are now readily available (Luzardo-Ocampo et al., 2021). Unlike gelatin jellies, which can be manufactured to soften and dissolve at body temperature, edible dessert jellies created from alginate gel do not melt and have a different, harder texture (P. Gupta et al., 2022). This is because different combinations of sodium alginate and calcium salts can be made to set to a gel at varying rates, contingent on how quickly the calcium salt dissolves (VERMA et al., 2023). By using a gel-retarder that reacts with the calcium before the alginate does, it is possible to postpone gel formation after all the ingredients have been combined (Laroche et al., 2009). This way, the alginate will not have access to calcium until the retarder has been completely consumed (P. Gupta et al., 2022; Yehia et al., 2023). The US Department of Agriculture authorised alginate as a binder for reformed or reorganised food items in 1986 (Anderson et al., 2015). Restructured foods can be made by joining and shaping animal portions to look like typical cuts of meat, such as streaks, meatloaves, roasts, and more (Jang & Lee, 2024; Susta et al., 2024). One can use a mix of lactic acid, calcium carbonate, sodium alginate powder, and calcium lactate instead of the binder (Quitério et al., 2021). When they use it, they make a calcium alginate gel that keeps the pieces of raw meat together (Nagamine et al., 2023; Peñaranda et al., 2025). This is common way to manufacture meatloaves for pets r people, such as chicken nuggets (Jaico et al., 2022). To make prawn or fish substitutes, yiu can use alginate, proteins such as soy protein concentration, and flavours in the sae way (Abbaspour et al., 2023; Lima et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2022). Each one is made with green manzanilla olives and an alginate-based paste that has the correct flavour in it (Adamiak & Sionkowska, 2023). Calcium aiginate coatings and films can also be used to keep frozen fish fresh (Martínez et al., 2018; Vlahova-Vangelova et al., 2021). Ihas ben shown that even when quickly frozen, the oils in fish, such as mackerel and herrings, can oxidise and become rancid (Sveinsdóttir et al., 2021; J. Wu et al., 2022). In accordance with other research fish that is frozen in calcium alginate jelly is safe from the air and doesn't go bad very quickly since it doesn't oxidise (Cheng et al., 2024; Russo et al., 2024). The fish and jelly are readily separated upon thawing (Fitri et al., 2022; Naberezhnykh et al., 2025). Additionally, the quality of meat is enhanced when calcium alginate films are applied to beef cuts prior to freezing (Rusman et al., 2024; X. Yang et al., 2021). In addition to the coating's ability to shield the meat from bacterial contamination, it appears that the meat's natural liquids are reabsorbed upon thawing (Ikram et al., 2025; X. Wang et al., 2022). If necessary, the calcium alginate coating is easily removed by dissolving it with sodium polyphosphate (Hurtado et al., 2022).

Functional properties of alginate as a food ingredient

As was previously mentioned, alginate's special thickening, gelling, stabilising, and film-forming biophysical qualities make it extremely beneficial in the food business (Kakuda et al., 2024). Furthermore, numerous biological processes that are extremely advantageous to human health are also connected to its distinct structure (U. M. Khan et al., 2021).

Alginates as encapsulation and immobilization agents

Achieving delayed release, stability, thermal protection, and suitable sensory profiles in the food industry would be difficult without employing microencapsulation and immobilization techniques (Mehta et al., 2022). Alginate can be utilized to fabricate gel-forming capsules by various advanced encapsulation methods, such as microfluidization, spray drying, spray cooling, and spray freezing (Zhuravlev et al., 2017). The food industry uses these microencapsulation products for a variety of purposes, including encapsulating volatile or reactive compounds like flavours, fats, and acidulants (Calderón-Oliver & Ponce-Alquicira, 2022). These technologies have been used for many various drugs delivery reasons, mot just in the food business (N. Kim et al., 2018). For example, it has beenshown that injecting type I diabetics with microencapsulated insulin-producing cell is effective (Montanucci et al., 2021). The food business uses alginate encapsulation and immobilization technologies for a number of things, such as making product more appealing, processing food, and making food work better (Mitropoulou et al., 2013). These immobilization technologies can boost the production, function, and reuse of the enzymes or cells that are trapped in them because they keep getting better (Karthik et al., 2021). Enzyme, amino acids, organic acids (like acetic acid), and alcohols are just a few example of te many bacterial metabolites that can be made utilizing immobilization or encapsulation or encapsulation techniques (Lopez-Gallego & Schmidt-Dannert, 2010; Marschner & Hamer, 2002; Ribeiro et al., 2011). Alginate is great for encapsulating enzyme or complete cells that would be harmed by harsher circumstances because it creates ionotropic gels on its own at low temperatures (A. Chaudhari et al., 2015). A new use for alginate in the food industry is to encapsulate live probiotic cells so that they can be delivered to the big intestine (Reque & Brandelli, 2021). Probiotics can change the microbiota and make the host healthier by colonizing or implanting themselves in a specific region of the host (Bezkorovainy, 2001; Sánchez et al., 2017). Research into the health advantages of probiotics is ongoing; nonetheless, it is clear that for probiotic bacterial to achieve optimal efficacy, the formulation of food must ensure the adequate distribution of viable cells to the large intestine (Nagpal et al., 2012; Sanders et al., 2010). In this uses, alginates help bacteria to stay alive while food is being stored and lower the death rate of microorganisms in the stomach and small intestine, which are not good for them (Mahmoud et al., 2020; Puscaselu et al., 2020). Also, encapsulation has been demonstrated to improve cell viability in a number of food processing methods, including freezing and freeze-drying (Eun et al., 2020; Rajam & Subramanian, 2022).

Alginate for food coating

When alginate dissolves in water and dries, it makes a clear, see-through film (Aderibigbe & Buyana, 2018). Alginate films have exceptional tensile strength and flexibility, possess oil impermeability, and are resistant to tearing (M. I. Khan et al., 2013). Alginate converts into an edible coating that imparts protective characteristics to food after being applied as an aqueous solution (Fabra et al., 2018). Alginate's capacity to produce films holds considerable potential in the functional food sector due to the ongoing trend to replace or reduce non-biodegradable or non-recyclable food packaging (Abdel Aziz et al., 2018; Metha et al., 2024; Priyadarshi et al., 2021). Alginate-based food coatings are simple to formulate, safe for application, and exhibit extended shelf life and excellent product stability (Raybaudi-Massilia et al., 2008; Song et al., 2011). But alginate gels are permeable to both water and oxygen because they have a porous structure (Barbetta et al., 2009; Demianenko et al., 2016; Martins et al., 2015). Alginate can gel at ambient temperature through ion exchange, which is not the case with agar and carrageenan (L. Li et al., 2019). This property is important because it allows food coatings to use antimicrobial compounds to protect meat and vegetables from microbial surface degradation (Sánchez-Ortega et al., 2014; Valencia-Chamorro et al., 2011). The cold manufacture of the alginate gel coating for marine items protest both the food and the antibacterial agents from damage (Mei et al., 2020; Neetoo et al., 2010). Alginate caotings can help foods like lettuce, freshly cut apples, and melon last longer, stay crispsy and fresh, and stop vitamin C from breaking down (Armghan Khalid et al., 2022; Perera et al., 2021).

Uses alginate lyases to cleave the alginate chains via β-elimination, producing unsaturated oligosaccharides

Alginate is a linear acidic polymer made up of β-D-mannuronate (M) and α-L-guluronate (G) (Russo et al., 2024). Alginate and its degradation products, referred to as alginate oligosaccharides, exhibit a diverse array of biological features, encompassing antibacterial, anticancer, and antioxidant activities (Gomez-Zavaglia et al., 2019). Because of this, alginate and its oligosaccharides are particularly useful in cooking, medicine, and farming (Aida et al., 2010). β-elimination reaction can be used by alginate lyase to break down alginate into alginate oligosaccharides (Xia et al., 2024). It is essential for both the extensive use of brown algae and the recycling of marine carbon (Buck-Wiese et al., 2017). We can learn more about how alginate lyase works as a catalyst by making its structural features clearer (Wargacki et al., 2012). As structural analysis tools have advanced, more and more alginate lyases have been structurally characterised. Therefore, summarising and discussing the most recent discoveries is crucial and beneficial (Arntzen et al., 2021; H. Sun et al., 2022; Xinxin He et al., 2022).

Extraction and structural characteristics

Sources of alginate

Alginate, the natural precursor of alginate oligosaccharides (AOS), is largely derived from the cell walls of brown seaweeds (Phaeophyceae) (Riseh et al., 2022). These seaweeds are plentiful in marine ecosystems and function as a renewable biomass for alginate production (Osman et al., 2023). The kind and quality of alginate depend on the type of seaweed, where it comes from, and when it was harvested (Twigg et al., 2024). Here are some of the most important types of seaweed that make alginate.

Methods of Depolymerization of alginate into alginate oligosaccharides (AOS)

To make alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) from alginate, which is a polysaccharide with a high molecular weight, depolymerisation must happen. This procedure cuts the long alginate chains into shorter, useful oligosaccharide units (Park et al., 2021; Subin et al., 2017). The two most common ways to do this are enzymatic hydrolysis and acid hydrolysis (Cheong, Liu, et al., 2025; G. Li et al., 2025). Different structural and functional properties of AOS produced by each approach affect their biological activity and how well they work in food (Saad et al., 2023). It is very crucial to know how depolymerisation works in order to figure out how well AOS works for food applications in terms of quality, structure, and function (M. Mohammed et al., 2024). Enzymatic hydrolysis is the best way to make bioactive, well-defined AOS for health-focused and high-value food products. Acid hydrolysis is still a good choice for making large amounts of AOS when cost and scalability are the most important things (Climent Barba et al., 2022; Mora & Toldrá, 2023). Research is still going on into hybrid and green depolymerisation technologies, which are helping to close the gap between efficiency, sustainability, and functional performance (Lobel et al., 2024; Lozano & García-Verdugo, 2023).

Structural features of alginate oligosaccharides (AOS): Mannuronic/Guluronic acid composition and molecular weight

The structural characteristics of alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) are essential factors influencing their biological activity, physicochemical qualities, and functional roles in food systems (M. Liu et al., 2024). The properties are primarily dictated by the configuration and content of mannuronic acid (M) and guluronic acid (G) units, in conjunction with the molecular weight (MW) or degree of polymerization (DP) of the resultant oligosaccharides (Boucelkha et al., 2017; Jiao et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2018; Pritchard et al., 2023).

The composition of Mannuronic and Guluronic Acid There are two types of uronic acid residues in alginate: β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and α-L-guluronic acid (G) (Gao et al., 2021; Madsen et al., 2021b). In three different ways, these monomers are grouped into blocks: Homopolymeric M-blocks (MMMM), Homopolymeric G-blocks (GGGG…), and Heteropolymeric MG-blocks (MGMGMG) (Thanh et al., 2021).

Molecular weight (MW) and degree of polymerization (DP)

The molecular weight of AOS typically fall between from several hundred to a few thousand Daltons, corresponding to 2 to 25 monomer units (degree of polymerization) (Askar et al., 2017). Low-Molecular-Weight AOS (DP 2–10) and High-water solubility and bioavailability. Better suited for prebiotic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory roles in functional foods. Easily fermentable by gut microbiota. Medium-to-High-MW AOS (DP 10–25): Retain some of the gelling or thickening behavior of alginate (Pizzi, 2021; Szydłowska & Sionek, 2023). Useful in texture modification, film formation, and encapsulation. It might not be as bioavailable, but it might work better for structural uses (Chaudhary et al., 2021; Zabot et al., 2022).

Biological activities of AOS relevant to food applications

Alginate oligosaccharides (AOS), derived from the depolymerisation of brown seaweed alginate, demonstrate several biological activities pertinent to food science and technology (G. Huang et al., 2019; Lobel et al., 2024). Their low molecular weight, biocompatibility, and functional groups let them interact with biological systems and food matrices, which is good for your health and makes food safer, more stable, and more useful (Edo et al., 2025). This section looks at the main biological actions of AOS that make them useful for making functional foods, nutraceuticals, and natural food preservatives (Faienza et al., 2024; Gul et al., 2016).

Antioxidant activity

AOS has strong antioxidant properties since it can neutralise free radicals: Reducing oxidative stress in living things and food by getting rid of reactive oxygen species like superoxide and hydroxyl radicals (Zahra et al., 2021; Zandi & Schnug, 2022). Metal chelation is the process of binding pro-oxidant metal ions, such Fe2+ and Cu2+, to halt Fenton-type reactions that speed up the oxidation of lipids and proteins (Cuajungco et al., 2000; Gulcin & Alwasel, 2022; Sears, 2013). It keeps meats, oils, and processed goods fresh for longer and keeps their nutritional content (Fadiji et al., 2023; Teshome et al., 2022).

Prebiotic activity

AOS are dietary fibers that can't be broken down by the body. They help good gut flora, such Bifidobacteria and Lactobacillus, grow more quickly (Antonino et al., 2024; Gómez-Gaete et al., 2024; Olvera-Aguirre et al., 2023). Fermentability of AOS occurs in the colon, yielding short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate, which improve gut health and immunological function (Šket et al., 2017; Zhu La et al., 2023).

Microbiome modulation: AOs help balance the populations of bacteria in the gut, which can help reduce inflammation and enhance nutrient absorption.

Application: in Food (Rajoka et al., 2021). Added to functional drinks, dairy products, and foods high in fiber that are good for digestive health (Al-Quwaie et al., 2023; Fakhri et al., 2023).

Anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activity

AOS regulates immunological responses by inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (H. J. Kim et al., 2023; Moni et al., 2022; Tuoheti et al., 2021) and turning on anti-inflammatory pathways in gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) (Brandtzaeg, 2009; Jain et al., 2023; Sajdel-Sulkowska, 2019). This helps lower chronic inflammation, boost the function of the intestinal barrier, and keep the immune system in balance (De Medina et al., 2014; Hou et al., 2020). Application in Food: Used in nutraceuticals and functional foods that help people with illnesses that cause inflammation, like metabolic syndrome and IBD (Cheong et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2020).

Antimicrobial activity

Alginate oligosaccharides exhibit antibacterial properties against several foodborne pathogens, including Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenes, and Salmonella spp. (Asadpoor et al., 2021; Yari et al., 2024). Mechanism: Disruption of microbial membranes and blocking of bacterial enzyme systems (Lade et al., 2014). When coupled with, it has a synergistic effect on other natural antimicrobials (e.g., essential oils), AOS enhance preservation efficacy (C. H. Huang et al., 2021). Application in Foods acts as a natural preservative in fresh produce coatings, minimally processed foods, and antimicrobial packaging (Díaz-Montes & Castro-Muñoz, 2021; Kontominas, 2020).

Anti-obesity and anti-diabetic effects (emerging)

Recent studies suggest that Alginate oligosaccharides reduce lipid accumulation in adipocytes (Karadeniz & Kim, 2014; Pan et al., 2021). Enhance insulin sensitivity and modulate blood glucose levels (Frost & Olson, 2011). These effects are believed to result from gut microbiota modulation and inhibition of lipid absorption (Schoeler & Caesar, 2019). Application in Food: Potential inclusion in diet-specific functional foods targeting weight management and glycemic control (X. Li et al., 2022).

Technological applications of alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) in the food industry

Alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) are useful in a wide range of food processing and preservation applications due to their technological qualities and array of biological activities (Tusi et al., 2011; Yu & Yoshikuni, 2025). Their physicochemical adaptability, biodegradability, and GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) designation support their use in the production of functional, sustainable, and clean-label foods (Choi et al., 2024; Roy et al., 2023). The most important technological applications of AOS in various food industry sectors are described in this section (Hassoun, Jagtap, et al., 2024; Ranjbari et al., 2025).

Functional food ingredient

AOS can be incorporated as a functional ingredient to improve the nutritional profile, texture, and health-promoting effects of foods (Benjamens et al., 2020; Saini et al., 2022).

Applications: As dietary fibers to enhance gut health, as fat replacers in low-calorie foods and dressings, to improve water retention, mouthfeel, and texture in bakery, dairy, and meat products, as stabilizers in plant-based milk and smoothies (Jung et al., 2024).

Edible films and coatings

AOS can be utilized to make biodegradable edible coatings and active packaging systems that keep food from going bad (D. Gupta et al., 2024; Jahangiri et al., 2024).

Applications: As coating agents for fruits, vegetables, cheeses, and meats, combined with essential oils or plant extracts for enhanced antimicrobial/antioxidant effects, used in multilayered films for packaging (Jamróz & Kopel, 2020).

Encapsulation and delivery systems

AOS act as encapsulation matrices for bioactive substances because of their gel-forming and binding capabilities, which allow for targeted delivery and controlled release (Lopez-Hortas et al., 2021). Probiotics, vitamins, enzymes, and flavours can all be microencapsulated to improve the stability of functional components in fortified beverages and powders and to safeguard delicate substances during processing and digestion (Tripathy et al., 2021).

Texturizer and rheology modifier

AOS contribute to the viscosity, gel strength, and emulsion stability in various food formulations (Padial-Domínguez et al., 2020).

Applications: As rheology modifiers in soups, sauces, and dairy alternatives, to improve creaminess and stability in yogurt and ice cream (Soukoulis et al., 2014).

Prebiotic functional additive

Prebiotic functional additives, which are substances that can't be digested, help good gut microorganisms grow and/or become more active, which is good for the host's health (J. Ji et al., 2023). Due to the rising interest in gut microbiota and its impact on health and disease, prebiotics are progressively incorporated into food formulations to enhance gastrointestinal health, strengthen immunity, and reduce the risk of chronic diseases (J. Ji et al., 2023; Y. Liu et al., 2022).

Prebiotics are typically grouped as a subset of dietary fibers that reach the colon without being broken down by the upper gastrointestinal system (Meldrum & Yakubov, 2024). Once they enter the colon, they serve as a substrate for beneficial bacteria, particularly Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria (Matera, 2024). Examples of common prebiotic substances include resistant starch, cellulose, galactooligosaccharides (GOS), inulin, alginate oligosaccharides (AOS), and xylooligosaccharides (XOS) (Cheong, Sabir, et al., 2025; C. Huang et al., 2022; M. Wang & Cheong, 2023).

Applications: Prebiotic functional ingredients are used extensively in functional dairy products like yogurt and kefir, bakery goods, infant formula, beverages, snack bars, cereals, and symbiotic foods that combine probiotics and prebiotics (H. Qin et al., 2022). In addition to increasing their nutritional value, their use improves the texture, mouthfeel, and shelf life of food products (Zahed & Esmaeilzadeh Kenari, 2025).

Safety and regulatory considerations of alginate oligosaccharides (AOS)

For the successful integration of alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) into food systems, their safety profile and regulatory status must be clearly established (Abril et al., 2024; Hernandez-Jerez et al., 2024). As food manufacturers and consumers increasingly seek natural and functional ingredients, regulatory bodies require that such compounds meet stringent safety and quality standards (Abdel-rahman et al., 2011; Bailey, 2020; Trienekens & Zuurbier, 2008). This section discusses the toxicological safety, regulatory approvals, and labeling implications of AOS in food applications (Hernandez-Jerez et al., 2024; Nascimento & Barros, 2025).

General safety

AOS are derived from alginate, a polysaccharide that is widely recognized as safe for consumption(Adimassu et al., 2025). Alginate and its oligosaccharides are non-toxic, non-mutagenic, and non-carcinogenic when consumed in typical dietary amounts (Carvalho et al., 2024). Numerous in vivo and in vitro studies report no adverse effects of AOS on organ systems, hematology, or metabolism, even at high doses (Rhazouani et al., 2021).

Digestibility and tolerance

AOS are non-digestible in the upper gastrointestinal tract, reaching the colon where they serve as prebiotic substrates (Fernández et al., 2015; Rhazouani et al., 2021). No significant gastrointestinal discomfort has been reported in human or animal studies at practical consumption levels (Guyonnet et al., 2013; Prasad & Tyagi, 2015).

GRAS status

Sodium alginate and alginic acid are listed as Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) under CFR 21 §184.1724, While AOS themselves may not be individually listed, they are generally considered safe as they are hydrolyzed forms of GRAS-listed alginates (Akshaya & Nathanael, 2024; Y. Qin et al., 2020; Sosnik, 2014).

Toxicological data and maximum use levels

No Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) has been established for AOS, indicating a low risk of toxicity (Dal’ Bó et al., 2022; Z. Liu et al., 2017). In food applications, use levels typically range from 0.1 % to 3 % w/w, depending on the product type and desired functionality (Alam, Kaur, et al., 2025). Long-term studies in animals have shown no adverse effects even at dosages up to 2 g/kg body weight/day (Salsabila et al., 2025). Alginate oligosaccharides are considered safe, non-toxic, and well-tolerated based on current scientific evidence and their derivation from GRAS-approved alginate (Benalaya et al., 2024). Even though rules for AOS as new food ingredients or functional parts may be different in different parts of the world, their natural origin, clean-label appeal, and many uses are helping them become more popular in food items (Alam, Kaur, et al., 2025; Chauhan & Rao, 2024). It is important for manufacturers to know about local rules for new ingredients, especially when they make health or prebiotic claims, so that they can follow the law and gain customers' trust (Sanders et al., 2011).

Challenges and future perspectives of alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) in the food industry

Even though more people are interested in using alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) as multifunctional ingredients in food systems, there are still certain problems that make it hard to fully use and sell them (Amorim et al., 2024; Watts et al., 2011). But these limits also create good chances for new ideas, multidisciplinary research, and industrial growth in the future (Mavrogeni et al., 2023). This part talks about the main problems that AOS applications in food science are encountering and looks at the ways they could be more useful and have a bigger effect in the future (Adrian et al., 2019).

Challenges

High production cost and limited commercial availability

The best way to make high-purity, bioactive AOS is through enzymatic hydrolysis, which is expensive because it requires finding, purifying, and controlling the enzymes (König et al., 2022). There is still not enough industrial-scale production, which means that supply chains are limited and costs are high (Zainol et al., 2023).

Lack of standardization

varying AOS products have varying molecular weights, M/G compositions, and block structures, which makes their biological efficacy and functional performance inconsistent (Hossain et al., 2024). To make sure that each batch is the same and that products can be traced back to their source, standardized standards for manufacture and characterization are essential (Doulgkeroglou et al., 2020).

Regulatory and legal uncertainty

Alginate is broadly accepted, however AOS may need further regulatory assessment as a new component when used for health or functional claims (Cherry et al., 2019). Complexities in global food regulations can delay approval timelines and discourage investment (Garton et al., 2021; Margulis, 2013; Schram & Townsend, 2021).

Limited clinical and Long-term human studies

Much of the current evidence on AOS is based on in vitro and animal models (B. Wu et al., 2011). There is a lack of robust human clinical trials to support specific health claims (e.g., prebiotic, anti-inflammatory effects) (Vries et al., 2022). Without validated human data, consumer trust and regulatory approval for health-positioned products remain limited (Malo et al., 2023).

Stability and compatibility in complex food matrices

AOS may interact with proteins, polyphenols, and other bioactive compounds, potentially affecting bioavailability, texture, or product stability (Cao et al., 2021; Pateiro et al., 2021). Optimization is needed for their functional integration into diverse food formulations (Ayele et al., 2022; S. Sharma et al., 2021).

Future perspectives

As a potential functional food for anti-obesity and other metabolic diseases

Obesity is a serious global health concern that increases the risk of contracting other chronic diseases (Egger & Dixon, 2014; Koehlmoos et al., 2011; Lin & Li, 2021; Mohajan & Mohajan, 2023). Obesity can be caused by a variety of things, including poor nutrition, inactivity, and drinking alcohol (Tarantino et al., 2022). Approximately 40 % of people globally were overweight in 2014, and 13 % were obese. WHO (2016) (Adeloye et al., 2021). Obesity is a common metabolic condition that has become a major global health problem due to its increasing prevalence and the associated health consequences (Górczyńska-Kosiorz et al., 2024). In Japan, certain oligosaccharides were designated as “foods for specified health use” (FOSHU) when the concept of functional foods was first introduced in 1991 (Z. F. Ma et al., 2025). These days, the demand for particular kinds of dietary carbohydrates has increased due to modern consumers' growing health consciousness (Clemente-Suárez et al., 2022). However, new research has examined a range of medications derived from marine sources, including fucoxanthin from brown algae, that support anti-obesity effects (Din & Alayudin, 2022; Yadav et al., 2024). Additionally, several earlier. Studies revealed that diets containing extracts from brown seaweeds had a beneficial anti-obesity impact (M. A. Abbas et al., 2022). According to that research, meals supplemented with seaweed extracts have an inhibitory effect on adipocyte lipogenesis, lower levels of blood glucose, insulin, total cholesterol, and triacylglycerol, and particularly lower body weight (Chaves Filho et al., 2022; H. G. Lee et al., 2021). The water-soluble, non-toxic nature of AOS and its derivatives suggests that they could be useful biomaterials for treating a range of metabolic diseases, including diabetes and obesity (M. Abbas, 2021; Ye et al., 2024). It is well recognized that alginate plays a significant role in food as dietary fiber, but little is known about its additional uses, including as a prebiotic and in preventing metabolic diseases (Ahmad et al., 2024; Bamigbade et al., 2025). Additionally, a multi-omics method must be used to investigate its mechanism of functions (C. Chen et al., 2023; Chu et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2022).

As a promising cancer drug

Cancer is a serious medical problem that needs effective treatment with fewer side effects (Debela et al., 2021; Rasool et al., 2022). Marine algae have recently been utilized as potential anticancer drugs, despite the heated debate surrounding the use of polysaccharides in cancer treatments (Dai et al., 2022; A. S. A. Mohammed et al., 2021; R. Sharma et al., 2023). Although research is still in its infancy, the anticancer properties of several polysaccharides found in marine species have been evaluated (Guo et al., 2022; Nova et al., 2024). Some of these studies have been extensively carried out both in vitro and in vivo (Bakun et al., 2021; Pai et al., 2025). Thanks to the rapid development of next-generation sequencing (NGS), liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC × MS/MS), and multi-omics approaches, it is now much easier and faster to identify more toxins and predictive functions with bioinformatics pipelines, which opens the door for the development of novel drugs (Halappanavar et al., 2021; Mok et al., 2024; Moukheiber et al., 2022).

As promising neurological diseases target treatment

These days, metabolic disorders and brain illnesses are major concerns for human health (X. Wang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023). AOS and its derivatives enhanced neuroinflammation inhibition and microglial β-amyloid phagocytosis (Unzeta et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2023). The findings according to (Shoaib et al., 2023) indicate that AOS and its derivatives may have potential as a nutraceutical or therapeutic agent for neurodegenerative diseases, particularly Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a neurodegenerative disease that is fatal and also known as Lou Gehrig's disease.

Regulatory considerations for AOS in the food industry

Although alginates are widely recognized as safe and approved for use as food additives (E400–E404 in the European Union; Generally Recognized as Safe, GRAS, by the U.S. FDA), specific fractions of alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) intended for use as functional foods or nutraceuticals may require additional regulatory approval. For example, under EU law, certain AOS preparations may fall within the “novel food” category and thus require safety dossiers addressing toxicology, allergenicity, and metabolic fate. In Japan, the Food for Specified Health Uses (FOSHU) framework already allows oligosaccharides with demonstrated health benefits to be marketed as functional ingredients, which provides a precedent for AOS. However, differences in regulatory approaches across jurisdictions highlight the importance of internationally harmonized safety assessments, standardized characterization of degree of polymerization (DP), and validated bioactivity claims. Establishing such standards will be critical to facilitate global commercialization of AOS-based functional foods.

Consumer acceptance and market translation

Beyond regulatory clearance, consumer perception will strongly influence the market success of AOS-containing foods. Current trends show that consumers are increasingly receptive to natural, plant-based, and marine-derived ingredients, especially when positioned within the “clean label” and “sustainable nutrition” narratives. However, awareness of seaweed-derived ingredients remains relatively low outside East Asia, and educational strategies will be needed to communicate both safety and health benefits. Sustainability of seaweed harvesting and processing may provide an additional positive marketing angle, but issues of taste, odor, and texture modification in food applications must be carefully managed. Integrating sensory optimization with health communication could accelerate consumer adoption of AOS in the global food sector.

Conclusion

Alginate oligosaccharides (AOS), low-molecular-weight derivatives of brown seaweed alginate, are gaining recognition as functional bioactives and revolutionary agents in food science and technology. This review has shown that AOS fulfil a distinct dual function, they act as physiologically active agents that modulate gut microbiota, reduce oxidative stress, and suppress pathogens, while simultaneously serving as adaptable technological enhancers in food systems. The collaboration between health promotion and technical performance is a unique invention that differentiates AOS from traditional polysaccharides, placing them at the convergence of biomedicine, food innovation, and sustainable bioprocessing. A significant innovation highlighted here is the circular economy potential of AOS. They can be sustainably sourced from marine biomass and seaweed processing byproducts, transforming underutilised resources into high-value, health-enhancing substances. Their interoperability with next-generation food items, such as plant-based alternatives, personalised nutrition platforms, and clean-label formulations, underscores their significance in influencing the future of functional foods. AOS exhibit traceable structural diversity and adjustable bioactivities, presenting novel opportunities for precision food design through molecular-level customisation.

Notwithstanding this assurance, considerable gaps in understanding and application persist. Present issues encompass the creation of economical, environmentally sustainable production platforms, the establishment of standardized characterization methodologies for regulatory adherence, and the generation of extensive human clinical data to prove efficacy. Addressing these issues necessitates transdisciplinary collaboration among food chemistry, biotechnology, microbiome science, and regulatory policy. The innovation of AOS resides in their potential to instigate a paradigm change in food systems from traditional additives to bioactive, multifunctional, and sustainable ingredients. Through the integration of advanced enzymatic bioprocessing, nanostructured delivery technologies, and systems-level nutritional research, AOS has the potential to transform the formulation, preservation, and fortification of foods. Ultimately, their trajectory indicates that AOS will improve food quality and consumer health while promoting the global shift towards sustainable, circular, and health-focused food economies in the 21st century.
