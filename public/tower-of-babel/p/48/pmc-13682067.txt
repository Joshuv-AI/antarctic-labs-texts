Physician perspectives on Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) in primary care: a cross-sectional study in Israel
Authors: Ilan Yehoshua, Dor Atias, Taly Birman, Alon Bar El, Limor Boker Faran, Miri Mizrahi Reuveni, Bar Cohen, Limor Adler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoint-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) offers significant potential to enhance diagnostic certainty and therapeutic decision-making in primary care settings. Despite widespread conceptual endorsement and the introduction of training programs, real-world adoption remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate attitudes, clinical practice patterns, and barriers to integration among Primary Care Physicians (PCPs) who received formal POCUS training within a large Israeli healthcare organization.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among PCPs in one Healthcare Maintenance Organization in Israel who had previously completed POCUS training. Data were collected via an electronic questionnaire assessing specific modules used, device types, satisfaction levels, and perceived barriers. Respondents were categorized as active, former, or never users, and statistical analyses were performed to identify correlations among device type, usage, and satisfaction.ResultsOf 341 eligible PCPs, 126 completed the survey (37% response rate). The majority (88%) were active users. The most frequently utilized applications were lung (93%), abdominal (79%), and musculoskeletal (55%) assessments, with a specific focus on lung parenchyma and urinary pathologies. Active users reported high levels of diagnostic confidence (84%) and believed POCUS improved treatment (77%). The primary barriers cited were a lack of time (60%), a lack of experience (50%), a lack of confidence (39%), and insufficient compensation (32%).ConclusionsTrained PCPs perceive POCUS as a valuable tool that enhances diagnosis and treatment. However, barriers regarding time constraints, confidence, and experience persist despite formal training. Successful integration of POCUS in primary care requires addressing these workflow-specific challenges and selecting devices that align with the unique needs of ambulatory practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03476-2.

Background

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is the bedside use of ultrasound imaging performed and interpreted by the treating clinician to enhance the physical examination [1, 2]. This approach enables rapid diagnostic assessment, improves procedural safety, and facilitates immediate therapeutic decision-making across a wide range of clinical scenarios [3, 4]. It was first introduced in Europe and the United States in the 1970s, mainly in emergency departments. The first official emergency POCUS use guidelines were published by the American College of Emergency Physicians in 2001 [5], after the organization first stated its support for this practice in the previous decade [6].

The adoption of POCUS holds particular promise within the primary care medicine setting. Primary Care Physicians (PCPs) manage a broad spectrum of conditions, from acute abdominal pain and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) concerns to complex chronic diseases like heart failure [7]. In this environment, POCUS offers PCPs an invaluable tool to increase diagnostic confidence at the initial point of contact [8]. By providing real-time, objective clinical data, POCUS reduces diagnostic ambiguity, decreases the reliance on delayed external imaging referrals, and streamlines patient management. Consequently, integrating POCUS into clinical practice has the potential to increase clinical confidence and physician autonomy, reduce stress and PCP burnout, and enhance overall professional satisfaction [7, 9, 10].

Specific applications of POCUS in the ambulatory setting include focused lung assessment for pneumonia or congestive heart failure, abdominal examination for gallstones or ascites, and musculoskeletal evaluation for abscesses, cysts, fractures, tendon tears, or effusions [8, 11]. Studies show that primary care POCUS use increases diagnostic certainty, changes management plans, reduces referrals and imaging studies, and decreases overall costs [7, 12–14].

The advantages of using POCUS in primary care settings include a handy, safe, non-radiating diagnostic modality that is highly useful for specific diagnoses [15]. For patients, studies found that it improves satisfaction and confidence in care providers [16]. Consequently, POCUS training has been rapidly integrated into Family Medicine residency programs and into dedicated continuing medical education courses designed for practicing PCPs; In 2016, the American Academy of Family Physicians recommended introducing POCUS into residency curricula [17–19]. In Israel, it was implemented in the curricula of family medicine residency in 2023 [20]. Since 2024, the training requirements for Israeli Family Medicine residents mandate attendance at a formal POCUS course during their first year of residency, followed by completion of at least 30 POCUS cases across a predefined list of clinical indications (Fig. 1).

While POCUS has been implemented and PCPs are often trained in its use, its real-world use in primary care settings has lagged significantly behind its conceptual endorsement. Studies show that less than 10% of PCPs use POCUS in their daily practice [13, 21]. Barriers to POCUS use include a lack of formal training, inadequate equipment and image storage solutions, a lack of confidence, and a lack of formal guidelines [17, 22–26].

The discrepancy between the potential benefits of POCUS and its underutilization in clinical practice warrants investigation into the specific barriers influencing its adoption among trained users. This study aimed to evaluate the attitudes of PCPs who underwent POCUS training within one Israeli Healthcare Organization. Additionally, we sought to characterize patterns of POCUS use and identify the principal reasons for its integration or non-integration into clinical routines.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted among PCPs trained in POCUS within Maccabi Healthcare Services (MHS). MHS, the second-largest healthcare maintenance organization in Israel, provides coverage for approximately 2.9 million residents and employs around 1,500 PCPs. MHS initiated POCUS integration into primary care in 2018, followed by the launch of structured PCP training courses in 2020. The training in MHS is structured across four training days, each dedicated to a specific clinical domain: cardiovascular, abdominal and deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary, and musculoskeletal applications. Prior to each practical session, participants are required to complete a preparatory at-home learning module covering the relevant theoretical foundations and specific pathologies. The hands-on training is primarily conducted using General Electric Venue Go ultrasound systems, although some participants utilize personal handheld devices, including Butterfly, Vscan, and Lumify models. The training is available for residents and specialists in Family Medicine. To promote adoption, MHS established a financial incentive, offering all trained PCPs an additional payment equivalent to seven euros (€7) per documented use. In addition, device distribution was determined by the organization’s centralized procurement tenders. Employed physicians received POCUS devices (mostly the Butterfly IQ) at no cost, while independent physicians were offered the same specific device at a significantly discounted rate through the organization’s purchasing agreement. Other device models (e.g., Lumify, Venue Go) were allocated directly to specific units such as home care or urgent care centers. Consequently, the prevalence of specific device types in this study reflects institutional availability and financial subsidies rather than individual physician preference.

All trained PCPs received an email invitation to complete the study’s questionnaire. The questionnaire was administered via the MHS internal survey platform. Multiple reminder emails were disseminated to maximize the response rate. The MHS ethics committee approved this study (0053-22-MHS). Participation in the study was voluntary. Informed consent was obtained electronically at the beginning of the questionnaire.

The questionnaire

The Hebrew-language questionnaire evaluated multiple domains of POCUS integration. The questionnaire was developed collaboratively by the study researchers and the Research Department of MHS. First, a targeted literature review was conducted to identify key domains related to POCUS use, barriers, and satisfaction in primary care settings. Based on this, an initial draft of the questionnaire was created. The draft was reviewed by two experts in Family Medicine and clinical ultrasonography to establish content and face validity. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus, resulting in the final instrument. Key assessment areas included usage patterns (specific modules, device types, and clinical scenarios), user experience (satisfaction levels, training quality, and barriers to adoption), and perceived clinical impact (reductions in ER visits and formal imaging). Additionally, participants were asked about their likelihood of recommending POCUS to peers. Demographic data, including age and gender, were collected alongside self-reported usage history. An English version of the questionnaire is available in the Supplementary Material.

Statistical analysis

Survey responses were summarized overall and stratified by self-reported POCUS adoption status: active users (used at least one clinical module), former users (stopped using POCUS), or non-adopters (trained but did not initiate use). For multi-select questionnaire items (e.g., modules used, reasons for satisfaction, barriers, and clinical indications), each response option was converted into a separate binary indicator (selected vs. not selected) and analyzed as a categorical variable. Continuous variables (age) are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as number and percentage.

For group comparisons, we used the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Missing data were reported explicitly for each variable; analyses were conducted using available (non-missing) responses per item, and “not applicable” items (e.g., questions relevant only to users) were treated as missing for non-users. Analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Participants

Between April and June 2024, the survey was distributed to 341 eligible PCPs within MHS. A total of 126 physicians completed the questionnaire (response rate: 37%). There were no significant differences in sex distribution between respondents and non-respondents (p = 0.705). Most participants were active users (N = 111). The rest were classified as former users (N = 10) and non-adopters (N = 5). There were no statistically significant differences in age across the three groups, with mean ages of 49 (SD = 9), 50 (SD = 13), and 47 (SD = 9) years, respectively (p > 0.9).

User categories and device usage

Among active users, the Butterfly device was the most prevalent, used by 56% (n = 62) of active users and 60% (n = 6) of stopped users, respectively. Other devices employed by active users included the Venue Go (21%, n = 23) and Vscan (9.1%, n = 10). In contrast, the Vscan Air and Lumify were less commonly used, accounting for 1.8% and 7.3% of the active cohort, respectively. The type of device used was correlated with use patterns. Butterfly and Lumify users were more likely to use POCUS than expected.

Regarding usage frequency relative to initial expectations, active users reported robust engagement. Approximately 44% of active users reported using POCUS “a lot more” or “a little more” than expected, while 31% used it “as expected.” Only a minority reported usage that was “a little less” (10%) or “a lot less” (15%) than anticipated.

Clinical applications and proficiency

Active users demonstrated a high utilization rate across several core anatomical modules. The lung module was the most widely adopted, used by 93% (n = 103) of active physicians, followed closely by the abdomen module (79%, n = 88) and the musculoskeletal module (55%, n = 61). When analyzing specific medical pathologies, lung parenchyma assessment (including pulmonary congestion, infiltrates, and pleural effusion) was the most common application, reported by 82% (n = 91) of active users. Other frequent applications included urinary tract assessment (including Kidney stones, hydronephrosis, renal cysts/masses, urinary retention, bladder masses) (66%, n = 73), gallbladder evaluation (including Gallstones, cholecystitis signs, CBD measurement) (59%, n = 66), evaluation of soft tissue infections (46%, n = 51), and DVT screening (44%, n = 49). Less frequent but notable applications included foreign body identification (32%, n = 35), pneumothorax/hemothorax (30%, n = 33), joint pathology (including Joint effusion, bursitis) (28%, n = 31), and long-bone fractures (27%, n = 30) (Fig. 2).

When asked to what extent the training prepared them for working with the device, 69% of active users (n = 64) and 43% of former users (n = 3) said that the training prepared them well or very well (p = 0.023).

Facilitators

The drivers for adopting and maintaining POCUS practice varied significantly between active and former users. Among active users, the most significant facilitator was the perception that POCUS improves medical care (77%, n = 85). This stands in sharp contrast to the former user group, where only a minority (30%, n = 3) agreed with this sentiment (p = 0.004). Similarly, more than half of active users (61%, n = 68) reported that POCUS enables accurate diagnosis, compared to a minority of former users (20%, n = 2) (p = 0.017). The reduction in burnout was another key factor, with active users (45%, n = 50) showing lower burnout than former users (10%, n = 1) (p = 0.043). Furthermore, more than half of active users valued POCUS for its ability to reduce emergency room (ER) referrals (53%, n = 50), a benefit recognized by only a third (38%, n = 3) of the stopped users (p = 0.07) (Fig. 3).

Barriers

Despite enthusiasm among active users, significant barriers to POCUS implementation were identified. These barriers were similar across groups, with no difference between active and former users. Time constraints emerged as the leading obstacle, reported by 60% of both active (n = 67) and former (n = 6) users (p > 0.9). Lack of experience was the second most common barrier, affecting 50% of both active (n = 56) and stopped (n = 5) physicians, p > 0.9. Lack of confidence in using the technology was also a limiting factor, cited by 39% of active users and 30% of stopped users (p = 0.7). Interestingly, technical limitations of the device itself were less frequently cited, noted by only 19% of active and 20% of stopped users (p > 0.9). Lack of sufficient reimbursement was noted as a limit by 32% of active users, but was not cited by former users (p = 0.061) (Fig. 4).

Among former users and non-adopters, 57% and 100% of respondents, respectively, reported that the POCUS training they received was “somewhat” or “only a little” adequate. The training was “very” or “very much” adequate in 69% of active users and 43% of former users (p = 0.023).

Satisfaction and future outlook

The survey revealed a stark contrast in satisfaction and trust between active and former users. Active users expressed high confidence in POCUS-assisted diagnosis, with 42% reporting “very” or “very much” confidence in the modality. Conversely, 75% of former users have only “a little” or no confidence in it. Similarly, regarding satisfaction with image quality, 60% of active users rated that they are satisfied with it (very/very much), whereas 63% of former users were not satisfied with it at all or only a little (p = 0.003) (Table 1).

Overall sentiment regarding the future of POCUS in primary care was positive in all three groups. Among active users, 52% predicted that there would eventually be a “device in every clinic,” and 41% foresaw that usage would “gradually increase”. Former users and non-adopters were also moderately optimistic, with 44% and 75% predicting a gradual increase in usage, respectively.

Satisfaction is best summarized by the likelihood of recommending POCUS to other PCPs. 85% of active users stated they would recommend POCUS “very” or “very much,” compared with only 43% of former users (p = 0.015).

Discussion

Main findings

This study demonstrates that the sustained integration of POCUS in primary care is driven by distinct clinical and personal benefits that distinguish active users from those who discontinue use. Our main findings reveal that while time constraints and lack of experience are universal barriers reported by all PCPs, the decision to maintain POCUS usage is strongly correlated with the physician’s perception of its utility—specifically its ability to improve diagnosis, optimize treatment, and mitigate professional burnout.

Interpretation

Usage patterns

Most respondents reported using POCUS “more than expected” or “as expected,” aligning with reports that usage gradually increases as the modality is introduced into primary care [27]. However, consistent with broader literature, absolute use rates among PCPs generally remain relatively low [13], despite overwhelming endorsement by professional organizations [17, 18, 28].

Our study demonstrated that lung parenchyma and the urinary tract were the most frequent indications. POCUS in the hospital has similar uses, including pulmonary, cardiac, abdominal, vascular, and musculoskeletal applications, as well as procedural guidance [29]. POCUS enables PCPs to diagnose and treat patients immediately in the clinic. Similarly, the high use of POCUS for soft-tissue (cellulitis and abscesses) and musculoskeletal applications highlights the case mix in primary care, including both infectious diseases and chronic pain complaints. Future POCUS curricula should tailor training to these high-frequency outpatient scenarios.

The “Value vs. Time” equation

When analyzing barriers, “lack of time” and “lack of experience” were universally cited, which is a major, consistent concern for PCPs globally [30]. However, our study suggests that time pressure is an inherent reality of primary care rather than a unique deterrent for those who quit. The critical difference lies in the perceived return on investment. While both active and former users perceive the same barriers to POCUS use (including time constraints, lack of experience, knowledge, and confidence), active users were significantly more likely than former users to believe that POCUS improves treatment diagnosis, reduces ER referrals, and reduces burnout among PCPs. For active users, the clinical clarity provided by the scan justifies the extra minutes spent. For former users who did not perceive this diagnostic benefit, the time cost likely became prohibitive.

Impact on physician well-being and burnout

A novel and significant finding in our study is the relationship between POCUS and physician well-being. Nearly half (45%) of active users reported that POCUS helps reduce burnout, a sentiment shared by only 10% of former users. This suggests that for proficient users, POCUS may restore a sense of bedside autonomy and clinical mastery.

Training gaps and device usability

Lack of training is frequently cited as a major barrier to the incorporation of POCUS [22, 24, 25]. Interestingly, while all our participants received formal training, “lack of confidence” and “lack of experience” remained the top barriers. This highlights a critical gap: initial training provides the skill, but likely lacks the longitudinal mentorship required to build confidence [22, 24–26]. In addition, active users felt the training prepared them better to use the device than former users and non-adopters.

Device characteristics also appear to play a decisive role in sustainability. Our results indicate that the specific device type correlates with usage patterns, with users of certain ultra-portable units (e.g., Butterfly, Lumify) being more likely to report using POCUS “more than expected.” The dominance of these handheld devices among our active group suggests that portability and an intuitive user interface are non-negotiable in the primary care clinic, where physicians need to work quickly and efficiently. While technical limitations (e.g., image quality) were not a primary barrier (cited by only ~ 20%), the physical “usability” remains critical. If the goal is to expand POCUS use, manufacturers and administrators must prioritize devices that fit seamlessly into the disjointed workflow of a busy clinic, rather than prioritizing raw image fidelity alone.

Systemic barriers and the “Paradox of Success”

Finally, our data reveals an evolution of barriers as physicians become more proficient. Interestingly, lack of reimbursement was cited as a limit by 32% of active users, but by none of the former users (p = 0.061). This suggests a “paradox of success”: low reimbursement rates are not what stop beginners (who are struggling with confidence), but it becomes a major frustration for advanced users who regularly perform scans without recognition. Sustainable implementation programs must therefore anticipate this shift; once training and confidence gaps are bridged, the inadequacy of systemic support will likely become the rate-limiting step for long-term retention [31].

To better understand this, the Israeli primary care system uses a hybrid model in which PCPs operate either as salaried employees or independent contractors within four healthcare maintenance organizations (HMOs). In MHS, the second largest HMO in Israel, most PCPs are independent physicians who receive dedicated financial reimbursement for performing POCUS.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several key strengths that contribute to understanding POCUS implementation in primary care. First, the uniformity of our cohort—in which all respondents had completed formal POCUS training—allowed us to control for lack of access to education. By isolating this variable, we identified barriers to integration that persist even after the initial skill-acquisition phase. Second, understanding specific PCP use patterns provides critical evidence for tailoring future curricula to the needs of community physicians.

However, these findings must be interpreted in light of several limitations. The most significant limitation is the selection bias towards active users, as the majority of respondents were currently using POCUS. This limited our statistical power to fully characterize the barriers specific to “non-adopters” or “former users.” We can assume that those who did not use POCUS preferred not to respond to this survey and, therefore, cannot analyze the true characteristics of non-adopters. In addition, due to reimbursement in MHS, our findings might not reflect PCPs who work in other healthcare systems where POCUS is not reimbursed. Second, the overall response rate was relatively low, a known challenge in surveys of physicians that may further contribute to non-response bias. Third, the study was conducted relatively shortly after training for many participants. Consequently, our data reflects the “adoption” phase rather than long-term “maintenance,” and it is possible that barriers and attitudes may evolve as users transition from novices to experienced practitioners. We did not collect data regarding the exact dates of the respondents’ initial POCUS training. Consequently, we are unable to determine the precise time elapsed between their course completion and their survey participation, which prevents us from analyzing the potential impact of skill decay over time. Time since training has been shown to affect confidence, utilization, and skill retention [21, 32]. Future longitudinal studies are needed to track how these factors shift over time.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study evaluated the integration of POCUS among trained PCPs within MHS. We established that active users perceive high clinical value in the modality, particularly for its ability to enhance diagnostic accuracy and optimize treatment, with usage rates often exceeding initial expectations. However, despite this perceived value and overall satisfaction, widespread adoption is hindered by persistent barriers—chiefly time constraints, limited clinical experience, and insufficient confidence—rather than by the devices’ technical limitations. Sustainable integration of POCUS into the community setting warrants a paradigm shift toward tailored curricula that address the unique case mix and workflow realities of the primary care physician.
