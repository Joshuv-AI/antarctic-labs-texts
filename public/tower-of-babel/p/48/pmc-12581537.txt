Cost-effectiveness of rapid-deployment aortic valve replacement over TAVI: a real-world retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kevin Maldonado-Cañón, Silvia Alejandra Prada Liévano, Germán Molina, Carlos Pérez Rivera, Héctor Olaya, Valentina Pérez-Ariza, Nicolás Forero, Santiago Niño, Javier Maldonado-Escalante
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAortic valve replacement (AVR) is the standard treatment for severe aortic stenosis. While transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has recently been widely recommended, concerns remain regarding real-world long-term valve durability and cost-effectiveness. Rapid deployment valves (RDV) have emerged as an alternative in surgical AVR, offering potential advantages in operative efficiency.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study compares the clinical outcomes and cost-effectiveness of TAVI versus RDV on patients undergoing AVR between 2012 and 2024 in a single center in Bogota, Colombia. The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality, structural valve deterioration (SVD), and non-structural valve deterioration (NSVD). Multivariable regression models were used to identify predictors of each outcome. The inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to further balance baseline differences. A cost-effectiveness analysis was performed on the IPTW-weighted sample. Probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess uncertainty.Results117 patients were included (TAVI: n = 70, RDV: n = 47). TAVI patients were older and had a higher preoperative risk profile than RDV patients. No significant differences were observed in 30-day or follow-up all-cause mortality. In unweighted analyses, SVD occurred more frequently in the TAVI group (aHR: 4.41, 95% CI: 1.22–16.0; p = 0.024), while NSVD was observed exclusively in TAVI patients (p = 0.0017). After IPTW, TAVI was associated with a significantly higher hazard of any form of valve deterioration (aHR: 5.08, 95% CI: 1.41–18.32; p = 0.013). The mean cost of RDV was $42,629, while the cost of TAVI was $73,403. The cost-effectiveness analysis indicated that TAVI was less effective and more expensive and was unlikely to be cost-effective at local willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds ($6,947–$20,842). Sensitivity analysis confirmed that RDV was the preferred strategy in 66.6% and 68.5% of simulations under the corresponding WTP thresholds.ConclusionsBoth RDV and TAVI exhibit comparable safety and mortality profiles. However, TAVI is associated with a higher incidence of valve deterioration and significantly higher costs, making it a less cost-effective option, particularly for the Colombian healthcare system. These findings highlight the importance of individualized procedural selection based on patient characteristics and economic factors. Future research should focus on long-term real-world cost-effectiveness in different socioeconomical contexts and strategies to mitigate valve deterioration in TAVI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-025-03609-1.Keywords: Aortic valve replacement, TAVI, Rapid deployment valve, Cost-effectiveness, Heart surgery

Background

Aortic stenosis is the most prevalent valvular disease, affecting between 2% and 10% of individuals over 65 years. In symptomatic patients without treatment, it has a considerable impact on mortality, with an average survival of only 2 to 3 years [1–3]. Aortic valve replacement (AVR) is an effective treatment for this condition, significantly improving life expectancy [4–6]. Two main techniques are available: surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) and transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) [7].

International guidelines now recommend TAVI for a broader group of patients, including those at intermediate or low risk [8–10]. Most supporting trials have been industry-sponsored and focused on older, higher-risk patients; while TAVI expands to younger and lower-risk populations, concerns remain about the strength of the evidence supporting its long-term durability and outcomes [11–14]. Studies in low-risk patients undergoing SAVR reported higher three-year survival rates than those treated with TAVI (83.4% vs. 72%, p < 0.001) [15]. TAVI has also been linked to higher rates of reintervention, postoperative stroke, and permanent pacemaker implantation [16]. Additionally, prosthetic valve deterioration appears faster in patients under 70, with severe aortic regurgitation occurring in up to 14% of cases and valve thrombosis rates range from 4% to 20.9% within six months post-procedure, while survival rates at five, seven, and eight years drop to 48%, 18–28%, and below 15%, respectively [17].

According to the Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology Transcatheter Valve Therapy Registry, the number of isolated TAVIs increased by 1,606.7% between 2012 and 2022 (from 4,457 to 76,069 procedures) [18]. Meanwhile, the incidence of cardiac surgery following TAVI rose by 4,235.3% overall and by an average of 144.6% per year between 2012 and 2023. Notably, SAVR after TAVI increased even more rapidly, with an average annual growth of 148.1% and a total rise of 5,914.3% [16]. Operative mortality for any cardiac surgery after TAVI reached 15.8%, with combined morbidity and mortality at 38.7%; further, 10.4% of patients required a new permanent pacemaker [16]. From a macroeconomic perspective, the rising burden of reinterventions and device-related complications increases healthcare costs and strains resource allocation.

Rapid deployment valves (RDV) have been developed to address these challenges. Implementing a minimally invasive open technique has allowed optimization and reduction of SAVR surgical times. A key feature of these valves is their quick deployment, leading to a reduction of over 48% in aortic cross-clamp time and more than 39% in cardiopulmonary bypass duration. This decrease in circulatory support time minimizes the risk of systemic inflammatory response syndrome, which is associated with a high risk of renal injury and coagulopathy, particularly in high-risk patients [19, 20].

On the other hand, the economic burden of TAVI versus SAVR is a crucial consideration, especially in healthcare systems with limited resources. TAVI is associated with higher costs due to device prices, procedural expenses, and immediate post-procedural care. While some studies suggest that reduced hospital stays and faster recovery might offset these costs, real-world follow-up data on durability are still lacking [21–24]. Given these concerns, the cost-effectiveness of TAVI is a critical issue.

Despite advancements and current interest in both techniques, uncertainties remain for real-world settings, particularly in Latin America. Our study aims to address this knowledge gap by assessing the long-term effectiveness, safety, and cost-effectiveness of TAVI and RDV in a cohort of patients who underwent first-time AVR. By analyzing mortality rates, complications, valve deterioration, costs, and associated factors, we seek to provide robust evidence to enhance patient selection, optimize treatment planning, and improve overall AVR outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

This study is a retrospective cohort analysis of patients aged ≥ 18 who underwent their first AVR procedure at a single referral institution in Bogota, Colombia, between 2012 and 2024. The study included patients who received either RDV or TAVI, excluding those whose procedures were converted and required a different technique. Data was collected by reviewing electronic medical records, capturing information from the index hospitalization and outpatient visits both before and after the procedure, including demographics, clinical characteristics, procedural details, and long-term outcomes. This enabled us to achieve complete data collection without the need for imputation.

Outcomes

A targeted search of inpatient and outpatient follow-up records was performed to identify all-cause mortality and to evaluate the presence of structural and non-structural valve deterioration, using the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 (VARC-3) definitions [25]: (a) Structural valve deterioration (SVD): Permanent intrinsic changes in the prosthetic valve, including leaflet wear and rupture, leaflet detachment, fibrosis and/or calcification, pivot fracture, or deformation; (b) Non-structural valve deterioration (NSVD): Any abnormality not intrinsic to the prosthetic valve that results in valve dysfunction (e.g., leaflet entrapment by pannus, tissue, or sutures; improper positioning or sizing; aortic root dilation following stentless prostheses or valve-sparing operations; prosthesis-patient mismatch; and embolization).

Statistical analysis

All collected data were subjected to a descriptive analysis. Continuous variables were summarized using means or medians, with standard deviations or interquartile ranges depending on their distribution, while categorical variables were expressed as absolute and relative frequencies. Additionally, 30-day and follow-up mortality, complications, SVD, and NSVD were presented overall and stratified by technique.

Given the lack of randomization in treatment assignment (TAVI or RDV), multivariable regression models and the inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) were used to adjust for confounding and to balance baseline characteristics. Furthermore, a cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted using the IPTW-weighted sample.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered the threshold for statistical significance. All effect measures were reported with their respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.0.3; R Core Team, 2020) (RRID: SCR_001905) [26].

Analyses on the unweighted sample

To identify risk factors associated with 30-day mortality, we fitted univariable and multivariable logistic regression models. Given the expected low number of deaths, we applied Firth’s penalized likelihood correction to reduce small-sample bias [27]. Since the timing of the event is important and not all patients may have experienced it during follow-up, Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted for SVD and NSVD.

For both models, backward stepwise selection was applied based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Some variables with p > 0.05 were retained because their exclusion resulted in a poorer model fit or because they were considered clinically important confounders. Model assumptions, goodness-of-fit, and predictive capability were evaluated using the c-statistic, Harrell’s c-index, and McFadden’s pseudo-R².

Analyses on the weighted sample

Inverse probability of treatment weighting

IPTW is a statistical approach designed to mitigate confounding in observational studies by assigning weights to individuals based on the inverse probability of receiving a specific treatment [28]. This method generates a synthetic sample in which baseline covariates are evenly distributed across treatment groups, simulating the conditions of a randomized controlled trial and minimizing bias in effect estimation.

We applied IPTW using sex, age, BMI, comorbidities (arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, peripheral artery disease, atrial fibrillation, COPD, pulmonary hypertension), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), baseline creatinine, NYHA III or IV, previous cardiovascular surgery, and EuroSCORE II as adjustment variables. Covariate balance was indicated by standardized mean differences (SMDs) below 10% in the weighted sample.

Although a small annulus is a pre-treatment variable, it was not included in the IPTW due to its potential as a collider (i.e., a variable associated with both treatment assignment and outcomes that could introduce artificial associations between treatment and unmeasured confounders) [29]. In our practice, RDVs are preferred in elderly patients with small aortic anatomy, allowing for valve implantation without annular enlargement.

Comparative survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the Log-rank test were used to estimate survival for mortality, SVD, NSVD, and any valve deterioration (i.e., SVD or NSVD) using the IPTW-weighted sample. Furthermore, for comparative purposes between techniques, effectiveness, defined as the absence of any valve deterioration, was assessed using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model with IPTW as sampling weights. Each IPTW-weighted model included the technique (TAVI or RDV) as the primary independent variable. Additionally, variables not considered in the IPTW (i.e., history of heart failure, small annulus, prosthesis size, and urgent surgery) were included as covariates to account for residual imbalance and confounding. Robust standard errors were used, considering the weighted nature of the data. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, with both global and covariate-specific tests.

Cost-effective analysis

Costs

A cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted from the healthcare system payer perspective, reflecting costs incurred by insurers. Cost data were obtained from the hospital’s Diagnosis-Related Groups division, which tracks resource consumption during hospitalizations. Cost calculations were based on expenditures from 2023, the most recent available data. No discount rate was applied, as all costs were collected within a year.

Costs for TAVI included specialist fees (one cardiovascular surgeon and two interventional cardiologists), anesthesia, specialized additional assistance (one cardiologist focused on intra-procedural echocardiographic monitoring and one perfusionist), catheterization lab rights (4 h), two days in the intensive care unit and one day in the general ward, necessary materials and supplies (including one NOVAFLEX® transfemoral access kit and/or one CERTITUDE® transapical kit, as well as general hemodynamic and anesthesia-related supplies), required medications, and special examinations (including two transesophageal echocardiograms, one chest X-ray, one EKG, and laboratory tests such as complete blood count, coagulation times, and renal function tests), while costs for RDV included specialist fees (cardiovascular surgeon and team and one perfusionist), anesthesia, general and specialized assistance, operating room rights (6 h), four days in the intensive care unit and four days in the general ward, necessary surgical materials, supplies, and medications, as well as special supplies, including the Edwards Intuity Elite® Valve System. Costs were reported in Colombian pesos and converted to USD using the 2023 purchasing power parity conversion factor (1.401) [30].

Decision model and statistical considerations

A decision tree model was constructed to estimate the costs associated with the specific clinical outcome (i.e., absence of any valve deterioration) and assess the relationship between incremental costs and clinical benefits. Each terminal branch was identified with its corresponding probability (derived from IPTW-weighted data), cost, and benefit.

Our rationale for using this composite outcome was primarily methodological and pragmatic: first, from a clinical and economic standpoint, both SVD and NSVD may lead to increased healthcare utilization, such as imaging, closer follow-up, or reintervention; therefore, for the purposes of modeling resource use and costs, grouping them under a single outcome allowed us to more comprehensively capture all valve-related events that could impact cost trajectories. Second, the number of events, particularly for NSVD, was expected to be limited, which could pose challenges for modeling each component separately without overfitting or compromising precision. By using the composite outcome, we ensured adequate statistical power for the cost model while maintaining a clinically relevant representation of deterioration.

While no direct patient or external stakeholder involvement occurred in the study design, findings were shared with clinicians and institutional stakeholders to contextualize results and inform future practice improvements.

Sensitivity analysis

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis accounted for cost and effectiveness data uncertainty by simulating variations across different probability distributions. A Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 iterations was performed, with cost estimates following a log-normal distribution (µ = natural logarithm of average cost, σ = 1) and effectiveness data modeled with a beta distribution (µ from IPTW-weighted data, σ = 0.05). Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) were constructed using the net benefit approach, presenting the proportion of ICERs falling below willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds per additional unit of effect. Since no explicit WTP threshold exists in Colombia, the analysis incorporated thresholds ranging from 1 to 3 times the per capita gross domestic product (GDP), in line with local cost-effectiveness guidelines [31].

Results

Population and outcomes

A total of 117 patients undergoing AVR were included. Of these, 47 patients underwent AVR with RDV, while 70 received TAVI. All patients were evaluated by an interdisciplinary Heart Team (cardiovascular surgery, cardiology, and hemodynamics), which determined the surgical or percutaneous approach based on individual risk factors.

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were observed in sex, weight, height, LVEF, or baseline creatinine; however, the TAVI group was older and exhibited a higher preoperative risk as measured by the EuroSCORE-II compared to the RDV group, although not statistically significant. Regarding comorbidities, heart failure, prior cardiovascular surgery, and NYHA functional class III or IV were significantly more common in the TAVI group.

In the RDV group, 78.7% (n = 37) of patients underwent a minimally invasive AVR, while 21.2% (n = 10) underwent conventional open surgery. In the TAVI group, the most common approach was transfemoral (81.4%, n = 57), followed by transapical (12.9%, n = 9), subclavian (4.3%, n = 3), and transaortic (1.4%, n = 1). Urgent surgeries were more frequent in the RDV group than in the TAVI group (46.8% vs. 25.7%, p = 0.031), with no emergency procedures performed in either group. A small aortic annulus was more commonly observed in the RDV group (52.1% vs. 2.9%, p < 0.001), which was reflected in significantly smaller prosthesis mean sizes in this group (21 vs. 23, p < 0.001).

Effectiveness and safety

Patients in the RDV group had a significantly longer intubation time; however, no significant differences were observed between groups regarding total hospital length of stay. Complication rates were similar across groups, with bleeding being the most prevalent complication in RDV and both bleeding and acute myocardial infarction in TAVI. Cerebrovascular events were infrequent in both groups. There was a trend toward a higher need for permanent pacemaker implantation in the TAVI group (10.0% vs. 4.3%, p = 0.43). The distribution of pacemaker implantation timing indicated that no patients in the RDV group required implantation within the first six postoperative days (Table 1).

Both groups had a similar length of follow-up (Table 1). According to the NYHA classification, the final functional class was comparable, with most patients classified as NYHA I or II. No significant differences were observed in all-cause mortality at 30 days or during follow-up. SVD was more frequent in the TAVI group, although not statistically significant. On the other hand, NSVD was significantly more common in the TAVI group, with no cases for RDV patients.

30-day mortality was observed in five RDV and four TAVI patients. In the RDV group, two patients with severe hypertrophic cardiomyopathy developed cardiogenic shock; postoperative management primarily focused on vasopressor support rather than volume optimization, which contributed to the development of renal failure and multiorgan dysfunction. One patient experienced massive intra-arterial thrombosis following protamine administration; one suffered uncontrolled bleeding, requiring multiple reinterventions and transfusions; another patient developed intestinal obstruction, whose delayed recognition led to ischemia and perforation. None of these cases, apart from bleeding complications, were directly attributed to the prosthesis or technique but rather to challenges in postoperative management. Among TAVI patients, one case developed acute postoperative stroke, while another presented with a severe inguinal and retroperitoneal hematoma complicated by Clostridium difficile infection. The remaining two cases were attributed to intraoperative ventricular fibrillation and postoperative ventricular dysfunction.

Variables already included in the EuroSCORE II were excluded from the final parsimonious multivariable logistic regression model; the following were retained: EuroSCORE II, Body Mass Index (BMI), hypertension, dyslipidemia, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, small aortic annulus, and technique (TAVI or RDV). For each one standard deviation increase in the EuroSCORE II, the odds of mortality increased by a factor of 2.06 (95% CI: 1.15–3.84; p = 0.016). On the other hand, the technique did not demonstrate any association in either univariable or multivariable analyses (Table 2).

Analyses on the weighted sample

Inverse probability of treatment weighting

In the unweighted sample, standardized mean differences exceeding 10% were observed for age (45.9%), BMI (13.8%), arterial hypertension (14.9%), diabetes (26.3%), peripheral artery disease (17.8%), COPD (13.5%), LVEF (24.4%), baseline creatinine (16.3%), NYHA III or IV (43%), previous cardiovascular surgery (41.1%), and EuroSCORE II (35.4%). The regression model used to estimate propensity scores (PS) for the IPTW reported a c-statistic of 0.77. We used a caliper width defined as 2 times the standard deviation of the logit-transformed PS to generate restricted weights (75th percentile: 2.16; minimum: 1.02; maximum: 6.46), yielding a synthetic weighted sample comprising 105.3 RDV and 116.8 TAVI patients. As presented in Table 4 and Figure S1, all SMDs in the weighted sample were below 10% indicating covariate balance.

Comparative survival analysis

The Kaplan-Meier survival curve (Fig. 1) using the IPTW-weighted balanced sample shows that patients undergoing AVR with RDV had a significantly higher probability of remaining free from SVD, NSVS, and any valve deterioration than those receiving TAVI over a follow-up period of up to 120 months. Although the probability declined in both groups over time, this decline was more pronounced in the TAVI group.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves using the IPTW-weighted sample. P-values correspond to the Log-rank test

The Cox regression model showed that treatment with TAVI was associated with a significantly increased hazard of any deterioration compared to RDV (aHR: 5.08; 95% CI: 1.41–18.32; p = 0.013). Other covariates were not significantly associated with the outcome: heart failure (HR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.28–3.04; p = 0.890), small aortic annulus (HR: 1.78; 95% CI: 0.42–7.54; p = 0.436), prosthesis size (HR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.68–1.18; p = 0.436), and urgent surgery (HR: 0.42; 95% CI: 0.04–4.01; p = 0.451). The model showed good discriminative ability (concordance = 0.745). Assessment of the proportional hazard’s assumption using Schoenfeld residuals did not indicate significant violations, either globally (p = 0.12) or at the level of individual covariates (all p > 0.05).

Cost-effective analysis

Base case results

In the weighted sample, the incidence of any valve deterioration was significantly higher in the TAVI group compared to the RDV group (29.9 vs. 5.1%; SMD: 60.4%). The mean cost of the RDV was $42,629, whereas TAVI costs $73,403.

While RDV demonstrated greater effectiveness in valve-deterioration-free survival, it was substantially less expensive than TAVI; this suggests that TAVI is dominated (i.e., it is both less effective and more costly). Adopting TAVI would require justification for expenditures significantly exceeding the local WTP threshold ($6,947 to $20,842 (equivalent to 1–3 times the GDP per capita)) given its modest incremental benefits.

Sensitivity analysis

Uncertainty in the results was assessed through probabilistic sensitivity analysis, incorporating parameter uncertainty via 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations (Figs. 2 and S2, Table S1). The cost-effectiveness plane demonstrated that most simulated cost-effectiveness pairs lay above the WTP threshold, indicating that TAVI is not cost-effective in most scenarios. Additionally, the CEACs showed that at WTP thresholds of $6,947 (1 GDP per capita) and $20,841 (3 GDP per capita), a RDV was the cost-effective alternative in 66.6% and 68.5% of simulations, respectively.

Discussion

Although the two groups differed in baseline characteristics, we applied multivariable regression models and IPTW to enable a valid comparison after adjustment and balance despite the non-randomized, observational nature of the data. We found that patients who underwent AVR with RDV had a significantly lower risk of long-term valve deterioration compared to those treated with TAVI. On the other hand, 30-day mortality and survival during follow-up after weighting did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that both techniques offer acceptable safety. Furthermore, from a cost-effectiveness perspective, given that TAVI was both less effective and more costly, it is considered a dominated strategy, meaning its adoption would require justification for expenditures significantly exceeding the local WTP threshold. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis confirmed these findings, with the CEAC indicating that RDV was the preferred alternative in 66.1% and 67.8% of simulations at WTP thresholds of $6,947 and $20,841, respectively.

Our cohort exhibits demographic and clinical characteristics comparable to those reported in other studies. The median age of patients undergoing TAVI (79.5 years) was slightly higher than that of the RDV group (76 years), aligning with studies by Theron et al. [32] and Eghbalzadeh et al. [33], where TAVI recipients were significantly older (81–83 years) than RDV patients (76–76.8 years). Hypertension was highly prevalent in both groups (85.1% in RDV and 90.0% in TAVI), consistent with cohorts from Iacovelli et al. and Eghbalzadeh et al. [33] (TAVI: 87–90.9%; RDV: 76.1–78.7%). Despite an intermediate-risk EuroSCORE II in both groups, the TAVI group exhibited a higher score (4.50 vs. 3.00 for RDV), reflecting a similar trend observed in Muneretto et al. [34], where low-risk TAVI patients had an average EuroSCORE II of 3.0 compared to 2.0 for RDV.

Interestingly, the RDV group had a higher proportion of patients with small aortic annulus and smaller prostheses, yet exhibited more favorable clinical outcomes. In our surgical practice, RDVs are preferred in elderly patients with small aortic anatomy, allowing for valve implantation without annular enlargement and with excellent postoperative gradients. From a methodological perspective, although a small annulus is a pre-treatment variable, it was not included in the IPTW model due to its direct influence on treatment choice and its strong collinearity with prosthesis size (Pearson’s r = −0.64). This decision helped ensure the validity of the cost-effectiveness comparison by avoiding bias introduced by overadjustment and potential colliders that could introduce artificial associations between treatment and unmeasured confounders [29]. Moreover, the direction of the observed effect -favorable outcomes despite a higher prevalence of a small annulus and smaller prostheses in the RDV group- suggests that these factors are unlikely to have biased the results in favor of RDV.

All-cause mortality was similar between groups (27.7% for RDV and 30.0% for TAVI) at a median follow-up of approximately three years, contrasting with studies such as Grigorios et al. [35], which reported three-year mortality rates of 17% for SAVR and 28% for TAVI. A declining mortality trend was observed over time, reaching 13% for both groups in procedures performed between 2020 and 2023, consistent with long-term mortality rates reported in moderate- and low-risk patients [13, 36–38]. This trend likely reflects improvements in surgical expertise and postoperative care. Notably, creatinine emerged as a risk factor for operative mortality, reinforcing the importance of meticulous volume management over reliance on inotropic agents commonly used in postoperative cardiovascular surgery [39].

Bleeding complications were more frequent in the RDV group (12.8%) compared to TAVI (5.7%), mirroring trends reported by Iacovelli et al. [40] (38.3% for SAVR vs. 4.44% for TAVI). Cerebrovascular events were rare (2.1% in RDV and 2.9% in TAVI), consistent with findings from Theron et al. [32], Iacovelli et al. [40], and Grigorios et al. [35] (0–3.3% for RDV; 0.75–1.47% for TAVI). Permanent pacemaker implantation was more frequent in the TAVI group (10.0% vs. 4.3% for RDV), aligning with Grigorios et al. [35] (14.2% vs. 8%). These similarities suggest that our cohort is representative of typical patients undergoing aortic valve replacement, strengthening the external validity of our findings.

In the unweighted analyses, no association was found between technique (TAVI or RDV) and 30-day mortality, both in univariable and multivariable models. As expected, the EuroSCORE II retained its prognostic value, with each standard deviation increase more than doubling the odds of early mortality. These findings highlight the relevance of established surgical risk models in predicting perioperative outcomes regardless of the intervention type.

When assessing long-term valve performance, our unweighted multivariable Cox regression models demonstrated that TAVI was independently associated with a higher risk of SVD. The protective effect of higher body weight against SVD observed in our sample remains hypothesis-generating and warrants further investigation, potentially reflecting a different hemodynamic burden or prosthesis-host interaction. In contrast, NSVD was more strongly influenced by clinical and procedural factors such as NYHA class III/IV at baseline, urgent surgery, and prosthesis size. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that procedural urgency and suboptimal hemodynamic conditions may predispose to functional deterioration unrelated to leaflet degeneration [41, 42].

To account for baseline imbalances between groups, we performed IPTW using a well-calibrated propensity score model, achieving adequate covariate balance. In the weighted sample, Kaplan-Meier analyses revealed that patients undergoing RDV had significantly better valve durability, with a higher probability of remaining free from SVD, NSVD, or any form of valve deterioration over the follow-up period. This survival advantage was confirmed in the Cox regression model, where TAVI was associated with a fivefold increased hazard of valve deterioration compared to RDV. These results align with emerging long-term data suggesting that although TAVI offers comparable short-term outcomes, its durability may be inferior, especially in younger or lower-risk populations.

We highlight the need for a critical appraisal of current international guidelines, which often favor TAVI based on data that may not be universally applicable. While TAVI is widely adopted for its minimally invasive nature, our findings underscore that its indiscriminate use does not necessarily translate into superior patient outcomes. The cost implications of widespread TAVI adoption are particularly concerning. While in Colombia, up to 1,000–1,300 valve replacements are performed annually [43], hints towards cost-effectiveness of TAVI have been provided by industry-sponsored randomized control trial data from upper-income countries [21–24], its high cost and lack of clear superiority in real-world effectiveness could impose significant financial burdens on resource-constrained healthcare systems. Given that RDV offers comparable, if not superior, outcomes in specific patient populations at a lower cost, our results advocate for a more personalized, evidence-based approach to treatment selection.

Our study is subject to certain limitations. Given the observational nature of this study, the choice between RDV and TAVI was made on an individual basis. This introduces a potential risk of confounding by indication, as some baseline differences may have influenced both treatment allocation and clinical outcomes. To address this, we used multivariable regression models and IPTW to adjust for measured confounders and to balance covariates between groups. While these methods help reduce bias, the presence of residual confounding due to unmeasured variables cannot be completely excluded. As such, the results should be interpreted with caution, considering the limitations inherent to non-randomized study designs. Additionally, the single-center nature of the study may limit generalizability to broader populations with different healthcare systems, surgical expertise, or procedural protocols. Another limitation is the potential underreporting of valve deterioration, particularly for non-structural complications that may not always be systematically captured in routine follow-up. Moreover, while our economic analysis provides valuable insights, cost estimates may not be directly transferable to other settings due to variations in healthcare costs, reimbursement policies, and resource availability outside Colombia.

Future research should focus on long-term economic evaluations, particularly in real-world settings, to clarify the impact of TAVI on healthcare expenditure. Additionally, strategies to mitigate NSVD risk in TAVI patients should be explored, including advancements in valve design and implantation techniques. Large-scale, longitudinal studies with extended follow-up are essential to confirm our findings and further delineate the long-term implications of both procedures on patient quality of life and cardiac function. This will enable a more comprehensive, individualized approach to treatment selection, emphasizing interdisciplinary decision-making within heart teams.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that RDV and TAVI are viable, effective, and safe AVR options after adjusting for confounders and balancing baseline characteristics. Differences in valve deterioration profiles highlight the importance of individualized procedural selection based on patient characteristics. Furthermore, our cost-effectiveness analysis demonstrates that TAVI is substantially more expensive while providing no clear survival benefit. We anticipate that the full impact of the widespread and often indiscriminate use of TAVI will become evident in the coming years. The increasing rates of reintervention and associated complications call for careful reflection on current trends and their long-term consequences for patients and healthcare systems.
