Perceptions on continuing medical education credit system: a cross-sectional study in Sichuan Province, China
Authors: Xuedong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe continuing medical education (CME) credit system is a core and indispensable component of CME management. Despite its critical significance, empirical research on this system in China remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the perceptions of CME administrators and healthcare professionals regarding the CME credit system in Sichuan Province, China. Combined on-site and online surveys were conducted using self-designed questionnaires. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and CME administrators’ perceptions on the credit system. Comparative analyses were performed using the Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and Kruskal-Wallis H test, with the Bonferroni correction for post hoc pairwise comparisons. A total of 51 CME administrators and 590 healthcare professionals were enrolled. Administrators identified standardized credit verification and the development of a unified credit management information system as two top priorities. Nurses reported a significantly higher proportion of obtaining CME credits through off-disciplinary training than public health personnel (61.6% vs. 0.0%). Healthcare professionals at grassroots institutions showed the highest proportion of acquiring credits via off-disciplinary training, whereas those at provincial-level institutions had the lowest (85.5% vs. 3.6%). Grassroots staff also relied least on academic conferences for credits, while provincial-level staff relied most on this approach (56.6% vs. 100.0%). Conversely, grassroots staff exhibited the highest utilization of distance education while provincial-level staff showed the lowest (22.4% vs. 0.0%). Additionally, provincial-level professionals were much more likely to perceive current CME credit requirements as “too high”, with grassroots professionals demonstrating the lowest tendency (57.1% vs. 23.7%). Compared with public hospital staff, professionals in private hospitals had higher proportions of prioritizing credit acquisition by attending CME activities (91.8% vs. 78.6%) and choosing off-disciplinary training (79.6% vs. 52.1%). CME administrators attached great importance to standardized credit verification and the construction of a unified information system. Nurses, as well as healthcare professionals working at grassroots and private medical institutions, commonly took off-disciplinary training to obtain mandatory credits, and grassroots professionals preferred distance education. In contrast, provincial-level professionals perceived current credit requirements as overly demanding. Private hospital professionals generally prioritized credit acquisition as the core motivation for CME participation. For CME administrative authorities, it is recommended to accelerate the development of a unified digital CME information platform, optimize credit rules for healthcare professionals across all institutional levels, rationalize the allocation of CME resources, and strengthen CME governance in private hospitals.Keywords: Continuing medical education, Credit system, Perception, Sichuan, ChinaSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

In the era of rapid medical advancement, the continuous expansion and renewal of evidence-based medical knowledge mandates that physicians pursue lifelong continuing education to maintain clinical competence and deliver timely, high-quality, and patient-centered healthcare. Globally, continuing medical education (CME) represents the most widely applied institutional strategy to keep physicians abreast of rapid medical advances1. CME is a broadly defined educational process that assists physicians in fulfilling professional responsibilities more effectively and efficiently2. It also plays a crucial role in upgrading physicians’ knowledge and skills, optimizing clinical practices, and ensuring medical safety3. Over recent decades, the connotation, attributes, and functions of CME have evolved continuously4. Currently, China’s national CME system covers all healthcare professionals across the country. It integrates leading medical experts and high-quality academic resources to organize targeted training programs, aiming to improve professional knowledge, practical competencies, and overall occupational literacy among the national healthcare workforce5,6.

The CME credit system constitutes an indispensable core component of CME management. Despite its critical significance, China’s formal national CME credit system was established relatively late than those in developed countries7. For instance, the American Academy of General Practice implemented mandatory and elective credit rules as early as 1948, marking the official establishment of a standardized CME credit system in the United States8. In 1968, the American Medical Association (AMA) launched the Physician’s Recognition Award (PRA) system, which defined Category I and Category II credit criteria to encourage and recognize physicians’ completion of 50 h of CME annually8. A recent systematic review on CME development across Europe indicated that 7 out of 15 European countries have instituted mandatory CME frameworks, compelling all practicing physicians to participate in CME as a prerequisite for medical licensing and clinical practice. Annual credit requirements vary substantially across these seven nations, ranging from 20 to 50 credits9.

In China, standardized national CME credit management was formally introduced in 1991, when the former Chinese Ministry of Health issued interim national regulations that set a minimum requirement of 25 annual CME credits for healthcare professionals, mainly to support professional title promotion10. The revised trial regulations promulgated in 2000 further refined credit management rules, retained the 25-credit annual threshold, and incorporated CME credit assessment as a prerequisite for employment, professional promotion, and practice re-registration among healthcare professionals11. The promulgation of national administrative measures for CME credits in 2006 established unified national standards for credit classification, accreditation, registration, assessment and certification12. Since then, China’s national CME credit system has operated routinely in accordance with these provisions.

The implementation of the CME credit system varies substantially across regions of China13. Sichuan Province rolled out local standardized CME credit management in 2005 and updated relevant policies in 201714,15, adopting differentiated credit criteria based on institutional type and professional title. Medical staff working at provincial, municipal and county-level institutions are uniformly required to complete 25 annual CME credits, with only minor discrepancies in Type I and Type II credit allocation requirements. By contrast, practitioners in township health centers (THCS), community health service centers (CHSCS), and clinics (CCS) face lower annual credit quotas, set at 20, 20, and 8 credits, respectively15.

Although the CME credit system is vital to CME governance in China, the perceptions and attitudes of CME administrators and healthcare professionals toward this system remain understudied. Through literature review, no prior research has explored perceptions of the CME credit system in Sichuan Province from the dual perspectives of CME administrators and healthcare professionals. Previous limited studies have primarily focused on CME credit completion rates and associated demographic disparities, or analyzed the CME credit system solely from the administrative perspective. For example, two studies have evaluated CME credit completion among healthcare professionals in THCS and explored relevant influencing factors, including demographics, training costs, time constraints, and personal participation motivation16,17. Other research has assessed compliance with CME credit requirements and suggested tailored training programs to fulfill the needs of different professional groups18. Additional studies have summarized practical experience in CME credit management and proposed targeted improvement strategies purely based on managerial experience19–21.

Considering the above background, this study employed a cross-sectional design to systematically investigate perceptions of the CME credit system among CME administrators and healthcare professionals in Sichuan Province. The findings are expected to provide practical implications for optimizing local CME credit management. Specifically, the results may support policy revision, refine credit standards for healthcare professionals across institutional tiers, and alleviate the training burden on relevant stakeholders. Moreover, this study may offer novel insights for the international research community on the construction and improvement of CME systems worldwide.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and time

This cross-sectional study was initiated by the former Sichuan Health and Family Planning Commission (SHFPC). In 2017, the SHFPC launched a reform of the existing CME credit system to offer more accessible and tailored CME services for local healthcare professionals. Based on this context, the Office of Sichuan Continuing Medical Education Commission (SCMEC) conducted this province-wide survey. The study aimed to systematically collect perceptions, practical experiences, and targeted suggestions from both CME administrators and healthcare professionals regarding current CME activities and the credit system. To ensure standardized and high-quality field data collection, a professional survey team composed of CME administrators was established. All team members completed unified pre-survey training and were divided into five subgroups to conduct face-to-face interviews with healthcare professionals between April 21 to April 26, 2017. Additionally, data from CME administrators were collected through an online platform operated by Haoyisheng22, a major remote CME service provider in Sichuan Province. All participating CME administrators were required to complete and submit the online questionnaire by April 26, 2017.

Study questionnaire

A region-specific questionnaire was developed, refined and validated by senior CME specialists from health administrative departments and public hospitals across Sichuan Province, to investigate the perceptions and suggestions of CME administrators and healthcare professionals regarding the provincial CME credit system. Briefly, the questionnaire covered five core domains: (1) demographic characteristics of participants; (2) available pathways for healthcare professionals to obtain CME credits; (3) predominant approaches used for credit attainment; (4) whether existing pathways can sufficiently meet annual mandatory credit requirements; and (5) the rationality and appropriateness of current annual credit requirements for healthcare professionals. Prior to formal investigation, the SCMEC Office assembled a group of CME experts to conduct a thorough review of the questionnaire from three dimensions: (1) comprehensiveness of content; (2) representativeness of survey items; and (3) logical rationality of design, to ensure the reliability and validity of the research tool.

Participant selection and sample size determination

Participants and sample size for this study were jointly determined by administrators of Sichuan Health and Family Planning Commission and field investigators. Considering the developmental levels of local CME services, the SCMEC Office designated five regions (Chengdu, Nanchong, Ya’an, Zigong, Liangshan; hereafter referred to as the Five Regions) as the study sites. All online and field participants were recruited from these five regions in strict accordance with the recruitment protocols formulated by the SCMEC Office. Detailed recruitment procedures are outlined below.

Phase 1: online participants (CME administrators)

The SCMEC Office directly recruited CME administrators at the provincial and municipal levels, covering those from the Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital, Sichuan Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and CME administrators from the Five Regions. Administrators in each of the Five Regions then recruited local online participants following unified selection criteria: (1) At least one municipal public health institution and two municipal-level hospitals were included per region; (2) At least three county-level CME administrative authorities were selected in Chengdu, and two in each of the other four regions. Each eligible county was required to recruit online participants from at least two county-level hospitals, two THCS, two CCS, and two private hospitals.

Phase 2: field participants (healthcare professionals)

Field participants were recruited from the institutions enrolled in Phase 1, following standardized sampling protocols: (1) For each provincial, municipal, and county-level hospital, at least three physicians, three nurses, and three pharmacists with senior, intermediate, and primary professional titles were enrolled, respectively; (2) A total of five participants covering physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and medical technicians were recruited from each THCS and CCS; (3) A total of five participants comprising physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and medical technicians were recruited from each private hospital; (4) A total of five participants were recruited from each public health institution.

These procedures yielded a final sample size of 51 CME administrators and 590 healthcare professionals. The detailed participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data collection and quality control

CME administrators were designated to complete and submit questionnaires via the designated online platform. All online data were exported from the platform in Microsoft Excel format. Field investigations were carried out across selected regions by five teams, each consisting of 1 to 3 members. Simple random sampling was employed to recruit field participants. All participants completed the enclosed questions of the questionnaire on site. Upon completion, investigators conducted face-to-face interviews. All enrolled participants fully finished the survey. The SCMEC Office checked all online responses from CME administrators for rationality and completeness. Investigators verified field data from healthcare professionals in real time to ensure validity and integrity. All collected data were then entered into a unified Excel template by field investigators, and double-entry verification was performed before submission. Finally, the SCMEC Office conducted a final assessment before formal data processing. All participants had complete data and were included in the formal analysis.

Statistical analysis

All raw data were compiled and organized using Microsoft Excel. Prior to formal statistical analysis, the research team performed logical checks and data cleaning to ensure data quality. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. All categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

A set of statistical methods was employed in this study. Descriptive statistics was carried out to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics of all participants, including CME administrators from administrative authorities and medical institutions, as well as healthcare professionals. Comparative analyses were then conducted to explore group disparities in perceptions of the CME credit system across participants with different sociodemographic backgrounds. The Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were utilized for unordered categorical variables. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was adopted for variables with bidirectional ordinal but heterogeneous attributes, as well as unidirectional ordinal variables (i.e., unordered grouping with ordered comparison indicators)23,24. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For variables yielding significant results via the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, post hoc pairwise comparisons were further conducted to examine specific intergroup differences. To control Type I error inflation, the Bonferroni correction was applied to calculate the adjusted significance level (ɑ) using the formula 25, where ɑ denotes the Type I error probability and K represents the number of comparison groups.

Perceptions on the CME credit system among healthcare professionals

Demographic characteristics of participating healthcare professionals

A total of 590 healthcare professionals were included in the field survey. The majority were from Chengdu (165, 28.0%), employed in public hospitals (550, 93.2%), and were affiliated with municipal-level healthcare institutions (282, 47.6%). By occupational category, there were 213 (36.1%) physicians, 202 (34.2%) nurses, 131 (22.2%) medical technicians, and 44 (7.5%) public health personnel. Specifically, healthcare professionals in private hospitals were significantly more likely to report credit attainment as their primary motivation for CME participation compared with those in public hospitals (91.8% vs. 78.6%, P = 0.027). Significant differences in prioritizing credit acquisition through off-disciplinary CME courses were observed across groups: by career type, the proportions were 61.6% (nurses), 59.0% (physicians), 54.1% (medical technicians), and 0.0% (public health personnel) (P < 0.001); by institutional level, the proportions were 85.5% (grassroots), 66.2% (county), 42.3% (municipal), and 3.6% (provincial) (P < 0.001); and private hospitals showed a higher proportion (79.6%) than public hospitals (52.1%) (P < 0.001).

Academic conferences represented the most prevalent pathway for credit acquisition. Institutional level and ownership significantly affected pathway selection: the proportion of professionals using academic conferences decreased sequentially from provincial to grassroots institutions, at 100.0% (provincial), 81.0% (municipal), 74.9% (county), and 56.6% (grassroots) (P < 0.001). Conversely, the use of distance education increased significantly from provincial to grassroots institutions, at 0.0% (provincial), 13.6% (municipal), 18.8% (county), and 22.4 (grassroots) (P < 0.001).

Professionals at provincial-level institutions were also more likely to perceive current CME credit requirements as “too high”, while those at grassroots institutions exhibited the lowest such tendency (57.1% vs. 23.7%, P < 0.001).

Post hoc pairwise tests were further conducted to explore disparities across the four dimensions: career type, institutional level, and institutional ownership (Table 2). Results confirmed significant variations in perceptions of the CME credit system across all three stratifying variables. Nurses displayed a significantly higher proportion (61.6%) of reporting credit attainment as the sole motivation for off-disciplinary training than public health personnel (0.0%). Healthcare professionals in grassroots institutions reported the highest proportion (85.5%) of prioritizing credit acquisition through off-disciplinary training, in contrast to those at provincial (3.6%), municipal (42.3%), and county (66.2%) levels. They also reported the lowest reliance on academic conferences as the primary credit acquisition pathway (56.6%) vs. provincial (100.0%), municipal (81.0%) and county (74.9%) institutions, but a significantly higher use of distance education (22.4%) compared with provincial-level counterparts (0.0%). Meanwhile, professionals at provincial-level institutions most likely to regard the current credit requirements as “too high” (57.1%), relative to those at county (26.1%) and grassroots (23.7%) levels. Professionals at private hospitals demonstrated higher proportions of choosing credit attainment as the primary motivation for general CME participation (91.8%) and off-disciplinary training (79.6%), as well as for selecting teaching and research activities as their main credit acquisition pathway (12.2%), compared with their peers in public hospitals (2.8%).

Discussion

Since the inception of CME in China, the CME credit system has become an indispensable core component of the national CME management system. After years of implementation, clinical and grassroots healthcare services have undergone profound transformations, which drives the continuous iteration and optimization of the existing CME management mechanism. Against this background, the National Health Commission has issued targeted policy adjustments in 2019. Major revisions include the official recognition of national Type Ⅰ and Type Ⅱ CME credits, a maximum 50% offset rule between annual Type Ⅰ and Type Ⅱ credits obtained from professional training, exemption from strict credit classification rules for medical staff working in county-level and grassroots medical institutions, and a reasonable increase in the proportion of credits obtainable through distance education platforms26.

To further refine CME credit management rules, the National Health Commission and Sichuan Provincial Health Commission successively updated relevant administrative regulations in 2024 and 2025, respectively27,28. Compared with previous provisions, the newly updated policies feature three key changes: the abolition of the classification of Type I and Type II CME credits, the removal of the annual 10-credit cap for distance CME education, and the unification of credit standards for healthcare workers across different professional levels. These policy revisions are largely consistent with the findings of the present study. For example, lifting the credit limit for distance education effectively reduces training burdens for grassroots practitioners. As observed in this study, grassroots staff mainly rely on distance platforms to obtain credits and seldom participate in offline academic conferences, which further reflects the practical value of the latest policy updates.

CME administrators regard standardized credit verification and the construction of a unified information management system as two key priorities in routine management. National regulations mandate that administrative authorities at all levels validate and register CME credits within their jurisdictions27. Nevertheless, the current CME credit system still faces prominent challenges, which may be primarily attributed to scattered credit-issuance bodies and non-unified specifications of traditional paper-based credit certificates. In Sichuan Province, health administrative departments, medical facilities, medical universities, and health associations are all permitted to issue CME credits to medical practitioners before 202529. However, the absence of unified formatting standards leads to substantial heterogeneity among certificates from different institutions. This could hinder the full verification of submitted certificates’ authenticity, especially for paper credits obtained across regions. Developing a unified digital credit management system has long been advocated in national policies to address these persistent problems27. Previous studies also indicated that information technology should be applied to optimize CME credit governance and achieve convenient, efficient, and cost-effective management30. A unified CME platform can support cross-regional issuance and verification of standardized electronic credits, and allow administrators to query and aggregate regional credit data to formulate targeted improvement strategies in a timely manner31. Overall, the lack of a nationwide unified CME credit management system has become a critical bottleneck restricting the standardized development of CME and may impede the achievement of national regulatory and administrative goals.

The results indicated that nurses were more likely to participate in off-disciplinary training solely for CME credit acquisition, compared with public health professionals. Prior studies have demonstrated a prominent work-study conflict among clinical nurses32. Furthermore, mainstream CME resources are predominantly concentrated on clinical medicine specialties4,33, whereas CME programs tailored specifically for nurses are in short supply and the overall resource allocation is unbalanced33. This structural imbalance may result in insufficient access to nursing-specific training activities. Although clinical nurses generally acknowledge the importance of updating professional knowledge and improving clinical competency through CME training32, they are confronted with extremely heavy daily workloads and a severe lack of discipline-matched CME resources. Under such combined pressures, nurses may turn to off-disciplinary courses to meet the annual mandatory credit requirements.

Grassroots healthcare professionals tended to obtain credits through off-disciplinary training and distance education, and were the least likely to gain credits by attending academic conferences. Compared with staff at higher-level medical institutions, grassroots professionals generally have lower academic qualifications, relatively limited clinical proficiency, and serve in rural regions, which may appear to result in relatively limited recognition of the long-term value of CME. To complete mandatory annual credit requirements, they tend to participate in locally available but limited courses (even off-disciplinary ones). Relevant studies have reported that rural healthcare professionals in China face inadequate CME training opportunities34. Most accredited academic conferences are more likely to be held in urban areas to expand influence and are rarely arranged at grassroots sites. In addition, such activities also usually charge registration fees. Restricted by travel costs, registration fees, and persistent work-study conflict, grassroots healthcare professionals may prefer distance education due to its affordability, convenience, and flexibility23,35.

Healthcare professionals working at provincial-level institutions were significantly more likely to perceive the current CME credit requirements as excessively burdensome than grassroots healthcare professionals. This discrepancy may be explained by two intertwined factors related to institutional credit rules and resource accessibility. First, grassroots medical institutions implemented much lower annual mandatory credit requirements than provincial ones before 2024. Second, grassroots staff also face limited access to high-level, high-standard training resources, which means they have fewer opportunities to engage in advanced CME activities that might raise their perception of credit pressure. In contrast, provincial-level institutions in Sichuan Province are mostly tertiary hospitals. Their employees were required to complete a total of 25 annual credits (including ≥ 3 national-level credits), with 15 Type Ⅰ credits and 10 Type Ⅱ credits before the 2025 policy update14. These staff bear heavy routine clinical workloads and have limited spare time for CME participation36. Meanwhile, they are required to attend diversified training activities to meet classified credit rules. These factors may collectively explain their stronger perception of credit burden.

Noticeable differences were observed between private and public hospital staff regarding CME participation objectives, selection of off-disciplinary training, and credit acquisition channels. Specifically, employees in private hospitals were more likely to regard credit earning as the sole purpose of CME participation and to choose off-disciplinary training. Multiple limitations of CME management in private hospitals have been reported in prior studies, including the lack of dedicated CME management departments and staff, insufficient financial investment, low institutional attention, and inadequate training supervision37–39. Besides, most staff in private hospitals are transferred from primary or secondary hospitals, while new recruits are mostly junior college graduates or retired employees from public hospitals. This group generally shows low organizational loyalty and high turnover rates39,40. Influenced by the above institutional and personnel characteristics, private hospital staff may tend to prioritize completing mandatory credit tasks at the lowest cost, rather than focusing on the practical value or discipline matching of training activities.

Limitations

This study has several notable limitations. First, the selection of merely 5 municipalities from 21 across Sichuan Province may cause selection bias and limit the representativeness of findings regarding regional CME credit management. Second, the sample distribution across healthcare professional subgroups was also unbalanced, and small sample sizes for provincial, grassroots, private, and public health personnel prevented a comprehensive understanding of their perspectives. Third, although the findings may provide practical insights for updating the local CME credit system, the outdated data mean some findings are not fully applicable to the current situation and require careful interpretation. Fourth, the sample size of this study was not calculated using a formal statistical formula. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting the adequacy of statistical power for the results deprived from statistical inference. Fifth, the self-designed questionnaire only received reviews from senior CME specialists, with no formal pre-testing or quantitative reliability and validity tests conducted. This may compromise the robustness of the survey results and introduce potential bias to the findings. Finally, all data rely on self-reports, social desirability bias, recall bias, and common method bias act as inevitable confounders that may affect the overall reliability of this study.

Conclusion

This study investigated the perceptions and practical implementation of the CME credit system among administrators and healthcare professionals in Sichuan Province, China. Differing from existing studies that focus solely on CME credit completion or feedback from a single stakeholder, this study offers empirical evidence from dual perspectives and multiple groups, addressing local research gaps in regional CME credit governance. CME administrators prioritized standardized credit verification and the development of a unified information system. Nurses, healthcare professionals at grassroots and private medical institutions frequently participate in off-disciplinary training to obtain credits, and grassroots healthcare professionals preferred distance education. In contrast, provincial-level healthcare professionals perceived current credit requirements as overly demanding. Furthermore, healthcare professionals in private hospitals generally prioritized credit acquisition as the core motivation for CME participation.

To address the above issues, several targeted recommendations are suggested. First, accelerate the development of a unified digital CME information platform to standardize credit verification and management procedures. Second, optimize the credit rules for healthcare professionals across all institutional levels. Third, balance CME resource allocation by expanding speciality coverage for special healthcare workers and facilitating access to high-quality courses for grassroots workers. Finally, strengthen CME governance in private hospitals to help raise employees’ awareness of the practical value of CME.
