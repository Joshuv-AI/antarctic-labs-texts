Systemic Inflammation in CKD: Signal, Surrogate, or Therapeutic Target?

Systemic inflammation is pervasive in advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), where it is a central component of the “malnutrition-inflammation-atherosclerosis” complex (Figure 1). Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and related biomarkers in this setting track with protein-energy wasting, vascular calcification, and an excess burden of cardiovascular events and premature death. However, key questions remain unsettled: how far does inflammation independently shape cardiovascular and renal trajectories in nondialysis CKD, and can simple markers such as high-sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) meaningfully refine risk stratification beyond traditional factors and kidney measures?Figure 1hsCRP as a marker of systemic inflammation and adverse outcomes in chronic kidney disease. In the article by Halimi et al.1, they tackle these questions using a very large real-world cohort assembled from the TriNetX federated electronic health record network. They identified 163,854 adults with CKD at appreciable risk of progression—defined by estimated glomerular filtration rate of 15 to 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes G3–G4) or albumin-to-creatinine ratio > 200 mg/g (A3)—who had hsCRP measured and no evidence of concomitant infection. This scale as well as the diversity of contributing health care organisations across 17 countries, confer substantial statistical power and broad external validity.

The authors dichotomized patients by hsCRP ≥ 2 versus < 2 mg/l, a threshold widely used in cardiovascular prevention, and perform 1:1 propensity score matching on 57 covariates covering demographics; comorbidities; laboratory values; and medications, including renin–angiotensin system blockade, statins, glucose-lowering drugs, and antithrombotic therapies. After matching, 27,580 patients in each hsCRP stratum formed a well-balanced main cohort with the following characteristics: mean age of 72 years; about 54% men; and with close alignment of blood pressure, lipid profile, glycated hemoglobin, estimated glomerular filtration rate, albuminuria, and cardio-metabolic treatments.

Within this framework, systemic inflammation emerges as a powerful marker of short- to medium-term risk. Over a median follow-up of approoximately 1.5 to 2 years (≤ 6 years in some), individuals with hsCRP ≥ 2 mg/l experienced almost a doubling of all-cause mortality compared with matched controls (hazard ratio: 1.94, 95% confidence interval: 1.85–2.03). In addition, they had substantially higher incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events and related outcomes, including ischemic stroke or thromboembolism, myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation, cardiac arrest, incident heart failure, and heart failure hospitalization. These associations persisted despite close matching for kidney function, albuminuria, and a broad array of cardiovascular risk factors and treatments.

By contrast, the risk for ESKD in the overall cohort was more modest. The annualized incidence of ESKD was low in both hsCRP strata (∼ 0.2%/yr), and the hazard ratio of 1.27 (95% confidence interval: 0.89–1.81) did not reach statistical significance. Given the predominance of stage 3 CKD (median estimated glomerular filtration rate of 52 ml/min per 1.73 m2) and limited median follow-up, this was not unexpected; ESKD is a late event, and more prolonged observation is usually needed to reveal differences in progression.

The prespecified analysis in patients with diabetes mellitus therefore carries particular weight. Among 53,078 individuals having diabetes mellitus with CKD and hsCRP data, the authors generated 10,850 matched pairs with hsCRP ≥ 2 versus < 2 mg/l. In this high-risk subgroup, elevated hsCRP again identifies a markedly higher risk of death and cardiovascular events. Importantly, however, the association with ESKD becomes stronger and reaches the conventional threshold of statistical significance (hazard ratio: 1.51, 95% confidence interval: 1.00–2.28; P = 0.05). In the equally well-matched nondiabetic cohort, the associations between hsCRP and cardiovascular events and mortality remain robust, but the ESKD signal remains nonsignificant. These findings suggest that systemic inflammation may contribute more directly to kidney disease progression in the metabolically vulnerable diabetic phenotype.

This work extends previous observational data in several ways. In the CRIC study, adding inflammatory markers to measures of kidney function improved prediction of atherosclerotic events and death in CKD.2 Smaller cohorts in advanced CKD and dialysis linked elevated CRP with cardiovascular disease burden and mortality.3,4 Halimi et al.1 generalize these observations to an order-of-magnitude larger, more heterogeneous population with earlier-stage CKD and severe albuminuria, using rigorous propensity matching across an unusually wide covariate set. Their detailed analysis of arrhythmic outcomes is particularly notable; patients with hsCRP ≥ 2 mg/l had substantially higher risks of atrial fibrillation and ventricular tachyarrhythmias, mirroring UK Biobank findings that inflammatory markers, including CRP, are positively and continuously related to incident atrial and ventricular arrhythmias in the general population.5 These convergent data strengthen the concept of inflammation as an arrhythmogenic driver in CKD, likely mediated via myocardial fibrosis, and adverse remodelling.6

In addition, the study situates itself squarely within the emerging therapeutic agenda around inflammation in cardio-renal disease. CANTOS showed that targeting interleukin-1β with canakinumab reduced recurrent cardiovascular events, with benefits closely tied to on-treatment hsCRP reduction and largely independent of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol change, including patients with CKD.7 More recently, RESCUE demonstrated that interleukin-6 ligand inhibition with ziltivekimab in patients with CKD stages 3 and 4, and systemic inflammation produced profound reductions in hsCRP (−77% to −92% vs. placebo) and favourable shifts in other inflammatory and thrombotic biomarkers.8 The ongoing ZEUS trial will test whether such modulation of interleukin-6 signaling in a population very similar to that studied by Halimi et al.1 translates into fewer cardiovascular events and slower CKD progression. Their epidemiologic findings therefore provide an important bridge between observational nephrology and these mechanistically targeted trials.

The usual limitations of electronic health record–based research apply. Coding of diagnoses, procedures, and outcomes, as well as laboratory methods and prescribing practices, vary across health care systems. Socioeconomic status, lifestyle, adherence, and frailty are incompletely captured, and residual confounding by unmeasured factors is inevitable despite meticulous propensity score matching. Exclusion of patients with documented infection lessens, but does not abolish, the risk that elevated hsCRP reflects other acute or chronic inflammatory states such as autoimmune disease or occult malignancy. The relatively short median follow-up restricts inferences about long-term renal trajectories.

What, then, are the practical implications? For clinicians, the data support hsCRP as a pragmatic integrative marker of cardiovascular and arrhythmic risk in CKD G3–G4 or A3, particularly in diabetes. Elevated hsCRP should prompt a systematic search for modifiable inflammatory drivers, reinforcement of guideline-directed renin-angiotensin system blockade, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibition and glucagon-like peptide- receptor agonism where indicated, and intensified cardiovascular surveillance. For investigators, the study sharpens the rationale for targeting inflammatory pathways in CKD, while underlining that lowering hsCRP is not itself proof of benefit. Only adequately powered randomized trials such as ZEUS can determine whether modulation of interleukin-6–driven inflammation confers durable cardio-renal protection.9

In summary, Halimi et al.1 convincingly show that in a large, contemporary CKD population, systemic inflammation indexed by hsCRP ≥ 2 mg/l is associated with substantially higher risks of major adverse cardiovascular events, arrhythmias and death, and, in diabetic CKD, with a higher risk of ESKD. Their work reinforces inflammation as a central axis of vulnerability in CKD and sets the stage for the definitive tests of targeted antiinflammatory therapy now underway.
