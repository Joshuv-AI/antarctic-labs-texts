Beyond chronological age: physiological reserve and myocardial recovery potential in a european registry of microaxial flow pump in cardiogenic shock
Authors: Connor O’Brien, Antoine Kimmoun, Bertrand Rozec
Published: 2026

Publication of the DanGer Shock trial has generated a fervor in the mechanical circulatory support (MCS) community. The field of MCS has been plagued by negative randomized controlled trials and largely relies on registry-based studies to guide evolution in practice. While the DanGer Shock trial carries significant limitations in its generalizability beyond acute myocardial infarction cardiogenic shock (AMI CS), the trial offers a tremendous opportunity for exploratory analyses to identify which AMI CS patients may benefit from routine use of a microaxial flow pump (mAFP) and which may not [1]. Systolic blood pressure, early lactate clearance, and age have been shown to predict responders and non-responders within the DanGer Shock cohort [[2], [3], [4]]. Opportunity exists to validate these findings in larger cohorts.

In this issue of Annals of Intensive Care, Ughetto et al. report a retrospective analysis of a multi-national, European cohort of CS patients supported with a mAFP (impella CP, 5, and 5.5) [5]. Impella CP provides short-term percutaneous left ventricular (LV) support with flow rates of around 3–4 L/min, whereas the larger Impella 5.0 and 5.5 pumps are surgically implanted for higher flow rates of around 5–5.5 L/min, enabling more durable unloading and circulatory support. Ughetto et al. evaluate in this population how age impacts survival to discharge and myocardial recovery (Central Figure) [5]. To perform this analysis, they separated patients by age quartile (Q1 ≤52 years, Q2 52–60 years, Q3 60–69 years, and Q4 ≥69 years. Given competing risks for death in this population, the Ughetto et al. use a Fine-Gray competing risks model to assess the association between age and mortality. Myocardial recovery was defined as ≥10% improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) [5]. Ughetto et al. assembled a cohort with even distribution of Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) stage shock severity across age quartile. Interestingly, Q4 had the greatest fraction of SCAI B shock, which will bare relevance later in this discussion. As expected, the older quartiles had a greater incidence of comorbidities and AMICS represented a large fraction of CS etiology in these patients. Unsurprisingly, mortality increased in a linear fashion across the cohorts both at 30 days and one year. Most death events occurred in the first 30-days, but Q3 and Q4 continued to experience greater mortality than Q1 and Q2 out to one year. Each additional year of age carried a 3.2% increase in mortality within this cohort [5].

The authors go on to study LVEF recovery finding a strong and linear trend in reduced recovery across quartiles with as high as 74.5% in Q1 and as low as 41.9% in Q4. Looking at these data differently, Q1 experienced a median 133% improvement in LVEF from baseline while Q4 experienced only 25% median improvement. Older patients were less likely to be supported with advanced intensive care unit (ICU) therapies and were offered LVAD and transplant less frequently than younger patients [5]. Taken together, these data inform the differences in cause of death seen between quartiles with younger patients dying more commonly from anoxic brain injury and multi-organ failure while older patients most commonly die from cardiovascular complications. Importantly, age was not associated with an increased risk of device related complications, although the shorter duration of mAFP support in Q4 should be taken into account, suggesting device complications do not bely differences in rates at which patients were supported with ICU therapies or offered transplant or left ventricular assist device (LVAD).

These results are highly consistent with a sub analysis by of DanGer Shock by Klein et al. showing a step-wise increase in mortality when patients were analyzed by age quartile [3]. Klein et al. showed no difference in mortality between those treated with mAFP vs standard of care in patients ≥77 years old. Patients ≥77 years old who died within this cohort had longer time from symptom onset to presentation (4 vs 8 h), lower LVEF on arrival (20 vs 30%), and were more likely to be SCAI D/E than survivors bringing into question whether the lack of treatment effect could be explained by differences in disease severity. Ughetto et al. both identify a similar mortality trend by age as well as show this trend holds up when LVEF and prevalence of SCAI D/E are similar by quartile. Importantly, Q4 in Ughetto’s study has the highest rate of SCAI B shock and the highest mortality of all quartiles [5].

Findings made by Ughetto et al. highlight important themes from the DanGer Shock trial that resonate across the CS literature. First, age is strong prognostic factor for poor outcome in CS. Much like Ughetto’s findings, in an all-comers CS population dividing patients into age quintiles shows a 47% increase in mortality between each quintile even when matched by SCAI shock stage and disease severity biomarkers [6]. In addition to carrying more comorbidities, older patients are less likely to recover LV function [7]. This holds significant relevance as across all CS trials refractory CS is a dominant cause of mortality even in patients treated with MCS [4,8,9]. Consistent across all studies, 70 years of age seems to be a threshold at which increased risk for poor LVEF recovery and mortality is reproducible. Given patients older than 70 are also less frequently candidates for transplant and LVAD they may not benefit from mAFP support like younger patients. Although differences in healthcare organization, reimbursement pathways, and access to advanced heart failure therapies could have been expected to result in heterogeneous mAFP indications across countries, Ughetto’s results suggest consistent managements across this European cohort [5]. However, access to definitive bridge therapies (LVAD, heart transplantation) may still vary substantially across European healthcare systems, as heart transplantation eligibility and prioritization criteria (MCS prioritization for heart transplantation) are not uniformly shared.

In an increasingly cost-constrained health systems, determining how mAFP can be used efficiently is an essential evolution in practice. Findings by Ughetto et al. highlight the challenges associated with improving outcome in CS patients ≥70 years of age [5]. Importantly, the adage that age is just a number likely holds truth here. The increased prevalence of comorbidities likely belies poor outcomes and offers an opportunity for nuanced study to understand the mechanistic underpinnings of poor outcomes in these patients.
