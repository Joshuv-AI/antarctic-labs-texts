A conserved function of EZH2-PRC2 in repression of primitive endoderm fate in mouse and human pluripotent cells
Authors: Junyu Chen, Boyan Huang, Xuzhao Zhai, Zhe Xu, Shu Sun, Jie Hu, Xinyi Dai, Guokai Chen, Dan Liang, Lin Liu, Man Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn early blastocyst, inner cell mass (ICM) undergoes a second wave of lineage commitment to generate epiblast (Epi) and primitive endoderm (PrE), restricting the developmental potential of their progeny. However, the epigenetic mechanism underlying this lineage segregation remains unclear. Here, we reveal that enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) inhibition with EPZ-6438 transdifferentiates murine embryonic stem cells (ESCs) into primitive endoderm stem cells (PrESCs). Genetic ablation of Ezh2 in ESCs reduces the trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone 3 (H3K27me3) deposition at the promoters of PrE-specific genes, derepressing their expression and facilitating the PrESC conversion. Reconstitution with wild-type EZH2, but not catalytically inactive or EED-binding-deficient mutants in EZH2-deficient ESCs, blocks this transition. Strikingly, EZH2 inhibition reduces epiblast cell numbers in blastocysts, with 45.9% of embryos exhibiting ICMs composed of no SOX2-positive cells. Furthermore, EPZ-6438 treatment in human naive ESCs upregulates hypoblast-associated genes. In sum, these results reveal an evolutionarily conserved role for EZH2-polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) in safeguarding pluripotency from primitive endoderm cell fate.Keywords: primitive endoderm, hypoblast, primitive endoderm stem cells, PrESCs, transdifferentiation, EZH2-PRC2 complex, pluripotent stem cells

Introduction

In mammals, shortly after fertilization, the totipotent embryo initiates the first cell fate segregation to generate trophectoderm (TE) and inner cell mass (ICM). While the TE gives rise to the placenta, the ICM undergoes a second lineage segregation, generating the pluripotent epiblast (Epi) and the primitive endoderm (PrE), another extraembryonic lineage that contributes predominantly to the yolk sac (Chowdhary and Hadjantonakis, 2022; Saiz and Plusa, 2013). These three lineages form a blastocyst before implantation and eventually develop to an embryo with all the extraembryonic and embryonic tissues. Stem cell lines derived from the blastocyst, including embryonic stem cells (ESCs, derived from Epi), trophoblast stem cells (derived from TE), and extraembryonic endoderm stem cells (XENCs) or primitive endoderm stem cells (PrESCs, derived from PrE), have been successfully established (Evans and Kaufman, 1981; Kunath et al., 2005; Ohinata and Koseki, 2022; Tanaka et al., 1998). These cell lines have been widely utilized to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the establishment and maintenance of their distinct cellular properties.

The PrE lineage is the origin of the extraembryonic endoderm of the visceral and parietal yolk sac (Jollie, 1990). These structures are essential for the development of the embryo, as they provide (1) nutritional support for embryo development and (2) signaling cues for anterior-posterior patterning and hematopoiesis in the yolk sac (Brennan et al., 2001; Jollie, 1990). It has been shown that PrE and Epi precursors are intermingled in the ICM of the E3.5 blastocyst, in a “salt and pepper” pattern (Chazaud et al., 2006; Rossant et al., 2003). Subsequently, these two populations separate into two defined layers by a series of mechanisms. Mutual exclusive expression of two master transcription factors, NANOG and GATA6, in ICM cells determined the cell fate of Epi and PrE in both mouse and human (Chambers et al., 2003; Schrode et al., 2014; Singh et al., 2007). Gata6-deficient mouse embryos fail to specify the PrE lineage in the blastocyst (Morrisey et al., 1998; Schrode et al., 2014), while forced expression of GATA6 in both mouse and human naive ESCs triggers their differentiation to PrE cells (Fujikura et al., 2002; Okubo et al., 2023). In addition, paracrine fibroblast growth factor/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling in ICM is critical for the PrE lineage commitment in both mouse and human embryos by repressing NANOG and reinforcing the transcriptional network of PrE (Chazaud et al., 2006; Kang et al., 2013; Molotkov et al., 2017). Albeit these mechanisms, however, the epigenetic mechanisms underlying this second cell fate determination need to be further addressed. Two types of stem cells, XENCs and PrESCs, can be established from the extraembryonic endoderm of blastocyst. XENCs do not fully recapitulate the properties of early PrE lineage of the blastocyst and mainly contribute to the parietal endoderm in the chimera assay (Kunath et al., 2005). However, PrESCs exhibited a transcriptomic profile more closely resembling that of embryonic day 4.5 founder PrE cells and were demonstrated to have the capacity to generate fully functional derivatives of PrE lineages (Ohinata and Koseki, 2022). Therefore, the transition between ESCs and PrESCs provides a more physiologically relevant in vitro model for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying cell fate segregation between the Epi and PrE. Moreover, while XENCs could be obtained from ESCs by adding RA plus Activin or CHIRON (Li et al., 2022; Niakan et al., 2013), whether PrESCs can also be successfully established from ESCs need to be assessed.

Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) is an essential chromatin modifier that represses the transcription of target genes through trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone 3 (H3K27me3). It is conserved across organisms from plants to flies and humans (O’Meara and Simon, 2012). The core PRC2 is composed of four subunits: enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) or its paralog EZH1, EED, SUZ12, and the histone chaperone RBAP46/48. Among them, EZH2 is the major catalytic subunit of the PRC2 that methylates lysine 27 of histone 3. EZH2 plays an important role at different stages of embryo development and in multiple cancers (Blackledge and Klose, 2021). Zygotic Ezh2-deficient embryos (with the first 200 amino acids of EZH2 fused with LacZ) either cease developing after implantation or fail to complete gastrulation. Interestingly, a portion of the mutant embryos (27%) seem to have no Epi but only extraembryonic tissues, suggesting the vital function of EZH2 in early embryogenesis (O'Carroll et al., 2001). Within pluripotent stem cells, EZH2 or PRC2 is dispensable for the maintenance of both mouse and human naive cells but is critical for the proper differentiation of ESCs (Chamberlain et al., 2008; Lavarone et al., 2019; Petracovici and Bonasio, 2021; Shan et al., 2017; Shen et al., 2008). Cells without the activity of PRC2 impaired the ectoderm differentiation and were prone to differentiate into meso-endoderm. In this work, we demonstrated that both genetic ablation and pharmacological inhibition of EZH2 in mouse ESCs significantly enhanced their transdifferentiation efficiency into PrESCs. Moreover, human naive ESCs treated with EZH2 inhibitor also increased the expression level of hypoblast-specific genes. These findings suggest a conserved function for the EZH2-PRC2 complex in repressing PrE cell fate in naive pluripotent ESCs in both mouse and human.

Results

EZH2 inhibition enhances ESC-to-PrESC transdifferentiation efficiency

Previous work identified platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRα) as an early marker of PrE cells in blastocysts (Artus et al., 2010). To indicate PrE cell identity, thosea asigna virus 2A (T2A)-GFP and T2A-BFP-H2B cassettes were respectively inserted upstream of the stop codon in the Nanog and Pdgfrα coding sequence to establish a Nanog-GFP/Pdgfrα-BFP (NGPB) dual reporter cell line from E14Tg2a ESCs (Figures S1A–S1C). To investigate the epigenetic mechanisms restricting PrE lineage specification in ESCs, we performed a small-molecule screen focusing on inhibitors of key epigenetic modifiers. It revealed that tazemetostat (EPZ-6438), a selective EZH2 inhibitor, gave rise to the highest population of GFP-negative/BFP-positive cells (29.7%) among these inhibitors in a concentration-dependent manner (Figures 1A and 1B; Figure S1D). Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) revealed that Pdgfrα-BFP+ cells sorted from EPZ-6438-treated ESCs at day 4 downregulated pluripotent genes Nanog and Sox2 and upregulated PrE-specific genes, including Sox17, Gata6, and Pdgfra, with mRNA expression levels comparable to embryo-derived PrESCs (C57-PrESCs) (Figure S1E). For stable PrESC derivation, NGPB reporter ESCs were treated with 2 μM EPZ-6438 in PrESC induction medium for 4 days, followed by fluorescence-activated sorting and expansion of Pdgfrα-BFP+ cells to establish stable lines (Figure 1C). Three independent EPZ-6438-induced PrESCs (EPZ-iPrESCs) were established from sorted Nanog-negative/BFP-positive cells. Two of them (EPZ-iPrESCs-1 and EPZ-iPrESCs-2) were used for the following characterization after more than 10 passages (Figures 1D and 1E). In addition, to exclude the possibility of clonal specificity of NGPB cells, two EPZ-iPrESC lines were also successfully established in the same way from wild-type E14tg2a by sorting the EPZ-6438-treated cells with CD140a (PDGFRα) antibody (Figures S1F and S1G). RT-qPCR revealed that EPZ-iPrESCs derived from NGPB cells expressed high mRNA expression levels of PrE-specific genes, Sox7, Sox17, Gata6, and Lama1, and low mRNA expression levels of pluripotency-specific genes, Nanog and Sox2, comparable to C57-PrESCs (Figure 1F). In addition, immunostaining revealed that SOX17 and GATA6 but not NANOG were expressed in EPZ-iPrESCs (Figure 1G). It is noted that while XENCs do not express OCT4 (Ohinata and Koseki, 2022), iPrESCs, which are similar to embryo-derived PrESCs, had OCT4 in cells (Figure 1G). Moreover, both principal-component analysis and Pearson correlation analysis of bulk transcriptome data revealed that EPZ-iPrESCs clustered closely with the C57-PrESCs while remaining distinct from parental NGPB ESCs (Figures 1H and 1I). In addition, both EPZ-iPrESC clones exhibited a similar gene expression pattern to C57-PrESCs, elevated expression levels of representative PrE marker genes, and downregulated expression of pluripotent genes (Figure 1J).

EPZ-iPrESCs exhibit the differentiation potential of PrE lineage in vivo

To assess the developmental potential of EPZ-iPrESCs in vivo, we labeled the NGPB-derived EPZ-iPrESCs with continuously expressed mCherry and C57-PrESCs with GFP, respectively, and then aggregated them with 8-cell stage mouse embryos (Figure 2A; Figure S2A). At the blastocyst stage, both GFP-positive and mCherry-positive cells efficiently incorporated into the ICM in majority chimeric embryos, predominantly localizing to the upper layer of ICM (Figure 2B; Figure S2B). Immunostaining revealed that mCherry-positive EPZ-iPrESCs, similar to GFP-positive C57-PrESCs, expressed the PrE-specific marker GATA6 but not the pluripotency marker SOX2 in chimeric embryos (Figure 2C; Figure S2B). It is noted that although both GFP- and mCherry-positive cells from PrESCs in the chimeric embryos expressed GATA6, their GATA6 levels were lower than those of endogenous PrE cells in host embryos. To further assess the differentiation potential of EPZ-iPrESCs in vivo, we treated the host embryos with PD0325901 (PD), a mitogen-activated protein kinase/ERK inhibitor, to block FGF4 signaling in the ICM to eliminate endogenous PrE cells in the blastocyst (Figures S2C and S2D) (Nichols et al., 2009; Yamanaka et al., 2010). We microinjected GFP-labeled C57-PrESCs or mCherry-labeled EPZ-iPrESCs cells into the PD-treated blastocysts and transferred them into the oviducts of pseudo-pregnant recipients. Embryos were then dissected at E7.5 (Figure 2D). PrESC injection significantly improved implantation and developmental rates of PD-treated embryos compared to controls (Figure 2E). Moreover, fluorescence-positive cells from C57-PrESCs or EPZ-iPrESCs were localized to Reichert’s membrane and the outer layer of epiblasts of the chimeras, regions typically occupied by parietal endoderm and visceral endoderm. Although EPZ-iPrESC incorporation was less efficient compared to C57-PrESCs (Figures 2E and 2F), immunostaining confirmed GATA6 expression in GFP+ or mCherry+ cells, verifying their parietal endoderm identity (Figure 2G). These results demonstrate that EPZ-iPrESCs retain the potential to differentiate into PrE-derived lineages in vivo.

EZH2-PRC2 complex suppresses PrE lineage fate in mouse ESCs

To directly assess the function of EZH2 in the suppression of PrE lineage fate in ESCs, we generated two independent Ezh2 knockout NGPB clones (Ezh2−/−-10 and Ezh2−/−-12) by deleting the entire genomic sequence of Ezh2 with Cas9 and two guide RNAs (Figure 3A; Figures S3A and S3B). Both qPCR and western blotting confirmed the successful deletion of Ezh2 in the two Ezh2−/− clones (Figures 3B and 3C). Consistent with previous works (Lavarone et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2008), EZH2-deficient ESCs were capable of self-renewal in the fetal bovine serum plus LIF medium (SL), albeit with a flattened morphology (Figure 3A). Moreover, rescue cell lines were generated by ectopic overexpression of wild-type EZH2 in EZH2-deficient clones (Figures 3B and 3C; Figure S3C). EZH2-deficient cells exhibited markedly reduced levels of H3K27me2 and H3K27me3 modifications compared to wild-type ESCs, while maintaining robust H3K27me1 modification consistent with compensatory activity of EZH1 (Figure S3D) (Shen et al., 2008). Several works reported that EZH2-deficient ESCs had no profound effects on the derepression of the endoderm-specific transcription factors, such as Gata6 or Gata4 (Lavarone et al., 2019; Qin et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2008). However, our RT-qPCR revealed that EZH2-deficient ESCs from both clones upregulated the mRNA expression levels of Sox7, Sox17, and Gata6, the three key transcription factors of PrE cells, and ectopic overexpression of EZH2 rescued the derepression (Figure 3D). This inconsistency between our data and that of previous works may attribute to the different culture conditions we used for the ESCs. In their work, EZH2-deficient cells were either cultured in LIF plus 2i medium (Lavarone et al., 2019; Ying et al., 2008) or on feeders (Qin et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2008) to suppress potential spontaneous differentiation, whereas, in our work, cells were cultured on gelatin with SL only. To test this hypothesis, we cultured the two Ezh2−/− clones in the 2i plus LIF medium (2iL). Notably, cells cultured in 2iL medium (over 6 passages) impaired the upregulation of the key transcription factors of PrE lineage (Figure S3E). Moreover, immunostaining for SOX17 revealed that while there were a considerable number of SOX17-positive cells in Ezh2−/− cells cultured in the SL medium, there were almost no SOX17-positive cells in 2iL medium (Figure S3F). We then cultured the wild-type and two Ezh2−/− NGPB clones in the PrESC medium for 4 days to induce PrESCs. Compared to the wild-type parental cells that contained around 1% GFP-negative/BFP-positive cells in the PrESC medium, both Ezh2−/− clones remarkably increased the population of GFP-negative/BFP-positive cells, reaching over 50% at peak levels (Figure 3E). Conversely, overexpression of EZH2 in the EZH2-deficient cells suppressed this increase, restoring the GFP-negative/BFP-positive population to near-control levels (Figure 3E). In addition, iPrESCs were successfully established from sorted Ezh2−/− GFP-negative/BFP-positive cells (Figures S3G, S3H, and S3I). EZH2 mainly function as the histone catalytic subunit of PRC2 to repress gene transcription through catalyzing H3K27me3. To further assess EZH2 function in repressing the PrE lineage, we constructed two mutated forms of EZH2 and introduced them into the genome of Ezh2−/− clones. One is the catalytic inactive point mutation (tyrosine-to-aspartic acid substitution at amino acid 726 of EZH2, Y726D) (Lavarone et al., 2019), and the other is the EED-binding domain-deleted (ΔEBD) mutation (Han et al., 2007) (Figure S3C). Those cells were then cultured in the PrESC medium for 4 days to induce PrE-like cells. Unlike the wild-type EZH2, both EZH2 mutations lost their repression function and showed a comparable ratio of GFP-negative/BFP-positive cells to their corresponding Ezh2−/− clones, indicating that both the catalytic activity and the interaction of EZH2 with its partner are essential to repress PrE cell fate in ESCs (Figures 3F and 3G). In addition, our data showed that knockdown of Eed or Suz12, the other two PRC2 core components (Figures S3J and S3K), increased the mRNA expression levels of PrE-specific genes and the population of GFP-negative/BFP-positive cells in the PrESC medium (Figures S3L and S3M). These results indicated that EZH2 functions as the catalytic subunit of PRC2 to suppress PrE lineage in ESCs.

EZH2-PRC2 represses PrE-specific genes by depositing H3K27me3 modification in their promoter regions

To further explore the underlying mechanism of EZH2-PRC2 in the repression of PrE cell fate, we first identified a group of PrE-specific genes (n = 1960), which are highly expressed in PrESCs by comparing the transcriptome of embryo-derived C57-PrESCs with C57-ESCs and E14Tg2a-NGPB ESCs (Figures S4A and S4B; Table S1). Bulk transcriptome analysis in wild-type ESCs, PrESCs, Ezh2−/− clone 10 (Ezh2−/−-10), and EZH2-rescued cells with overexpression of wild-type Ezh2 (Ezh2−/−-10-OE) identified 2,364 upregulated and 1,604 downregulated genes in EZH2 knockout cells (fold change >2, adjusted p value <0.05). Notably, the majority of these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were restored by Ezh2 overexpression (Figure 4A). Among those upregulated DEGs in Ezh2−/− cells, 421 genes were PrE-specific genes, including the PrE determinants Gata6, Gata4, Sox17, and Sox7 (Figure S4C). Interestingly, consistent with the aforementioned results, Ezh2−/− cells cultured in the 2iL medium impaired the upregulation of the PrE lineage determinant gene expression. 971 genes were downregulated in the 2iL medium, with 100 of them being PrE-specific genes, including Gata6, Gata4, Sox17, and Sox7 (Figures S4D–S4F). Furthermore, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) for H3K27me3 was performed in wild-type ESCs, PrESCs, Ezh2−/− clones, and EZH2-rescued cells. The data revealed that 79.4% (1,557/1,960) of PrE-specific genes had H3K27me3 modification around their transcription start site (± 1 kb) in wild-type ESCs. These modifications showed reduced levels in PrESCs. In Ezh2−/− clones, H3K27me3 levels were dramatically decreased in the majority of these regions but were rescued by wild-type EZH2 overexpression (Figure 4B). Furthermore, 434 of those genes were bound by EZH2 (GEO: GSE23943) (Marks et al., 2012) and EED (GEO: GSE217134) (Weigert et al., 2023) in the regions with H3K27me3 modification (Figures 4B and 4C). Among these, 44.7% (194/434) exhibited upregulated mRNA expression in Ezh2−/− ESCs cultured in the SL medium, including the PrE-specific genes Gata6, Gata4, Sox7, Sox17, Pdgfrα, and Foxa2, suggesting that those genes were directly regulated by the EZH2-PRC2 complex (Figure 4D; Table S1). It is noted that definitive endoderm and extraembryonic endoderm share key transcription factors, such as Gata6, Gata4, and Sox17. To clarify whether the EZH2-PRC2 complex directly repressed primitive endoderm cell fate in ESCs, we performed differential gene expression analysis by comparing our in-house single-cell RNA-seq data (scRNA-seq) of primitive endoderm cells from E4.0–E4.5 embryos and definitive endoderm cells from E7.0–E7.5 embryos. The analysis identified 6,215 genes that were highly expressed in embryonic PrE cells, among which 903 genes overlapped with our PrE-specific gene list (Figures S4G–S4I; Table S1). Additionally, 546 genes were upregulated in definitive endoderm cells. Notably, among the 194 genes whose promoter regions were bound by EZH2 and EED and showed upregulated expression in Ezh2−/− cells, 108 of them (55.7%) were enriched in embryonic PrE cells, including Sox7, Pdgfrα, Lama1, and Foxq1, whereas, only 7 genes were definitive endoderm-specific genes, including Tmprss2, a marker gene of midgut and hindgut endoderm (Moore-Scott et al., 2007) (Figure 4C; Table S1). These results indicated that the EZH2-PRC2 complex directly bound the promoter regions of not only endoderm-shared transcription factors but also PrE-specific genes to repress their transcription by depositing H3K27me3 in their regulatory regions.

Moreover, both our previous work (Huang et al., 2024) and our screening results showed that treatment with histone deacetylase inhibitor, like sodium butyrate (NaB), also induced extraembryonic endoderm-like cells in ESCs. We then cultured Ezh2−/− clones in the PrESC medium for 4 days with the addition of NaB. The cytometry analysis revealed that NaB addition greatly increased the proportion of GFP-negative/BFP-positive cells from 68.6% up to 88.3% in Ezh2−/− clone 10 and from 45.1% up to 85.7% in Ezh2−/− clone 12, respectively, suggesting that HDACs and PRC2 function synergistically to repress PrE fate in mouse ESCs in an EZH2-independent manner (Figures 4E and 4F).

Interestingly, previous work showed that MGA, a core subunit of PRC1.6, could stabilize the PRC1.6 assembly including HDAC1/2 to repress endoderm-specific gene expression, partially by involving H2AK119ub1 in their promoter regions (Qin et al., 2012, 2021). H2AK119ub1 could be recognized by JARID2 (Cooper et al., 2016), a subunit of PRC2.2, to drive de novo enrichment of PRC2 to their target sites (Blackledge et al., 2014). MGA and H2AK119ub1 ChIP-seq analysis revealed that among the 434 PrE-specific genes marked by H3K27me3 and bound by EZH2/EED, the promoter regions of 329 (75.8%) genes were also bound by MGA and exhibited H2AK119ub1 modification (Figure S4J). Moreover, it was reported that ZFP281 interacted with SUZ12 to recruit PRC2 to repress endoderm genes in ESCs (Huang et al., 2017, 2021). ZFP281 knockout promoted the transition of mouse ESCs into XEN-like cells (Huang et al., 2017). Notably, among the 329 genes marked by both H3K27me3 and H2AK119ub1 and bound by EZH2/EED/MGA, 165 (50.2%) of them were also bound by ZFP281 (Figure S4K). These included key PrE regulators such as Gata6, Sox17, Sox7, Pdgfrα, and Foxa2 (Figures S4K and S4L). These findings suggest that both PRC1 and ZFP281 may contribute to recruit PRC2 in the regulatory regions to repress PrE-specific genes.

EZH2 inhibition compromises Epi and ICM size in mouse blastocysts

Given that the aforementioned findings suggest the EZH2-PRC2 complex directly regulates PrE cell fate in mouse ESCs, we further assessed whether EZH2 also plays a role in Epi/PrE lineage segregation in vivo. To investigate this, mouse embryos at 8-cell stage (E2.5) were treated with EPZ-6438 to inhibit the activities of EZH2 and cultured in vitro for another 3 days (Figure 5A). Immunostaining for SOX2, GATA6, and CDX2, the markers of Epi, PrE, and TE lineages, was performed on EPZ-treated and untreated embryos. The results revealed that while the untreated embryos contained average 8 SOX2-positive cells and 9.5 GATA6-positive cells in their ICM, in contrast, all EPZ-treated embryos exhibited a significant reduction of both SOX2- and GATA6-positive cells, with the average number decreasing to 1.0 for SOX2-positive cells and 3.6 for GATA6-positive cells in their ICM (Figures 5B and 5C). Notably, 45.9% of EPZ-treated embryos displayed a complete absence of SOX2-positive cells, retaining only GATA6-positive cells in their ICM. Interestingly, a subset of GATA6-positive cells in EPZ-treated embryos co-expressed CDX2. This aligned with previous findings reporting that while PrE cell fate can be initiated in the absence of Epi cells, its maintenance requires continuous Epi-derived signals (Frankenberg et al., 2011).Furthermore, we transplanted both EPZ-treated and untreated E4.5 embryos into the oviducts of pseudo-pregnant females and dissected the embryos at E7.5. While 9 morphologically normal embryos were obtained in 10 deciduas derived from untreated embryos, in contrast, only two embryo structures with Epi developmental defects were found in 19 deciduae derived from EPZ-treated embryos (Figures 5D and 5E). To further evaluate whether EPZ treatment affects the developmental potential of the two extraembryonic lineages, we injected GFP-labeled C57BL/6-derived ESCs (isolated from C57BL/6 blastocysts) into E4.5 EPZ-treated ICR blastocysts and transferred them into the oviduct of pseudo-pregnant females. Chimeras were successfully obtained, indicating that EPZ-treated embryos retain the ability to support normal embryo development (Figures 5F–5H).

EZH2 inhibition upregulates the expression of hypoblast-specific genes in human naive ESCs

Human hypoblast specification shares some conserved mechanisms with mice (Dattani et al., 2024; Okubo et al., 2023). To investigate whether EZH2 also represses the hypoblast cell fate in human ESCs, we differentiated the naive human H1 ESCs cultured in the PXGL medium (PD0325901, XAV939, Gö6983, and human leukemia inhibitory factor) in N2B27 with or without EPZ-6438 (Figure 5I). After 3 days of differentiation, RT-qPCR revealed that EPZ treatment significantly increased the mRNA expression levels of hypoblast-specific genes GATA4, GATA6, SOX17, and COL4A1 (Figure 5J). Moreover, cells were collected for the bulk transcriptome analysis (Figure S5A). Compared to naive H1 ESCs, EPZ-treated cells showed upregulated expression of 1,755 genes (log2FC > 1), including hypoblast markers GATA6, GATA4, SOX17, and COL4A1. In contrast, while 1,402 genes were upregulated in the N2B27 condition, only COL4A1 among these four hypoblast-specific genes showed increased expression (Figure 5K; Table S1). Interestingly, a re-analysis of published H3K27me3 ChIP-seq data in human naive cells (Zijlmans et al., 2022) revealed that the levels of H3K27me3 in the promoter region of GATA6, GATA4, and SOX17 but not the COL4A1 decreased upon the treatment of an inhibitor of EZH1/2, UNC1999 (Figure S5B). Previous studies revealed that the EZH2-PRC2 complex protects naive human ESCs from TE differentiation. Treatment of naive human ESCs with EPZ-6438 in the PXGL medium led to the upregulation of TE-specific transcription factors (Kumar et al., 2022). To further investigate this, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from human pre-implantation embryos (GEO: GSE171820) (Yanagida et al., 2021) and identified two gene sets: 158 hypoblast-specific genes and 152 trophoblast-specific genes (Figures S5C–S5E; Table S1). Intriguingly, our data revealed that EPZ-6438 treatment resulted in the upregulation of a comparable number of both hypoblast markers and TE lineage markers (Figure 5L; Figures S5E and S5F).

Discussion

Although several mechanisms, including cell signaling and transcription factors, underlying the PrE/Epi segregation were reported, the epigenetic regulation of this process remains to be elucidated. In our work, we demonstrated that EZH2-PRC2 plays an important role in safeguarding pluripotent cells from PrE lineage commitment.

Previous work showed that EED deficiency induced endoderm-specific transcription factors but not EZH2 (Boyer et al., 2006; Qin et al., 2021), likely due to the compensatory activity of EZH1 (Shen et al., 2008). In this work, our data showed that Ezh2-knockout mouse ESCs cultured in the SL medium upregulated primitive endoderm-specific genes in both mRNA and protein level and exhibit a flattened morphology, suggesting their active lineage priming toward the PrE lineage. Moreover, cells in SL expressed a comparable level of Ezh1 to that in 2iL medium, in both wild-type and Ezh2−/− cells (Figure S5G), indicating that EZH1 compensation is not enough to repress those genes in SL condition. In contrast, in 2iL medium, the MEK inhibitor PD0325901 suppresses ERK1/2 signaling, leading to the impaired upregulation of PrE-specific gene expression in EZH2 knockout cells (Ying et al., 2008).

Furthermore, our findings reveal that inhibition of EZH2 upregulates hypoblast-specific genes in human naive ESCs. Notably, previous studies have indicated that EZH2-PRC2 represses TE-specific transcripts in human naive cells (Zijlmans et al., 2022). Consistent with this, our data also revealed that EPZ-6438 treatment led to the upregulation of TE-specific transcripts. These results suggest that the PRC2 complex represses both TE and PrE lineage-specific transcripts in human naive ESCs. However, unlike in human naive ESCs, while EZH2-PRC2 represses the PrE lineage in mouse ESCs, our earlier work showed that the LSD1-HDAC1/2 repressor complex is responsible for repressing the TE lineage in them (Huang et al., 2024). These distinct epigenetic regulatory mechanisms may underlie the different lineage potential between human and mouse ESCs. Moreover, re-analysis of ChIP-seq datasets for MGA, H2AK119ub1, and ZFP281 suggests that both PRC1 and ZFP281 may contribute to recruit PRC2 in the regulatory regions to repress PrE-specific genes (Figure S5H). However, whether this regulatory mechanism is conserved in human naive ESCs remains to be investigated.

Methods

Mice

Animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou National Laboratory. Mice were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and housed in a temperature-controlled room under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to water and standard laboratory chow provided ad libitum.

Cell lines

Mouse ESC lines, PrESC lines, and human H1 ESC lines were employed for this study. E14Tg2a was used to generate NGPB (Nanog::GFP; Pdgfrα::BFP) reporter cell lines. The Ezh2 knockout cell lines and corresponding rescued cell lines were subsequently generated in-house from NGPB cell lines. NGPB cell line was additionally used to generate iPrESC cell lines. C57-PrESC cell lines were derived in-house from C57BL/6 mouse embryos. All cell lines used in this study were tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma, bacterial, and fungal contamination.

Establishment of Ezh2 knockout and Ezh2-rescued cell lines

The small-guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the Ezh2 locus was designed using the CRISPOR web tool (v.5.01; http://crispor.tefor.net) and commercially synthesized by Beijing Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The designed sgRNA was subsequently cloned into the pSpCas9-T2A-Puro (PX459) V2.0 vector (62988, Addgene). The recombinant plasmid was transfected into NGPB cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. The puro-resistant colonies were manually picked into 24-well plates and expanded. The sgRNA sequences and corresponding genotyping primers are detailed in Table S2.

For rescue experiments, PiggyBac (PB) transposon vectors encoding murine wild-type Ezh2, the Y726D point mutation variant, or EBD (EED-binding domain) deletion mutant were co-transfected with PB transposase vector into Ezh2−/− cells at a ratio of 3:1. Transfected cells underwent puromycin selection (1 μg/mL) to establish stable rescued cell lines.

Additional methods can be found in the supplemental methods.

PrESC induction

For PrESC induction, ESCs were dissociated and suspended in serum/LIF medium. A total of 3 × 105 cells were seeded per well in 6-well culture plates (3516, Corning) pre-coated with MEF feeder layers. Cells were maintained in 2 mL PrESC medium containing either vehicle control or 2 μM EPZ-6438 (S712807, Selleck Chemicals) for 4 days, with medium replacement every 48 h. Following this induction period, cells were dissociated using TrypLE Express. Subsequently, Pdgfrα::BFP-positive cells were sorted by fluorescence-activated cell soring and cultured in the PrESC medium on fresh MEF-coated 6-well plates. Established PrESC lines were passaged every 2–3 days.
