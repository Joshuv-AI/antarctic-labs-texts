Low-dose radiotherapy combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors in advanced malignancies: a real-world exploratory study
Authors: Kai Xin, Liping Deng, Meiqing Ding, Mengmeng Feng, Xiahui Wu, Lijing Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with advanced malignancies often have limited treatment options after failure of systemic therapy, and the prognosis remains poor. Low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT) has been proposed to enhance antitumor immunity and may improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). We aimed to evaluate the clinical activity and safety of LDRT combined with ICIs in patients with advanced malignancies in a real-world clinical setting.MethodsThis single-center retrospective real-world study included patients with advanced malignancies treated at Taikang Xianlin Drum Tower Hospital who received LDRT (2 Gy × 3 fractions, total dose 6 Gy) directed to progressive lesions in combination with programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors. Clinical characteristics, treatment details, tumor response, follow-up data, and treatment-related adverse events were retrospectively collected and analyzed. The primary endpoints were objective response rate (ORR) and progression-free survival (PFS), while the secondary endpoint was disease control rate (DCR). Safety was assessed according to the incidence of treatment-related adverse events.ResultsThirteen patients with various advanced malignancies were included in the analysis. The ORR was 38.5% and the DCR was 76.9%. The median PFS was 6.7 months. Notably, all documented disease progression occurred outside the irradiated fields, whereas the irradiated lesions remained locally controlled during follow-up. Treatment-related adverse events were mainly chemotherapy-related hematologic toxicities. No clinically significant immune-related adverse events, radiotherapy-related toxicities, or treatment-related deaths were observed.ConclusionsLDRT combined with ICIs showed preliminary clinical activity with acceptable tolerability in this small real-world cohort of patients with advanced malignancies. Although limited by the small sample size, this approach may represent a feasible treatment option for selected patients and should be further evaluated in prospective studies.Keywords: Low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT), immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), advanced malignancies, real-world study, immunotherapy

Highlight box

Key findings

• Low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT; 2 Gy ×3 fractions) combined with programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1 inhibitors demonstrated preliminary clinical activity in a real-world cohort of patients with advanced malignancies, achieving an objective response rate of 38.5% and a disease control rate of 76.9%.

• All documented disease progression events occurred outside the irradiated fields, suggesting potential local disease stabilization with LDRT.

• The combination was well tolerated, with no clinically significant immune-related or radiotherapy-related adverse events observed.

What is known and what is new?

• Radiotherapy can remodel the tumor immune microenvironment and enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). However, evidence in patients with advanced malignancies and poor performance status remains limited.

• This study provides real-world evidence supporting the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of LDRT combined with ICIs in this clinically challenging population.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• LDRT combined with ICIs may represent a feasible treatment option for selected patients with advanced malignancies and limited therapeutic alternatives.

• Prospective studies are warranted to validate this strategy and identify predictive biomarkers.

Introduction

Patients with advanced malignancies often have limited treatment options after failure of systemic therapy and frequently present with poor performance status and high tumor burden, resulting in an overall unfavorable prognosis (1). Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved survival outcomes in selected tumor types, a considerable proportion of patients exhibits primary or acquired resistance in real-world clinical practice (2). Increasing evidence suggests that an “immune-cold” tumor microenvironment plays a pivotal role in limiting the efficacy of immunotherapy (3).

Low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT), typically defined as radiotherapy delivered with a single fraction dose ≤2 Gy and a total dose substantially lower than conventional curative regimens (4), has emerged as a potential immunomodulatory intervention rather than a purely cytotoxic modality. At sub-ablative doses, radiotherapy may enhance effector T-cell infiltration, promote dendritic cell-mediated antigen presentation, induce tumor-associated macrophage polarization toward an M1 phenotype, and normalize tumor vasculature, thereby remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment (5-7). These biological effects provide a rationale for integrating LDRT with ICIs to overcome immunotherapy resistance.

Importantly, for patients with advanced disease and poor performance status, conventional full-dose radiotherapy may not be clinically appropriate due to the risk of treatment-related toxicities, particularly when large irradiation volumes or cumulative doses are required (8). In this context, an LDRT strategy directed to progressive lesions may serve primarily as an immunomodulatory intervention rather than definitive local tumor control.

Based on this rationale, we adopted a fixed-dose LDRT regimen (2 Gy ×3 fractions) delivered to selected progressive lesions in combination with ICIs. The present study retrospectively evaluated the clinical activity and safety of this LDRT strategy in a real-world cohort of patients with advanced malignancies who had limited therapeutic options. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0709/rc).

Methods

Study design

This was a single-center, single-arm, retrospective real-world study. Clinical and follow-up data of eligible patients with advanced malignant tumors treated at Taikang Xianlin Drum Tower Hospital were retrospectively collected and analyzed. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Taikang Xianlin Drum Tower Hospital (No. 2025-034-02). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were collected, including sex, age, tumor type, disease stage, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS2002) score, height, weight, and peripheral blood total lymphocyte count (TLC). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using height and weight.

Treatment schedule

The treatment schedule is illustrated in Figure 1. LDRT was delivered to locally progressive lesions with a total dose of 6 Gy administered in three fractions (2 Gy ×3 fractions). PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors were administered on day 3 of radiotherapy or within 24 hours after completion of LDRT. All patients included in this study received combined treatment with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and LDRT during the same treatment period.

Treatment schema of LDRT combined with ICIs. In each treatment cycle, LDRT was delivered as 2 Gy per fraction for 3 consecutive days (total dose 6 Gy). A PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor was administered on day 3. The regimen was repeated every 3–4 weeks according to clinical practice. ICI, immune checkpoint inhibitor; LDRT, low-dose radiotherapy; PD-1, programmed cell death protein 1; PD-L1, programmed death-ligand 1; RT, radiotherapy.

The indication for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor therapy was determined by the treating physicians based on prior treatment history, patient condition, available therapeutic options, and multidisciplinary clinical judgment. For tumor types without established routine indications for ICIs, treatment decisions were individualized after discussion with patients and families because of limited standard treatment options in the advanced disease setting.

Concomitant systemic therapies, including chemotherapy, were permitted at the discretion of the treating physicians according to individual patient conditions and routine clinical practice. Details of concomitant systemic therapies are summarized in Table S1.

Efficacy assessment

Tumor response was evaluated according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. Measurable target lesions were identified on baseline imaging, and irradiated lesions were included in RECIST-based response assessment when they had been selected as target lesions at baseline. Initial radiographic assessment and best overall response (BOR) were recorded. In addition to RECIST-defined responses, the location of disease progression was descriptively classified as in-field or out-of-field progression during follow-up. The primary endpoints were objective response rate (ORR) and progression-free survival (PFS). Secondary endpoints included disease control rate (DCR). PFS was defined as the time from initiation of combined treatment to radiographic disease progression or death.

Safety assessment

Treatment-related adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were primarily descriptive. Categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables as medians with ranges. PFS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. A total of 13 patients were included, comprising 8 males (61.5%) and 5 females (38.5%), with a median age of 61 years (range, 42–74 years). All patients had stage IV disease. Tumor types included pancreatic cancer (n=5), esophageal cancer (n=2), and one case each of small-cell lung cancer, bladder cancer, cervical cancer, rectal cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and hypopharyngeal cancer.

Except for one patient who had not received prior systemic therapy because of poor baseline clinical condition and was deemed unsuitable for systemic treatment, all other patients had progressed after one or more lines of systemic or local therapies. Six patients progressed after first-line therapy, two after second-line therapy, and four after third-line or beyond. Several patients had previously received chemotherapy combined with or followed by radiotherapy, indicating that the cohort predominantly consisted of pretreated patients in later-line settings.

ECOG performance status scores were 1 in 4 patients, 2 in 6 patients, and 3 in 3 patients. The median NRS2002 score was 3 (range, 1–4), with most patients exhibiting moderate to high nutritional risk. The median TLC was 0.71×109/L (range, 0.43–2.03×109/L). Overall, the enrolled population demonstrated poor performance status, nutritional status, and immune reserve, reflecting a clinically challenging group with limited treatment options.

Efficacy

According to RECIST 1.1, 10 patients (76.9%) achieved disease response or stabilization at the first radiographic assessment. Based on BOR, partial response was observed in 5 patients (38.5%), stable disease in 5 patients (38.5%), and progressive disease in 3 patients (23.1%); no complete responses were observed. The ORR was 38.5%, and the DCR was 76.9% (Table 2).

Safety

Overall, treatment was well tolerated (Table 2). Treatment-related adverse events were mainly chemotherapy-related hematologic toxicities, such as leukopenia, with some patients experiencing grade ≥3 hematologic toxicity. Non-hematologic adverse events were generally mild to moderate and included fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and transient elevations in liver enzymes. No clinically significant immune-related adverse events were documented in the available medical records, and no radiotherapy-related severe toxicities were observed. No treatment-related deaths occurred during follow-up.

An exploratory analysis was performed to evaluate the potential association between baseline nutritional and immune-related parameters and treatment outcomes (Table S2). Patients with higher baseline TLC and better performance status (ECOG ≤1) tended to demonstrate improved disease control and longer PFS. Higher baseline prognostic nutritional index (PNI) appeared to be associated with improved disease control, although the relationship with PFS was less consistent.

Discussion

Most patients included in the present study had advanced malignancies that had progressed after second-line or later systemic therapies and presented with poor performance status and compromised nutritional reserve (1). In such patients, the expected benefit from additional systemic therapy alone is often limited, while conventional full-dose radiotherapy may significantly increase the risk of treatment-related toxicities due to limited normal tissue tolerance (9,10). In this clinical context, radiotherapy delivered at sub-ablative doses may represent a strategy-driven intervention aimed at immunomodulation rather than definitive local tumor eradication.

LDRT has attracted increasing attention as a potential immunomodulatory approach. Preclinical and early-phase clinical studies suggest that low-dose radiation may remodel the tumor immune microenvironment by enhancing antigen presentation, promoting effector T-cell infiltration, inducing tumor-associated macrophage polarization toward an M1 phenotype, and improving tumor vasculature (11-13). Prospective studies of LDRT have been conducted in selected tumor types. However, evidence in patients with poor performance status who have received multiple lines of prior therapy remains limited (14). In the present real-world cohort, a proportion of patients achieved meaningful disease control following LDRT combined with ICIs (ORR 38.5%, DCR 76.9%), suggesting that this strategy may offer potential clinical benefit in carefully selected patients with limited therapeutic options (15).

An important observation was that all documented progression events occurred outside the irradiated fields, while no clear in-field progression was observed during follow-up. Although this study was not designed to formally assess local control, this finding suggests that LDRT may provide local disease stabilization in treated lesions (16,17). Whether this effect primarily reflects local tumor control or immune-mediated mechanisms cannot be determined from the current data and warrants further investigation.

The exploratory analysis further highlighted the potential importance of host immune reserve and performance status in determining treatment outcomes. These findings suggest that baseline immunological and nutritional parameters may serve as candidate biomarkers for patient selection in future prospective studies, though formal validation is required. They underscore the need for prospective biomarker-driven studies in this population (18,19).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This study was retrospective and conducted at a single center, which may introduce selection bias. The small sample size and heterogeneity of tumor types limit generalizability, and the absence of a control group precludes definitive conclusions regarding the independent contribution of LDRT. Mechanistic investigations were not performed, and therefore the biological effects of LDRT could not be directly assessed. The role of LDRT in combination with ICIs should be further evaluated in prospective studies (20).

Conclusions

LDRT combined with ICIs showed preliminary clinical activity with acceptable tolerability in this small real-world cohort of patients with advanced malignancies. Although limited by the small sample size, this approach may represent a feasible treatment option for selected patients and should be further evaluated in prospective studies.
