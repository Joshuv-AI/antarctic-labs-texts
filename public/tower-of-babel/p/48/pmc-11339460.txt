Using DeepSignalingFlow to mine signaling flows interpreting mechanism of synergy of cocktails
Authors: Heming Zhang, Yixin Chen, Philip Payne, Fuhai Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Complex signaling pathways are believed to be responsible for drug resistance. Drug combinations perturbing multiple signaling targets have the potential to reduce drug resistance. The large-scale multi-omic datasets and experimental drug combination synergistic score data are valuable resources to study mechanisms of synergy (MoS) to guide the development of precision drug combinations. However, signaling patterns of MoS are complex and remain unclear, and thus it is challenging to identify synergistic drug combinations in clinical. Herein, we proposed a novel integrative and interpretable graph AI model, DeepSignalingFlow, to uncover the MoS by integrating and mining multi-omic data. The major innovation is that we uncover MoS by modeling the signaling flow from multi-omic features of essential disease proteins to the drug targets, which has not been introduced by the existing models. The model performance was assessed utilizing four distinct drug combination synergy evaluation datasets, i.e., NCI ALMANAC, O’Neil, DrugComb, and DrugCombDB. The comparison results showed that the proposed model outperformed existing graph AI models in terms of synergy score prediction, and can interpret MoS using the core signaling flows. The code is publicly accessible via Github: https://github.com/FuhaiLiAiLab/DeepSignalingFlow

Introduction

Complex signaling pathways play a crucial role in driving drug resistance in various types of cancer. These pathways involve a multitude of mechanisms such as the activation of signaling pathways promoting cell survival and proliferation, upregulation of drug efflux pumps, emergence of cancer stem cells, and genetic and epigenetic changes1. For example, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway has been identified as a key player in resistance development in triple-negative breast cancer, interacting with receptor tyrosine kinase signaling pathways, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and metastasis2. Additionally, signaling pathways like MAPK, PI3K/PKB, Wnt/β-catenin, and Notch have been associated with multidrug resistance in colorectal cancer, affecting cell survival, and drug metabolism3. Thus, targeting these intricate signaling pathways is essential for developing effective strategies to overcome drug resistance in cancer therapy. Previous research has demonstrated that the inhibition of a single component within a signaling network may not be sufficient to effectively treat cancer due to the robustness and resistance of network signaling4. Thus, drug combinations inhibiting more signaling targets, compared with single drug treatment, in cancer-related signaling networks have the potential to be effective in reducing drug resistance. For example, the RAS/ERK signaling pathway, which has been shown to inhibit the efficacy of certain anti-cancer drugs, was found to be synergistic with autophagy inhibitors in RAS-driven cancers. This is notable, given that autophagy signaling was activated after treatment with RAS inhibitors5,6.

However, it is challenging to discover effective and synergistic drug combinations in clinical and laboratory settings because of the unclear and complex mechanism of synergy (MoS) for such drug combinations. Recent analyses of reported drug combinations indicate that there are complex, diverse, and heterogeneous MoS for different drug combinations in different diseases7. Despite this evolving body of research concerning the targeting of synergistic cancer therapeutics, there remain few effective drug combinations that are used broadly in the clinical environment. Therefore, novel and effective drug combinations are needed in order to expand the scope and impact of precision cancer therapy and ultimately improve cancer treatment outcomes.

To help facilitate the discovery of synergistic drug combinations for use in a variety of cancers, we sought to understand the associations between multi-omic features and drug response, as well as the mechanism of drug synergy, using computational methods. The National Cancer Institute (NCI) has previously generated a comprehensive screening dataset of 5232 combinations from ~100 drugs in 60 human tumor cell lines (NCI-60), which is publicly available as the NCI-ALMANAC Drug Combination Data Set8. This dataset is valuable for developing computational models for drug combination prediction. Aside from that, O’Neil dataset9 is another drug combination screening dataset containing 38 compounds and 39 cancer cell lines with 583 combinations and 22,737 experiments. In addition, DrugComb10 is an innovative web-based data portal designed to standardize and harmonize drug combination screening data for cancer cell lines. It features a comprehensive collection of 437,923 drug combination experiments, encompassing 7,423,800 data points across 93 human cancer cell lines. DrugCombDB11 is also a comprehensive dataset that includes 448,555 drug combinations derived from high-throughput screening (HTS) assays. It encompasses 2887 unique drugs tested across 124 human cancer cell lines. With over 6,000,000 quantitative dose responses, DrugCombDB computes multiple synergy scores to assess the overall synergistic or antagonistic effects of drug combinations, providing a valuable resource for advancing cancer treatment research.

There have been a variety of computational and deep-learning models described in the scientific literature concerning the application of machine learning techniques to such data. For example, random forest12 (RF) and XGBoost13 models have been used, integrating chemical and genomics features of source data, to predict drug combinations14. The regression model was used in a feedback system control (FSC) framework15,16 for drug combination prediction. In addition, the use of multi-task learning with a transfer learning model has been proposed to predict drug combinations17. Then, deep-learning models have also been developed for drug combination prediction. For example, Matchmaker18 was developed to analyze chemical and gene expression profiles using a deep-learning framework, which is similar to the DeepSynergy19 and AuDNNsynergy20, and DeepSignalingSynergy21,22. Multi-omic data were concatenated as integration in those deep-learning models23 to improve the model robustness and accuracy23. Moreover, the Visible Neural NetworkVNN (VNN) models (e.g., DCell and DrugCell)24–26 were proposed, using large hierarchical deep-learning architecture to model the hierarchical organization of biological processes, to predict drug response with important biomarkers. However, the signaling cascade level activity was not specifically investigated in the DCell and DrugCell models. These models concatenate a large number of genomics and chemical structure features using dense connection layers for drug combination prediction. However, the signaling pathway information was not incorporated. Thus, these models are less interpretable compared with models integrating omic data with signaling pathways.

Despite the promise of the existing body of work in this domain, there remain two limitations of the existing deep-learning models. First, the signaling interactions in gene regulatory signaling networks are often not incorporated in the existing deep-learning models, despite their criticality in terms of treatment efficacy. Second, complex mechanisms of synergy (MoS) are usually not investigated in these existing models. As such, these models are difficult to interpret and explain, which hinders the identification of MoS of synergistic drug combinations. Recently, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have gained prominence due to their capability to model relationships within graph-structured data27–29 having been applied to numerous studies for the integration of multi-omics data. Compared with DNN models, GNN models can naturally integrate the signaling pathways or protein-protein interactions (networks/graphs—node and node interactions) with omic features (features of nodes). Then the target ranking and subnetwork or pathway inference can be achieved by using node and edge ranking operations in GNN. Therefore, GNN or graph AI models are ideal models for integrative and interpretable omic data analysis and for mining the MoS of synergistic drug combinations using the top-ranked signaling flow/cascades. For example, MOGONET30 initially creates similarity graphs among samples by leveraging each omics data, then employs a graph convolutional network (GCN) to learn a label distribution from each omics data independently. Subsequently, a cross-omics discovery tensor is implemented to refine the prediction by learning the dependency among multi-omics data. MoGCN31 adopts a similar approach by constructing a patient similarity network using multi-omics data and then using GCN to predict the cancer subtype of patients. GCN-SC32 utilizes a GCN to combine single-cell multi-omics data derived from varying sequencing methodologies. MOGCL33 takes this further by exploiting the potency of graph contrastive learning to pre-train the GCN on the multi-omics dataset, thereby achieving impressive results in downstream tasks with fine-tuning. DeepDDS34 incorporated the chemical structure information of drugs into the GNN framework to achieve better performance on drug response prediction. Nevertheless, none of the aforementioned techniques contemplate incorporating structured signaling pathway data into the model to mine the detailed signaling cascades/flows associated with the MoS of drug cocktails.

In the specific context of cancer studies, signaling pathways playing important roles in terms of tumor development and drug response, are of critical importance when seeking to identify single or synergistic therapeutic targets. For example, in one set of studies, 10 signaling pathways were systematically analyzed across 9,125 tumor samples in 33 cancer types (64 subtypes)35. The results of this study indicated that 89% of tumor samples had at least one driver alteration in one of these pathways, and 57% and 30% had one and multiple potentially druggable targets, respectively. These results indicate the importance of identifying or repurposing effective drugs and drug combinations that target these signaling pathways. Therefore, in our study, we proposed a novel computational graph neural network (GNN) model, DeepSignalingFlow, to investigate the aforementioned issues as they relate to the use of deep-learning models for drug combination therapy prediction, by incorporating core cancer signaling pathways and multi-omic data into such analyses. What’s more, the interpretability of the signaling flow was provided for analyzing the key signaling and targets for each cancer in our model.

Experimental setup

To validate the proposed model, a 5-fold cross-validation was employed. Specifically, <DA, DB, CC, SABC> (DA, DB are drug combinations, CC is the cell line name, which will be combined with the multi-omics data as the details and SABC is the synergistic drug effect on the cell line CC), a 4-element tuple, represents an input data point. In total, there are 2788, 1008, 27,781, and 21,399 model input data points for NCI ALMANAC8, O’Neil9, DrugComb10, and DrugCombDB11 datasets. In each dataset, 4 folds of the data points will be used as the training dataset, and the rest 1 fold will be used as the test dataset. Considering all possible 2 drug permutations, each generated graph comprised 2016 gene nodes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${n}_{1}$$\end{document}n1=2016), and the number of drug nodes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${n}_{2}$$\end{document}n2) varied across different datasets with each node in these graphs having 4 initial features. For those 2016 genes, their 4 features indicate the RNA sequence number, copy number variation, and whether they have a connection spanning DA and DB respectively. For each 2 drug pair, their 4 features are initiated with zeros. Additionally, to indicate connections between nodes, adjacency matrices are also created, being formed from the KEGG44 database which contains gene pairs with sources and destinations, resulting in directed graphs. For drug-gene edges, the connections are bi-directional, meaning that in adjacency matrices, these elements are symmetric. Concurrently, in-degree and out-degree matrices are formed according to the original input adjacency matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{n\times n}$$\end{document}A∈Rn×n.

Discussion

Drug resistance is a common challenge in cancer therapy due to the complex signaling pathways of cancers. Drug combinations inhibiting more and multiple signaling targets, compared with single drug treatment, in cancer-related signaling networks have the potential to be effective in reducing drug resistance. However, it remains an open problem and challenging because of the mysterious, heterogeneous molecular mechanisms of synergy of drug combinations. Deep-learning models have been reported to predict drug combinations by simply concatenating a large number of multi-omic data and chemical structure information using the deep neural network (DNN) model. One limitation of the existing DNN models is the missing of complex and biologically meaningful gene regulatory relationships. Thus, it is hard to interpret these models to support real clinical use, which requires clinical experts to understand the molecular mechanisms used in the AI prediction.

In this study, we aim to explore the feasibility of building an interpretable AI model using the proposed DeepSignalingFlow model. The model is built on a core cancer signaling network consisting of 2016 genes, with gene expression and copy number data derived from 48 core cancer signaling pathways. The novel upstream signaling flow (from upstream signaling to drug targets), and the downstream signaling flow (from drug targets to downstream signaling), were designed using trainable weights of network edges. The selected signaling interaction edges with large weights indicate (1) the important biomarker genes; (2) the signaling cascades among them, which can potentially interpret the model prediction results; and (3) the potential synergistic drug targets in the cell line-specific core signaling pathways. In conclusion, the first-time proposed upstream and downstream signaling flow design is novel and can potentially interpret the mechanism of drug combination response and offer the potential targets of future drug targets. Understanding the upstream and downstream signaling pathways/mechanisms of drugs is crucial, as drugs can perturb disease-related dysfunctional signaling by either ‘blocking’ the signaling cascades/flow from upstream dysfunctional signaling or ‘inhibiting’ the downstream dysfunctional signaling. By mapping the signaling flow dynamics, this model helps elucidate how signals propagate through the network to achieve a synergistic response. Gaining such an understanding of the mechanistic basis of drug combinations is instrumental in designing drug combinations that can overcome resistance, ultimately leading to more effective therapies. The model can be also applied to general multi-omic data analysis studies.

This is still an exploratory study to predict and investigate drug combination response using deep graph neural network models. There are some limitations to be further investigated. For example, additional large-scale signaling pathways and protein-protein interactions can cover more proteins and drug targets and will include more drugs, which will be further investigated. In addition, our current analysis is a global analysis or pan-cancer analysis by considering all drugs and all cell lines together. However, the drug combination and cell line-specific signaling flows might be different. Thus, it is interesting to investigate the drug- and cell-specific signaling flows that are informative for the drug combination response prediction. Therefore, it would be ideal to interpret the MoS of synergistic drug combinations using the detailed signaling flows/interactions that are specific to cell lines and specific to drug combinations. We will investigate these challenges in future work. Moreover, considering the presence of various edge types in signaling pathway databases (e.g., KEGG), it is possible to incorporate edge information into graph neural network models to enable a more detailed analysis of the edges, considering that edge information has been incorporated into various graph AI framework33,48–52. In addition, the model is trained using a given set of synergistic scores of drug combinations, thus, currently, it is limited to predicting the synergy effects of new unseen drug pairs. When more cell lines and drug pairs (drug-targets) are available, the model can facilitate the discovery of new drug pairs by investigating the patterns of the drug-target and disease gene signaling in the learned MoS. In future work, we will further investigate these questions.
