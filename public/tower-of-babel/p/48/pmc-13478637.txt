Bacteriophage-mediated restoration of epithelial integrity, DNA damage response, and transcriptomic homeostasis in colon organoids exposed to ETBF and pks + E. coli
Authors: Myoung Hyoun Song, Eun Kyoung Oh, In Hwang Kim, Jee-Soo Son, Seong Jun Yoon, Jin-Young Kim, Min Jung Kim, Seung-Yong Jeong, Ji Won Park, Ja-Lok Ku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC)–associated pathobionts such as Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) and colibactin-producing (pks+) Escherichia coli disrupt epithelial barrier function, promote chronic inflammation, and induce genotoxic stress. To investigate how these pathogenic interactions with the host epithelium can be modulated, we employed patient-derived normal human colon organoids as an ex vivo model. Two lytic bacteriophages, Bac-FRP-3 (targeting ETBF) and Esc-COP-23 (targeting pks + E. coli), were applied to bacterial cultures, and the resulting phage-conditioned supernatants were used to challenge organoids. Exposure to ETBF and pks + E. coli induced strong pro-inflammatory transcriptional responses, DNA damage signaling, and epithelial disruption, whereas phage treatment attenuated these effects. Transcriptomic profiling revealed restoration of inflammatory and DNA damage–related gene expression, while confocal imaging confirmed recovery of epithelial junctional integrity and reduced DNA double-strand breaks. These findings highlight phage-mediated modulation of host–pathobiont interactions and provide mechanistic insight into how targeted viral predation can reshape pathogenic impacts within the human intestinal environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56205-1.

Introduction

As the increasing evidence of the significant association between bacteria and human diseases and life expectancy, research on antibiotic resistance, mutation, and emergence of novel bacteria is exponentially growing. In 2022, the number of papers related to microbiome was 226.3 times higher than that of 2002, 20 years earlier. However, the need for studies related to the association between bacterial research technique and the recently developed in vitro models is gaining attention, but the progress of such studies is relatively slow compared to each individual research field. Therefore, we conducted this study with realizing the importance of such association research, and successfully co-cultured aerobic and anaerobic bacteria: pks + E. coli and Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) with normal colon organoid models.

Discovery of antibiotics could save many people over half a century and results in prolonged average life expectancy of the human. However, as the use of antibiotics increased, the frequency of newly appeared antibiotics-resistant species increased accordingly. Furthermore, additional causes including inappropriate prescribing and sales has exacerbated the issues of antimicrobial resistance1. Approximately 25,000 deaths in Europe and exceeding expenditure for $20 billion in US are originated from the matter of antibiotics-resistant microbiota2,3. Consequently, antibiotics are no longer recognized as “miracle drugs” as efficient in infectious diseases such as sepsis and pneumonia. Accordingly, the newly conceived and introduced phage therapy is currently receiving a lot of attention. Phage therapy refers to a technique that kills and suppresses specific pathogens using bacteriophage before invasion and infection of pathogens has a more serious effect on the human body. Various pharmaceutical applications employing phage therapy are also being discussed as the results that bacteriophage could exert influences on degradation of biofilm that is well known factor causing inflammation and cancer in colon microbial environment4.

We focused on two well informed tumorigenic bacterial strains which are ETBF and pks + E. coli (Polyketides synthase positive Escherichia coli). ETBF (Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis) is an anaerobe and oncogenic facilitator that produce and secret 20-kDa zinc-dependent metalloprotease toxin also known as BFT (Bacteroides fragilis toxin) disconnecting the linkage between molecules of tight junction such as occluding and e-cadherin5,6. Breakage of tight junction smoothen the physical barrier that results in increased permeability of pathogens and other toxigenic molecules including BFT and colibactin into epithelial monolayers7,8. Continuous influx of toxins and bacterial metabolites could arouse chronic inflammation that could exert influences on tumorigenesis9.

On the other hand, pks + E. coli is also recognized as oncogenic bacteria due to its genotoxic effects with colibactin-encoding genome island in contrast to normal E. coli strain. Unlike pks-mutant E. coli, pks + E. coli has been reported to produce and secrete the genotoxic agent colibactin, causing DNA double-strand breakage, cell cycle arrest, DNA damage response and apoptosis in HeLa epithelial cells10. Colibactin is also considered as DNA alkylator on adenine (A) and suggested to have a notable potent in colorectal cancer (CRC) tumorigenesis11–13. Recently, Genotoxic effects and sequential genotypic alterations were clearly demonstrated by observation after repeated luminal injection of pks + E. coli into human intestinal organoid14.

3D culture model already has drawn much consensus for striking next-generation technic as a result of its character for mimicking physiologically similar microenvironment. Especially, Organoid models derived from various organ have been established and enabled researchers to take a step closer to the in vivo system15.

Here, we also employed organoid culture model to investigate the anti-cancer effects of bacteriophage in conditions of cotreatment with ETBF and pks + E. coli. We hypothesized that secretion of BFT from ETBF would help colibactin or other genotoxic molecules to be easily penetrated the gap between cell-to-cell barrier. This suggests that both bacteria may contribute to initiating and accelerating tumor formation.

Materials and methods

Tissue processing and organoid culture

Colon normal tissues surgically resected from patients at Seoul National University Hospital were utilized for the isolation of tumor epithelium. Resected tissues were finely minced using sterile surgical scissors under aseptic conditions. The tissue fragments were then incubated in a digestion solution containing hyaluronidase (Gibco, 17101-015-1G), collagenase II (Sigma-Aldrich, H3506-100MG), and the ROCK inhibitor Y-27,632 (Sigma-Aldrich, Y0503-1MG) at 37 °C for 45 min with continuous rotation.

Following enzymatic digestion, the dissociated epithelial fragments and associated stem cells were filtered through a 100 μm nylon mesh to remove undigested debris. The filtrate was subsequently centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 3 min, and the resulting pellet was washed by resuspension in DME/F-12 medium supplemented with 5% bovine calf serum (BCS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

The washed epithelial cells were then embedded in 50 µl of Matrigel (Trevigen, 3533-001-02) and evenly plated into 24-well culture plates. All organoid lines used in subsequent experiments were cultured in Human Intestinal Stem Cell (HISC) medium for expansion.

For passaging, organoids were dissociated into single cells using TrypLE Express (Gibco, 12604). Matrigel domes containing organoids were first treated with 2 ml of a gel-dissolving solution and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. Mechanical dissociation was further achieved by gentle pipetting. Enzymatic activity was quenched by adding 8 ml of Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS), followed by centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 3 min. After aspirating the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in 50 µl of fresh Matrigel and replated for continued culture.

Bacterial strains, culture media and growth conditions

Escherichia coli used in this study was pks + E. coli strain (a gift from Dr. Lee16. Bacteroides fragilis strain was obtained from Gyeongsang National University Hospital, a branch of National Culture Collection for Pathogens (GNUH-NCCP, Jinju, Korea). To identify whether B. fragilis is Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (ETBF) or not, PCR based on the detection of the enterotoxin gene (B. fragilis toxin; bft) was performed17. Escherichia coli was cultured at 37 °C in Tryptic soy broth (TSB) or tryptic soy agar (TSA). Bacteroides fragilis were grown at 37 °C in Brain heart infusion (BHI) broth containing hemin (10 µg/ml) under anaerobic condition using a GasPack system. All media used in this study were purchased from Difco (Detroit, MI, USA) and used according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bacteriophage preparation and purification

Bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 and Bac-FRP-3 was isolated from domestic wastewater sources as previously described18. The bacteriolytic activity and host range of these phages are now detailed in Supplementary Table S2. Selection was prioritized for phages with a broad host range for their respective target species, while phages showing narrow activity (lytic against only 1–2 strains) were excluded. Experimental data confirms that these phages exhibit specific lytic activity against target pathogenic strains, including pks + E. coli and bft-positive B. fragilis (ETBF). Bacteriophage suspensions were prepared as follows. Bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 was co-cultivated with log-phase E. coli cultured in TSB at 37 °C until the lysis of the host bacterial cell. The culture was centrifuged at 8,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resultant supernatant was filtered with a 0.45-µm membrane filter. Bacteriophage Bac-FRP-3 was prepared as the same method, except culture media and anaerobic conditions. The procedure, from co-cultivation to filtration, was repeated three times to enhance phage titer. And then, bacteriophages were precipitated for 2 h at 4 °C with 10% (w/v) polyethylene glycol (PEG) 8,000 and 0.5 M NaCl. The bacteriophages were pelleted by centrifugation at 8,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C and suspended in binding buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0). The bacteriophage suspension was purified by anion-exchange chromatography using a 2-µm pore-CIMmultus™ QA-8 Advanced Composite column (BIA Separations, Ljubljana, Slovenia) attached to an ÄKTA pure™ FPLC system (Cytiva, Freiburg, Germany), using UNICORN 7.3 software for analysis. The above binding buffer was used for loading the bacteriophage and achieving equilibration, and a 1 M NaCl solution was used as the elution buffer. The final titer of the purified bacteriophage was confirmed with plaque assays using the conventional double-layered agar method.

Organoid medium

To prepare the complete organoid culture medium, conditioned medium and basal culture medium were combined in a 1:1 ratio and supplemented with various growth factors and additives, including B27 (1×, Gibco, 17504-044-50X, 10 ml), N-acetyl cysteine (1.25 mM, Sigma Aldrich, A7250-5G), nicotinamide (10 mM, Sigma Aldrich, 72340-100G), hEGF (0.1 mg/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific, PHG0311L-0.1 mg), hFGF10 (10 ng/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific, PHG0204-25 µg), A83-01 (500 nM, Sigma Aldrich, SML0788-5MG), SB202190 (3 µM, Sigma Aldrich, S7067-5MG), prostaglandin E2 (10 nM, Sigma Aldrich, P5640-1MG), and a broad-spectrum antibiotic (Primocin, 100 µg/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific, ant-pm-1–10*1 ml 500 mg).

Conditioned medium was pre-prepared using a stably transfected cell line (L-WRN, ATCC CRL-3276) obtained from ATCC, which constitutively secretes Wnt-3a, R-spondin, and Noggin into the culture medium. To generate the conditioned medium, L-WRN cells were cultured in T-175 flasks (Nunc, 159910) containing DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S), and the selection antibiotics Hygromycin B (500 µg/ml) and G418 (2.0 mg/ml). The L-WRN cell line carries resistance genes to both antibiotics, allowing selective expansion under dual selection pressure while eliminating non-transfected cells.

Once the cells reached full confluency, they were expanded into six T-175 flasks. From this point onward, to generate conditioned medium suitable for organoid culture, the cells were maintained in 30 ml of DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The medium was replaced daily for 7 consecutive days, and the spent medium was collected each day and stored at 4 °C. After the 7-day period, the accumulated conditioned medium was pooled into a sterile 3 L autoclaved bottle and filtered through a 0.2 μm pore-size bottle-top filter (Nalgene, 595–4520) to obtain the final conditioned medium.

The basal culture medium consisted of DMEM/F-12 supplemented with GlutaMax (10 mM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 35050-061 100 ML), 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin19–23.

DNA and RNA preparation

Organoids that had grown to full size and were encased in a dome-shaped Matrigel were extracted using a mechanical pipette and transferred to a 1.5 ml tube. The sample was then centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min, and the supernatant was aspirated and replaced with 1 ml of TrypLE Express Enzyme (1×, no phenol red, Gibco, Ireland). The process for extracting gDNA and RNA is described in detail in the laboratory protocol24,25.

Bacteria proliferation reduction assay

Based on PCR result, we checked B. fragilis is the Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (data not shown) and then used Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fagilis (ETBF, Anaerobe) and pks + E. coli (Facultative anaerobe) as bacteria model test strain. To determinate lysis curve, bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 for pks + E. coli and bacteriophage Bac-FRP-3 for ETBF were prepared. The data for bacteria growth curves were measured and normalized by spectrophotometer MultiSkan GO (ThermoFischer Scientific, USA). Each bacterial strain was grown and added to their culture broth in 15 ml conical tubes (1:100 ratio). The conical tubes were incubated overnight in shaking incubator (120 rpm and 37℃). After 24 h, each bacterial strains were diluted 1:50 in culture medium (BHI for ETBF and TSB for pks + E. coli) and incubated at 37℃ with shaking again. The incubation was terminated when they reached the early logarithmic phase (OD600nm = 0.1). We considered the paper performed similar experiment26. The tubes containing bacterial solutions were inoculated with bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 and Bac-FRP-3 (1 × 108 pfu/mL).

RLU measurement for cell viability assay

Cell viability and cytotoxicity were evaluated using a 3D organoid culture system with CellTiter-Glo® 3D to measure intracellular ATP levels.

Organoids were harvested and passaged as described in Sect. 2.1, and 4,000 cells per well were seeded into 96-well white-bottom plates (SPL, 30196) in 80 µL of culture medium containing 50% Matrigel. Plates were incubated at 37 °C to allow gelation, followed by the addition of 80 µL of culture medium. Organoids were cultured for 7 days to reach full growth, with medium replacement performed on day 4 using a multichannel pipette.

For treatment, culture supernatants from ETBF, pks + E. coli, or their corresponding bacteriophage-treated cultures (Bac-FRP-3 and Esc-COP-23) were added to the organoid medium at a final concentration of 20%. Organoids were incubated for 72 h following treatment and subsequently subjected to a cell viability assay.

The reagent of CellTiter-Glo® 3D (Promega, G9683) was thawed and equilibrated to room temperature before use, and all experimental procedures were performed at room temperature.

The half volume of the culture solution (containing bacterial supernatant) was replaced with CellTiter-Glo® 3D and thoroughly resuspended using a multi-channel pipette.

Plates for screening were sealed with aluminum foil and incubated for 30 min.

After the light-excluded incubation, the luminescence of intra-organoid ATP content was quantified by Varioskan Lux (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

RNA-seq processing and differential expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from colon organoid samples (SNU-7321 N and SNU-7514 N) subjected to treatment with ETBF and pks+ Escherichia coli alone or in combination with the specific bacteriophage Bac-FRP-3 and Esc-COP-23. RNA-seq libraries were generated using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina), and 150 bp paired-end reads were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR (v2.7.9a), and gene-level read counts were quantified using featureCounts (Subread v2.0.1). Normalization and differential gene expression analysis were performed using DESeq2 (v1.36.0), with log₂ fold-change (log₂FC) values computed relative to untreated control conditions for each organoid line.

Heatmap visualization

Heatmaps of log₂FC values were generated using the seaborn (v0.12.2) clustermap and heatmap functions in Python (v3.10). Rows (genes) were hierarchically clustered using average linkage and Euclidean distance metric. Color scaling was applied from − 2.5 to + 2.5 (Fig. 4) and − 8 to + 8 (Supplementary Fig. 1 And 2) to highlight relative expression changes. Pathway groupings were annotated using color-coded sidebars and categorical row index labels. Linewidths were set to zero to emphasize the continuous data distribution.

Bar plot generation and statistical analysis

To compare pathway-level expression changes across conditions, mean log₂FC values were calculated per pathway and visualized using bar plots (matplotlib.pyplot and seaborn). Group comparisons between treatment conditions (e.g., pks+ alone vs. pks + and Esc-COP-23) were evaluated using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, with significance thresholds set at p < 0.05. Statistical annotations (e.g., p-value markers, “ns” for non-significant) were added using statannotations (v0.5.0).

We stained bacterial strains, which were treated with their corresponding bacteriophages, with a LIVE/DEAD labelling kit (Fig. 3B). Images were acquired using a 64× objective lens with immersion oil. Because high-magnification imaging is highly sensitive to the subtle motility of bacteria, obtaining precise Z-stacked images was technically challenging. To minimize bacterial movement during imaging, samples were solidified with 1% agar, which effectively restricted bacterial motility and allowed the acquisition of stable, high-resolution images.

All data were plotted with uniform aesthetics, including pastel heatmap palettes and a simplified bar plot background (no grid lines, custom y-axis limits, and legend extracted separately when needed).

Software and reproducibility

All analyses were conducted in a reproducible environment using Python 3.10, with key packages including: numpy (v1.23), pandas (v1.5), matplotlib (v3.7), seaborn (v0.12), and scipy (v1.9). Data visualization scripts and annotated gene lists are available upon reasonable request.

Gene set definition and pathway assignment

A curated list of 40 genes associated with cell cycle, DNA damage response, NF-κB signaling, and immune response was compiled based on Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotations. Each gene was mapped to its respective pathway category and used for downstream visualization27–29.

Confocal microscopy

The organoids were harvested using a gentle pipette resuspension method and washed twice with DME/F12 supplemented with 5% BCS. The washing step involved adding the solution to a 15 ml conical tube up to 10 ml and centrifuging at 1,500 rpm for 3 minutes. Cytofixation and permeabilization were carried out simultaneously using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ solution (BD biosciences, #554714) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To block non-specific surface binding, 0.1% BSA (Bovine Serum Albumin) in 0.25% PBS-T (Triton X-100 diluted in Phosphate Buffered Saline) was treated for 45 minutes. For the detection of E-cadherin cleavage, primary monoclonal E-cadherin antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Cell signaling, #3199) was attached to its target. Monoclonal phospho-histone H2A.X (γH2AX), which detects phosphorylation on residue serine 139, and its corresponding 2’ fluorescence antibody (Alexa Fluor™ 568) was sequentially treated to confirm DDR (DNA Damage Response) evolved from pks + E. coli. The fluorescence was captured using the STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope system (Leica). The intensities of fluorescence in each channel were amended with all-in-one arranger (equally adjusted the intensities of all channels) to avoid biased data. Adjustment of fluorescence was performed only for improved visibility.

Image-based high-throughput screening

In this study, we utilized the Image Xpress Confocal 4 (Molecular Devices, USA) to screen the organoids treated with microbial supernatants. We also conducted a LIVE/DEAD assay on bacteria treated with specific bacteriophages. Organoids were observed using a 4 × objective lens, while bacteria were observed using a 64 × apochromat objective lens. To ensure accurate results, we stained each bacterial strain with the Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit for Bacteria Live & Dead Cells (Biotium, #30027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For more information about the methodology of the HTS (high-throughput screening) system, please refer to our previous study30.

Results

Research flow

We generated a schematic diagram illustrating the colorectal cancer–associated mechanisms of ETBF and pks + E. coli (Fig. 1). To assess the therapeutic efficacy of their corresponding bacteriophages, Bac-FRP-3 and Esc-COP-23, we treated patient-derived normal colon organoids with each pathobiont and evaluated transcriptomic and protein-level changes. ETBF and pks + E. coli exposure elicited distinct proinflammatory and genotoxic transcriptional signatures, which were markedly attenuated by co-treatment with their respective phages. Notably, bacteriophage treatment reversed the pathological effects induced by bacterial culture supernatants at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels, indicating restoration of epithelial homeostasis through targeted microbial clearance.

Establishments of normal colon organoid lines

We successfully established two normal colon organoid lines derived from colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, designated as SNU-7321 N-NO and SNU-7514 N-NO, based on their corresponding tissue numbers. Our labeling system indicates whether the tissue is Normal (N), the origin of the sample (Seoul National University Hospital), and whether the tissue was used to establish a normal organoid line (NO). Short tandem repeat (STR) analysis confirmed that each organoid line matched its parental tissue, verifying the identity of the established lines (Supplementary Table 1). The general morphology and culture environments of the organoids were observed under optical microscopy, as shown in Fig. 2A. Additionally, the expression and pattern of the intestinal stem cell marker LGR5 was confirmed in the normal colon organoids (SNU-7514 N-NO only) using confocal microscopy, as shown in Fig. 2B. To evaluate potential cross-contamination between the original tissues and the derived organoids, short tandem repeat (STR) profiling was conducted using 27 genetic markers, including D5S818, D13S317, D7S820, D21S11, and vWA. The results confirmed that each organoid line was genetically identical to its corresponding parental tissue (Supplementary Table 1).

Determination of bacteria-killing efficiency from treatment of specific bacteriophages

The bacteria death of pks + E. coli at MOI (multiplicity of infection) 0.01 and ETBF at MOI 10 were notably high, indicating that both types of bacteriophages have a rapid bacteria-killing potential (Fig. 3A). Consistent bactericidal patterns were reproducibly observed across triplicate conditions within each experiment, conducted in two independent experiments.

We conducted a bacteria-killing assay using an HTS system for the analysis of captured images. We stained bacterial strains, which were treated with their corresponding bacteriophages, with a LIVE/DEAD labelling kit (Fig. 3B). Images were acquired using a 64× objective lens with immersion oil. Because high-magnification imaging is highly sensitive to the subtle motility of bacteria, obtaining precise Z-stacked images was technically challenging. To minimize bacterial movement during imaging, samples were solidified with 1% agar, which effectively restricted bacterial motility and allowed the acquisition of stable, high-resolution images. This experiment was designed to serve as a visual complement to the quantitative bactericidal data presented in Fig. 3A. While Fig. 3A provides quantitative evidence of bacteriophage-mediated bacterial killing, the imaging data in Fig. 3B were included to facilitate intuitive understanding of this effect at the single-cell level. Using ETBF as a representative example, the LIVE/DEAD staining clearly illustrates the reduction in viable bacterial populations following bacteriophage treatment.

Accordingly, this analysis was not intended as a primary quantitative assessment, but rather as supportive visual evidence to reinforce the bactericidal activity of bacteriophages.

Bacteriophage efficiently suppressed the pathogenic colonic bacteria in vitro

We evaluated four different MOI conditions to determine the optimal bacteriophage treatment parameters. The pks⁺ E. coli-specific bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 effectively suppressed pks⁺ E. coli from the early stage of interaction. Similarly, bacteriophage Bac-FRP-3 efficiently inhibited ETBF. Overall, both bacteriophages demonstrated effective antibacterial activity against their respective target strains (Fig. 3A). We ended the time-point screening at 8 h because the OD600 values of ETBF and pks + E. coli were so saturated in the control group that we could not accurately determine the efficiency of the bacteriophage. However, we observed that the optical density of the bacteriophage-treated group recovered after 6 h. Based on these results, we decided to measure phenotypical parameters and observe morphological alterations at 4 h after the treatment of bacteria and bacteriophage. The obtained result data was then normalized with the data obtained at zero time (0 h).

To evaluate the cytoprotective effect of bacteriophage against ETBF and pks + E. coli-induced cellular damage, relative cell viability was quantified across various infection and treatment conditions. As shown in Fig. 4, untreated control cells exhibited 100% relative viability, serving as the baseline for all comparisons.

Exposure to ETBF alone resulted in a significant reduction in cell viability (approximately 55%), indicating that ETBF infection exerts substantial cytotoxic effects (P < 0.05 versus control). Introduction of the ETBF-targeting bacteriophage Bac-FRP-3 restored viability to near-baseline levels (95%), demonstrating its protective efficacy (P < 0.05 versus ETBF without bacteriophage; ns versus control). Treatment with the bacteriophage Bac-FRP-3 resulted in an 88.9% recovery in cell viability relative to ETBF-induced damage.

Similarly, infection with pks + E. coli alone led to a marked decrease in viability (68%), which was significantly ameliorated upon treatment with the specific bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 (73%), confirming the bacteriophage’s functional role in neutralizing genotoxic E. coli-mediated cell injury. Bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 treatment partially restored cell viability, achieving a 15.6% recovery relative to pks + E. coli-induced damage.

Treatment with bacteriophages alone (Bac-FRP-3 + Esc-COP-23) in the absence of bacterial infection did not significantly alter cell viability compared to control, indicating minimal cytotoxicity of bacteriophage themselves.

Notably, co-treatment of supernatant ETBF and pks + E. coli led to the most profound reduction in viability (35%), revealing a synergistic deleterious effect of the two pathobionts. However, co-treatment with both bacteriophage Bac-FRP-3 and Esc-COP-23 significantly rescued cell viability to approximately 98% (P < 0.05 versus co-infection without bacteriophage), fully reversing the combined cytotoxic effects of ETBF and pks + E. coli. Importantly, the observation of epithelial cell death induced by pks + E. coli and ETBF is particularly significant, as both pathobionts possess potent pathogenic mechanisms capable of directly damaging and killing colonic epithelial cells. Previous studies have shown that such epithelial injury and cell death in vivo can trigger immune-inflammatory responses and contribute to the development of ulcerative colitis (UC), which is closely associated with colorectal cancer (CRC) progression31–33. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the extent to which bacteriophages could selectively suppress ETBF and pks + E. coli, thereby mitigating their cytotoxic effects on colonic epithelial cells.

Targeted bacteriophage therapy alleviates pathogen-induced genotoxic stress and epithelial disruption in intestinal organoids

To investigate the impact of bacteriophage treatments targeting ETBF and pks + E. coli on epithelial integrity and DNA damage, we performed immunofluorescent confocal microscopy on intestinal organoids under various treatment conditions (Fig. 5). Normal colon organoids were passaged and cultured for 7 days until fully grown. The organoids were then treated with bacterial culture supernatants derived from ETBF, pks + E. coli, and their corresponding bacteriophage-treated groups (Bac-FRP-3 and Esc-COP-23, respectively). After 24 h of incubation, the organoids were fixed and subjected to confocal microscopy. Organoids were stained for DAPI (blue) to visualize nuclei, E-cadherin (green) to assess epithelial junctional integrity, and γH2AX (red) as a marker of DNA double-strand breaks.

In both SNU-7321 N-NO and SNU-7514 N-NO control organoids (top row), epithelial structures maintained strong and continuous E-cadherin expression, outlining intact epithelial architecture. Minimal γH2AX expression was observed, indicating negligible baseline DNA damage. The merged images showed a clean overlay of nuclear and epithelial markers with minimal red signal, confirming a physiologically stable baseline.

Organoids exposed to ETBF without bacteriophage treatment demonstrated preserved epithelial morphology with moderate but increased γH2AX foci formation, compared to controls. However, since colibactin is a contact-dependent genotoxin, previous studies have reported that treatment with culture supernatant from pks + E. coli alone does not induce a detectable DNA damage response in cultured cell lines34,35. Therefore, we speculate that the observed γH2AX expression induced by the pks + E. coli supernatant is not attributable solely to colibactin-mediated DNA damage, but rather to unidentified DNA-damaging factors secreted by the pks + E. coli strain NCI101 used in this study. Accordingly, our focus was not to demonstrate the suppression of colibactin-mediated DNA damage by treatment with the Esc-COP-23 phage, but instead to determine whether pathogenic phenotypes—such as the DNA damage response and inflammation typically induced by pks + E. coli—could be alleviated by phage treatment.

Unlike colibactin produced by pks + E. coli, the BFT (B. fragilis toxin) secreted by ETBF is a non-contact, secreted metalloprotease toxin that is actually present in the culture supernatant. Previous studies have demonstrated that treatment of epithelial cell lines with ETBF supernatant containing BFT induces cytotoxic and inflammatory responses, including E-cadherin cleavage, activation of β-catenin, MAPK, and NF-κB signaling pathways, and elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-8, IL-6, and TNF-α36–39.

Significantly increased γH2AX signals were observed in organoids infected with genotoxic pks + E. coli in the absence of bacteriophage intervention, particularly in the SNU-7321 N-NO line. This was accompanied by preserved but somewhat distorted E-cadherin staining, suggesting that pks + E. coli induces a pronounced DNA damage response while starting to compromise epithelial organization. The merged images confirmed extensive colocalization of γH2AX with nuclei, further supporting a substantial genotoxic effect.

As previously mentioned, pks+ Escherichia coli secretes colibactin only upon adhesion to host cells, and the toxin is highly unstable, undergoing rapid degradation once released40. In this study, due to experimental limitations, we employed an indirect approach using bacterial culture supernatants rather than direct organoid–bacteria co-culture. Therefore, it was difficult to precisely determine the effects specifically attributable to colibactin.

Bacteriophage treatment targeting ETBF (Bac-FRP-3) has shown that E-cadherin expression remained robust and uninterrupted. This suggests effective mitigation of ETBF-induced epithelial cell junction damage via bacteriophage Bac-FRP-3 application.

Similarly, administration of bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 targeting pks + E. coli substantially reduced γH2AX signal intensity. Epithelial integrity was largely preserved as indicated by continuous E-cadherin staining. The therapeutic application of bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 effectively diminished pks + E. coli-mediated DNA damage, supporting its specificity and functional efficacy.

In the dual-pathogen condition without bacteriophage intervention, both γH2AX and E-cadherin staining revealed pronounced pathophysiological disruption. Widespread γH2AX expression was noted, with nuclear fragmentation and partial epithelial breakdown as evident from the irregular E-cadherin patterns. This synergistic interaction between ETBF and pks + E. coli significantly amplified genotoxic stress and epithelial injury.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the specificity and bactericidal efficacy of bacteriophages against individual bacterial strains. The co-treatment condition (ETBF and pks + E. coli) was therefore included as an additional experiment to assess the synergistic pathogenic effects of the bacterial strains.

Although not examined in the present study, the inclusion of a dual-phage treatment in this context could provide further insight into whether the observed synergistic effects can be mitigated. Such data would be valuable in supporting the potential of combined phage therapy as an effective therapeutic strategy under more physiologically relevant conditions, where multiple bacterial strains interact in vivo.

This data collectively demonstrates the specificity and efficacy of the treatment for targeted bacteriophages (Bac-FRP-3 and Esc-COP-23) in suppressing bacterial-induced epithelial damage and DNA stress responses. Particularly under co-infection conditions, the bacteriophage cocktail approach shows potent protective effects, suggesting a promising therapeutic strategy for microbiota-mediated genotoxicity in gastrointestinal tissues.

ETBF-specific bacteriophage Bac-FRP-3 administration selectively suppresses ETBF-induced NF-κB activation in colon organoids

First, to evaluate the effects of ETBF and pks + E. coli and their respective bacteriophages, Bac-FRP-3 and Esc-COP-23, four experimental conditions were established: each bacterium alone and each co-treated with its corresponding phage. Bacterial cultures under each condition were grown, and the resulting supernatants were collected and diluted to 20% (v/v) in organoid culture medium. These diluted supernatants were then applied to colonic organoids for three days, after which total RNA was extracted for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and subsequent transcriptomic analysis.

BFT, secreted by ETBF, and genotoxic factors and colibactin, produced by pks + E. coli, are well known to induce tight junction disruption, immune activation, genomic instability, and DNA damage response. To assess whether these phage treatments could restore the epithelial barrier at the protein level, confocal microscopy was employed. However, the primary aim of RNA-seq in this context was to determine whether bacteriophage administration could reverse the transcriptional alterations induced by BFT and colibactin exposure.

Because BFT cleaves epithelial junctional proteins such as E-cadherin and tight junction components at the protein level, its effects are difficult to capture through transcriptomic profiling alone. Furthermore, BFT exerts rapid and potent cytotoxicity, leading to abrupt reductions in cell viability, complicating experimental control and reproducibility. To overcome these limitations, we optimized the treatment concentration to minimize cell death while still capturing the pathogenic and restorative effects of BFT and phage, respectively. Based on preliminary titration assays, a 20% concentration of bacterial supernatant was selected as the optimal working dilution under our laboratory conditions.

However, due to the lack of precise metabolomic and component-level quantification, it was not feasible to normalize BFT or the DNA damage response-inducing factor concentrations to fixed molar units across samples. The variability in BFT and genotoxin secretion depends on the strain of ETBF or pks + E. coli and the specific culture medium composition and growth environment. To address this, we standardized bacterial culture conditions by inoculating bacteria at an initial optical density (OD₆₀₀) of 0.1, treating with the respective bacteriophages, and culturing until an OD₆₀₀ of 0.4 was reached, at which point the supernatants were harvested for downstream application.

To evaluate the transcriptional effects of ETBF and the potential restorative impact of targeted bacteriophage treatment, we analyzed key signaling pathways using RNA-seq of SNU-7321 N and SNU-7514 N normal colon organoids. ETBF infection markedly upregulated a panel of NF-κB–related genes, including CXCL8, IL1B, ICAM1, and TNFAIP3, in both donors. Heatmap visualization revealed that phage treatment substantially attenuated these responses, reverting expression levels toward baseline (Fig. 6A).

In our analysis, pathways were not pre-selected a priori; rather, we performed an unbiased, data-driven approach by ranking significantly enriched pathways derived from the RNA-seq dataset and visualizing the top-ranked candidates based on statistical significance and enrichment scores.

Notably, upon treatment with bacteriophages targeting ETBF and pks + E. coli, the NF-κB signaling pathway exhibited the most pronounced and statistically significant restoration toward control levels among the identified pathways. In particular, NF-κB signaling was the only pathway consistently observed across both conditions (Fig. 6A and B) that showed a common and robust recovery pattern, which led us to highlight it as a representative pathway.

Previous studies have demonstrated that exposure to ETBF-derived culture supernatants or infection with pks + E. coli leads to upregulation of NF-κB–associated gene expression, which is consistent with our observations. However, the precise mechanism underlying why NF-κB signaling appeared as the most prominently affected pathway in our RNA-seq dataset following treatment with bacterial culture supernatants remains to be fully elucidated.

In contrast, MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin pathways exhibited modest or inconsistent transcriptional changes upon ETBF exposure, with no statistically significant recovery observed upon phage treatment. Quantitative comparison of average log₂ fold-change values confirmed a significant reduction in NF-κB pathway activation upon phage treatment (p = 0.038), whereas changes in MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin pathways did not reach statistical significance (all p > 0.05). These findings highlight the selective inhibitory effect of bacteriophage on ETBF-mediated inflammatory signaling, particularly via the NF-κB axis.

Bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 also restores pks + E. coli–induced transcriptional dysregulation in colonic organoids

To assess the ability of bacteriophage Esc-COP-23 to reverse pks + E. coli–mediated transcriptional perturbations, we analyzed the gene expression profiles of two normal colon organoid lines (SNU-7321 N-NO and SNU-7514 N-NO) following treatment with pks + E. coli alone or in combination with Esc-COP-23. A focused panel of 40 genes implicated in the cell cycle, DNA damage response, NF-κB signaling, and immune response pathways was examined by log₂ fold-change calculation relative to untreated controls (Fig. 6B).

Heatmap clustering revealed that pks + E. coli induced a marked upregulation of genes involved in the NF-κB pathway (e.g., CXCL8, TNF, IL1B), as well as cell cycle regulators (MKI67, CCND1, CDK1) and DNA repair genes (TP53, CHEK1/2, BRCA1/2). Notably, co-treatment with Esc-COP-23 led to consistent attenuation of these upregulated signals across both organoid lines, indicating a reproducible phage-mediated suppressive effect.

Quantitative bar plot analysis of mean log₂ fold-changes across pathways confirmed significant transcriptional restoration by Esc-COP-23. NF-κB activity was significantly reduced in both SNU-7321 N (p < 0.05) and SNU-7514 N (p < 0.05) following phage treatment. Similarly, DNA damage response genes showed significant downregulation after phage co-treatment in both lines (p < 0.01), indicating reversal of pks + E. coli-induced genotoxic stress. Restoration of cell cycle homeostasis was also evident, although the change in SNU-7321 N was not statistically significant (p = 0.0839). Genes associated with immune activation, including ICAM1, HLA-B/C, and TNFAIP3, were significantly normalized by Esc-COP-23 (p < 0.05), suggesting modulation of inflammatory responses.

These findings demonstrate that Esc-COP-23 effectively counteracts pks + E. coli–induced transcriptional dysregulation across multiple oncogenic and inflammatory pathways in colonic organoids, supporting its potential as a targeted microbiome-intervention tool.

The Supplementary Data are directly related to this analysis and provide additional support for pathway selection. Specifically, heatmaps of the top 50 genes showing the most pronounced and consistent restoration patterns are presented for ETBF and Bac-FRP-3 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1) and for pks + E. coli and Esc-COP-23 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S2) across two normal colon organoid lines (SNU-7321 N-NO and SNU-7514 N-NO). These results further support the selection of NF-κB signaling as a key responsive pathway in our data.

Discussion

In this study, we employed patient-derived normal colon organoids to investigate the transcriptional impact of two well-characterized colorectal cancer–associated pathobionts—Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) and pks+ Escherichia coli—and evaluated the protective effects of their corresponding phages, Bac-FRP-3 and Esc-COP-23, respectively. This model allowed direct observation of epithelial responses to microbial challenge and phage intervention under controlled, reproducible conditions.

Over the past decade, advances in microbiome research have deepened our understanding of the microbial contributions to disease pathogenesis and led to the development of microbiome-targeted therapeutics. In parallel, the resurgence of interest in bacteriophage therapy has emerged as a promising response to the rise of antibiotic-resistant pathogens. Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics that indiscriminately affect both pathogenic and commensal bacteria, bacteriophages offer highly specific antibacterial activity with minimal off-target effects on the native microbiota.

While animal models have historically dominated bacteriophage research, their use is constrained by ethical concerns, high costs, and limited scalability. In contrast, three-dimensional (3D) organoid cultures recapitulate the cellular and molecular characteristics of human tissues and provide a rapid, ethically viable, and physiologically relevant platform for high-throughput screening. Organoid models thus hold significant promise for accelerating translational research in microbiome-associated diseases, including cancer.

Transcriptomic profiling revealed that ETBF exposure significantly activated NF-κB signaling, as evidenced by upregulation of IL1B, CXCL8, RELA, and TNFAIP3. These genes are central mediators of epithelial inflammation and have been implicated in ETBF-driven tumorigenesis. Co-treatment with Bac-FRP-3 substantially attenuated their expression, particularly in the SNU-7321 N line, indicating effective suppression of ETBF-induced inflammatory responses. In contrast, MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways showed minimal or statistically insignificant changes, suggesting that NF-κB activation is the predominant transcriptional response to ETBF in this context, and the main axis mitigated by phage intervention.

Similarly, pks + E. coli induced robust upregulation of inflammatory and immune response genes, including TNF, IL1B, CXCL8, CCL20, and HLA-B. Treatment with Esc-COP-23 significantly reversed these changes across both organoid lines. Notably, this effect does not stem from direct phage-epithelium interaction, but rather from the selective elimination of the pathogenic bacterial population. By removing the source of microbial genotoxic stress producing E. coli with the phage prevents activation of epithelial defense pathways and allows restoration of transcriptomic equilibrium.

While changes in DNA damage response and cell cycle–associated genes (TP53, CHEK1, CCND1, CDC25C) were also statistically significant, their fold-change magnitude was modest. This suggests that pks + E. coli-induced genotoxic effects may result in persistent cellular stress, whereas phage-induced suppression of inflammation is more readily reversible.

The transcriptional changes observed in our organoid supernatant-treated group are likely to reflect responses to other soluble bacterial factors—such as outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), lipopolysaccharides (LPS), or secreted inflammatory mediators—rather than the direct action of intact colibactin. Future studies employing direct E. coli–organoid contact models or clb mutant strains will be essential to precisely dissect colibactin-dependent versus colibactin-independent host responses.

The active colibactin molecule is highly unstable and rapidly decomposes outside bacterial or host membranes, and therefore is generally undetectable in cell-free supernatants of pks + E. coli cultures. Although active colibactin is not secreted into culture supernatants, trace breakdown products or adduct-derived fragments might be present under co-culture conditions, though these remain to be characterized in detail.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that bacteriophage therapy can indirectly restore epithelial transcriptomic homeostasis by targeting disease-associated bacteria. The dual-phage strategy proved effective in mitigating distinct yet converging inflammatory responses elicited by bacterial culture supernatants from ETBF and pks + E. coli, supporting its translational relevance in microbiome-based cancer prevention.

Our use of non-tumorigenic, patient-derived colon organoids represents a shift toward more human-relevant preclinical models. This approach enables real-time visualization of host-microbe interactions and phage-mediated rescue without reliance on animal models. Furthermore, the ability to capture transcriptional recovery across multiple inflammatory pathways provides a robust phenotypic readout for evaluating microbiome-modulating interventions.

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations and technical challenges should be acknowledged. First, the organoid model inherently lacks immune, stromal, and vascular components, which are critical for reproducing the full physiological complexity of the human intestine. Second, our experimental design focused on short-term exposure to microbial culture supernatants and therefore did not capture the longer-term dynamics of host–microbiota interactions. Third, the present analysis was restricted to two bacterial strains and their respective phages, which may not fully represent the diversity and complexity of the gut microbiome. In vivo validation will ultimately be required to confirm the safety and efficacy of the phage-mediated effects observed in vitro.

Beyond these study-specific constraints, current microbiome research itself faces clear technological limitations. The human microbiome comprises a vast array of bacterial species whose composition and function are regulated through intricate interspecies interactions. To faithfully reproduce these interactions in vitro, culture systems must support the co-culture of multiple bacterial strains; however, the coexistence of both aerobic and anaerobic species poses significant technical challenges. It remains extremely difficult to establish a single microenvironment that simultaneously maintains both oxygenated and anaerobic conditions. Furthermore, mammalian in vitro models such as cell lines or organoids inherently depend on oxygen-rich culture environments.

Additional challenges arise from the incompatibility of bacterial and mammalian culture media. Co-culture experiments require the mixing of these distinct media types, often compromising the optimal growth of both host cells and bacteria. As the number of bacterial species increases, not only inter-bacterial interactions but also physicochemical interactions between media become major variables, complicating reproducibility and standardization. Another factor is the routine use of antibiotics in mammalian cultures to prevent contamination. The inclusion or omission of antibiotics markedly affects bacterial proliferation, making it technically difficult to achieve stable and reproducible infection levels across experiments.

Although the field of microbiome research has progressed rapidly and continues to gain attention, these methodological hurdles underscore that substantial improvements are still required. Overcoming these limitations will enable the development of integrated organoid–microbiome systems that more accurately model the human gut ecosystem and advance translational applications such as precision phage therapy.
