Reprogramming gut microbiota and targeting CXCL1: The multifaceted mechanism of PLP1 in ameliorating alcohol-induced liver disease
Authors: Zhu Yang, Qiuyue Lv, Qiangbao Xu, Yan Chen, Yiping Yang, Zihan Li, Hui Che, Guodong Wang, Wei Wei, Jiangping Wu, Jun Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatic inflammation and gut barrier dysfunction are core pathologies of alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), yet effective therapies remain limited. Pueraria lobata polysaccharide (PLP1), a key active constituent of P. lobata Radix, has demonstrated hepatoprotective potential; however, its underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we sought to elucidate the therapeutic mechanism of PLP1 against experimental ALD by integrating proteomics, metabolomics, and gut microbiota analyses. We demonstrated that PLP1 initially functions through a microbiota-dependent pathway, transferable by fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), to restore gut barrier integrity and suppress the gut‒LPS‒TLR4 inflammatory axis. Furthermore, PLP1 exerted a direct, microbiota-independent effect, maintaining its therapeutic efficacy in antibiotic-depleted mice. Mechanistically, PLP1 directly and specifically targets CXCL1, promoting its ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation as confirmed by biophysical assays. The functional necessity of this interaction was definitively established via comprehensive genetic manipulation of CXCL1 in vitro and in vivo. Specifically, CXCL1 overexpression reversed PLP1's benefits, whereas CXCL1 knockdown mimicked its effects and occluded any additional benefit from PLP1. Overall, PLP1 ameliorates ALD by independently targeting both extrinsic and intrinsic inflammatory triggers. This study reveals the multifaceted pharmacology of a natural polysaccharide and validates a dual-pronged therapeutic strategy for ALD.KEY WORDS: Pueraria lobata polysaccharide, Alcohol-associated liver disease, Gut microbiota, CXCL1, Neutrophil infiltration, Gut–liver axis, iNOS-NO pathway, Ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation

Introduction

Alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) is a major global health concern, responsible for a significant proportion of cirrhosis cases and liver transplantations worldwide1. ALD encompasses a pathological spectrum ranging from simple steatosis to severe alcohol-associated hepatitis, a clinical syndrome with high mortality. Despite its growing prevalence, the pathogenesis of ALD remains incompletely understood, and there are currently no US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved targeted therapies2. Corticosteroids offer limited efficacy, leaving a substantial number of patients with liver transplantation as the only curative option. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets and develop safer, more effective treatment strategies3.

The gut‒liver axis is now recognized as a key player in ALD pathogenesis4. The gut microbiota, a complex microbial ecosystem, plays an essential role in host metabolism and immune homeostasis. Chronic alcohol consumption disrupts this ecosystem, causing dysbiosis that impairs the intestinal barrier5. This “leaky gut” permits the translocation of microbial products, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), into the portal circulation, where they can directly trigger inflammatory cascades in the liver2,6. While targeting this gut-centric pathway is a promising therapeutic strategy, the inflammatory processes within the liver itself represent another critical component of the disease.

Direct hepatic inflammation is central to the progression of ALD7. This pathological process is driven by a complex interplay between extrinsic pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as gut-derived lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and intrinsic damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from metabolically injured hepatocytes8,9. These signals converge to activate pro-inflammatory pathways, particularly the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88)/nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) axis10. Activation of this axis drives the expression of chemokines such as CXCL1, a potent recruiter of neutrophils to the liver11. These infiltrating neutrophils then directly contribute to liver damage by releasing a variety of harmful substances, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) and destructive enzymes such as myeloperoxidase (MPO)12, thereby injuring hepatocytes and propagating a vicious cycle of inflammation, driving disease progression from simple steatosis to more severe steatohepatitis13. Given the pivotal role of CXCL1 in this inflammatory cascade, blocking the CXCL1 signal to prevent this neutrophil-driven injury is a particularly promising therapeutic strategy.

Pueraria lobata Radix, a traditional Chinese herbal medicine, is documented in the Supplement to Materia Medica (Ben Cao Shi Yi) for its properties to “detoxify alcohol, alleviate body heat and redness, treat jaundice, and relieve painful urination”. Modern studies have revealed its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and blood circulation-enhancing properties. These findings highlight the potential of P. lobata as a therapeutic or preventive agent for ALD14. Among its bioactive components, P. lobata polysaccharides (PLPs) have garnered increasing attention from researchers for their potential in reducing oxidative stress, modulating immune responses, and promoting gut health by enhancing intestinal barrier function and supporting the growth of beneficial gut microbiota15. Their capacity to alleviate liver inflammation and improve metabolic parameters positions PLPs as a promising agent for ALD treatment. Our research group previously extracted and isolated a novel polysaccharide, PLP1, from P. lobata Radix, which significantly mitigated acute alcohol-induced liver injury in mice16. PLP1 is a homogeneous neutral polysaccharide with a moderate molecular weight of approximately 10.43 kDa, a property that may favor its solubility, bioavailability, and biological activity. In vivo experiments further demonstrated that PLP1 preferentially accumulated in liver tissue17, suggesting its potential for targeted liver therapy.

Here, we systematically validated our proposed dual-mechanism hypothesis for PLP1 using both the NIAAA mouse model and an ethanol-induced AML-12 hepatocyte injury model. To elucidate the microbiota-dependent pathway, we integrated 16S rRNA sequencing and metabolomics with functional validation via fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) and antibiotic (ABX) depletion experiments. Concurrently, to investigate the direct host-targeting pathway, we utilized proteomics to identify key molecular targets. We subsequently dissected the underlying mechanisms using a comprehensive suite of techniques. This included advanced biophysical interaction analyses using surface plasmon resonance (SPR), microscale thermophoresis (MST), and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), as well as cellular mechanistic studies involving protein stability and genetic transfection. Furthermore, we employed AAV-mediated in vivo gene intervention to rigorously verify the functional targets. Through this integrated and multi-level approach, we aimed to provide a holistic understanding of the regulatory network through which PLP1 ameliorates ALD. This work establishes a robust theoretical foundation for the clinical application of PLP1 and supports the broader development of P. lobata polysaccharide-based therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

PLP1 was extracted and purified from P. lobata Radix in our laboratory as previously described16. The extraction method and its applications are protected under Chinese Patent No. CN119060210A. Its purity was assessed using a Lc-10Avp high-performance gel permeation chromatography (HPGPC) system (Shimadzu, Japan), which revealed a high level of homogeneity, with a final purity of 96.6%. A structural diagram of PLP1 is also presented in Supporting Information Fig. S1. Additional details regarding reagent catalog numbers and antibody clones are provided in Supporting Information Table S1.

Animals and experimental design

Male SPF-grade C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were obtained from VITAL RIVER Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). Mice were housed under controlled conditions (23 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 5% humidity) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wannan Medical College (approval number: WNMC-AWE-2024306).

Based on the results of a preliminary dose-finding experiment, which demonstrated significant dose-dependent hepatoprotective effects at 200 and 400 mg/kg (Supporting Information Fig. S2), mice were randomly divided into five groups, with 12 mice in each group: control group (CON), model group (MOD), positive drug group (ETOH + silymarin), low-dose PLP1 group (ETOH + PLP1-L), and high-dose PLP1 group (ETOH + PLP1-H). The high-dose selection (400 mg/kg) was also justified by calculating the human equivalent dose (HED) based on FDA guidance (animal dose/12.3). The HED corresponds to approximately 32.5 mg/kg in humans (∼1.95 g/day for a 60 kg adult), which aligns with the standard clinical dosage range (1–5 g/day) of approved polysaccharide-based therapeutics such as Ganoderma and Astragalus polysaccharides. The mouse model was established using chronic and binge ethanol feeding (NIAAA model) as described previously14. PLP1 and silymarin were suspended in a 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na) solution (vehicle) for administration. Lieber-Decarli liquid diet and alcohol were used as dietary sources, with each mouse receiving 12.5 mL per day. The ETOH + silymarin group was fed the alcohol diet and gavaged with 150 mg/kg/day of silymarin. The ETOH + PLP1-L group received the alcohol diet and 200 mg/kg/day of PLP1 via gavage. The ETOH + PLP1-H group was also given the alcohol diet but gavaged with 400 mg/kg/day of PLP1. Mice in the CON group were fed the Lieber-Decarli liquid diet and administered an equal volume of the vehicle via gavage. Mice in the ETOH group were given the alcohol diet and administered an equal volume of the vehicle via gavage. From Day 1 to 5, the alcohol concentration gradually increased from 0% (v/v) to 4% (v/v), followed by a 5% (v/v) alcohol-containing diet from Day 6 to Day 15. During this period, mice in the PLP1 treatment groups were administered PLP1 once daily by oral gavage, while the control and model groups received an equal volume of the vehicle. On Day 16, mice in the model and PLP1 groups were given a single gavage of 31.5% (v/v) ethanol solution to induce acute liver injury, whereas mice in the CON group received an isocaloric 45% (w/v) maltodextrin solution. All mice were euthanized 9 h after ethanol administration for sample collection and further analysis18.

For the FMT experiment, 100 mg of feces from the MOD and PLP1 groups were collected under sterile conditions and thoroughly mixed in 1 mL of sterile saline solution. The mixture was then centrifuged at 800 × g for 3 min, and the resulting supernatant, containing the fecal microbiota suspension, was used for the FMT procedure. To deplete the gut microbiota prior to FMT, the mice were treated with a cocktail of four antibiotics administered in their drinking water for 7 consecutive days. After 7 days of antibiotic treatment, fecal samples were collected from a subset of mice to confirm successful microbiota depletion via qPCR of the 16S rRNA gene, and the remaining mice were fasted for 1 h before intestinal cleaning with polyethylene glycol. A total of 200 μL of polyethylene glycol solution (425 g/L in water) was administered by gavage over a 4-h period. Following intestinal cleaning, the mice were gavaged with the fecal microbiota suspension. The FMT was carried out every other day.

For the ABX experiment, six to eight-week-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade C57BL/6 male mice were randomly divided into three groups (ABX-CON, ABX-MOD, and ABX-PLP1, n = 8 per group) and received alcohol liquid feed. Mice in the ABX-PLP1 group were given daily gavage of PLP1 at a dose of 400 mg/kg. The ABX-CON and ABX-MOD groups received gavage with an equal volume of the vehicle. Mice in the three groups were treated with a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail for two weeks, followed by weekly administration until the end of the experiment to deplete the gut microbiota, as previously reported19.

For in vivo gene intervention, AAV vectors carrying shRNA targeting mouse Cxcl1 (AAV-shCxcl1), the full-length cDNA of mouse Cxcl1 (AAV-Cxcl1), or the corresponding control vectors were purchased from Cyagen. One week before the end of the NIAAA modeling period, mice were administered a single tail vein injection of the AAV vectors at a dose of 1 × 1011 viral genomes (vg)/mouse. Successful knockdown or overexpression of CXCL1 in the liver was confirmed by qPCR and Western blotting. The standard ALD modeling and PLP1 treatment were then continued as described.

During the experiment, mice were weighed daily at a fixed time, and their weights were recorded. At the end of the experiment, mice were anesthetized with an overdose of Avertin (tribromoethanol) within 9 h after the final ethanol administration. Blood, colon, colonic contents, and liver tissues were subsequently collected and stored at −80 °C for further experiments.

Biochemical detection

The levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG) in serum, were measured following the protocols provided by the respective manufacturers. The concentrations of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), MPO, and LPS in serum were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Following euthanasia, liver tissues were promptly fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 h. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 5 μm slices using a microtome, and subjected to standard hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining protocols.

Sirius red staining

Paraffin-embedded tissues were first processed by deparaffinization and rehydration, then incubated with 0.1% Sirius Red dissolved in saturated picric acid for 1 h. After rinsing in 0.5% acetic acid, the sections were dehydrated, coverslipped, and observed under bright-field.

Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining

Paraffin-embedded colon sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The sections were oxidized with 0.5% periodic acid solution for 10 min, rinsed with distilled water, and then incubated with Schiff reagent for 15 min in the dark. After washing under running water, the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, followed by dehydration and mounting.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

The localization of Occludin and ZO-1 in the colon was evaluated via immunofluorescence staining. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections from the colon were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked using standard reagents. A tissue autofluorescence suppressor was applied at ambient temperature for 30 min, followed by a 5-min rinse with distilled water. Sections were then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. On the next day, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were introduced, and the samples were incubated in the dark for 1 h. To reduce background signal, the autofluorescence quencher was reapplied at room temperature for 5 min, then rinsed for 3 min under running water. Finally, the sections were sealed with a DAPI-containing anti-fade mounting medium.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Paraffin-embedded liver sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the slides in citrate buffer. After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity and non-specific binding, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Ly6G, myeloperoxidase (MPO), TLR4, MyD88, NF-κB P65, and iNOS. The sections were then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody, followed by visualization with a DAB substrate kit.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from mouse liver tissues using the RNAiso Plus reagent. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from RNA using the TransScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR, which includes genomic DNA removal. Quantitative PCR was carried out using the PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix kit, following a two-step amplification protocol. The thermal cycling conditions were set as follows: 94 °C for 30 s, 94 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 30 s, for a total of 40 cycles. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Biophysical interaction assays

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis: The interaction between PLP1 and recombinant chemokines (CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL5) was analyzed using a Biacore 8K system (Cytiva, USA). CXCL1 was immobilized onto a CM5 sensor chip via standard amine coupling. A series of concentrations of PLP1 dissolved in HBS-EP + buffer was then injected over the chip surface. The association and dissociation phases were monitored in real-time. The resulting sensorgrams were fitted to a 1:1 binding model to calculate the dissociation constant (KD).

MicroScale thermophoresis (MST) assay: MST experiments were performed using a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies, Germany). Recombinant CXCL1 was labeled with a fluorescent dye according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The labeled CXCL1 was kept at a constant concentration, while PLP1 was titrated in a series of 16 dilutions. Samples were loaded into standard capillaries and the thermophoresis movement was measured. The change in the normalized fluorescence was plotted against the logarithm of the PLP1 concentration, and the data were fitted to derive the dissociation constant (KD).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC): The interaction between PLP1 and the chemokines CXCL1 and CCL2 was evaluated using a PEAQ-ITC microcalorimeter (Malvern). To minimize heat artifacts from solvent effects, all protein and PLP1 samples were prepared in PBS buffer. Final concentrations were set at 20 μmol/L for both CXCL1 and CCL2, and 200 μmol/L for PLP1. A total of 280 μL of protein solution was loaded into the sample chamber. The assay was carried out at 25 °C under constant stirring at 500 rpm. Following thermal equilibration of the instrument, the PLP1 solution was incrementally injected into the cell in 20 steps, starting from 0.4 μL and increasing by 2 μL at 90-s intervals.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA): Cells were either treated or not treated with PLP1 (1 μmol/L) for 24 h. Following trypsin digestion, the cells were lysed, and total protein was collected. The protein lysate was then divided into equal parts and heated at the indicated temperatures (46, 49, 52, 55, 58, and 61 °C) for 3 min each. The corresponding sample buffer was added to the protein samples. The melting curve of CXCL1 was determined by Western blot (WB) in the presence or absence of PLP1.

Biotin pull-down assay: To prepare conditioned medium, AML-12 cells were cultured to 80‒90% confluency and then incubated in serum-free medium for 24 h. The conditioned medium was then collected and centrifuged to remove cells and debris. For the pull-down assay, the conditioned medium was incubated with biotinylated PLP1 or biotin as a negative control overnight at 4 °C. Streptavidin-agarose beads were then added and incubated for an additional 2 h. The beads were washed extensively with PBS, and the bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by Western blotting using an anti-CXCL1 antibody.

Western blotting

Proteins were isolated from liver tissues using a commercial protein extraction kit supplemented with 1% phosphatase inhibitor and 1% PMSF. The obtained protein samples were separated by electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at ambient temperature for 2 h. Primary antibodies targeting TLR4, CXCL1 and NF-κB were then applied, and the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were added, followed by a 1-h incubation at room temperature after three washes with TBST. After an additional three washes, the membranes were treated with ECL reagents and visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system. Grayscale intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software.

16S rRNA analyses

In accordance with published methods, genomic DNA was extracted from colonic contents and used for 16S rRNA gene sequencing by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., located in Hangzhou, China.

Untargeted liver metabolomics analyses

Liver tissues were thawed on ice, and metabolites were isolated using a 50% methanol solution. In brief, 100 mg of each sample was homogenized with 1 mL of pre-chilled 50% methanol, vortexed for 1 min, and allowed to incubate at ambient temperature for 10 min. The mixture was then stored at −20 °C overnight. After centrifugation at 4000 × g for 20 min, the resulting supernatant was transferred into a new 96-well plate. Samples were preserved at −80 °C until further LC‒MS detection. For quality control, 10 μL of each extract was combined to generate a pooled QC sample. All sample injections were conducted using the LC‒MS platform according to the manufacturer's instructions. Mass spectrometry data acquisition and calibration were performed on a high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer prior to data analysis.

Olink-based proteomic analysis

Protein quantification was performed using the Olink® 96-panel platform (Olink Proteomics AB, Uppsala, Sweden), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. This platform employs the Proximity Extension Assay (PEA), which enables multiplex detection of 92 protein targets using only 1 μL of each sample. In this method, oligonucleotide-conjugated antibody pairs bind to their respective protein targets and, upon spatial proximity, trigger DNA polymerization to generate a specific PCR sequence. The resulting DNA was quantified through microfluidic-based real-time PCR (Signature Q100, LC-Bio, Hangzhou, China). Ct-values were subjected to quality assurance and normalized using both internal and external reference standards. Protein expression levels were expressed as NPX (Normalized Protein eXpression), calculated on a log2 scale, where a higher NPX corresponds to increased protein abundance.

Cell culture and treatment

AML-12 cells (obtained from Guangzhou Cellcook Biotech Co., Ltd., China; Cat# CC9038) were maintained in DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin), insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS) solution, and 40 ng/mL dexamethasone (Sigma‒Aldrich). Cultures were kept in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and the culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days. Upon reaching 70%–80% confluence, cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution. Cells were seeded into plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL for experiments, using passages 3 to 15 to maintain consistency. For induction of injury, AML-12 cells were exposed to 250 mmol/L ethanol for 24 h. To assess the protective role of PLP1, co-treatment with PLP1 at various concentrations (0–200 μmol/L) was performed during the same period. To verify the functional involvement of the iNOS pathway, specific inhibition experiments were conducted using the nitric oxide synthase inhibitor NG-monomethyl-l-arginine (L-NMMA; MCE). Cells were treated with l-NMMA (50 μmol/L) concurrently with ethanol and PLP1 exposure.

Cell transfection

pcDNA plasmids and negative controls were transfected into AML-12 cells and followed by a 24-h incubation. The transfection of siRNA or overexpression plasmids was conducted using Lipofectamine 3000, adhering to the manufacturer's guidelines. After transfection and a 24-h incubation, cells were processed for subsequent assays or treatments based on the experimental setup.

Protein stability and degradation assays

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay: To assess the stability of the CXCL1 protein, AML-12 cells were treated with PLP1 or vehicle for 12 h. Subsequently, the protein synthesis inhibitor Cycloheximide (CHX, 100 μg/mL) was added to the culture medium. Cells were harvested at various time points (0, 2, 4, 8 h) after CHX addition, and the remaining levels of CXCL1 protein were assessed by Western blotting at the indicated time points to determine the protein half-life.

Proteasome and lysosome inhibition assay: To investigate the degradation pathway, AML-12 cells were pre-treated for 1 h with either the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (10 μmol/L) or the lysosome inhibitor Chloroquine (CQ, 50 μmol/L). Following pre-treatment, cells were co-incubated with PLP1 for 12 h, and the total cellular protein levels of CXCL1 were then assessed by Western blotting.

Imaging and analysis

All histological and immunofluorescence images were acquired using a panoramic tissue section imaging system (PanoBrain, Meca Scientific). This system was utilized to capture whole-slide images for H&E, PAS, Sirius Red, and IHC stainings ensuring comprehensive field selection. For immunofluorescence, images were acquired using the fluorescence module of the same system. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH). Specifically, IHC were quantified by measuring the positive staining area or integrated optical density (IOD), while immunofluorescence intensity was analyzed for target protein expression.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Tests for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) were performed on all datasets. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were assessed via Student's t-test, while differences among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test (for two groups) or Kruskal‒Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc test (for multiple groups) was applied. For microbiota community analysis, PERMANOVA was used to assess group separation. For metabolomics, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify functionally relevant pathways. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PLP1 ameliorates alcohol-induced liver injury, steatosis, and inflammation in vivo

To evaluate the hepatoprotective effects of PLP1 against ALD, we established a chronic-plus-binge NIAAA mouse model (Fig. 1A). During the modeling period, mice in the alcohol-fed groups lost weight over time, a trend that was not significantly affected by PLP1 or silymarin treatment (Fig. 1B). Compared to the control group, mice in the model (MOD) group showed a significant increase in the liver/body weight ratio (Fig. 1C), accompanied by markedly elevated serum levels of the liver injury biomarkers ALT and AST (Fig. 1D and E). Treatment with both low and high doses of PLP1, as well as silymarin, significantly reversed these changes.

Histopathological analysis revealed the extent of liver damage (Fig. 1H). H&E staining showed that control mice had normal liver architecture, whereas mice in the MOD group displayed severe steatosis, hepatocyte ballooning, disordered hepatic cords, and significant inflammatory cell infiltration. Oil Red O staining further confirmed massive lipid accumulation in the livers of MOD mice. Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining for Ly6G, a neutrophil-specific marker, revealed a dramatic increase in neutrophil infiltration in the MOD group. All these pathological changes were substantially alleviated by treatment with PLP1 and silymarin.

To quantify these observations, we analyzed biochemical and molecular markers of steatosis and inflammation. Consistent with the histological findings, PLP1 treatment significantly reduced the elevated levels of serum TG and TC (Fig. 1F and G) and hepatic TG and TC (Fig. 1J and K). Quantitative analysis of the Oil Red O-stained area also confirmed a dose-dependent reduction in lipid accumulation (Fig. 1I). Moreover, PLP1 markedly suppressed the hepatic inflammatory response. This was evidenced by a significant decrease in neutrophil infiltration, as quantified by Ly6G integrated density (Fig. 1L), reduced hepatic MPO expression (Fig. 1M), and lower serum levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Fig. 1N‒P).

PLP1 ameliorates ALD-induced gut dysbiosis, restores intestinal barrier integrity, and suppresses gut-derived hepatic inflammation

Changes in the gut microbial composition have been demonstrated to strongly influence ALD development20. Given that polysaccharides are known to be metabolized by the gut microbiota into bioactive compounds, we investigated whether PLP1's protective effects involved modulation of the microbial community. We conducted 16S rRNA sequencing analysis on colon contents to investigate how PLP1 impacts the gut microbiota. Alpha diversity analysis showed that key indices for microbial richness and diversity, including Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and Observed OTUs, were significantly reduced in the model (MOD) group, and PLP1 treatment effectively restored all of these indices (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, beta diversity analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed a distinct structural separation between the MOD and CON groups, with the PLP1-treated group clustering more closely with the CON group. At the phylum and genus levels (Fig. 2C and D), PLP1 treatment reversed the alcohol-induced increase in the phylum Proteobacteria. Notably, many bacteria within this phylum, such as the genus Escherichia–Shigella which was also significantly suppressed, are Gram-negative and represent the primary source of endotoxin LPS. Concurrently, PLP1 increased the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, which are known to be crucial for gut health. Given that gut dysbiosis is a primary driver of intestinal barrier dysfunction in ALD, we investigated the integrity of the colonic barrier. Histological analysis (Fig. 2E) revealed that mice in the MOD group had depleted goblet cells (PAS staining), reduced mucus production (MUC-2 staining), and disrupted localization of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin. PLP1 treatment substantially reversed all of these pathological changes. Consistent with these observations, the mRNA expression of the tight junction genes Zo-1 and Ocln was significantly downregulated by alcohol, an effect that was reversed by PLP1 treatment (Fig. 2F and G). A compromised barrier allows for the translocation of bacterial endotoxins like LPS into circulation, and accordingly, we found that serum LPS levels were markedly elevated in the MOD group, an effect that was significantly suppressed by PLP1 (Fig. 2H). As the influx of gut-derived LPS is a primary trigger for hepatic inflammation via the TLR4 pathway, we next examined this signaling in the liver. Both Western blotting (Fig. 2I) and immunohistochemistry (Fig. 2M) showed that the expression and activation of key pathway components (TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB P65) were significantly increased in the livers of ALD mice, while quantitative analysis confirmed that PLP1 treatment markedly inhibited this entire inflammatory axis (Fig. 2J‒L, N‒P). Together, these results demonstrate that PLP1 remodels the gut microbiota, which, in turn, restores intestinal barrier integrity, reduces the translocation of LPS, and ultimately suppresses TLR4-mediated inflammatory signaling in the liver.

PLP1 regulation of hepatic metabolic pathways in ALD mouse model

Given that the gut microbiota profoundly influences hepatic health through the production of bioactive metabolites that regulate liver inflammation and lipid deposition21, we performed untargeted metabolomics profiling of mouse hepatic tissue to deeply investigate the protective mechanism of PLP1. Through partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), we found that the metabolic profiles of the CON, MOD, and PLP1 groups were distinctly separated, suggesting that ethanol significantly disrupted liver metabolism, while PLP1 intervention effectively corrected this metabolic imbalance in ALD mice (Fig. 3A).

(E) Representative IHC images of iNOS in liver tissue (magnification, 200 ×; scale bar, 100 μm). (F) Representative Western blot bands for iNOS and GAPDH. To prioritize pathways relevant to the inflammatory pathology of ALD, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). This analysis highlighted ‘arginine and proline metabolism’ as a key, significantly enriched pathway functionally linked to the disease state (Fig. 3B). Based on these results, we focused our validation on the rate-limiting enzymatic step in this pathway, which is the conversion of arginine to NO and citrulline by iNOS19. We first verified that alcohol significantly increased the level of citrulline, a direct co-product of the iNOS reaction, indicating pathway overactivation. PLP1 treatment effectively reversed this increase and normalized citrulline levels (Fig. 3C). This metabolic regulation translated into a functional anti-inflammatory outcome, as the pathologically elevated levels of the other product, NO, were also markedly suppressed by PLP1 (Fig. 3D). To confirm that these metabolic changes stemmed from the regulation of the enzyme itself, we assessed iNOS expression. We found that PLP1 significantly suppressed the alcohol-induced upregulation of iNOS at both the protein level, as evidenced by IHC (Fig. 3E) and Western blot (Fig. 3F and G), and the mRNA level (Fig. 3H).

Causal validation of the iNOS/NO pathway in PLP1-mediated protection. To verify whether the observed metabolic regulation of arginine/iNOS is causally linked to the anti-inflammatory outcome, we treated ethanol-injured AML-12 cells with PLP1 in the presence of the specific NOS inhibitor, L-NMMA (Supporting Information Fig. S3A). As expected, L-NMMA treatment significantly suppressed NO production (Fig. S3B) and downstream inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, at both protein (Fig. S3C and S3D) and transcriptional levels (Fig. S3E and S3F). Crucially, co-treatment with PLP1 did not produce an additive anti-inflammatory effect when compared to L-NMMA alone. This indicates that PLP1's anti-inflammatory effect is strictly dependent on iNOS activity, as blocking this pathway abolishes its efficacy.

In summary, our integrated analysis reveals a specific mechanistic pathway. By suppressing the expression of iNOS, PLP1 modulates the hepatic arginine metabolism pathway, reduces the production of the pro-inflammatory mediator NO, and thereby alleviates ALD-associated inflammation.

The gut microbiota mediates the therapeutic effects of PLP1 in ALD mice

Correlation analysis revealed strong associations between the PLP1-induced shifts in specific bacterial genera and the amelioration of key pathological parameters and hepatic metabolites (Supporting Information Figs. S4 and S5). This strong association provided a clear rationale to directly assess whether this relationship was causal. To this end, we transplanted the fecal microbiota from the MOD and PLP1-H donor groups into two cohorts of antibiotic-pretreated recipient mice (FMT-MOD and FMT-PLP1, respectively), which were then subjected to the ALD model (Fig. 4A). Analysis of fecal DNA from recipient mice prior to transplantation confirmed that the antibiotic regimen had successfully depleted the native microbiota, ensuring the mice were suitable for transplantation (Supporting Information Fig. S6). Subsequent analysis of the recipient gut microbiota confirmed that the community profile was successfully transferred, mirroring key changes observed in the donor mice. Specifically, at the phylum level, the FMT-PLP1 group exhibited a lower relative abundance of Proteobacteria, a primary source of endotoxin (Fig. 4J). At the genus level, this was accompanied by a significant reduction in pro-inflammatory genera such as Escherichia–Shigella and an enrichment of beneficial genera including Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, changes highly consistent with the donor microbiota profile (Fig. 4I).

Crucially, the transplantation of the PLP1-modified microbiota was sufficient to recapitulate the therapeutic phenotype in recipient mice. The FMT-PLP1 group exhibited a significantly lower liver/body weight ratio (Fig. 4C) and reduced serum levels of ALT and AST (Fig. 4D and E) compared to the FMT-MOD group. Histological analysis of the liver (Fig. 4B) confirmed these findings, revealing that the FMT-PLP1 group had markedly alleviated steatosis (Oil Red O), inflammation (H&E), and neutrophil infiltration (Ly6G). Consistent with this, serum levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were also significantly suppressed in the FMT-PLP1 group (Fig. 4F‒H).

Furthermore, the PLP1-modified microbiota restored gut barrier function in the recipient mice. Histological analysis of the colon showed improved goblet cell numbers, mucus production, and tight junction integrity in the FMT-PLP1 group (Fig. 4K). This was accompanied by a significant reduction in serum LPS levels (Fig. 4L) and an upregulation of the tight junction genes Zo-1 and Occludin (Fig. 4M and N). Downstream in the liver, this reduction in endotoxemia led to a marked suppression of the entire TLR4/NF-κB and iNOS-NO inflammatory signaling pathways (Fig. 4O‒X). To further solidify this functional link, a correlation analysis confirmed that the abundance of key transferred genera in the recipient mice, such as Akkermansia, was significantly and negatively associated with markers of liver injury and inflammation (Supporting Information Fig. S12). Taken together, the FMT experiment demonstrates that the gut microbiota from PLP1-treated mice is sufficient to improve ALD. These findings indicate that PLP1 exerts its protective effect, at least in part, by modulating the gut microbiota to restore barrier function and suppress the downstream gut–liver inflammatory axis.

PLP1 alleviates alcoholic liver injury through both microbiota-dependent and direct mechanisms

To determine whether the gut microbiota is essential for PLP1's therapeutic effect, C57BL/6 mice were treated with a cocktail of antibiotics (ABX) to deplete the gut microbiota (Fig. 5A). In this microbiota-depleted state, PLP1 treatment still provided robust protection. It significantly reduced the alcohol-induced elevation of serum ALT and AST (Fig. 5B and C) and suppressed the increase in hepatic triglycerides (Fig. 5E and F). Histological analysis further validated this direct hepatoprotective effect, showing that PLP1 markedly alleviated hepatic steatosis (Oil Red O), inflammation (H&E), and neutrophil infiltration (Ly6G) (Fig. 5D). Consistent with the histology, PLP1 also reduced the levels of serum inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. 5G and H). These results clearly demonstrate that PLP1 has a direct, host-targeting therapeutic effect.

To understand the mechanism behind this direct effect, we examined key inflammatory pathways in the liver. Interestingly, in this microbiota-depleted state (as confirmed by low serum LPS, Fig. 5I), PLP1 did not significantly inhibit the alcohol-induced activation of the upstream TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway (Fig. 5J‒Q). In striking contrast, PLP1 still robustly suppressed the downstream iNOS–NO axis (Fig. 5R and S). This crucial finding suggests that PLP1's microbiota-independent effect is mediated through a distinct mechanism that specifically targets the iNOS–NO pathway, rather than the upstream, endogenously-activated TLR4 pathway.

PLP1 mitigates alcohol-induced liver injury by directly targeting CXCL1 and promoting its ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation

Having established that PLP1 exerts direct, microbiota-independent hepatoprotective effects, we first verified its ability to target hepatocytes. Fluorescence tracking revealed a specific and time-dependent accumulation of PLP1 in the liver and its energy-dependent uptake into AML-12 hepatocytes (Supporting Information Fig. S7). Subsequently, we validated the in vitro model and determined the optimal safe and effective concentration of PLP1 for downstream assays (Supporting Information Fig. S8).

With the cellular model established, we next sought to identify the specific host molecular target using Olink proteomic analysis. The results indicated that PLP1 plays a critical role in the expression and modulation of inflammation-associated proteins in the alcoholic liver damage model (Fig. 6A). Correlation analysis identified a strong positive association between CXCL1 and CCL2 (Fig. 6B), suggesting that these two molecules may act synergistically in the inflammatory response. GO enrichment analysis further suggested that PLP1's function is closely associated with biological processes related to “neutrophil migration” and “chemokine-mediated signaling pathway”. With these findings, we hypothesized that PLP1 may exert its direct protective effects by targeting key chemokines. To identify the most likely candidate, we first performed molecular docking simulations. The results predicted a high-affinity interaction between PLP1 and CXCL1, with a binding energy of −7.3 kcal/mol, which was notably stronger than its predicted interaction with other chemokines like CCL2 (Fig. 6C). To experimentally validate this computational hypothesis, we employed two independent, gold-standard biophysical assays. Both SPR (Fig. 6D–F) and MST (Fig. 6H) conclusively demonstrated a direct physical interaction, yielding highly consistent dissociation constants of approximately 6 μmol/L. Specificity was further confirmed by SPR and ITC, showing that PLP1 had no discernible binding affinity for structurally homologous chemokines (CXCL2, CXCL5) or CCL2 (Supporting Information Fig. S9). Having identified CXCL1 as the direct target, we next sought to elucidate the precise molecular consequence of this interaction within the cellular environment. We first confirmed target engagement in live cells. A CETSA demonstrated that PLP1 treatment increased the thermal stability of endogenous CXCL1 (Fig. 6G), and a biotin-streptavidin affinity pull-down assay successfully captured intracellular CXCL1 in a specific and competitive manner (Fig. 6I and J). This direct binding prompted us to investigate its effect on protein stability. A CHX chase assay revealed that PLP1 treatment significantly accelerated the degradation of CXCL1, shortening its cellular half-life (Fig. 6K and L). To identify the specific degradation pathway, we used inhibitors and found that the proteasome inhibitor MG132, but not the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine, rescued CXCL1 from PLP1-mediated degradation (Fig. 6K and L). To substantiate this mechanism with direct evidence, we performed an in cellulo ubiquitination assay. The results conclusively demonstrated that in the presence of MG132, PLP1 treatment significantly enhanced the poly-ubiquitination of CXCL1 (Fig. 6M). Conversely, removal of MG132 resulted in the rapid clearance of these ubiquitinated species, confirming that PLP1 promotes CXCL1 turnover via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Finally, to confirm that this axis is functionally required for hepatoprotection, we performed genetic epistasis experiments using Cxcl1 overexpression and knockdown plasmids (Supporting Information Fig. S10A and S10I). As a baseline, PLP1 treatment effectively rescued AML-12 hepatocytes from ethanol-induced injury. However, when CXCL1 was overexpressed, this protective effect was substantially blunted, with injury markers (ALT, AST) and inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) remaining high despite PLP1 treatment (Fig. S10B–S10H). Conversely, knocking down CXCL1 with shRNA mimicked the protective phenotype of PLP1, and in this context, PLP1 treatment did not confer any additional significant benefit (Fig. S10J–S10Q). Collectively, these converging lines of evidence demonstrate that PLP1 physically binds to CXCL1, promotes its ubiquitin–proteasomal degradation, and thereby attenuates the inflammatory injury.

The results of this rescue experiment were clear. Hepatic overexpression of CXCL1 substantially blunted the therapeutic effects of PLP1, as the improvements in serum ALT and AST were significantly reversed (Fig. 7B and C). Critically, the ability of PLP1 to reduce hepatic neutrophil infiltration, as quantified by MPO and Ly6G staining, was also completely counteracted by CXCL1 overexpression (Fig. 7D‒F). This demonstrates that PLP1's ability to prevent neutrophil accumulation is causally dependent on its antagonism of CXCL1.

We next examined the downstream and upstream signaling pathways to dissect the mechanism. Consistent with the neutrophil data, overexpression of CXCL1 reversed PLP1's suppression of the downstream iNOS‒NO axis (Fig. 7J–O). Importantly, we then checked the upstream TLR4/NF-κB pathway. As expected, overexpression of CXCL1 alone did not further activate the TLR4 pathway compared to the ETOH group. Furthermore, PLP1 retained its ability to significantly suppress this pathway even upon AAV-mediated CXCL1 overexpression (Fig. 7K‒M). This confirms that PLP1's effect on the TLR4 pathway (likely via its microbiota-LPS action) is separate and operates in parallel to its direct targeting of the CXCL1‒iNOS axis.

Collectively, these in vivo data provide conclusive evidence that PLP1's direct hepatoprotective effect is critically dependent on its ability to antagonize the CXCL1-mediated neutrophil recruitment and iNOS‒NO inflammatory axis in the liver, a mechanism that is distinct from its effects on the TLR4 pathway.

The results demonstrated a clear mimicking and occlusion effect for PLP1's direct actions. Knockdown of CXCL1 alone was highly protective, significantly reducing serum ALT/AST and hepatic neutrophil infiltration to a degree comparable to that of PLP1 treatment (Fig. 8B‒F). Most importantly, in the context of CXCL1 already being knocked down, the additional administration of PLP1 conferred no further significant benefit. This occlusion was observed not only for liver injury markers and neutrophil infiltration (Fig. 8B‒G) but also for the downstream iNOS-NO axis (Fig. 8N and O). This indicates that once the primary direct target, CXCL1, and its downstream inflammatory cascade are suppressed, this arm of PLP1's therapeutic efficacy is occluded.

In striking contrast, when we examined the upstream TLR4/NF-κB pathway, a different pattern emerged. As expected, CXCL1 knockdown alone did not significantly affect the alcohol-induced activation of the TLR4 pathway. However, PLP1 treatment was still able to exert a significant inhibitory effect on this pathway, even in the absence of its direct target CXCL1 (Fig. 8J‒M).

Collectively, these genetic knockdown experiments provide a powerful and elegant dissection of PLP1's dual mechanism. They conclusively demonstrate that PLP1's direct, microbiota-independent effect is critically and necessarily mediated through its functional antagonism of the CXCL1-driven inflammatory cascade, a mechanism that operates in parallel to its separate, indirect effect on the gut‒LPS‒TLR4 axis.

Discussion

Our study expands upon the traditional paradigm regarding polysaccharide bioavailability and mechanism of action. While acknowledging the importance of gut microbiota modulation, we reveal that PLP1 acts via a novel dual therapeutic strategy in the context of ALD22. On one hand, acting via the canonical gut–liver axis, PLP1 functions as a prebiotic to remodel the gut ecosystem, restore barrier integrity, and suppress the LPS–TLR4 pathway, a mechanism that was causally verified by our FMT experiments. On the other hand, more importantly, our antibiotic-depletion experiments unveiled a second, microbiota-independent mechanism. Specifically, we demonstrate that PLP1 is absorbed and acts directly within the liver, where it physically binds to CXCL1 and promotes its proteasomal degradation, thereby dismantling the downstream iNOS-NO inflammatory axis. Thus, PLP1 provides comprehensive protection by concurrently targeting the extrinsic, PAMP-driven gut pathway and the intrinsic, DAMP-driven hepatic inflammatory loop.

Consistent with the canonical gut‒liver axis hypothesis23, we confirmed that PLP1 acts as a prebiotic to restore intestinal barrier integrity. Alcohol-induced dysbiosis disrupts the mucus barrier, facilitating the translocation of endotoxin (LPS) and activating the hepatic TLR4/NF-κB pathway24. Our 16S rRNA sequencing revealed that PLP1 reversed this phenotype by specifically suppressing pro-inflammatory genera, notably Escherichia–Shigella25, while concurrently enriching beneficial populations, including Akkermansia26 and Bifidobacterium27, which play crucial roles in mucus production and immune modulation. This microbial remodeling led to a structural restoration of tight junctions (ZO-1, Occludin) and a functional reduction in serum LPS levels28, thereby suppressing the downstream hepatic inflammatory response. The causal role of this axis was definitively established by our FMT experiments, where the transfer of PLP1-modified microbiota was sufficient to ameliorate ALD in recipient mice.

Regarding targeting specificity, our biophysical assays confirmed that PLP1 directly binds to CXCL1 but does not interact with its close structural homologs, including CXCL2 and CXCL5, or with the CC chemokine CCL2. These findings suggest a preferential recognition of CXCL1. However, given the large size of the chemokine superfamily, we acknowledge that the current study has not exhaustively ruled out interactions with all other chemokines. Therefore, while our data supports a specific mode of action, future broad-panel screening is warranted to fully define the global selectivity profile of PLP1. Furthermore, regarding the spatial mechanism, while we have confirmed intracellular degradation, it remains to be determined whether PLP1 can also induce the degradation of secreted CXCL1 in the extracellular environment. Given the high binding affinity confirmed by MST and SPR, extracellular neutralization is probable, and future studies will specifically investigate whether PLP1 targets both the intracellular and extracellular pools of CXCL1.

Although the microbiota-dependent pathway was anticipated, a central and novel finding of our study emerged from our antibiotic-depletion experiments, which unveiled a potent and microbiota-independent mechanism. We identified the chemokine CXCL1, a critical driver of neutrophil-mediated liver injury29,30, as a direct molecular target of PLP1. This interaction was rigorously validated through a comprehensive suite of biophysical assays, including SPR, MST, cellular CETSA, and pull-down experiments. Crucially, we elucidated a novel functional consequence of this binding at the molecular level. Our ubiquitination assays and protein stability analysis revealed that PLP1 binding significantly enhances the poly-ubiquitination of CXCL1, thereby accelerating its turnover via the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway. While the identification of the specific E3 ubiquitin ligase involved remains an important area for future investigation31, we firmly established the downstream therapeutic causality of this axis. Our metabolomics screen initially linked this regulation to the arginine–iNOS–NO pathway32. We subsequently confirmed this causal link through convergent evidence, demonstrating that pharmacological inhibition of iNOS in vitro completely negated the anti-inflammatory efficacy of PLP1, and genetic knockdown of CXCL1 in vivo abolished the ability of PLP1 to further suppress inflammation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PLP1 targets CXCL1 to dismantle the downstream iNOS/NO inflammatory cascade.

The findings of this study have broader implications, particularly for the debate regarding polysaccharide bioavailability. This direct targeting mechanism is particularly significant because it challenges the conventional view that large polysaccharides act solely within the gut. We significantly expand upon the traditional paradigm where large polysaccharides primarily exert their effects within the gastrointestinal tract. By providing conclusive evidence for a direct, systemic, and microbiota-independent mechanism, our study contributes to the growing body of literature suggesting that some orally administered polysaccharides can be absorbed to engage host targets directly. While the precise absorption mechanism warrants further investigation, our current fluorescence tracking data provide direct visual confirmation of PLP1's specific accumulation in hepatic tissue and its subsequent uptake by hepatocytes. Together with our previous pharmacokinetic evidence17, these findings establish a solid material basis for the intracellular pharmacological actions observed here. Furthermore, our comprehensive validation of the CXCL1 axis, which ranges from direct binding verification to definitive in vivo causal proof, strongly reinforces its role as a high-potential therapeutic target for ALD.

While our study is the first to reveal the novel dual-pronged mechanism of PLP1 targeting both the gut–liver axis and the hepatic CXCL1 pathway, we acknowledge certain limitations regarding the experimental model. The NIAAA protocol used here primarily induces steatohepatitis and is not sufficient to produce advanced fibrosis. Although significant histological fibrosis was not observed, PLP1 significantly suppressed key molecular markers of early fibrogenesis, such as TGF-β1 and α-SMA (Fig. S8). Interestingly, this preliminary anti-fibrogenic potential appeared to be mediated by both microbiota-dependent and microbiota-independent pathways. Therefore, evaluating the efficacy of PLP1 in a long-term model of established chronic fibrosis represents a critical direction for future research. These limitations pave the way for exciting future directions. Future studies should focus on elucidating the precise pharmacokinetic properties of PLP1 and identifying the specific E3 ubiquitin ligase complex involved in the PLP1-mediated CXCL1 degradation. Validating the efficacy of PLP1 in long-term models of chronic liver fibrosis is also a critical next step. Ultimately, the detailed mechanistic insights provided by our study highlight the potential of developing novel therapeutics that mimic the dual-action strategy of PLP1, simultaneously restoring gut homeostasis and neutralizing the CXCL1-driven inflammatory cascade for the comprehensive treatment of ALD. Furthermore, while our data robustly support an intracellular action of PLP1 on CXCL1 degradation, the potential for extracellular neutralization of secreted CXCL1, given the high binding affinity, remains to be fully elucidated in future studies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the potent therapeutic efficacy of PLP1 in a preclinical model of ALD and elucidates its novel dual mechanism of action. First, PLP1 acts indirectly via a microbiota-dependent pathway, where it restores gut homeostasis, reduces endotoxemia, and suppresses the hepatic TLR4 inflammatory axis. Second, PLP1 exerts a direct, microbiota-independent effect by targeting a key inflammatory cascade within the liver itself. We provide conclusive evidence, ranging from biophysical assays and specificity testing to mechanistic ubiquitination studies and genetic intervention, that PLP1 specifically binds to the chemokine CXCL1, promotes its ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation, and thereby suppresses the CXCL1-driven neutrophil infiltration and the downstream iNOS–NO pathway. This dual-pronged mechanism, which simultaneously targets both extrinsic (gut-derived) and intrinsic (hepatic) inflammatory triggers, validates PLP1 as a promising multi-target therapeutic candidate and provides a strong rationale for the further development of polysaccharide-based therapies for ALD.
