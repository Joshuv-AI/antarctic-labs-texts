Task-oriented visual SLAM: a comprehensive map classification framework for dynamic indoor robot manipulation
Authors: Xuerou Shen, Mingzhang Pan, Ke Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVisual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (vSLAM) is fundamental to enabling robotic mobile manipulation—i.e., the seamless integration of navigation, perception, and dexterous interaction with objects in unstructured environments. Yet current vSLAM research largely lacks a principled, task-oriented framework for map classification, resulting in suboptimal map representations that hinder robustness and efficiency in dynamic indoor settings. To bridge this gap, we propose a purpose-driven taxonomy of vSLAM maps specifically designed for mobile manipulation tasks. This taxonomy comprises four complementary categories: geometric 3D maps, semantic maps, object-level maps, and hybrid maps—each distinguished by its representational granularity, functional scope, and suitability for downstream manipulation primitives. We provide a systematic comparative analysis of their construction pipelines, underlying technical assumptions, and real-world deployment contexts, evaluating them rigorously across three critical dimensions: environmental adaptability, pose estimation accuracy, and real-time computational feasibility. Finally, we synthesize key limitations in existing approaches and identify concrete, high-impact directions for future work—including tight coupling between mapping semantics and manipulation affordances, and scalable learning-based map fusion.Keywords: Robot mobile manipulation, Visual SLAM, Dynamic indoor environments, Map classificationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The mobility of robots underpins their ability to perform daily tasks. Enabling autonomous execution and self-control in mobile manipulation fundamentally relies on the construction of an accurate environmental model. Different types of environmental models serve distinct roles in supporting mobile manipulation, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Selecting appropriate technologies to construct effective environmental models is therefore essential for successful robotic manipulation.

Roles of different types of maps in supporting mobile manipulation. (a) 3D maps provide an intuitive representation of the spatial structure of the environment. (b) Semantic maps associate objects with relevant semantic information. (c) Object-level maps capture detailed properties and states of individual objects. (d) These maps collectively guide robotic task execution, highlighting their distinct roles from environmental modeling to actual manipulation1–4.

Currently, visual sensors, LiDAR, ultrasound, and inertial navigation are widely employed to perceive the environment and establish models. Each traditional sensing modality, however, presents inherent limitations. For instance, ultrasound suffers from limited range and resolution, while inertial navigation accumulates drift over time. LiDAR provides precise depth information and maintains robustness under extreme lighting conditions or in texture-less environments, but it offers limited semantic understanding. In contrast, visual sensors excel in dynamic indoor environments, particularly in semantic perception and texture-rich scenarios, though they may struggle in low-texture or large-scale scenes. Consequently, multi-sensor fusion approaches, such as visual-inertial or visual-LiDAR systems, have garnered increasing attention. Among these technologies, visual SLAM (vSLAM) has emerged as a prominent research focus due to its ability to balance perceptual richness and real-time performance using low-cost cameras. SLAM technology is particularly critical for addressing challenges in dynamic indoor environments, such as fluctuating lighting, complex spatial structures with numerous moving objects, and substantial variations in object scale. By enabling real-time perception of environmental changes, efficient processing of dynamic objects, and accurate map construction, vSLAM has become a central topic in mobile robotics research.

A large body of research has explored and reviewed vSLAM methods. Early approaches primarily relied on feature-point-based techniques. Among these, Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF SLAM (ORB-SLAM) is a representative method that employs ORB features for image matching and achieves rotation invariance in dynamic environments. The ORB feature-matching results for a sample image are shown in Fig. 2. By performing multi-scale image analysis, ORB-SLAM improves feature extraction efficiency. Qing et al.6 provided a chronological survey of milestone methods and the standard vSLAM pipeline, highlighting the advantages of combining point and line features. Kazerouni et al.5 classified SLAM methods based on feature types and summarized existing vSLAM approaches. Despite their success, feature-point methods are limited by computational demands and difficulties in handling complex scenes, motivating the development of direct methods. Chen et al.7 reviewed both feature-based and direct visual odometry methods, detailing their implementation and providing a comparative analysis. However, direct methods remain sensitive to lighting variations, which limits their practical applicability.

The ORB image matching results for the sample image5.

With the rise of deep learning, researchers have integrated neural networks into vSLAM. Li et al.8 compiled state-of-the-art end-to-end deep learning SLAM approaches, combining semantic segmentation with emerging semantic SLAM techniques to achieve simultaneous ego-motion estimation and high-level scene understanding. Li et al.9 examined the application of deep learning in vSLAM from four neural network-based perspectives, categorizing existing studies according to their characteristics, strengths, and limitations, and discussing potential future directions. Arshad et al.10 proposed a taxonomy for state-of-the-art deep learning-based loop closure detection algorithms, providing detailed comparative metrics and a comprehensive review of deep learning-based methods.

Existing surveys on vSLAM have offered valuable insights from multiple perspectives. Some categorize methods according to algorithmic paradigms, such as feature-based versus direct approaches5,7 or deep learning-based techniques8–10, while others focus on sensor configurations or application domains11. Although these classifications effectively capture the evolution of SLAM algorithms, they do not directly address a question central to robotic manipulation: which type of map should a robot construct to optimally support a given task?

To address this gap, we propose a task-oriented map taxonomy that organizes existing methods according to the type of output map—3D, semantic, object-level, and hybrid—rather than the underlying algorithms. Unlike prior classifications that emphasize how maps are constructed, our framework emphasizes what maps are generated and the specific tasks they enable. This distinction is particularly important for manipulation applications, where the choice of map representation directly affects task feasibility and efficiency. We further refine our discussion to dynamic indoor environments, where moving objects and changing layouts impose additional requirements on map representation.

As summarized in Table 1, existing classifications are largely method-centric, organizing works by algorithmic implementation, including feature extraction, optimization strategies, or learning architectures. In contrast, our taxonomy is task-centric, classifying maps by their representational output (3D, semantic, object-level, hybrid) and explicitly linking each map type to the manipulation tasks it facilitates.

Overview of vSLAM

Basic principles of vSLAM

SLAM is a technique that estimates sensor motion while reconstructing the structure of unknown environments. When implemented using cameras, it is referred to as visual SLAM (vSLAM). This approach relies solely on camera data to simultaneously generate a map of the environment and perform self-localization. The accuracy and quality of the map produced by SLAM are critical, as they directly influence the performance of subsequent autonomous functions, including positioning, path planning, and obstacle avoidance.

Basic process of vSLAM

vSLAM relies on sequential image data captured by a camera to reconstruct the spatial relationships between the environment and the camera itself. By exploiting image information and camera imaging models, vSLAM incrementally generates a map of the surrounding environment while simultaneously estimating the camera’s position within it. The general processing workflow of vSLAM can be divided into three stages: front-end processing, back-end optimization, and loop-closure verification11, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The front end, often referred to as the visual odometry module, processes image data to estimate the camera’s pose at each time step, thereby forming the trajectory of the camera. The back end refines these pose estimates to minimize accumulated errors and complete the construction of the environmental map. In frameworks that incorporate loop closure detection, images captured by the camera are used to identify previously visited locations, and the resulting information is fed back to the back-end optimization for further refinement of both the trajectory and the map.

Existing theories of vSLAM

vSLAM can be broadly categorized into three main approaches. The first is the feature-based method, which completes the SLAM process by detecting and matching feature points in the images. This approach is computationally efficient and performs well in environments with rich textures. The second approach is the direct method, which bypasses feature extraction and directly utilizes pixel intensity information for SLAM. By leveraging all available image information rather than only feature points, the direct method is effective even in low-texture environments. However, it entails a higher computational burden and demands greater computing resources. The third approach is based on deep learning, employing neural networks to extract semantic information and features for pose estimation and map construction. This approach can automatically learn robust representations, exhibits strong scene understanding capabilities, and demonstrates enhanced robustness and adaptability, particularly in dynamic indoor environments.

During mobile manipulation, a robot’s localization typically relies on the construction of an environmental map. By continuously sensing the surroundings and updating the map, the robot determines exploration directions and action strategies, thereby enabling comprehensive coverage of the environment. Based on visual SLAM (vSLAM) technology, we classify the maps required to support robot mobility in dynamic indoor environments into four categories according to their representation, as shown in Fig. 4: three-dimensional (3D) maps, semantic maps, object-level maps, and hybrid maps. Together, these two dimensions define a design space for task-oriented mapping. Specific manipulation tasks may demand particular combinations of abstraction and integration; for instance, grasping requires object-level abstraction with geometric accuracy, whereas long-term operation in dynamic indoor environments benefits from hybrid maps to maintain global consistency while updating local changes. This principled, task-oriented basis distinguishes our taxonomy from existing classifications, which primarily organize methods by algorithmic implementation5,7–10 rather than by representational suitability for tasks.

Three key differences distinguish our taxonomy from prior classifications. First, existing surveys categorize SLAM methods by algorithmic approach (feature-based, direct, or deep learning), reflecting how sensor data are processed. In contrast, our taxonomy categorizes maps by representation type (3D, semantic, object-level, hybrid), reflecting the nature of the produced output. Second, existing classifications are method-centric, emphasizing algorithmic performance metrics such as accuracy and efficiency, whereas our taxonomy is task-centric, explicitly linking map types to the manipulation tasks they enable (e.g., object-level maps for grasping, hybrid maps for navigation in cluttered environments). Third, prior studies often treat map construction as the endpoint of SLAM, whereas our framework positions maps as a means to an end—a core enabler of the robot’s mobile manipulation loop. Consequently, our taxonomy is complementary to, rather than a replacement of, existing classifications.

To demonstrate the practical value of the proposed framework, we establish a mapping between typical mobile manipulation tasks and the optimal map types recommended by this taxonomy, as shown in Fig. 5. This mapping is derived from the functional requirements of each task: Obstacle-avoidance navigation prioritizes geometric accuracy (3D maps); specific object grasping requires precise object pose estimation (object-level maps); human–robot collaborative interaction benefits from semantic understanding (semantic maps); and pick-and-place in complex scenes or long-term operation in dynamic environments requires integrated representations (hybrid maps). This hierarchical task–map mapping provides intuitive validation, demonstrating how the classification framework informs map selection in real-world scenarios—a perspective largely absent in methodology-centric reviews.

Based on this task-oriented taxonomy, we subsequently examine each map type in detail, discussing construction methods, technical characteristics, and applicability to mobile manipulation.

3D maps

In dynamic indoor environments, mobile robot manipulation faces a range of complex challenges. Frequent human movement, rearrangement of facilities, multi-floor structures, and dynamic elements such as temporary obstacles impose stringent requirements on real-time performance, positional accuracy, and three-dimensional spatial perception and management. 3D maps, with their capabilities for stereoscopic modeling, real-time updating, and multi-source data fusion, are well-suited to address these challenges.

A 3D map represents geographic spatial information in three dimensions, providing a realistic depiction of terrain, buildings, and objects. In dynamic indoor settings, 3D maps capture structural details, object locations, and shapes, enabling robots to achieve comprehensive and in-depth perception of their surroundings. This enhanced spatial understanding forms a critical foundation for subsequent mobile manipulation tasks.

Technical support for 3D maps

3D maps are grounded in the principles of three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction technology. 3D reconstruction refers to the process of generating a three-dimensional model of a given scene from multiple viewpoints. This process typically involves acquiring real-world information through visual sensors and subsequently applying information processing techniques or projection models to extract the three-dimensional structure of objects. 3D reconstruction is of significant research interest within the field of computer vision and constitutes a fundamental focus in machine vision and digital image processing.

Construction methods of 3D maps

In mobile robot manipulation, robust 3D map construction is essential for accurate localization, navigation, and task execution. Existing methods, including OctoMap, NeRF, and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D GS), exhibit distinct characteristics and trade-offs in terms of representation, computational requirements, and applicability.

OctoMap12 is an octree-based probabilistic 3D mapping framework capable of representing occupied, free, and unknown space. Its low computational cost and efficient point cloud processing make it suitable for large-scale environments. However, OctoMap suffers from limited detail resolution and reduced accuracy in complex scenes, and it encounters voxel-mesh issues during ray-casting. Enhancements such as OctoMap-RT employ GPU–CPU hybrid strategies to improve voxel resolution and build speed.

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)13–23 leverage implicit neural representations to reconstruct high-fidelity photorealistic scenes. Variants such as MP-NeRF13, SNeRG14, Kilo-NeRF15, AdaNeRF16, SinNeRF17, CCNeRF18, Super-NeRF19, and Ref-NeRF20 address challenges in scene complexity, dynamic content, single-image reconstruction, surface smoothness, and cross-view consistency. Dynamic scene reconstruction is further advanced by KFD-NeRF21 and optical flow–guided methods22. Despite these improvements, NeRF-based approaches are limited by high computational complexity and long training times, constraining their applicability in real-time mobile manipulation.

3D Gaussian Splatting (3D GS)24–31 provides a practical alternative for real-time applications. By representing scenes with compact 3D Gaussian primitives and performing block-parallel rasterization, 3D GS achieves fast training and rendering while maintaining high scene fidelity. Extensions such as MPGS25, O-GS27, GaussianPro28, Mini-Splatting29, FreGS30, and GES31 further enhance rendering quality, scene detail, and computational efficiency. 3D GS has also been integrated into SLAM systems, including HumanGaussian32, GAPSLAM33, and CG-SLAM34, enabling robust real-time mapping and manipulation in dynamic indoor environments.

To improve semantic awareness, Semantic SLAM approaches such as NEDS-SLAM35, SGS-SLAM36, and DFS-SLAM37 integrate semantic features into 3D GS representations. These systems support semantic mapping, object-level localization, and manipulation, mitigating cumulative errors and enhancing robustness in dynamic scenes.

In addition to the previously discussed methods, several specialized 3D reconstruction techniques have been developed to address complex or application-specific scenarios. For example, Kim et al.44 proposed a real-time pipeline reconstruction method using RGB-D cameras, employing superpixel decomposition, sequential grouping of pipeline elements, and parametric equation modeling to enhance 3D map expressiveness for linear infrastructures. For 3D object recognition in voxel data, Karambakhsh et al.45 introduced a hybrid framework combining three compact convolutional neural networks (CNNs), SparseNet, and a feature representation technique, achieving a balance between efficiency and accuracy.

Transformer-based approaches have also been applied to 3D reconstruction46. Conventional Transformer methods typically establish 2D-to-3D relationships or perform feature extraction, but often fail to capture deep, multi-scale voxel representations. To overcome this limitation, the Transformer with Multi-Scale Dense Network (TMSDNet)47 was proposed, providing enhanced multi-scale feature representation and improving the reliability of robot mobile manipulation in complex environments.

Discussion and analysis of 3D maps

In summary, the construction of 3D maps plays a critical role in supporting autonomous navigation and environmental perception for mobile robots across diverse scenarios. OctoMap, based on an octree structure with probabilistic node representations, offers low storage requirements, efficient data updates, and multi-resolution mapping capabilities. However, in complex dynamic environments, its performance is highly dependent on sensor quality and computational resources. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) enable reconstruction of complex scenes without precise geometric models and generate photorealistic 3D renderings, facilitating high-fidelity mapping. Yet, NeRF suffers from slow rendering, high training data requirements, and limited generalization, often necessitating retraining for novel scenes. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D GS) provides fast rendering, high-quality scene representation, and easy scene manipulation, making it particularly suitable for online SLAM and real-time 3D map construction. Nonetheless, its geometric accuracy and measurability remain limited, restricting applicability in tasks that require precise spatial reasoning.

Despite its advanced rendering capabilities, direct application of 3D GS to robot manipulation must account for physical constraints. For collision avoidance and motion planning, the map representation must support rapid occupancy queries, distance computation, and differentiable collision checking. Current 3D GS methods excel in novel view synthesis but do not inherently provide signed distance fields or occupancy probabilities, which are essential for safe motion planning.

Recent efforts have begun addressing these limitations. SPLANNING48 introduces a risk-aware trajectory optimizer operating directly on Gaussian Splatting models. By deriving a rigorous upper bound on collision probabilities and employing a normalized reformulation for computational efficiency, SPLANNING enables collision-free trajectory optimization in cluttered environments. Experimental validation with a real-world robot manipulator demonstrates its effectiveness, highlighting a promising pathway for integrating 3D GS into task-oriented SLAM systems for physical execution. Nevertheless, challenges remain: computational demands are substantial, and robustness under environmental disturbances and varying lighting conditions requires further improvement.

Task suitability of 3D maps

3D maps provide metric accuracy and geometric occupancy information, making them inherently suitable for robot navigation and obstacle avoidance. Their precise spatial representation enables accurate localization and collision-free path planning in complex environments. As illustrated in Fig. 6, a mobile robot navigates toward the target while avoiding moving cylindrical obstacles in an unknown dynamic environment49. For such tasks, OctoMap-based representations achieve a favorable balance between computational efficiency and geometric fidelity. In large-scale environment reconstruction, octree-based methods ensure efficient storage, while 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D GS) enables fast rendering for real-time applications.

However, 3D maps alone are insufficient for object manipulation tasks, as they lack the object-level semantic information required for grasp pose estimation. This limitation motivates the adoption of semantic and object-level maps for manipulation scenarios that involve direct physical interaction with objects. Consequently, a task-oriented trade-off arises: for navigation on resource-constrained platforms, pure 3D maps remain the most practical option, whereas for tasks involving object interaction, 3D maps need to be augmented with semantic or object-level information, as discussed in “Semantic maps” and “Object-level maps” sections.

Semantic maps

3D maps provide a “spatial skeleton” of dynamic indoor environments through precise geometric modeling. However, they are limited to representing object positions and environmental changes and do not inherently differentiate between object types or explain the causes of scene variations. Consequently, 3D maps alone cannot support robots in understanding object categories or the logic underlying dynamic changes during navigation or manipulation.

To overcome this limitation, semantic maps have been developed. Building upon 3D maps, semantic maps assign semantic labels to objects, regions, and dynamic events within the three-dimensional space. These maps not only preserve the geometric information provided by traditional maps—such as object locations, shapes, and spatial relationships—but also incorporate rich semantic attributes, including object categories, scene functions, and other contextual properties.

By integrating geometric and semantic information, semantic maps provide robots with a more sophisticated understanding of their environment, enabling more informed decision-making, planning, and improved efficiency and accuracy in movement and task execution. As illustrated in Fig. 7, a 3D semantic map construction algorithm can generate a globally consistent 3D semantic map50 in a laboratory environment, accurately labeling the semantics of various objects and demonstrating the effectiveness of semantic augmentation in practical robotic scenarios.

Semantic mapping technology support

The construction of semantic maps relies fundamentally on semantic information, which refers to concepts that can be intuitively perceived and interpreted by humans. In the context of images, semantic information encompasses object contours, categories, and significance within a scene. Semantic vSLAM leverages this information to enhance both localization accuracy and robustness while simultaneously improving environmental perception through object-level reconstruction.

In a semantic map, each map point is associated with a specific target object, and each keyframe is indirectly linked to one or more target objects via these map points. This association establishes a structured correspondence between the image data and the objects in the environment, enabling robots to interpret scenes at a higher semantic level and to perform tasks that require object-specific understanding, such as targeted manipulation or context-aware navigation.

Construction methods of semantic maps

Sensor fusion methods integrate multi-source information through loose or tight coupling schemes, enhancing geometric accuracy while simultaneously extracting semantic features from images. This dual capability enables robots to perceive their environment more accurately during mobile manipulation. Representative examples include Nvblox51, which leverages NVIDIA’s CUDA parallel computing architecture to process RGB-D data, integrating depth and color information to build real-time 3D voxel maps while performing semantic segmentation and object recognition. VDBblox52 fuses RGB-D and LiDAR data to construct Euclidean Signed Distance Fields (ESDF) incrementally from Truncated Signed Distance Fields (TSDF), improving mesh reconstruction quality. Similarly, Voxfield53 and VINS-based methods54 integrate multi-sensor data to generate high-quality maps and robust monocular visual-inertial navigation, demonstrating strong adaptability to dynamic indoor environments. Despite these advantages, sensor fusion approaches face challenges when processing complex scenes or large-scale datasets, and their semantic understanding remains limited by the inherent capabilities of individual sensors.

Deep learning-based methods address these limitations by automatically extracting complex semantic features from large labeled datasets. Techniques combining Mask R-CNN with SLAM, such as the indoor 3D semantic vSLAM proposed by Tao et al.55, perform real-time object detection and instance segmentation, reducing the impact of dynamic objects. IEMask R-CNN56 further improves instance classification accuracy and mask quality by preserving channel information and refining features, achieving substantial benchmark gains on MS COCO2017, Cityscapes, and LVIS1.0 datasets. Other variants, such as edge-enhanced Mask R-CNN57, probabilistic Faster R-CNN58, and YOLO-based approaches (e.g., ERF-YOLO59, YOLO*C60, EdgeTrim-YOLO61), demonstrate improvements in segmentation completeness, inference speed, and contextual understanding. Deep learning-based SLAM strategies such as RGB-PD62 and MD-SLAM63 leverage predicted depth maps and multi-task networks to provide scale-consistent depth estimation and dynamic object detection, enhancing environmental perception in complex indoor scenarios.

Probabilistic graphical model-based methods are particularly useful for handling sensor noise and incomplete data by performing probabilistic reasoning to infer more accurate semantic labels. Examples include CP-SLAM64, which integrates historical associations with feature points and semantic information to transform target detection into a probabilistic graph, improving indoor localization accuracy by approximately 20% on the TUM dataset. RDP-SLAM65 mitigates dynamic object interference by removing associated feature points, reducing absolute trajectory error (APE) in ORB-SLAM3 by 80% in highly dynamic environments. GGC-SLAM66 combines predicted static probabilities with conditional random fields to refine dynamic-static distinctions, significantly improving trajectory estimation and mobile manipulation accuracy.

Their complementary strengths make these methods suitable for different semantic map construction scenarios. Recent advances have significantly enhanced semantic mapping in dynamic environments. Dense semantic methods enable per-pixel labeling for fine-grained scene understanding, while multi-feature fusion combining point and line features provides additional geometric constraints in texture-poor regions. A representative example is Dynamic Point and Line Features SLAM (DPL-SLAM)68, which integrates target detection with optical flow and epipolar constraints to identify dynamic features. Beyond SLAM, feature selection techniques further leverage semantic guidance. Mutual Information-guided Angle Reconstruction and Semantic Alignment (MIARS)69 improves feature discriminability through semantic alignment. Multi-label Feature Selection with High-level Semantic Relationships (HSR-MLFS)70 uses inter-label relationships to guide feature selection, while Hierarchical Feature Selection Driven by Inter-category Relevance and Semantic Independence (HFSIS)71 preserves semantic independence by weakening highly dependent feature relationships. Collectively, these methods demonstrate the utility of semantics in guiding geometric feature processing.

In addition to semantic-guided selection, two complementary mechanisms have emerged to handle dynamics more robustly: motion-consistency reasoning and probabilistic dynamic filtering. Motion-consistency reasoning exploits geometric constraints across frames to verify whether semantically dynamic objects are truly moving. Unlike early epipolar-based approaches, which fail to detect motion along the viewing direction, Trifocal SLAM72 introduces a three-frame coplanarity constraint, enabling reliable separation of static and dynamic points even for objects moving directly toward the camera. Integrated with YOLOv8-seg semantic segmentation, it achieves up to 98.38% improvement in RMSE of absolute trajectory error over ORB-SLAM2 on public datasets (TUM RGB-D, BONN).

Probabilistic dynamic filtering maintains uncertainty estimates for map elements, supporting informed decisions about dynamic content. Bayesian Dynamic Gaussian Splatting SLAM (BDGS-SLAM)73 constructs a Bayesian dynamic prior for Gaussians and employs multi-view probabilistic updates with exponential decay, restoring static elements erroneously removed.

Together, semantic-guided feature selection, motion-consistency reasoning, and probabilistic dynamic filtering represent a shift from reactive dynamic object removal to proactive reasoning about scene dynamics, substantially enhancing robustness in real-world mobile manipulation and navigation tasks.

Advantages and disadvantages of semantic maps

Semantic maps offer several key advantages. First, compared with purely geometric image features, semantic information exhibits greater temporal and spatial consistency and aligns more closely with human perception. Second, integrating semantic information enhances the positioning accuracy and robustness of vSLAM while enriching environmental perception. Third, semantic maps are easily updated and maintained: when objects or environmental elements change, local updates based on semantic labels can be performed, reducing computational load and time.

However, semantic maps also have limitations. One challenge lies in the optimization framework for objects. Current approaches primarily adopt either tightly coupled or loosely coupled schemes, each suited to specific scenarios. Notably, a tightly coupled scheme does not always improve localization accuracy and can sometimes degrade it. For instance, DS-SLAM67, proposed in 2018, combines semantic segmentation with motion-consistency checking. Experiments on the TUM RGB-D dataset and real-world environments showed that the loosely coupled DS-SLAM achieves an order-of-magnitude improvement in absolute trajectory accuracy compared to tightly coupled ORB-SLAM2, a feature-based monocular SLAM system. Another limitation is the reliance on high-precision mapping equipment, such as LiDAR, for constructing prior maps, which increases cost and limits scalability. Consequently, further research is needed to develop semantic mapping solutions that are more adaptable to complex mobile manipulation tasks.

Task suitability of semantic maps

Semantic maps extend geometric representations by incorporating categorical labels and scene functions, enabling robots to understand not only object locations but also their identities. This capability is particularly valuable for human–robot collaboration and task-oriented navigation.

As illustrated in Fig. 8, in a human–robot collaborative indoor scenario, robots equipped with semantic maps can safely navigate around humans by recognizing labels such as “person,” “workspace,” and “social zone”74. For such applications, Zheng et al.75 proposes a semantic map construction method that integrates semantic and geometric information through three modules: semantic information generation, geometric information generation, and semantic-geometric fusion. The semantic module removes dynamic object features, and together, these modules enhance localization accuracy in dynamic environments. For task-oriented navigation, semantic labels guide robots to functional areas—such as kitchens, doorways, or storage zones—enabling goal-directed actions. For example, a robot tasked with fetching a cup can navigate directly to the kitchen rather than searching the entire environment.

Human–robot collaborative interaction based on semantic map in indoor environments74.

Despite these advantages, semantic maps are limited for fine-grained manipulation tasks that require instance-level precision. While they identify object categories (e.g., “cup”), they do not distinguish individual instances or provide the precise 6-DoF poses required for grasp planning. Real-time deployment on low-power devices is also challenging due to the computational demands of semantic segmentation networks.

Thus, a clear guideline emerges: for tasks requiring high-level scene understanding—such as human–robot collaboration, service robotics, or assistive applications—semantic maps are essential. For resource-constrained platforms, lightweight semantic networks76–78 provide a practical trade-off between semantic richness and computational efficiency. However, for precise object manipulation, semantic maps should be augmented with object-level information, as discussed in “Object-level maps” section.

Object-level maps

Object-level maps represent a refinement of semantic maps, shifting from category-level recognition to instance-level perception. While semantic maps provide information on object categories and regional functions, object-level maps assign unique identifiers, attribute databases, and real-time status information to individual objects. This enables robots to understand detailed scene semantics, respond effectively to frequent changes, and meet complex demands in dynamic indoor environments, thereby improving the efficiency and accuracy of mobile manipulation.

As high-granularity representations, object-level maps go beyond spatial regions by specifying detailed object properties, including location, category, and attributes such as size, color, and orientation. For instance, an indoor map may capture the precise placement and characteristics of items on a table. Constructing these maps relies on advanced sensors and target recognition algorithms, which allow mobile robots to perceive their surroundings accurately and execute task-oriented manipulations more effectively.

Object-level map technology support

Object-level maps encode detailed information on object attributes and inter-object relationships, enabling robots to accurately identify and manipulate specific objects, thereby enhancing the efficiency and precision of tasks such as object retrieval. These maps leverage data associations across multiple frames to integrate detection results and initialize object representations. In the back-end optimization process, both camera poses and object poses are refined, while object model parameters are adjusted. Through this procedure, the system constructs a comprehensive object-level map, providing semantic-rich perception of the environment for task-oriented mobile manipulation.

Methods for constructing object-level maps

Object-level SLAM, which integrates object detection and instance segmentation, has emerged as a prominent approach in object-level mapping research. This method leverages multi-view geometric constraints and detection bounding boxes to reconstruct object models in mobile manipulation scenarios. Several systems and improvements have been proposed. MaskFusion2, for instance, is a real-time, object-aware, semantic RGB-D SLAM system that surpasses traditional static, geometry-only mapping approaches. Unlike earlier recognition-based SLAM systems, MaskFusion does not require prior object models and utilizes instance-level semantic segmentation to incorporate semantic labels into object maps. Its convolutional mask module operates asynchronously at 5 Hz, imposing significant computational demands and necessitating a dedicated GPU for real-time performance (Fig. 9).

ASF-YOLO79 employs attentional scale sequence fusion within a YOLO framework, integrating the Scale Sequence Feature Fusion (SSFF) and Triple Feature Encoder (TFE) modules to enhance multi-scale feature extraction. On the 2018 Data Science Bowl dataset, it achieves a box mAP of 0.91, mask mAP of 0.887, and an inference speed of 47.3 FPS. CoT blocks46 further exploit static and dynamic contextual information, enhancing feature representation. OA-SLAM80 demonstrates improved SLAM repositioning in unknown environments through advanced object landmarks, while Song et al.81 utilize object-level landmarks and prior size constraints to reduce scale drift and improve monocular localization accuracy and robustness.

Another common approach combines deep learning with point cloud processing. This strategy uses advanced networks for semantic and instance segmentation on 3D point clouds, often supplemented with IMU data to enhance pose estimation and adapt to dynamic environments. For example, PointSeg82 performs instance segmentation on images via YOLACT + + 83 and associates the results with point clouds, achieving instance-level segmentation with ~ 75 ms for images and ~ 178 ms per point cloud instance on standard hardware. LO-Net84 applies a layer-wise feature optimization network for accurate point cloud classification. CrossFusion Net85 fuses lidar and RGB features hierarchically to improve environmental perception. Other methods, such as automated BIM generation86, HPCT87, and PointFilterNet (PFN)88, enhance semantic segmentation, feature extraction, and noise reduction. Chen et al.89 address adversarial vulnerabilities in point cloud processing, improving safety and reliability in robotic manipulation.

For resource-constrained deployment, lightweight networks such as MobileNet variants have been explored. Thin MobileNet90 reduces model size to 9.9 MB using separable convolutions, Slim MobileNet91 achieves 7.3 MB while improving accuracy, and MobileNet-SA92 leverages self-attention mechanisms to reach 93.5% accuracy on the SketchyCOCO dataset. NodeSLAM93 introduces multi-class object descriptors and a differentiable probabilistic rendering engine for robust 3D reconstruction. DynaQuadric94 proposes tightly coupled optimization for quadric surface representation combined with multi-object tracking (MOT), enabling object perception in dynamic environments. KN-SLAM95 integrates keypoint localization with neural implicit representations, achieving high-quality reconstruction while maintaining precise localization through global and local feature fusion.

Advantages and disadvantages of object-level maps

In dynamic indoor environments, object-level maps offer several advantages for mobile manipulation. First, incorporating semantic attributes from object detection into vSLAM improves localization accuracy by distinguishing static from dynamic objects, effectively mitigating interference from moving elements. For instance, OA-SLAM80 integrates feature descriptors and object reprojection observations, leveraging relative object position constraints to achieve robust localization under dramatic perspective changes. Similarly, object-level and deep network descriptors enhance localization robustness in textureless or challenging scenarios. To improve environmental perception, some studies construct quadratic surface maps of objects94, simultaneously estimating size, orientation, and position. Others incorporate dynamic object information into SLAM, as exemplified by OTE-SLAM98, which tracks both camera and object motion, providing a more reliable basis for decision-making in mobile manipulation.

Despite these advances, object-level maps face significant practical challenges. First, their computational demands are high: introducing semantic attributes, constructing object databases, and performing joint optimization require high-performance hardware, limiting deployment on resource-constrained platforms. Second, object detection algorithms remain vulnerable to complex scenarios; abrupt lighting changes or severe occlusions can lead to recognition errors, adversely affecting localization. Third, map construction is costly, as data collection and processing are time-intensive, posing obstacles for large-scale applications. Existing methods often lack versatility, being optimized for specific scenarios and requiring significant adaptation for new environments.

To address these limitations, cross-scenario adaptation strategies have been explored. Transfer learning can leverage models trained in one environment to other scenarios, reducing repetitive training. For example, Henein et al.99 incorporated meta-structural information related to geometric primitives into SLAM, enabling accurate online estimation of environmental structure and robot pose. Zhuang et al.100 evaluated over 20 transfer learning models, demonstrating the importance of model selection for practical deployment. Local instance-based transfer learning for reinforcement learning101 uses Q-values of local trajectories to evaluate similarity, significantly improving transfer efficiency and enabling rapid adaptation in dynamic indoor environments. Domain-adaptive strategies bridge distribution gaps between datasets. Huang et al.102 proposed a semi-supervised domain adaptation (SSDA) method—bidirectional sample-class alignment (BSCA)—for remote sensing cross-domain scene classification, achieving compact feature representation and low-entropy classification boundaries, thereby providing reliable environmental information for mobile manipulation.

Task suitability of object-level maps

Object-level maps assign unique identities, dynamic attributes, and precise geometric models to individual objects. This fine-grained representation makes them particularly suited for robotic tasks that require precise interaction with specific objects, such as grasping and persistent target tracking in dynamic environments. As illustrated in Fig. 10, object-level maps provide not only spatial locations but also accurate poses, dimensions, and contour profiles of objects103. Such high-precision perceptual information is essential for grasp planning and force-closure computation. Unlike conventional 3D metric maps, which represent the environment as undifferentiated geometric primitives, object-level maps enable robots to discriminate individual entities, maintain long-term object tracking, and devise manipulation strategies tailored to the physical characteristics of each object. In dynamic scenes, they enhance SLAM robustness by decoupling static background elements from moving objects. For example, OA-SLAM80 achieves reliable localization under significant perspective variations by fusing object feature descriptors with reprojection constraints.

Robotic grasping based on object-level map information103.

Despite their advantages for fine manipulation, object-level maps face limitations in large-scale navigation or environments with numerous unseen objects. Their construction and maintenance impose substantial computational overhead, including high GPU usage and memory consumption, which reduces efficiency in large-area navigation tasks where lightweight geometric maps suffice. Moreover, most object-level mapping frameworks rely on pre-trained detection models that may fail when encountering novel object categories, limiting generalization.

Consequently, a task-oriented selection principle is evident. Object-level maps are recommended for manipulation tasks requiring precise interaction with specific targets, such as grasping, assembly, or object fetching. Deployment on resource-limited platforms necessitates hardware acceleration or model compression, while transfer learning strategies99–102 can improve cross-scenario generalization. Conversely, standard 3D metric maps remain more cost-effective for large-scale navigation or tasks that do not require object-level discrimination.

Consider a robot tasked with retrieving a designated cup from a cluttered table. Using a standard 3D map, the robot can navigate to the table but cannot identify the target cup among surrounding objects, resulting in grasp failure. In contrast, an object-level map provides the precise pose, size, and shape of the cup, enabling correct object recognition, optimized grasp planning, and task completion. This example underscores the indispensability of object-level maps for interactive manipulation, while highlighting the need to balance semantic richness with computational resources based on task requirements.

Hybrid maps

The evolution from 3D maps to semantic maps and finally to object-level maps represents a progressive refinement from geometric representation to semantic understanding to instance-level perception. To fully exploit the strengths of each map type, hybrid maps have been proposed, enabling multi-level integration of spatial, semantic, and object-specific information. A hybrid map combines diverse representation methods or data modalities to provide a more comprehensive and accurate depiction of the environment. By integrating geometric elements (e.g., points, lines, voxels), semantic labels and topological relationships, and multi-modal sensor data (e.g., LiDAR, RGB-D), hybrid maps overcome the limitations of individual map types. This multi-layered representation enhances environmental understanding and provides robust support for mobile manipulation in dynamic indoor scenarios.

Classification of hybrid maps

Hybrid maps integrate multiple map representation methods or data modalities to achieve a more comprehensive and accurate depiction of environmental information. As illustrated in Fig. 11, in dynamic indoor environments, hybrid maps can be categorized into hierarchical maps and multimodal maps. Hierarchical maps combine local metric details with global topological structures. At the local level, high-precision laser-scanned point clouds capture fine-grained spatial information within confined areas, supporting accurate positioning and motion planning. The global topology abstracts indoor spaces as nodes and edges, representing inter-area connectivity and facilitating efficient global path planning. This hierarchical integration allows robots to execute detailed tasks while maintaining an understanding of the overall layout.

Classification map of the hybrid map.

Multimodal maps, on the other hand, fuse visual data with LiDAR information104. Vision provides rich texture, color, and semantic cues for object recognition and scene interpretation, whereas LiDAR delivers precise 3D geometry and distance measurements. By combining these modalities, multimodal maps simultaneously capture the environment’s geometric structure and semantic content, furnishing a robust foundation for mobile manipulation and enabling robots to perceive and adapt effectively to dynamic indoor environments.

Application and improvement of hybrid maps

Hybrid maps are typically constructed using point and planar features. They first extract feature points and planes, associate data through decision trees, and apply smoothing mapping tools to construct factor graphs for joint optimization of robot poses and landmarks, enabling real-time updates. Such maps form the basis of indoor navigation systems, providing critical support for mobile manipulation tasks.

Early hybrid mapping efforts include the work of Tomatis et al.105, which integrated metric and topological paradigms using a 360° laser scanner, achieving multi-modal topological localization without requiring global metric consistency. Subsequent developments employed Rao-Blackwellized particle filters for real-time data association, mapping, and spatial reasoning with hybrid map representations106. Konolige et al.107 combined topological maps with local occupancy grids for large-scale navigation, while Choi et al.108 extended RBPF-based hybrid SLAM to the Velodyne 3D HDL-64 scanner, providing rich spatial information. OGI-SLAM2109 and Kimera2110,111 further improved localization accuracy through optimized keyframe selection, multiple input modes, and enhanced pose map backends.

Recent advances in hierarchical maps focus on increasing accuracy and efficiency. Kong et al.112 proposed a points-of-interest-driven method to extract local topological features, while GTCAlign112 encodes node embeddings for graph alignment guided by global topology. H-SLAM113 uses descriptor sharing to generate a single inverse depth representation, and UL-SLAM114 employs multi-scale line matching to reduce motion errors.

Multimodal hybrid maps have also advanced significantly. Dynam-SLAM115 integrates dynamic and static features with IMU measurements for nonlinear optimization. MSSKF116 and FMF117 improve multi-modal fusion for object detection and small-target perception. Filter Fusion118 couples camera and LiDAR features, while TextSLAM119 incorporates text objects as semantic priors for mixed-reality navigation. ESLAM120 uses signed distance field representations with NeRF integration to enhance dense SLAM efficiency, and AVLMaps121 leverage audio-visual-language cues for multi-level indexing. HERO-SLAM122 combines neural implicit fields with feature-metric optimization, and HVL-SLAM123 integrates visual-LiDAR data for robust tracking. Ji et al.124 introduced object-level SLAM using spatial layout and semantic consistency for loop closure and manipulation accuracy. Local control-based multisensor fusion125 mitigates cumulative trajectory errors from asynchronous sensor measurements.

A critical aspect of hybrid mapping in dynamic environments is the joint utilization of semantic and structural features to mitigate dynamic interference. Rather than treating semantics as an independent layer, recent approaches127–129 integrate semantic understanding directly into geometric mapping pipelines. This integration enables the system to more reliably distinguish between static and dynamic elements. For instance, semantic labels can identify potentially moving objects (e.g., humans, vehicles), while structural features—such as points, lines, and planes—provide geometric consistency checks. When a semantic label indicates an object is dynamic but geometric features remain consistent, the system can probabilistically decide whether to include or exclude that element from the map.

This synergy is particularly effective in multimodal mapping, where visual data is combined with other sensor modalities such as LiDAR or IMU. Semantic segmentation can be applied across modalities, enabling cross-modal validation of object dynamics. Structural features extracted from different sensors provide complementary information: visual data contributes rich texture and color, while LiDAR supplies precise depth and geometric structure. By fusing these modalities under semantic guidance, hybrid maps achieve robust performance even under challenging conditions, including lighting variations, occlusions, or rapid motion. A notable example is SLAM2130, a semantic RGB-D SLAM system that integrates point, line, and planar features with deep learning to detect both known and unknown dynamic objects, demonstrating enhanced robustness in dynamic environments.

Thus, combining semantic understanding with multimodal structural features provides a principled approach to mitigating dynamic interference. Leveraging the strengths of each modality and using semantics to guide feature selection and fusion allows hybrid maps to maintain consistency and accuracy where traditional methods often fail.

Hybrid maps have been extensively applied in robotic localization and mapping. They integrate metric and topological paradigms by connecting local metric maps through global topological structures to form compact environment representations. Using 360° laser scanners and visual features, hybrid maps can handle environmental loops and support precise positioning in unknown spaces. Their advantages are particularly evident in complex dynamic environments. For example, in multi-floor warehouse navigation, a single metric map is insufficient due to the large environment, complex cargo layouts, and frequent movement of personnel and equipment. Hybrid maps overcome these limitations by combining local metric maps, which capture precise dimensions and locations of shelves and aisles, with global topological maps, which encode floor-to-floor connections such as staircases and elevators. This combination enables robots to navigate accurately through narrow aisles, avoid obstacles, and plan optimal paths across floors, thereby supporting efficient transportation and sorting operations.

Advantages and disadvantages of hybrid maps

Hybrid maps, constructed from point and plane features, leverage the complementary advantages of each representation to enhance navigation practicality. They integrate diverse types of information, combining geometric features for intuitive path planning with connectivity features that capture the overall structural relationships of the environment. This multi-level representation also makes hybrid maps well-suited for complex manipulation tasks requiring both geometric precision and semantic understanding.

Despite their strengths, hybrid maps face inherent limitations. The increasing algorithmic complexity can hinder stable operation across large and diverse environments, complicating map construction. Additionally, current hierarchical SLAM approaches lack standardized map model conversion methods, creating challenges in data processing and interoperability. Future research should focus on reducing algorithmic complexity, unifying model transformation protocols, and improving the accuracy and efficiency of data association.

Task suitability of hybrid maps

Hybrid maps integrate multiple representation types within a unified framework, combining geometric, semantic, topological, and multimodal information. This integration makes them particularly suitable for complex manipulation tasks that require both geometric precision and semantic understanding.

As illustrated in Fig. 12, which depicts top-down pick-and-place manipulation using hybrid maps, these maps merge fine-grained object geometry from object-level representations with scene semantics to identify safe manipulation paths and avoid collisions131. For long-term operation in dynamic environments, hybrid maps support efficient static-dynamic distinction and selective map updating, maintaining global consistency while updating only regions affected by changes. This capability is essential for robots operating over extended periods in environments with moving people or rearranged furniture. In multi-floor navigation scenarios, such as warehouse operations, hybrid maps leverage local metric maps for precise positioning on each floor while preserving global topological consistency for cross-floor path planning.

Top-down pick-and-place manipulation using hybrid maps. Object-level geometry is fused with scene semantics to enable collision-free grasping and accurate placement in cluttered environments131.

Despite these advantages, hybrid maps are less suitable for deployment on highly resource-constrained platforms or latency-sensitive applications. They require multiple sensors (cameras, LiDAR, IMU) and GPU acceleration, making deployment on low-power platforms challenging. Real-time multimodal data synchronization and fusion remain computationally intensive, and current hierarchical SLAM approaches still lack standardized map model conversion methods, complicating data processing.

Accordingly, hybrid maps are recommended for scenarios that demand both high geometric accuracy and rich semantic understanding, such as cluttered pick-and-place tasks or long-term autonomous operation in dynamic indoor environments. For simpler applications, such as basic navigation or coarse grasping, a single map type—either 3D or object-level—may suffice and be more computationally efficient.

In conclusion, 3D maps have been extensively studied and provide the foundation for environmental representation. Recent research has increasingly focused on semantic and object-level maps to enable higher-level scene understanding and task-oriented manipulation. Hybrid maps, integrating multiple representation modalities, represent an emerging direction with significant potential for future exploration.

No single map type is optimal across all dimensions. The choice should be guided by task requirements and available hardware. For resource-constrained platforms, such as UAVs or low-cost mobile robots, real-time operation often outweighs representational richness. Table 8 illustrates that 3D maps can operate efficiently on CPU-only systems, supporting fast navigation. In contrast, richer representations—semantic, object-level, or hybrid maps—enhance manipulation performance (e.g., grasping, pick-and-place) but typically require GPU acceleration. Consequently, 3D maps are recommended when strict real-time performance is critical, while richer maps should be adopted only when task demands justify the additional computational resources.

Future prospects

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, visual segmentation and vision-language models (VLMs) have opened new opportunities for robotic mapping in dynamic indoor environments. Visual segmentation models can accurately distinguish between dynamic and static objects, while VLMs enable robots to interpret human commands and environmental descriptions, optimizing maps based on semantic information. However, integrating these models into robot mapping pipelines remains a challenge, particularly due to the complexity of multimodal fusion132. Multimodal data fusion algorithms have enormous potential, but several issues must be addressed. We outline five key research directions for future exploration:

Integration of 3D maps with vision-language models

Combining visual and linguistic information allows robots to understand human intentions and execute autonomous tasks. For example, Prompt-RSVQA133 balances visual and linguistic modalities by converting visual features into text, providing language-contextualized understanding. In the 3D domain, 3D visual encoders can extract Mixture of Features (MoF) from map data and convert them into coordinate-annotated textual descriptions. Methods such as MMVP and MoF134 integrate self-supervised visual features with multimodal large language models (MLLMs) for enhanced localization. Generative vision-language models135 offer cross-lingual, lightweight, and data-efficient representations, improving 3D map encoding while reducing computational costs. Bridging 3D generative scene (GS) rendering with physical executability is also critical. Pipelines like Splat-Nav136 enable real-time navigation in dynamic environments by combining semantic GSplat scenes with online replanning (2 Hz) and pose estimation (~ 25 Hz), outperforming NeRF-based methods. Future research should focus on hybrid representations supporting both high-fidelity visualization and physically executable planning, including Gaussian-to-mesh pipelines137 and 3D GS-mesh hybrid frameworks exemplified by EnliveningGS138.

Optimization of semantic maps for computational efficiency

Semantic maps enhance localization accuracy but introduce computational overhead. Future research should balance these trade-offs by developing lightweight architectures, edge-computing optimizations, and model compression techniques. MobileNet-based networks76, efficient transformers77, and edge-deployed frameworks78 allow real-time semantic perception on resource-constrained platforms. Techniques such as quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation reduce memory footprint and inference time. For instance, vision-based obstacle avoidance strategies139 integrate optical flow with ORB-SLAM338 to enable CPU-only deployment while maintaining reliable performance. Furthermore, methods like Dual Semantic Correlation Alignment (DSCA)140 demonstrate effective utilization of semantic information to improve localization across domains.

Enhancing object-level maps with event cameras

Event cameras141 emulate biological retina characteristics, providing high dynamic range, low latency, high temporal resolution, and low power consumption. They enable robust perception in complex, dynamic indoor environments. Key challenges include quantifying trade-offs (e.g., latency vs. power, accuracy vs. sensitivity) and developing native event-driven, bio-inspired systems142. Sundar et al.143 proposed a generalized event camera that preserves scene intensity in a bandwidth-efficient manner, addressing dynamic perception bottlenecks in object-level mapping.

Open-vocabulary and zero-shot understanding

Traditional semantic SLAM systems are restricted to a closed set of object categories, limiting adaptability to novel objects. Integrating VLMs144–147 and foundation models148 enables zero-shot recognition and flexible task specification (e.g., “find the small, red container”), supporting dynamic object queries in real time. Research priorities include lightweight open-vocabulary segmentation models for embedded deployment, zero-shot object pose estimation, and natural language-based retrieval from object-level maps.

Real-time multimodal synchronization for hybrid maps

Hybrid maps rely on synchronized multimodal data (e.g., vision, LiDAR, IMU) for accurate representation. Hard real-time synchronization schemes149 are essential for stable data collection under high computational load. Additionally, multimodal knowledge representation frameworks150 facilitate the integration of heterogeneous data, enabling efficient reasoning and task execution in dynamic environments.

Addressing these challenges will drive the development of multimodal fusion technologies, providing robust support for map construction and task execution in dynamic indoor environments.

Conclusion

In dynamic indoor environments, selecting an appropriate map type is a prerequisite for robots to perform tasks stably and efficiently. Motivated by this need, this paper presents a comprehensive study of map types for mobile manipulation in such settings. Focusing on robot mobility, four primary map types are examined—3D maps, semantic maps, object-level maps, and hybrid maps—considering their structure, capabilities, and limitations. This study is operationalized through a task-to-map selection guide, which links each map type to specific manipulation tasks and corresponding evaluation metrics. This framework shifts the evaluation paradigm from purely geometric accuracy to task-level performance, aligning directly with the needs of task-oriented SLAM. The key findings are summarized as follows:

3D maps

3D maps are the most widely used, offering high spatial accuracy, efficient rendering, compact storage, and strong perceptual capabilities. They are particularly suitable for navigation-focused tasks due to their computational efficiency. However, challenges remain in real-time performance, sensor dependency, and computational demands for large-scale environments.

Semantic maps

Semantic maps integrate multiple sensors and probabilistic models to provide robots with enhanced understanding of object categories, scene functions, and environmental attributes. They exhibit strong semantic understanding and noise resilience, enabling improved decision-making in complex scenarios. Nevertheless, these maps rely heavily on large amounts of labeled data, high-quality sensors, and substantial computing resources, which limits their generalizability to novel environments.

Object-level maps

Object-level maps generate detailed, semantically annotated object models in real time, providing essential information such as object pose, size, and shape for manipulation tasks. While they can be optimized for embedded platforms, they depend on GPU acceleration, extensive training data, and precise sensor calibration. Consequently, their real-time performance and adaptability in dynamic scenarios are still limited.

Hybrid maps

Hybrid maps combine the advantages of 3D, semantic, and object-level maps, offering a multi-level, multimodal representation that is highly suitable for complex dynamic environments. They improve accuracy, adaptability, and flexibility in mobile manipulation, enabling both geometric precision and semantic reasoning. However, challenges remain in synchronizing real-time multimodal data and establishing unified standards, limiting widespread deployment in real-world applications.

Overall, research on mapping for task execution in dynamic indoor environments plays a pivotal role in advancing mobile manipulation capabilities. While significant progress has been made, widespread deployment of robots in such environments remains a challenge, and numerous technical and practical issues require further investigation.
