Biogenic Silver Nanoparticles Synthesised Using Staphylococcus saprophyticus Cell‐Free Supernatant Exhibit Potent Antimicrobial Activities via Oxidative Stress
Authors: Nidhi Chandrakar, Dugeshwar Karley, Sudhir K Shukla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this study, we report the eco‐friendly synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) using cell‐free supernatant of Staphylococcus saprophyticus as a biological reducing and stabilising agent. Nanoparticle formation was confirmed by visible colour change and UV–visible spectroscopy (λ max at 418 nm). Structural characterisation by SEM, XRD and FT‐IR indicated predominantly spherical, crystalline, silver‐containing nanoparticles associated with extracellular biomolecules that may contribute to stabilisation; XRD analysis indicated a surface phase dominated by silver halides (AgBr/AgCl) rather than confirming a purely metallic Ag0 lattice. The biosynthesised AgNPs exhibited strong antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa , with minimum inhibitory concentrations of 3 and 0.5 μg/mL, respectively. Significant antifungal activity was also observed against Candida albicans , with marked growth suppression at 100 μg/mL. The AgNPs effectively reduced biofilm formation, extracellular matrix production and virulence traits. DCFH‐DA fluorescence and MTT assays showed increased reactive oxygen species generation and reduced cellular metabolic activity in treated microbes, suggesting oxidative stress‐associated antimicrobial effects. Cytotoxicity testing indicated low toxicity towards HeLa cells at lower concentrations, with dose‐dependent effects observed only at higher exposure levels. Overall, results demonstrate that biogenic AgNPs possess antibacterial, antifungal and antibiofilm properties, highlighting their potential as sustainable and eco‐friendly antimicrobial candidates.Keywords: antibacterial activity, biofilm, reactive oxygen species, silver nanoparticles, Staphylococcus saprophyticus

Introduction

The rapid rise in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses one of the most significant threats to global health, food security and development [1]. The inefficacy of conventional antibiotics against resistant pathogens has prompted an urgent need for alternative antimicrobial strategies. In this context, nanotechnology has emerged as a promising field, with nanoparticles offering novel solutions to combat microbial infections [2]. Among these, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have garnered substantial attention due to their potent antimicrobial properties. Biogenic synthesis of AgNPs (bAgNPs), utilising biological entities such as bacteria, fungi and plants, presents an eco‐friendly and sustainable approach, circumventing the environmental and health hazards associated with chemical and physical synthesis methods [3]. This research delves into the biogenic synthesis using Staphylococcus saprophyticus , exploring their broad‐spectrum antibiofilm and antimicrobial activities.

Nanoparticles, due to their unique physicochemical properties that have revolutionised various fields, including medicine, electronics and environmental science [4]. Silver nanoparticles, in particular, are renowned for their antibacterial, antifungal and antiviral activities, attributed to their high surface area‐to‐volume ratio, which enhances their interaction with microbial cells [5]. Traditional methods of synthesising AgNPs involve toxic chemicals and high‐energy processes, raising concerns over environmental pollution and human health risks [6]. Consequently, biogenic synthesis has emerged as a green alternative, utilising microorganisms that can reduce silver ions to nanoparticles through metabolic processes. Staphylococcus saprophyticus , a Gram‐positive bacterium that is well‐known for its role in urinary tract infections, has also been identified as a potential biological factory for nanoparticle synthesis [7]. This bacterium produces bioactive compounds that not only facilitate the reduction of silver ions but may endow the nanoparticles with enhanced antimicrobial properties. Recent years have seen a surge in research focused on biogenic synthesis [8]. Studies have demonstrated the efficacy of AgNPs synthesised using various microorganisms in inhibiting the growth of pathogenic bacteria and fungi [9]. The antimicrobial mechanisms of AgNPs include disruption of microbial cell membranes, generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and interference with cellular processes such as DNA replication [10]. Moreover, AgNPs have been shown to possess antibiofilm activity, disrupting the extracellular polymeric substance matrix that protects biofilm‐associated cells from antibiotics.

Despite these advances, the specific bioactive compounds produced by microorganisms like S. saprophyticus and their roles in enhancing the antimicrobial activity of biogenic AgNPs remain underexplored [11]. Several gaps persist in the current research landscape. One of the primary challenges is the limited understanding of the precise mechanisms of action and the identity of bioactive metabolites involved in nanoparticle synthesis [12]. Existing research often assumes that biogenic nanoparticles are inherently safer than their chemically synthesised counterparts, an assumption that requires rigorous validation through systematic investigations [13].

This study aims to fill these gaps by isolating and identifying the bioactive compounds from S. saprophyticus and evaluating their contributions to the antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities of the synthesised AgNPs [14]. The primary purpose of this study is to elucidate the role of these compounds in enhancing the AgNPs efficacy. By addressing this question, the research aims to advance the understanding of biogenic nanoparticle synthesis and its potential applications in combating AMR [15]. This study employs a multidisciplinary approach, integrating microbiological, biochemical and nanotechnological techniques. The methodologies include the isolation and characterisation of bioactive compounds from Staphylococcus saprophyticus , followed by the synthesis and characterisation of AgNPs. The antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities of the synthesised AgNPs are assessed using a variety of bacterial and fungal pathogens, providing a comprehensive evaluation of their broad‐spectrum efficacy [16]. Advanced analytical techniques, such as Gas Chromatography—mass spectrometry (GC–MS) and electron microscopy, are utilised to identify the bioactive compounds and characterise the nanoparticles [17].

Material and Methods

Study Site and Sample Process

The hospital is located in Raipur, Chhattisgarh, India from where samples were collected. The latitude is 21.405842 and longitude is 81.926201. The geographic coordinates are 21°24′21.0312″ N and 81°55′34.3236″ E. The temperature ranged from 28°C to 43°C. The elevation was around 100 m, and samples were gathered from the hospital grounds. The bacterial strain was obtained using the conventional plate dilution technique on Luria Bertani agar and incubated for 24 h at 37°C. The growing bacteria, identified as Staphylococcus saprophyticus , were confirmed via 16S rRNA gene sequencing and BLAST comparison. The acquired gene sequence was subsequently submitted to the NCBI gene bank and accession number has been granted (GenBank# PV919037). The bacteria pure culture was stored at 4°C for further investigation.

Cell Free Supernatants (CFS) Preparation From Staphylococcus saprophyticus

The bacterial suspension was prepared using the Luria Bertani broth at 37°C for 24 h, the pure culture in the inoculating loop full of S. saprophyticus and incubated in rotary shaker at room temperature for 24–48 h [18]. and centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 rpm (eppendorf Centrifuge 5810 R). Supernatants were collected by passing through a 0.22 μm sterile syringe filter (AANIJ All pure syringe filter). The bacterial supernatant was stored at 4°C and used for nanoparticle synthesis.

Biogenic Silver Nanoparticle (bAgNPs) Synthesis and Characterisation

The synthesis of biogenic silver‐based nanoparticles was carried out using the method described by Karley et al. [19], with minor modifications. Briefly, 10 mL of filtered S. saprophyticus cell‐free supernatant was added to 100 mL of freshly prepared 1 mM AgNO3 solution under sterile conditions, and the pH was adjusted to approximately 10. The reaction mixture was incubated at 60°C for 5 days in the dark by covering the flask with aluminium foil to prevent photo‐oxidation. A visible colour change from pale yellow to dark brown/black was taken as the preliminary indication of nanoparticle formation. The suspension was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to pellet the nanoparticles. The pellet was washed three times with sterile distilled water by repeated dispersion and centrifugation to remove residual impurities. The purified pellet was dried, ground into powder and stored for further characterisation and biological assays.

Characterisation Studies on AgNPs

UV–Vis spectroscopy is the widely used technique to characterise and confirm the presence of reduced Ag ion as AgNPs and measured for absorbance ranging from 250 to 700 nm using the spectrophotometer. The size and shape of AgNPs were obtained using the scanning electron microscope (SEM), and the crystalline structure of the AgNPs was obtained using the XRD. The functional group present in the bacteria mediated AgNPs was analysed with Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR).

Agar Well Diffusion Assay

S. aureus (MTCC 10787) and P. aeruginosa (MTCC 12011) were subjected to testing the inhibitory effect of AgNPs. Bacteria were cultured in LB broth at 37°C for 20–24 h. Bacterial suspension was initially diluted to 0.5 O.D. (~108 CFU/mL) and performed spread plate method onto LB agar. Then, the inoculated LB agar was pierced with the sterile 8 mm diameter cork borer to create wells. The volume of 100 μL of AgNPs was loaded into wells and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. After incubation, the inhibition zone will be measured in each well. To validate the result, LB broth (pH 6.0) was conducted as a negative control.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) Determination

The MIC of synthesised silver nanoparticles (bAgNPs) against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa was measured by broth dilution in a 96‐well microtiter plate. bAgNPs were used at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 10 μg/mL. The AgNPs were added to wells with LB broth and bacterial inoculum in a volume ratio that kept the final volume at 200 μL per well. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Following incubation, the MIC was measured by visually checking each well for turbidity, which indicates bacterial growth. The lowest concentration of AgNPs that resulted in no apparent growth (clean wells) was recorded as the MIC of bAgNPs for that bacterium. The assay was carried out in triplicates and statistical computations were performed. Two wells, one with culture medium and bacteria and one with nanoparticle and no bacteria, were utilised as control.

The antifungal activity of bacteria‐mediated AgNPs was assayed by a well diffusion method using fungal pathogens, C. albicans . Antibiotic Susceptibility Test

Antibiotic susceptibility pattern of isolates was performed using the agar well diffusion method on LB Agar (Hi‐media) according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guideline for the following antibiotics: Erythromycin (20 μg), Ampicillin (100 μg), Gentamycin (50 μg), Kanamycin (50 μg), Cefotaxime (30 μg), Azithromycin (15 μg). The MIC of antibiotic was determined using the broth microdilution method as described above. Bacterial isolates resistant to three or more different antibiotic classes were identified as. S. aureus (MTCC 10787) and P. aeruginosa (MTCC 12011) were used as control strains.

Effect on Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

Intracellular ROS was detected in P. aeruginosa, S. aureus and C. albicans cells was measured using 2′,7′‐Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFHDA) (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), after 4 h bAgNP exposure [20]. For this, cells were treated with inhibitory and sub‐inhibitory doses (0.5 MIC and 0.25 MIC) in triplicates. After incubation, cells were washed and treated with 20 μM DCFH‐DA at 37°C in the dark for 30 min. The DCF fluorescence was measured using a multimode reader (Tecan, USA) with 485 nm excitation and 538 nm emission parameters.

Mitochondrial Enzyme Activity (MTT Assay)

The MTT assay was used to assess the number of cells of P. aeruginosa , S. aureus and C. albicans . The biofilm was developed in a plate by inoculating 200 μL of test culture in the LB broth and incubated for 24 h for the bacterial growth. After the given incubation, the plate was washed with sterile phosphate buffer saline (PBS) to remove debris, then the different concentrations of bAgNPs [21] were added to the plate and kept for 3 h in the dark. After the incubation period, 50 μ of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added and incubated again for 5 h at 37°C. Then wells were washed with the PBS to remove all the trace and then added 1 mL of DMSO to solubilise the MTT formazan and measured at 540 nm.

Cytotoxicity Test Using the HeLa Cell Line

The MTT‐based cytotoxicity assay for the sample was performed in Hela, cervical cancer cell line. Ten thousand (10 × 103) cells/well were seeded in a 96‐well plate and were incubated for 48 h, before drug exposures. 48 h post drug treatment, 5 mg/mL MTT reagent was added for 4 h at 37°C. The MTT solution was removed and 100 μL of isopropanol with 1 N HCL, was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals by gentle shaking for 20 min and measured at 570 nm. Biosynthesis of AgNPs Using S. saprophyticus Culture and Its Characterisation Studies

A shift in the colour of the reaction mixture was the initial indication of the obtained synthesis of bAgNPs. Later the colour of the mixture changed from dark yellow to dark brown colour within few hours of incubation and the intensity of colour increased with the increase in incubation time, due to bio‐reduction of Ag+ ions to Ag0 (Figure 1). The UV–Vis spectroscopy showed a potent absorbance peak at 418 nm confirming AgNP formation (Figure 2). This unique peak confirmed the successful reduction of silver ions and the creation of AgNPs, implying that the bacterial supernatant utilised as the reducing and stabilising agent helped to maintain their stability and functionality. SEM images demonstrated that the bAgNPs were incorporated in the bacteria‐produced EPS (Figure 3). The presence of EPS was crucial for the nanoparticle delayed release of Ag+ ions, which improved their stability and antibacterial activity. This finding is consistent with prior research showing that bacterial EPS can act as a natural fixer for nanoparticles [22], avoiding agglomeration and ensuring controlled release of silver ions. It is essential to emphasise that, while some microbial secretomes and EPS may have pathogenic features, the S. saprophyticus organism and its supernatant were heat‐treated and cell‐free, considerably reducing possible pathogenicity hazards. Furthermore, recent research works have demonstrated the safe use of cell‐free supernatants from comparable strains in the production of nanomaterials, demonstrating their biocompatibility [23].

X‐Ray Diffraction Study

XRD characterisation was performed using X‐rays of wavelength 1.789010 Å (consistent with C o ‐K α radiation), over a 2θ range of 24.570°–118.283°. Phase identification indicated that the diffraction pattern was best matched by silver halide phases rather than metallic silver: bromargyrite (AgBr) gave the highest figure‐of‐merit (FOM = 0.8305), followed by chlorargyrite (AgCl, FOM = 0.8246), with silver niobate (AgNbO3) and a silver niobium tantalum oxide identified as lower‐probability alternative matches. Rietveld refinement of the dataset did not converge to a satisfactory fit; the computed pattern shown in Figure 4 represents the best available fit to the chlorargyrite (AgCl) reference, obtained after background subtraction. Accordingly, we interpret the XRD data as indicating a silver halide‐dominated surface phase most likely arising from residual chloride/bromide during synthesis or sample handling rather than confirming a purely metallic Ag0 core; complementary surface‐sensitive techniques (e.g., XPS or selected‐area electron diffraction) would be needed to fully resolve the core/surface phase composition.

XRD pattern of the synthesised biogenic silver‐based nanoparticles. The diffraction profile indicates crystalline silver‐associated phases, with AgCl/AgBr‐related signatures among the closest database matches.

Antibacterial and Anibiofil Activity Studies of Biosynthesised AgNPs

The antibacterial activity of the biosynthesised nanoparticles was evaluated using the agar well diffusion assay. Clear zones of inhibition were observed around wells containing the nanoparticles against the Gram‐positive bacterium S. aureus and the Gram‐negative bacterium P. aeruginosa . The zones of inhibition measured approximately 26 mm for P. aeruginosa and 23 mm for S. aureus , indicating strong antibacterial activity (Figure 5B,D respectively). MIC analysis further confirmed concentration‐dependent inhibition, with MIC values of 3 μg/mL for S. aureus and 0.5 μg/mL for P. aeruginosa . These results demonstrate that the nanoparticles possess broad antibacterial activity against both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative pathogens.

Antibacterial and antibiofilm activity of the synthesised nanoparticles against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa . (A–D) Representative agar well diffusion plates showing inhibition zones. (E–H) Quantitative inhibition of biofilm growth at different nanoparticle concentrations. Data are presented as mean ± SD of triplicate experiments.

The antibiofilm efficacy to prevent P. aeruginosa and S. aureus from forming biofilms was assessed using the CV assay (Figure 5G,H). While comparing S. aureus biofilm and P. aeruginosa biofilms inhibition, results demonstrate that P. aeruginosa biofilm showed a higher level of sensitivity to the produced bAgNPs, with effective suppression taking place at far lower doses. In particular, biofilm formation was significantly reduced at doses as low as 3 μg/mL, demonstrating the pathogen's increased sensitivity.

Planktonic Growth Kinetics Studies

The growth kinetics in a 24‐h experiment show a concentration‐dependent inhibition. At 0.375 μg/mL for S. aureus and P. aeruginosa , and 6.25 μg/mL for C. albicans , growth rates were only modestly reduced compared to the control (just bacteria). Planktonic growth was significantly suppressed at concentrations ranging from 1 to 1.5 μg/mL and 12.5 to 25 μg/mL, with a delayed exponential phase and lower maximum OD than the control. Indicating significant reduction. At 1.5, 3 and 25 μg/mL, there was complete inhibition of planktonic growth over 24 h for P. aeruginosa, S. aureus and C. albicans , respectively (Figure 6). Antifungal Activity of Biosynthesised AgNPs

The antifungal activity of bAgNPs against C. albicans was determined using the spot agar diffusion technique. The results showed that bAgNPs had strong antifungal activity against the test strain. At a concentration of 100 μg/mL, bAgNPs efficiently inhibited fungal growth, as indicated by a significant reduction in colony formation on agar plates compared to the control group, which showed strong growth of C. albicans . After the initial screening with the spot agar diffusion method, the MIC of AgNPs was determined using a 96‐well microtiter plate assay (Figure 7). The MIC was recorded as 100 μg/mL, representing the lowest concentration at which visible fungal growth was completely inhibited under the tested conditions. These results indicate that bAgNPs are effective at reducing fungal vitality, making them promising candidates for antifungal applications.

Antifungal activity of the synthesised nanoparticles against C. albicans. (A) Percentage growth inhibition at different nanoparticle concentrations. (B) Representative agar plates showing growth in control and nanoparticle‐treated conditions.

Virulence Attenuation Properties of bAgNPs

The effect of bAgNPs on the virulence factors such as swarming motility, swimming motility and biofilm formation was evaluated. The swarming motility test revealed a considerable decrease in growth when compared to the control group (Figure 8). The bAgNPs had a MIC of 3 μg/mL, indicating efficient inhibition of bacterial movement. Similarly, swimming motility studies confirmed these findings, revealing a similar decrease in bacterial movement, emphasising the nanoparticle anti‐virulent properties. We also assessed antibiofilm activity using the CV assay. In this experiment, the control plates showed a noticeable colouration due to biofilm formation, indicating strong bacterial growth and biofilm development. In the presence of the bAgNPs at their MIC, less biofilm was found on the plates. This sharp contrast demonstrates the nanoparticle ability to disrupt biofilm development, hence inhibiting the formation of bacterial colonies. The consequences of these findings are substantial, since they indicate that the synthesised nanoparticles not only inhibit bacterial movement but also have powerful antibiofilm characteristics.

Effect of synthesised nanoparticles on virulence‐associated motility traits, including swarming, swimming and twitching motility. Representative images and quantitative measurements show concentration‐dependent inhibition after nanoparticle treatment. Data are presented as mean ± SD of triplicate experiments.

Antibiofilm Activity Studies of Synthesised Nanoparticle by Fluorescence Microscopy

Epifluorescence microscopy was used to further assess the nanoparticle antibiofilm activity against S. aureus , P. aeruginosa and C. albicans biofilms (Figures 9, 10, 11). bAgNPs were added to the culture media inoculated with bacteria to evaluate their capacity to inhibit initial biofilm formation, which involved biofilm formation without any treatment. Microscopic analysis revealed that the application of AgNPs significantly reduced the production of biofilms in both S. aureus , P. aeruginosa and C. albicans when compared to the control. Additionally, the treatment resulted in a decrease in the cellular density inside the biofilm structure.

After exposure to bAgNPs at inhibitory doses, the reactive oxygen species (ROS) activity against S. aureus, P. aeruginosa , was evaluated with DCFH‐DA assay (Figure 12). The findings showed that ROS levels in the treated cells were much higher than those of the control groups, suggesting that bAgNP exposure causes oxidative stress in all tested microbes. The fluorescence intensity in S. aureus cells treated with AgNPs was significantly greater, indicating that these nanoparticles increase the production of ROS, which could be a factor in their antibacterial activity. The idea that AgNPs can disturb cellular homeostasis through oxidative pathways is further supported by the observation that P. aeruginosa showed increased ROS levels after treatment. Additionally, after being exposed to the AgNPs, the cells also displayed enhanced ROS generation, demonstrating the broad‐spectrum efficacy of these nanoparticles against bacterial and fungal pathogens. By utilising ROS‐mediated pathways, AgNPs have the potential to be efficient antibacterial agents, as evidenced by the increased oxidative stress seen in all tested strains. All things considered, these results indicated that the use of AgNPs can dramatically increase ROS levels in undesirable microbes, resulting in oxidative damage and eventual cell.

Effect of bAgNPs on Cellular Metabolic Activity by MTT Assay

The MTT assay was used to evaluate the effect of the synthesised bAgNPs on cellular metabolic activity in S. aureus and P. aeruginosa (Figure 13). MTT reduction reflects the activity of cellular reductases/dehydrogenases in metabolically active cells. After nanoparticle treatment, both bacterial strains showed a concentration‐dependent decrease in formazan formation compared with untreated controls. At 3 μg/mL, a marked reduction in absorbance was observed, indicating strong inhibition of cellular metabolic activity. These results support the antimicrobial findings and suggest that nanoparticle exposure compromises the microbial metabolic function. However, for bacterial cells, this reduction should be described as decreased cellular dehydrogenase activity or metabolic inhibition rather than mitochondrial dysfunction.

Effect of synthesised nanoparticles on cellular metabolic activity in S. aureus and P. aeruginosa , assessed by MTT reduction. Decreased formazan formation indicates reduced cellular dehydrogenase activity/metabolic activity after treatment.

Cytotoxicity Test Using the HeLa Cell Line

The cytotoxic potential of the bacterial extracts was determined using the MTT test on the HeLa (human cervical cancer) cell line. This test is commonly used to measure cell viability by determining how metabolically active cells reduce the MTT reagent to insoluble formazan crystals. The study examined cytotoxicity at concentrations of 0.1, 1, 10, 100 and 1000 μg/mL to analyse the dose‐dependent response of HeLa cells (Figure 14). At lesser concentrations (0.1, 1, 10 and 100 μg/mL), the extracts had minimal cytotoxic effects on HeLa cells. The cell monolayers remained intact, with just minor morphological modifications, indicating that the bulk of the cells preserved their original form and vitality. Cell viability decreased slightly to 1000 μg/mL, but not significantly compared to the control group receiving only culture media. Cell viability decreased significantly at the highest concentration (1000 μg/mL). Morphological examinations under the microscope verified these findings, as cells appeared smaller, rounded and separated from the surface, indicating high‐level cytotoxic stress (Figure 14A). The MTT assay data revealed a significant decrease in absorbance values, indicating severe inhibition of cellular metabolic activity and enhanced cell death (Figure 14B). Overall, the cytotoxicity experiment revealed that the bacterial extract was non‐toxic to HeLa cells at low to moderate concentrations, but cytotoxic effects became apparent only at higher dosages. Cytotoxicity was seen at the highest dose of 1000 μg/mL, with a considerable drop in OD570 values indicating cell death. These findings suggest that the extracts have a concentration‐dependent cytotoxic profile, making them suitable for further biological or antimicrobial research while providing no substantial toxicity hazards at lower concentrations.

Discussion

The rise of multidrug‐resistant (MDR) bacteria has contributed to global demand for new antimicrobial treatments. In this context, the biogenic production of AgNPs with microbial supernatants represents a promising, environmentally benign alternative to traditional chemical approaches. This study demonstrates the effective green synthesis of AgNPs using S. saprophyticus and assesses their antibacterial, antifungal, antibiofilm and virulence‐inhibitory capabilities. The findings support and expand on recent breakthroughs in green nanotechnology, emphasising the therapeutic potential of biologically produced nanomaterials.

The visual shift of the reaction mixture from pale yellow to dark brown confirmed the decrease of Ag+ ions, which is consistent with recent results on microbial‐mediated nanoparticle production [8, 22]. UV–VIS spectroscopy revealed a clear peak at 418 nm, indicating surface plasmon resonance and successful nanoparticle production. SEM investigation revealed spherical nanoparticles embedded in EPS, possibly contributing to their stability and controlled Ag+ ion release. XRD analysis indicated that the crystallite surface predominantly comprised silver halide phases (AgBr and AgCl) rather than confirming a purely metallic Ag0 core; such silver halide phases have themselves been reported to contribute to antibacterial activity, potentially via Ag+ release and synergistic ROS generation. Raj et al. [13] reported comparable findings, highlighting the role of mixed‐phase silver‐based nanostructures in bactericidal activity. Given the non‐converging refinement, we interpret our XRD data cautiously and note that complementary surface‐sensitive techniques would help fully resolve the phase composition of the synthesised particles. These findings support the efficacy of S. saprophyticus supernatant as both a reducing and capping agent, reflecting the findings of Vadakkan et al. [7], who demonstrated the use of Gram‐positive bacterial extracts for green nanoparticle production.

The biogenic AgNPs in this study showed strong antibacterial activity against both a Gram‐positive ( S. aureus ) and Gram‐negative ( P. aeruginosa ) pathogen, with large inhibition zones measuring 26 mm and 23 mm, respectively. Notably, bAgNPs were effective even at very low concentrations, with MIC values of 3 μg/mL for S. aureus and 0.5 μg/mL for P. aeruginosa . The slight difference in susceptibility suggests that the AgNPs may penetrate or disrupt the outer structures of Gram‐negative bacteria even more effectively than those of Gram‐positives, or it could reflect inherent differences in strain susceptibility. These findings are consistent with those published by Huq and Akter [9], who discovered identical MIC values for biogenic AgNPs synthesised with Bacillus species.

The AgNPs also showed significant antifungal effects, with complete inhibition of C. albicans at 100 μg/mL. This concentration is within the range of other studies; for example, Trzcińska‐Wencel et al. [16] reported that nano‐silver had broad antifungal potential against C. albicans and other opportunistic fungi. Further studies are needed to detail the effects of these bAgNPs on fungal cell structure and metabolism. The mechanisms of antifungal action are believed to be similar to those in bacteria, including membrane rupture due to ROS generation [22], protein denaturation and DNA interference, according to Girma [10], who highlighted the diverse modes of action of metallic nanoparticles.

Biofilms are widely antibiotic‐resistant and contribute to persistent illnesses. Our results show that bAgNPs successfully reduce biofilm development in S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, with 100% inhibition at 3 μg/mL. P. aeruginosa was particularly sensitive, requiring lower amounts (1 μg/mL) to break biofilms. These findings are consistent with those of Sarkar et al. [14], who evaluated the antibiofilm capability of nanoparticles against ESKAPE infections. Epifluorescence microscopy confirmed a marked decrease in biofilm biomass and disruption of biofilm architecture after bAgNP treatment. The Congo Red assay validated EPS suppression, as treated plates lacked the distinctive black colonies typical of robust biofilm formers. These findings are consistent with Joshi et al. [11], who highlighted the importance of bAgNPs in targeting biofilm matrix components. Furthermore, the nanoparticles reduced bacterial mobility, including swarming and swimming behaviours that are required for biofilm development and pathogenicity.

One of the primary antibacterial actions of AgNPs is the induction of oxidative stress via production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). In the present study, DCFH‐DA fluorescence showed increased ROS levels in nanoparticle‐treated S. aureus and P. aeruginosa , suggesting that oxidative stress contributes to the observed antimicrobial activity. These findings are comparable with those of Dayem et al. [20], who identified ROS‐mediated cytotoxicity as a central mechanism of nanoparticle activity. ROS can damage cellular components such as lipids, proteins and nucleic acids, thereby impairing microbial viability. MTT results (Figure 14) revealed a marked reduction in cellular dehydrogenase activity in treated bacterial cells, reflecting impaired respiratory/metabolic function rather than mitochondrial dysfunction, since bacteria lack mitochondria; this pattern is consistent with the reduced formazan production reported by Sabaeifard et al. [21] in AgNP‐treated P. aeruginosa biofilms. Nevertheless, ROS generation and MTT reduction alone do not fully define the complete mechanism of action. The antimicrobial effect is likely multifactorial and may involve membrane damage, Ag+ release, interaction with cellular proteins, disruption of biofilm matrix components, altered motility and interference with intracellular metabolic processes.

The utilisation of S. saprophyticus supernatants adds a new dimension to green nanotechnology. Microbial enzymes and metabolites present in the supernatant likely contribute to nanoparticle nucleation and stabilisation. While the exact chemicals have yet to be fully characterised, previous research works [17, 24] suggest that proteins, peptides and secondary metabolites play important roles in capping nanoparticles and enhancing their antibacterial activity. This biogenic approach has several advantages over chemical synthesis, including lower toxicity, environmental sustainability and potential synergistic effects. However, the idea of intrinsic safety requires further validation, as emphasised by Patel et al. [12], who advocated for comprehensive cytotoxicity and ecotoxicity tests.

In summary, the findings of this study have important implications for clinical microbiology and nanomedicine. The capacity of AgNPs to attack MDR bacteria and fungi, inhibit biofilms and reduce virulence factors makes them versatile antimicrobial agents. Their biogenic origin is consistent with global aspirations towards sustainable and green technologies. Future study should centre on: (i) assessing cytotoxicity and biocompatibility in mammalian cell lines to assure therapeutic safety (ii) exploring synergistic effects with conventional antibiotics to improve efficacy and prevent resistance (iii) assessing environmental impact by long‐term ecotoxicological investigations. Recent studies, by Duman et al. [6] and El‐Sayyad et al. [8], have emphasised the need for translational research in nanoparticle‐based treatments. By advancing our understanding of biogenic AgNPs and addressing these future directions, we move closer to developing effective, eco‐friendly nanotherapeutics to combat MDR infections and biofilm‐associated conditions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the synthesis of biogenic silver‐based nanoparticles using Staphylococcus saprophyticus cell‐free supernatant provides a sustainable approach for generating antimicrobial nanomaterials. The synthesised nanoparticles showed antibacterial activity against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa , antifungal activity against C. albicans and strong inhibition of biofilm formation and motility‐associated virulence traits. ROS and MTT assays indicated increased oxidative stress and reduced cellular metabolic activity after nanoparticle exposure, suggesting that oxidative stress‐associated metabolic impairment contributes to the antimicrobial effects. However, these findings should be viewed as supportive mechanistic evidence rather than a complete explanation of the mode of action. The XRD findings indicate crystalline silver‐associated phases, including AgCl/AgBr‐related signatures and therefore the material should be described cautiously as a biogenic silver‐based nanomaterial. Overall, the study highlights the potential of S. saprophyticus ‐mediated nanoparticles as multifunctional antimicrobial agents, while future work should focus on detailed phase confirmation, identification of capping biomolecules, cytotoxicity profiling, ecotoxicological assessment and evaluation of synergistic activity with conventional antimicrobials.
