Biomarker-stratified efficacy and safety of biologics in systemic lupus erythematosus: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Safi G Alqatari, Mohanad A Alkuwaiti, Faisal A Al-Harbi, Marj M Alabdullah, Adel A Zeidan, Noor H AlYousef, Mohammed A Alsaad, Rena Y Abualjamal, Feras Ahmed Alkuwaiti, Abdullah A Al-Abdulwahab, Hajer Musaab AlZuhair, Danya Y AlNujaidi, Nora A Alasaker, Manal A Hasan, Ahmed Y Azzam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBiologics for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) demonstrate variable treatment responses across trials. We evaluated baseline biomarkers as predictors of response to guide personalised therapy selection.MethodsWe searched PubMed, OVID, Scopus, Cochrane and PsycInfo (January 2000–October 2025) for randomised controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating biologics (anti-BAFF, JAK inhibitors, anti-interferon, anti-IL-12/23, BTK inhibitors and T-cell modulators) in adult SLE patients. Primary outcomes included SLE Responder Index-4 response and time-to-flare. We performed random-effects meta-analysis stratified by interferon gene signature (IGS), serologic activity (anti-double-stranded DNA positivity and/or low complement) and high disease activity (Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) ≥10), with false discovery rate correction for multiple comparisons.ResultsThirty-one RCTs (17 374 participants) were included. Overall, biologics improved SLE Responder Index-4 response versus control (risk ratio (RR) 1.26, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.33, p <0.0001). Serologically active disease showed the strongest predictive value (RR 1.45, 95% CI 1.28 to 1.63, p<0.0001, I²=0%, number needed to treat (NNT)=6.5). High baseline SLEDAI ≥10 also predicted response (RR 1.22, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.32, I²=0%). IGS showed non-significant trends (RR 1.39, 95% CI 0.97 to 2.00, p=0.074, I²=68.5%). Efficacy varied by mechanism, with anti-BAFF agents showing consistent benefit (RR 1.35, 95% CI 1.26 to 1.45). Biologics reduced serious adverse events (SAE) (RR 0.90, p=0.009); this should be interpreted considering lupus flares contribute to SAE counts. Individual non-disease adverse events showed expected patterns, with herpes zoster significantly increased (RR 1.19, p=0.017).ConclusionSerologically active disease and high baseline disease activity significantly predict biologic therapy response with zero heterogeneity, while IGS showed inconsistent predictive value. These findings support using serological markers to guide biologic selection in real-world practice settings. Biologics demonstrate favourable safety profiles.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251247531.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) affects approximately 3.41 million people globally, with women being affected eight times more than men.1 Despite the recent approval of three biologics: belimumab, anifrolumab and voclosporin,2 the findings of these clinical trials have been inconsistent. Anifrolumab did not achieve its primary endpoint in TULIP-1 but demonstrated efficacy in TULIP-2,3 whereas the response rates to B cell-targeted therapies vary significantly among patient populations.4 Heterogeneity in treatment response poses a significant obstacle to disease management and underscores the urgent need for predictive biomarkers to inform therapy selection.

Besides, multiple baseline biomarkers have been suggested for the stratification of patients with SLE, including the interferon gene signature (IGS), anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies (anti-dsDNA), complement levels and disease activity scores such as Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) ≥10.5 A post hoc analysis of five phase III belimumab trials with 3086 patients revealed that people with SLEDAI ≥10 or anti-dsDNA positivity had significant treatment benefits, while those without these markers did not differ from the placebo group.6 A network meta-analysis of 17 biologics from 29 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) revealed that anti-interferon biologics exhibit superior efficacy, especially in patients with elevated baseline interferon signatures.7

Furthermore, exploratory analysis revealed that baseline IgA2 anti-dsDNA concentration serves as a predictor of response to rituximab combined with belimumab, with patients exhibiting elevated IgA2 anti-dsDNA levels demonstrating a 48% increase in response rates.4 Cell-bound complement activation products demonstrate higher sensitivity than traditional complement measurements.2 However, the evidence is disjointed across trials due to inconsistent biomarker definitions and diverse study designs. A recent scoping review determined that no single biomarker demonstrates adequate sensitivity and specificity for clinical application, with numerous instances lacking validation.5 No comprehensive synthesis has quantified the comparative predictive value of these biomarkers across biological mechanisms, thereby restricting their translation into personalised treatment.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the biomarker-stratified efficacy of biologic therapies in patients with SLE. The specific objectives were to (1) quantify treatment effects across biomarker-defined subgroups, including IGS (IFN-high vs IFN-low), serologically active disease (anti-dsDNA positivity and/or low complement), high baseline disease activity (SLEDAI ≥10) and individual serological markers; (2) compare the predictive value of these biomarkers; (3) determine whether biomarker associations differ by biological mechanism class (anti-BAFF, JAK inhibitors, anti-interferon, anti-IL-12/23, BTK inhibitors and T-cell modulators); and (4) assess safety outcomes and time-to-flare endpoints. We searched major databases from January 2000 to October 2025, encompassing Phase 2–4 RCTs of biologics in adult patients with SLE that reported SLE Responder Index outcomes or time-to-event data. We used a random-effects meta-analysis with false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment and conducted sensitivity analyses categorised by trial characteristics.

This study addresses a critical gap in the management of SLE by providing significant quantitative evidence for the role of biomarkers in guiding therapy selection. By integrating biomarker-stratified efficacy data across diverse therapeutic mechanisms and patient populations, our findings have important implications for clinical trial design, regulatory decisions on biomarker-enriched indications and the development of precision medicine frameworks that optimise treatment selection while minimising unnecessary exposure to ineffective therapies

Methods

Protocol registration and reporting guidelines

This systematic review and meta-analysis were prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251247531) and conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.8 The protocol specified all eligibility criteria, search strategies, outcomes of interest and planned analytical approaches prior to study identification. No amendments were made to the registered protocol.

Search strategy and study selection

We conducted a comprehensive systematic search of PubMed/MEDLINE, OVID, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and PsycINFO from January 2000 to October 2025. The search strategy combined MeSH terms and free-text keywords for systemic lupus erythematosus (including “SLE”), biologic agents (belimumab, anifrolumab, rituximab, obinutuzumab, abatacept, blisibimod, tabalumab, epratuzumab, ustekinumab, litifilimab, dapirolizumab, KP104, efalizumab, ocrelizumab, tibulizumab, IL-2, interleukin-2 and voclosporin), study design terms (randomized controlled trial, RCT), outcome terms (efficacy, effectiveness, safety, adverse events) and follow-up terms using Boolean operators (AND, OR). The search strategy was adapted to the specific syntax and subject headings of each database. No language restrictions were applied. Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible trials. Two independent reviewers screened all titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review of potentially eligible articles. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion with a third senior reviewer.

Eligibility criteria

We included Phase 2, 2b, 3 or 4 RCTs evaluating biologic therapies in adult patients with SLE compared with placebo or standard care. Eligible biologics included agents targeting B-cell pathways (anti-BAFF, anti-CD20, BTK inhibitors), cytokine pathways (anti-interferon, anti-IL-12/23) or intracellular signalling (JAK inhibitors, T-cell modulators). Trials were required to report at least one of the following outcomes: SLE Responder Index (SRI-4 or variants), time to disease flare or safety outcomes. We excluded extension studies, pooled post hoc analyses of primary trials, observational studies and trials exclusively enrolling patients with lupus nephritis as the primary indication, though lupus nephritis trials reporting general SLE activity outcomes were included.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers used standardised forms to extract data on the study design, participant demographics, intervention details, outcome definitions and results for all prespecified outcomes. For biomarker-stratified analyses, we extracted subgroup data for IGS (IFN-high/low), anti-dsDNA antibody positivity, low complement (C3/C4), serologically active disease (defined as anti-dsDNA positive and/or low complement) and high baseline disease activity (SLEDAI ≥10). Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, evaluating the randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes and selection of reported results. Overall bias judgments represented the highest risk level across all domains.

Biomarker subgroup definitions

Serologically active disease was defined as the presence of elevated anti-dsDNA antibodies and/or reduced complement components, with specific thresholds varying by trial (anti-dsDNA ≥30 IU/mL or assay-positive; C3/C4 below the normal range). High baseline disease activity was defined as an SLEDAI score ≥10 at study entry, representing moderate-to-severe disease. For IGS analyses, we accepted trial-specific definitions based on median or prespecified cut-offs from validated multi-gene expression panels (4-gene, 5-gene or 21-gene signatures), acknowledging the lack of standardised assays across trials as a source of heterogeneity.

Synthetic individual patient data reconstruction

Individual patient-level time-to-event data were reconstructed from published aggregate statistics using piecewise-constant hazard calibration methods. For each study, endpoint and treatment arm, we extracted sample size, total event count at study end and survival probabilities at 24 and 52 weeks when reported. Piecewise-constant hazards for intervals zero to 24 weeks, 24 to 52 weeks and 52 weeks to study end were calculated to exactly reproduce published survival probabilities. Event times were generated from exponential distributions with interval-specific hazards, with administrative censoring applied to subjects without events. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were estimated using standard product-limit methods, from which we extracted survival probabilities, restricted mean survival time using trapezoidal integration and median time to event. HRs were estimated using the Mantel-Haenszel method from log-rank statistics. For meta-analytic pooling by biological mechanism, individual study survival curves were interpolated onto common weekly time grids and pooled using inverse-variance weighting with weights proportional to the number of subjects at risk at each time point.

Statistical analysis and meta-analytic methods

Primary efficacy outcomes were analysed using random-effects meta-analysis with the DerSimonian-Laird method for binary outcomes (risk ratios with 95% CIs) and inverse-variance methods for time-to-event outcomes (HRs). Treatment effects were pooled overall and stratified by biological mechanism class (anti-BAFF, JAK inhibitors, anti-interferon, anti-IL-12/23, BTK inhibitors and T-cell modulators). For biomarker-stratified analyses, we performed random-effects meta-analyses within each biomarker-defined subgroup and applied Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for multiple comparisons. Meta-regression was performed to analyse the correlation between the baseline SLEDAI score as a continuous predictor and the treatment response. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out analyses, stratification by trial phase (Phase 2/2b vs Phase 3), geographic region and comparisons of alternative meta-analytic methods (restricted maximum likelihood, Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman). All analyses were performed using R V.4.3.1 with the meta and metafor packages.

Assessment of heterogeneity and publication bias

Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and τ² (between-study variance), with I² values of 25%, 50% and 75% corresponding to low, moderate and substantial heterogeneity, respectively. Sources of heterogeneity were explored through prespecified subgroup analyses, meta-regression and influence diagnostics using Baujat plots to identify studies that contributed disproportionately to heterogeneity or influenced pooled estimates. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots, Egger’s regression test for funnel plot asymmetry and trim-and-fill analysis to estimate the impact of potentially missing studies. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p<0.05 for all analyses.

Quality of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology. Evidence from RCTs commenced with a high level of certainty but was subsequently downgraded due to significant concerns regarding risk of bias, inconsistency (unexplained heterogeneity), indirectness, imprecision (broad CIs or limited sample sizes) or publication bias. Separate GRADE assessments were performed for overall efficacy outcomes, biomarker-stratified analyses, safety outcomes and time-to-event endpoints. The quality of evidence was categorised as high, moderate, low or very low according to a comprehensive evaluation across various domains.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Our systematic search identified 296 records from database searches (figure 1). After removing 99 duplicates, we screened 197 unique records and assessed 49 full-text reports for eligibility. We excluded 19 reports: 16 were extension studies or pooled analyses of primary trials, and 3 had inappropriate study designs. Thirty publications reporting 31 RCTs met inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. One publication reported results from two separate trials.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 flow diagram.

The 31 included trials enrolled 17 374 participants across diverse geographic regions and evaluated multiple biologic mechanisms (table 1, online supplemental tables S1-S2). Sample sizes ranged from 52 to 1584 participants per trial. Trials evaluated anti-BAFF agents (10 trials), JAK inhibitors (three trials), anti-interferon therapies (three trials), anti-IL-12/23 agents (two trials), BTK inhibitors (one trial), T-cell modulators (one trial) and combination biologics (two trials). Primary endpoints varied across studies, including SRI-4 or variants (16 trials), BICLA (four trials), renal response measures (three trials), time to flare (two trials) and other composite measures (six trials). Thirteen trials (42%) met their primary endpoints. Biomarker data availability varied substantially: high SLEDAI data were available in 24 trials (77%), anti-dsDNA antibody analyses in 21 trials (68%), low complement in 20 trials (65%), serologically active composite in 10 trials (32%) and IGS in only seven trials (23%).

Overall efficacy and mechanism-specific effects

The primary efficacy analysis included 16 trials reporting SRI-4 response rates in 7302 participants. Biologic therapies significantly improved SRI-4 response compared with control (risk ratio 1.26, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.33, p<0.0001), with substantial heterogeneity (I²=68.2%). Efficacy varied by biological mechanism (online supplemental figure S1). Anti-BAFF agents showed consistent benefit across seven trials (risk ratio (RR) 1.35, 95% CI 1.26 to 1.45, I²=33.0%), though individual trial outcomes varied with 6 of 10 published trials meeting primary endpoints (online supplemental figure S2). JAK inhibitors showed significant efficacy in three trials (RR 1.19, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.34, I²=69.3%). Anti-interferon agents demonstrated limited efficacy in three trials (RR 1.21, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.40, I²=75.3%). Anti-IL-12/23 agents showed non-significant results in two trials (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.75 to 1.14), with the CI crossing unity. The single BTK inhibitor trial (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.84 to 1.57) did not achieve statistical significance.

Biomarker-stratified efficacy analysis

Biomarker stratification revealed substantial variation in treatment response (table 2). High baseline disease activity (SLEDAI ≥10) emerged as a robust predictor across eight studies with 2683 participants, demonstrating significant benefit (RR 1.22, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.32, p <0.0001, FDR-adjusted p<0.0001) with perfect homogeneity (I²=0.0%). The serologically active subgroup, defined as anti-dsDNA positive and/or low complement, showed the strongest treatment effect across eight studies with 1655 participants (RR 1.45, 95% CI 1.28 to 1.63, p<0.0001, FDR-adjusted p <0.0001) and zero heterogeneity (I²=0.0%), corresponding to a number needed to treat of 6.5 patients (online supplemental figure S3, online supplemental table S3).

Anti-dsDNA positivity alone predicted response in five studies with 1805 participants (RR 1.29, 95% CI 1.16 to 1.44, p=0.0049, FDR-adjusted p=0.0066) but with moderate heterogeneity (I²=53.6%). Low complement alone, analysed in only two studies with 263 participants, showed non-significant results (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.63 to 2.11, p=0.65) with substantial heterogeneity (I²=61.7%).

Analysis of the IGS yielded inconsistent, non-significant results (online supplemental table S4, figure 2). In the IFN-high subgroup, a pooled analysis of four studies with 704 participants showed a non-significant trend (RR 1.39, 95% CI 0.97 to 2.00, p=0.074) with substantial heterogeneity (I²=68.5%). Individual study results were highly variable, ranging from RR 0.92 to 2.20. Notably, a non-IFN-targeted therapy (ustekinumab) demonstrated the strongest effect in IFN-high patients (RR 2.20, 95% CI 1.07 to 4.52), while pooled IFN-targeted therapies showed non-significant benefit (RR 1.28, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.86, p=0.205), suggesting IGS predictive utility may be mechanism-specific rather than universal. The IFN-low subgroup analysis in 2 studies with 98 participants was similarly non-significant (RR 1.11, 95% CI 0.62 to 2.00, p=0.716). Meta-regression examining baseline SLEDAI score as a continuous predictor revealed no significant association with treatment response (online supplemental figure S4). The regression equation (log RR=−0.465 + 0.067 × SLEDAI) explained only 3.5% of variance (R² = 3.5%, p=0.476), with the slope confidence interval (-0.117 to 0.252) including zero.

Safety outcomes

Pooled safety analysis across 31 trials with 17 374 participants demonstrated favourable biologic therapy profiles (table 3). Serious adverse events (SAE) occurred less frequently with biologics (13.1% vs 16.4%; RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.97, p=0.009); this should be interpreted considering lupus flares contribute to SAE counts in trials. Individual non-disease adverse events showed expected patterns: herpes zoster was significantly increased (RR 1.19, p=0.017). Mortality was rare in both groups (0.4% vs 0.6%; RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.47 to 1.12, p=0.148) with zero heterogeneity (I²=0.0%). Treatment discontinuations due to adverse events showed a borderline significant reduction (7.3% vs 8.2%; RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.75 to 1.00, p=0.055, I²=13.6%).

Time-to-event outcomes

Time-to-first-flare analysis across 17 studies demonstrated a significant benefit of biologic therapy (online supplemental table S5-6, online supplemental figure S5). The pooled HR was 0.70 (95% CI 0.61 to 0.80, p<0.001), representing a 30% reduction in flare risk with low-to-moderate heterogeneity (I²=37.7%). Individual study HRs ranged from 0.12 to 1.15 (online supplemental figure S6), with the most dramatic benefit observed in an anti-IL-12/23 trial (HR 0.12) and anti-BAFF trials showing consistent moderate-to-strong effects. Pooled Kaplan-Meier curves stratified by biological mechanism revealed differential kinetics of flare prevention (online supplemental figure S7-S8). For severe flares, anti-IL-12/23 inhibitors demonstrated the most dramatic curve separation (HR 0.348, 95% CI 0.263 to 0.461, ΔS(52)=+0.085), followed by BAFF-axis inhibitors (HR 0.755, 95% CI 0.710 to 0.803, ΔS(52)=+0.060) and type I interferon antagonists (HR 0.896, 95% CI 0.807 to 0.995, ΔS(52)=+0.043). JAK inhibitors, BTK inhibitors and T-cell modulators showed smaller or non-significant effects. For time to first flare of any severity, similar patterns emerged (online supplemental figure S8), with IL-12/23 inhibitors showing exceptional benefit (HR 0.146, ΔS(52)=+0.126) and BAFF-axis inhibitors demonstrating protective effects (HR 0.361, ΔS(52)=+0.156).

Heterogeneity exploration and sensitivity analyses

The risk of bias assessment showed that the evidence was of high quality (online supplemental table S7). Twenty-two trials (71%) had a low overall risk of bias, while nine trials (29%) raised some concerns, primarily related to the selection of reported results or missing outcome data. No trials were rated as high risk of bias. Baujat influence diagnostics identified van Vollenhoven (LOTUS) as an extreme outlier contributing disproportionately to heterogeneity and influencing the pooled estimate (online supplemental figure S9). Five additional high-influence studies (TULIP-1, ILLUMINATE-1, ILLUMINATE-2, PAISLEY and BRAVE-II) exceeded median influence thresholds, warranting sensitivity analyses.

Comprehensive sensitivity analyses demonstrated the robustness of the findings (online supplemental table S8). Leave-one-out analyses showed pooled estimates ranging from RR 1.25 to 1.31. Phase 2/2b trials showed larger effects (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.72, I²=35.3%) compared with Phase 3 trials (RR 1.24, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.39, I²=73.3%). Asia-predominant trials demonstrated perfect consistency (RR 1.33, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.50, I²=0.0%), while global multiregional trials showed substantial heterogeneity (I²=76.0%). Stratification by trial size revealed that small trials demonstrated stronger hazard ratios (HR 0.610) compared with large trials (HR 0.779), suggesting possible small-study effects (online supplemental figure S10). Stratification by baseline event risk showed treatment benefit decreased with increasing baseline severity: low-risk patients (HR 0.675), medium-risk patients (HR 0.716) and high-risk patients (HR 0.785) (online supplemental figure 11).

Publication bias and evidence quality

Funnel plot examination and statistical tests revealed no evidence of publication bias (online supplemental figure S12). The funnel plot distribution appeared symmetric around the pooled effect estimate. Egger’s regression test was non-significant (p=0.555, intercept=1.01, SE=1.71), and trim-and-fill analysis imputed zero missing studies. GRADE evidence quality assessment varied by outcome (online supplemental table S9). Safety outcomes and time-to-flare received high-quality ratings. Overall, SRI-4 response and biomarker-stratified analyses received moderate-quality ratings, with the exception of SLEDAI ≥10 which received a high-quality rating due to zero heterogeneity. IGS analysis received a very low-quality rating due to substantial inconsistency, indirectness from heterogeneous assays and imprecision with CIs crossing unity.

Discussion

Our study represents a structured, systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating and investigating biomarker-stratified efficacy of biologic therapies in patients with SLE. Including 31 RCTs with 17 374 participants, our findings demonstrate that biologics significantly improve SRI-4 response compared with control, with serologically active disease and high baseline disease activity emerging as significant predictors of treatment response, with no heterogeneity.

The overall pooled estimate in our study aligns with findings from a recent network meta-analysis by Ding et al7 that included 29 RCTs with 13 712 patients and reported SRI-4 heterogeneity of I²=67.4%. The significant heterogeneity observed in overall efficacy analysis in our study was anticipated, given the different biological mechanisms, patient populations and trial designs across included studies. However, stratification by biomarker subgroups provided important insights into sources of this variability.

The serologically active subgroup, defined as anti-dsDNA positive and/or low complement, has demonstrated the most potent treatment effect with zero heterogeneity, corresponding to a number needed to treat of 6.5 patients. This finding is supported by post-hoc analyses from the BLISS programme, where Stohl et al have demonstrated that belimumab-treated patients who were anti-dsDNA positive with low C3/C4 at baseline showed significantly higher SRI response rates and reduced risk of severe flares.9 Similarly, Parodis et al10 analysed data from five phase III belimumab trials (3086 patients) and have demonstrated that patients with SLEDAI ≥10 or positive anti-dsDNA showed significant treatment benefits, while those without these markers did not differ from placebo. The high degree of homogeneity observed in our serologically active and high SLEDAI subgroups suggests that these biomarkers identify patient populations with relatively predictable treatment responses across different biologic mechanisms and trial settings.

In contrast, the IGS showed inconsistent and non-significant results with substantial heterogeneity. This finding may appear contradictory given that type I interferon pathway activation is considered central to SLE pathogenesis. Post-hoc analyses of the TULIP-1/TULIP-2 trials by Morand et al have demonstrated that anifrolumab treatment differences were greater in IFNGS-high patients (18.2%), with limited differences observed in the IFNGS-low subpopulation.1113 However, several factors may explain the inconsistent predictive value of IGS in our analysis. The lack of standardised assays across trials, with different studies using four-gene, five-gene or 21-gene expression panels with varying cut-off definitions, likely contributed to between-study heterogeneity. In addition, our analysis included biologics targeting mechanisms beyond the interferon pathways, and the IGS may be a more relevant predictor for anti-interferon therapies.

Notably, we found that a non-IFN-targeted therapy (ustekinumab) demonstrated the strongest effect in IFN-high patients, while pooled IFN-targeted therapies showed non-significant benefit, suggesting IGS predictive utility may be mechanism-specific rather than universal. This paradoxical finding, where IFN-low patients showed the highest response to IL-12/23 inhibition, indicates the relationship between IGS and treatment response is more complex than initially hypothesised and cannot be generalised across all biologic mechanisms.

Mechanism-specific subgrouping revealed that anti-BAFF agents showed consistent benefit, though individual trial outcomes varied with 6 of 10 published trials meeting primary endpoints. This finding aligns with the observation that serological markers showed the strongest predictive value. This finding is biologically plausible, as BAFF-targeted therapies directly impact B-cell survival and autoantibody production, the same pathways reflected by anti-dsDNA positivity and complement consumption.1417 JAK inhibitors and anti-interferon agents have demonstrated significant but more variable efficacy, suggesting that patient selection for these therapies may require additional biomarkers beyond those currently evaluated.

The safety analysis provided reassuring results, with biologics showing reduced rates of SAE compared with control. These findings are noteworthy given historical concerns about infection risk with immunomodulatory therapies. The reduced SAE rate may reflect overall disease control and reduced need for high-dose corticosteroids, as suggested by real-world evidence from the OBSErve studies showing that up to 78% of belimumab-treated patients were able to reduce or discontinue oral corticosteroid therapy.18 19

The interpretation of SAE reductions requires careful consideration of outcome definitions. In SLE trials, lupus flares meeting severity criteria (SLEDAI >12, organ-threatening manifestations or requiring hospitalisation) are classified as SAEs. Therefore, the observed reduction in SAEs reflects both genuine safety benefits and therapeutic efficacy in preventing disease flares. Individual non-disease adverse event categories showed expected patterns consistent with mechanism of action, with herpes zoster significantly increased but other events similar between treatment arms. This distinction between disease-related and treatment-related adverse events is critical for informed clinical decision-making.

Trial populations demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in background immunosuppressive therapy. Hydroxychloroquine use ranged widely across trials, as did mycophenolate use, with lupus nephritis trials requiring MMF by design and general SLE trials allowing variable background therapy. Baseline corticosteroid doses varied considerably across studies. This medication heterogeneity may have influenced treatment response variability, though our biomarker-stratified analyses showed remarkable consistency despite varying background therapy regimens. Future studies should evaluate whether biomarker-stratified responses vary by specific immunosuppressive combinations.

Eligibility criteria varied across trials, with minimum SLEDAI scores ranging from 4 to 10, and some trials requiring specific BILAG scores. All trials excluded patients with severe active central nervous system lupus and recent serious infections. Prior biologic therapy was universally excluded, though timing of prior cyclophosphamide exposure varied from immediate exclusion to longer washout periods. This eligibility heterogeneity limits generalisability to real-world populations with more diverse treatment histories. The restriction to biologic-naïve patients means our findings may not predict responses in patients who have failed prior biologics.

Time-to-flare analysis has demonstrated a significant flare risk reduction, with anti-IL-12/23 inhibitors showing the most pronounced curve separation. This outcome is particularly relevant for management, as flare prevention is a primary goal in SLE treatment and contributes to long-term morbidity and healthcare utilisation.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. No RCTs directly compared different biologics head-to-head as a network analysis approach, requiring indirect comparisons across trials. Biomarker data availability varied, with IGS reported in only seven trials (23%), limiting the precision of these analyses. The heterogeneity in biomarker definitions and assay methodologies across trials, particularly for IGS, may have contributed to inconsistent results. Publication bias assessment was limited by the relatively small number of studies in some subgroup analyses. In addition to that, most included trials were of 52 weeks duration, and longer-term outcomes require further evaluation.

Based on our findings, we recommend that physicians prioritise serological assessment (anti-dsDNA positivity and/or low complement) and disease activity scoring (SLEDAI ≥10) when selecting patients for biologic therapy initiation. Patients meeting these criteria have demonstrated a significant likelihood of response with a number needed to treat of 6.5. Anti-BAFF agents should be considered among first-line biologics given their consistent efficacy profile, though clinicians should note that individual trial success rates varied, with 6 of 10 published trials meeting primary endpoints. The IGS should not be used as a sole criterion for patient selection until standardised assays and validated cut-offs are developed. For trial design, we recommend stratification by serological activity status and baseline SLEDAI score. Future studies should evaluate whether combining multiple biomarkers improves predictive accuracy beyond individual markers. Long-term studies exceeding 52 weeks are needed to assess durability of biomarker-stratified responses.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis of 31 RCTs with 17 374 participants has demonstrated that biologic therapies significantly improve SRI-4 response in SLE patients. Serologically active disease (anti-dsDNA positive and/or low complement) and high baseline SLEDAI ≥10 emerged as the most reliable predictive biomarkers, both characterised high degree of homogeneity and significant treatment effects that remained significant to multiple testing corrections. The IGS failed to demonstrate consistent predictive value due to assay heterogeneity and complex mechanism-dependent relationships. Anti-BAFF agents showed consistent efficacy among biologic classes. Biologics exhibited favourable safety profiles. These findings demonstrate serological markers as validated tools for precision medicine approaches in SLE, enabling optimised patient selection that maximises therapeutic benefit while minimising exposure to ineffective treatments.
