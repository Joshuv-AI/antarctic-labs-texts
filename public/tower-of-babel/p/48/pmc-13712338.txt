Total secondary saponins from Anemone raddeana suppress pancreatic cancer via Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis
Authors: Luanwei Han, Mingshu Li, Qiao Li, Anqi Lu, Chongning Lv, Yang Yu, Jincai Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePancreatic cancer (PC) is highly lethal and resistant to current therapies, underscoring the need for novel strategies that target regulated cell death. This study aimed to evaluate the anti-PC efficacy of total secondary saponins (TSS) from Anemone raddeana and elucidate the mechanism by which TSS induces pyroptosis.MethodsTSS components were characterized by mass spectrometry. Anti-tumor effects were assessed in vitro using human pancreatic cancer cell lines (PANC-1 and AsPC-1 cells) and in vivo using xenograft mouse model. Integrated multi-omics analyses (network pharmacology, proteomics, metabolomics, bioinformatics) identified core components, targets, and pathways. Key findings were validated by molecular docking, immunohistochemistry, and Western blotting.ResultsMass spectrometry identified 17 C-3 monosaccharide triterpenoid saponins in TSS. Both in vitro and in vivo models confirmed that TSS can significantly inhibit PC cell proliferation by inducing pyroptosis through the Caspase-3/gasdermin E (GSDME) pathway. Multi-omics highlighted hederacolchiside A1 as a core compound and revealed its regulation of inflammation, proliferation, and oxidative stress through the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathways. Molecular docking and Western blotting analysis confirmed activation of downstream pyroptosis mechanisms.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that TSS suppresses PC progression by regulating Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis through modulation of PI3K/Akt and NF-κB signaling pathways, supporting its potential as a novel therapeutic candidate for PC.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is one of the deadliest cancers, with a five-year survival rate of under 10% (Mizrahi, Surana, Valle, & Shroff, 2020). Due to its insidious early symptoms, high invasiveness, and strong resistance to traditional chemotherapy, approximately 80% of patients are ineligible for surgery by the time of diagnosis, presenting a severe challenge in clinical treatment (Han, Zhang, Wang, Yu, & Liu, 2020). At present, the treatment of PC mainly relies on chemotherapy. However, the increasing resistance problem of first-line treatment based on gemcitabine has brought a heavy burden to patients. Studies have demonstrated that gemcitabine treatment triggers abnormal activation of the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, which leads to suppression of apoptosis and contributes to gemcitabine resistance (Li, Tang, Yang, Qian, & Xu, 2022). Therefore, exploring new mechanisms and drugs for treating PC has become an urgent need in current research.

To address this urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches, pyroptosis, a newly discovered form of programmed inflammatory cell death, has gradually become a research hotspot in cancer therapy (Alishvandi, Aram, Shahrivar, Kesharwani, & Sahebkar, 2025). Characterized by cellular swelling, plasma membrane pore formation, release of intracellular contents, and pro-inflammatory responses (Hu et al., 2023), pyroptosis is primarily executed by members of the gasdermin family. Among these, gasdermin E (GSDME) has gained prominence in tumor cell pyroptosis studies. Activated Caspase-3 cleaves GSDME, liberating its N-terminal fragment (GSDME-N) that oligomerizes to form plasma membrane pores, leading to pyroptosis (Ahmad Bhat et al., 2023). This pathway has been shown to play a role in various cancers, including gastric and breast cancer (Wang et al., 2018, Zhang et al., 2021). However, the potential of Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis as a therapeutic strategy for PC has not been fully explored.

While exploring new therapeutic targets for PC, triterpenoid saponins derived from traditional Chinese medicine have attracted attention due to their extensive anti-tumor activities (Zhu et al., 2023). Among them, triterpenoid saponins containing C-3 monosaccharides have stronger anti-tumor effects than those with C-3 and C-28 disaccharide structures (Bang et al., 2005, Barthomeuf et al., 2002). The previous research also showed that the total secondary saponins (TSS) extracted from Anemone raddeana Regel, which are rich in C-3 monosaccharides (accounting for more than 60%), exhibited stronger anti-breast cancer activity than triterpene saponins containing both C-3 and C-28 disaccharides, exhibiting anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic activities (Zhang et al., 2017, Zhang et al., 2020, Zhao et al., 2018). However, current research on the anti-tumor activity mechanism of triterpenoid saponins, including TSS, has primarily focused on apoptosis, while their potential to induce pyroptosis remains largely unexplored, particularly in the context of PC. Notably, evidence indicates that triterpenoid saponins can induce pyroptosis through mechanisms such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and inflammasome regulation. For example, glycyrrhizic acid increases ROS to suppress breast cancer (Cheng et al., 2018), while pulchinenoside C inhibits NOD-like receptor pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and modulates NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects in arthritis (Ni et al., 2025). Based on these findings and considering the strong anti-tumor activity of TSS rich in C-3 monosaccharide triterpenoid saponins, this study investigates whether TSS inhibits PC by inducing Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis and explores the underlying mechanisms.

This study identified the saponin components of TSS and confirmed its anti-PC activity in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, TSS was shown to induce Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. To further clarify the upstream regulatory events, an integrated multi-omics strategy combining proteomics, network pharmacology, and metabolomics was employed, revealing key bioactive constituents, potential targets, and signaling pathways underlying its anti-tumor effects. Collectively, these findings provide the first evidence that TSS, rich in C-3 monosaccharide triterpenoid saponins, exert anti-PC effects by inducing pyroptosis, and highlight it as a promising natural product-derived candidate with potential to overcome chemoresistance in PC.

Preparation of total secondary saponins (TSS)

The preparation process was carried out following our previously reported protocol (Zhang, Zhang, Zheng, & Lu, 2020). Rhizomes of A. raddeana were soaked overnight in a 70% ethanol-alkali solution with a pH of 13, followed by three rounds of reflux extraction at 90 °C for 1.5 h each. The extracts were then dried at 60 °C under reduced pressure. Using an HPD-400 macroporous adsorption resin column, the residue was chromatographed and eluted with gradients of water–ethanol, starting with water, then 20% ethanol, and ending with 80% ethanol. The 80% ethanol eluates were collected, combined, and vacuum-dried to obtain the TSS, which are rich in C-3 monosaccharide triterpenoid saponins. The phytochemical constituents of TSS were determined to use Bruker UHPLC and compact QTOF. An ACQUITY UPLC®BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) was used to separate components at 30 °C. The injection volume was 1 μL, flow rate 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase was methanol (A) and 0.1% formic acid (B). Elution conditions: 0–10 min, 45%−75% A; 10–25 min, 75%−100% A; 25–35 min, 100% A. Mass spectrometry was analyzed using ESI-Q-TOF-MS/MS in positive and negative modes. Parameters: capillary voltage 4 500 V, end plate voltage ± 500 V, dry gas temperature 200 °C, flow rate 5.0 L/min, nebulizer pressure 100 kPa. The mass range recorded m/z 50–1 300.

Cell culture

Human PANC-1 and AsPC-1 pancreatic carcinoma cells (Pricella Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) were cultured in DMEM (PANC-1) or RPMI 1640 (AsPC-1) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cells were passaged at a ratio of 1:3 when reaching approximately 80%−90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) digestion. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase and within 3–8 passages were used for all subsequent experiments. For experiments, cells were seeded at appropriate densities and allowed to adhere overnight until reaching 60%−70% confluence before treatment. For functional assays, both cell lines were used. However, subsequent mechanistic experiments on pyroptosis were conducted exclusively in PANC‑1 cell, as PANC‑1 is a more invasive and drug‑resistant pancreatic cancer cell line that better represents the features of clinically challenging PC.

Cell cytotoxicity assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5.0 × 103 cells/well) and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were divided into the following groups: control group (0 μg/mL) and TSS-treated groups (5, 10, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL). As no prior dose information was available for TSS, a wide concentration range (5–200 μg/mL) was initially screened. The selected six concentrations covered the transition from no effect to near‑complete inhibition, enabling accurate calculation of the IC50 value. TSS was dissolved in sterile distilled water and diluted with culture medium. After treatment for 24 or 48 h, the CCK-8 solution was added and incubated for 3 h, with absorbance at 450 nm measured. The inhibition ratio was calculated as: Inhibition ratio (%) = (Acontrol − Asample)/Acontrol × 100. Based on the IC50 value (7.93 μg/mL) from the CCK-8 assay, three concentrations-4 μg/mL (0.5 × IC50), 8 μg/mL (1 × IC50), and 16 μg/mL (2 × IC50)-were selected for subsequent experiments.

For inhibitor assays, PANC-1 cells were pretreated with 40 μmol/L Z‑VAD‑FMK (a pan‑caspase inhibitor), 40 μmol/L necrostatin‑1 (a necroptosis inhibitor), or 20 μmol/L disulfiram (a pyroptosis inhibitor) for 1 h, based on previously reported effective concentrations (Li et al., 2025). Then, the cells were co‑incubated with TSS (16 μg/mL) for 24 h. Cell viability was evaluated using the same CCK‑8 method as described above.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 3.0 × 105 cells/well and cultued to 90%−100% confluence. A sterile 200 μL pipette tip was used to create a uniform scratch. Cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove detached cells and then cultured in serum-free medium containing TSS (4, 8, and 16 μg/mL). Images were collected under an inverted microscope (IX73, OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan) at 0 and 24h.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500–800 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were treated with TSS (4, 8, and 16 μg/mL) and cultured for 10–14 d until visible colonies formed. Culture medium containing TSS was replaced every three days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min.

Transwell assay

Matrigel gel was diluted with serum-free DMEM. Then, the gel was applied to the upper chamber’s surface and incubated for 30 min. The lower chamber was filled with complete medium and different concentrations of TSS (4, 8, and 16 μg/mL). The transwell chamber was assembled, and cell suspension was added to the upper chamber. After the experiment, migrated cells were fixed with 0.1% crystal violet, washed, and quantified using microscopic imaging.

Microscopy imaging

PANC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2.0 × 105 cells/well) and treated with TSS (4, 8, and 16 μg/mL) for 24 h. Cell characteristic morphological changes of pyroptosis (cell swelling, membrane bubbling) were observed and imaged using an inverted microscope.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

PANC-1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (1.0 × 105 cells/well) and treated with TSS (4, 8, and 16 μg/mL) for 24 h. LDH release was measured using an LDH assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance value at 440 nm was analyzed via a microplate reader.

Annexin V-FITC/ propidium iodide (PI) dual staining

PANC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2.0 × 105 cells/well) and treated with TSS (4, 8, and 16 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following the instructions, the cells were collected, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer. Then, 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL PI were added and incubated for 20 min under dark conditions at 25 °C. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a FACSVerse system (BD Biosciences, San Jose, USA).

Hoechst 33342/PI fluorescent staining

PANC-1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at 1.0 × 105 cells/well. After treatment with TSS (4, 8, and 16 μg/mL) for 24 h, cells were stained with 5 μL of Hoechst 33342 and 15 μL of PI in the dark conditions at 25 °C for 20 min according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Fluorescence imaging was subsequently conducted using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

ROS measurement

PANC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 3.0 × 105 cells/well. After treating with TSS (4, 8, and 16 μg/mL) for 24 h, a reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kit was used. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, cells were incubated with 10 μmol/L 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe at 37 °C for 20 min. ROS levels were analyzed by a flow cytometer.

Cytokine evaluation

PANC-1 cells (3.0 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates) were treated with TSS (4, 8, and 16 μg/mL) for 24 h. Cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove debris. Levels of IL-1β, IL-18, and TNF-α were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of standard or samples were added to the pre‑coated wells and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, followed by biotinylated detection antibody and HRP‑conjugated streptavidin incubations. After washing, tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added for 15–20 min, and the reaction was stopped with stop solution. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cytokine concentrations were calculated from the standard curve.

Western blotting analysis

Total proteins were extracted using radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After quantification, proteins were resolved on sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Blocking with fast blocking buffer was followed by 1 h incubation with primary antibodies at 37 °C. Primary antibodies against RIP (1:1 000), p-RIP (Ser166) (1:1 000), PI3K p85 (1:1 000), p-PI3K p85 (Tyr458)/p55(Tyr199) (1:1 000), Akt (pan) (1:1 000), p-Akt (Ser473) (1:1 000), Caspase-3/cleaved Caspase-3 (1:1 000), cleaved Caspase-8 (1:1 000), GSDME (1:1 000), GSDMD (1:1 000), NF-κB p65 (1:1 000) and phospho-NF-κB p65 (S529) (1:1 000), and GAPDH (1:5 000) were used. After Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBS-T) washing and labeling with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5 000), proteins were detected using an Amersham Imager 680 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA).

Tumor xenograft studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University (approval no. SYPU-IACUC-S2024-0228-104). Male BALB/c-nude mice (18–20 g, 5 weeks old) were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (production license no. SCXK2020-0001). PANC-1 cells were harvested during the logarithmic growth phase (80%−90% confluence), washed with PBS, and resuspend in ice-cold PBS. Each mouse was subcutaneously inoculated with 8.0 × 106 PANC-1 cells in 0.1 mL PBS into the right flank region. When the average tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned into four groups: control (saline), 50 mg/kg TSS, 100 mg/kg TSS, and gemcitabine (25 mg/kg). The dosing concentrations of TSS were selected based on in vitro efficacy and commonly used dosage ranges reported in previous studies (Zhang, Zhang, Zheng, & Lu, 2020). Mice body weight and tumor volume were monitored every three days. After 21 d, mice were sacrificed, and tumors and organs were collected. Tumor growth inhibition rate was calculated. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Ki-67 immunohistochemistry, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining, and tumor immunohistochemistry were performed as described in the supplementary materials. Blood was also collected via retro-orbital bleeding. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3 000 r/min for 10 min after clotting, and then used for non-targeted metabolomic analysis.

Astral DIA proteomic analysis and non-targeted metabolomic analysis

Briefly, proteins were extracted from tumor tissues for proteomic analysis and serum was extracted for metabolomic analysis, which were performed by Shanghai Bioprofile Technology Co., Ltd. Detailed sample preparation, instrument parameter settings, and data acquisition can be found in supplementary materials.

Network pharmacology analysis

The molecular structures of TSS components were obtained from the CAS SciFinder database (https://scifinder-n.cas.org) based on UHPLC-Q/TOF-MS/MS analysis. Potential targets were predicted using the Super Pred database (https://prediction.charite.de/index.php), with compounds having a probability ≥ 80% selected for accuracy. These targets were standardized in Uniprot to obtain drug target genes. For PC targets, the OMIM (https://www.omim.org) and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org) databases were searched with keywords “Pancreatic cancer” or “Pancreatic carcinoma”, yielding PC-related genes after merging and deduplication. Proteomic data from TSS-treated and model groups were processed to obtain a list of proteins, which were subsequently mapped to their corresponding human genes. These genes were then intersected with PC-related genes to generate a candidate target set for further analysis. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis of these intersecting genes was conducted using STRING database (https://string-db.org) with a confidence score ≥ 0.7. Results were visualized in Cytoscape 3.9.1 and further analyzed via gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment using DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov).

Molecular docking

To assess the binding affinity between the main bioactive compound and key target proteins, 3D structures were sourced from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org). PyMOL 3.1 software removed ligands and water molecules. Ligands and receptors were modeled with AutoDock tools and PyMOL, then converted to pdbqt format. AM Dock evaluated binding affinities, where < −7.0 kcal/mol (1 kcal = 4.2 kJ) indicated strong interactions. PyMOL visualized the docking results, with selections based on minimal binding energy and optimal conformations.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software, with results presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). To compare multiple groups and find significant differences, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05 and highly significant at P < 0.01.

Phytochemical analysis of TSS

The total saponin content of TSS was determined by UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy, and the findings indicated that the content exceeded 90% (Fig. S1). By reference to molecular formulas, MS/MS data, and literature, the identification of most triterpenoid saponins was based on their elemental compositions, which were determined through accurate molecular weight measurements and/or fragment ion analysis with mass accuracy within 1.0 × 10−5. The positive and negative base peak intensity chromatograms of TSS were shown in Fig. S2A–B. A total of 17 triterpenoid saponins were identified (Fig. S2C) and listed in Table S1. The HPLC chromatogram of TSS was shown in Fig. S2D. Peak 4 indicates the main component, hederacolchiside A1.

TSS suppresses PC progression in vitro and in vivo

The CCK-8 assay assessed cytotoxicity of TSS on two PC cell lines at varying concentrations for 24 and 48 h. IC50 values for PANC-1 cells were 7.93 ± 0.30 μg/mL (24 h) and 5.76 ± 0.09 μg/mL (48 h), they were 5.69 ± 0.35 μg/mL (24 h) and 3.47 ± 0.13 μg/mL (48 h) for AsPC-1 cells. Results showed significant inhibition of cell viability in a time- and dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). TSS also suppressed cell migration and invasion in a concentration-dependent way (Fig. 1B–E). Furthermore, the colony formation assay indicated that higher TSS concentrations reduced colony formation, with almost none at 16 μg/mL (Fig. 1F–G). TSS induces pyroptosis of PC cells through Caspase-3/GSDME pathway

To explore cell death forms, this study examined changes in cell morphology and found that TSS causes pyroptosis-like changes unexpectedly. It was observed that TSS caused cells to swell, become rounded, and form bubble-like vesicles on their membranes, which are typical features of pyroptosis (Fig. 3A). LDH release tests showed TSS-treated cells had high LDH in the supernatant, indicating membrane damage (Fig. 3B). Inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-18, and TNF-α levels also rose in the high-dose TSS group (Fig. 3C). As necroptosis leads to cell membrane damage, this study introduced inhibitors like Z-VAD-FMK, necrostatin-1, and disulfiram. The results indicated that all three inhibitors partially reversed TSS-induced cytotoxicit, with no significant differences among them (Fig. 3D). These results suggest TSS inhibits PC cell proliferation through multiple cell death pathways. Additionally, intracellular ROS levels increased after TSS treatment, indicating ROS-mediated cell death (Fig. 3E). To confirm the nature of cell death after TSS treatment, flow cytometry and fluorescent staining assays were used. Results showed that Annexin V-PI double-positive staining in PANC-1 cells increased with TSS concentration (Fig. 3F). Inverted fluorescence microscopy also revealed that the number of cells stained with PI rose alongside the increasing concentration of TSS (Fig. 4A). These findings suggest that TSS induces cell death through a process that transitions from apoptosis to pyroptosis. After TSS treatment in PANC-1 cells, this study observed elevated expression of GSDME-N protein in a dose-dependent manner, while GSDMD-N levels remained unchanged. Since Caspase-3 is vital for GSDME-mediated pyroptosis, this study detected its levels and found that TSS could significantly upregulate cleaved Caspase-3. Given that the receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1 (RIPK1)-Caspase-8 axis is an important upstream regulator of Caspase-3 activation, this study further assessed their expression. TSS significantly upregulated Caspase-8 and RIPK1 protein expression, suggesting the involvement of upstream apoptotic signaling (Fig. 4B–C). Non-targeted metabolomics reveals TSS-induced differential metabolites and pyroptosis related metabolic pathways

To explore alterations in TSS-related metabolites in mice, a non-targeted metabolomics analysis of mouse serum was performed. The findings revealed distinct metabolic differences between the Model and TSS groups (Fig. S3A). PCA plots showed metabolite profiles for the different groups, and the OPLS-DA analysis supported by 200 permutation tests highlighted a clear metabolic separation between these groups, supported by Q2 (cum) of 0.718 and R2Y (cum) of 0.999 (Fig. S3B–C). Volcano plots identified 165 significantly differential metabolites, with 89 upregulated and 76 downregulated (Fig. S3D). Fig. S3E displayed some of the more significant differential metabolites, including isoplumbagin, cediranib, gibberellin a 12 aldehyde, malonic semialdehyde, dihydro-3-coumaric acid, colneleic acid, caffeoylcholine, methylthioadenosine, trimethyloxamine, and PC(O-18:1/20:4). These differential metabolites mainly regulate cellular metabolism, induce inflammation, manage oxidative stress, and affect cell proliferation and migration. A heatmap visualized differential metabolite changes among groups (Fig. S3F). In addition, these metabolites were classified by structure and function, with lipid-related metabolites being the most significant (31.61%) (Fig. S3G). Further analysis revealed the top 10 metabolic pathways influenced by TSS (Fig. S3H), including Central carbon metabolism in cancer, Arginine biosynthesis, mTOR signaling pathway, Oxidative phosphorylation, and Glycine, serine and threonine metabolism. Central carbon metabolism (CCM) is the hub of cellular energy and substance synthesis, encompassing four interrelated pathways: glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and glutaminolysis. Pyroptosis is closely regulated by CCM in cancer. Research has demonstrated that cancer-derived succinate can stimulate macrophages through disruption of the TCA cycle and binding to the succinate receptor 1 (SUCNR1) receptor, which can release IL-1β to encourage tumor cell metastasis and indirectly promote pyroptosis (Wu et al., 2020). These results indicate that the differential metabolites and metabolic pathways after TSS treatment are closely related to pyroptosis.

Results showed consistent protein profiles within same groups, confirming reproducibility and validity. Network pharmacology analysis was conducted to determine TSS’s main active components, core targets, and signaling pathways in PC improvement. From GeneCards, OMIM, and DEPs, 5 735 PC-related genes were obtained, and 195 component-related targets were found in the SuperPred database. In Fig. 6F, the intersection identified 28 common TSS targets for PC treatment. A compound-target network via Cytoscape 3.9.1 revealed raddeanoside Ra, raddeanoside 20, and hederacolchiside A1 as significant active components (Fig. 6G). Additionally, combined with HPLC results, hederacolchiside A1 was the most abundant compound in TSS. Therefore, hederacolchiside A1 was considered the main active components in TSS. The core target PPI network indicated TLR4, PIK3R1, and NFKB1 as key targets (Fig. 6H). Raw data were shown in Table S2–S5. GO analysis of core targets showed biological processes (BP) like inflammatory response and regulation of cytosolic Ca2+ concentration. Cellular components (CC) included cytoplasm and plasma membrane roles. Molecular functions (MF) mainly involve protein lysine deacetylase activity and ATP binding (Fig. 6I). KEGG analysis highlighted key pathways such as PI3K-Akt, HIF-1, and NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 6J). Cross-referencing network pharmacology and proteomic results indicated PI3K-Akt and NF-κB pathways are crucial for TSS. Pathway-related proteins like CDK2, PDGFRA, TLR4, PTPN1, NFKB1, and PIK3R1 were also identified as potential key targets.

Molecular docking experiments targeted core component of TSS, hederacolchiside A1, calculating binding energies and visualizing results (Fig. 7A). The docking results revealed that hederacolchiside A1 could bind to all targets with binding energies below −7.0 kcal/mol, especially showing high binding energy interaction with proteins associated with the PI3K-Akt and NF-κB signaling pathways (Fig. 7B–G). Western blotting validation revealed that TSS exerted distinct effects on NF-κB and PI3K/Akt signaling at different concentrations in PANC-1 cells (Fig. 7H–I). At a low concentration (4 µg/mL), TSS increased Akt phosphorylation while suppressing NF-κB activation, accompanied by limited cleaved Caspase-3 activation and incomplete GSDME cleavage, suggesting a sub-threshold response insufficient to fully engage pyroptosis. In contrast, higher concentrations (8, 16 µg/mL) significantly increased NF-κB phosphorylation and decreased PI3K and Akt phosphorylation, along with robust cleaved Caspase-3 activation and extensive GSDME cleavage, ultimately leading to pronounced pyroptotic cell death, consistent with canonical pro-pyroptotic signaling patterns reported in other contexts (Yu et al., 2022). These findings suggest that the anti-tumor effects of TSS are associated with differential modulation of the PI3K/Akt and NF-κB pathways, leading to Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. Discussion

PC remains highly lethal due to late-stage diagnosis and therapy resistance, underscoring the need for new treatment strategies (Espona-Fiedler, Patthey, Lindblad, Sarró, & Öhlund, 2024). Pyroptosis, a programmed inflammatory cell death, has garnered attention for cancer therapy. In contrast to canonical inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis, GSDME-mediated pyroptosis is activated by Caspase-3 (Rao et al., 2022) and is notably upregulated in PC tissues, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target (Li et al., 2023). While chemotherapy drugs can induce pyroptosis via the Caspase-3/GSDME pathway (Wei et al., 2022), the ability of natural compounds such as TSS from A. raddeana to trigger this process remains largely unexplored. Previous studies on triterpenoid saponins have mainly focused on apoptosis, leaving their role in pyroptosis, particularly in PC, poorly understood.

This study demonstrates for the first time that TSS inhibits PC growth by inducing Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis through modulation of PI3K/Akt and NF-κB pathways. These findings provide compelling evidence that TSS could serve as a novel natural product for PC therapy, especially for patients with high GSDME expression. Integrative multi-omics analysis revealed a convergent mechanism underlying TSS-induced pyroptosis. Proteomics showed significant activation of NOD-like receptor signaling and inflammatory response pathways, consistent with downstream cytokine network engagement. Mechanistically, NOD1/2 recruit RIP2 to activate inhibitor of kappa B kinase (IKK)-NF-κB, priming inflammatory effectors such as pro-IL-1β (Hasegawa et al., 2008). In parallel, network pharmacology identified PI3K-Akt and NF-κB as central pathways targeted by key TSS constituents. The PI3K-Akt pathway regulates cell growth and proliferation, and its inhibition boosts cleaved Caspase-3, enhancing GSDME cleavage (Yu et al., 2022). The NF-κB pathway has dual roles in pyroptosis. It not only has anti-apoptotic effects but also induces the expression of pro-inflammatory factors and GSDME genes, thereby producing bidirectional regulation of pyroptosis. Research has shown that NF-κB can bind to the promoter region of the GSDME gene, directly promoting its expression (Wang et al., 2017). Untargeted metabolomics provided complementary evidence, with metabolites, such as methylthioadenosine and isoplumbagin, and enriched pathways, such as central carbon metabolism in cancer, mTOR signaling, and oxidative phosphorylation, all reflecting disturbed redox and mitochondrial function (Tsao, Chang, Wang, & Chen, 2020). These metabolic shifts promote ROS accumulation and mitochondrial membrane permeabilization, creating upstream signals that facilitate gasdermin pore formation. Collectively, these findings support a unified mechanistic axis whereby TSS perturbs metabolic–inflammatory homeostasis and activates the NOD (NOD1/2)-RIP2/IKK-NF-κB signaling cascade, which, together with PI3K-Akt modulation and enhanced ROS production, promotes Caspase-3 cleavage of GSDME, ultimately driving pyroptotic cell death.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Although in vitro and in vivo models confirmed the anti-tumor role of TSS, its translational relevance remains uncertain without clinical validation. Moreover, while multi-omics provided a broad mechanistic map, the specific contributions of individual metabolites and targets require further verification. To address this, the follow-up work will prioritize biological validation of top-ranked metabolites through independent replication, perturbation of key enzymes and pathways, metabolite supplementation/depletion, and isotope tracing to establish causal links between metabolic changes and Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis.

Clinically, these findings suggest that targeting Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for PC, particularly for patients with high GSDME expression or chemotherapy resistance. As a natural product with multi-target activity, TSS holds promise as an integrative anti-cancer candidate. Future work should focus on its pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy in clinical trials to fully assess its therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

This study confirms the effect of TSS on PC and uncovers its mechanisms using multi-omics and network pharmacology. It shows TSS inhibits PC growth in vitro and in vivo by modulating PI3K/Akt and NF-κB pathways to induce Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis, suggesting TSS is promising for developing novel therapeutic strategies against PC.
