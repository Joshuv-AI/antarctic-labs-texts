PD-1 and PD-L1 expression in molecular subtypes of muscle-invasive bladder cancer: immunohistochemical characterization and correlation with clinicopathological features
Authors: Farhang Hooshmand, Maral Mokhtari, Shiva Aminnia, Azin Dashtestani, Ali Reza Rezvani, Mohammadhossein Khorraminejad-Shirazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMuscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) is a heterogeneous disease with variable outcomes, necessitating practical classification systems. Molecular subtyping using immunohistochemical (IHC) markers offers a cost-effective approach for therapeutic guidance and assessing survival. Moreover, MIBC molecular subclassification provides a practical approach for guiding immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy.MethodsWe evaluated 124 MIBC cases using IHC markers GATA3, CK5/6, and p16. Cases were classified as luminal (GATA3+, CK5/6-), basal (GATA3-, CK5/6+), or other (GATA3-, CK5/6-). Luminal cases were further subdivided into luminal unstable (LumU; p16+) and luminal papillary (LumP; p16-). Clinicopathological characteristics of MIBC molecular subtypes were also assessed. PD-1 and PD-L1 expression were evaluated relative to clinicopathological features and MIBC subtypes.ResultsIn our study, 36.2% of the cases were LumU, 27.6% LumP, and 24.8% basal. The basal subtype generally shows a significantly higher tumor stage (p < 0.05). PD-1 was expressed in 70.5% of cases, with the highest expression in LumU (84.21%). PD-1 expression was significantly higher in the luminal compared to the basal subtype (82.1% vs. 53.8%, p < 0.01). PD-L1, expressed in 40% of cases, was significantly elevated in stage III and considerably higher in basal than luminal subtype (57.7% vs. 34.3%, p < 0.05).ConclusionMIBCs were practically subclassified into LumU, LumP, basal, and other subtypes using three IHC markers. PD-1 expression was higher in the luminal subtype, while PD-L1 was predominantly elevated in the basal subtype. These findings highlight the potential of IHC-based subtyping to guide prognosis and treatment in MIBCs.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Immunohistochemistry, PD-1, PD-L1, Immune checkpoint inhibitor

Background

Bladder cancer (BC) remains a significant global burden and ranks as the seventh most frequently diagnosed cancer globally in 2020 [1]. Although bladder carcinomas show some histological diversity, most BCs are of urothelial differentiation, which can be further classified as low-grade non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) [2]. MIBC, representing about 30% of all BC cases, is distinguished by its high metastatic potential and mortality rate [3].

MIBCs are a group of molecularly heterogeneous diseases that could be classified into subtypes with distinct clinical courses and sensitivities to chemotherapy [4]. Several studies have been directed toward creating a molecular classification system for bladder urothelial carcinoma to predict patient prognosis and guide new therapeutic approaches. As a result, various molecular classifications for urothelial bladder carcinomas have emerged during the last decade [5]. These molecular classifications are generally based on transcriptomic profiles, generating highly diverse categories with limited correlation. However, in clinical practice, using whole transcriptome profiling for all patients is typically limited due to the high complexity of the required technology, the elevated costs, and the limited availability of this technology worldwide [6, 7] Subtyping bladder tumors using a small number of immune markers is regarded as an alternative for clinical settings because immunohistochemistry (IHC) studies are readily accessible and reliable techniques that could be incorporated into pathologists’ everyday practice and provide a more cost-effective approach.

IHC-based subtyping of bladder tumors using a small number of immune markers is considered an alternative in clinical settings. Recent studies offered a practical and reliable method to classify MIBC into distinct molecular subtypes by using a panel of markers like CK 5/6, GATA3, and p16. These markers show distinct expression patterns in basal and luminal subtypes of MIBC that are associated with different clinical behaviors and therapeutic responses [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

Current treatment of MIBCs is neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy or chemoradiation with cystectomy [12]. In unresponsive cases to conventional treatments, there is growing interest in using immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Anti-programmed cell death 1 (PD1) and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) are now considered a part of the standard of care for patients with locally advanced or metastatic urothelial cancer [13, 14]. However, in unselected patients, the response rate of urothelial carcinoma to PD1 and PD-L1 inhibitors is only 15–21% [15]. Subsequently, these suboptimal response rates, along with ICIs adverse effects, substantially limit their clinical use [16]. Evaluating the PD-L1 expression in tumor or tumor-infiltrating immune cells has been used to predict ICI response for several tumor types [17]. However, the expression of PD-L1 alone is inadequate to identify all responsive patients. Moreover, there are several clones of PD-L1 for use in IHC studies that could change the patient selection [17, 18]. As a result, there is a crucial need to find markers to accurately identify the responsive patient group that will benefit from ICIs.

In this study, we intended to classify MIBC cases into distinct molecular subtypes using the IHC markers GATA3, CK5/6, and p16 and assess the association of different demographics and clinicopathological features with these molecular subtypes. Furthermore, we investigated the expression of ICI markers, PD-1 and PD-L1, in different molecular subtypes of MIBC.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

We conducted a cross-sectional study of patients who underwent transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURB) and radical cystectomy for urothelial carcinoma (UC) at Namazi Hospital, Shiraz, Iran. Data were collected retrospectively from 2020 to 2023. Inclusion criteria were cases with a primary diagnosis of MIBC (≥ pT2) and patients with no previous therapy other than the surgical procedure. Patients with metastatic BC and cases with inadequate tissue samples for immunohistochemical study were excluded. Patient clinical data were collected from hospital medical records. This study was approved by the ethical review committee of the Jahrom University of Medical Sciences (IR.JUMS.REC.1402.111). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of the study.

Histopathological evaluation

Two experienced pathologists reviewed the Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)–stained sections to confirm the diagnosis of MIBC. All relevant pathological data, including histologic subtype, lymph node involvement, tumor extension, presence of lymphovascular and perineural invasions, and tumor stage, were recorded for each case according to the World Health Organization grading system 2022 [19] and AJCC Cancer Staging Manual (8th edition) [20]. For each case, one H&E slide with its corresponding formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue block was selected for immunohistochemical staining.

Immunohistochemical study

For each case, a section was taken from the tissue block and placed in the peroxidase-blocking reagent for 10 min and then incubated with primary antibodies against GATA3 (Medac-diagnostika, Cat# MAD-000632Q ), CK5/6 (Medac-diagnostika, Cat# MAD-000651Q ), p16 (1:100, Medac-diagnostika, Cat# MAD-000690Q), PD1(1:50, clone NAT 105, Medac-diagnostika, Cat# MAD-000671Q), and PD-L1 (1:50, clone CAL10, Medac-diagnostika, Cat# MAD-000740Q) with appropriate positive and negative controls. Next, slides will be incubated with horseradish peroxidase polymer reagent and then diaminobenzidine chromogen. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin.

An expert pathologist evaluated the prepared IHC slides, and the cut-off threshold for each marker was determined according to previously published recommended cutoffs [8, 10]. GATA3 and CK 5/6 were considered positive if ≥ 20% of tumor cells exhibited diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic/membranous staining, while p16 was considered positive if ≥ 70% of tumor cells showed strong and diffuse nuclear/cytoplasmic staining. Subsequently, MIBC cases were classified as luminal (GATA3+, CK 5/6 -), basal (GATA3 -, CK 5/6 +), and other (GATA3+, CK 5/6 -) subtypes. Depending on the p16 expression, luminal cases were further classified as luminal unstable (LumU) (p16 +) and luminal papillary (LumP) (p16 -).

Finally, PD-1 and PD-L1 expression were evaluated among the mentioned MIBC molecular subtypes, e.g., LumU, LumP, basal, and other subtypes. PD-L1 was assessed using a Combined Positive Score (CPS), calculated as the number of PD-L1–positive tumor and immune cells with cytoplasmic/membranous staining pattern divided by the total number of viable tumor cells. PD-1 score was recorded as the percentage of membranous stained mononuclear immune cells. Both 1% and 10% cut-offs were used for PD-1 and PD-L1 to compare the results.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Quantitative data are displayed as frequencies and percentages. To compare differences between categorical variables, the Chi-Square test was used, and when appropriate, Fisher’s exact test was employed as a non-parametric test. All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

PD-1 and PD-L1 expression across MIBC molecular subtypes

PD-1 expression

When comparing the expression of PD-1 between the four molecular MIBC subtypes (Fig. 2), a statistically significant difference was noted with both the 1% and 10% positivity cut-offs (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively).

Positive PD-1 expression in infiltrating immune cells (black arrow) and tumoral cells (black star). The positive cytoplasmic/membranous staining pattern of PD-L1 is also displayed

At the 1% positive cut-off, the LumU subtype displayed the highest PD-1 expression (84.21%), followed by the LumP subtype (79.31%) and the basal subtype (53.85%). When comparing luminal and basal subtypes, PD-1 expression was markedly higher in the luminal group (82.1% vs. 53.8%, p < 0.01). Across the luminal subtypes, the expression of PD-1 was higher in the LumU than the LumP (84.21% vs. 79.31%); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.21).

Similarly, with a 10% positivity cut-off, the highest PD-1 expression was seen in the LumU subtype (81.58%), followed by LumP (65.52%) and basal (34.62%) subtypes. The difference in PD-1 expression across luminal and basal subtypes remained statistically significant (74.6% vs. 34.6%, P < 0.001). Within the luminal subtypes, although PD-1 expression was higher in LumU than in LumP (81.58% vs. 65.52%), this difference was not statistically significant (p-value = 0.07) (Table 4).

PD-1 and PD-L1 expression in MIBC molecular subtypes

PD-L1 expression

PD-L1 expression varied across the four MIBC subtypes (Fig. 2). However, when analyzed collectively, PD-L1 expression did not show statistically significant differences among the four MIBC subtypes at either the 1% or 10% positivity cut-offs (P = 0.14 and P = 0.44, respectively). At the 1% positive cut-off, the highest PD-L1 expression was seen in the basal subtype (57.70%), and the lowest was in the LumP subtype (27.59%).

To further explore the PD-L1 pattern of expression, pairwise comparisons between specific subtypes were performed. When comparing specifically the luminal and basal subtypes, PD-L1 expression was significantly higher in the basal subtype (57.70% vs. 34.3%, p < 0.05). Moreover, when comparing LumP and basal subtypes specifically, PD-L1 expression was significantly higher in the basal subtype (57.70% vs. 27.6%, p-value = 0.024). However, this trend was not observed when comparing only the LumU and basal subtypes (57.70% vs. 39.5%, p-value = 0.15). While LumU shows an elevated PD-L1 expression, there was no significant difference between LumU and LumP subtypes (39.47% vs. 27.59%, p-value = 0.125).

With a 10% positivity cut-off, a similar trend in the expression of PD-L1 was observed. The basal subtype displayed the highest expression of PD-L1 (38.46%), and there was no statistically significant difference between the expression of PD-L1 among luminal and basal subtypes (29.9% vs. 38.5%, p-value = 0.140). Likewise, no significance was found between LumU and LumP subtypes (34.21% vs. 24.14%, p-value = 0.146) (Table 4).

Discussion

BC is a molecularly heterogeneous group of diseases with diverse tumor morphologies that influence treatment strategies and outcomes. In this study, using IHC markers GATA3, CK5/6, and p16, we stratified 124 patients with MIBC into LumU, LumP, basal, and other (double negative) subtypes and compared their clinicopathologic features. We also analyzed PD-1 and PD-L1 expression patterns based on clinicopathologic and MIBC molecular classifications.

Luminal and basal subtypes of bladder carcinoma are the two primary molecular groups with unique clinical behavior and treatment responses. A 2016 meta-analysis of 937 BC samples showed that just two markers, GATA3 for luminal and CK 5/6 for basal, can identify molecular subtypes with over 90% accuracy [11]. Subsequent studies have validated this approach [13, 21]. Based on this evidence, we used GATA3 and CK5/6 to classify luminal and basal subtypes of MIBCs. To address luminal subtype heterogeneity, we included p16 to identify the more aggressive unstable luminal subtype, as supported by the Lund classification and other studies [8, 22, 23, 24]. Most MIBCs in our study were classified as luminal (63.8%), while 24.8% were basal. In concordance with our results, Hodgson et al. classified their MIBC samples using GATA3 and CK 5/6, reporting 85.2% luminal and 14.8% basal subtypes. Their study also suggested a trend toward worse cancer-specific survival in the basal subtype [25]. While survival outcomes were not assessed in our study, basal subtypes were diagnosed at more advanced stages, potentially impacting prognosis and survival.

Furthermore, 36.2% of the cases in the current study were classified as LumU and 27.6% as LumP. Similarly, Hesswani et al. used the same markers to classify tumor tissues of 104 patients into 31.2% as urothelial-like (GATA+, P16-) and 14.0% as unstable [24]. Other studies also show similar results with these three IHC markers, further validating their use for MIBC classification [13, 26, 27]. Additionally, we showed that LumU cases showed a more aggressive phenotype, with higher tumor extension and stage than the LumP subtype. However, these differences between Luminal subtypes were not significant in our study, most probably due to the low sample number in these two subtypes. Further studies are needed to compare the phenotype, prognosis, and survival between luminal subtypes.

The BC microenvironment promotes immune tolerance and immune evasion through mechanisms like PD-1 and PD-L1 expression [28]. Consequently, ICIs have shown promising efficacy in treating advanced BC. Assessing PD-1 and PD-L1 expression in the tumor microenvironment might be a useful approach to predicting the effectiveness of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies [15]. Here, we found high PD-1 expression (70.5%) and PD-L1 expression (38.1%) in the MIBC microenvironment, affirming BC’s immunogenicity. Also, we showed significantly increased PD-L1 expression in MIBC cases with higher tumor extension and stage. Parallel to our results, Goutas et al. (2021) observed higher PD-L1 expression in stage pT2 and high-grade tumors [5]. Similarly, Li et al. reported higher PD-L1 scores in high-grade invasive urothelial carcinoma and increased PD-1 in carcinoma in situ compared to more aggressive BC [15]. In contrast, Kumar et al. found only 32.7% PD-1 positivity in 116 cases of BC using NAT105 clones with higher PD-1 expression in high-grade and muscle-invasive tumors [18]. Kawahara et al. also noted higher PD-1 expression in higher-grade BCs [29]. These discrepancies might arise from different antibody clones, intra-sample diversity, and positive cut-offs in each study.

In line with previous studies, we reported significantly higher PD-L1 expression in the basal subtype of MIBC. In a study by Goutas et al., they found higher expression of PD-L1 in the basal compared to the luminal subtype [5]. Since the basal subtype appears to have more aggressive clinical behavior, higher PD-L1 expression may indicate a greater ability of these tumors to evade the immune system and potentially make them the candidate group for ICI-based therapies. Notably, when comparing within the luminal subtypes, we reported a higher, but not statistically significant, PD-L1 expression in the LumU compared to the LumP group. Furthermore, our data showed significantly higher PD-1 expression in the luminal subtype, particularly in LumU. As discussed, the heterogeneity in PD-1 expression as an important immune checkpoint regulator protein and its relationship to clinicopathological features and ICI responsiveness [30] underscores the necessity of reassessing this marker in BC. These findings warrant further research on MIBC molecular subtypes in a larger population.

Our study faces several limitations. First, the heterogeneity in IHC methodology, especially different primary antibody clones used in the IHC staining, might influence the results. Additionally, we did not assess survival outcomes; instead, we focused on expression patterns across MIBC molecular subtypes and clinicopathologic features. Also, a larger sample population is needed to compare expression patterns among different MIBC molecular subtypes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, using only three IHC markers, we classified MIBCs into four distinct molecular subtypes, including LumP, LumU, basal, and other subtypes. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to report higher PD-1 expression in the luminal subtypes, e.g., LumP and LumU subtypes. Furthermore, we reported significantly elevated PD-L1 expression in the basal subtype. Our findings highlight the potential of IHC-based subtyping to provide valuable molecular classification for MIBC, enabling more accurate insights into diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in routine clinicopathological settings.
