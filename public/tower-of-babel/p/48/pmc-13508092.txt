Design, Synthesis, and Applications of Fluorescent Amino Acids in Bioimaging
Authors: Yanyang Zhao, Xu Wang, Lan Wang, Marc Vendrell, Huan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluorescent unnatural amino acids (FlAAs) provide a powerful molecular bridge between small-molecule fluorophores and biomacromolecules, enabling minimally perturbative optical interrogation of peptides and proteins. Over the past decade, substantial progress has been made in the rational design, synthesis, and application of FlAAs, driven by advances in organic synthesis, C–H functionalization, and translation-based incorporation technologies. This review summarizes contemporary strategies for constructing FlAAs, with a particular emphasis on structure–property relationships and modular design principles. Beyond chemical synthesis, we summarize translation-machinery-mediated strategies, including chemical tRNA aminoacylation, flexizyme-based charging, and genetic code expansion, which collectively enable the incorporation of FlAAs into peptides and proteins in vitro and in living cells. Finally, we showcase representative applications of FlAAs in live-cell imaging, super-resolution microscopy, and photodynamic therapy, illustrating how compact fluorescent amino acids can serve as both imaging reporters and functional photoactive units. Together, this review summarizes current molecular design strategies for FlAAs and discusses how chemical modification of amino acid scaffolds can be linked to specific biological applications.

Introduction

Fluorescence-based methods are widely used to study biomolecules and biological processes, with applications including disease diagnosis, molecular interaction analysis, and monitoring of dynamic biological events. − These approaches enable visualization of molecular localization, interactions, and activity with high spatial and temporal resolution, and are routinely employed in chemical biology and molecular imaging. A central consideration in fluorescent probe design is achieving precise spatial labeling while minimizing perturbation to molecular structure and function. This balance has motivated the development of labeling strategies that integrate optical functionality directly into biomolecular building blocks.

FlAAs provide a means to introduce fluorescence at defined positions within peptides and proteins. By modifying individual amino acid residues, FlAAs allow site-specific labeling without reliance on bulky fluorophore conjugates or fluorescent protein tags. This sequence-level integration minimizes perturbation to local structure and interactions, which is particularly important for peptides and small proteins that are sensitive to labeling geometry and probe size. These features make FlAAs well suited for peptide- and protein-based imaging and functional studies.

Recent years have seen steady progress in the development of FlAAs, driven by advances in molecular design, synthetic chemistry, and incorporation technologies. Structural modification of aromatic amino acids, including tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine, has enabled tuning of photophysical properties, while late-stage functionalization and modular conjugation strategies have expanded the diversity of accessible FlAA scaffolds. In parallel, genetic code expansion and in vitro translation methods have facilitated their site-specific incorporation into peptides and proteins. This Review summarizes recent design and synthesis strategies for FlAAs, highlights representative applications in peptide-based imaging, super-resolution microscopy, and photoactive systems, and briefly discusses current incorporation approaches and remaining challenges.

Design and Synthesis of Fluorescent Unnatural Amino Acids

Structural Fine-Tuning Strategies Based on Tryptophan

FlAA design generally focuses on modifying aromatic side chains to tune their electronic properties, thereby improving fluorescence brightness, photostability, and sensitivity to environmental changes. ,, Current strategies to construct FlAAs with improved photophysical properties include electronic tuning, π-conjugation extension, and de novo chromophore construction.

Among the canonical amino acids, tryptophan (Trp) is an optimal starting point for FLAA development due to the unique electronic properties of its indole chromophore. Classical strategies follow the “fine structural perturbation” principle, maintaining the natural scaffold of Trp while modulating its electronic structure to enhance brightness, photostability, and spectral accessibility. Introducing small electron-donating or electron-withdrawing substituents (e.g., cyano, amino, hydroxy, or halogen groups) onto the indole ring enables modulation of the HOMO–LUMO gap and excited-state charge-transfer characteristics, resulting in enhanced fluorescence and microenvironment sensitivity (Figure A). ,

From the perspective of electronic transitions, the lowest-energy excitation of indole involves partial charge transfer from the pyrrole to the benzene ring. As a result, electron-withdrawing substituents on the benzene moiety are particularly effective in tuning the excitation energy. Cyanotryptophans exemplify this effect: installation of a single cyano group produces red-shifted absorption, enhanced quantum yield, and strong solvent sensitivity. Among them, 4-CN-Trp (4) − is the most favorable blue-emitting amino acid, displaying λem ≈ 420 nm and λabs up to 325 nm, and a remarkably high quantum yield of 0.88 in waterfar exceeding that of native Trp (0.14). Related derivatives such as 6- and 7-CN-Trp also show improved quantum yields (∼0.53 and ∼0.40), though their spectral enhancements are less pronounced, underscoring the exceptional electronic sensitivity of the indole C4 position. Consistent with these observations, structure–property analyses identify C4 substitution as the most effective handle for modulating transition energy due to the substantial electron-density redistribution occurring at this site upon excitation.

Despite their favorable photophysical properties, many finely tuned Trp derivatives rely on de novo indole synthesis and multistep amino-acid construction, which severely limits accessibility and structural diversification. These challenges have in turn driven the development of late-stage and site-selective strategies for direct diversification of Trp residues without rebuilding the amino acid scaffold.

Modification of Tryptophan Side Chain for Fluorescence Enhancement

Recent advances in C–H activation chemistry have enabled unprecedented late-stage diversification of the Trp indole side chain, providing a powerful platform for fine-tuning its photophysical properties. − Regioselective functionalization at the C2, C4, and C7 positions has been achieved, allowing the installation of diverse substituents through alkylation, − alkenylation, − alkynylation, , arylation, amidation, and borylation reactions (Figure B). These transformations substantially expand the structural and electronic space of Trp-based fluorophores beyond what is accessible through classical derivatization strategies.

The indole ring of Trp contains multiple C–H bonds with comparable bond dissociation energies and generally low intrinsic reactivity, rendering site-selective functionalization intrinsically challenging. As a result, high regioselectivity generally relies on the use of directing groups installed at either the α-amine or the indole N1 position to override innate reactivity and impose catalyst control. Commonly employed auxiliaries include triflyl (Tf), pyrimidyl (pym), pivaloyl (Piv), and phosphine-based PtBu2 groups.

Among the available positions, the C2–H bond exhibits the lowest bond dissociation energy and was therefore the first to be selectively addressed under basic or transition-metal-catalyzed conditions. From a photophysical design perspective, extension of π-conjugation, typically via arylation, alkenylation, , or alkynylation, is an effective strategy for fluorescence enhancement. Increased conjugation reduces the HOMO–LUMO gap, red-shifts optical transitions, and improves brightness by stabilizing the excited state. Ackermann and co-workers developed a Ru­(II)-catalyzed C2 arylation of Trp using pyridine-based directing groups and aryl bromides as coupling partners, enabling the direct installation of aromatic substituents onto the indole core (Figure C). The resulting C2-arylated Trp derivatives show significantly extended π-conjugation, with emission wavelengths reaching ∼485 nm (9) and fluorescence intensities enhanced by up to 20-fold relative to native Trp. Complementary to arylation strategies, Zhu and co-workers developed a Rh-catalyzed, directing-group-assisted C2 alkenylation of Trp using pyridine or pyrimidine auxiliaries, affording derivatives with markedly enhanced fluorescence, including a red-shifted emission (∼460 nm) and over 10-fold intensity increase relative to native Trp.

Building on the expanding repertoire of Trp modification chemistries, Wang and co-workers integrated complementary site-selective strategies to access substituted Trp derivatives (Figure D), uncovering synergistic photophysical enhancements from combinations such as C2/C7 and C4/C7 modifications. In particular, sequential C2/C7 arylation followed by C4 alkenylation enabled the construction of a trisubstituted Trp analogue with substantially red-shifted absorption and emission (λabs ≈ 460 nm; λem ≈ 500 nm), representing a level of spectral red shift unattainable through single-site or mild electronic perturbations alone.

Beyond static spectral tuning, multisite substitution not only systematically improved the intrinsic fluorescence of Trp derivatives but also dramatically amplified their sensitivity to the microenvironment. For example, C2/C4-alkenylated Trp derivatives exhibit pronounced environment-responsive fluorescence, with emission intensity increasing sharply as the proportion of hydrophobic solvents (e.g., cyclohexane) rises. In monosubstituted Trp analogues, the introduction of an alkenyl group already significantly enhances fluorescence sensitivity toward solvent polarity. Iterative installation of multiple substituents further strengthens this effect, leading to highly amplified and nonlinear responses to hydrophobic environments. A representative example is compound 12, in which combined C4 alkenylation and C7 arylation result in a dramatic fluorescence “turn-on” behavior. Specifically, the fluorescence intensity of compound 12 in cyclohexane is 296-fold higher than that observed in aqueous solution (Figure D). This enhancement illustrates the synergistic advantage of multisite Trp functionalization for amplifying environment-sensitive fluorescence signals and for rationally tuning the optical behavior of the indole side chain. Collectively, these advances highlight how functionalization transforms Trp from a merely “tunable” residue into a genuinely “programmable” scaffold, providing a robust chemical foundation for modular fluorophore engineering.

Fluorescent Unnatural Amino Acids Based on Phenylalanine and Tyrosine

Although tryptophan is the most extensively explored scaffold for FlAA development, phenylalanine (Phe) and tyrosine (Tyr) provide complementary platforms for fluorescence engineering. Both lack intrinsic visible fluorescence and possess simpler aromatic side chains than Trp, making their native photophysical properties less favorable while offering chemically modular starting points for fluorescence tuning through electronic perturbation, π-extension, or scaffold rigidification (Figure ).

Modification of phenylalanine and tyrosine for fluorescence enhancement. (A) Chemical structures and basic spectral properties of fluorescent analogues of phenylalanine. (B) Chemical structures and basic spectral properties of fluorescent analogues of tyrosine.

Native phenylalanine exhibits weak ultraviolet fluorescence with a low quantum yield (Φ < 0.05) and poor photostability. Accordingly, phenylalanine-based fluorescent unnatural amino acids (FlAAs) have been developed primarily through electronic modulation and π-conjugation extension. Among minimally modified analogues, p-cyanophenylalanine (4-CN-Phe) (14) displays a quantum yield approximately 5-fold higher than that of native Phe, with excitation around 240 nm and emission near 300 nm. , The cyano substituent introduces charge-transfer character, rendering fluorescence sensitive to local polarity and hydrogen-bonding environments; positional isomers (2- and 3-CN-Phe) exhibit similar absorption and emission profiles with modest variations in quantum yield. ,

More pronounced photophysical enhancement is achieved by extending the aromatic framework of phenylalanine. Palladium-catalyzed cross-coupling reactions afford biphenyl-, terphenyl-, and polyaromatic Phe analogues with absorption shifted beyond 300 nm and emission extending into the visible region. Increased π-conjugation and chromophore rigidification markedly improve brightness, as exemplified by terphenyl-extended phenylalanine derivatives with quantum yields approaching ∼0.7. Further extension through alkene conjugation leads to bathochromic shifts in both absorption and emission, as exemplified by stilbene-extended Phe analogues displaying emission around 350 nm and quantum yields of ∼0.05 to 0.07, representing a clear enhancement over native phenylalanine. Insertion of an alkyne spacer between aryl units affords arylalkynyl phenylalanine derivatives with absorption up to ∼330 to 340 nm and emission around 340–360 nm, with representative 1-naphthylalkynyl analogues reaching quantum yields of ∼0.65 and brightness values exceeding 1.5 × 104 M–1 cm–1.

Native tyrosine similarly exhibits weak intrinsic fluorescence and strong environmental sensitivity, limiting its utility as a standalone fluorescent reporter. Early Tyr analogues, including nitro- and fluoro-substituted derivatives, display negligible emission but function as efficient quenchers of aromatic fluorophores through spectral overlap and electron-transfer pathways. In contrast, effective fluorescence enhancement has been achieved by covalent modification or annulation of the phenolic core to generate rigid, π-extended chromophores. Representative tyrosine-based FlAAsincluding benzothiazole-, dibenzofuran-, and stilbene-derived scaffolds exhibit absorption maxima in the near-UV to visible range (typically >300 nm) and emission spanning the visible region, in some cases extending toward the red or near-infrared. Concomitantly, conjugation extension and structural rigidification lead to substantial increases in fluorescence efficiency, with reported quantum yields reaching ∼0.3 to 0.6, markedly higher than that of native tyrosine.

Modular Construction of Amino Acid–Fluorophore Conjugates

While Trp-based electronic tuning and single/multisite modifications offer powerful means to enhance fluorescence, a complementary and increasingly important direction involves the development of modular frameworks to conjugate classical fluorophores onto amino acids. These modular strategies seek to maximize structural diversity and synthetic generality by employing chemical reactions capable of coupling fluorophores to a wide range of amino-acid side-chain functionalities, including Cys–SH, Lys–NH2, Tyr–OH, Asp/Glu–COOH, and even inert C–H bonds. Representative reaction types include: (1) Nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr), applicable to highly electron-deficient fluorophores such as NBD and coumarin, enabling conjugation to Cys and Lys; (2) Transition-metal-catalyzed coupling, allowing direct installation of BODIPY, NBD, or aromatic fragments onto Trp or Cys positions; (3) Alkenyl/alkynyl coupling reactions; (4) Photochemical conjugation. This “multi-position, multi-reaction, multi-chromophore” toolbox transforms FlAAs from Trp-centric molecules into a generalizable platform covering different amino acids and creates new opportunities for peptide-based probe design.

The construction of FlAAs requires consideration across three design levels: (1) Physicochemical consequences of conjugation: whether the fluorophore increases steric bulk or hydrophobicity to a degree that disrupts peptide structure or function. (2) Photophysical consequences of linkage mode: the amino acid or linker acts as a substituent on the fluorophore, modulating its absorption/emission profile, quantum yield, and brightness; therefore, electronic coupling and conjugation patterns must be considered rather than simply “attaching a dye.” (3) Compatibility with peptide synthesis: fluorophores must either withstand the acidic and basic conditions of SPPS or be installable through mild and robust postsynthetic labeling reactions. These considerations collectively define the design space for FlAAs and underscore why different conjugation chemistries can lead to distinct structural and photophysical outcomes.

Direct conjugation strategies offer practical advantages in applications that require compact FlAAs. By forming a direct bond between the fluorophore and the amino acid side chain, this approach minimizes overall molecular size and structural perturbation, a feature particularly valuable when incorporating FlAAs into peptide probes where maintaining native peptide conformation, binding, or cellular uptake is essential. Because different amino-acid side chains show widely varied intrinsic reactivity, such “minimal-labeling” designs are most commonly applied to nucleophilic or regioselectively modifiable residues, with cysteine being the dominant example. A representative example is provided by Vendrell and co-workers, who developed a modular strategy for directly conjugating nitrobenzodiazole (NBD) fluorophores to amino-acid side chains. Starting from commercially available 2-fluoro-1,2-phenylenediamine, the bridging heteroatom was first introduced via selenium dioxide–mediated oxidation, followed by acid-promoted nitration to generate a fluoronitrobenzodiazole intermediate. This activated NBD scaffold, then underwent nucleophilic aromatic substitution with either Boc-L-diaminopropionic acid (Dapa) or Boc-l-cysteine (Cys) to afford directly heteroatom-linked conjugates (Figure A). Notably, this synthetic route is readily adaptable to different NBD derivatives bearing sulfur, selenium, or carbon linkages (S/C/Se), enabling the preparation of a diverse set of Dapa- and Cys-based FlAAs modified with distinct NBD variants.

NBD-based probes feature a pronounced push–pull electronic structure and are highly sensitive to substitution at the 4- and 7-positions, such that introducing different atoms at these sites can markedly modulate their photophysical properties. , Consistent with this principle, the Cys-NBD conjugates obtained through direct linkage exhibit pronounced bathochromic shifts in both absorption and emission compared with the parent NBD fluorophore (λabs ≈ 460 nm, λem ≈ 534 nm). Among these derivatives, Cys-(Se-NBD) 23 (λabs ≈ 485 nm, λem ≈ 600 nm) displays improved optical and chemical stability relative to its oxygen-linked analogue 22, whereas the carbon-linked Cys-(C-NBD) 24 shows an even more substantial red shift, with λabs ≈ 540 nm and λem ≈ 652 nm. These results clearly illustrate how direct conjugation, combined with heteroatom variation, provides a powerful means to fine-tune the spectral properties of compact FlAAs without introducing bulky linkers.

Moreover, the rapidly expanding Trp C–H functionalization allows direct Trp-fluorophore conjugation. Beyond serving as a site for further chemical modification, the indole ring functions as an electron-rich substituent capable of modulating the electronic properties of the attached fluorophore. Leveraging these features, Vendrell and co-workers employed Pd-catalyzed C2 arylation to couple BODIPY cores directly onto the Trp indole, affording compact Trp–BODIPY amino acids 25 with λabs ≈ 501 nm and λem ≈ 515 nm that reflect the limited electronic coupling between the two π-systems. , To overcome the short-wavelength limitations of Trp–BODIPY constructs, Vendrell and co-workers developed Apotracker Red 26 (λem ≈ 600 nm), optimized for multiphoton excitation (∼1070 nm). The scope was further expanded by substituting Trp with Phe, yielding methoxy-phenyl BODIPY–Phe derivatives 27 (λem ≈ 620 nm) that support high-contrast, wash-free live-cell imaging. Recognizing that classical BODIPY scaffolds are unstable under strongly acidic conditions such as trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), which hampers solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS), the Vendrell group introduced Trp-BODIPY PLUS 28, the first TFA-resistant BODIPY amino acid that is compatible with standard SPPS workflows.

Although direct fluorophore to amino acid coupling is conceptually straightforward and yields highly compact FlAAs, limitations in fluorophore reactivity, stability, and synthetic compatibility have driven the development of linker-based modular strategies. When direct conjugation proves synthetically demanding or incompatible with fluorophore integrity, linker-mediated conjugation using more reactive functional handles provides an important and versatile alternative (Figure B). Importantly, linkers do not merely resolve reactivity mismatches; they also function as tunable substituents that modulate fluorophore photophysics, including absorption/emission wavelengths, quantum yields, and environmental responsiveness. A commonly adopted strategy involves the use of fluorophores such as BODIPY or NBD functionalized with activated alkyne handles, which can undergo conjugation with amino-acid side chains (e.g., Trp) under mild, fluorophore-compatible conditions. The resulting alkenyl- or alkynyl-linked FlAAs often exhibit optimized optical properties as a direct consequence of linker-induced electronic effects. For example, Vendrell and co-workers synthesized a series of alkene- and alkyne-linked FlAAs, all of which displayed pronounced bathochromic shifts relative to their directly conjugated counterparts. Among these derivatives, alkenyl-linked Trp–BODIPY 30 conjugates showed the strongest sensitivity to microenvironment polarity, consistent with earlier observations that alkenyl substitution on Trp enhances environment-responsive fluorescence. These results suggest that alkenyl linkers simultaneously amplify environmental sensitivity in both the Trp indole and the appended fluorophore, leading to synergistic signal enhancement.

Consistent with this design principle, Vendrell and and co-workers reported alkenyl-linked Trp–linker–(S-NBD) 31 derivatives with λabs/λem values of 496/658 nm, respectively, featuring exceptionally large Stokes shifts exceeding 150 nm. Notably, Trp–S–NBD conjugates exhibited more than a 100-fold fluorescence enhancement in hydrophobic environments, highlighting their strong environment-sensitive behavior. In comparison, Ackermann’s alkenylated coumarin and xanthone derivatives satisfy compact labeling requirements but generally afford shorter-wavelength and lower-intensity emissions. The Vendrell team also successfully employed BODIPY-derived diazonium salts for coupling with Tyr. The FlAA 32, featuring a diazo-based linker, exhibits absorption and emission wavelengths similar to those of BODIPY. However, it expands the types and strategies of linker conjugation and enhances its responsiveness in hydrophobic environments, demonstrating promising applications within biological systems. Collectively, these studies underscore that linker incorporation alone does not guarantee optimal performance; rather, careful matching of electronic structure and conjugation between the amino acid, linker, and fluorophore is critical for achieving superior photophysical outcomes.

Late-Stage Generation of FlAAs in Peptides via Bioconjugation

Distinct from strategies that rely on the synthesis of FlAAs before peptide assembly, an alternative approach focuses on late-stage site-selective construction of fluorophores directly on native peptide scaffolds (Figure C). For example, the Anslyn group exploited the unique reactivity of N-terminal glycine to trigger a cascade reaction with dibenzoylmethane, enabling in situ formation of a strongly fluorescent pyrrole core. This transformation is inert toward internal residues, thereby affording strict N-terminal selectivity. Similarly, the Raj group developed an “addition–cyclization–aromatization” strategy that selectively targets the primary amine of lysine side chains, directly generating fluorescent pyridinium salts within peptide sequences.

In addition to native residues, functionalized unnatural amino acids can further expand the scope of late-stage fluorophore generation. The Song group reported an efficient Rh-catalyzed strategy that exploits intrinsic 4-CN-Phe­(Cph) residues, wherein C–H activation followed by annulation with external alkynes enables one-step construction of complex polycyclic aromatic fluorophores directly on the peptide backbone. Collectively, these site-selective bioconjugation strategies enable precise in situ installation of fluorescent motifs while minimizing perturbation to the native peptide framework.

Live-Cell Imaging with Peptide Probes Containing FlAAs

Several classes of FlAAs derived from native aromatic residues were employed to report local microenvironments and conformational changes. Representative examples include 4-CN-Trp, , carbazole-Phe and pyrrolo-isoquinoline-Trp, which can be embedded into protein and peptide frameworks with minimal structural perturbation and have been used to monitor protein folding, membrane insertion, β-hairpin formation, and ligand-induced conformational changes. Collectively, they laid the foundation for optical interrogation of structural dynamics in living cells. However, such FlAAs are limited by modest brightness, restricted spectral coverage, and insufficient penetration depth, making it challenging to meet the demands of high signal-to-noise imaging in complex cellular environments.

Trp–fluorophore conjugates have substantially expanded live-cell imaging capabilities. Compact FlAAs generated by direct C2-functionalization of the Trp indole, exemplified by Trp-BODIPY and Trp-(redBODIPY), display pronounced environment-sensitive fluorescence, becoming strongly emissive in hydrophobic settings such as lipid membranes. This binding-induced fluorescence turn-on has enabled their broad application in targeted fluorescent peptide probes. For example, incorporation of Trp-BODIPY into the antifungal peptide PAF26 enabled wash-free imaging of fungal cells and infected tissues. Similarly, cyclic peptides targeting phosphatidylserine and labeled with Trp-BODIPY, including cLac-BODIPY and Apo-15, enabled continuous, multiscale imaging of apoptosis from the cellular to the whole-body level (Figure A).

Moreover, introduction of red-emissive Trp­(redBODIPY) into keratin-1 (KRT1)–targeting peptides yielded probes with improved tissue penetration, allowing precise localization of KRT1+ cells in intact breast cancer tissues. In parallel, Apotracker Red, a cyclic phosphatidylserine-targeting peptide incorporating Trp­(redBODIPY), enabled deep-tissue and whole-body imaging of apoptosis with improved signal-to-noise ratios (Figure B).

Despite these advances, the acid sensitivity of conventional BODIPY coresparticularly toward trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)has limited their compatibility with solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS). To address this limitation, the Vendrell group developed Trp-BODIPY PLUS, which combines acid stability with environment-sensitive fluorescence. Incorporation of this FlAA into kisspeptin-10 (KP-10)–derived targeting peptides yielded high-signal-to-noise fluorescent probes while preserving receptor agonist activity (Figure C). These probes enabled wash-free imaging and spatial mapping of GPR54-exporessing living cells and intact mouse pancreatic islets, providing a direct tool for studying the tissue distribution of metabolism- and endocrine-related GPCRs.

Alkenyl-linked Trp-BODIPY derivatives exhibiting molecular rotor behavior have been developed as membrane-sensitive FlAAs. Upon fatty-acid conjugation, these probes respond sensitively to membrane fluidity, enabling real-time monitoring of lipid composition and viscosity in immune-cell membranes. Notably, they directly report plasma-membrane cholesterol levels in CD8+ T cells, thereby distinguishing resting from activated states and facilitating the screening of immunomodulatory small molecules. By an analogous strategy, near-infrared–emissive alkenyl-linked Trp-NBD derivatives were incorporated into membrane-affine cationic amphiphilic antimicrobial peptides, enabling wash-free NIR live-cell imaging of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria for rapid pathogen visualization and mechanistic studies (Figure D). Most fluorescent peptide probes described above are constitutively emissive, limiting spatiotemporal control. To address this constraint, the Vendrell group integrated environment-sensitive Trp-BODIPY FlAAs with a photochromic furylfulgimide (FF) unit, generating Trp-BODIPY-FF with reversible photoswitching capability. This design enables light-controlled, reversible labeling in living cells and supports super-resolution imaging techniques such as RESOLFT and PAINT.

Beyond the Trp framework, compact fluorescent units derived from other amino acids provide complementary functionalities for live-cell studies. For example, Phe-BODIPY probes developed by the Vendrell group enable rapid, wash-free identification of Candida species in live-cell settings while maintaining high contrast in complex biological samples. In drug-monitoring applications, recent work has employed site-specific Tyr labeling to construct BODIPY-immunoaffinity protein probes for wash-free detection of the immunosuppressant tacrolimus, with successful validation in urine samples from transplant patients. Meanwhile, benzodiazole-based FlAAs have been applied in peptide-PAINT super-resolution imaging, enabling nanometer-scale localization of receptor proteins and synaptic structures in living cells and primary tissues. Taken together, aromatic amino acid–derived fluorescent units constitute a comprehensive toolkit for peptide-based live-cell imaging, supporting multilevel applications ranging from cellular dynamics and infection monitoring to nanoscale structural analysis.

Photodynamic Therapy with Peptide Probes Containing FlAAs

FlAAs have been further developed into compact photosensitizing “reactive units” capable of selectively eradicating pathogenic or tumor cells upon light activation. From a molecular design standpoint, this transformation relies on redirecting excited-state deactivation from fluorescence-dominated singlet decay toward efficient intersystem crossing (ISC) and triplet-state population, which are prerequisites for reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. In contrast to conventional fluorogenic amino acids optimized for high fluorescence quantum yieldsoften at the expense of triplet formationphotosensitizing FlAAs are deliberately engineered to enhance ISC through heavy-atom incorporation (e.g., Se-containing heterocycles), donor–acceptor electronic architectures, or intramolecular energy and electron transfer processes that favor long-lived triplet states. When incorporated into peptides or metabolic substrates, these compact photosensitizing amino acids efficiently generate singlet oxygen and other ROS under visible-light excitation. Subsequent light irradiation induces localized oxidative stress following selective cellular accumulation, enabling highly precise cell ablation.

Building on an amino-substituted benzoselenadiazole photosensitizer platform, the Vendrell group developed metabolite-mimicking photosensitizers that exploit endogenous transport pathways (amino acids and glucose) for selective cellular uptake, enabling efficient singlet oxygen generation and light-triggered ablation of tumor cells or bacteria with low toxicity toward normal cells. More recently, a benzodiazole pairing strategy was introduced by linking a photosensitizing benzoselenadiazole to a nonphotosensitizing benzodiazole via a short tether within targeting peptides. Synergistic intramolecular energy and electron transfer markedly enhanced singlet oxygen yields and photodynamic efficacy, resulting in broadened antimicrobial activity, effective biofilm eradication, and favorable performance in corneal infection models (Figure E).

Overall, these compact photosensitizing amino acids endow peptide probes with dual imaging-and-therapy functionality, enabling spatial mapping of metabolically active cells, early lesion detection, and highly selective photodynamic intervention in infection models, brain tumors, and organoid systems.

Translation-Based Strategies for FlAA Incorporation

In the preceding sections, we summarized the molecular design and synthetic strategies of FlAAs, as well as their chemical incorporation into fluorescent peptide probes for bioimaging applications. This section focuses on translation-machinery–mediated incorporation strategies, highlighting key technological advances that enable the in vitro and in vivo synthesis of peptides and proteins containing FlAAs beyond direct chemical synthesis, as well as their emerging functional applications.

One important class of approaches relies on chemical aminoacylation of tRNA, in which FlAAs are preloaded onto tRNA (tRNA) through chemo-enzymatic ligation and subsequently incorporated into peptides or proteins using cell-free translation systems. Because this strategy bypasses cellular metabolism and transport processes, it can accommodate bulky and structurally complex fluorescent or photoactive side chains that are otherwise incompatible with cellular uptake or biosynthesis. Moreover, the cell-free format allows precise control over translation conditions. Vendrell and co-workers demonstrated that this approach enables efficient incorporation of fluorescent and photocatalytic Si-rosamine (SiR)–derived amino acids into proteins (Figure A).

In recent years, the flexizyme system has emerged as a powerful, ribozyme-based platform for universal aminoacylation, further expanding the chemical space of amino acids that can be loaded onto tRNA. Flexizymes catalyze the direct attachment of a wide range of activated amino acids to the 3′ end of tRNA, with minimal constraints on side-chain size or chemical structure. As a result, structurally unconventional, backbone-extended, or bulky FlAAs can be charged onto tRNA and utilized for in vitro translation (Figure A). For example, Jewett and co-workers incorporated a variety of FlAAs bearing pyrene, coumarin, nitrobenzoxadiazole, and fluorescein variants into functional peptides using the flexizyme-ribosome system. The exceptional substrate tolerance of flexizyme-mediated aminoacylation provides an important methodological foundation for the synthesis of increasingly complex fluorescent peptides, and the exploration of novel fluorophore architectures. Beyond flexizyme-mediated systems, chemically aminoacylated tRNA strategies in cell-free translation have enabled the incorporation of functionally engineered fluorescent amino acids (FlAAs) with advanced optical behaviors. Recent studies have leveraged this flexibility to combine translation compatibility with environment-responsive or photochemically active properties, allowing precise optical reporting and regulation at the peptide and protein level. These advances illustrate the shift from simple fluorophore installation toward the rational design of FlAAs with programmable photophysical and functional behavior, expanding the utility of in vitro translation platforms for chemical biology and bioimaging.

Compared with these two in vitro FlAA incorporation systems, genetic code expansion (GCE) offers distinct advantages for the direct incorporation of FlAAs in living cells. Through engineered orthogonal aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase/tRNA (aaRS/tRNA) pairs, FlAAs can be site-specifically introduced into peptides and proteins in vivo, enabling real-time studies of protein folding, conformational dynamics and subcellular localization within their native cellular context (Figure B). For instance, molecular rotor–based amino acids derived from 3-(6-acetylnaphthalen-2-ylamino)-2-aminopropionic acid (Anap), such as RBF-ncAAs, enhance π-conjugation and molecular-rotor effects to achieve highly sensitive responses to local polarity and viscosity changes. These probes have been successfully applied to monitor protein aggregation and microenvironmental variations in living cells (Figure C). Furthermore, multisite encoding of Anap in transmembrane transporters has enabled real-time reporting of ion-binding-induced conformational transitions, underscoring the unique advantages of FlAAs in probing dynamic mechanisms. In parallel, fluorescent molecular rotor amino acids (FMR-AAs) integrate “folding-activated” fluorophores directly into the amino acid backbone, effectively converting intrinsically nonfluorescent proteins into artificial fluorescent proteins with enhanced signal-to-noise ratios and strong conformation dependence.

Overall, chemical tRNA aminoacylation, flexizyme-mediated aminoacylation, and in vivo genetic code expansion collectively constitute a complementary toolkit for FlAA incorporation, spanning in vitro to in vivo systems and bridging methodology with functional applications. Together with the chemical synthesis and peptide-probe strategies discussed earlier, these approaches establish a comprehensive technological landscape for FlAAsfrom molecular design and synthesis to their integration into biological systems and functional investigation.

Conclusion and Outlook

In summary, the development of FlAAs has evolved from early, modest perturbations of native aromatic residues into a highly programmable chemical platform that integrates molecular design, synthetic methodology, and biological application. As highlighted throughout this Review, Trp occupies a central position in FlAA research, owing to the unique electronic plasticity of its indole chromophore and the recent emergence of powerful late-stage C–H functionalization strategies. These advances have transformed Trp from a merely tunable residue into a genuinely modular scaffold, enabling systematic control over emission wavelength, brightness, and environmental sensitivity through site-selective and multisite modification.

Furthermore, modular amino acid–fluorophore conjugation approaches have expanded the chemical space of FlAAs well beyond indole chemistry. Direct conjugation offers compactness and minimal perturbation, whereas linker-based designs provide synthetic flexibility and additional photophysical control. Importantly, these studies collectively reveal that optimal FlAA performance is not dictated solely by fluorophore choice, but rather by the precise electronic matching among the amino acid, linker, and chromophore, underscoring the need for rational, structure-guided design rather than empirical dye attachment.

Equally significant are advances in translation-based incorporation technologies, which now allow FlAAs to be site-specifically embedded into peptides and proteins across in vitro and in vivo contexts. Chemical tRNA aminoacylation, flexizyme-mediated charging, and GCE form a complementary toolkit that bridges chemical synthesis with functional biological interrogation, enabling FlAAs to probe protein dynamics, microenvironmental changes, and cellular processes with unmatched precision.

Recent advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence (AI) are increasingly influencing molecular design and protein engineering. In the context of fluorescent unnatural amino acids, AI-driven approaches have the potential to reshape both amino acid design and application by jointly considering fluorophore structure, protein microenvironment, and translational constraints, enabling more context-aware and predictive development beyond empirical screening.

Looking forward, several challenges and opportunities remain. Improving the photostability, red/NIR emission, and multiphoton compatibility of compact FlAAs will be critical for deep-tissue and long-term imaging. Integrating FlAAs with emerging modalitiessuch as optogenetics, proximity labeling, and AI-assisted peptide designmay further expand their functional scope. Ultimately, by uniting chemical innovation with biological relevance, fluorescent unnatural amino acids are poised to become indispensable tools for chemical biology, offering new ways to visualize, manipulate, and understand complex biological systems at molecular resolution.
