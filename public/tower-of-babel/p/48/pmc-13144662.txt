Old Lesions and New Targets: sEEG-Identified Onsets in Traditionally Inoperable Epilepsy Lesions and Associated Surgical Outcomes
Authors: Yosef M. Dastagirzada, Spencer Frome, Daniel J. Curry, Howard L. Weiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with epilepsy who do not respond to pharmacotherapy are often offered a noninvasive diagnostic workup to localize an epileptogenic focus that might be treated with resection, neuromodulation, or ablation. Yet in many cases, this evaluation fails to identify surgical candidates.SummaryPatients with tuberous sclerosis, periventricular nodular heterotopia, or polymicrogyria may have clearly visible lesions on MRI, but the resulting epileptogenic onset zones/networks are often too complex for scalp electroencephalography to decode. Similarly, seizures arising from the cingulate gyrus originate in deep structures that are inaccessible to noninvasive techniques. In post-traumatic epilepsy without a dominant lesion such as mesial temporal sclerosis, injury is often too diffuse to define a clear seizure-onset zone. Stereoelectroencephalography (sEEG), with its ability to map deep brain networks in detail, offers a unique solution for these challenging cases.Key MessagesIn this review, we highlight how sEEG has expanded surgical options and provided hope for seizure freedom in patients once considered medically and surgically untreatable, and we aim to encourage further study into its potential applications.

Introduction

In the treatment of drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), or persistent epilepsy despite the proper usage of two antiepileptic medications, surgery may prove to be an effective modality for seizure control and even seizure freedom. Thirty to forty percent of patients experience DRE and it is recommended that they are referred to comprehensive epilepsy centers for multidisciplinary evaluation [1, 2]. Wiebe et al. [3] demonstrated in a landmark randomized control trial that 58% of patients undergoing anterior temporal lobectomy for temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) became seizure free, in comparison to 8% in the medical group at 1 year. In conjunction with other high-level studies, surgical epilepsy research and technological advancement in the 21st century has been transformative [4, 5]. Prognostic calculators like the Epilepsy Surgery Grading Scale (ESGS) have been instrumental in stratifying patients and helping manage postoperative expectations [6]. Additionally, large database univariate analyses have identified factors like abnormal preoperative MRI, presence of mesial temporal sclerosis, and electroencephalography/imaging concordance to be associated with better postoperative seizure outcomes [7].

A pivotal development in the history of surgical epilepsy was the inception of minimally invasive stereoelectroencephalography (sEEG) in the 1950s [8]. Unlike subdural electrode implantation, which involves a craniotomy and surface electrode placement, sEEG utilizes stereotactically guided depth electrodes inserted through small burr holes to record intracranial activity from both cortical and subcortical structures in three dimensions. This approach allows for targeted investigation of deep or bilateral brain regions while minimizing morbidity. Due to sEEG’s value in patients with non-lesional MRI, discordant noninvasive data, or suspected multifocal/deep seizure foci, it has become the preferred method of intracranial EEG (iEEG) over subdural grids for the majority of patients with DRE [9]. Studies have demonstrated greater odds of seizure freedom in patients who underwent resection after sEEG in comparison to subdural electrode [10]. By providing high-resolution, spatially precise recordings of ictal and interictal activity, sEEG plays a critical role in surgical planning and the evaluation of candidacy for epilepsy surgery.

These advancements not only benefit patients with lesional pathologies or sEEG-confirmed concordant data, but, more recently, neuromodulating technologies have provided hope for patients with complex networks, specifically those with multifocal epilepsy and/or those whose seizure network inhabits eloquent areas. For example, in patients with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome (LGS) – one of the most severe epileptic encephalopathies that is extremely resistant to conventional therapies – vagus nerve stimulation has shown promising data [11]. Furthermore, the Stimulation of the Anterior Nucleus of the Thalamus for Epilepsy (SANTE) trial provided class 1 evidence supporting bilateral DBS to the anterior nucleus of the thalamus for adults with medically refractory partial seizures, including secondary generalized seizures [12]. Lastly, the advent of closed-loop responsive neurostimulation has proven to benefit patients with 1–2 seizure foci in eloquent areas that would lead to morbidity if resected or destroyed [13].

Despite the progress that has been achieved, subgroups remain that are deemed poor surgical candidates. Examples include those with cingulate gyrus (CG) epilepsy, tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), polymicrogyria (PMG), periventricular nodular heterotopia (PVNH), and post-traumatic epilepsy (PTE). This is thought to be due to the poor predictive value of noninvasive testing in predicting the epileptogenic seizure onset zone or networks. Recent studies have shown poor overlap between lesions seen on MRI and epileptic activity captured by iEEG in PMG [14], PVNH [15], cingulate epilepsy [16], and PTE [17]. Furthermore, the network of tubers seen in TSC can produce seizure activity directly from the tubers, the cortical areas surrounding the tubers, or networks between tubers [18]. This poses one of the greatest challenges in pediatric epilepsy surgery.

In this review, we aim to illustrate the utility of sEEG for surgical planning in the treatment of patients with cingulate epilepsy, TSC, PMG, PVNH, or PTE. This technique provides newfound hope for these patients with DRE previously thought to be poor surgical candidates, and it is essential in identifying future areas for growth.

Cingulate

CG epilepsy encompasses a heterogeneous constellation of seizure manifestations given the broad function and connectivity of this region [19]. Early CG seizure semiology data have mostly been extrapolated from studies of lesional cases and/or animal models [20–22]. Evaluation of the neuronal architecture of the varying locations of the CG reveals distinct morphologies that likely account for the various functions the gyrus performs [23]. While the anterior cingulate has been identified as critical to emotional regulation, the pain network, and decision-making, the posterior cingulate has connections integral to visuospatial and memory functions [24].

Understanding the cingulate cortex connectivity and function allows us to better predict its involvement in certain seizure semiology. Anterior cingulate seizures are typically nocturnal and predominantly hypermotor/hyperkinetic. Patients can experience mood changes or fear, elastic seizures, or autonomic symptoms [25]. Ictal “pouting” has also been shown to be specific to anterior cingulate seizures. Posterior cingulate seizures have clinically and electrographically been disguised as temporal lobe epilepsies, presenting with sensory auras and bilateral asymmetric tonic activity [26]. Scalp EEG poses a diagnostic challenge in cingulate epilepsy given its deep and mesial location. With the development of sEEG, many have found it useful in recognizing the CG as the culprit seizure onset zone and defining boundaries for surgical intervention. sEEG trajectories to the CG can be orthogonal to the coronal plane on imaging, through longitudinal parasagittal trajectories, and can even reach bilateral CGs via one electrode passing beneath the falx [27]. Studies have also studied the feasibility of sampling both cingulate gyri with a single interhemispheric depth electrode [28]. It is important to note that the thin cortex of the gyrus should be reached by the deepest lead, reaching the pial surface of the interhemispheric fissure [16].

When analyzing the data surrounding surgical outcomes, one must be privy to the fact that the incidence of pure CG epilepsy is unknown and is limited to case reports. Marashly et al. describe sEEG localization of seizure onset to the middle CG in a 17-year-old male with refractory nocturnal seizures characterized by bilateral arm stiffening. Stereotactic laser ablation was performed without postoperative complication or deficits, leading to seizure freedom at 17 months of follow-up [29]. Upadhyayula et al. [30] reinforces the strength of sEEG in defining the seizure onset zone for nonlesional CG epilepsy and partial response to laser ablation. Majority of the limited intervention outcome data incorporates intervention involving the CG in conjunction with other parts of the brain, including lesional pathologies [16, 19]. A group in Germany reported the outcomes of 22 patients undergoing open surgical resection of an epileptic focus in the CG (86% lesional), evaluated preoperatively with interhemispheric electrodes [31]. Sixty two percent of patients were seizure free but a transient neurologic deficit occurred postoperatively in 41%, largely in cases with supracingular resections involving the supplemental motor area. Furthermore, they reported a permanent neurologic deficit in 9% of the cohort. Other reported morbidity includes transient aphasia with intervention of the anterior cingulate [32]. This is due to the intimate connectivity of the CG to other critical portions of the brain.

A large systematic review and meta-analysis including 21 studies (105 patients) on CG epilepsy displays that the most common semiology included hypermotor, dystonic, and vocal seizures and that over 60% of cases included lesional pathology. Invasive EEG monitoring was performed in 67% of patients and 91% underwent open resection. Notably, the use of laser ablation in the non-resectable group was skewed toward patients without identifiable lesions. Seizure freedom rates were 76% and 65% for patients with lesional and non-lesional epilepsy, respectively, but did lead to 24% of patients developing temporary neurologic deficits [33]. This provides us with level 2 evidence for patient counseling and expectation management in this rare pathology. It also sheds light on the potential expansion of laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) over open surgery for lesional CG epilepsy cases.

Tuberous Sclerosis

TSC is an autosomal dominant neurocutaneous disorder with an incidence of approximately 1:6000 to 1:10,000 live births characterized by variants in the tumor suppressor TSC1 or TSC2 genes that allow for unchecked cell growth in various areas of the body [34]. Examples include cardiac rhabdomyomas, renal angiomyolipomas, facial angiofibromas, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, and CNS tumors. Cortical hamartomas or tubers and subependymal giant cell astrocytomas are characteristic manifestations of this aberrant growth in the brain (shown in Fig. 1) [35]. Other CNS findings include white matter lesions and subependymal nodules [36].

Epilepsy and its subsequent deleterious neurocognitive effects are common to the TSC population. In the largest international cohort of TSC patients, 84% had epilepsy with up to 49% having infantile spasms [37]. Most were diagnosed in infancy or early childhood (<2 years of age), but there was a small cohort of older patients (>40 years of age) who presented with focal seizures, demonstrating an elevated lifetime risk. Seizure control was found to be associated with lower rates of intellectual disability, and various treatments like vigabatrin, vagus nerve stimulation, adrenocorticotropic hormone, everolimus, the ketogenic diet, and surgical tuber resection are indicated to achieve this. The heterogeneity of TSC-related epilepsy can be quite complex and is illustrated by the phenotype with multiple tubers, several seizure semiologies, and discordant presurgical evaluations. Furthermore, the variation in sEEG evaluation and operative approaches also reinforces the uncertainty of epileptogenicity in TSC. This dates back to the longstanding debate regarding whether tubers themselves house the epileptogenic zone (EZ) or whether the perituberal cortex is the culprit [38–41]. Histochemical data have demonstrated abnormal molecular signaling in both cortical tubers and the perituberal cortex (dysplastic neurons and giant cells, dysregulated cell growth signaling, increased axonal connectivity, and hypomyelination). Technological progress has been instrumental in exploring this conundrum and revealing the intricacy of widespread epileptic networks involved in seizure production, which has steered the field away from a “single focus” paradigm.

Radiologic advancements like magnetoencephalography (MEG) have shown promising results in localizing epileptogenic tubers. MEG is a noninvasive recording of cerebral activity measured in the form of magnetic fields that originate from neuronal electrical currents [42]. The equivalent current dipole clusters that are identified via MEG have been shown to determine the specific tuber related to the epileptic focus [43]. Interictal and ictal MEG has also been advocated for in the preoperative evaluation of pediatric TSC patients [44]. MEG data can delineate multiple phenotypes as detailed by Iida et al. [45] (Table 1). FDG-PET and EEG-fMRI have also shown promising results in localizing epileptogenic tubers [46, 47].

The advent of sEEG has also added to the knowledge base surrounding epileptogenicity and localization in TSC patients. In the largest sEEG study looking at epileptogenicity in TSC, authors discovered two main EZ patterns: focal and complex [48]. First, the focal pattern was characterized by a dominant tuber as the EZ with a gradient of epileptogenicity from tuber center to rim. Intratuberal continuous interictal discharges were seen in all these cases, and tuber stimulation-induced habitual seizures were seen in 71%. Of note, a type C dominant tuber was seen in 86% of these cases, suggesting that this radiographic pattern indicates tuber epileptogenicity. Figure 1 demonstrates the radiographic characteristics of type B and C tubers. Among the focal tuber EZ organization cases, 80% experienced Engel 1A outcome with tuberectomy. Second, the complex pattern involved the normal-appearing cortex and less so, the dominant tuber. Only 40% of patients with this pattern achieved Engel 1A with tuberectomy.

Other sEEG-based studies have reinforced the presence of intertuberal connectivity using the quantitative method of coherence, which determines whether two or more sensors implanted in different brain regions have similar neuronal oscillatory activity [49, 50]. Fast ripples (200–500 Hz) – a potential biomarker for epileptogenicity – were also detected in cortical tubers of TSC patients undergoing sEEG, specifically at the junction between tuber and adjacent cortex [51]. This provides the foundation for the postulation that this junction area may be responsible for the EZ. Lastly, one must remember that the power of sEEG in evaluating TSC patients also includes the ability to potentially induce habitual seizures with stimulation, map eloquent nearby areas, and create corridors for potential minimally invasive approaches.

Early leaders in the field of TSC and epilepsy surgery advocated for expanding surgical indications to even the complex cases with bilateral tubers starting in the turn of the 21st century [52, 53]. Even with the expanding use of sEEG and aforementioned advancements, studies still demonstrate the underutilization of surgical interventions for TSC populations [54, 55]. Large cohort data including open resective epilepsy surgery have indicated that younger age at seizure onset, larger size of predominant tuber, and resection larger than a tuberectomy are associated with longer seizure freedom [39]. Other factors that provide higher odds for good seizure control after surgery include absence of generalized seizure semiology, no or mild developmental delay, unifocal ictal scalp EEG abnormality, and EEG/MRi concordance [56]. Moderate/severe intellectual disability and the presence of tonic seizures is related to seizure recurrence [39, 57–59]. Laser ablative therapy shows similar results with small series demonstrating 71.4% of patients experiencing a reduction of AEDs and 43% seizure free with a more favorable safety profile [60]. Ablation volume to tuber volume ratios seem to be a simple, useful parameter in predicting post-ablative seizure outcomes [61].

The multidisciplinary team caring for TSC patients can face multiple challenges when evaluating for candidacy of epilepsy surgery. This is due to the various phenotypes and heterogeneity of epilepsy brought to the table. With the expansion of both noninvasive and invasive preoperative assessments, the field has broadened its indications and requires more multi-institutional collaboration to enhance surgical outcomes.

Polymicrogyria

PMG is a malformation of cortical development (MCD) that is radiologically defined by an excessive number of abnormally small, partly fused cortical gyri and is thought to be caused by a disruption in neuronal migration and cortical development [62, 63]. One study showed that this lesion accounts for 16% of MCD in children [64]. It has been associated with certain genes (SRPX2, RAB3GAP1, EOMES, TUBB2B) and syndromes like Aicardi, Delleman, DiGeorge, and Warburg Micro syndromes [65]. Epilepsy has been shown to be present in up to 87% [66] of PMG cases and is frequently found to be medically refractory [67]. Despite this fact, literature surrounding epilepsy surgery for PMG remains sparse.

Anatomo-electro-clinical investigations have led us to better understand the EZ in this patient population. Recent literature has discovered the following findings that elucidate the network complexity intrinsic to this pathology (Table 2) [68–70]. In a recent review of 49 patients with PMG with sEEG-identified EZs, only 16% had complete overlap between the PMG and the EZ. The remaining EZs were only partially concordant with the lesion and often involved unrelated cortical areas [14]. Furthermore, they found that the EZ was involved in remote areas amongst 43% of cases and 70% of cases had partial or no involvement of the PMG in the EZ [14]. These findings not only pose challenges when attempting to localize the EZ but also for potential future surgical interventions. Radiologic literature have attempted to use MEG and FDG-PET to better delineate the EZ in PMG cases [68, 69, 71]. The latter has been validated by sEEG data and is thought to be useful in presurgical evaluation, specifically in patients with unilateral PMG.

Surgical outcome data are limited and biased by the heterogeneity of PMG cases. Three of the largest surgical outcome cohorts involving PMG cases include 12, 24, and 39 patient cases [14, 72, 73]. Among these larger cohorts, postoperative seizure freedom ranged from 50 to 72%. Surgical interventions ranged from partial vs. total PMG resections, disconnections, and hemispherectomies. Cossu et al. [72] noted the limitation of selection bias in their surgical treatment population of 32, which were almost exclusively unilateral lesions. When analyzing outcome data for rare pathologies with small cohorts, one must investigate limitations like selection bias, variability of interventions, and length of follow-up to evaluate for seizure recurrence.

sEEG data in the last decade have allowed us to better comprehend PMG and allow for certain cases to enter the arena of surgical epilepsy potential. These early experience data have better demonstrated the complex network beyond the radiographic margins of PMG. Medically refractory PMG cases may warrant a comprehensive presurgical evaluation and be considered for phase 2 monitoring. The multidisciplinary evaluation looking at semiology, imaging, and sEEG data may be able to help certain cases but this calls for more research trying to better understand the best candidates in an already complex patient population.

Periventricular Nodular Heterotopia

PVNH is another MCD characterized by abnormal migration of neurons resulting in gray matter nodules within the periventricular white matter. This disruption of migration can be associated with genetic syndromes or perinatal exposure to toxic substances like alcohol [74]. However, PVNH can also arise idiopathically and present with DRE in adults [75]. One study found that 82% of their cohort with PVNH experienced recurrent seizures [76]. However, given the potential for subclinical PVNH, its true prevalence and risk for seizures is not known. Similarly to PMG, the area of seizure onset is not always concordant with the MRI-defined lesion. Mirandola et al. [77] showed that the connection between the heterotopia and the involved cortex is the most common location, around 70% of their cohort, for seizure onset in these patients (Fig. 2). Only 20% of the 64 patients analyzed had seizures arising from the heterotopia alone.

Furthermore, the aberrant migration of neurons results in lesions localized near eloquent structures involved in connecting remote brain structures. This deep localization, along with the complicated epileptogenic networks that arise from the heterotopic foci, make patients with PVNH poor resection candidates [78]. The nature of PVNH’s deep location makes them mostly inaccessible by traditional microsurgical techniques. Due to these complexities, PVNH has become a popular subject of sEEG studies. Both Zanello et al. [79] and Pizzo et al. [80] reported multiple unique patterns of epileptogenesis that often involved a seemingly normal cortex. Pizzo’s group added to Mirandola et al. findings with only 6 of their 66 sEEG-recorded seizures involving solely the imaging-defined heterotopia. Interestingly, 5 of the 19 patients experienced seizures that did not involve any heterotopia [80]. Zanello’s study of 14 patients used analysis of outgoing and incoming signals from each sEEG node to illustrate that seizures may be driven by the heterotopia or the normal surrounding gray matter. Notably, they found no correlation between the size of heterotopia and strength of sEEG signal [79]. Unlike the previously discussed lesions, ablative techniques have been shown to be the most effective treatment for DRE due to PVNH [15, 77, 81]. Ablation allows for sEEG-identified seizure foci to be inactivated while optimizing safety to nearby structures.

The optimal method of ablation is still being studied. sEEG-guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTC) utilizes the implanted electrodes used for sEEG to coagulate areas of seizure onset. One institution reported 46% seizure freedom for PVNH patients who underwent this treatment [15]. Mirandola’s study in 2017 showed sustained treatment response to RFTC in 76% of their patients [77]. Patients with seizures arising at least partially from the heterotopia rather than from a network involving multiple structures responded best to RFTC [77]. In parallel to RFTC, magnetic resonance-guided LITT has also emerged as a treatment option for PVNH. One study showed 80% seizure freedom after ablation in patients with unilateral PVNH. Response to magnetic resonance-guided LITT was even better for patients with onset solely in the heterotopia with 90.5% of this subset achieving seizure freedom. In this study of 39 patients, those with bilateral PVNH or lower seizure zone ablation had the highest rates of seizure persistence [82]. Due to the multiple ways PVNH can be involved in epilepsy onset, sEEG is uniquely necessary for treating this MCD.

Trauma

Seizures are a common sequela of traumatic brain injury that can occur in the acute recovery phase or years after the initial insult. Trauma induces multifocal neuronal damage and scar formation, which can become foci for the development of PTE. Incidence of PTE varies between studies, but risk increases with severity of injury and has been estimated to be 11.5% 5 years after a severe traumatic brain injury [83]. Patients who experience a subdural hematoma or brain contusion after trauma have been shown to be at the highest risk for seizures [84].

Given the broad range of structural and functional changes that trauma to the brain can induce, these patients are often poor surgical candidates when anti-seizure medications fail. This has led to limited publication of surgical treatments for PTE [85–87]. Kazemi et al. [85] illustrated the importance of imaging findings in the early surgical treatment of PTE by focusing their resections on completely removing MRI-defined encephalomalacia. In Hartzfeld’s study of 57 patients who received surgery for PTE, they resected a portion of the temporal lobe in 6 of the 15 patients with normal MRIs, which resulted in 3 poor outcomes [87]. When trauma induces a clear seizure focus, patients may be amenable to surgical resection. A 2012 retrospective review of patients with PTE undergoing surgery highlighted this concept by showing better outcomes for patients with focal abnormalities on MRI over patients with unrevealing workups [88]. In a recent single institutional review of 12 years of epilepsy surgery, 23 out of their 389 surgical patients had PTE. Sixteen of these patients were candidates for temporal lobe or cortical resection as defined by scalp or iEEG and experienced 68.8% complete seizure freedom after surgery with findings of mesial temporal lobe sclerosis conferring increased odds of a favorable outcome. The patients who received vagus nerve stimulation or responsive neurostimulation only experienced moderate benefit, which highlights the importance of identifying resectable foci in PTE [89]. A recent meta-analysis further showed superiority of resection, when possible, compared to neuromodulation. Within the resection group, 89.9% of patients who underwent temporal lobectomies experienced seizure reduction while 84% of patients received this benefit after a nontemporal lobectomy [90].

sEEG offers an important means of revealing resectable portions of the brain in patients with drug-resistant PTE. A recent study of sEEG recordings in patients with PTE illustrated the benefits patients gain from undergoing this preoperative procedure. These recordings can adequately identify poor candidates in the case of bilateral foci or offer exact road maps to ensure minimal necessary resection. This study found seizures mostly arising from the temporal lobe. Importantly, many patients with PTE were shown to have focal-onset epilepsy illustrating that post-traumatic patients may be better surgical candidates than previously thought [17]. A recent case study highlighted the role of sEEG in PTE by showing that MRI findings of encephalomalacia may not overlap with the seizure-onset zone [91]. Given the low reliability of MRI or scalp EEG in predicting seizure onset for patients with PTE, sEEG is an essential tool for identifying surgical candidates.

Conclusion

Epilepsy is a heterogenous disease that requires a broad set of diagnostic and therapeutic tools to properly treat each patient. With each new technological advancement, a new cohort of previously untreatable patients are given the hope of seizure treatment. When the wide arsenal of pharmaceutical options is exhausted, physicians are forced to turn to more invasive options like surgery or ablation. However, these treatments are limited by the need to identify a clear epileptogenic focus to target. In patients with CG epilepsy, TSC, PMG, PVNH, and PTE, MRI and scalp EEGs often fail to properly delineate the EZ. MRIs can clearly identify the tubers that define TSC or the lesions in PVNH and PMG, but evidence has shown that this does not reliably correlate to seizure-onset zones. Furthermore, these lesions are often too deep or complex for scalp EEGs to clearly pinpoint a resectable region of the brain. The deep location of seizures also causes limited effectiveness of noninvasive diagnosis in CG epilepsy. In PTE, patients with temporal sclerosis have great outcomes with resection, but other PTE patients do not have that option without an MRI definable lesion.

sEEG’s ability to record signals from multiple locations within the brain while maintaining a high safety profile provides an important new tool for these patients. Many studies have already shown that this technique can identify focal EZs in patients with complicated, previously unresectable epilepsy. In the case of thermocoagulation for PVNH, sEEG can even be diagnostic and therapeutic. More studies are needed to truly illustrate how sEEG will continue to change the landscape of epilepsy treatment and physicians should be hesitant in ruling out surgery as an option for patients with CG epilepsy, TSC, PMG, PVNH, and PTE who have not received this invasive monitoring.
