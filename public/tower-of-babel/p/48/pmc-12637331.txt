Real-World Evidence of the Long-Term Clinical Utility of a Vibrating Capsule in the Management of Chronic Idiopathic Constipation
Authors: Darren M. Brenner, Satish S.C. Rao, Bryan Curtin, Eamonn M.M. Quigley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Pharmacological therapies for chronic idiopathic constipation (CIC) are useful, but many patients report dissatisfaction from a lack of efficacy and occurrence of adverse events. The vibrating capsule (VC) is a US Food and Drug Administration approved nonpharmacologic treatment of CIC. However, its long-term usefulness in a community setting is unknown. The goal of this study was to assess the long-term efficacy and safety of VC treatment in a real-world community setting.METHODS:We conducted a postmarketing analysis of CIC patients prescribed VC who completed at least 3 or 6 months of treatment. The clinical utility was assessed by patient reported symptoms in an electronic stool diary. Safety data were also collected.RESULTS:One thousand seven hundred twenty-two patients were prescribed VC, and 491 and 298 took the VC and kept stool diaries for 3 and 6 months, respectively. Approximately 46% of patients were older than 55 years of age and 85% were women. Compared with baseline, complete spontaneous bowel movement rates increased significantly throughout the 3 and 6-month periods (average increase of >1 complete spontaneous bowel movement per week; P < 0.0001). Mean stool consistency (Bristol Stool Form Scale) improved from 2.9 (baseline) to 4.1 during treatment (P < 0.0001), mean straining effort (1–4) decreased from 2.9 to 1.6 (P < 0.0001), and toileting time also significantly decreased (P < 0.0001). Safety analysis revealed that 4.6% of patients reported feeling a sensation of vibration, 1.8% reported abdominal pain and 0.64% reported diarrhea.DISCUSSION:In a community setting, the VC seems both effective and safe for long-term treatment of chronic constipation with diarrhea being notably uncommon.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic idiopathic constipation (CIC) affects up to 14% of individuals worldwide, with a greater prevalence in women and the elderly (1). Approximately 60% of CIC patients express dissatisfaction with current treatments (2–4) which significantly increases the frequency of physician visits and healthcare expenditures (1). Many effective pharmacological treatments are accompanied by adverse events (AEs) such as diarrhea, abdominal pain, abdominal bloating/distention, and nausea that contribute to patient and physician dissatisfaction resulting in treatment discontinuation and the persistence of symptoms. Consequently, there is a need for alternative therapies. In a recent phase 3 trial, an orally administered vibrating capsule (VC) device (Vibrant Gastro, Yokneam, Israel) was shown to be efficacious and safe for the treatment of adults with CIC (2).

A significant challenge in the management of chronic constipation is the lack of information on patient compliance with CIC therapy, especially in a community setting. Furthermore, there is a paucity of objective technology to monitor patient symptoms and/or adherence to prescribed therapies. Such information holds significant potential to inform healthcare professionals' (HCP) decision-making processes regarding continuation or optimization of treatment.

The current US Food and Drug Administration cleared VC system includes a downloadable electronic stool diary app for patient use, and each patient is encouraged to record their daily bowel patterns in this e-diary. The system also includes a pod that registers and activates each of the VC before its ingestion, thereby providing a record of the patient's adherence to therapy. These features, inherent to the VC system, allow for accurate monitoring and evaluation of the clinical utility of VC treatment in a real-world community setting. Our aim was to assess the long-term efficacy and safety of VC treatment in a real-world community setting by conducting a postmarketing analysis of CIC patients who were prescribed VC for at least 3 or 6 months.

METHODS

Vibrant system

The Vibrant system (Vibrant Gastro INC, Yokneam, Israel) consists of VCs, an activation base unit (pod), and a companion application (app) including an electronic stool diary (not mandatory). The VC are 24.2 mm in length and 11.3 mm in diameter. The capsules are activated by an electromagnetic signal delivered by the pod before capsule consumption. Patients are provided with a video tutorial and detailed written instructions on how to use the Vibrant system. They are also encouraged to download a companion app containing an e-diary for recording of baseline and subsequent bowel symptoms. This data can be shared with HCPs allowing for continuous monitoring of progress.

Clinical efficacy

This postmarketing analysis was conducted by patient reporting between February 2023 and September 2024. All patients with suspected CIC who were prescribed VC at a dose of 5 capsules per week and completed 3 and 6 months of treatment were included in this analysis. Clinical effectiveness was compared with baseline by analyzing the e-diary data for the following parameters: (i) frequency of complete spontaneous bowel movements (CSBM); (ii) stool consistency (Bristol Stool Form Scale, type 1 (hard lumpy) to 7 (liquid with no solid pieces); (iii) straining effort (1–4, 1 = normal straining; 4 = severe straining) (5); (iv) duration of time spent toileting. Baseline data was collected in initial reports before starting treatment. All data subsequently recorded by patients provided information regarding changes in bowel symptoms throughout the treatment period.

Safety

The safety evaluation included all AEs reported by patients and HCPs during the analysis period. Safety data were collected from the patients at 4 time points: during an onboarding call within 2 weeks of initiating treatment and follow-up calls at the completion of 1, 3, and 6 months of treatment. Patients who seemed to have stopped using VC were also contacted.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to compare the baseline data with changes observed during treatment. Paired t-tests were utilized to compare the weekly number of CSBMs, stool consistency, straining, and toilet-time. The principal end point of interest was the change in weekly CSBMs per patient during the treatment periods. The weekly number of CSBMs during treatment was calculated as the sum of CSBMs per week and per patient and then averaged by patient.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics–patients

Overall, 1722 CIC patients were prescribed VC during the assessment period. Of these, 491 and 298, respectively, completed 3 and 6 months of treatment, filled out their e-diaries, and were included in this analysis. VC treatment was prescribed by community HCPs throughout the United States. The analyzed population included both men (N = 117) and women (N = 672). The overall retention rate was defined as the percentage of patients who continued to activate capsules compared with the total number of patients who began treatment. At 3 and 6 months, respectively, 491/1,215 (40%) and 298/1,067 (28%) completed 3 and 6 months of treatment and were included in this analysis.

CSBMs

At baseline, patients completing 3 and 6 months of treatment reported an average of 1.44 CSBMs per week. Significant improvements in the average number of CSBMs/wk were observed for these patients from baseline (3 months: 2.37; 6 months 2.51; average across entire treatment duration; P > 0.0001; Figure 1a,b).

Average complete spontaneous bowel movement (CSBM) frequency per patient per week for those completing 3 (a) and 6 (b) months of treatment. P < 0.0001 vs baseline across the entire treatment period.

Stool consistency

Significant improvements in stool consistency were also observed. Overall, mean stool consistency improved from 3.4 at baseline to 4.1 during treatment (average across entire treatment duration; P < 0.0001). Furthermore, the percentage of individuals experiencing Bristol 1 or 2 stools decreased in both the 3- and 6-month cohorts from 56% and 45% to 20% and 19%, respectively (Figure 2a,b).

Comparison of average stool consistency (BSFS) at baseline to stool consistency at 3 (a) and 6 (b) months of treatment. P < 0.0001 vs baseline across the entire treatment period.

Straining

The straining effort was also significantly decreased from a mean score of 2.9 at baseline to 1.6 during treatment (average across entire treatment duration; P < 0.0001). In the 3-month treatment group, a subjective sensation of normal straining was reported by only 12% of the patients at baseline which increased to 57% during treatment (Figure 3a). Severe straining was reported by 28% of the patients at baseline and decreased to 2% during treatment (Figure 3a). Similarly, in the 6-month cohort, a subjective sensation of normal straining was reported by 13% of the patients at baseline, which increased to 57% of the patients during treatment, whereas severe straining was initially reported by 19% at baseline, and this decreased to 2% during treatment (Figure 3b).

Comparison of straining at baseline to straining at 3 (a) and 6 (b) months of treatment. P < 0.0001 vs baseline across the entire treatment period.

Time spent on the toilet

Significant improvements were also evident in the time spent on the toilet which decreased from 33 minutes at baseline to 15 minutes during treatment (average across entire treatment duration; P < 0.0001). In the 3-month treatment group, the average time on the toilet decreased from 33 minutes at baseline to 15 minutes during treatment with 76% of patients reporting a toileting time of less than 10 minutes (Figure 4a). In the 6-month treatment group, the average time on the toilet decreased from 37 minutes at baseline to 15 minutes during treatment with 74% of patients spending less than 10 minutes on the toilet (Figure 4b).

Comparison of time spent on the toilet at baseline to time spent at 3 (a) and 6 (b) months of treatment. P < 0.0001 vs baseline across the entire treatment period.

Adverse events

The safety evaluation included all AE reported by patients treated with VC during the analysis period. Overall, 12.4% of patients experienced at least one AE (Table 1). The most common AE was a sensation of vibration, reported by 4.6% of the patients. Because vibration is part of the product's features, it is arguable whether a feeling of vibration during administration of the capsule truly represents an AE. In this analysis, a sensation of vibration was considered an AE only if it was reported as bothersome by the patient.

Delayed capsule expulsion (capsule not expelled after 2 weeks of treatment) was reported by 2.97% of patients. However, evidence (imaging) for retention was provided in only 7% of cases. The remaining 93% was based on patient subjective reports (without supporting evidence such as imaging). Most identified capsules were evacuated using laxatives, suppositories, or enemas. Nonurgent interventional colonoscopy was required for removal of retained capsules in 3 cases. In the first, the colonoscopy was performed specifically for removal of non-expelled capsules; however, it was not confirmed that the patient suffered any ill effects from the retained capsules. In the other 2 cases, per the treating physicians, the capsules were identified during diagnostic colonoscopies for conditions not attributable to the VC treatment.

Abdominal pain, bloating, and nausea were each reported by <2% and diarrhea by <1% of the patient population. During the analysis period, 5 serious adverse events requiring hospitalization were reported: viral gastroenteritis (1), obstipation (1), and abdominal pain (3). Each of these cases resolved and was not considered by the treating physician to be related to the VC.

DISCUSSION

In this community-based, observational, cross-sectional, postmarketing analysis, we assessed the clinical utility of VC by analyzing prospective data reported by patients using Vibrant' dedicated electronic diary. We found that the VC, in both short and long term, significantly improved constipation symptoms among ambulatory patients. A significantly greater proportion of patients reported improvements in the number of CSBMs/wk, straining efforts to pass stool, stool consistency, and the duration of time spent on the toilet. These effects were observed within 2–3 weeks of starting treatment and were stable over the 3- and 6-month time periods. This suggests persistence of the initial response and maintenance of efficacy over the long-term treatment of CIC patients.

The Vibrant system's unique therapeutic platform (activating pod and e-diary) enabled the systematic monitoring of patient adherence and provided additional insights regarding treatment effects. Patients using the app were more likely to reorder the VC than those who never activated or discontinued reporting within the app (6). This electronic method of prospective data collection ensures real-time and accurate documentation throughout the treatment period, reduces the likelihood of recall bias (7), and provides a comprehensive understanding of the impact of VC treatment on CIC. Although data were obtained from only from 30% of patients who maintained the e-dairy, this study represents the longest analysis of constipated patients to date in a real-world community setting.

Our safety assessment reflects the use of VC during the analyzed period and reveals that the VC is safe and well tolerated by a large cohort of patients for an extended treatment period. A small percentage of patients experienced a vibrating sensation which was reported as bothersome by 4.6% of patients. Importantly, <1% of individuals consuming the VC reported diarrhea, and there was only a 1.3% incidence of bloating. These rates are considerably lower than those identified in registration trials of pharmacological therapies for CIC (8–10). Delayed capsule expulsion or retention was reported in 2.9% of cases. Among these, a small proportion of cases had radiological confirmation, and the majority (>93%) were based on non-validated patient assumptions. No serious AEs attributable to the VC were documented.

A few limitations merit discussion. First, baseline bowel symptom data were collected in a single report from patients before they received VC treatment. However, we observed a significant increase in the number of CSBMs/wk from baseline and improvements in all other bowel symptoms during the ensuing 3 and 6 months, attesting to an overall therapeutic benefit. In addition, many of the patients were using concomitant over the counter and prescription therapeutics during VC treatment, but the absence of prospective data limited our ability to assess the impact of Vibrant therapy on the use of these other medications. Furthermore, as the willingness to document in the e-dairy was optional, the safety data were based on voluntary reports made by the patients. However, Vibrant Ltd did contact patients at 4 time points, and collected data during these discussions. Finally, as only approximately a one-third of patients elected to keep an e-diary it remains unclear whether those who did not, had less favorable outcomes.

In conclusion, in a community setting, the VC system seems to be effective and safe for the short and long-term management of chronic constipation. The e-diary provides unique insight into patient response and adherence to therapy and may contribute to continued use through positive reinforcement of improvement.
