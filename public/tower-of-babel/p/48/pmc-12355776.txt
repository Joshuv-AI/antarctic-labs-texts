Associations between unintentional injuries and bullying victimization in adolescents: evidence from a large-scale cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Yawen Xuan, Wenkang Zhou, Shuting Kong, Xudong Zhou, Hui Zhu, Jingjing Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLittle is known about the associations between unintentional injuries from the perspective of parents and their children’s bullying victimization. This study aimed to examine the links between parent-reported unintentional injury subtypes and student-reported experiences of bullying victimization from a school-based large-scale survey.MethodsA total of 30,386 students from 35 primary schools, 27 middle schools, and 6 high schools with available data on unintentional injuries, school bullying victimization, and covariates were included in this study. Multivariable and multinomial logistic regression analyses were used to scrutinize the association of 12 types of unintentional injuries with any type of school bullying victimization, as well as multiple types of bullying experienced concurrently.Results10 out of 12 types of unintentional injuries were found to be associated with elevated risks of bullying victimization, with children and adolescents whose parents reported being struck by thrown, projected, or falling objects demonstrating the greatest vulnerability to school bullying victimization, followed by accidental drowning and submersion, contact with sharp instrument, etc. And there was also a positive association between the number of unintentional injuries and bullying victimization. The associations varied among children and adolescents who reported the presence of multiple bullying.ConclusionsCertain types and cumulative circumstances of unintentional injuries of children and adolescents necessitate vigilant attention from parents due to the potential indication of bullying. Subsequent research should delve into the underlying mechanisms linking unintentional injuries to school bullying victimization, focusing on specific injury types to provide more targeted guidance for identifying bullying.Keywords: School bullying victimization, Unintentional injury, Children and adolescents, Parental perspective, A large-scale cross-sectional study

Introduction

School bullying is a widespread social problem affecting children and adolescents globally, with an approximately estimated prevalence of 30% worldwide and 33.5% in China [1–3]. This phenomenon is characterized by intentional and repetitive acts of aggression, ranging from physical assaults to verbal abuse and social exclusion, which are underpinned by a power imbalance between the victim and aggressor [4]. Such bullying causes adverse consequences for the victims’ health, especially mental health, often manifesting in depression, anxiety, self-harm or even suicide [5–8]. A meta-analysis suggested a significant positive correlation between bullying victimization in childhood and the development of internalizing disorders, externalizing disorders, and criminality in adulthood [9]. Additionally, it can impose an economic burden on society [10]. To this end, the identification and prevention of bullying among school-aged children and adolescents has become a matter of paramount importance, garnering increasing concern from the general public.

There exist a wide range of methods for identifying instances of school bullying from the perspectives of students themselves, teachers, caregivers and doctors. However, prior work demonstrates that only 60% of bullied students seek assistance after experiencing school victimization [11, 12]. These experiences often remain concealed, primarily due to the students’ aversion to being labeled victims, coupled with feelings of shame and distrust towards adults [13]. Moreover, although teachers can detect the existence of school bullying through daily relationships with students, hidden timing of bullying that often occurs during breaks, after school, or on online platforms [14], subtle forms of bullying like subtle verbal insults, emotional abuse, or anonymous online attacks, and inadequate knowledge and awareness from teachers [15], make it difficult for teachers to identify, especially when victims choose to remain silent. This is even more true for caregivers. Additionally, doctors in outpatient and emergency departments can identify and detect signs of school bullying or domestic violence against children and adolescents through assessments of cutaneous manifestations [16, 17], but it usually makes sense among those with serious injuries predominantly stemming from physical abuse. Therefore, the mere incidents of school bullying that are officially documented represent merely the visible fraction of a much larger, concerning issue.

Notably, unintentional injuries, as a spectrum of health concerns that are readily observable to caregivers, may potentially serve as a crucial indicator in the process of school bullying identification. Several studies have reported a positive correlation between school bullying victimization and the occurrence of unintentional injuries. In three countries in Western sub-Saharan Africa, bullying victimization has emerged as a pivotal factor significantly associated with heightened probabilities of sustaining severe injuries [18]. Similarly, in a study of Chinese left-behind children, researchers revealed that in-school adolescents who experienced bullying were more prone to reporting multiple injuries compared to their counterparts who did not endure bullying [19]. Furthermore, an analysis of 87,269 individuals from 26 countries found that this association remains consistent, transcending geographical boundaries and income disparities [20]. Unfortunately, previous research has typically used composite indicators of unintentional injuries or school bullying, which may obscure more specific associations and limit the preventive value of the research. The effects of school bullying victimization on children and adolescents are intricate and multifaceted, with various forms of bullying potentially associated with the incidence or subsequent reporting of diverse accidental injuries to varying degrees. This complexity remains underappreciated among children and adolescents, emphasizing the need for further exploration and awareness.

The current study was designed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between unintentional injuries and school bullying and to make targeted recommendations by conducting a school-based large-scale survey, taking different types of unintentional injuries and multiple bullying victimizations into account.

Methods

Data collection

In the current study, we utilized data from a large-scale mental health census of Nanling County, Anhui Province, which is located in eastern China. The economic and social development levels in Nanling County can be considered representative of the average conditions across China. This census was carried out between November 2022 and January 2023. It covered all students from grades 5 to 12 in all educational institutions, encompassing 35 primary schools, 27 middle schools, and 6 high schools, Passive informed consent was implicitly granted by the parents of adolescents through the notification that their children would undergo a mental health screening at school unless they refused. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the School of Public Health, Zhejiang University. Further information has been published in detail previously [21].

Out of the 33,820 students, 3,434 were excluded from the survey due to various reasons including developmental disorder, mental illness during the survey period, or parental opposition. Consequently, a total of 30,386 students aged 11–17 completed the questionnaire with a response rate of 89.85% across all schools. For the present study, 2,527 students were excluded due to missing data on crucial aspects such as school bullying victimization, unintentional injuries reported by their parents, and covariates including sex, grade, parental marital status, parental highest education, family economic status, only child, self-reported health status, and parental absence status. Thus, 27,859 individuals were included in the final analysis.

Measures

School bullying victimization

The Chinese version of the Olweus Bully/Victim Questionnaire (OBVQ) was used to determine whether participants had been exposed to bullying at school in the current semester. The OBVQ contained seven questions: one question for the overall victimization situation and six questions for three types of bullying victimization (physical, verbal, and relational), possible responses of which were “never,” “only 1–2 times,” “2–3 times per month,” “once per week,” and “more than once per week.” The participants who answered “2–3 times per month” or more frequently to any of the seven questions above were classified as school bullying victimization. Additionally, we categorized participants into groups based on the number of types of bullying victimization: “none,” “1 type,” and “2–3 types.”

Unintentional injuries

Based on the codes for external causes of injury from the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision [22] and the Chinese CDC-National Injury Surveillance System, Work Manual of National Injury Surveillance System, 12 types of unintentional injuries including vehicle and traffic injuries; falls; struck by thrown, projected or falling objects; caught, crushed, jammed or pinched in or between objects; explosion and rupture of objects; eating foreign objects; bitten or struck by animals; accidental drowning and submersion; exposure to electric current; exposure to smoke, fire and flames, contact with heat and hot substances; poisoning; and contact with sharp instruments were examined separately using questions like “Have your children ever suffered from vehicle and traffic injuries accidentally in the past year?” Furthermore, based on these 12 injury categories, a continuous variable, namely number of unintentional injuries suffered, was devised. The number of different types of unintentional injuries suffered by each participant was directly aggregated in the continual variable.

Covariates

Covariates included socio-demographic characteristics (sex, grade, parental marital status, parental highest education, family economic status, only child, and parental absence status) and self-reported health status. Parental absence status was assessed by the following questions: “Has your father/mother ever migrated to another place for more than six months or more ever since you were born?” Students who responded “Yes, currently migrating” to either question were classified as “current.” Those who responded “No, never” to both questions were classified as “never.” Those who responded “I don’t know” to either question were classified as “unsure.” And any other responses were classified as “previous.”

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, chi-squared tests and student’s t-tests were employed to compare differences between students with and without school bullying victimization. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to explore the association of 12 types of unintentional injuries with school bullying victimization after adjustment for covariates. We first included each type of unintentional injury individually, and subsequently, we incorporated all of them simultaneously to provide a comprehensive assessment. Then, we used multinomial logistic regression analyses to examine the association of 12 types of unintentional injuries with multiple school bullying victimization. Additionally, we explored associations between the number of unintentional injuries suffered and school bullying victimization in binary and multiple frameworks with logistic regressions. All data cleaning and statistical analyses were carried out in the R Studio (version 4.3.2; R Studio) using the following packages: readxl, dplyr, data.table, Table 1, nnet, mice. P-values of less than 0.05 (2-sided) were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 shows descriptive statistics of students stratified by school bullying victimization. Of 27,859 participants, 5,885 (21.1%) experienced school bullying victimization, regardless of the type of bullying. The proportion of those who experienced physical, verbal, and relational bullying victimization among participants is 6.3%, 14.7%, and 10.6%, respectively. Compared with students who had not experienced school bullying this semester, school bullying victims were more likely to be male, in grades 5–9, only child, and report parental divorce, low parental highest education, poor family economic status, poor self-reported health status, and parental absence status (p < 0.05).

Table 2 presents unintentional injuries of all 12 types reported by students’ parents and the number of unintentional injuries suffered. Of 12 types of unintentional injuries, falls (44.1%) had the highest incidence, followed by eating foreign objects (28.0%); contacting with sharp instruments (26.4%); bitten or struck by animals (24.6%). Remarkably, school bullying victims had a significantly higher rate of all 12 unintentional injuries, compared with non-victims (all p < 0.001). In addition, compared with non-victims, school bullying victims suffered more unintentional injuries in the past year (2.80 [2.48] vs. 1.56 [1.89], p < 0.001).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to employ a comprehensive, population-based survey to investigate the association between an array of unintentional injuries and diverse forms of school bullying victimization sustained by children and adolescents outside of clinical settings in China. In this study, a total of 10 types of unintentional injuries were found to be associated with a higher prevalence of bullying victimization, with children and adolescents whose parents reported being struck by thrown, projected, or falling objects demonstrating the greatest vulnerability to school bullying victimization, followed by accidental drowning and submersion, contact with sharp instrument, etc. Moreover, the more types of unintentional injuries children and adolescents experienced, the higher the likelihood that they were exposed to school bullying. These associations varied among children and adolescents who reported the presence of multiple forms of bullying.

Our study, echoing prior research, found a positive link between unintentional injuries and school bullying victimization [23, 24]. Nevertheless, a panel analysis, drawing from a German survey spanning four waves, found no immediate causal relationship between bullying victimization and increased risks of unintentional injuries among students and attributed the cross-sectional association demonstrated earlier to a shared characteristic between bullying victims and injury sufferers [25]. Although this is the case, it’s crucial to note the methodological differences between these studies. Our research utilized parent-reported data on unintentional injuries of children and broadened the scope of bullying to encompass multiple forms, thus offering a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon.

Moreover, an enduring and affirmative correlation between accidental drowning and submersion, as well as instances of being struck by thrown, projected, or falling objects, with bullying victimization has been discerned, spanning diverse bullying scenarios and multiple victimization instances. As for accidental drowning and submersion, drowning incidents frequently originate from bullying scenarios, where students may be coerced or physically propelled by their peers, yet these occurrences are often misinterpreted by parents as innocuous playfulness among peers [26]. Drowning stands as one of the top ten lethal causes for children and adolescents aged 5 to 19, having exacted a toll of 2.5 million lives over the preceding decade [27]. As children may lack adult supervision, parents must be vigilant about the drowning risk of their children and endeavor to ascertain the underlying causes of such incidents to prevent recurrence of drowning and mitigate the broader impacts of bullying.

Concerning the matter of being struck by thrown, projected or falling objects, the strong association we observed may stem from the fact that throwing something at others is a common form of physical bullying [28]. While the resulting bruises and other injuries are often noticed by parents, the victim, due to shame or other reasons, may tend to conceal the true cause of these injuries akin to concealed incidents of domestic violence and self-harm. A study based on the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System’s All Injury Program, indicated that intimate partner violence victims often misreported accidental falls for their injuries to conceal intimate partner violence when seeking medical treatment [29]. And a significant positive correlation between children’s contact with sharp instruments and varying frequencies of self-harm behaviors was also revealed, inferring that some instances of self-harm or attempted suicide may be masked as accidental injuries [21]. Additionally, a meta-analysis that included 18 cross-sectional studies summarized a link between the physical inactivity and an increased risk of bullying victimization, suggesting that this could be a response to experiencing physical bullying during exercise, leading to a withdrawal from physical activities [30]. Therefore, recurrent injuries sustained from thrown, projected or falling objects among children and adolescents should raise suspicion as a potential indicator of bullying victimization, particularly when coupled with a child’s aversion to physical exercise.

Furthermore, when compared to their counterparts, individuals who experienced poisoning or drowning may have endured multiple types of peer victimization simultaneously, rather than a single type. Several studies have demonstrated that the deleterious effects of bullying are often amplified when they occur concurrently [31–33]. Severe bullying may induce a trance-like state in victims, causing them to disregard their own safety and life, thereby increasing the risk of unintentional injuries. Moreover, a greater extent of bullying victimization corresponds to a greater frequency of suicide attempts [34, 35]. Drowning and poisoning could potentially be disguised acts of self-harm, mistakenly interpreted as accidental occurrences. Hence, parents need to attach great importance to these two types of ostensibly “accidental” injuries, given the potential underlying severe bullying victimization and its associated internal and external challenges.

Unsurprisingly, our research also revealed that experiencing a greater variety of unintentional injuries simultaneously was associated with an increased prevalence of bullying victimization, which held true for both 1 and 2–3 types of bullying. This may better reflect the multiple vulnerabilities of bullied individuals via direct or indirect pathways as mentioned above. It’s not always the child who asks for help that needs it, but the one who doesn’t. Unintentional injuries may act as a catalyst for parents to discern and address the underlying tribulations of school bullying that their children might be facing, thereby providing crucial support and intervention.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, although bullying can extend beyond school hours and into other contexts, unintentional injuries reported by parents may have occurred outside the school context and may not necessarily be connected to bullying experiences, which limits the extent to which some injuries can be interpreted as indicators or warning signs of bullying. Secondly, despite the exceptionally large sample size, it’s important to note that this study was confined to children and adolescents aged 11–17 within a single county in China, which might restrict the generalization of our findings. Thirdly, the assessment of unintentional injuries in this study relied solely on a set of dichotomous questions, limiting our exploration to the correlation between recurring unintentional injuries and bullying victimization. Future research should delve into the connection between the frequency of significant unintentional injuries and bullying victimization, providing parents with more targeted guidance. Fourthly, as with the feature of any cross-sectional study, only association but no causal link could be determined. Lastly, we did not assess underlying mechanisms linking unintentional injuries to bullying victimization. Thus, future studies ought to employ longitudinal data to conduct pathway analysis, which could help figure out the potential possibilities so as to deepen the understanding of bullying victimization.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study found associations between certain types of unintentional injuries and the number of injuries suffered simultaneously from a parental perspective, and children’s bullying victimization. Therefore, parents are advised to vigilantly scrutinize the unintentional injuries in their offspring in order to promptly identify potential bullying victimization and take appropriate responsive actions.
