Evaluation of the color adjustment potential of single-shade universal composites in 3D-printed teeth under different ambient light sources: an in vitro study
Authors: Merve Aksoy Yüksek, İkbal Esra Pehlivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe objective of this study was to evaluate the color adjustment potential (CAP) of single-shade composites in three-dimensional (3D)-printed teeth with a Black’s Class IV cavity design under different ambient lighting conditions.MethodsThree different single-shade composites—GC Essentia Universal (GC), Omnichroma (OMNI), and Zenchroma (ZEN)—were tested. Both dual and single specimens were prepared. Dual specimens were prepared by restoring 3D-printed teeth with built-in Class IV cavities using resin composites. Single specimens were prepared by making resin composite replicas from intact tooth specimens (n = 10). The 3D-printed teeth served as the control group for intact teeth. Color measurements were performed using a spectrophotometer for 24 h, 1 week, and 1 month. The CAP-I values were calculated. For statistical analysis, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality, followed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare the groups.ResultsThe CAP of the resin composites varied depending on the type of ambient light (p < 0.001). At 3000 K, GC exhibited a statistically significantly higher CAP value than OMNI (p = 0.001). At 6500 K, OMNI exhibited a statistically considerably lower CAP value than GC and ZEN (p = 0.001).ConclusionsThe CAP of single-shade composites may be influenced by ambient lighting conditions. In restorations with extensive tissue loss, such as Black’s Class IV cavity designs, considering the effects of resin composite selection and ambient light can help improve aesthetic outcomes.Clinical relevanceEvaluating aesthetic harmony under different ambient lighting conditions during the application of single-shade composite restorations in anterior restorations may help clinicians achieve more predictable aesthetic outcomes.Keywords: Color adjustment potential, Single-shade composites, Blending, Color

Background

In restorative dentistry, treatment success depends not only on functional outcomes but also on aesthetic results, such as the optical integration of the restoration with the natural tooth structure. Achieving harmony in color, shape, size, position, and function is fundamental to both aesthetic and functional success. This is especially critical in anterior restorations, where color mismatches can significantly impact patient acceptance and satisfaction [1, 2]. Consequently, precise shade selection is regarded as one of the most crucial factors in achieving clinical success in restorative treatments [3].

Natural dentition exhibits a wide range of color tones, with distinct chromatic variations observed across different anatomical regions of the tooth. To achieve a natural aesthetic outcome in resin composite restorations, clinicians often base their approach on layering resins with varying opacities and shades [4]. In conventional multi-shade resin composite systems, selecting the appropriate shades and applying them in layered increments is a process that demands both clinical experience and considerable time [5].

Single-shade universal composites are innovative materials designed to simplify the restorative process while delivering consistent aesthetic results. These resin composites exhibit advanced optical properties known as the “chameleon effect,” which enables them to adapt their color to match the surrounding tooth structure [6]. This effect results from how light is scattered and absorbed within the resin composite, allowing it to respond effectively to different lighting conditions. The material’s translucency and filler particle distribution closely mimic the natural way light diffuses through tooth enamel, enhancing its ability to match colors across various conditions [7].

In this context, single-shade universal composites—developed to shorten clinical procedure times and simplify the shade selection process—offer the potential to adapt to a wide range of tooth colors [6]. Single-shade materials have gained popularity as practical, time-saving alternatives. However, the optical adaptation performance of single-shade universal composites may vary depending on factors such as the surrounding tooth color, ambient lighting conditions, and cavity design [5, 8, 9]. These variables can directly influence the clinical success of restorations.

In dentistry, color adjustment potential (CAP) describes a restorative material’s ability to blend visually with the surrounding natural tooth structure. Various studies in the literature have reported that the CAP values of different materials can vary depending on factors such as the material’s optical properties, thickness, translucency level, type and size of fillers, storage time, and the color of the surrounding tissues [10–12]. A review of the literature indicates that there is virtually no research on the CAP of resin composite materials used in Black Class IV fracture restorations in terms of cavity design. Moreover, comparative studies on the impact of various light sources on CAP are absent in the current literature.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the CAP of different single-shade universal composite materials in Black Class IV cavity designs under various ambient light sources representative of phone flash, clinical, office, and natural daylight environments. Accordingly, the effect of metamerism—defined as the phenomenon in which colors are perceived differently under different light sources—was investigated. The first null hypothesis was that there is no statistically significant difference in CAP among the tested single-shade universal composites. The second null hypothesis stated that different ambient light sources do not have a statistically significant effect on the CAP of these materials.

Methods

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using a priori power analysis in G*Power 3.1 for a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A moderate effect size (f = 0.25), 80% statistical power (1 − β = 0.80), and a 5% significance level (α = 0.05) were assumed. Based on these parameters, a minimum of 11 specimens per group was calculated. However, because previous in vitro studies with similar designs commonly used 10–12 specimens per group, the present study was conducted with 10 specimens per group (n = 10). This sample size was deemed sufficient to detect statistically meaningful differences among the groups.

Specimen preparation

In this study, 3D-printed teeth were fabricated by Edudent Educational Materials (İstanbul, Turkey) using HEX284 (Hexagon İleri Malzemeler ve Teknolojiler Tic. Ltd. Şti., İstanbul, Turkey), a urethane acrylate-based ultraviolet light curable resin. As the material does not have bio-compatibility certification, it is classified as a model resin. No specific optical property data is available from the manufacturer. Intact tooth models and Black’s Class IV cavity designs were digitally designed with the bevel incorporated into the model and fabricated using a three-dimensional (3D) printer (Fig. 1). For Class IV cavities, a 1 mm-wide bevel, oriented at 45° was incorporated along the enamel margins during the digital design phase. Incorporating the bevel at the design stage standardized the cavity geometry across all specimens and eliminated the variability associated with manual bevel preparation. The digitally integrated bevel was intended to simulate clinical cavity preparation better and to promote a more homogeneous optical transition at the tooth–restoration interface, thereby enabling a more reliable evaluation of the CAP of the tested resin composite materials.

Workflow of the experimental design for CAP evaluation

In this study, the CAP of three single-shade universal composite materials was evaluated. Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the resin composites. Two types of specimens were prepared. (1) Single specimens were fabricated by replicating intact 3D-printed teeth in resin composite using a silicone index (Fig. 2). The resin composite replicas were light-cured for 20 s from both surfaces using a light emitting diode (LED) curing unit (Elipar S10, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA; 1200 mW/cm²). (2) Dual specimens were prepared by restoring 3D-printed teeth with built-in Class IV cavities using a silicone index (Fig. 2).

During preparation, the cavity surfaces of the denture teeth were etched (GC Etchant, 35% phosphoric acid, GC, Tokyo, Japan) for 20 s according to the manufacturer’s instructions to enhance the adhesion of the resin composite. Next, the teeth were rinsed with water and air-dried for 10 s at 0.25 MPa from approximately 2–5 mm distance. Subsequently, a universal adhesive (G-Premio Bond, GC, Tokyo, Japan) was applied by scrubbing with a micro brush. The adhesive was air-thinned for 5 s at 0.25 MPa from approximately 2–5 mm distance. Next, the adhesive was light cured for 20 s using an LED curing unit. The cavities were restored with a resin composite using a silicone index, and the resin composite was light-cured for 20 s with an LED curing unit. The light power density (1200 mW/cm²) was measured every five specimens using a digital dental radiometer (Hilux Light Meter, Benlioğlu, Türkiye).

The single and dual resin composite specimens were finished and polished using a two-step system (Clearfil Twist Dia; Kuraray, Tokyo, Japan). Intact 3D-printed tooth specimens served as the control group. The specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C until use.

Color measurements

A darkened enclosure was constructed for tooth color measurements, with an opening designed to accommodate the spectrophotometer tip. Type A silicone sockets were prepared for the roots and secured within the measurement apparatus. For color evaluations, the specimens were consistently positioned in these sockets by their roots. Ambient lighting conditions were simulated using a device capable of producing a range of Kelvin values. The device was positioned within the apparatus to illuminate the specimens at a 45° angle. The lights used in the study, along with their Kelvin values and characteristics, are summarized in Table 2.

A spectrophotometer (Spectroshade Micro, Medical High Technologies, Italy) was used to measure tooth color, and the device was calibrated every five measurements. Color measurements were performed on three composites under four lighting conditions at three times: 24 h, 1 week, and 1 month. The L, a, and b values were recorded for each measurement, enabling ΔE calculations.

ΔEsingle represents the color difference between intact teeth and resin composite replicas. In contrast, ΔEdual represents the color difference between intact teeth and teeth restored with resin composite. ΔE was calculated using the following formula: ΔE = [(ΔL) ² + (Δa) ² + (Δb) ²] ½ [18]. Subsequently, the color adjustment potential-instrumental (CAP-I) was calculated using the formula: CAP-I: 1- (ΔEdual / ΔEsingle) [19]. For each resin composite, the CAP values were recorded at specified times under each ambient light source.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi software (version 2.7.6). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As CAP values were not normally distributed across resin composite types, light sources, and measurement times, a robust ANOVA was applied using the Walrus package. Where appropriate, post hoc comparisons were conducted using Holm-adjusted robust t-tests. Descriptive statistics for quantitative variables are presented as trimmed means with standard errors (SE). The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

When data were analyzed without regard to light source or measurement time, a statistically significant difference in mean CAP values was observed among the tested composites (p < 0.001). GC Essentia Universal had the highest CAP value (0.06 ± 0.025), followed by Zenchroma (ZEN) (− 0.07 ± 0.01), with Omnichroma (OMNI) showing the lowest (− 0.13 ± 0.015). All three resin composites differed significantly from one another (p < 0.001). Higher CAP values represent improved color adjustment potential, whereas lower or negative values indicate reduced blending and aesthetic integration with the surrounding tooth structure.

A statistically significant difference in CAP values was also observed among the tested light sources, regardless of resin composite type and measurement time (p < 0.001). CAP values under 3000 K illumination (− 0.08 ± 0.025) were significantly lower than those under 4000 K (− 0.05 ± 0.019) and 6500 K (− 0.02 ± 0.022) lighting conditions (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were detected among the remaining light sources.

Measurement time significantly affected CAP values, regardless of resin composite type or light source (p = 0.01). CAP values at T3 (1 month) (-0.23 ± 0.012) were significantly lower than those at T1 (24 h) (0.02 ± 0.019) and T2 (1 week) (p = 0.01) (0.05 ± 0.012), whereas no significant difference was observed between T1 and T2 (p > 0.05).

The interaction between resin composite type and light source was statistically significant (p = 0.001). At 3000 K, GC (0.06 ± 0.055) had significantly higher CAP values than OMNI (-0.16 ± 0.031) and ZEN (-0.12 ± 0.026) (p = 0.001), while ZEN had higher CAP values than Omnichroma without reaching statistical significance. No significant differences among resin composites were observed at 4000 K and 5500 K (p > 0.05). At 6500 K, OMNI (-0.16 ± 0.028) had significantly lower CAP values than both GC (0.11 ± 0.047) and ZEN (0 ± 0.013) (p = 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed between GC and ZEN (p > 0.05).

A significant resin composite × time interaction was observed (p = 0.001). At T1 and T2, GC (0.25 ± 0.023 at T1; 0.19 ± 0.012 at T2) had significantly higher CAP values than OMNI (-0.07 ± 0.018 at T1; -0.02 ± 0.008 at T2) and ZEN (-0.09 ± 0,023 at T1; -0.01 ± 0.012 at T2) (p = 0.001), with no significant difference between OMNI and ZEN (p > 0.05). At T3, ZEN (-0.11 ± 0.01) showed significantly higher CAP values than GC (-0.27 ± 0.017) and OMNI (-0.32 ± 0.011), whereas no significant difference was observed between GC and OMNI (p = 0.001).Within-resin composite analysis showed that CAP values for GC and OMNI were significantly lower at T3 than at T1 and T2 (p = 0.001), with no significant difference between T1 and T2 (p > 0.05). In contrast, ZEN had significantly higher CAP values at T2 (-0.01 ± 0.012) than at T1(-0.09 ± 0,023) and T3 (-0.11 ± 0.01) (p = 0.001), with no significant difference between T1 and T3 (p > 0.05).

The interaction between light source and measurement time was also statistically significant (p = 0.001). Across all light sources, CAP values at T3 were significantly lower than those at T1 and T2, whereas T1 and T2 did not differ significantly. No statistically significant differences were found among the light sources at the same measurement time.

Finally, the significant three-way interaction (resin composite × light source × time, p = 0.001) indicates that the effect of light source on CAP values varied across resin composite materials and measurement times. Detailed descriptive statistics and post hoc comparisons for all interaction effects are presented in Tables 4 and 5, and the distribution of mean CAP values by resin composite type, light source, and time is illustrated in Fig. 3. As presented in Fig. 3, at 1 week, the CAP values of the tested resin composites exhibited distinct trends depending on the light source. For Zen, CAP increased at all light sources except 5500 K. GC exhibited decreased CAP at all light sources except 4000 K. OMNI showed an increase in CAP at 3000 K and 6500 K, but a decrease at 4000 K and 5500 K.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that the CAP of single-shade universal composites is significantly influenced by both the material type and ambient lighting conditions. GC exhibited the highest CAP values, followed by ZEN and OMNI. Therefore, the first null hypothesis—stating that no significant differences exist among the resin composites—was rejected. CAP values also varied across different ambient light sources, and the time-dependent analysis showed an increase over one week and a decline over one month. The second null hypothesis—stating that ambient lighting does not affect CAP—was also rejected.

Shade-matching performance can vary among resin composites, as different single-shade systems interact differently with tooth and background shades. In a study by Erçin et al. in which artificial teeth in shades A1, A2, and A3 were used, OMNI exhibited better shade matching than ZEN in teeth with A1 shade [20]. Consistent with the findings of Erçin et al., ZEN demonstrated the lowest CAP values against the A1 and A2 background shades. In another study, the effect of background shade variation on the CAP values of OMNI, ZEN, Vittra APS Unique, and Clearfil Majesty Universal composites was evaluated [21]. In contrast to these studies, the present investigation utilized 3D-printed teeth produced exclusively in the B1 shade, suggesting that the shade-matching performance of resin composites may be sensitive to variations in both tooth and background color.

Recent literature indicates that cavity configuration and the degree of support from the surrounding tooth structure strongly influence the optical blending behavior of resin composites. Several studies have evaluated CAP in different cavity configurations, particularly Class I, Class III, and Class V restorations, where the surrounding tooth structure influences optical blending. In a study investigating the effect of cavity wall thickness, wall thicknesses of 1 mm, 2 mm, and 3 mm were tested, and a reduction in thickness was shown to negatively affect CAP [12]. Similarly, Akgül et al. reported that restorations in Class V cavities with a 3-mm depth exhibited higher CAP values than those with a 2-mm depth [10]. Additionally, the effect of bevel application on CAP has been investigated. Sarı et al. reported that bevel applications had no significant effect on the CAP of resin composites; however, studies on this topic are limited [22]. In this study bevel was applied to standardize the procedure and optimize both the adhesive surface area and aesthetic integration of resin composite restorations.

Collectively, these findings indicate that factors such as cavity depth, resin composite thickness, and cavity wall thickness play essential roles in determining CAP. Ruiz-Lopez et al. assessed CAP in different cavity configurations and found that Black’s Class I restorations exhibited better shade matching than Black’s Class V restorations, particularly in darker teeth [23]. Taken together, these findings indicate that adequate support of the restoration by surrounding and underlying tooth structure contributes positively to shade-matching performance.

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have evaluated Class IV cavity designs in this context. In contrast to previous research, this study used a Class IV cavity design to investigate how the absence of an underlying tooth structure affected the color match and CAP of the resin composite.

Ambient lighting is another key factor in color matching. Restorations that appear well matched under one light source may look less harmonious under another, underscoring the importance of ambient lighting for the aesthetics of resin composite restorations. In most studies, shade evaluations have been performed under a single light source such as CIE D65 standard illuminant or natural daylight [10, 11, 16, 17, 24, 25]. Given that the characteristics of the light source can markedly affect color perception and measurement, this highlights a significant gap in the current research. To date, however, the influence of different light sources on the CAP of resin composites has not been systematically investigated. Therefore, this study sought to explore how varying illumination conditions affect CAP, providing a more detailed understanding of the optical behavior of these materials.

This study demonstrated that the type of ambient light source can significantly influence the CAP of resin composite restorations. According to the results, a restoration that appears well-matched under clinical fluorescent lighting (4000 K) or natural daylight (6500 K) may appear less well-matched under office LED lighting (3000 K). The lower CAP values observed under 3000 K for OMNI and ZEN may be related to the warmer spectral characteristic of LED illumination (3000 K) compared the neutral 4000 K clinical fluorescent and cooler 6500 natural daylight conditions. Warm LED light has a spectral power distribution skewed tower the longer wavelengths, which could affect the way of resin composite fillers and 3D printed teeth and absorb light. Accordingly, this could have led to lower CAP values. The results indicate that ambient light can affect how restorations blend with surrounding teeth. As stated above, the second null hypothesis was also rejected, as ambient light sources were found to significantly influence CAP.

Building these findings three-way interaction (Composite × Light × Time) further highlights how both the type of resin composite and the evaluation period can modulate the effect of ambient light on CAP values. The three-way interaction indicates that the effect of the light source on CAP values varied according to both the composite material and the evaluation period. Although lighting conditions influenced the initial CAP measurements, OMNI and ZEN demonstrated consistently lower CAP values at the one-month evaluation across all light sources, suggesting that time-dependent changes became more influential than the initial lighting effect. In contrast, GC exhibited comparatively highest CAP values over time. Clinically, this finding implies that the initial color match observed under specific lighting conditions may not reliably predict the one-month aesthetic outcome, particularly for certain single-shade universal composites.

Previous studies have employed both instrumental and visual methods for CAP evaluation, with several studies reporting no significant differences between the two methods [17, 25–27]. Instrumental evaluation is considered advantageous because it can eliminate certain human-related variables, such as fatigue, age, and visual impairments. It can also minimize the influence of metamerism, which arises from differences in material and light properties rather than human factors. In the present study, a spectrophotometer was used for instrumental color evaluation. However, the findings indicate that instrumental measurement cannot eliminate metamerism and that different light sources alter the color values of resin composites.

This study demonstrated that the CAP varied among the tested resin composites. GC exhibited higher CAP values than OMNI and ZEN, while ZEN showed higher CAP values than OMNI. These differences may be attributed to variations in material composition, filler content, and optical properties, which influence how resin composites interact with light and integrate optically with the surrounding tooth structure. GC exhibited the highest CAP values than the other materials. This can be attributed to better filler–matrix refractive index matching, homogeneous light scattering from its nano–micro hybrid filler structure, and a pigment/translucency balance that allows for more effective optical integration with surrounding tissue.

In contrast, ZEN, which contains micro- and nano pigments and exhibits more limited translucency, has higher opacity. This higher opacity decreases the restoration’s ability to transmit light from the surrounding tooth structure, resulting in lower CAP values than those observed for Essentia. However, these values remain higher than those of OMNI. The lowest CAP values observed for OMNI may be attributed to its color formation, which is entirely based on the structural color principle and Mie scattering from spherical particles. This mechanism can produce a strong chameleon effect in cavity types with adequate underlying support. However, in cavity designs such as Class IV, where such support is lacking, insufficient control of light from the surrounding tissue limits color matching. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting that material composition influences the CAP of resin composites [9, 11, 17, 23].

Similar to this study, Altınışık et al. reported that GC had higher CAP values than OMNI [25]. In contrast, Kovacks-Vajna et al. reported that OMNI had better CAP values than GC [28]. This was attributed to the increased translucency of OMNI on a black background, which resulted in higher CAP values. However, it has been reported that this may not always provide a clinical benefit. Sanchez et al. and Kobayashi et al. reported that OMNI showed higher CAP values [16, 27]. In Sanchez et al.’s study, denture teeth were used similarly to this study. However, unlike this study, the evaluation of only OMNI as a single-shade universal composite material and the use of a Black Class I cavity design may have partially contributed to the differences observed between their findings and those of this study [27]. In Kobayashi et al., only OMNI was evaluated as a single-shade universal composite and compared with conventional A2-shade resin composites. In addition, the use of human incisor teeth and cervical cavity designs supported by the underlying tooth structure may have contributed to the differences between their findings and those of this study [16].

OMNI contains no color pigments. Instead, its nanoparticles are engineered and arranged to manipulate light reflection, allowing the resin composite to blend with the surrounding tooth shade [29]. ZEN, in contrast, uses Smart Chromatic technology and incorporates micro- and nano-sized color pigments. While this enables color adaptation, its limited transparency and light-reflecting properties can reduce effectiveness in very light or very dark teeth [30]. GC achieves shade matching through a careful balance of pigment and translucency, performing best on light to medium shades [31]. In this study, the 3D-printed teeth had a noticeably light B1 shade. Although all three resin composites were indicated for use in Black’s Class IV cavities by their manufacturers, none achieved an acceptable CAP value under the conditions of this study. This may be due to the use of Black’s Class IV cavity design, which lacks support from the underlying enamel and dentin. In such cases, the color adaptation and chameleon effect of all three resin composites were found to be limited.

The influence of storage time was also evaluated. In this study, color measurements were performed at three time points: 24 h, 1 week, and 1 month. For both OMNI and ZEN, higher CAP values were observed at 1 week than at 24 h. This may be due to post-polymerization being nearly complete after one week and to an increase in the refractive index during this period [32, 33]. The lowest CAP values were recorded after 1 month, regardless of the material used or the lighting conditions. This may be attributed to water sorption, hydrolytic degradation of the resin composites, surface degradation, and microcrack formation in the interface contours [34].

In contrast to the present results, Akgül et al. reported higher CAP values after 1 month of storage in distilled water than at 24 h [10]. Differences in the materials tested, cavity designs, and ambient lighting conditions may have contributed to the discrepancies between their findings and those of this study.

This study has several limitations. Although the use of 3D-printed teeth and standardized cavity preparations ensured consistency across specimens, these materials may not fully replicate the complex optical behavior of natural enamel and dentin, including fluorescence, opalescence, and the multilayered structure of natural teeth. In addition, the exclusive use of B1-shade 3D-printed resin teeth is a limitation because it cannot represent the wide range of tooth shades encountered in clinical practice. As an in vitro study, the experimental design could not fully replicate intraoral factors such as humidity, saliva, exposure to dietary substances, oral hygiene practices, and temperature variations. Given these limitations, future research may benefit from incorporating natural tooth substrates and evaluating a broader range of tooth shades. Simulating oral conditions through thermal cycling or saliva-like storage media may also yield more clinically relevant results, and in vivo studies could provide further insight into the clinical behavior of these materials. In addition, CAP assessments provide an estimate of how closely the restorative material can approximate the natural tooth structure in terms of color matching. However, CAP evaluation alone may not be sufficient for a comprehensive clinical assessment of color matching. Complementing CAP-I with additional evaluations, such as the color blending threshold in the study by Kovacks-Vajna et al., or color adjustment potential-visual (CAP-V) evaluations in the study by Zhu et al., may be effective in evaluating color matching [28, 35].

Conclusions

The CAP of single-shade universal composites is influenced by both material type and ambient lighting conditions. The blending ability of these resin composites was limited in cavity designs lacking underlying tooth support. Storage time also affected CAP values, with the highest values observed at 1 week and a decline after 1 month. The effect of storage time on CAP suggested changes in color adaptation over the first month. These findings suggest the importance of considering both resin composite materials and ambient lighting conditions to improve aesthetic outcomes. They also indicate that future studies using natural teeth, and a wider range of shades are needed to better understand clinical performance.
