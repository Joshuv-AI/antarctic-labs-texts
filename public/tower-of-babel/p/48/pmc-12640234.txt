Technology support preferences for prospective memory among older adults with subjective cognitive complaints
Authors: Edie C Sanders, Walter R Boot, Michelle Putnam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesProspective memory (PM), the ability to remember to execute an intention in the future, is critical for performing everyday tasks contributing to health, financial, and social outcomes. PM declines with greater age and in the presence of a cognitive impairment. Toward the goal of developing technology-based support for older adults experiencing cognitive challenges, this study aimed to assess the attitudes of older adults with subjective cognitive complaints (SCCs) toward technology to support PM and their preferences regarding device type and the type of activity being supported.Research Design and MethodsIn an online survey, 188 older adults with SCCs and 190 older adults without SCCs indicated their attitudes toward 3 technologies to support PM (mobile apps, smartwatches, voice-activated assistants) and toward 6 PM-related activity areas (relationships and social activities, healthcare activities, transportation, shopping, housework and laundry, managing finances).Results, Discussion, and ImplicationsResults showed that attitudes toward technology to support PM among older adults with SCCs were relatively positive overall and (1) differed based on the type of device and the type of activity the technology is meant to support, (2) were more positive than attitudes of older adults without SCCs, and (3) were associated with individual difference factors such as general attitudes toward technology, technology proficiency, frequency of memory failures, health, and age. Results indicate older adults with SCCs might prefer using mobile apps relative to other devices and technology to support healthcare relative to supporting other activities. Further, older adults with SCCs showed privacy concerns for voice-activated assistants and technology to manage finances. Older adults with SCCs are a promising population to target using technology-based solutions, and the type of device and type of activity being supported are important to consider while developing technology solutions to enhance independence and well-being.Keywords: Assistive technology, Reminder aids, Cognitive impairment

This study addressed a gap in the knowledge of preferences among older adults with subjective cognitive complaints regarding technology-based support for prospective memory (PM). Results showed that attitudes toward technology-based PM support among this population were positive overall and varied based on the type of device (showing a preference for mobile apps), and the type of activity being supported (showing a preference for supporting healthcare activities). Results can inform the development of acceptable—and ultimately effective—technology-based interventions for PM, a crucial factor in enhancing everyday independence and wellbeing among older adults experiencing cognitive challenges.

Background and objectives

Prospective memory (PM), the ability to remember and carry out an intention in the future (Henry et al., 2004), is crucial for maintaining health, independence, and social connections. Failing to remember tasks, such as taking medication, paying bills, or attending appointments, can lead to health decline, financial hardship, and isolation. PM ability tends to decline with age, particularly for strategically demanding tasks (Henry et al., 2004; Kliegel et al., 2016; Park et al., 1997) and is linked to the successful performance of daily living activities among older adults (Hering et al., 2018; Tierney et al., 2016; Woods et al., 2012; Woods et al., 2015). PM decline represents an issue for functional independence in older adulthood, especially among those reporting cognitive challenges.

Subjective cognitive complaints (SCCs) are self-reported difficulties in areas of cognition, such as memory and thinking, and are an important factor in an individual’s well-being. The presence of SCCs is a prerequisite for subjective cognitive decline (SCD), the self-reported experience of more frequent or worsening memory loss and/or confusion in the absence of objective cognitive impairment (National Association of Chronic Disease Directors, 2018; Jessen et al., 2014). According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 11.7% of adults aged 65 years and older in the United States have SCD, compared to 10.8% of adults 45–64 years of age. SCD is one of the earliest noticeable symptoms of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (Alzheimer’s Association, 2018; Jessen et al., 2014), though it does not imply a diagnosis of cognitive decline by a healthcare professional. Individuals with cognitive complaints such as those consistent with SCD are more likely to progress to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia compared to older adults without cognitive complaints (Mitchell et al., 2014; Parfenov et al., 2020), but cognition in these cases can also reverse or remain stable (Jessen et al., 2020).

Nearly 40% of adults aged 65 years and older with SCD report difficulties completing everyday tasks and/or engaging in activities outside of the home, many of which rely on the functioning of PM (Anderson et al., 2015; National Association of Chronic Disease Directors, 2018; Jessen et al., 2014). Compared to older adults without SCD, older adults with SCD have been shown to perform worse on naturalistic, time-based PM tasks, such as returning a postcard a week after being instructed (Hsu et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2018; Rabin et al., 2014) and show greater decline in habitual PM, which involves repeatedly completing a task at the same time on a regular basis (Kamberis et al., 2021). The presence of these challenges in the absence of objective cognitive impairment, and the fact that 36.2% of adults aged 65 years and older with SCD live alone and may have little support for everyday activities (National Association of Chronic Disease Directors, 2018), indicate the importance of targeting technology-based support toward older adults experiencing subjective cognitive challenges. Solutions targeting areas of difficulty, particularly PM, may make a substantial difference in maintaining independence and quality of life for this population.

Technology holds promise for providing everyday PM support for older adults experiencing cognitive challenges. Rather than calendars or diaries that require the user to remember to check them to receive reminders, technology devices can attract the user’s attention and instruct them to carry out specified actions at the appropriate time, potentially providing more appropriate support for individuals with impaired PM (Henry et al., 2004; McDonald et al., 2011; Tierney et al., 2016; Woods et al., 2015). Mobile phone apps (e.g., Google Calendar) may be convenient to users who already own a mobile phone, and they can provide notifications that may serve as more effective reminders than notes written on paper (McDonald et al., 2011). Wearable devices (e.g., smartwatches) may provide unique benefits as they can move with the person, can be used as an aid inside and outside of the home, and can send visual, audio, and tactile alerts that can be highly noticeable to the user but subtle enough to not be disruptive in social settings (Charness et al., 2020; Ehrler & Lovis, 2014). Further, voice-activated assistants (VAAs; e.g., Amazon Echo Dot) can provide PM support while bypassing the motor elements required for the operation of other technologies (Aggarwal et al., 2006; Wasserman et al., 2020).

However, older adults, especially those experiencing cognitive challenges, may face barriers in using and adopting these devices, limiting the technologies’ potential to support independence in older age. A “digital divide” persists in the United States; approximately 12% of adults aged 65 years and older are not online, and approximately 24% do not own a smartphone, compared to the near universal adoption and ownership of these technologies among younger adults (Pew Research Center, 2024). Models of technology acceptance such as the Technology Acceptance Model, the Senior Technology Acceptance Model, and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology have posited factors that contribute to technology acceptance and can be used to understand technology adoption in younger and older adults (Chen & Chan, 2014; Chen & Lou, 2020; Davis et al., 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003, 2012). The two most investigated and seemingly replicable factors predicting technology acceptance in the general population are perceived usefulness, the belief that technology will support an activity or enhance performance, and perceived ease of use, the perceived effort required to use the technology (Ma et al., 2021). Technology self-efficacy is also argued to play a major role in older adults’ technology acceptance (Chen & Chan, 2014; Chen & Lou, 2020; Czaja et al., 2006). Other identified factors to predict technology acceptance include privacy concerns, prior experience with technology, age, level of education, income level, race/ethnicity, and gender (Chen & Chan, 2014; Chen & Lou, 2020; Czaja et al., 2006; Davis et al., 1989; Mitzner et al., 2010, 2019). It is also possible that, compared to older adults without cognitive complaints, older adults with cognitive complaints may be more likely to perceive technology-based PM solutions to be more useful (Alwahaishi & Snášel, 2013), and they might be more willing to sacrifice some privacy to compensate for cognitive challenges (Beach et al., 2009).

For technological solutions to be acceptable and successful in terms of remediating problems, they must be designed to fit the needs and preferences of the users. Unfortunately, older adults experiencing cognitive challenges are often overlooked in technology design. To facilitate this population’s use of technology to support PM, it is important to first understand their attitudes and preferences toward such solutions. This work examined attitudes of older adults with SCC toward technology to support PM and their preferred type of support. In an online survey, older adults with cognitive complaints and those without cognitive complaints rated the perceived usefulness, privacy concern, and interest in various technologies to support PM in a variety of activity domains for themselves and for a hypothetical person experiencing PM challenges. Analyses assessed how attitudes toward technology to support PM were influenced by technology type and activity type, and whether attitudes were associated with individual difference factors such as perceived cognitive ability and technology proficiency.

Research design and methods

Participants

Participants were adults aged 60 years or older. Half, referred to here as SCC participants, reported cognitive complaints. The other half of the participants did not report cognitive complaints and are referred to here as non-SCC participants. Our SCC classification was modeled after CDC recommendations and studies of SCD and was assessed with the question “During the past 12 months, have you experienced difficulties with thinking or memory that are happening more often or are getting worse?” from an updated version of Module 13 of the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS; Anderson et al., 2015; National Association of Chronic Disease Directors, 2018), adapted to be administered online via Qualtrics. Participants were classified as SCC if they responded yes and were classified as non-SCC if they responded no. The present study did not include a standardized cognitive assessment and thus did not distinguish SCC from SCD or MCI, allowing for a cognitively diverse pool of potential participants. Self-reported cognitive complaints, a requisite of both SCD and MCI, are an important construct that could be used to help target technology solutions; these early stages may be crucial in setting the groundwork for long-term, adaptive, technology-based solutions.

Participants met all the following criteria: (1) were 60 years of age or older; (2) lived independently; (3) did not report serious difficulty seeing or hearing; and (4) were proficient in English. Participants were excluded if they reported that they had received a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease or other dementia.

Participants were recruited from the volunteer population of 3100+ adults age 60+ maintained by the Florida State University Institute for Successful Longevity (ISL) and from a third-party research recruitment website—Prolific. Prolific was used in addition to the ISL database to gain access to a wider pool of older adult participants across the United States.

Procedure

Prescreening

Potential participants completed a brief online prescreening in which they were asked to self-report their age, living situation, vision and hearing ability, English language proficiency, perceived cognitive decline, whether they have access to a tablet, laptop, and/or desktop computer with a microphone and webcam (to determine if they would be eligible for another study), and whether they have been diagnosed with Alzheimer’s or other dementias. Participants were then automatically redirected to a new page, which informed them of whether they were eligible to complete the online survey. Participants recruited in Prolific automatically received $1 for completing the prescreening, regardless of their eligibility for the study. Eligible participants recruited from ISL were asked to enter their contact information if they were interested in participating further and did not receive compensation for completing the prescreening.

Online survey

Participants recruited via Prolific were invited to complete the survey within Prolific. Participants recruited via ISL received an email with an explanation of the study and a link to complete the Qualtrics survey. After indicating consent to participate in the Qualtrics survey, participants completed the measures listed in Materials. SCC participants also completed the last three follow-up questions from the BRFSS-Module 13 (see Supplementary Material), while these questions were omitted for non-SCC participants. Upon survey completion, participants recruited via Prolific were automatically paid $12. Participants recruited from ISL were redirected to a separate Qualtrics form where they could provide their contact information to enter a raffle to win one of two $50 cash prizes.

Materials

Prospective memory technology scenarios

Participants were presented with a description of PM, then a description of a hypothetical person experiencing PM challenges (see the interview guide in Supplementary Material). Participants were randomly assigned to be presented with either “Jan,” a female persona, or “John,” a male persona. Participants were told that the person had recently learned of a technology device (mobile app, smartwatch, or VAA) that can be used to provide PM support. In every scenario, examples were provided for how the technology could be used to support PM (e.g., “The smartwatch can be used to receive reminders for upcoming tasks and/or events, check and edit [her] calendar, and create lists”). Participants completed questions about the first technology before being presented with the second technology, and so on to the third. The presentation order of the mobile app, smartwatch, and VAA was randomized across participants. In a second set of questions, the hypothetical person was presented as experiencing challenges in a specific activity domain (relationships and social activities, healthcare activities, transportation, shopping, housework and laundry, or managing finances). Participants completed questions about technology to support the first activity before being presented with the second activity, and so on to the sixth. The presentation order was randomized across participants.

Questions were adapted from Fowe and Boot (2022). For each type of technology and activity, participants were asked whether they agree with statements that (1) the technology would be useful to the person described in the scenario (useful), (2) they would recommend the technology to the person described in the scenario (recommend), (3) the person in the scenario should be concerned about their privacy when using the technology (privacy concern), (4) the participant themselves was interested in learning more about the technology (interest), and (5) the participant themselves would consider adopting the described technology (adopt). A Likert scale was presented with the following options: (1) Strongly Disagree, (2) Disagree, (3) Neutral, (4) Agree, and (5) Strongly Agree. For all analyses, privacy concern is reversed-scored and is referred to as data security so that higher scores on all questions indicate more positive attitudes toward the technology.

Questionnaire battery

In addition to the PM technology scenarios, participants completed a questionnaire battery. Each measure is described below.

Demographics.

A brief demographics survey asked participants about their age, gender, race, ethnicity, education, income, and health status.

Mobile Device Proficiency Questionnaire-16 item.

The Mobile Device Proficiency Questionnaire-16 (MDPQ-16) (Roque & Boot, 2021) is a 16-item measure of the ability to use a mobile device such as a smartphone or tablet. The MDPQ-16 asks about tasks related to device basics, communication, data and file storage, Internet, calendar, entertainment, privacy, and troubleshooting and software management. Participants are presented with a task such as “use the onscreen keyboard to type” and are asked to indicate how easily they can perform the task, or if they have never tried. Higher scores on the MDPQ-16 correspond to higher mobile device proficiency, with a maximum possible score of 40.

Technology Readiness Index 2.0.

The Technology Readiness Index 2.0 (TRI 2.0) (Parasuraman & Colby, 2015) is a 16-item measure of readiness to accept technology. The measures include statements such as “Technology gives people more control over their daily lives” and “People are too dependent on technology to do things for them.” Subjects indicate the extent to which they agree or disagree with each statement. The measures assess four domains of attitudes toward technology: optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity. Scores from the latter two subscales are reverse-scored to calculate the overall score. A higher overall score (out of five possible points) indicates more positive attitudes toward technology.

Senior Technology Acceptance scale.

Participants completed a 14-item scale measuring senior technology acceptance (Chen & Lou, 2020). Questions relate to attitudinal beliefs, control beliefs, gerontechnology anxiety, and health conditions. Scores for each domain are reported separately, with a maximum of 10 points for each domain.

Prospective and Retrospective Memory Questionnaire.

The Prospective and Retrospective Memory Questionnaire (PRMQ) (Smith et al., 2000) is a 16-item questionnaire that asks participants to rate the frequency with which they experience different types of memory failures on a 5-point scale from “Never” to “Very Often.” Scores from the PRMQ can be reported and interpreted in terms of memory type (prospective and retrospective), time span (long-term and short-term), and type of memory cue (self-cued and environmentally-cued). Higher scores on the PRMQ correspond to more frequent memory failures, with a maximum score of 5.

All questions regarding hypothetical technology scenarios were entered into a principal components analysis. A three-factor solution (using Varimax Rotation) was found, explaining 59% of observed variance. Questions related to perceived usefulness of the technology (useful) and whether participants would recommend the technology to a hypothetical person (recommend) were loaded onto one component. Questions related to data security were loaded onto another component. Questions related to interest in learning more about the technology (interest) and whether participants would consider adopting the technology (adopt) were loaded onto the third component. These factors are labeled as useful/recommend, data security, and interest/adopt and are used in all survey analyses.

A Bonferroni correction is applied to all pairwise comparisons, and Greenhouse-Geisser degrees of freedom are reported for cases where Mauchly’s test of sphericity is significant.

Overall attitudes

SCC participants’ ratings were averaged across all devices and activities to understand their overall attitudes toward technology to support PM. Participants generally agreed that technology solutions would be useful for the hypothetical person, or they would recommend the technology solutions to the hypothetical person (useful/recommend M = 4.50, SD = .45 on a 1–5 agreement scale; 5 = “strongly agree”). Participants also gave relatively positive ratings regarding their interest in learning more about or adopting and using the technology solutions (interest/adopt M = 3.67, SD = .93). Although participants were generally positive toward the technologies to support PM, they were relatively neutral in terms of their privacy concerns (data security M = 2.68, SD = 1.10).

Types of devices

Responses to the first set of PM technology questions were entered into a 2 (group) × 3 (device) × 3 (question) analysis of variance (ANOVA) with group (SCC, non-SCC) as a between-subject factor and device (mobile app, smartwatch, VAA) and question (useful/recommend, data security, interest/adopt) as within-subject factors. Analyses described in this section investigated whether the type of technology device influenced the attitudes of older adults with SCC toward technology to support PM (Figure 1).

Ratings of mobile apps, smartwatches, and voice-activated assistants (VAAs) to support prospective memory among SCC participants. Error bars indicate one standard error above and below the mean. Higher ratings on all questions indicate more positive attitudes. SCC = subjective cognitive complaints.

Interactions

Three-way interaction

There was a significant three-way interaction between device, question, and group, F(3.71, 1394.34) = 3.23, p = .014, η2 = 01, indicating that participants’ ratings of the devices differed by question (useful/recommend, data security, interest/adopt), and these trends differed between SCC and non-SCC participants.

Two-way interactions

There was a significant interaction between device and SCC status, F(1.95, 733.23) = 3.95, p = .021, η2 = 0.01, indicating that SCC and non-SCC older adults differed in their device preferences. There was also a significant interaction between question and SCC status, F(1.60, 601.51) = 8.21, p < .001, η2 = 0.02, indicating that SCC and non-SCC older adults differed in their ratings of devices to support PM. Finally, there was a significant interaction between device and question, F(3.71, 1394.34) = 23.96, p < .001, η2 = 0.06, indicating that older adults’ attitudes differed between the three devices differentially based on question (useful/recommend, data security, and interest/adopt).

Given these significant interactions and our focus on attitudes of SCC older adults, we proceeded by examining differences in device ratings among SCC participants.

Comparing device ratings among SCC participants

To examine how the attitudes of SCC older adults differed between the three devices, pairwise comparisons examine differences between devices for each question (useful/recommend, data security, interest/adopt) within the SCC participant group (Figure 1).

Mobile apps were rated significantly more positively than smartwatches for useful/recommend, Mdiff = 0.21, SE = 0.07, p = .004, and interest/adopt, Mdiff = 0.43, SE = 0.08, p < .001. Mobile apps and smartwatches did not significantly differ in terms of data security. Mobile apps were rated significantly more positively than VAAs for data security, Mdiff = 0.20, SE = 0.07, p = .007, and marginally more positively than VAAs for interest/adopt, Mdiff = 0.19, SE = 0.08, p = .068. VAAs were also rated significantly less positively for data security compared to smartwatches, Mdiff = −0.32, SE = 0.06, p < .001. However, VAAs were rated significantly more positively than smartwatches for useful/recommend, Mdiff = 0.20, SE = 0.07, p = .011, and adopt/interest, Mdiff = 0.24, SE = 0.09, p = .020.

Main effect of device

There was a significant main effect of device, F(1.95, 733.23) = 22.31, p < .001, η2 = 0.06, where the most positive attitudes among older adults were for mobile apps. Mobile apps were rated significantly more positively than smartwatches, Mdiff = 0.17, SE = 0.03, p < .001, and VAAs, Mdiff = 0.22, SE = 0.04, p < .001. Smartwatches and VAAs did not differ significantly from each other.

Comparing device preferences by SCC status

There was no significant main effect of participant group, indicating that SCC and non-SCC participants did not differ in their overall attitudes toward technologies to support PM. However, a significant interaction between group and device—and between group and question—suggest that SCC participants differed in their device preferences compared to non-SCC participants. Please see Supplementary Analysis 2 for detailed analyses comparing participant groups.

Types of activities

Responses to the second set of PM technology questions were entered into a 2 (group) × 3 (activity) × 3 (question) ANOVA with group (SCC, non-SCC) as a between-subject factor and activity (relationships and social activities, healthcare activities, shopping, housework, finance) and question (useful/recommend, data security, and interest/adopt) as within-subject factors. Analyses described in this section investigated whether the type of activity being supported influenced the attitudes of older adults with SCC toward technology to support PM (Figure 2).

Ratings of technology to support prospective memory among all online survey participants, separated by type of activity. Error bars indicate one standard error above and below the mean. Higher ratings on all questions indicate more positive attitudes.

Interactions

Three-way interaction

There was no significant three-way interaction between activity, question, and group.

Two-way interactions

There was not a significant interaction between activity and group, indicating that SCC and non-SCC older adults did not substantially differ in their relative preferences for technology to support PM in different activity domains. However, there was a significant interaction between question and group, F(1.59, 599.04) = 9.83, p < .001, η2 = 0.03, indicating that SCC and non-SCC older adults differed in their individual ratings of technology to support PM-related activities. There was also a significant interaction between activity and question, F(7.58, 2848.55) = 63.53, p < .001, η2 = 0.15, indicating that older adults’ attitudes toward technology to support the six activities differed based on question (useful/recommend, data security, interest/adopt).

Comparing activity ratings

As there was no significant interaction between activity and group, we examined differences between activity type within each question among all participants (Figure 2).

Useful/recommend

In general, older adults gave the highest useful/recommend rating to technology to support healthcare. Technology to support housework received the lowest useful/recommend ratings, followed by technology to support transportation.

Healthcare differed significantly from finance, Mdiff = 0.15, SE = 0.03, p < .001, shopping, Mdiff = 0.17, SE = 0.03, p < .001, relationships, Mdiff = 0.23, SE = 0.03, p < .001, transportation, Mdiff = 0.32, SE = 0.04, p < .001, and housework, Mdiff = 0.45, SE = 0.04, p < .001. Finance, shopping, and relationships did not significantly differ from each other. Finance received a significantly higher useful/recommend rating than transportation, Mdiff = 0.17, SE = 0.04, p < .001, and housework, Mdiff = 0.30, SE = 0.04, p < .001. Shopping also received a significantly higher useful/recommend rating than transportation, Mdiff = 0.16, SE = 0.04, p = .004, and housework, Mdiff = 0.28, SE = 0.04, p < .001. Relationships received a significantly higher useful/recommend rating than housework, Mdiff = 0.22, SE = 0.04, p < .001, but did not differ significantly from transportation. Transportation also received significantly higher useful/recommend ratings compared to housework, Mdiff = 0.13, SE = 0.04, p = .046.

Data security

Older adults had the least privacy concern for technology to support housework, and they had the most privacy concern for technology to support finance.

Housework received the most positive data security rating, significantly higher than shopping, Mdiff = 0.26, SE = 0.04, p < .001, transportation, Mdiff = 0.35, SE = 0.05, p < .001, relationships, Mdiff = 0.38, SE = 0.05, p < .001, healthcare, Mdiff = 0.46, SE = 0.05, p < .001, and finance, Mdiff = 0.84, SE = 0.06, p < .001, indicating that participants had the least privacy concerns for housework relative to all other activities. Shopping did not differ significantly from transportation and was only marginally more positive than relationships, Mdiff = 0.12, SE = 0.04, p = .069, but shopping was significantly more positive than healthcare, Mdiff = 0.20, SE = 0.05, p < .001, and finance, Mdiff = 0.58, SE = 0.05, p < .001. Transportation did not differ significantly from relationships and was only marginally more positive than healthcare, Mdiff = 0.11, SE = 0.04, p = .087 but was significantly more positive than finance, Mdiff = 0.49, SE = 0.05, p < .001. Relationships did not differ significantly from healthcare but were significantly more positive than finance, Mdiff = 0.47, SE = 0.05, p < .001. Healthcare also received a significantly more positive data security rating compared to finance, Mdiff = 0.38, SE = 0.05, p < .001, indicating that participants had the most privacy concerns for finance relative to all other activities.

Interest/adopt

In general, older adults were most interested in learning about or adopting technology to support healthcare and were least interested in technology to support housework.

Healthcare had the highest interest/adopt rating and differed significantly from shopping, Mdiff = 0.15, SE = 0.04, p = .014, finance, Mdiff = 0.18, SE = 0.05, p = .003, relationships, Mdiff = 0.28, SE = 0.05, p < .001, transportation, Mdiff = 0.39, SE = 0.05, p < .001, and housework, Mdiff = 0.57, SE = 0.05, p < .001. Shopping, finance, and relationships did not significantly differ from each other, though shopping was marginally higher than relationships, Mdiff = 0.13, SE = 0.05, p = .053. Shopping received a significantly higher interest/adopt rating than transportation, Mdiff = 0.25, SE = 0.05, p < .001, and housework, Mdiff = 0.42, SE = 0.05, p < .001. Finance also received a significantly higher interest rating than transportation, Mdiff = 0.22, SE = 0.05, p = .001, and housework, Mdiff = 0.39, SE = 0.05, p < .001. Relationships did not differ significantly from transportation but were significantly more positive than housework, Mdiff = 0.29, SE = 0.05, p < .001. Transportation also received significantly higher interest/adopt ratings than housework, Mdiff = 0.18, SE = 0.05, p = .015.

Main effect of activity

There was a significant main effect of activity, F(4.62, 1,738.20) = 22.05, p < .001, η2 = 0.06, where the most positive attitudes were for technology to support healthcare and shopping, and the least positive attitudes were for technology to support finance. Healthcare was rated significantly higher than relationships, Mdiff = 0.14, SE = 0.03, p < .001, finance, Mdiff = 0.24, SE = 0.03, p < .001, transportation, Mdiff = 0.20, SE = 0.03, p < .001, and housework, Mdiff = 0.19, SE = 0.03, p < .001. Shopping was rated significantly higher than relationships, Mdiff = 0.11, SE = 0.03, p = .003, finance, Mdiff = 0.20, SE = 0.03, p < .001, transportation, Mdiff = 0.17, SE = 0.03, p < .001, and housework, Mdiff = 0.15, SE = 0.03, p < .001. Relationships were significantly higher than finance, Mdiff = 0.09, SE = 0.03, p = .030. All other comparisons were nonsignificant.

Comparing activity preferences by SCC status

There was a significant main effect of group, where SCC participants gave significantly more positive ratings overall compared to non-SCC participants. The significant interaction between question and group suggests that SCC and non-SCC older adults differed in how they rated technology to support PM-related activities. Correlations

Useful/recommend was significantly positively associated with mobile device proficiency, r(186) = .24, p < .001, technology readiness, r(186) = .36, p < .001, Senior Technology Acceptance (STA) attitudes, r(186) = .38, p < .001, and STA control, r(186) = .26, p < .001. Data security ratings were significantly negatively associated with STA gerontechnology anxiety, r(186) = −.17, p = .023, meaning that as gerontechnology anxiety scores increased, SCC participants had less positive views of data security (more privacy concerns) related to technology to support PM.

Discussion and implications

This study investigated the attitudes of older adults with SCC toward technology to support PM. An online survey presented SCC and non-SCC participants with a description of a hypothetical person experiencing PM challenges. Participants were told that the person had recently learned of a technology device (mobile app, smartwatch, or VAA) that can be used to provide PM support. The hypothetical person was then presented as experiencing challenges in a specific activity domain (relationships and social activities, healthcare activities, transportation, shopping, housework and laundry, or managing finances). For each case, participants were asked to indicate their attitudes toward the technology for the hypothetical person and for themselves. Analyses investigated whether the attitudes of participants with SCC (1) differed based on the type of technology or the type of activity the technology is meant to support, (2) differed from attitudes of older adults without SCC, and (3) were associated with individual difference factors.

Results showed that the attitudes of older adults with SCC toward technology to support PM were relatively positive overall but differed significantly based on the type of device (Figure 1). Mobile apps received the most positive ratings, while smartwatches were rated the least positive. Specifically, SCC participants rated mobile apps significantly higher than smartwatches in terms of perceived usefulness or whether they would recommend the device to a hypothetical person (useful/recommend), and in terms of whether they would be interested in learning more about or adopting and using the technology themselves (interest/adopt). SCC participants indicated the most privacy concern for VAAs (lowest data security ratings), but they still rated VAAs significantly higher than smartwatches and no ­different from mobile apps in terms of useful/recommend and interest/adopt. As perceived usefulness is argued to be one of the most important factors in technology acceptance (Davis et al., 1989; Ma et al., 2021; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Venkatesh et al., 2012), results indicate that compared to smartwatches, mobile apps or VAAs may be better options for implementing technology-based PM support for older adults who report cognitive challenges. Lower data security ratings for VAAs indicate a potential barrier to their adoption among this population, though it is possible that privacy concerns might differ depending on the activity the technology is used to support.

In fact, attitudes of older adults toward technology to support PM varied depending on the activity supported by the technology (Figure 2). For interest/adopt and useful/recommend, healthcare support received the most positive ratings, while housework support received the least positive ratings. However, privacy concerns were greatest for technology to support finance and healthcare, while technology to support housework raised the least concern. This suggests that technology-related privacy concerns among older adults may need to be addressed for specific activities, particularly finance and healthcare, though technology to support finance and healthcare received generally positive evaluations. However, previous research shows that older adults are willing to sacrifice some privacy in order to use technology to compensate for cognitive challenges (Beach et al., 2009). It is possible that older adults with cognitive complaints might be more willing to adopt a technology despite privacy concerns if the support the technology offers is perceived as more useful. For example, this population might be more willing to use VAAs to support healthcare rather than housework, despite increased privacy concerns for healthcare-related tasks.

Among SCC participants, higher technology readiness and lower gerontechnology anxiety were associated with more positive useful/recommend, data security, and interest/adopt ratings, suggesting that these two constructs might be especially important for predicting or targeting attitudes toward technology to support PM among older adults with SCC. Additionally, higher mobile device proficiency, more positive attitudes toward technology in general, and higher technology control beliefs were associated with more positive attitudes for both useful/recommend and interest/adopt, while better perceived health, more frequent memory failures, and lower age were associated only with more positive interest/adopt ratings, suggesting that the latter three factors might be uniquely important for predicting or targeting acceptance—and ultimately actual use—of technology to support PM.

The association between poorer perceived memory and more positive interest/adopt ratings for technology to support PM is consistent with our finding that older adults with SCC had significantly more positive attitudes toward technology to support PM compared to older adults without SCC (Figure 3), especially apparent in their interest/adopt ratings. These results suggest that older adults with SCC may be a promising population to target using technology-based solutions. This is also consistent with previous research showing that individuals who have poorer health and cognition may have more positive attitudes toward technology compared to healthier individuals and may be more likely to use technology to compensate for health limitations (Alwahaishi & Snášel, 2013; Beach et al., 2009; Chen & Chan, 2014). However, inconsistent with these trends, better perceived health was associated with more positive interest/adopt ratings in this study. Perhaps if the technology had been described as supporting health rather than PM, outcomes might have differed. Though in a study examining older adults’ attitudes toward technology to support health and cognition, Fowe and Boot (2022) found that poorer self-rated health was related to more negative attitudes toward the technology, consistent with the present study. As suggested by Fowe and Boot, associations may be different when considering health and cognition in relation to usage behaviors rather than attitudes, and when considering general technology use compared to the specific technologies described in this study.

In summary, results suggest that older adults with SCC would prefer using mobile apps to support PM compared to using smartwatches or VAAs, and they are most interested in using technology to support healthcare-related activities. Further, older adults with SCC who have more positive attitudes toward technology in general, better perceived health, more frequent memory failures, and a lower age might be most receptive to a technology-based solution to support PM.

Limitations and future directions

This study’s results come with several limitations. First, the online survey required participants to have access to a device connected to the Internet. Thus, we examined attitudes toward technology among individuals who already use technology, which could lead to an overestimate of attitudes among older adults with SCC in the broader population. However, with approximately 88% of older adults now online in the United States (Pew Research Center, 2024), the study sample may not be completely unrepresentative of this population in terms of technology experience. Second, the sample was predominantly white and highly educated. Technology use, particularly Internet use, is associated with being White, being more educated, and having a higher income (Pew Research Center, 2024). Thus, results might differ in a more diverse population regarding race, education, and socio-economic status.

Another limitation is that SCC and non-SCC participants were unevenly distributed across recruitment sources, potentially impacting the interpretation of between-group differences. Notably, the Prolific sample was younger and had more individuals currently working compared to the older and mostly retired ISL sample. As we found in our results, younger age may correspond with more positive attitudes toward technology, and being in the workforce might increase one’s exposure to technology. These characteristics of the Prolific sample may have contributed to their more positive attitudes toward technology to support PM. Encouragingly, however, a trend toward more positive attitudes among SCC participants was still observed within the Prolific sample (p = .068). This pattern was not observed in the ISL sample, though this group comprised less than one-third of the total sample, substantially limiting power.

Further, participants could not indicate in the survey whether they already owned the technologies assessed. Several reported giving neutral responses to whether they would be interested in learning more about or adopting and using the device because they were already familiar with it and already used it, potentially lowering interest/adopt ratings relative to useful/recommend ratings. However, participants may have given higher useful/recommend ratings relative to interest/adopt ratings based on perceived aspects of the hypothetical person that did not apply to themselves, or perhaps due to the effort and cost required to adopt and use the technology. Follow-up studies should investigate why older adults with SCC might be more willing to recommend technologies to others than to adopt and use it themselves.

Future studies should also examine the preferences of older adults with SCC regarding which devices to use for which activities. For example, individuals might be less interested in using a device to support an activity with more privacy concerns (e.g., managing finances) if they also have more privacy concerns for that device (e.g., VAAs). Alternatively, participants might prefer to use one device for all activities regardless of privacy concerns. Future research should also investigate more types of technologies to support PM in addition to mobile apps, smartwatches, and VAAs, such as desktop, laptop, and tablet computers, as well as emerging technologies such as extended reality, as these might differ in perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Further, future work should include a measure of instrumental activities of daily living to assess whether areas in which participants report difficulty are associated with attitudes toward technology supporting PM in different activity domains.

Finally, this study classified participants based on self-reported cognitive complaints and did not include an objective cognitive assessment to distinguish between SCD and MCI. Future work should include a more extensive assessment of participants’ cognition to gain more insight into the effects of perceived and objective cognitive challenges on attitudes toward technology to support everyday activities.

Conclusion

As the older adult population grows and technology diffuses and evolves, research must focus on technology-based support for older adults, particularly those with cognitive impairment. Targeting early stages of cognitive impairment, such as the presence of cognitive complaints, may be key in setting the groundwork for long-term, adaptive solutions. Indeed, results of this study show that interest in the technology was positively associated with reports of more frequent memory failures, and older adults with SCC had more positive attitudes toward technology to support PM-related activities compared to older adults without SCC. This suggests that older adults with SCC, particularly those experiencing memory difficulties, may be a promising target for technology-based solutions. Toward this end, researchers and developers should consider that mobile apps may be more acceptable compared to VAAs or smartwatches, while smartwatches may be the least acceptable. Further, technology to support healthcare may be most desirable, while technology to support housework may be least desirable. Privacy concerns related to VAAs and technology to manage finances must also be addressed. Overall, this study emphasizes the need for insights from the target population to effectively guide the formation of interventions to enhance independence and well-being.
