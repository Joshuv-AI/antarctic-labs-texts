Reverse blood flow treatment for aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage due to interrupted aortic arch with aberrant right subclavian artery
Authors: Lun-Zhe Yang, Yong Yang, Chao Peng, Hua-Wei Ye, Yu Song, Guang-Zhong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterrupted aortic arch (IAA) is a rare congenital defect involving complete discontinuity between the aortic arch and descending aorta. Aberrant right subclavian artery (ARSA) is a common variant, occasionally causing vascular complications. Intracranial aneurysm (IA) rupture leads to subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), a life-threatening event. The coexistence of IAA, ARSA, and IA is extremely rare and poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case presentationThis report describes a young patient with type B IAA and ARSA, who also presented with multiple IA. The patient was admitted due to SAH. The extreme vessel angle of the right subclavian artery to the aorta and the reversal of blood flow in bilateral vertebral arteries made cerebral angiography and therapy difficult.ConclusionIn young patients with IA, underlying vascular anomalies affecting hemodynamics should be carefully evaluated. IAA with ARSA poses challenges for vascular access but does not preclude safe and effective endovascular treatment. Comprehensive assessment and individualized planning are essential for favorable long-term outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04466-0.

Background

Interrupted aortic arch (IAA) is a rare vascular malformation with complete separation between the aortic arch and the descending aorta [1]. Blood circulating to the descending aorta comes from arch branches or by an unenclosed ductus arteriosus. Most cases involve severe cardiac defects and result in death, or lead to other complications due to the occlusion or rupture of collateral circulation [1, 2]. Aberrant right subclavian artery (ARSA) occurs when right subclavian artery originates from distal aortic arch or descending aorta. It’s usually asymptomatic but can cause dysphagia or, rarely, dissection or aneurysm [3]. Intracranial aneurysm (IA) is localized dilations of cerebral arteries. Once ruptured, they cause subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), leading to severe complications [4].

We report the first case of concurrent IAA, ARSA, and IA in a patient admitted for SAH. The direction of blood flow in the intracranial vessels and the presence of ARSA influenced the choice of interventional treatment methods. We present the clinical features, successful neurosurgical management, and 9-year follow-up.

Case presentation

The patient was admitted with sudden severe headache and vomiting on September 30, 2015. The patient was in a lethargic state. Glasgow Coma Scale score was 14 and Hunt-Hess grade was III. Cranial computed tomography (CT) revealed SAH. We performed cerebral digital subtraction angiography (DSA) to identify etiology. However, guidewire could not pass through aortic arch, only accessing the left subclavian artery. Angiography showed reversed blood flow prevented visualization of intracranial vessels, suggesting structural vascular anomalies.

Cranial CT angiography (CTA) revealed multiple aneurysms at the terminal branch of the both internal carotid arteries and right posterior communicating artery; a basilar artery aneurysm, measuring 11 × 13 mm with an irregular shape, was considered the source of SAH(Fig. 1). Cervical and aortic CTA showed aortic arch interrupted between left common carotid artery and left subclavian artery. Both common carotid arteries arising from the proximal end of the IAA, while the left subclavian artery originated from the distal end. The right subclavian artery arose from descending aorta and traveling behind trachea, consistent with type-B IAA and ARSA(Fig. 2). [5] The left carotid and vertebral arteries were notably dilated, with extensive collateral circulation forming a dense vascular network(Fig. 3). Echocardiography revealed a complex aortic arch anatomy with discontinuity at the arch. Numerous collateral vessels were observed around the arch, and no patent ductus arteriosus was detected.

Treatment

The patient had type-B IAA, ARSA, and multiple IAs. The basilar aneurysm posed a high re-rupture risk, warranting urgent treatment. Cardiac function was unremarkable and IAA has established good collateral circulation, with both internal carotid arteries suppling the bilateral vertebral arteries via the circle of Willis, which in turn supplied the descending aorta. Treating the ruptured aneurysm first, followed by arch surgery, is a safe strategy for better long-term outcomes.

Given the patient’s complex vascular anatomy and the high risk associated with craniotomy for clipping, we prioritized endovascular intervention. Based on the CTA, it was considered that both internal carotid arteries supplied blood to the bilateral vertebral arteries via the circle of Willis, which in turn supplied the descending aorta. If accessed through the radial or femoral artery, the catheter would not reach the bilateral common carotid arteries, and performing angiography through the vertebral arteries would result in the contrast agent flowing backward, failing to adequately visualize the intracranial vessels and aneurysms. Therefore, we thoroughly utilized 3D CTA images preoperatively to understand the course of the left vertebral artery to the basilar artery, including the branching points and their orientations, the size and orientation of the IA, the pathway for catheter access, the choice of working angles, the shaping of the microcatheter, and the estimated diameter and quantity of coils needed. It was determined that the neck of the basilar artery aneurysm was not wide, and although the course of the left vertebral artery was tortuous, it had a relatively large diameter, providing a potential route to reach the aneurysm neck under the guidance of micro-guidewire technology. Furthermore, the presence of ARSA could complicate the establishment of a route from the right radial artery to the basilar artery. Therefore, we decided to use the selected working angle of the CTA images as reference. We accessed the aneurysm via the right femoral artery, guiding the catheter through the descending aorta, left subclavian artery, and left vertebral artery to reach the basilar artery, where we performed simple coil embolization of the basilar artery aneurysm under fluoroscopy without route map. Although theoretically a direct carotid puncture could be used for contrast injection to obtain a roadmap, this approach was abandoned after thorough preoperative evaluation. CTA revealed that the patient’s neck contained numerous compensatory tortuous and dilated vessels, as well as abundant subcutaneous collateral branches (Fig. 3). Under these anatomical conditions, carotid puncture would carry a high risk of uncontrolled major bleeding. Moreover, the transcarotid route via the internal carotid artery–posterior communicating artery–posterior cerebral artery–basilar artery would result in a long and technically challenging pathway. Therefore, this approach was not pursued.

The patient underwent intervention on October 13, 2015. The right femoral artery was punctured, and 6 F guiding catheter was blocked from traveling forward as soon as it reached the proximal part of the descending aorta, and angiography showed that the aortic arch was interrupted between the left subclavian artery and the left common carotid artery, consistent with type B aortic arch discontinuity. Continuing to put the catheter into the left vertebral artery, angiography showed that the contrast agent flowed back down into the descending aorta, with some flowing into the left subclavian artery, while the intracranial vessels were not adequately visualized, aligning with the preoperative assessment. Based on the preoperative vascular evaluation, a microcatheter was shaped, and direct coil embolization was performed, successfully embolizing the aneurysm (Fig. 4).

The patient subsequently had an aortic surgery on July 6, 2016. After satisfactory anesthesia, a median sternotomy was made, revealing moderate cardiomegaly. The ascending aorta gave rise to both common carotid arteries, and the aortic arch was separated from the descending aorta, consistent with IAA. A ligamentum arteriosum was noted at the pulmonary artery bifurcation and the descending aorta, and the left subclavian artery originated from the apex of the descending aorta with marked dilation. Intracardiac structures were not explored. A pressure catheter in the right common carotid artery demonstrated a proximal pressure approximately 30 mmHg higher than the peripheral pressure. The ascending aorta and aortic arch branches were carefully mobilized, and the ligamentum arteriosum was divided and sutured. After systemic heparinization, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was established. Side-to-side anastomosis between the descending aorta and the posterior wall of the aortic arch was performed and reinforced as needed. Vascular clamps were released, CPB was discontinued, and no difference was noted between central and peripheral arterial pressures. Protamine was administered to reverse heparin. The patient tolerated the procedure well and had an uneventful postoperative recovery.

Outcome and follow-up

In May 2017 the DSA was performed. Aortic arch angiography showed good continuity of aortic arch; bilateral carotid arteries and left subclavian artery arose from aortic arch; right subclavian artery arose from descending aorta. In the bilateral carotid artery system, the angiography revealed a fetal-type posterior cerebral artery in the intracranial segment of the right internal carotid artery. Previously existing multiple aneurysms had disappeared. The left vertebral and basilar artery was well visualized, with the coil remaining in the basilar artery aneurysm, which was not visualized. Additionally, new findings showed occlusion of previously thin right vertebral artery.(Supplemental Fig. 4).

The DSA in October 2021 showed a saccular expansion of the right ascending pharyngeal artery. The left internal carotid artery supplied blood to the basilar artery through the posterior communicating artery, and the basilar artery aneurysm was densely embolized and not visible. The left subclavian artery angiography showed insufficient visualization of the left vertebral artery V4 segmentand the basilar artery, possibly due to a sudden decrease in blood flow from the circle of Willis. The remaining vessels appeared normal.(Supplemental Fig. 5).

The cerebral DSA in October 2024 revealed the right carotid artery system was similar to previous examinations. Blood from the left internal carotid artery flowed backwards to the basilar artery through the posterior communicating artery, and the basilar artery aneurysm did not be visible. The increasing blood supply from the aortic arch has led to a significant improvement in the visibility of the left vertebral artery compared to before, while the right vertebral artery and basilar artery were not visible.(Supplemental Fig. 6).

The patient has had a good recovery over the 9 years post-surgery, with an mRS score of 0 and no new functional impairments or symptoms. Regular follow-up is still being conducted.

Discussion and conclusions

The hemodynamic abnormalities caused by increased intracranial blood flow due to IAA may be significant factors contributing to IA, leading to endothelial damage and inflammation. This triggers vascular smooth muscle cells to enter a pro-inflammatory state, resulting in internal elastic lamina destruction and extracellular matrix degradation, ultimately causing aneurysm formation and rupture [6]. IAA is a specific type of aortic coarctation, which causes increased blood pressure due to reduced distal blood supply, decreased arterial compliance, and abnormalities in the renin-angiotensin system, particularly proximal to the narrowing. Elevated arterial pressure in IAA patients can weaken intracranial vessel walls, leading to aneurysm formation [7, 8]. On the other hand, the disappearance of multiple IAs and the abnormally dilated rich vascular network in the neck after elimination of intracranial abnormal perfusion by aortic arch surgery demonstrates that hemodynamics is the mechanism of association between IAA and IA.
