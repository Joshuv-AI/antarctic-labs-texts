Research on optimal deep learning modeling in HaiNan dialect recognition
Authors: ZiXuan Qi, FuYun Li, HaiXia Long
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The speech recognition task of the HaiNan dialect faces significant differences in phonology, intonation, and grammatical structure among dialects, which in turn show significant regionalization characteristics, which makes the task of dialect-to-Mandarin conversion more complex. Currently, the research on the HaiNan dialect speech recognition is still in its early stages and lacks sufficient corpus resources, especially in the task of multi-dialect recognition. Traditional models are difficult to solve with the problem of data scarcity and diverse dialect characteristics effectively. To overcome these challenges, this study explores the application of multiple deep learning models in the task of converting HaiNan dialect to Mandarin, aiming to identify the optimal deep learning model in HaiNan dialect recognition, and through extensive experimental comparative analysis, proposes a fusion model combining Convolutional Neural Networks and Multi-Head Self-Attention Mechanism (ConvMHANet). It demonstrates excellent performance in different dialectal scenes, especially in solving complex dialectal phonetic and contextual dependencies, with an accuracy of 97.58% and a character error rate down to 0.0163 in the multi-classification mixing task, showing strong generalization capabilities.

Introduction

Dialect speech recognition technology is crucial in preserving linguistic diversity and promoting digital inclusivity. It holds irreplaceable strategic value in the fields of cultural heritage preservation and the equalization of socials1. There are regional variants of WenChang, WanNing and DongFang, which show significant differences in phonology: the WenChang dialect has a seven-tone system and unique glottal stop end rules, the WanNing dialect retains the time varying phonological change of turbid cleansing in Middle Chinese, and the DongFang dialect has a special phenomenon of Yin and Yang counter transposition due to the underlying residue of Old Chinese. The heterogeneity of these phonetic parameters presents a significant cross-dialect generalization challenge for traditional speech models. Current mainstream research focuses on high-resource languages. For example, the English phrase recognition model optimized based on quaternion clusters improves the accuracy to 98%2, but its architecture is not adapted to low-resource dialect characteristics. The HMM-GMM model achieves 88.44% accuracy in Amazigh letter recognition, but the word error rate still reaches 11.52% when facing complex tonal dialects3. This technical limitation severely restricts the intelligent transformation of public4. Consequently, constructing multi-dialect annotated datasets and developing deep learning architectures that combine local acoustic features with global context have become key approaches to overcoming the technological barriers of low-resource dialects. Our architecture therefore targets three phonological challenges specific to HaiNan dialects: the dense seven-tone inventory, the abrupt spectral transitions produced by glottal and nasal-stop codas, and the homophonous syllables that differ only in tone across sub-dialects.

Related works

In recent years, dialect speech recognition technology has progressively transitioned from a singular model design to multi modal fusion. Previous research predominantly utilized conventional statistical models, specifically Hidden Markov Models (HMM) in conjunction with Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM), to attain an 88.44% letter identification rate in Amazigh speech recognition3, but it is difficult to model dynamic vocal pitch changes. Following the emergence of deep learning, hybrid LSTM and CNN architectures have gained prominence: the Sichuan dialect recognition system reduces the character error rate to 11.34% through a hybrid HMM-LSTM model5. The CNN-LSTM two-branch structure was evaluated in Arkhangelsk dialect vocabulary classification for the complementary of local features and temporal modeling6. The extensive application of Transformer in speech processing7, innovations in Self-Attention mechanisms continue to emerge. The LETCNN-P Transformer enhances contextual modeling for low-resource languages through product computation8. The reverse positional encoding mechanism effectively improves the translation quality of right-to-left text9, while the parameterized Self-Attention structure provides a 20% relative performance enhancementin streaming multilingual ASR models10.

Aiming at the issue of limited data resources, phonetic enhancement technology decreases the word error rate of multilingual ASR models by 5% through cross-language representation11, and large-scale language models (LLMs) require only a 10,000-level parallel corpus to achieve dialect phonetic normalization12. The Meta-Adaptable-Adapter framework employs task-agnostic adapters to facilitate efficient feature transfer across 31 low-resource languages13, and a transduction migration learning strategy improves the BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) score for Algerian dialect translation from 0.3 to 3514. In the field of dialect translation, the incremental transfer learning method enhances the performance of Arabic dialect translation by up to 98% through phased fine-tuning15, while the unsupervised training strategy achieves 25.1 BLEU scores in Mandarin-Cantonese interpretation16. The successful application of transfer learning in English speech emotion recognition17 substantiates the viability of cross-task knowledge transfer.

The integrated learning approach for multi-dialect hybrid processing decreases the Irish recognition error rate by 14% by combining ASR with confidence weighting of the end-to-end model4, and the Arabic children’s literature translation system based on a large language model enhances the BLEU score by 3% through semantic similarity evaluation18. The speech-to-text multimodal fusion method attains a BLEU score of 9.88 in Arabic cross-dialect translation19, but there are significant gaps in the existing research in modeling the tonal co-articulation of Chinese dialects. The Bengali dialect translation model has only 36.75 BLEU in Chittagong dialect20, and the standard Arabic conversion system fails to address the issue of acoustic-phoneme joint mapping21.

Neural machine translation models have achieved significant advancements in low-resource contexts: BanglaT5 achieves mutual translation between standard Bengali and regional dialects (CER 12.3%)22; and the hybrid PBMT and NMT system enhances the quality of Hakka translation through recursive learning23. However, text-only models have difficulty in capturing phonetic features, such as the Egyptian dialect translation system, which depends on shared vocabulary embedding while ignoring acoustic features24. End-to-end speech translation systems provide a new technological structure for dialect translation through joint acoustic-semantic modeling25, while the Lahjawi-D2D model enables cross-dialect interpreting among 15 Arabic dialects19, showing the potential of multi modal fusion.

Methods

Figure 1 demonstrates the ConvMHANet model architecture for HaiNan dialect to Mandarin. First, a corpus for model training is created by collecting and labeling dialectal speech data from different regions of HaiNan (e.g., WenChang, WanNing, DongFang, etc.). In the feature extraction stage, speech features such as Mel Frequency Cepstrum Coefficients (MFCC) and Filter Banks (FBank) are extracted from the audio signals, and noise reduction is performed to improve the learning effect of the model. Next, local speech features are extracted through Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), global dependencies are modeled by combining with the Multi-Head Self-Attention mechanism, and finally the conversion from HaiNan dialect to Mandarin is achieved by Connecting Temporal Classification (CTC) output layer. In the model evaluation stage, the model performance is comprehensively evaluated through the accuracy rate, Character Error Rate (CER), and Word Error Rate (WER) metrics. Additionally, the analysis of model performance is enhanced by using the confusion matrix and various multi dimensional performance evaluation methods. The architecture efficiently completes the speech conversion task from HaiNan dialect to Mandarin by effectively combining the local feature extraction capability of CNN with the global dependency modeling advantage of the Self-Attention mechanism.

Datasets

There remains a lack of studies on the conversion of HaiNan dialects to Mandarin, particularly concerning the comparative performance of different regional dialects and the integration of several regional dialects. A publicly accessible datasets of HaiNan dialect speech is unavailable, mostly due to the complex and costly procedures required for its gathering and organization. On the hand, acquiring raw speech data from public sources is challenging due to Mandarin being the official language of China; on the other hand, gathering raw speech data necessitates an expensive effort for data labeling. To create this speech datasets, we first collected a number of texts containing most of the commonly used words in the HaiNan dialect and expressions from daily conversations. Subsequently, participants from various regions of HaiNan organized the recording of dialectal speech data by reading the provided text content. After measurement and marking of the audio recordings, the final HaiNan dialect data, as shown in Table 1, includes 249 words and 685 sentences from the WenChang dialect, 206 words and 282 sentences from the WanNing dialect, 695 words from the DongFang dialect, 308 words from the HaiKou dialect, and 111 individual words from the DingAn dialect. As shown in step 1 of Fig. 1, we have collected the dialectal speech data from WanNing, WenChang, DongFang, HaiKou and DingAn in HaiNan, as well as the corresponding translated Mandarin text. The speech files in the datasets are preprocessed, including audio cutting, noise reduction and other operations. Datasets containing various multi-regional dialects are generated to assess the performance of the model in complex conditions.

Data preprocessing

In the data preparation stage, as shown in step 2 of Fig. 1, this study employs various techniques to process and enhance the audio data, which improves the reliability of the model and prevents over fitting. First, the audio label information is loaded from an Excel file that includes the audio file name and its connected label. The labels are converted into a dictionary format, with the file name as the key and the matching label as the value.

In order to improve the generalization ability of the model, we employ several audio data augmentation techniques. The improvement procedure begins by modeling various noise environments by the addition of Gaussian noise to the audio source. In the enhancement process, different noise environments are firstly modeled by adding Gaussian noise to the audio signal. Suppose the original audio signal is x(t), Gaussian noise ε(t) can be generated by random numbers and satisfies a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance σ2. Where, α represents the noise factor, which controls the intensity of the noise, and y(t) is the signal after noise addition.

Additionally, the amplitude of a frequency signal can be changed by modifying the gain parameter of the system. For each sample of the frequency signal x(t ), the modified frequency signal y(t) can be expressed as follows:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y\left(t\right)=\beta\:\cdot\:x\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, β is the gain factor, which controls the level of the signal amplitude.

To augment the diversity of the data, we applied a transformation on the frequency signal to emulate several probably conditions in the recording process. Where, T represents the total duration of the frequency, t is the time point, and y(t ) is the transformed frequency signal.

Data augmentation is performed by choosing between traditional augmentation techniques (such as spectrum scaling and loudness changes) and extra augmentation methods (such as noise addition and audio inversion) with a probability rate of 99:1. a method is randomly selected to be applied to the audio data. To ensure consistent audio length after augmentation, all audio signals are adjusted to the target length (e.g., 45,000 samples) through cutting or padding. These data augmentation methods not only increase the diversity of the training datasets but also significantly improve the reliability of the model in various audio environments.

Feature extraction

Audio feature extraction is a crucial step in processing speech signals, improving the performance of the model by extracting significant features from the audio. To better represent the speech signal, we extracted extended Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC) and Constant-Q cepstral coefficients (CQCC) features from the raw audio. MFCC is a widely used feature in speech processing, capable of capturing the spectral characteristics of speech signals, while CQCC improves the spectral features of speech through the Constant-Q transform, more accurately simulating the auditory characteristics of the human ear. To provide a more intuitive representation of the distribution of these features, as shown in Fig. 3, we selected the 1-Dimension and 2-Dimension principal components for visualization. This choice was primarily driven by their ability to clearly reflect the core acoustic differences between the two dialects. This pattern indicates that the extracted MFCC and CQCC hybrid features successfully capture the distinctive short tone and glottal stop characteristics of HaiNan. For the model, this feature distribution carries dual significance: firstly, the local perceptual capacity of CNN allows for rapid localization of tonal transitions in HaiNan, enhancing feature extraction efficiency; secondly, the clustering in the feature space provides clear dialectical differentiation cues for the Self-Attention mechanism. During the computation of cross-frame associations, the model naturally focuses more on the high-density regions of HaiNan, thus optimizing the weight distribution for phoneme alignment. Essentially, this distribution creates “acoustic landmarks” for the model, guiding the network to prioritize the learning of key dialect features while ensuring the preservation of HaiNan phonetic traits when generating Mandarin speech.

Then, to enhance the representation of the flexible changes in the speech signal, we calculated the first-order delta and second-order delta-delta features of the MFCC, which represent the short-term changes and the acceleration of changes in the signal, respectively. Model architecture

As shown in step 3 of Fig. 1, this study proposes a deep learning model architecture that combines Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) with the Self-Attention mechanism, aimed at solving the speech recognition task of converting HaiNan to Mandarin. The model effectively extracts local features and uses the Self-Attention mechanism to catch global dependencies, significantly improving the accuracy of HaiNan-to-Mandarin speech conversion, especially when handling complex speech data with dialects.

In this model, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) are used to extract local features from the input HaiNan speech sequence. The convolution operation is mathematically represented as follows:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y\left(t\right)=\sum\:_{k=0}^{K-1}x\left(t-k\right)\cdot\:w\left(k\right)$$\end{document}

Where x(t) represents the input sequence, w(k) is the convolutional kernel, y(t ) is the output after the convolution operation, and k is the length of the convolutional kernel. Through multiple convolution operations, the CNN layer can extract low-level features from the speech signal. The subsequent pooling layer further diminishes the dimension of the feature map, thus decreasing computing complexity and enhancing the representational abilities of the features. As the network deepens, more abstract features are gradually extracted through multiple layers of convolution. After the convolution, the ReLU activation function and batch normalization are applied to enhance the non-linear expressive power, and the max pooling layer is used to reduce the spatial dimensions.

After the convolutional layer, the Self-Attention mechanism is introduced to model the global dependencies between the HaiNan speech and Mandarin. The Self-Attention mechanism calculates the similarity between different positions in the input sequence, allowing it to effectively catch long-range dependencies, which is crucial for the conversion between dialects and standard Mandarin. The mathematical expression for the Self-Attention mechanism is as follows:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Attention\left(Q,K,V\right)=softmax\left(\frac{Q{K}^{T}}{\sqrt{{d}_{k}}}\right)V$$\end{document}

In this context, Q, K, V and represent the Query, Key, and Value, and dk represents the dimension of the key. By calculating the dot product between the query and the key, and applying softmax, the model assigns attention weights to each position, which are then used to generate the final output from the values.

To enhance the expressive capability of the model, the Multi-Head Self-Attention mechanism is employed. The Multi-Head Self-Attention mechanism maps the input sequence into multiple subspace, where each subspace can independently learn the dependencies between different positions in the sequence. The outputs from multiple heads are concatenated, resulting in a richer feature representation. The mathematical expression for Multi-Head Self-Attention is as follows:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}MultiHead\left(Q,K,V\right)=Concat\left({head}_{1},\cdots\:,{head}_{h}\right){W}^{O}\end{array}$$\end{document}

Where the computation for each head is as follows:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{head}_{i}=Attention\left(Q{W}_{i}^{Q},{KW}_{i}^{K},{VW}_{i}^{V}\right)$$\end{document}

WiQ, WiK, and WiV are the linear transformation matrices for the Query, Key, and Value, WO is the linear transformation matrix for the output.

To prevent over fitting and improve generalization, the Dropout layer is introduced in the model. Dropout randomly drops the activation of certain neurons, forcing the model to learn more generalized features during training. This strategy especially when handling speech data from HaiNan and Mandarin, significantly enhances the performance of the model on the test set.

The output layer of the model is a fully connected layer, used to map the features extracted by the Convolutional layer and Self-Attention mechanism to the target output space. Since the goal of this task is to convert HaiNan into Mandarin, the output layer is designed to ensure that the output at each time step depends not only on local features but also on global context. To ensure the stability of the training process, both the Convolutional layer and the fully connected layer use the Xavier initialization method, which effectively prevents the challenges of missing or expanding gradients during the early stages of training, thus accelerating network convergence and improving final performance.

The model architecture proposed in this study creatively combines the local feature extraction ability of CNN with the global dependency modeling ability of the Self-Attention mechanism (the CNN layers extract the short-span spectral transitions produced by glottal- and nasal-stop codas, whereas the multi-head self-attention module models long-range tonal coarticulation across successive syllables). Through the use of Multi-Head Self-Attention, Dropout layers, and optimized weight initialization strategies, the model is able to effectively train and predict in the HaiNan-to-Mandarin speech recognition task. This architecture catches the linguistic differences between HaiNan and Mandarin, significantly improving overall performance, particularly excellent in dialectal voice detection and interlingual conversion tasks.

Training process

We divided the dataset into a training set and a validation set in a ratio of 8:2. The model is trained with the cross-entropy loss function and the Adam optimizer during the training process. At the same time, to prevent the model from overfitting, we employed a learning rate decay strategy.

The training process involves several steps, including forward propagation, loss calculation, back propagation, optimization, and validation evaluation. We used CTC Loss (Connectionist Temporal Classification Loss) as the training objective function, which is highly effective for sequence-to-sequence tasks. CTC loss calculates the difference between its output and the actual label sequence without requiring a related match of the labels. After each forward propagation, the output of the model sequence is changed through the log softmax activation function for easy comparison with the true label sequence and to evaluate the log-likelihood. Specifically, lets assume its output is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}$$\end{document} and the true label is y. The loss is then:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{L}=-logP\left(y|x\right)=-\sum\:_{t=1}^{T}\text{log}P\left({y}_{t}|{x}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{t}$$\end{document} is the label at time step t, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{t}$$\end{document} is the input feature at time step t, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\left({y}_{t}|{x}_{t}\right)$$\end{document} is its predicted probability for the label at time step t, and T is the total number of time steps in the sequence.

When calculating the loss, we need to consider the lengths of the input and label sequences. Specifically, the length of the input is provided by input_ lengths, representing the number of time steps in each input sequence, while the length of the label is provided by target_ lengths, representing the actual number of characters in each label, excluding the padding. The CTC loss function uses length information to match the input and label sequences, subsequently effectively connecting each sequence pair.

After each loss calculation, we perform back propagation. Before back propagation, we first reset the gradients from the previous computation to prevent gradient accumulation. Then, we evaluate the gradient of the current loss and update the gradients. Before updating the model parameters, we use gradient cutting to prevent the issue of exploding gradients. Specifically, gradient cutting ensures the stability of the network during training by limiting the maximum norm of the gradients. Let g be the gradient vector and be the maximum gradient norm, the gradient cutting process can be represented as:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{g}_{clipped}=\frac{\tau\:}{max\left(\tau\:,{||g||}_{2}\right)}g$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{||g||}_{2}$$\end{document} is the L2 norm of the gradient.

Finally, the Adam optimizer is used to update the parameters of the model. The update rule of the Adam optimizer is based on the estimates of the mean and variance of the gradients, and its update formula is as follows:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{m}_{t}={\beta\:}_{1}{m}_{t-1}+\left(1-{\beta\:}_{1}\right){\nabla\:}_{\theta\:}{\mathcal{L}}_{t}\end{array}$$\end{document}20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{v}_{t}={\beta\:}_{2}{v}_{t-1}+\left(1-{\beta\:}_{2}\right){\left({\nabla\:}_{\theta\:}{\mathcal{L}}_{t}\right)}^{2}\end{array}$$\end{document}21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat {m}_t}=\frac{{{m_t}}}{{1 - \beta _{2}^{t}}},\;{\hat {v}_t}=\frac{{{v_t}}}{{1 - \beta _{2}^{t}}}$$\end{document}22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\theta _t}={\theta _{t - 1}} - \eta \frac{{{{\hat {m}}_t}}}{{\sqrt {{{\hat {v}}_t}+\varepsilon } }}$$\end{document}

Where mt and vt represent the momentum and variance of the gradient, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\hat {m})$$\end{document}t and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\hat {v})$$\end{document}t are their bias-corrected versions, η is the learning rate, β1 and β2 are the decay rates for the momentum and variance, and ε is a small constant to prevent division by zero.

At the end of each training epoch, we evaluate its performance on the validation set. During the evaluation, we evaluate measures including Character Error Rate (CER), Accuracy, Word Error Rate (WER), ROC AUC, and PR AUC. These evaluation measures offer comprehensive insights into its performance. The evaluation on the validation set helps in identifying whether the model has converged and if over fitting has occurred.

This study uses an early stopping method to prevent over fitting. If there is no significant improvement in the Character Error Rate (CER) on the validation set during several consecutive epochs, the training process will stop early, thus avoiding computational resources and ensuring optimal model performance. Additionally, a learning rate scheduler is used to adjust the learning rate according to the CER on the validation set. When the CER on the validation set stops decreasing, the learning rate is reduced according to a predefined strategy, helping the network to perform more fine-tuned optimization at a smaller learning rate.

Experimental results and discussions

Experimental setup

The research experiments were executed on a Windows 11 operating system using the PyTorch system for model deployment and instruction. All code was run on Python 3.9 environment. High-performance equipment was used to ensure the efficiency and rapid computation of the experiments. The system contains an Intel Core i7-12700 H 14-core processor, 16GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU. The setup supports the processing of large amounts of data and the building of deep learning models, offering enough computing power for model training.

In terms of software, PyTorch version 1.10 was used as the deep learning framework, along with CUDA 11.3 to accelerate GPU computation. All experiments were conducted with GPU acceleration to improve the efficiency of training and testing. The Adam optimizer was used for parameter optimization during training, and a learning rate scheduler (ReduceLROnPlateau) was developed to change the learning rate, thus reducing over fitting and speeding model solution.

During model training, to improve the generalization ability of the experiment and avoid over fitting, we also employed data augmentation techniques, such as adding Gaussian noise, volume variations, audio flipping, and spectrum scaling, further enriching the diversity of the training set. All experiments used a training batch size of 32, and evaluation was performed on the validation set to evaluate the performance of the model. This hardware and software setup enabled us to rapidly perform several tests for the HaiNan-to-Mandarin task, ensuring accuracy and consistency of the results.

Evaluation metrics

Accuracy

Accuracy is the proportion of correct predictions made by the model. It is one of the most commonly used metrics in classification tasks and represents the proportion of correct predictions across all samples. The expressions is as follows:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Acc=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}$$\end{document}

Where TP represents True Positives, TN represents True Negatives, FP represents False Positives, and FN represents False Negatives.

CER

Character Error Rate (CER) measures errors at the character level. It is commonly used in tasks such as speech recognition and Optical Character Recognition (OCR). The expressions for CER is as follows:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CER=\frac{S+D+I}{N}$$\end{document}

Where S is the number of substitution errors, D is the number of deletion errors, I is the number of insertion errors, and N is the number of characters in the reference string. This reflects the performance of the model at the character level, with a smaller value indicating higher recognition accuracy.

WER

Word Error Rate (WER) is a commonly used metric to measure the difference between the recognition result and the true label in speech recognition tasks. The expressions for WER is as follows:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:WER=\frac{S+D+I}{N}$$\end{document}

Where S is the number of substitution errors, D is the number of deletion errors, I is the number of insertion errors, and N is the number of words in the reference label. WER represents the error rate at the word level, with a lower value showing higher accuracy of the model.

Classification report

The classification report provides detailed performance metrics for the model on each class, including Precision, Recall, and F1-score. For each class, the expressions for these metrics are as follows:

Precision: The proportion of true situations of a class among all cases predicted as that class:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Precision}_{c}=\frac{T{P}_{c}}{T{P}_{c}+F{P}_{c}}$$\end{document}

Recall: The proportion of samples that are correctly predicted as class c among all samples that truly belong to class c:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Recall}_{c}=\frac{T{P}_{c}}{T{P}_{c}+F{N}_{c}}$$\end{document}

F1 Score: The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, used to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the classification performance:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F1\:Score}_{c}=2\times\:\frac{{Precision}_{c}\times\:{Recall}_{c}}{{Precision}_{c}+{Recall}_{c}}$$\end{document}

The classification report calculates the Precision, Recall, and F1-score for each class and returns the Macro Average and Weighted Average values.

Confusion matrix

A confusion matrix is used to describe the prediction performance of a classification model across different classes, particularly highlighting which classes are most likely to be confused with each other. Each element of the matrix represents the number of samples that are actually of class i but predicted as class j. The confusion matrix is crucial for analyzing multi-class tasks.

Receiver operating characteristic curve and area under curve

The ROC curve is an evaluation tool for the performance of a binary classification model, representing the relationship between the False Positive Rate (FPR) and the True Positive Rate (TPR). AUC (Area Under the Curve) is the area under the ROC curve and shows the ability of the model to distinguish between positive and negative classes. The closer the AUC is to 1, the better its performance.

Precision-recall curve and area under precision-recall curve

The PR curve shows the relationship between Precision and Recall at different thresholds. AUPR (Area Under the Precision-Recall curve) is the area under the PR curve and shows the performance of the model on imbalanced datasets. The larger the AUPR, the better the model is at keeping a high recall rate with a low false positive rate.

Comparative experimental analysis of different models

The ConvMHANet model proposed in this study shows significant performance in the HaiNan dialect speech recognition task. To further verify its effectiveness, we conducted multiple sets of comparison experiments.

The CNN-Siamese dual-stream fusion model proposed by Suwon Shon uses Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) to extract MFCC/FBANK/spectrum acoustic features and learns a dialect embedding space based on ASR textual features through a Siamese network26. The Multi-Scale Product Analysis-Hamiltonian Neural Network (MPA-HNN) constructed by Nejib Tibi extracts physiological acoustic parameters such as Glottal Closure Instant (GCI) and Fundamental Frequency (F0) through wavelet transforms, and constructs a quaternion recurrent neural network using Hamiltonian product operations27. The CNN-Self-Attention-DNN architecture designed by Chengtao Cai replaces the traditional LSTM with a four layer Self-Attention module, using 2D Convolutional layers to implement spectrogram positional encoding. It achieved a 15.7% Character Error Rate (CER) on a 170-hour Mandarin datasets28. For completeness, we additionally evaluate K-Wav2Vec 2.029, a wav2vec 2.0 derivative that employs hierarchical fine-tuning and joint graphemesyllable decoding, and has reported compet- itive results on large-vocabulary ASR benchmarks. The experimental results show that existing models exhibit significant differences in feature representation dimensions and context modeling approaches: The CNN-Siamese model effectively mitigates the phoneme overlap problem between dialects through acoustic-text dual-modal feature fusion, but its reliance on ASR system textual features increases computational complexity (requiring the parallel computation of five different features). Despite the potential advantages of MPA-HNN in identifying physiological audio data, the significant computational requirements related to multi-scale product analysis limit its use in resource-constrained environments. The CNN-Self-Attention-DNN model breaks through the local receptive field limitation of traditional CNNs through a global Attention mechanism. However, the single Mandarin recognition objective fails to account for differences in tonal balance and pronunciation characteristics among dialects.

Statistical significance and ablation experiments

A paired t-test (α = 0.05) was conducted to compare the original model of ConvMHANet with several parts using deletion experiments, to evaluate the contribution of each component to overall performance. The ablation experiments consist of the LSTM-based model, the model using only Self-Attention, the model using only CNN, and the combination model mixing CNN, LSTM, and Self-Attention. By comparing the performance of these models with the baseline model, ConvMHANet provides deeper analysis into the influence of each module on the performance of the model.

The ConvMHANet model (Table 1) demonstrated optimal performance in terms of Accuracy (ACC) and Error Rates (CER, WER). On the WanNing (Mixed) datasets, the baseline model achieved an accuracy of 0.9974, a Character Error Rate (CER) of 0.0026, a Word Error Rate (WER) of 0.0026, and both Precision and Recall of 1.00, showing that the model can achieve high accuracy and low error on the datasets.

It compares various metrics like Accuracy, CER, WER, Precision, Recall, F1-score, AUC, and AUPRC for each model.The LSTM model shows a significant performance difference when handling complex speech recognition tasks. Taking the multi-region mixed datasets as an example, the LSTM model achieved an accuracy of 0.3907, a Character Error Rate (CER) of 0.3807, and a Word Error Rate (WER) of 0.6250. This shows that LSTM struggles to effectively capture key features in the speech signal for this task, resulting in a high error rate.

We compared the model that uses only Self-Attention. As can be seen, the self-attention model performs relatively well on some datasets, especially on the WenChang (Mixed) datasets, where the accuracy is 0.1274. However, its performance is significantly lower than that of the baseline model. On the WanNing (Mixed) datasets, the self-attention model’s accuracy is only 0.2853, with a Character Error Rate (CER) of 0.4657 and a Word Error Rate (WER) of 0.7121. This shows that when using the self-attention method alone, the performance of the model is clearly limited and fails to fully leverage its potential advantages.

In contrast, the standalone CNN model performs slightly worse across all metrics. On the WanNing (Mixed) datasets, the CNN model achieved an accuracy of 0.9949. Although the accuracy is still high, it shows a slight decline in performance compared to the baseline model. Additionally, on the WenChang (Mixed) datasets, the CNN model exhibited a Character Error Rate (CER) of 0.0152 and a Word Error Rate (WER) of 0.0124. Although it is higher than the LSTM and Self-Attention models, it did not achieve a Precision and Recall of 1.00.

Next, we compared the CNN + LSTM + Self-Attention model. The hybrid model combining CNN, LSTM, and Self- Attention showed significant performance improvement across all datasets. On the WanNing (Mixed) datasets, the CNN + LSTM + Self-Attention model achieved an accuracy of 0.9743, a Character Error Rate (CER) of 0.0026, a Word Error Rate (WER) of 0.0244, Precision of 1.0000 and Recall of 0.9700, an F1-score of 0.9900, AUC of 0.9893, AUPRC of 0.9787, and a training time of 1467.54 s. This shows that the CNN + LSTM + Self-Attention model outperforms the models using CNN, LSTM, or Self-Attention individually on all metrics. Furthermore, the model exhibits significant expressiveness on complex datasets, although its training time is relatively long.

Figures 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 and 13 display the confusion matrices of the ablation models and the baseline model on multiple dialect test datasets. Each confusion matrix shows the relationship between the true labels and predicted labels during the prediction process. The values in the matrix reflect the prediction accuracy for each class and the classification errors that may occur. Through these confusion matrices, we can visually observe the classification performance and types of errors for different models across various datasets, particularly the confusion between different dialects.

Figure 14 shows the multi-dialect performance evaluation radar charts for different models in the HaiNan-to-Mandarin task. The figure includes four plots, each representing a different model: Plot A represents the pure Self-Attention model, Plot B represents the LSTM model, Plot C represents the CNN + Self-Attention + LSTM hybrid model, and Plot D represents the CNN model. Each Plot uses a radar chart to display the performance of each model across multiple evaluation metrics, including Accuracy (ACC), Character Error Rate (CER), Word Error Rate (WER), AUC, and AUPRC. Compared to ConvMHANet model, it is evident that the ConvMHANet model excels in all evaluation metrics, particularly in terms of accuracy and error control. The ConvMHANet model outperforms the other models, especially on the complex dialect mixed datasets, where it achieves high accuracy and significantly lower CER and WER. Through this comparison, we can confirm that the ConvMHANet model delivers the best performance in the HaiNan-to-Mandarin conversion task, especially in terms of effectiveness and adaptability in multi-dialect mixed environments.

Table 6 presents an ablation study on the number of self-attention heads for HaiNan-dialect-to-Mandarin conversion. The ConvMHANet with eight heads, is compared with CNN models that incorporate self-attention variants using 2, 4, 6, 12, and 16 heads, and performance is reported on five single-dialect test sets and one mixed-dialect set. The baseline consistently achieves the best overall results, reaching an accuracy of 0.9974 on the WanNing-Mixed datasets and 0.9758 on the Mix datasets, while keeping both CER and WER on Mix below 0.0163. When the head count is reduced to two or four, The accuracy of Mix datasets drops sharply to 0.7235 and 0.7823, showing that limited attention capacity cannot capture long-range tonal context. Increasing the head count beyond eight offers only marginal or even negative gains: accuracies with 12 and 16 heads fall to 0.6912 and 0.7846, and training time rises by roughly 30% and 60%, respectively. These findings demonstrate that eight self-attention heads provide the optimal balance between tonal-context modeling and computational efficiency for the HaiNan speech-conversion task.

Through statistical significance analysis and comparative evaluation of various models, the following conclusions can be drawn: The baseline ConvMHANet model substantially outperforms the other ablation models across key metrics, including accuracy, Character Error Rate (CER), and Word Error Rate (WER). Particularly, the performance advantage of ConvMHANet is more evident compared to the LSTM and self-attention models. Its superiority across multiple evaluation metrics indicates that incorporating the Self-Attention mechanism significantly enhances the model’s performance, especially in terms of accuracy and error rate control.

Conclusion

The innovation of this work consists of creating the first speech datasets covering five major dialect regions in HaiNan, and introducing an organizational combination model architecture that achieves an optimal level in both single-dialect and mixed scenarios through the synergistic optimization of convolution and attention methods. Moreover, targeted data enhancement strategies enhance the ability of the model for prediction across differences in speech rate and noise in the environment. However, the limited performance of low-resource dialects and the computational cost of the combination model remain current limitations.

The experiments in this study show that the ConvMHANet model exhibits significant results both in the face of complex tonal systems (e.g., seven tones in WenChang dialect) and long temporal dependencies (e.g., ancient Chinese rhythms in Oriental dialect), as well as in mixed-dialect scenarios. It is also pointed out in the experimental study that some of the models perform poorly in low-resource tasks due to ignoring local details and relying on large-scale data, emphasizing the necessity for multimodal feature combination.

Future work will focus on adaptive model lightening, small-sample migration learning, and dynamic enhancement strategies to enhance the practicality and promote the application of multi-dialect speech technology in public services, smart terminals, and other contexts.

Taken together, these results confirm that ConvMHANet is purpose-built for the phonological characteristics of HaiNan dialects rather than being a generic hybrid network.
