A Guillain Barre Syndrome (GBS): a case report
Authors: Sara Timerga, Aynalem Befkadu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGuillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) is an acute, immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy, with several clinical variants including the acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) subtype. AMAN often presents with rapid motor weakness, respiratory compromise, and may follow antecedent infections. We report a rare and severe case of AMAN in a 25-year-old female presenting with rapidly progressive quadriplegia and type II respiratory failure. She required prolonged mechanical ventilation, developed multiple hospital-acquired infections, and experienced infusion-related adverse reactions to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg). Despite these complications, gradual improvement was achieved through multidisciplinary care. This case underscores the potential severity of AMAN, especially when complicated by immunotherapy intolerance and secondary infections. It highlights the importance of early diagnosis, vigilant respiratory support, and individualised treatment strategies in improving outcomes in severe GBS variants.Keywords: Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS), Acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN), Respiratory failure, Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), Infusion reaction, Prolonged mechanical ventilation

Background

Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) is an acute, immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy with different presentations and it presents most commonly with symmetrical limb weakness and hyporeflexia or areflexia, cranial nerve involvement in more than half of patients, raised CSF protein concentrations without pleocytosis and it is triggered by a proceeding infection. The pathophysiology is based on the concept of molecular mimicry, which involves antiglycolipid antibodies to glycolipids, generated by immunoreaction against glycoconjugates in pathogens causing antecedent infections, which target peripheral nerves and nerve roots [1].

Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) is an acute, immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy with different presentations such as symmetrical limb weakness and hyporeflexia, or areflexia, raised CSF protein concentrations without pleocytosis and more than half of patients with GBS present with cranial nerve involvement, it often follows an infectious illness. The pathophysiology involves antiglycolipid antibodies to glysolipids generated by immunoreaction against glycoconjugates in pathogens causing antecedent infections, targeting of peripheral nerves and nerve roots [1, 2].

GBS presents in different subtypes based on pathophysiology, which present in different clinical variants. The acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP) is characterized by immune-mediated demyelination, T-cell and macrophage infiltration targeting myelin proteins. Clinically AIDP presents with progressive, symmetrical limb weakness, areflexia and variable sensory symptoms, as well as common cranial nerve involvement [1]. The acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) subtype results from the attack of macrophages on the nodes of Ranvier and their infiltration between the Schwann cell axolemma and the axon. Patients with AMAN variant present with purely motor involvement and reach nadir and recover faster due to the pathology, in which the conduction is blocked without any damage to the axon [1]. The acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN) resembles AMAN in pathology; however, in case of AMSAN both the dorsal and ventral roots are affected [3]. The Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS) is another variant, which is associated with anti-GQ1b antibodies, which target glycolipids highly expressed in cranial nerves. The variant is characterized by the triad of areflexia, ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia [4]. Respiratory compromise is a known and potentially life-threatening complication of GBS which affects more than 25% of patients, necessitating prompt recognition and intensive supportive care, including mechanical ventilation when required [5].

The incidence and subtype distribution of GBS varies significantly by geographic region, likely influenced by environmental, genetic, and infectious factors. In North America and Europe, the most common variant is AIDP, accounting for up to 90% of cases. In contrast, the axonal forms AMAN and AMSAN are more prevalent in parts of Asia, Central and South America, and some regions of China, where they may account for up to 30–50% of GBS cases. This regional difference is thought to be linked to variations in the prevalence of antecedent infections, particularly Campylobacter jejuni, which is strongly associated with AMAN [3]. Additionally, outbreaks of GBS have been reported following Zika virus outbursts in Latin America and the Caribbean, further highlighting the influence of infectious agents on regional GBS patterns [6]. These geographical differences underscore the importance of understanding local epidemiology when diagnosing and managing GBS.

Treatment typically involves intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) and plasma exchange (PEx), aimed at halting the autoimmune attack. Early initiation of therapy is associated with improved outcomes, although some patients may experience adverse reactions or inadequate response. Recovery can be prolonged, particularly in severe cases, and requires multidisciplinary management, including rehabilitation and supportive therapies [1].

This case describes a rare and severe presentation of GBS (AMAN subtype) in a young adult female, complicated by type II respiratory failure and prolonged ICU stay, with multiple challenges in immunotherapy administration and recurrent infections. The report highlights the importance of early diagnosis, vigilant supportive care, and a tailored approach to managing complications in GBS patients.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old female presented with progressive weakness affecting all four extremities over four days. The weakness began in the bilateral upper limbs and progressed to the lower limbs within 48 h. She denied any sensory changes, trauma, recent illness, fever, respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms, or bowel/bladder dysfunction. On examination, she was alert and oriented with stable initial vitals. Neurological assessment revealed symmetrical flaccid quadriparesis. Muscle strength testing using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale showed 2/5 strength in proximal upper limbs (deltoids, biceps), 3/5 distally (wrist extensors, grip), 1/5 strength in proximal lower limbs (hip flexors, quadriceps), and 2/5 distally (ankle dorsiflexors, toe extensors). Deep tendon reflexes were globally diminished (1/4), and hypotonia was noted in all four limbs. Sensory function and cranial nerves were intact. Despite preserved sensation and normal bowel and bladder function, her single breath count (SBC) declined rapidly from 19 on initial evaluation to 1–2 within a short time, prompting ICU transfer, intubation, and mechanical ventilation. She was diagnosed with Guillain-Barré Syndrome – acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) subtype, complicated by type II respiratory failure and mild hypokalemia (K⁺ 3.2 mmol/L). Other laboratory values and organ function tests were within normal limits. She was managed with ventilatory support, electrolyte correction, and appropriate supportive care.

A 25-year-old female presented with progressive weakness affecting all four extremities over a period of four days. The weakness initially involved the bilateral upper limbs and progressed to the lower limbs within 48 h. She had preserved sensation throughout, but deep tendon reflexes were globally diminished (1/4), and she exhibited bilateral hypotonia. There was no history of trauma, loss of consciousness, fever, cough, shortness of breath, vomiting, diarrhea, diabetes, hypertension, paresthesia, urinary or bowel incontinence, or recent illness. She was unable to lift her arms and showed no signs of altered bowel or bladder function. The patient’s single breath count (SBC) declined progressively from 19 on initial evaluation to 13–10 in a day, 5 by the following morning, and further dropped to 1–2 within the next 12 h, prompting ICU admission and respiratory support.

She was diagnosed with quadriplegia secondary to GBS, AMAN subtype, complicated by type II respiratory failure and mild hypokalemia. On ICU admission, her vital signs were as follows: BP 145/85 mmHg, HR 80 bpm, RR 28/min, Spo2 92%, GCS 15, and a temperature of 36.2 °C. Laboratory findings showed potassium at 3.2 mmol/L, sodium at 143 mmol/L, WBC at 8 × 10⁹/L an elevated protein and creatinine at 0.75 mg/dL. Renal and liver function tests, as well as ECG, were within normal limits. She was immediately intubated and placed on mechanical ventilation. She was ventilated in SIMV mode with tidal volume of 400 mL, FiO₂ of 40%, RR 16, and PEEP of 5 cmH₂O. Following intubation, her NIBP decreased to 110/70 mmHg, HR rose to 100 bpm, and oxygen saturation was 94%.

Initial medical management included omeprazole 40 mg IV BID, unfractionated SC heparin 5000 IU BID, pregabalin PO 75 mg BID, morphine 4 mg IV QID and potassium chloride PO 600 mg TID. Morphine was introduced for pain management on the fifth day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was initiated on the fifth day of admission. However, by the third dose in 3 h, the patient developed fever (39.4 °C) and headache unresponsive to paracetamol, and an infusion reaction was suspected. IVIG was discontinued. She was started on ceftriaxone 1 g IV BID and later metronidazole 500 mg IV TID on the 6th day of admission. Hypokalemia was corrected after five days of treatment with KCL, and potassium supplementation was discontinued. Subsequent labs on the 9th day of admission showed elevated WBC (12.07 × 10⁹/L), hemoglobin of 12.6 g/dL, neutrophils at 91.65%, and platelet count of 156 × 10⁹/L.

On the tenth ICU Day, IVIG was reinitiated at a slower rate (5 g over 12 h from 0.4 g/kg/day/15 g/day) with premedication including 500 mL normal saline, 650 mg paracetamol, hydrocortisone 50 mg iv and diphenhydramine 25 mg orally. Despite premedication, she again developed fever and a global headache without other symptoms. By the twelfth day, her regimen included omeprazole 400 mg IV daily, UFH 5000iu SC, pregabalin PO 75 mg BID, ceftriaxone 1 g IV BID and later metronidazole 500 mg IV TID, morphine 4 mg IV QID, and IVIG with additional premedication including hydrocortisone. Bisacodyl 5 mg daily was added on day 13 due to constipation. Vitals remained stable, and no new complaints were reported electrolyte K = 3.7, Na + = 128, Cr = 0.52.

On the seventeenth day, she developed high-grade fever and extremity pain. After a full 7-day course of ceftriaxone and metronidazole, she was switched to ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV BID on day 19. The bisacodyl dose was increased to 10 mg daily, and the morphine dose was reduced to 3 mg. By day 20, new antibiotics were added cefepime 1 g IV BID, vancomycin 1 g IV BID, and norfloxacin 400 mg IV BID for suspected gastrointestinal source sepsis. She was also given topical tetracycline eye ointment. On day 27, bisacodyl was reduced to 5 mg, and lactulose 15 mL daily was started. Her ventilator mode was changed to CPAP. On day 36, lactulose was increased to 30 mL, and by day 39, morphine was replaced with tramadol 50 mg IV TID.

She was successfully extubated on day 41 of mechanical ventilation. However, the next day, she developed shortness of breath, chest pain, and hyperventilation, necessitating re-intubation. A tracheostomy was performed on day 48. By day 58, she was weaned off the ventilator and transitioned to catheter oxygen therapy, having spent five days on CPAP prior. On day 64, she was restarted on cefepime and vancomycin for ventilator-associated pneumonia.

After significant improvement, she was transferred out of the ICU to the surgical ward on day 76. At that time, her vitals were stable BP 132/85 mmHg, HR 85 bpm, SpO₂ 95% on room air and GCS was 15. Motor strength remained 0/5, though other systemic evaluations were normal. She was readmitted on day 92 for shortness of breath and respiratory distress but recovered quickly without mechanical ventilation. She was managed with supportive care and antibiotics, and discharged after six days to the medical ward, with plans for continued outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

This case is notable for several reasons. First, the patient’s weakness progressed rapidly over 48 h, with preserved sensation, consistent with the AMAN subtype. Despite early intubation, the patient developed prolonged respiratory failure, requiring over 40 days of ventilator support, a tracheostomy, and eventual transition to oxygen therapy via nasal catheter. This highlights the severity of respiratory muscle involvement in AMAN and the need for long-term ventilatory planning.

The patient experienced complications related to both the disease process and its treatment. Notably, she had adverse reactions to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) on two separate occasions, manifesting as fever and headache, suspected to be infusion-related. Despite premedication with acetaminophen, diphenhydramine, corticosteroids, and IV fluids, these reactions limited the utility of standard immunotherapy and complicated her clinical course. While IVIG is a first-line therapy for GBS, not all patients tolerate it well, and in settings where plasmapheresis is unavailable, treatment options may be limited.

During her ICU stay, ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) emerged as a potential complicating factor. Given the duration of mechanical ventilation, the risk for VAP is significant. The presence of new or worsening infiltrates, fever, increased secretions, and hypoxemia should prompt evaluation for VAP. However, distinguishing between progression of GBS-related respiratory failure and superimposed VAP remains challenging. In this case, while infection was not microbiologically confirmed, the clinical team initiated empirical antibiotic therapy based on clinical suspicion and risk factors. This highlights the need for a high index of suspicion for VAP in prolonged ventilated GBS patients and the utility of bronchoalveolar lavage and serial inflammatory markers for diagnosis.

Treatment was further complicated by adverse reactions to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). The patient experienced significant side effects on two separate occasions manifesting as fever and headache despite premedication with acetaminophen, diphenhydramine, corticosteroids, and IV fluids. While these symptoms are commonly associated with infusion-related reactions, the possibility of more severe adverse events must be considered. Serious reactions to IVIG include: Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) presenting as non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema with acute hypoxemia and bilateral infiltrates; Hemolytic anemia often due to passive transfer of anti-A or anti-B antibodies, resulting in hemoglobin drop, hyperbilirubinemia, and elevated LDH; Aseptic meningitis, thromboembolic events, and renal dysfunction [7].

Given the adverse reactions, IVIG infusions were discontinued. In such scenarios, the decision to reinitiate IVIG must weigh risk vs. benefit. Strategies to consider upon re-challenge include: Slower infusion rates; Increased IV hydration; Alternative IVIG formulations (with lower sugar content or fewer stabilizers); Enhanced premedication protocols; Close cardiorespiratory monitoring during infusion.

This case illustrates the challenges in managing AMAN-associated GBS, particularly when complicated by prolonged ventilatory dependency, possible VAP, and intolerance to first-line immunotherapy. A multidisciplinary approach with early respiratory support planning, vigilant infection surveillance, and tailored immunomodulatory therapy is essential for optimizing outcomes.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of early recognition and aggressive supportive care in GBS patients, especially those with rapidly progressing motor weakness. It also highlights the need for vigilance regarding complications of therapy, including IVIG-related adverse effects and nosocomial infections. Given the limited response to immunotherapy and the prolonged ventilation course, this case may represent a severe phenotype of AMAN. Multidisciplinary management, including critical care, neurology, infectious disease, and rehabilitation, is essential to optimize outcomes in such complex cases.
