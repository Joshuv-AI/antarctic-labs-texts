Longitudinal effects of physical activity on university students’ mental health via self-efficacy and the moderating role of peer support
Authors: Renjie Ma, Hongyuan Liu, Mengyu Shi, Qiuhan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the longitudinal mechanisms through which physical activity (PA) impacts students’ mental health (well-being and emotional problems), and to systematically test the mediating role of self-efficacy alongside the moderating role of peer support. A two-wave longitudinal panel design with a 5-month time lag was conducted among 847 Chinese university students. Validated and culturally adapted scales were utilized to precisely assess physical activity (incorporating PE course exertion), well-being, emotional problems, general self-efficacy, and perceived peer support. Data were analyzed using Mplus 8.3 to construct cross-lagged panel models and longitudinal moderated mediation models, rigorously controlling for baseline variables and demographic covariates. (1) Cross-lagged analyses demonstrated that T1 physical activity significantly and positively predicted T2 well-being (β = 0.15, p < 0.01) and negatively predicted T2 emotional problems (β = -0.12, p < 0.01), with no significant reverse causality paths identified. (2) Longitudinal mediation analyses revealed that T2 self-efficacy fully mediated the pathways from physical activity to both mental health indicators, accounting for 61.8% and 64.3% of the total effects, respectively. Upon the inclusion of this mediator, the direct effects of physical activity on mental health were rendered non-significant. (3) Moderated mediation models confirmed that peer support significantly moderated the initial segment of the mediational chain (Physical Activity → Self-efficacy; interaction term β = 0.09, p < 0.01). Simple slope and conditional indirect effect analyses indicated that a high-level peer network synergistically amplified the efficiency with which physical activity translated into self-efficacy. Physical activity exerts a robust temporal protective effect on university students’ mental health, a macro-level benefit realized primarily through the cultivation of deep-seated self-efficacy. Furthermore, a high-quality peer support environment serves as a critical socio-ecological catalyst that amplifies these psychological dividends. By partially addressing the temporal limitations of cross-sectional designs, this study provides empirical foundations for a psychologically-oriented paradigm shift in PE curricula and the development of cross-departmental synergistic interventions. Introduction

Mental health problems among university students have emerged as a critical public health crisis, significantly impacting the quality of higher education and the development of national human capital1,2. During the pivotal transition from adolescence to adulthood, university students face a confluence of stressors, including intensifying academic competition, the restructuring of interpersonal relationships, and profound uncertainties regarding future employment3,4. The co-occurrence of these stressors renders this population highly vulnerable to clinically significant anxiety, depression, and adjustment difficulties5. In light of this severe public health challenge, there is an urgent need to identify effective, evidence-based, and highly scalable intervention strategies for promoting mental well-being.

In this context, university physical education (PE) has garnered increasing attention for its unique advantages in fostering holistic student health6. As a fundamental component of the higher education curriculum, PE serves not merely as an arena for imparting motor skills, but more importantly, as a vital institutional platform for the preventive intervention of psychological distress7. Crucially, however, the primary behavioral conduit through which PE exerts its mental health benefits is the actual physical activity (PA) in which students engage8. Structured PE courses are explicitly designed to elevate students’ PA levels—encompassing exercise intensity, duration, and frequency—through goal setting, organized group exercises, and the dissemination of fitness knowledge9. From both neurobiological and psychological perspectives, it is this tangible engagement in PA that triggers positive neuroendocrine changes and builds psychological resilience. Consequently, to robustly evaluate the efficacy of PE-based mental health interventions, it is methodologically imperative to center the investigation on the precise measurement of students’ actual PA levels.

Although a substantial body of literature has established a significant positive correlation between regular PA and mental health, these studies present three notable limitations10. First, the predominant reliance on cross-sectional designs precludes rigorous causal inferences regarding the temporal directionality between PA and mental health, thereby providing a relatively weak theoretical foundation for utilizing PE as a preventive intervention11. Second, existing research has rarely delineated the internal psychological mechanisms underlying how PA affects mental health; this mechanistic “black box” limits the optimization of precision intervention protocols12. Third, while human psychological development is fundamentally embedded within socio-ecological systems, current theoretical models have insufficiently accounted for social-environmental factors, particularly peer relationships, which constitute a highly influential social context for university students13.

To elucidate the internal mechanisms linking PA and mental health, the present study incorporates self-efficacy, a core construct from Bandura’s social cognitive theory14. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their capability to successfully execute specific tasks or manage challenging situations15. The PE environment provides an ideal practical arena for cultivating self-efficacy: students acquire “mastery experiences” and “vicarious experiences” as they confront physical challenges and master new athletic skills. Crucially, these “I can do it” beliefs cultivated in the athletic context can generate cross-situational generalization effects, translating into psychological resilience when individuals face academic and life stressors, thereby alleviating negative emotions and enhancing subjective well-being16. Nevertheless, there remains a paucity of longitudinal research systematically validating the temporal chain of “PA → self-efficacy → mental health.”

Furthermore, individual psychological development is inevitably contingent upon external social networks. For university students, peer groups represent one of the most critical social environments. Peer support—encompassing emotional validation, instrumental assistance, and informational guidance—has proven to be a vital protective resource for buffering stress and facilitating positive development17. Given the highly socially interactive nature of sports and exercise, physical activities serve as unique platforms for establishing and maintaining peer relationships18. Drawing upon social learning theory and social identity theory, an environment characterized by high peer support can amplify individuals’ positive experiences and buffer feelings of defeat during setbacks19. Consequently, peer support is hypothesized to play a critical moderating role in the pathway from PA to self-efficacy.

To address the limitations of previous cross-sectional studies and systematically examine the underlying mechanisms, the current study is grounded in Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) and Ecological Systems Theory (EST). SCT suggests that human functioning is the product of a dynamic interplay among behaviors, personal factors, and the environment. From this perspective, physical activity serves as a behavioral factor that fosters mastery experiences, which in turn enhances self-efficacy—a personal cognitive factor closely related to mental health. Meanwhile, EST highlights the importance of the immediate social environment. In the university context, peer support acts as a key microsystem factor that may interact with physical activity to facilitate psychological development.

By utilizing a two-wave longitudinal panel design with a 5-month time lag, the main contributions of this study are twofold: first, to clarify the temporal mechanism of how physical activity influences mental health (via self-efficacy); second, to identify the boundary conditions of when this effect is optimized (depending on peer support), thereby providing empirical evidence for related health interventions.

Based on these theoretical foundations and research contributions, we propose a longitudinal moderated mediation model (illustrated in Fig. 1) and test the following directed hypotheses:

H1

T1 physical activity positively predicts T2 well-being and negatively predicts T2 emotional problems.

H2

T2 self-efficacy mediates the longitudinal relationship between T1 physical activity and T2 mental health outcomes.

H3

T2 peer support moderates the pathway from T1 physical activity to T2 self-efficacy, such that this association is stronger for students reporting higher levels of peer support.

Participants and methods

Participants

This study employed cluster sampling to recruit undergraduate students from Zhengzhou University. To systematically explore the dynamic relationships among variables over a complete academic semester, a two-wave longitudinal panel design was implemented with a 5-month time lag. Data were collected at Time 1 (T1; September 2024, beginning of the semester) and Time 2 (T2; January 2025, end of the semester). At T1, 1,200 questionnaires were distributed, yielding 1,156 responses (response rate = 96.3%). After excluding invalid responses (e.g., patterned answers, > 20% missing data, or logical inconsistencies), a baseline sample of 1,089 valid questionnaires was established (validity rate = 94.2%). At T2, the T1 participants were followed up, returning 923 valid questionnaires. Following rigorous data cleaning and matching, 847 paired cases of valid longitudinal data were retained, representing a retention rate of 77.8%. To ascertain whether the sample attrition introduced systematic bias, independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare the attrited sample (n = 242) and the final retained sample (n = 847) on T1 baseline continuous variables. Results revealed no significant differences in physical activity (t = −1.43, p = 0.153), well-being (t = 0.87, p = 0.385), emotional problems (t = −0.62, p = 0.535), self-efficacy (t = 1.21, p = 0.227), or peer support (t = 0.95, p = 0.342). Furthermore, chi-square tests indicated no significant differences in the distributions of gender (chi^2 = 1.25, p = 0.264) or year of study (chi^2 = 3.14, p = 0.370) between the two groups, thereby robustly excluding the risk of systematic attrition bias. The demographic characteristics of the final sample are detailed in Table 1. All participants provided informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Zhengzhou University.

Methods

Physical activity rating scale

Physical activity was evaluated using the Chinese version of the Physical Activity Rating Scale-3 (PARS-3), initially revised and validated by Liu et al.20. As one of the most classic and widely endorsed psychometric instruments in Chinese sports psychology, it exhibits excellent cultural suitability. The scale assesses recent physical activity across three dimensions: intensity, duration per session, and frequency, each rated on a 5-point scale. The total score is computed as: Intensity × (Duration − 1) × Frequency. To explicitly align this measurement with the context of university physical activity, the administration instructions strictly required participants to incorporate the physical exertion from structured PE classes into their evaluations. The Cronbach’s α coefficients in this study were 0.82 at T1 and 0.85 at T2. However, the scale evaluates their overall PA levels, which inherently encompasses both curricular PE exertion and any extracurricular exercises. Therefore, the exposure variable is strictly conceptualized and termed “Physical Activity” rather than “Physical Education” in our models.

Mental health comprehensive questionnaire

To yield a comprehensive psychological profile, mental health was assessed via both positive and negative dimensions. The instruments utilized are well-established in the Chinese context and extensively documented in the authoritative Mental Health Rating Scale Manual.

Well-being: The culturally validated Chinese version of the World Health Organization Five Well-Being Index (WHO-5) was employed to assess positive affective experiences over the preceding two weeks21(e.g., “I have felt cheerful and in good spirits”). Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (at no time) to 5 (all of the time). The Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.89 at T1 and 0.91 at T2.Emotional problems: The Chinese translation of the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) was utilized22. This brief scale comprises core symptom items from the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2)23 for depression and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2)24 for anxiety. Participants rated the frequency of being bothered by issues such as “feeling down, depressed, or hopeless” over the past two weeks on a 4-point scale (0 = not at all, 3 = nearly every day). The Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.84 at T1 and 0.86 at T2.

It is worth noting that we operationalized mental health using overarching, stable indicators of psychological functioning (i.e., sustained well-being measured by the WHO-5, and core internalizing symptoms measured by the PHQ-4) rather than measuring transient affective states (e.g., fleeting happiness). This approach is highly recommended for public health surveillance in longitudinal designs, as it captures robust psychological changes over a 5-month period. Furthermore, the construct of “self-confidence” was not included as an outcome variable because it is theoretically and inherently captured by our core mediating variable: general self-efficacy, which provides a more precise, action-oriented measure of individuals’ belief in their capabilities.

General self-efficacy scale

General self-efficacy was measured using the Chinese version of the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), translated and psychometrically validated by Zhang and Schwarzer25. This culturally adapted version has demonstrated rigorous measurement equivalence and robust reliability among Chinese university student cohorts. The 10-item scale evaluates individuals’ overarching confidence in coping with novel situations or adversities (e.g., “I can always manage to solve difficult problems if I try hard enough”). Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale. The Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.93 at T1 and 0.94 at T2.

Peer support scale

Peer support was assessed using the culturally adapted Chinese version of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS), originally developed by Zimet et al.26. While the original overarching MSPSS comprises 12 items across three dimensions (family, friends, and significant others), the present study specifically extracted the “friends/peer support” subscale to align with our research focus. This specific subscale consists of 4 items (e.g., “My friends really try to help me”). The Chinese adaptation of this scale has been widely validated and demonstrated excellent reliability and validity among Chinese university populations. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for this subscale were 0.88 at T1 and 0.90 at T2.

Procedure

Group administration was conducted on a class-by-class basis via an online survey platform. Prior to administration, trained research assistants (psychology graduate students) delivered standardized instructions, emphasizing the anonymity, confidentiality, and importance of authentic responding. To facilitate longitudinal data matching across the two waves, participants provided the last six digits of their student identification numbers as unique, de-identified codes.

Data analysis

Data processing and statistical analyses were executed using SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3. Initially, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to evaluate potential common method bias. Subsequently, descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations were computed. Finally, longitudinal moderated mediation models were constructed in Mplus 8.3. In Mplus, the models were estimated using the Maximum Likelihood Robust (MLR) estimator, which provides standard errors that are robust to non-normality. Missing data were handled using the Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) approach. Model fit was evaluated using multiple established indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). According to the classical guidelines27, a good model fit is indicated by CFI and TLI values ≥ 0.90, and RMSEA and SRMR values ≤ 0.08. The models specified T1 physical activity as the independent variable, T2 mental health as the dependent variable, T2 self-efficacy as the mediator, and T2 peer support as the moderator. These pathways were tested while adjusting for T1 baseline variables (T1 mental health and T1 self-efficacy) as well as gender and year of study. Preliminary analyses of variance (ANOVAs) demonstrated that place of origin and academic major exerted no significant effects on the primary dependent variables (p > 0.05). Consequently, to maintain model parsimony and avoid overfitting, these demographic variables were excluded, with the final models controlling exclusively for the key covariates. The bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method (5,000 resamples) was utilized to determine the significance of the mediation and moderation effects, with 95% confidence intervals excluding zero indicating statistical significance.

Results

Common method bias test

Given that all data in this study were collected via self-report measures, there was a potential risk of common method bias. To assess this issue, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted separately on the data collected at T1 and T2. The unrotated exploratory factor analysis revealed 12 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 at T1, with the first common factor accounting for 23.47% of the total variance. Similarly, at T2, 11 factors exhibited eigenvalues greater than 1, with the primary factor explaining 24.13% of the variance. Because the variance explained by the first unrotated factor at both time points fell substantially below the stringent 40% threshold, it was concluded that common method bias was not a serious concern in the current investigation.

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis

The descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation matrix for all core variables across the two time points are detailed in Table 2. At baseline, the mean score for T1 physical activity was 32.56 (SD = 18.73). In the follow-up wave, students demonstrated adequate variability in T2 well-being (M = 3.42, SD = 0.89), T2 emotional problems (M = 1.87, SD = 0.76), T2 self-efficacy (M = 2.91, SD = 0.68), and T2 peer support (M = 3.87, SD = 0.82).

The longitudinal correlational patterns aligned robustly with theoretical expectations. Specifically, T1 physical activity was significantly and positively correlated with T2 well-being (r = 0.34, p < 0.01) and negatively correlated with T2 emotional problems (r = −0.28, p < 0.01). T1 physical activity also positively predicted T2 self-efficacy (r = 0.41, p < 0.01). Concurrently, T2 self-efficacy, acting as the proposed mediator, showed a strong positive correlation with T2 well-being (r = 0.58, p < 0.01) and a strong negative correlation with T2 emotional problems (r = −0.52, p < 0.01). T2 peer support exhibited significant positive associations with T2 self-efficacy (r = 0.46, p < 0.01) and T2 well-being (r = 0.43, p < 0.01), alongside a negative correlation with T2 emotional problems (r = −0.37, p < 0.01). These significant bivariate relationships furnished strong preliminary empirical support for the construction of subsequent cross-lagged and mediation models.

Cross-lagged analysis of physical activity and mental health

To rigorously establish the temporal directionality between physical activity and mental health, cross-lagged panel models were constructed using Mplus 8.3. Two separate, parallel models were evaluated with well-being and emotional problems serving as independent mental health indicators.

Model 1: cross-lagged model of physical activity and well-being

The structural equation model demonstrated excellent data-model fit: χ²/df = 2.34, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.05 (90% CI: 0.04–0.06), and SRMR = 0.03. Path analysis results, as illustrated in Fig. 2a, revealed that after controlling for autoregressive stability effects—where T1 physical activity robustly predicted T2 physical activity (β = 0.31, p < 0.01) and T1 well-being predicted T2 well-being (β = 0.48, p < 0.01) —the cross-lagged predictive path from T1 physical activity to T2 well-being remained significant (β = 0.15, p < 0.01). Conversely, the reverse cross-lagged effect from T1 well-being to T2 physical activity was not significant (β = 0.06, p = 0.142). The concurrent correlation between T1 variables was positive and significant (r = 0.29, p < 0.01).

Model 2: cross-lagged model of physical activity and emotional problems

This model also yielded superb fit indices: χ²/df = 2.28, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.05 (90% CI: 0.04–0.06), and SRMR = 0.03. Path analysis, depicted in Fig. 2b, confirmed the presence of strong autoregressive effects (β = 0.31, p < 0.01 for PA; β = 0.52, p < 0.01 for emotional problems). Regarding the cross-lagged paths, T1 physical activity significantly and negatively predicted T2 emotional problems (β = −0.12, p < 0.01), whereas T1 emotional problems failed to predict T2 physical activity (β = −0.04, p = 0.328). Baseline T1 physical activity and T1 emotional problems were negatively correlated (r = −0.24, p < 0.01). Collectively, the cross-lagged analyses consistently designated physical activity as the temporal antecedent to mental health, providing no empirical support for reverse causality pathways. These findings provide compelling longitudinal evidence solidifying the “physical activity → mental health” trajectory, thereby supporting H1.

Testing the longitudinal mediation effect of physical activity on mental health

To illuminate the underlying mechanisms, longitudinal mediation models were tested in Mplus 8.3 to examine the mediating role of T2 self-efficacy in linking T1 physical activity to T2 mental health outcomes. All analyses strictly controlled for covariates including gender, year of study, respective T1 baseline mental health, and T1 baseline self-efficacy. The significance of indirect effects was estimated utilizing the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples.

Model 1: T2 well-being as outcome variable

The structural equation model exhibited strong fit: χ²/df = 2.14, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.04 (90% CI: 0.03–0.05), and SRMR = 0.03. As illustrated in Fig. 3a and summarized in Table 3, T1 physical activity exerted a significant positive predictive effect on T2 self-efficacy (β = 0.23, p < 0.01). Furthermore, controlling for baseline psychological statuses, T2 self-efficacy significantly and positively predicted T2 well-being (β = 0.42, p < 0.01). Notably, upon the inclusion of self-efficacy as a mediator, the direct path from T1 physical activity to T2 well-being was rendered non-significant (β = 0.06, p = 0.132). The bootstrap test confirmed a robust indirect effect of 0.097 (95% CI: [0.069–0.128]), which accounted for an impressive 61.8% of the total effect. This indicates that, within the scope of this two-wave design, self-efficacy served as a primary and sufficient mediating mechanism translating physical activity into enhanced well-being.

Model 2: T2 emotional problems as outcome variable

The model fit for emotional problems was equally robust: χ²/df = 2.07, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.04 (90% CI: 0.03–0.05), and SRMR = 0.03. Path analyses (Fig. 3b; Table 3) revealed that T1 physical activity significantly bolstered T2 self-efficacy (β = 0.23, p < 0.01), which in turn significantly mitigated T2 emotional problems (β = −0.36, p < 0.01). Parallel to the previous model, the direct effect of T1 physical activity on T2 emotional problems was statistically non-significant (β = −0.05, p = 0.167). The significant indirect effect was estimated at −0.083 (95% CI: [−0.110 to −0.059]), explaining 64.3% of the total effect. These synergistic results validate that self-efficacy operates as a crucial mediational bridge by which physical activity acts to diminish subsequent emotional distress. Collectively, these results confirm the mediational role of self-efficacy across both mental health outcomes, providing full support for H2.

Testing the moderating role of peer support

To ensure strict methodological consistency, the moderated mediation analyses were likewise executed using Mplus 8.3. This step aimed to investigate whether T2 peer support moderated the initial segment of the mediational chain. Methodologically, although peer support was assessed at T2, it was conceptualized in this model as a proxy for the cumulative and stable psychosocial environment students experienced throughout the academic semester (for the conceptual framework, see Fig. 1). Prior to the construction of interaction terms, all continuous variables were mean-centered to mitigate multicollinearity concerns.

Model 1: T2 well-being as outcome variable

The results of the moderated mediation analysis are synthesized in Table 4. Controlling for relevant baseline covariates, both T1 physical activity (β = 0.18, p < 0.01) and T2 peer support (β = 0.28, p < 0.01) emerged as significant independent predictors of T2 self-efficacy. Crucially, the interaction term (T1 Physical Activity times T2 Peer Support) significantly and positively predicted T2 self-efficacy (β = 0.09, p < 0.01), verifying the moderating influence of peer support.

To unpack this interaction, simple slope analyses were plotted at varying levels of peer support (M ± 1 SD) (Fig. 4a). For students situated in low peer support environments, the predictive effect of T1 physical activity on T2 self-efficacy was significant but modest (β = 0.12, p = 0.002, 95% CI: [0.04, 0.19]). Conversely, for students embedded in highly supportive peer networks, this positive association was substantially amplified (β = 0.25, p < 0.001, 95% CI: [0.18, 0.33]). Advanced Johnson-Neyman technique analysis indicated that the positive predictive effect of T1 physical activity was consistently significant so long as the T2 peer support score surpassed 2.87 (covering 81.2% of the sample distribution). The test for the index of moderated mediation, detailed in Table 5, indicated that the conditional indirect effect grew from 0.050 (95% CI: [0.018, 0.088]) under low peer support to 0.105 (95% CI: [0.070, 0.145]) under high peer support. The overall index of moderated mediation was 0.038 (95% CI: [0.014, 0.065]), whose confidence interval strictly excluded zero, thereby providing definitive evidence for the moderated mediation mechanism.

Model 2: T2 emotional problems as outcome variable

This identical interaction pattern was successfully validated in the emotional problems model. The interaction term significantly predicted T2 self-efficacy (β = 0.08, p < 0.01). Simple slope analyses (Fig. 4b) corroborated that physical activity yielded a weaker impact on self-efficacy among students with low peer support (β = 0.11, p = 0.005, 95% CI: [0.03, 0.18]) compared to those enjoying high peer support (β = 0.24, p < 0.001, 95% CI: [0.17, 0.32]). The subsequent moderated mediation analysis (Table 5) demonstrated a significant amplification in the mediational pathway: the conditional indirect effect shifted from − 0.040 (95% CI: [−0.072, −0.013]) in the low-support condition to −0.086 (95% CI: [−0.120, −0.056]) in the high-support condition. The index of moderated mediation was significant at −0.032 (95% CI: [−0.056, −0.012]).

Sensitivity Analysis: To ensure the robustness of the moderated mediation model, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by incorporating baseline peer support (T1) as an additional covariate. The results demonstrated that after controlling for T1 peer support, the core interaction effect (Physical Activity × Peer Support) remained robust and statistically significant (β = 0.08, SE = 0.03, p < 0.01), which is fully consistent with the primary model (see Table S1 in the Supplementary Material). This confirms that the moderating role of peer support is stable and independent of baseline levels.

Taken together, these primary and sensitivity analyses consistently confirm that peer support significantly amplifies the pathway from physical activity to self-efficacy, thereby supporting H3.

Discussion

Longitudinal predictive effects of physical activity on university students’ mental health

By employing a two-wave longitudinal panel design, this study firmly established the long-term beneficial impacts of physical activity on university students’ mental well-being. Consistent with H1, T1 physical activity significantly and positively predicted T2 well-being (β = 0.15) while negatively predicting T2 emotional problems (β = −0.12), with no evidence of reverse causality. The findings demonstrated that, after controlling for baseline psychological statuses, T1 physical activity significantly and positively predicted T2 well-being, while significantly and negatively predicting T2 emotional problems. This provides robust longitudinal evidence supporting the promotion of physical activity as a viable mental health intervention strategy. Compared to the plethora of prior studies relying on cross-sectional data, this investigation utilized cross-lagged models to rigorously mitigate the confounding effects of reverse causality (i.e., the premise that healthier individuals simply exercise more), thereby solidifying the robustness of the conclusions.

These predictive effects can be comprehensively understood through multiple theoretical lenses. First, from a neurobiological perspective, regular physical activity stimulates the secretion of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and enhances the structural and functional integrity of brain regions critical for emotion regulation, such as the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus28. Additionally, physical activity modulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, systematically lowering cortisol levels to alleviate symptoms of anxiety and depression29. Importantly, these neurobiological and psychological pathways are fundamentally interconnected within an integrated regulatory framework. While neurobiological adaptations (e.g., HPA axis modulation) establish the physiological substrate for stress resilience, self-efficacy functions as a critical cognitive mechanism that orchestrates the appraisal and internalisation of these physiological improvements into sustained psychological well-being. Second, from a psychological standpoint, engagement in physical activity offers students a necessary “psychological time out” from relentless academic and interpersonal stressors. The immersive experience of “flow” states achieved during physical exertion carries intrinsic therapeutic value. Finally, from a socio-psychological perspective, structured physical education provides invaluable opportunities for social interaction, fostering a sense of belonging and social identity that serves as a fundamental safeguard for mental health30. Specifically, the present study found that the direct effect of physical activity on mental health was rendered non-significant when self-efficacy was included in the model as a mediator. This indicates that self-efficacy fully mediated the longitudinal relationship from physical activity to mental health. This suggests that physical activity does not dictate mental health mechanically or directly; rather, it operates by activating profound internal psychological mechanisms. This finding challenges the simplistic “exercise-health” linear model and underscores the necessity of exploring underlying psychological mediators. Practically, this implies that merely increasing students’ exercise volume is insufficient; educators must prioritize cultivating and fortifying individual psychological resources within the context of physical education.

It is worth noting that while the standardized cross-lagged path coefficients in this study (β = 0.15 for well-being and β = −0.12 for emotional problems) might appear modest in absolute terms, they are highly robust. In cross-lagged panel models, these coefficients represent the prediction of residual change over a 5-month period after strictly controlling for baseline autoregressive effects (which typically account for the majority of the variance). In the field of exercise psychology, comparable longitudinal studies typically report cross-lagged effect sizes ranging from 0.05 to 0.15. Therefore, the magnitude of the effects found in our study demonstrates a substantial and clinically meaningful shift in students’ psychological trajectories driven by physical activity.

The mediating mechanism of self-efficacy: from “mastery experience” to mental Health

Supporting H2, a central finding of this study is that self-efficacy fully mediated the longitudinal association between physical activity and mental health. This discovery successfully elucidates the mechanistic “black box” through which physical activity promotes psychological well-being. Rather than being a direct equivalent to mental health, physical activity serves as a potent stimulus that bolsters self-efficacy, a robust psychological resource that subsequently sustains and enhances mental health. The mediation effect was confirmed across both well-being and emotional problems models, accounting for substantial proportions of the total effects (61.8% and 64.3%, respectively), indicating a highly robust and universal underlying mechanism.

Grounded in social cognitive theory, the university physical education environment constitutes an exceptional practical arena integrating the four primary sources of self-efficacy31. First, during physical activities, students acquire direct “mastery experiences”—the most potent source of self-efficacy—by repeatedly practicing, acquiring new skills, and pushing physical limits. Whether successfully executing a complex gymnastics routine or breaking a personal running record, these embodied achievements rapidly internalize into “I can do it” beliefs. Second, within team sports, students glean “vicarious experiences” by observing peers’ successes and failures, which recalibrates their own capability appraisals. Third, encouragement from instructors and teammates serves as effective “verbal persuasion”. Lastly, positive physiological arousal associated with exercise is interpreted as an indicator of optimal functioning, further cementing self-efficacy. The deeper mechanistic value lies in the “cross-situational generalization” characteristic of sports-derived self-efficacy. Although initially forged in athletic tasks, this resilience permeates broader academic and social domains. When confronting academic hurdles or future uncertainties, the tenacity cultivated on the sports field empowers students to approach real-world stressors as “controllable challenges,” thereby reducing emotional distress and elevating well-being. This generalization aligns with cognitive appraisal theory: individuals with high self-efficacy tend to interpret stressors as manageable opportunities rather than insurmountable threats, avoiding the pitfalls of “learned helplessness”. However, while this study utilized a longitudinal design, the mediator and outcome were assessed concurrently at T2; future research should employ three-wave designs to further rigorously validate this causal chain.

The moderating role of peer support: the complexity of social contexts

This study revealed that peer support significantly moderated the initial segment of the mediational pathway (Physical Activity → Self-Efficacy). In line with H3, high levels of peer support substantially amplified the promotive effect of physical activity on self-efficacy. Specifically, high levels of peer support substantially amplified the promotive effect of physical activity on self-efficacy. This finding highlights the critical role of social-contextual factors in individual psychological development, perfectly aligning with ecological systems theory, which posits that development results from continuous interactions between internal traits and external environments. Theoretically, this “synergistic amplification effect” can be interpreted through social learning theory and social identity theory32. In physical activities, peers act not merely as co-participants but as emotional anchors and behavioral models. When students perceive strong peer support, the incremental progress they achieve in sports is more readily reinforced by positive peer feedback. Concurrently, peer support acts as a formidable “emotional buffer”: when individuals encounter setbacks during skill acquisition, an inclusive peer environment helps attribute these failures to temporary, modifiable factors, thereby shielding self-efficacy from the corrosive effects of negative experiences33. Conversely, in low-support or exclusionary environments, the sense of achievement derived from exercise may be undermined by feelings of social defeat, hindering its translation into stable self-efficacy.

The simple slope analysis profoundly illustrates the essence of this interaction: peer support does not simply substitute for a lack of exercise; rather, it functions as a potent “catalyst,” enabling high-intensity physical activity to be maximally converted into robust psychological capital. In practical terms, considering that the peer support scale ranges from 1 to 5, the threshold of 2.87 falls slightly below the midpoint. Within the current sample, the vast majority (81.2%) of students scored above this threshold, indicating a moderate to normative level of peer support. This signifies that the “synergistic amplification effect” does not require extraordinarily intimate or intense peer networks to be activated. Rather, achieving a universally inclusive, non-hostile, and moderately supportive environment is sufficient to catalyze the psychological benefits of physical activity. For intervention practices, this implies that physical educators do not necessarily need to engineer deep, interpersonal friendships; simply fostering a basic climate of teamwork, mutual respect, and basic peer acceptance in physical education classes is a highly cost-effective strategy to unlock the protective power of exercise. However, as the peer support measured herein primarily assessed general interpersonal quality, future research should distinguish between “sport-specific peer support” and general social support to delineate the precise boundaries of peer influence.

Theoretical contributions

The theoretical contributions of this study are primarily reflected in the integration of Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) and Ecological Systems Theory (EST) to elucidate the complex mechanisms linking physical activity to mental health. First, by applying SCT, this study moves beyond merely confirming the positive outcomes of physical activity, providing a theoretical explanation for its temporal mechanism. We demonstrate that physical activity functions as a behavioral catalyst that fosters “mastery experiences,” which subsequently build self-efficacy—the core cognitive software necessary for improving psychological well-being. Second, by incorporating EST, this study theoretically delineates the boundary conditions of this mechanism. We conceptualize peer support not merely as a passive buffer, but as an active microsystem factor that creates a “synergistic amplification effect.” Specifically, a supportive peer environment interacts with individual physical behavior to maximize the cognitive translation of exercise into psychological capital. Together, these theoretical integrations provide a comprehensive socio-ecological perspective on university students’ mental health.

Practical contributions

The empirical findings offer highly actionable implications for the reform of physical education and the enhancement of mental health intervention systems. On one hand, it provides empirical traction for a “psychologically-oriented” pedagogical restructuring. Since physical activity heals primarily through self-efficacy, PE instructors should design “moderately challenging, progressively structured” tasks ensuring that students across all fitness levels can accumulate mastery experiences. Performance-only evaluation metrics should be replaced with self-referenced assessments to protect athletic confidence. On the other hand, it pioneers novel pathways for collaborative mental health interventions. University counseling centers can partner with physical education departments to prescribe integrated “exercise-psychology” regimens. For students exhibiting low self-efficacy, resilience can be awakened through low-barrier, engaging team sports; for marginalized students lacking social networks, implementing an “exercise partner matching system” can facilitate the simultaneous reconstruction of social ties and mind-body health.

Conclusions, recommendations, and future directions

Conclusions

In conclusion, this two-wave longitudinal study elucidates the mechanism through which physical activity enhances mental health among university students. Our findings yield three key, data-driven conclusions. First, baseline physical activity significantly predicts longitudinal improvements in subsequent well-being (β = 0.15) and reductions in emotional problems (β = −0.12). Second, general self-efficacy fully mediates these longitudinal associations, acting as the core cognitive mechanism. Third, peer support significantly moderates the initial pathway; specifically, the psychological benefits of exercise are synergistically amplified when students perceive a moderate-to-high level of peer support (i.e., exceeding the threshold score of 2.87 on a 5-point scale). Practically, these results highlight that fostering a universally inclusive and moderately supportive peer environment in physical activity is a highly cost-effective strategy for maximizing the psychological capital derived from student exercise.

Recommendations

Grounded in these empirical insights, we propose the following structural recommendations to optimize university health promotion paradigms:

Initiate a “Psychologically-Oriented” Pedagogical Paradigm Shift in PE Curricula. PE course design must transcend the singular objectives of physiological fitness or motor skill acquisition by formally embedding the cultivation of self-efficacy and psychological resilience into the core syllabus. Instructors should implement differentiated pedagogical strategies and personalized goal-setting to ensure students across all baseline fitness levels accumulate “mastery experiences”. Furthermore, a strategic emphasis should be placed on team-based and cooperative sports; by deploying “exercise partner matching systems” and peer-assisted learning groups, educators can proactively engineer highly supportive peer interaction contexts.Establish Synergistic “PE-Psychology” Cross-Departmental Interventions. University mental health counseling centers should dismantle departmental silos to forge normalized, collaborative mechanisms with physical education departments. Jointly developing integrated programs—such as “mindfulness-in-motion workshops” or “exercise psychology salons”—can yield compounding benefits. For cohorts identified through psychometric screening as experiencing mild psychological distress, customized “exercise prescriptions” should be prioritized as first-line preventive interventions. For highly stressed populations, such as first-year or graduating students, targeted stress-relief exercise cohorts should be formed alongside time-management counseling.Optimize the Ecological Campus Environment and Health Evaluation Policies. Physically, institutions must augment investments in sports infrastructure, extend facility operating hours, and lower access barriers to stimulate spontaneous exercise engagement. Institutionally, we recommend founding a cross-departmental “Student Physical and Mental Health Promotion Committee” to orchestrate comprehensive campus health blueprints. Policymakers should explore integrating routine physical activity participation and psychological well-being metrics into the holistic student evaluation matrices, providing academic credits or honors as incentives. Hosting regular campus sports festivals and utilizing digital media matrices to disseminate psycho-educational knowledge regarding exercise benefits can foster an inclusive, vibrant campus sports culture.

Limitations and directions for future research

While rigorous in its theoretical architecture and analytical execution, this study acknowledges several methodological constraints that warrant attention in future empirical endeavors:

Temporal Constraints and Causal Inference Depth. Although this study spanned five months, the mediator and outcomes were measured concurrently at T2, which limits definitive causal inference. Future research should employ three-wave longitudinal designs to isolate variables on separate temporal axes. Additionally, incorporating a broader range of covariates is necessary to better rule out unmeasured confounding factors and clarify the dynamic “PA → self-efficacy → mental health” trajectory.Monolithic Measurement Modality and Potential Bias. The core constructs heavily relied on self-report questionnaires. Despite statistical verification precluding severe common method bias, subjective measures of physical activity are inherently susceptible to recall inaccuracies and social desirability effects. Future Directions: Future investigations should strive for multi-modal data fusion. Incorporating objective, device-based measurements (e.g., triaxial accelerometers or wearables) to quantify exertion, utilizing peer-nomination or Social Network Analysis (SNA) to objectively map the centrality and quality of peer networks, and integrating the Experience Sampling Method (ESM) to capture high-frequency, real-time fluctuations in emotions and efficacy within naturalistic settings will drastically enhance measurement validity.Sample Homogeneity and Boundary Complexities. Conducted exclusively within a single Chinese institution (Zhengzhou University), the geographical, cultural, and institutional specificity of the sample may constrain the broad external validity of the findings. Moreover, the translation of exercise into mental health is an exceedingly complex ecosystem; omitted internal variables (e.g., emotion regulation capacity, trait mindfulness) and external macrosystems (e.g., instructor support, family dynamics) likely play critical mediating or moderating roles. Future Directions: Multi-center, cross-regional, and cross-cultural large-cohort studies are urgently needed. Additionally, interdisciplinary research should construct highly complex ecological models encompassing chain mediations or cross-level moderations, ideally supplemented by qualitative inquiry to unearth the profound subjective meaning-making processes students undergo during exercise interventions.
