Characterizing the Patient Experience of Idiopathic Subglottic Stenosis in Pregnancy
Authors: Sarah Benyo, Radhika Duggal, Daniela Schmulevich, Justin Lau, Katherine Miller, Anastasia Christ, Laurence Gascon, Katherine Singh, Rebecca C Nelson, Paul C Bryson, Michael S Benninger, Robert R Lorenz, William S Tierney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo characterize surgical interventions in pregnancy and postpartum periods and compare them to baseline periods (outside of pregnancy/postpartum) for patients with idiopathic subglottic stenosis (iSGS).MethodsA retrospective chart review was performed for patients undergoing endoscopic treatment for iSGS during pregnancy, postpartum, and baseline periods at The Cleveland Clinic between July 1, 2000 and January 1, 2025. Data collected included disease characteristics, surgery characteristics, and pregnancy history. The primary endpoint was the surgical interval between surgical interventions, defined as the number of days until the following surgical intervention during pregnancy, postpartum, and baseline periods.ResultsNine patients were included in the analysis. Among these patients, there were 17 term deliveries, 1 pre‐term delivery, 2 miscarriages, and 2 terminations. There was no statistically significant difference in the surgical interval for patients undergoing surgery during pregnancy when compared to their surgical interval in the one‐year postpartum period (p = 0.69) or when compared to the same patients' baseline surgical interval (p = 0.69). Four patients (45%) experienced more surgical interventions following a pregnancy as compared to before pregnancy.ConclusionSurgical management of iSGS is safe during pregnancy, and although we observed no significant difference in the surgical interval during pregnancy, postpartum, and baseline periods, four patients did require more surgical interventions after pregnancy, highlighting the importance of counseling patients on the potential risk of iSGS recurrence after pregnancy and closely monitoring these patients during the postpartum period.Level of EvidenceIV.Keywords: pregnancy, recurrence, subglottic stenosis, surgery

Introduction

Idiopathic subglottic stenosis (iSGS) is a rare fibroinflammatory disease that causes recurrent narrowing of the upper airway, often necessitating repeated operative interventions to maintain airway patency [1, 2, 3]. While uncommon, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 400,000, iSGS disproportionately affects Caucasian women in their reproductive years (age 30 to 50) [4]. Recent studies have proposed the safety of surgical management of iSGS during pregnancy [5, 6]; while patients with iSGS have been shown to have symptomatic recurrence approximately 12.6 months following surgical management [3], recent studies have suggested an increase in the severity of airway symptoms during pregnancy and an increased risk of iSGS recurrence associated with pregnancy [7, 8].

There remains a paucity of literature on iSGS recurrence necessitating operative intervention during pregnancy and the postpartum periods when comparing the same patients during nonpregnant periods. Therefore, our objective was to characterize the surgical interventions during pregnancy, 1 year postpartum, and outside of these periods for patients with iSGS to gain a better understanding of the patient experience of iSGS in relation to pregnancy.

Methods

IRB approval at The Cleveland Clinic was obtained, and eligible patients were identified via electronic medical record systems at The Cleveland Clinic in Cleveland, Ohio. Patients 18 years of age and older with a diagnosis of iSGS identified from July 1, 2000 to January 1, 2025, seen at Cleveland Clinic associated sites in Ohio were included in the initial chart review. Patients were determined to have iSGS on the basis of a negative workup for autoimmune etiologies of SGS and the absence of an iatrogenic etiology including intubation and previous airway surgery (i.e., tracheostomy). All patients had a history of airway evaluation and endoscopic surgical intervention for iSGS including balloon dilation, corticosteroid injection, and/or scar lysis with laser or cold steel. We then identified iSGS patients with obstetric and pregnancy information available in the electronic medical record including the type of pregnancy (term, premature, miscarriage, termination), date of delivery, gestational age at the time of delivery, method of delivery, and any adverse events for the fetus or mother during delivery. Patients were included if they were identified to have endoscopic airway intervention for iSGS during pregnancy and/or within 1 year postpartum in addition to iSGS surgery outside of the pregnancy and postpartum periods.

The demographic data collected on chart review included age, race, and ethnicity. We documented confirmation of idiopathic etiology of SGS and relevant laboratory results (p‐ or c‐antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody, anti‐nuclear antibody, rheumatoid factor). We also documented the use of serial intralesional steroid injections (SILSI).

Descriptive statistics were generated to describe characteristics of the overall cohort of patients, including baseline demographics, disease characteristics, and iSGS‐related therapies received. Each surgery was characterized as occurring during pregnancy, during the postpartum period (defined as 1 year from the date of delivery), or in neither of these periods (baseline period). Surgical characteristics, including surgery type, ventilation type, patient position, and intraoperative steroid use, are described across each of these three groups for qualitative comparison. The surgical interval for each surgery was defined as the number of days until the following surgical intervention. The baseline surgical interval was defined as the time between two surgeries outside of the pregnancy and postpartum periods. The pregnancy surgical interval was defined as the time between a surgical intervention during pregnancy and the subsequent intervention. The postpartum surgical interval was defined as the time between a surgical intervention during the postpartum period and the subsequent intervention. The interval following the most recent surgery was excluded for all patients due to a lack of consistent follow‐up for patients who did not require an additional intervention. Given the small sample size and non‐normal distribution of data, exact Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were utilized to compare the surgical intervals achieved during pregnancy compared to the postpartum period, during pregnancy compared to the patient's baseline (surgical intervals achieved outside of pregnancy and the postpartum period), and during the postpartum period compared to baseline paired across each patient. All statistical tests were conducted using R version 4.3.2 and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Nine patients who underwent surgical intervention for iSGS during either pregnancy or postpartum were identified, and their clinical course was compared to their baseline outside of pregnancy. The average age at the time of surgery during pregnancy and postpartum was 29 and 32, respectively. Among the nine patients, there were 17 term deliveries (defined as after 37 weeks of gestation), 1 pre‐term spontaneous delivery, 2 miscarriages, and 2 terminations. No maternal or fetal complications for any of the pre‐term or term deliveries were reported.

Seven patients underwent surgical intervention during pregnancy with a median of 1 surgery during this period (IQR: 1, 2). The average gestational age at the time of surgery was 21 weeks. Two surgeries were performed during the first trimester, five during the second trimester, and five during the third trimester. One hundred percent of these pregnancies were term. Fifty‐five percent were vaginal deliveries, 22% required cesarean section, and 22% were unknown methods of delivery due to unavailability of records for some patients who delivered outside of our healthcare system. There were seven patients who required surgical intervention in the postpartum period, five of whom also required surgery in pregnancy, with a median of 1 surgery postpartum (IQR: 1, 2). For all patients, there was a total of 35 iSGS surgeries recorded outside of the pregnancy and postpartum periods. Table 1 demonstrates the surgical characteristics by the period of intervention. In all three of the time intervals, balloon dilation with surgical steel lysis was most commonly used. Intermittent apnea with intubation was used most commonly during pregnancy (46%), versus 27% and 26% in the postpartum and baseline periods, respectively. Other ventilation methods in pregnancy included jet ventilation (31%), transnasal humidified rapid insufflation ventilatory exchange (THRIVE; 8%), and combined modalities (8%). Left lateral decubitus positioning was used in 23% of pregnant patients, and only supine positioning was recorded in postpartum and baseline periods (Table 1). There were no maternal or fetal adverse events for surgeries performed during pregnancy such as post‐operative ventilation support, intubation, or admission to a neonatal or surgical intensive care unit. The anesthesia records were available for six of the seven patients who had surgery during pregnancy, which demonstrated oxygen saturations maintained above 90% for the entirety of each case.

Regarding adjuvant treatments, one patient received SILSI during pregnancy. For this patient, the dose range per injection of triamcinolone was 30–60 mg, and 7.5–30 mg for dexamethasone. Four patients had SILSI during baseline intervals. The dose range for triamcinolone for these patients was 30–80 mg. Of note, no patients experienced adverse events related to SILSI.

Discussion

This study aims to highlight how surgical intervals for iSGS are affected by pregnancy, specifically exploring the changes in disease progression during times of large hormonal shifts (pregnancy and postpartum periods) in individual patients. Of the nine patients who underwent surgical management of their stenosis during pregnancy, 100% had term deliveries, and 55% were vaginal deliveries. We observed no significant difference between the surgical intervals during pregnancy, postpartum, or in baseline periods, though we noticed that nearly 50% required more surgical interventions following a pregnancy. Although not significant in the present study, this may support a recent study comparing patients who had at least 1 term pregnancy before their first iSGS procedure to patients without any term pregnancy before the first procedure, which demonstrated that parity increases the risk of iSGS recurrence [8]. They reported that the surgical interval did not change regardless of pregnancy status, which is similar to our findings. Our study adds to this existing literature by examining all patients with documented pregnancy regardless of the timing of the first surgical intervention for iSGS, with the goal of capturing the disease process for individual patients across the pre‐ and post‐pregnancy time intervals.

Although there was not a statistically significant difference in surgical interval, we did observe that four patients required more surgical interventions following pregnancy (Figure 1). McCrary et al. conducted a survey of 413 patients' experiences with iSGS in pregnancy and found that there was an increased proportion of patients who reported requiring operative interventions in the postpartum period [7]. Additionally, prior studies have examined the presence and expression of estrogen and progesterone receptors in iSGS and found overexpression of estrogen‐alpha and progesterone receptors in stenotic tissue [9]. The role of estrogen in wound healing is well documented, with increased cellular growth and division associated with increased estrogen, which in turn promotes wound healing; therefore, increased exposure to estrogen during pregnancy may influence inflammation and granulation tissue formation [10, 11]. Nanda et al. evaluated 533 women in the North American Airway Collaborative data of adults with iSGS and found that women with active estrogen exposure had faster and more frequent iSGS recurrence [10]. Therefore, the heightened hormone exposure during pregnancy may potentially contribute to disease recurrence necessitating operative intervention over a period of time following pregnancy. Nevertheless, our data highlight that the natural history and recurrence are quite variable for patients with iSGS, and pregnancy may contribute to subsequent accelerated disease in some patients. Therefore, it may be warranted for providers who treat this disease to monitor these patients closely in the postpartum interval and educate patients on the possibility of disease progression following this period.

In the present study, the most common surgical intervention during pregnancy was balloon dilation with either surgical steel (46%) or laser lysis (46%). Previous studies have evaluated the efficacy of various treatment modalities in nonpregnant patients, and while endoscopic dilation is the most widely and frequently utilized intervention, it has reported higher rates of recurrent stenosis as compared to other treatment modalities [1, 4, 12]. However, there is no definitive consensus on the single best treatment of iSGS, and decision‐making must balance survival, symptoms, and quality of life considerations [13]. Our results are consistent with reported practice patterns among North American academic tertiary care centers with airway expertise, with balloon dilation with laser lysis reported as the most common intervention for iSGS in pregnancy [14]. A notable difference was the choice of ventilation mode during surgery, with intermittent apnea with intubation being the most common choice in pregnancy versus jet ventilation in the postpartum and baseline periods (Table 1). The physiological changes during pregnancy, such as the gravid uterus and weight gain, may contribute to a restrictive lung pattern and reduction in the forced vital capacity and compliance. Despite these changes, jet ventilation and THRIVE have been shown to be safe and effective choices for ventilating pregnant patients [15]. Ultimately, the goals of ventilation and anesthetic options should carefully consider the risks of surgery to both mother and fetus. Although the operative time for endoscopic intervention for iSGS is relatively short, likely decreasing risks of surgery and anesthesia as compared to other types of surgery during pregnancy, there is still a considerable risk of hypoxia and related injuries [16]. To reduce these risks as much as possible, it is warranted for the surgical team to always be prepared to escalate airway management with close collaboration with anesthesia.

Surgery was performed during all three trimesters. Two surgeries were performed in the first trimester, at 9 weeks and 10 weeks of gestation. Surgical intervention was pursued because the risk of airway compromise was considered to be too great to delay surgery. Both of these pregnancies were term, vaginal deliveries. A meta‐analysis of the maternal and fetal outcomes in pregnant patients undergoing cardiac surgery found that fetal mortality was lowest in the second trimester, but this was not statistically significant when compared to the first and third trimester [17]. Our findings are consistent with a recent scoping review by Miller et al. regarding the surgical management of SGS in pregnancy, which found that the majority of operations were performed in the second and third trimesters and all went on to deliver term infants without clear maternal or fetal complications [6]. The ideal timing for surgery, especially in the first trimester, should be determined by a multidisciplinary team by considering the severity of the stenosis and the risks that stenosis places on potential maternal hypoxia and, therefore, fetal development, and the risks of anesthesia to maternal health.

In our small patient cohort, there was one patient treated with SILSI during two separate pregnancies. The patient had one term and one pre‐term delivery due to spontaneous rupture of membranes at 35 weeks of gestation, without any maternal or fetal adverse effects during the pregnancies or at the time of birth. A study of the systemic safety of SILSI for iSGS showed acute hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis suppression with normalization by day 7, suggesting that there are no cumulative systemic steroid side effects [18]; however, there are no studies specifically examining the safety of SILSI for iSGS in pregnancy. It is also currently unclear if pregnant women benefit from SILSI more than non‐pregnant persons, although this technique has the potential to decrease anesthetic use during pregnancy. A number of retrospective reviews have shown varying efficacy of SILSI, with many favoring longer time intervals between surgical interventions [19, 20, 21, 22]; however, a large prospective trial by the North American Airway Collaborative comparing patients who received SILSI to those who did not found no difference in the time to recurrence [23]. Future studies are required to further elucidate the safety and efficacy of SILSI in pregnant women. Ultimately, it is critical to discuss potential steroid side effects with the patient and to consider a multidisciplinary approach when weighing the risks and benefits of the timing of individualized treatment plans for iSGS in pregnancy.

Limitations

While this study gave insight into the patient experience with iSGS in relation to pregnancy, there were limitations to consider. First, the sample size of patients with surgical interventions during pregnancy and/or within one year postpartum as well as during a nonpregnant period was limited. The number of patients was further limited by the availability of pregnancy information in the electronic medical record. To be included, patients had to establish with us prior to a pregnancy and then be followed through the postpartum period yielding a small sample with very high‐fidelity data on included patients. Due to this small sample size, our study is underpowered to detect a difference in the surgical intervals between baseline, pregnant, and postpartum periods. We do feel that the trends observed are valuable both to clinicians and researchers. Future studies with larger sample sizes could provide valuable insight into this topic. Additionally, the surgical interval may be influenced by specific patient preferences or subjective provider clinical decision‐making, which may be influenced by factors external to the disease process. This, in turn, may influence the reported surgical intervals in our study. Lastly, given the retrospective nature of this study, we could not control for potential confounders such as birth control usage or adjuvant medical therapies such as inhaled steroids, PPIs, and antibiotics, which may also influence disease severity and the need for surgical intervention.

Conclusions

The present study evaluates the patient experience with iSGS in relation to pregnancy, specifically examining the surgical interval for the same patients across pregnant, postpartum, and baseline periods. We observed no significant difference in the surgical interval during these three time periods. Four patients did require more surgical interventions after a pregnancy. Providers who care for pregnant persons with iSGS should feel confident in the decision to proceed with surgical management in the appropriate patients and are urged to counsel patients on the potential risk of iSGS recurrence after pregnancy and closely monitor these patients during the postpartum period.
