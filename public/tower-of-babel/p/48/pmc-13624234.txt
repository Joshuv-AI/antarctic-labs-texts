How the Reclassification of Gabapentinoids Reshaped Neuropathic Pain Treatment in Saudi Arabia: A Survey of Healthcare Professionals
Authors: Abdullah Kaki, Yousef Hamdy, Ashraf Amir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study aimed to assess knowledge gaps, perceptions, and prescribing approaches of healthcare professionals (HCPs) in Saudi Arabia regarding pain management, particularly neuropathic pain, in the context of recent reclassification of gabapentinoids.MethodsA three-lecture webinar was attended by 4922 HCPs across Saudi Arabia. Two structured surveys were embedded within the lectures: a 9-item knowledge questionnaire on pain management and a 10-item questionnaire on HCPs’ opinions and attitudes on gabapentinoid use and prescribing challenges. Responses were recorded on the platform’s interactive dashboard as percentage. The knowledge scores were normalized to percentage.ResultsOverall knowledge levels varied across specialties with internal medicine (68.7%) and pain specialists (68.3%) having the top scores; non-pain organ specialists scored the lowest (59.1%). The majority of HCPs (68.8%) agreed that prescribing gabapentinoids has become more challenging than prescribing tramadol post reclassification, with patients’ concerns related to substance dependence (32.6%) and extended process in obtaining medication (27.9%) cited by the majority. Gabapentinoids remained the preferred treatment for diabetic peripheral neuropathy by most HCPs (36.8%). Most HCPs (47.4%) reported initiating gabapentinoids at the lowest possible dose and increasing as needed, while a smaller proportion preferred initiating an appropriate dose with minimal adjustments (18.5%). Among pain specialists, these approaches were nearly evenly divided (37.7% and 31.9%, respectively). The majority (45.7%) selected pregabalin 300 mg as their preferred daily dose for transitioning from gabapentin 900 mg.ConclusionsThis nationwide survey highlights substantial variability and knowledge gaps related to general pain, neuropathic pain management, and excessive caution among HCPs regarding prescribing gabapentinoids and patient concerns of developing dependence. To maintain gabapentinoid access and timely care for patients with legitimate needs, there is a need to implement educational programs for HCPs across specialties and develop a unified treatment approach that fulfils the guidelines and addresses exaggerated patient worries along with patient-focused initiatives to reduce hesitancy post reclassification of gabapentinoids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40122-026-00874-3.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain, as defined by the International Association for the Study of Pain, refers to the pain resulting from a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system [1]. Often described as a burning, pricking, shooting, squeezing, or freezing sensation, this condition is difficult to treat, significantly impacts patients’ quality of life, and poses considerable burden on healthcare systems [2]. The global prevalence of neuropathic pain in the general population is estimated to be between 7% and 10%, which corresponds to approximately 15–25% of all chronic pain cases [3, 4]. In people with diabetes the prevalence of neuropathic pain has been estimated to be higher at around 20–30% [5]. Recent studies from Saudi Arabia have reported the prevalence of painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) ranging from 11.2% to 44.1% [6–10].

Unlike nociceptive pain, which arises from actual or threatened damage to non-neural tissue, neuropathic pain is characterized by complex pathophysiological mechanisms involving both peripheral and central nervous system alterations, including neuronal hyperexcitability, altered synaptic transmission, and central sensitization [2, 11]. These mechanisms result in persistent pain signaling even in the absence of ongoing tissue injury. In conditions such as DPN, persistent metabolic and inflammatory factors contribute to nerve damage and sustained sensitization, making effective long-term management particularly challenging [2, 11]. Current recommended treatment options for neuropathic pain are categorized into first-line therapy (tricyclic antidepressants, gabapentin, pregabalin, and serotonin noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors), second-line therapy (tramadol, lidocaine, and capsaicin), and third-line therapy (strong opioids such as morphine and oxycodone and botulinum toxin-A) [12, 13].

Gabapentinoids (gabapentin and pregabalin) have an established role in the pharmacological management of neuropathic pain by modulating voltage-gated calcium channels through binding to the α2δ subunit, thereby reducing excitatory neurotransmitter release and neuronal hyperexcitability [13, 14]. Their efficacy across several neuropathic pain conditions, including DPN, post-herpetic neuralgia, and peripheral nerve injury, has led to their recommendation as first-line treatment options in multiple international guidelines [12, 15–17]. However, concerns regarding misuse, abuse potential, and concomitant use with opioids have prompted regulatory restrictions in countries including Saudi Arabia. In 2017, the Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) reclassified gabapentin and pregabalin as controlled medications [18–21]. Under the revised regulations, prescribing and dispensing of gabapentinoids became subject to additional monitoring and documentation requirements, including the use of controlled prescription forms and enhanced prescription oversight. To strengthen regulatory surveillance, the SFDA also implemented the Raqeeb electronic system, a centralized platform designed to monitor the prescribing and dispensing of controlled medications and facilitate tracking of prescription patterns across healthcare institutions [19, 22]. These measures were introduced to track the use of gabapentinoids. An interrupted time series analysis from Saudi sales data showed a marked reduction in pregabalin use after the restriction [18]. Globally, the UK reclassified gabapentinoids as controlled drugs (Schedule 3/Class C) in 2019 and other countries have also issued secure-prescription requirements [23–27].

These regulatory shifts raise potential implications for patient access, prescriber decision-making, and treatment heterogeneity across specialties and practice settings. Building upon the authors’ earlier expert opinion article that explored the challenges associated with gabapentinoid access under the new prescription regulations, the present study aims to capture healthcare professionals’ (HCPs’) opinions through webinar-embedded questionnaires to evaluate their knowledge, attitudes, and prescribing practices related to gabapentinoids following their reclassification in Saudi Arabia [28]. By gathering real-world insights, the study seeks to identify areas of uncertainty, potential practice gaps, and variation in clinical decision-making. It also explores trends associated with clinician seniority, specialty, and institutional affiliation, and geographic region within Saudi Arabia.

Methods

A three-lecture webinar titled “Pain Experts’ Assembly” was organized by Viatris on 15 November 2024 for healthcare practitioners in Saudi Arabia. The webinar was designed to identify gaps in pain management with a particular focus on neuropathic pain, and the implications of recent regulatory restrictions on gabapentinoid use in Saudi Arabia. Two structured interactive surveys preceded both the first and second lectures. A 9-question knowledge questionnaire addressed pain classification, general pain management, neuropathic pain treatment, and medication knowledge. The knowledge scores were normalized to percentage values for ease of comparison across groups. A second 10-question questionnaire focused on HCPs’ insights into patients’ perspectives on gabapentinoid use, as well as HCPs’ practices and strategies, prescribing challenges, and treatment preferences. The responses to this questionnaire were recorded and presented as percentage. The first set of questions for the questionnaire assessing HCPs’ knowledge was developed by Prof Abdullah Kaki and the second set of questions on HCPs’ insights was created by Viatris Medical Affairs.

The webinar was advertised nationally through electronic mailings and professional communication channels targeting HCPs across Saudi Arabia. Participation was voluntary, and no predefined sampling strategy or eligibility criteria were applied beyond webinar attendance. The webinar was hosted on the Zoom platform and was attended by 4922 HCPs from across Saudi Arabia. Demographic and professional characteristics were collected during the registration process and were linked to survey responses. The variables captured included gender, professional seniority, primary area of specialization, institutional affiliation, and geographic region within Saudi Arabia. Survey responses were recorded automatically within Zoom platform, stored in de-identified form, and exported from the platform’s interactive dashboard for analysis. The data is presented as percentages.

The present study is based on the results of a voluntary, anonymous survey. The purpose of the study was to gather insights through the opinion of the participants and no efficacy, safety, or clinical parameters were investigated. The study did not fall under the scope of biomedical research requiring ethics committee approval according to the Saudi National Committee of BioEthics Law of Ethics of Research on Living Creatures [29]. The chairperson moderating the webinar presented and explained the consent-related information and informed participants that their responses would be used for research purposes if they chose to proceed. While explicit mention of publication was not separately stated, participation was voluntary and based on implied consent, with the understanding that the data would contribute to the broad research initiative.

The overall average knowledge score was 65.2. Gender-wise score distribution showed that female HCPs across specialties (with the exception of internal medicine) had overall higher knowledge scores than their male counterparts (Fig. 1a). However, despite this overall trend, female HCPs scored lower on items assessing neuropathic pain management compared to male HCPs (49.0 vs. 54.2). The junior HCPs from the Northern region had the highest average score (78.3), followed by female HCPs from the Southern region (73.3) and HCPs affiliated to government institutions from the Eastern region (72.2) (Fig. 1c, d; Supplementary Fig. 1c). The lowest average knowledge score was noted in senior HCPs from the Northern region (46.7) and HCPs affiliated with private institutions (56.7) from the Northern region (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Fig. 1c). Scores based solely on regional distribution were comparable, except for the Southern region, where HCPs had the lowest recorded score of 60.9 (Fig. 1a). Interestingly, female doctors from the Southern region had a higher score of 73.3 compared to male doctors and junior doctors in the same region who scored 58.2 and 56.7, respectively (Fig. 1c, d).

The top two scorers based on specialty were HCPs specializing in internal medicine (non-surgical) and neuropsychiatry & pain specialists (68.7 and 68.3, respectively), whereas the lowest scores were noted in non-pain organ specialists (59.1) (Fig. 1a). Among the internal medicine (non-surgical) specialists, the male HCPs, those affiliated with government institutions, and mid-career HCPs scored the highest (70.9, 70.7, and 69.7, respectively) (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Among neuropsychiatry & pain specialists, comprising HCPs specializing in anesthesiology, psychiatry, and neurology who are commonly involved in prescribing gabapentinoids, the scores were higher for female HCPs (74.4), senior HCPs (73.3), and those affiliated with government institutions (69.3) (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Similarly, among the surgical and emergency care specialists, female HCPs had a higher score compared to male HCPs (70.7 vs 63.9, respectively) (Fig. 1b).

No remarkable differences were observed in responses based on seniority level of HCPs except that a slightly lower percentage of senior HCPs (51.8%) agreed to have encountered patients refusing to take gabapentinoids compared with junior (54.5%) and mid-level HCPs (54.7%) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Over 50% of HCPs across all regions agreed to have encountered patients refusing to continue gabapentinoid treatment, with a comparatively higher proportion of HCPs from the Western (57.7%) and Northern region (56.8%%) expressing this concern (Supplementary Fig. 2c). In comparison to HCPs affiliated with government institutions, a relatively higher percentage of those affiliated with private institutions expressed agreement (50% vs. 59.3%, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

Interestingly, a higher percentage of HCPs directly involved in pain management (53.7%), i.e., those specializing in neuropsychiatric & pain care and surgical & emergency care, disagreed to have noted patient refusal to take gabapentinoids. On the contrary, a higher proportion of HCPs specializing in internal medicine (non-surgical) (69%) and other non-pain organ specialists (68.3%) agreed to have encountered patient refusal to continue gabapentinoids compared to HCPs from other specialties (Fig. 2b).

Patient Concerns Post Reclassification

The HCPs were asked about what they perceived to be the most pressing concerns among patients with neuropathic pain following the reclassification of gabapentinoids (Fig. 2c, d; Supplementary Fig. 3a–d). According to HCP responses, the leading concerns were the risk of developing drug dependence (32.6%) and the extended process required to obtain these medications post reclassification (27.9%) (Fig. 2c).

The concern regarding drug dependence was particularly high among patients of junior HCPs (43.2%) and across all specialties except internal medicine specialists. The internal medicine specialists (40.4%) and senior HCPs (32.1%) expressed that the extended process for obtaining these medications was the foremost concern of their patients (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Fig. 3b). In contrast, the stigma associated with others knowing about their use of gabapentinoids was reported as the least concerning factor (7.8%) (Fig. 2c). These response patterns were consistent across gender, specialty, region, and institutional affiliation (Supplementary Fig. 3a–d).

Key Challenges Post Reclassification

The HCPs were asked to identify the most formidable challenge they face following the reclassification of gabapentinoids. The largest proportion of respondents (32.3%) indicated that the most significant challenge is the need to consider alternative treatments and discontinue gabapentinoids (Fig. 3b). Other notable concerns included addressing patient hesitancy in taking gabapentinoids (27.9%), difficulties in prescribing these medications (20.2%), and fear of potential repercussions associated with prescribing them (19.7%) (Fig. 3b).

Senior HCPs (29.2%), internal medicine specialists (31.3%), those affiliated with government institutions (30.7%), and those from the Western region (29%) chose patient hesitancy to take gabapentinoids as their primary concern (Fig. 3c, d; Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). Across seniority levels, a higher proportion of junior HCPs expressed concern about potential repercussions (24.2%) compared to mid-career (18.3%) and senior HCPs (17.6%) (Fig. 3c).

HCP Practices and Strategies

Dealing with Psychological Attachment to Therapeutic Medications

To assess HCPs’ attitudes toward psychological dependence on gabapentinoids, in the absence of any physical dependence or abuse, and whether this differs from their response to confirmed abuse or is managed with similar intensity, the respondents were asked on how they would address a patient’s psychological attachment to gabapentin or pregabalin. The majority of HCPs (65.9%) expressed the view that they would gradually stop the medication and transition to an alternative treatment (Fig. 4a). This opinion was more pronounced among the surgical & emergency care specialists (73.2%) and HCPs from Southern region (72%) (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 6c). While a relatively lower percentage of HCPs from the Western region (60%) expressed this opinion (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Responding to Patient Anxiety

To understand how clinicians manage patient anxiety related to regulatory changes, respondents were asked what steps they would take when a patient with well‑managed neuropathic pain expressed worry about continuing gabapentinoids after their reclassification. The majority of HCPs (64.5%) indicated that they would reassure the patient, emphasizing that the medication remains safe when taken as prescribed (Fig. 4c). Among HCPs across specialties, relatively higher proportions of surgical & emergency care and primary & rehabilitative care specialists (71.6% and 70.6%, respectively) chose this option for addressing patients’ concerns (Fig. 4d). Referring the patient to a psychiatrist was the least selected response (7.8%) (Fig. 4c).

A notable percentage of responders among the internal medicine specialists (20%), non-pain organ specialists (18.4%), and neuropsychiatric & pain specialists (16.9%) stated that they would choose to stop gabapentinoid treatment and switch to another treatment to alleviate the patients’ concern (Fig. 4c). Apart from the above mentioned trends, the responses were mostly consistent across gender, seniority level, region, and institutional affiliation (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d).

Initiating Gabapentinoid Therapy

Another survey question examined HCP’s preferred treatment strategies when initiating gabapentinoid therapy in patients requiring neuropathic pain management. The majority of the HCPs (47.4%) reported that they typically start with the lowest possible dose and increase it later as needed or by titrating the dose to find the most suitable dosage (28%) (Fig. 4e). A small proportion (6.1%) indicated that they have ceased prescribing gabapentinoids altogether due to the imposed restrictions (Fig. 4e).

Among different specialties, neuropsychiatric and pain care specialists were almost equally divided between starting at the lowest dose and titrating upward (37.7%) and initiating treatment at the clinically appropriate dose based on experience, with minimal subsequent adjustments (31.9%) (Fig. 4f). The responses were otherwise consistent irrespective of the gender, seniority level, region, and institutional affiliation (Supplementary Fig. 8a–d).

Treatment Preferences, Dose Transition, and Safety Perceptions

Preferred Alternatives for DPN Management

The majority of HCPs (36.8%) stated that gabapentinoids remained their preferred treatment option for DPN (Fig. 5a). Other choices included referral to pain specialists (17.6%), amitriptyline (15.3%), duloxetine (13.2%), and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (12.7%). Tramadol was the least selected option (4.4%). Referral to pain specialists option was selected by a higher percentage of surgical & emergency care (24.3%), non-pain organ specialists (20.9%), and HCPs from the Central region (19.8%) (Fig. 5b; Supplementary Fig. 9c). Only 28.5% preferred neuropathic pain medications other than gabapentinoids (amitriptyline and duloxetine), while 17.1% chose medications other than the first-line recommended drugs (NSAIDs and tramadol), for instance, 12.7% overall, 18.6% of junior doctors, and 16.5% of surgical & emergency care specialists chose NSAIDs (Fig. 5a, b; Supplementary Fig. 9b). Dose Transitioning Practices

To understand clinician approaches to dose conversion, respondents were asked to select the pregabalin dose they would select as an appropriate equivalent for a patient transitioning from a daily dose of gabapentin 900 mg. The most preferred choice was pregabalin 300 mg (45.7%), followed by 150 mg (26.3%), 75 mg (14.9%), 450 mg (8.2%), and 600 mg (4.8%) (Fig. 5c). More than half of senior HCPs (51%) selected pregabalin 300 mg as their preferred alternative dose (Fig. 5d). On the contrary, 19.3% of the junior HCPs, 19.4% of female HCPs, and 18% of surgical & emergency care specialists chose pregabalin 75 mg as the alternative dose when transitioning from gabapentin 900 mg (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). However, 34.7% of neuro-psychiatric pain specialists, 34.2% of HCPs from the Southern region, and 26.3% of overall responders chose the alternative dose of pregabalin 150 mg (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Fig. 10b, c).

Risk of Abuse

The HCPs were asked to identify the most likely reason pregabalin might pose a risk of abuse. The majority of the overall HCPs (35.1%) and internal medicine specialists (42.1%) attributed the abuse potential of pregabalin to its effectiveness and tolerability in treating neuropathic pain (Fig. 5e, f). Furthermore, 34.5% of neuro-psychiatric & pain specialists, 33% HCPs from the Southern region, 30.6% of senior HCPs, and 30.4% of female HCPs agreed that the reason for risk of abuse was mainly due to sedation and improved sleep (Fig. 5f; Supplementary Fig. 11a–c). Whereas 28.2% of junior HCPs and 26.6% of primary & rehabilitative care specialists believed that the risk of abuse was because it causes euphoria (Fig. 5f; Supplementary Fig. 11b). Overall, 13.1% HCPs and 18% of those from the Southern region stated that abuse risk could be because it is used with opioids to enhance their effect (Fig. 5e; Supplementary Fig. 11c).

Discussion

The study aimed to identify areas of uncertainty, potential practice gaps, and variations in clinical decision-making related to neuropathic pain management and gabapentinoid prescribing following the reclassification of gabapentinoids in Saudi Arabia. In the following section, we interpret these findings through individualized profiles across specialties, gender, age groups, institutional affiliations, and geographic location.

Insights from HCP Knowledge Scores

A relatively low overall average score on knowledge related to pain management was noted, which may reflect gaps in knowledge indicating the need for improved educational programs and introduction of formal curriculum on pain medicine [30, 31]. Integration of diagnosis verification tools such as the DN4 questionnaire with Raqeeb is expected to support clinical decision-making [28]. Gender-wise comparison showed that female HCPs performed better compared to the male HCPs with relatively higher overall scores across specialties (except internal medicine); however, they scored lower on questions specific to neuropathic pain. The findings from another study on Saudi nursing students also demonstrated female nursing students had a better level of knowledge and attitude towards pain management compared to male student [32]. Region-wise distribution of knowledge score showed that the HCPs from the Northern region scored lowest, particularly senior HCPs from the region, highlighting the need for continuous medical education programs as also proposed by other similar studies [33].

Specialty-Based HCP Perspectives

Internal Medicine Specialists (Non-surgical)

Internal medicine specialists had a higher knowledge score compared to other specialties, with male specialists and those affiliated with government institutions scoring relatively higher compared to others. This may be attributable to internal medicine specialists’ routine management of chronic diseases associated with neuropathic pain, particularly diabetes and its complications, which likely provides greater exposure to neuropathic pain assessment and treatment. In their opinion, patients’ foremost concern was the extended process required to obtain these medications, which could indicate the need for a more simplified process to alleviate the burden on neuropathic pain patients [28]. Around one in five internal medicine specialists indicated they would switch to an alternative treatment for patients who were anxious to continue treatment with gabapentinoids. This suggests that there is a need to equip this specialty with tools and communication strategies to manage patient worries while maintaining evidence-based care. When asked about pregabalin’s abuse potential, the majority attributed it to its perceived effectiveness and tolerability in neuropathic pain management. However, gabapentinoids remained their preferred choice for the management of pain associated with DPN followed by duloxetine. Although most HCPs across specialties perceived gabapentinoids prescribing to be more challenging than tramadol, agreement with this view was relatively lower among internal medicine specialists. This difference may reflect their greater familiarity with gabapentinoid use, and comparatively less experience with tramadol, in the context of chronic disease management. Internal medicine specialists have been shown to be the leading prescribers of gabapentinoids relative to neurologists and surgeons [34]. While initiating gabapentinoid treatment, most of the internal medicine specialists agreed that they would start with the lowest possible dose and increase it later, as needed. However, while transitioning from a daily dose of gabapentin 900 mg, approximately 18% indicated that they would select pregabalin 450 mg or 600 mg daily, which exceeds the usually recommended dose when transitioning from 900 mg of gabapentin in clinical practice [35].

Neuropsychiatrists & Pain Specialists

Neuropsychiatrists & pain specialists reported that they do not often encounter patients refusing gabapentinoids after the reclassification. This may reflect greater severity of pain among patients consulting pain specialists, making them more willing to continue treatment to achieve adequate relief. For DPN management, most pain specialists continued to select gabapentinoids as their primary option, with amitriptyline as the preferred alternative when needed, consistent with established practice guidelines for neuropathic pain [12, 15–17, 36–38]. Their prescribing approaches were divided between starting at the lowest dose with titration and initiating treatment at the clinically appropriate dose informed by experience, both practices that have been shown to be associated with improved treatment adherence [39]. Furthermore, pain specialists were evenly divided in their views regarding the abuse or misuse potential of pregabalin, attributing this risk both to its efficacy and tolerability in neuropathic pain management and to its sedative effects and associated improvements in sleep, which have also been shown by other studies [40]. These findings point to the importance of balanced clinical judgement for assessment of abuse risk in routine practice [28, 41]. Given that many patients referred to pain clinics have already tried lower doses or other strategies without sufficient improvement, pain specialists often feel confident determining an effective dosing regimen with greater precision. Overall, neuropsychiatrists and pain specialists were among the top two highest-scoring groups in pain management knowledge, closely following internal medicine specialists, and this was also reflected in their responses to the survey questions.

Non-pain Organ Specialists

According to non-pain organ specialists the main patient concern following gabapentinoid reclassification was development of substance dependence, and the majority reported having come across patients who were hesitant to continue gabapentinoids. Non-pain organ specialists identified the need to choose alternative treatments as their primary challenge post gabapentinoid reclassification. This may indicate that, given their lack of specialization in pain management, non-pain organ specialists may feel less equipped to select suitable alternatives. In comparison to other specialties, a notably higher percentage of non-pain organ specialists agreed that prescribing gabapentinoids has become more challenging than prescribing tramadol. For the management of DPN-associated pain, non-pain organ specialists demonstrated a notably high preference for referral to a pain specialists. Further, they had the lowest preference for amitriptyline relative to other specialties, which could reflect their greater awareness of its well-recognized adverse effects such as tachycardia and hypertension, which is closely related to their specialization [42, 43]. Non-pain organ specialists, particularly male HCPs and those affiliated with private institutions, had the lowest knowledge score, suggesting that they may have limited exposure to neuropathic pain management principles, as their clinical focus is directed primarily toward the conditions within their own specialty. This narrower focus may not be enough to counsel patients effectively about most appropriate pain‑specific treatment options.

Primary and Rehabilitative Care Specialists

When asked about the key challenge they faced post reclassification of gabapentinoids, the primary and rehabilitative care specialists were almost equally divided between patient reluctance to continue gabapentinoids, having to prescribe alternative therapy, and the fear of potential repercussions arising from prescribing gabapentinoids. In addition to recognizing the effectiveness of gabapentinoids for neuropathic pain as a key factor contributing to pregabalin’s abuse potential, a notable proportion of primary and rehabilitative care specialists also identified its euphoric and sedative effects as probable contributors. However, in response to patient concerns about continuing gabapentinoids even when clinically effective, only a small proportion indicated that they would refer the patient to a psychiatrist, suggesting a willingness to manage their patients’ worries and concerns themselves.

Surgical and Emergency Care Specialists

Similar to pain specialists, the majority of the surgical and emergency care specialists mentioned that they do not frequently come across patients who are hesitant to continue gabapentinoids. This may reflect the pain severity among patients in emergency and acute care settings, where achieving adequate relief is often the primary priority. Although most surgical care specialists still relied on gabapentinoids for the management of DPN-related pain, they chose referral to neurologists as the second option rather than deciding the most suitable treatment approach themselves, which could put additional burden on patients. Notably, compared with other specialties, the lowest proportion of surgical care specialists selected duloxetine as a preferred treatment option for DPN. Most surgical and emergency care specialists reported reassuring their patients to address their concerns about continuing gabapentinoids following reclassification; however, a substantial proportion indicated that they would discontinue gabapentinoids and transition to alternative therapies if they perceived a risk of developing psychological attachment. This practice is concerning, as a patient’s positive response or “liking” of a medication does not equate to abuse and should not be dealt with in the same drastic manner. It is important to note that this is a critical decision and should be guided by the HCP’s assessment of their patient’s behavior, including the risk of actual drug abuse [41]. Otherwise, patients with neuropathic pain may be unjustifiably deprived of an effective treatment on which they are already well controlled. These findings highlight a key area for improvement, i.e., strengthening HCPs’ ability in distinguishing therapeutic benefit, psychological dependence, physical dependence, and abuse in their clinical decisions. It is likely that some HCPs may have interpreted psychological attachment as synonymous with abuse, reflecting limited awareness of distinctions between psychological attachment without physiological features and abuse. Nearly 1 in 5 surgical and emergency care specialists selected pregabalin 75 mg as the appropriate daily dose when transitioning a patient from gabapentin 900 mg, a dose generally considered subtherapeutic. This choice may reflect heightened caution or emerging fear toward this drug class following reclassification which aligns with their view that prescribing gabapentinoids has become more challenging than prescribing tramadol. Their perspective in this regard is particularly insightful, given their experience in routinely prescribing both opioids and gabapentinoids for postoperative pain management [44, 45].

Other Key Insights

From the patients’ perspective, most HCPs across specialties identified concerns about drug dependence as a major issue, suggesting the need for improved public awareness initiatives that address exaggerated fears while simultaneously providing clear guidance on the safe and appropriate use of gabapentinoids. This perception raises concerns about growing hesitancy across specialties, regions, and experience levels, which may unintentionally limit access to an effective treatment option for neuropathic pain. For transitioning a patient from a daily dose of gabapentin 900 mg, 70–80% selected either 150 mg or 300 mg, reflecting broad agreement on therapeutic equivalence. This aligns with published recommendations suggesting an approximate gabapentin‑to‑pregabalin conversion ratio of 6:1 when switching between gabapentinoids [35, 46].

Current guidelines recommend gabapentinoids (gabapentin or pregabalin), tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline), and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (e.g., duloxetine) as first‑line pharmacologic options with proven efficacy and safety against DPN and other neuropathic pain conditions [12, 15–17, 36–38, 47, 48]. In this study, most HCPs reported choices aligned with these recommendations; however, a notable proportion expressed a preference for NSAIDs and tramadol as options following gabapentinoid reclassification, neither of which is recommended as first‑line therapy for neuropathic pain. This is notable, as NSAIDs and tramadol are not expected to provide meaningful relief in neuropathic pain as a result of their mechanisms of action.

A multicenter observational study from Saudi Arabia reports a 21.5% reduction in pregabalin users and a 21% increase in tramadol and 16.1% increase in acetaminophen/codeine prescriptions after the restriction on gabapentinoids [49]. The findings from the current study as well as evidence from recent published literature point to a need for targeted education across specialties to strengthen guideline‑based DPN management pathways.

HCP Opinions Across Gender, Seniority Level, and Institutional Affiliation

Overall, a slightly higher percentage of female HCPs responded in agreement that they had encountered patients who were hesitant to continue treatment with gabapentinoids post reclassification. This may suggest gender-related communication dynamics within clinical encounters, where female HCPs are often perceived as more approachable, leading patients to more openly express concerns, negotiate treatment decisions, or question continuation of treatment [50, 51]. In addition, a comparatively higher proportion of female HCPs selected NSAIDs as an alternative treatment option for DPN‑related pain and chose pregabalin 75 mg as the preferred transition dose from gabapentin 900 mg. These choices may reflect heightened caution or apprehension related to gabapentinoid prescribing. Alternatively, they could be indicative of gaps in knowledge regarding neuropathic pain management among female HCPs, as suggested by their lower scores in this domain. Nonetheless, additional research is required to elucidate the underlying factors contributing to these observed differences.

After gabapentinoids a higher proportion of the junior HCPs selected duloxetine or NSAIDs as their preferred option for DPN-related pain, whereas senior and mid-career HCPs more often favored referral to a neurologist and prescribing amitriptyline as their second and third choices, respectively. Although junior HCPs identified gabapentinoids as their first-line choice for managing DPN, their responses across related survey questions suggest a lower likelihood of continued prescribing. This is reflected in their greater willingness to transition to alternative therapies in the presence of perceived psychological dependence and in their identification of selecting alternative treatments as the primary challenge following gabapentinoid reclassification. In addition, a notably high proportion of junior HCPs chose pregabalin 75 mg (a subtherapeutic dose) as the transition dose from gabapentin 900 mg daily dose. This suggests that junior HCPs may benefit from additional education and support in the evidence-based management of neuropathic pain. This is particularly important because a subtherapeutic or low-dose of gabapentinoids may increase the possibility of its abuse for sedative effects instead of pain management [31]. According to senior HCPs, patients were more anxious about the extended process required to obtain their medication, whereas junior and mid-career clinicians reported that their patients were more concerned about developing drug dependence. Taken together, these concerns and challenges may place pressure on HCPs to prescribe alternative therapies that may be suboptimal, inadequately effective, or associated with additional clinical or safety concerns.

HCPs affiliated with government or private institutions differed on the primary challenge that they face following gabapentinoid reclassification. The former selected patient hesitancy to continue gabapentinoid therapy, whereas the latter found it more challenging to discontinue gabapentinoids and prescribe alternative therapy. HCPs in private institutions more often reported encountering patients who were worried about continuing gabapentinoid therapy compared to HCPs in government institutions. These patterns may suggest that HCPs affiliated with private institutions may be more likely to opt for alternate therapies to mitigate patient worries, while the government-affiliated HCPs may be comparatively more likely to reassure their patients about safety of gabapentinoids when taken as prescribed. Differences in institutional contexts, such as broader formularies in private institutions and the availability of free medications in government hospitals, may contribute to these contrasting perspectives. Further research is needed to clarify how institutional factors influence HCPs’ decision‑making.

Summary

The findings of this survey suggest that the reclassification of gabapentinoids in Saudi Arabia has influenced clinician attitudes and prescribing behaviors. A majority of HCPs reported encountering patients who had been taking gabapentinoids for several months but were now refusing to continue treatment. In particular, clinicians reported challenges related to prescribing gabapentinoids, choosing appropriate doses, and considering alternative treatments as a result of concerns or exaggerated fear or apprehension in patients towards this drug class. Similar challenges have been described in other countries that have implemented regulatory measures to address gabapentinoid misuse, although their impact on prescribing practices has varied considerably.

Studies from the UK found that reclassification had limited impact on overall gabapentinoid utilization, although it increased regulatory scrutiny and altered prescribing practices [23, 24]. Similarly, evidence from Scotland and Northern Ireland suggested that prescribing patterns and associated mortality were largely unchanged following reclassification, whereas France reported a reduction in pregabalin use accompanied by a compensatory increase in gabapentin prescribing, highlighting the potential for therapeutic substitution rather than a reduction in overall demand [25, 26]. In Germany, where comparable nationwide reclassification measures have not been implemented, studies have identified potential pregabalin misuse primarily among individuals with substance use disorders and concomitant opioid use, emphasizing the importance of targeted risk assessment and monitoring strategies [52]. There remains no international consensus on the optimal regulatory approach to gabapentinoid prescribing. Different jurisdictions have adopted varying strategies ranging from formal drug reclassification and secure‑prescription requirements to enhanced monitoring and risk‑mitigation measures. Collectively, this suggests that blanket regulatory restrictions alone may be insufficient to curb potential abuse or illegitimate use and may need to be complemented by additional measures, such as accurate diagnostic assessment and targeted restrictions for patients at higher risk of abuse.

In this study, discontinuation of gabapentinoids and transition to a different medication was the most common action selected when HCPs perceived psychological dependence, even when no actual risk of misuse or abuse was evident. This may reflect a high level of caution among HCPs towards prescribing this drug class, aligning with their view that it has become even more challenging to prescribe gabapentinoids than tramadol after their reclassification. Consequently, this could indicate reduced access to gabapentinoids for patients with legitimate needs or could make it more difficult to obtain their medication because of extended processes.

Despite their inclination to switch to alternative medication, most HCPs, irrespective of their specialties, agreed that identifying a suitable alternative was their most formidable challenge post reclassification. Moreover, gabapentinoids remained their preferred choice for managing DPN. Regarding their strategy when initiating treatment with gabapentinoids, most HCPs reported that they would start with the lowest possible dose and increase later, as needed. When asked to select a transition dose from gabapentin 900 mg daily, the majority chose pregabalin 300 mg; however, pregabalin 75 mg, which is considered a subtherapeutic dose, was chosen by a notable proportion of junior and female HCPs.

Given that patients with neuropathic pain seek care from a wide range of specialties, maintaining appropriate access to gabapentinoids requires a unified and evidence-based treatment approach. Targeted education for HCPs, practical guidance on neuropathic pain diagnosis and management, and balanced assessment of therapeutic benefit versus abuse risk are likely to be essential for ensuring that regulatory safeguards do not unintentionally restrict access for patients with legitimate clinical needs. To reduce hesitancy in prescribing gabapentinoids, institutions and relevant authorities should provide clear assurance to HCPs that no punitive actions would be taken if gabapentinoids were prescribed after accurate diagnosis and assessment of potential misuse or abuse. In parallel, patient education initiatives are needed to address the perceived stigma surrounding gabapentinoids and promote their understanding of appropriate gabapentinoid use and the risks of abrupt discontinuation of treatment.

The key strength of this study is the inclusion of HCPs from a broad range of specialties, with representation across seniority levels, institutional affiliations, geographic regions of Saudi Arabia. This allowed for meaningful subgroup comparisons of differences in real-world clinical perspectives following gabapentinoid reclassification. The findings should, however, be considered in the context of certain limitations. The modest sample size and voluntary participation may limit generalizability. In addition, the survey format did not allow more advanced inferential statistical analysis, and the questionnaire was not formally validated, which may affect the interpretability of respondents’ perceptions. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable early insights into implications of recent regulatory restrictions on gabapentinoids in Saudi Arabia and indicates targeted clinician education and patient awareness initiatives could help support timely care for patients with neuropathic pain.

Conclusion

This survey-based study identified evident knowledge gaps and differences in clinical practices pertaining to pain management, particularly neuropathic pain, in HCPs across specialties in the context of regulatory restrictions on gabapentinoid use in Saudi Arabia. Increased caution among HCPs, concerns regarding potential dependence and prescribing repercussions, together with heightened patient apprehension, appear to have contributed to greater hesitancy toward gabapentinoid use, even when clinically indicated. As patients with neuropathic pain seek care across multiple specialties, these findings underscore the need for a unified, evidence-based approach to pain management. This can be achieved through focused educational programs for HCPs that address the gaps identified in this survey, provide guidance on neuropathic pain diagnosis, support clinical decision-making, help address exaggerated patient concerns, and promote a balanced assessment of therapeutic benefit versus potential abuse risk when prescribing gabapentinoids. In addition, patient education initiatives are needed to address stigma and risks associated with abrupt treatment discontinuation. Collectively, these steps may mitigate the post-reclassification challenges faced by HCPs in Saudi Arabia, reduce patient hesitancy, and maintain gabapentinoid access and timely care for patients with legitimate needs.
