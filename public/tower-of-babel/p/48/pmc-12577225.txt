Factors influencing shared decision-making and decision regret in parents of children undergoing myopia control within one year
Authors: Li-Ling Hung, Chi-Chin Sun, Li-Chun Chang, Li-Ling Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood myopia is highly prevalent in East Asia, where treatment decisions are typically made by parents. Although effective interventions are available, little is known about how parental knowledge and involvement influence decision quality. This study examined the relationships among parental knowledge, shared decision-making (SDM), and decision regret in parents of children with myopia, and assessed how treatment type and follow-up behaviors affect decision-making experiences.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 314 parents of children newly diagnosed with myopia. Data were collected using an online questionnaire that captured demographic and clinical characteristics, treatments received by the children, parents’ knowledge of myopia control, SDM, and decision regret. Multiple regression analysis was performed to identify the predictors of decision regret.ResultsShorter follow-up intervals (≤ 6 months), higher parental knowledge, and the use of orthokeratology were significantly associated with lower decision regret, whereas atropine therapy was associated with higher regret. However, when SDM was included in the final model, treatment type was no longer a significant predictor, and SDM emerged as the strongest negative predictor of regret (unstandardized regression coefficient [B] = -0.410, 95% confidence interval [CI] [-0.507, − 0.314], p < .001). This attenuation suggests that the observed relationship between treatment type and decision regret may be partly explained by differences in SDM, potentially reflecting more extensive consultation and engagement in certain modalities such as orthokeratology. The final model explained 27.4% of the variance in decision regret.ConclusionsSDM plays a critical role in reducing parents’ decision regret, regardless of the treatment modality. Enhancing communication about treatment options, particularly atropine therapy and axial length monitoring, may support more informed and satisfactory decision-making. The development of longitudinal studies and SDM tools tailored for parents with lower literacy is recommended to strengthen treatment adherence and improve clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04433-w.

Background

Myopia is a major global health concern, affecting 28% of the world’s population in 2010, with projections suggesting it may increase to 50% by 2050 [1]. Myopia complications, such as retinal detachment, cataracts, and macular degeneration, can cause visual impairment and substantially increase medical and social costs [2]. This burden is especially pronounced in East Asian countries, where the prevalence of myopia is high [3, 4]. For children with myopia, timely interventions can reduce the risk of vision-threatening complications in adulthood [5]. Both established and rapidly advancing therapeutic strategies aim to slow the progression of myopia or delay its onset [6]. Effective management in children requires not only controlling the axial elongation associated with disease progression but also correcting blurred vision [6, 7]. Atropine eye drops and orthokeratology, each demonstrating around 50% efficacy in myopia control on average, are among the most commonly used methods for managing myopia progression in East Asia [6].

In myopia management, therapeutic interventions should be continued to prevent rebound, which refers to a rate of myopia progression after treatment cessation that exceeds the age-expected norm [8]. A recent review by Bullimore and Brennan (2024) [9]reported that rebound in axial elongation is typically greater (≥ 0.14 mm) after discontinuation of higher-concentration atropine, whereas it is generally smaller after discontinuation of orthokeratology. Consistent adherence to follow-up care, along with active participation in treatment decisions and discussions, is essential for ensuring compliance with the treatment regimen [10]. For younger children, the effectiveness of treatments such as atropine eye drops and orthokeratology depends heavily on parental engagement and cooperation [11]. Consequently, the success of these interventions relies largely on parents’ willingness and ability to support the treatment regimen [12].

Shared decision-making (SDM) is widely recognized as a pivotal component of patient-centered care [13]. In pediatric care, SDM is a collaborative, family-centered process in which healthcare professionals and parents work together to make treatment decisions for the child, integrating the best available clinical evidence with the family’s values, preferences, and unique circumstances [14]. Lower satisfaction with the information provided has been shown to be associated with higher levels of decision regret [15]. In contrast, greater parental involvement in the decision-making process is associated with reduced decision regret [16], and parents who feel more involved report less decisional conflict and regret [17, 18]. Furthermore, a comprehensive assessment of parents’ knowledge of treatment options during the decision-making process is important for supporting informed choices and reducing decision regret [19].

In Taiwan, students up to high school receive school nurse-led visual acuity examinations each semester. Those with uncorrected vision below 0.9 (equivalent to Snellen 20/22, logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution [logMAR] = 0.05) are required to visit an ophthalmologist and return the completed referral form to the school. The school nurse then records this information into the Student Health Examination Information System [20]. If a student requires optical correction, they must obtain a prescription from an ophthalmologist. In Taiwan, prescriptions for children with myopia are issued primarily by ophthalmologists, while optometrists provide optical correction services based on these prescriptions [21]. Additionally, parents of children with myopia can obtain medication information and optical correction services only under the authorization of an ophthalmologist.

Existing studies on myopia control in children have focused primarily on treatment effectiveness [5], while parents’ attitudes, knowledge, and treatment preferences are also known to influence treatment outcomes [22]. Although our previous study examined the relationship between parental SDM, decision regret, and vision-related quality of life in children with myopia [23], the factors influencing SDM and decision regret in myopia treatment have not yet been explored.

SDM is associated with reduced decision regret among patients, suggesting that it may enhance perceptions of treatment decision quality [15]. Building on findings from previous analyses of treatment modality, decision regret, SDM, and vision-related quality of life, which highlighted the significant impact of decision regret and SDM on visual quality of life [23], identifying the factors that influence decision regret and SDM is an essential next step toward designing aids that not only strengthen SDM but also improve vision-related quality of life. This study examined how parental knowledge of myopia, treatment type, follow-up behaviors, and SDM relate to decision regret among parents of children with myopia. The results are intended to guide the design of SDM aids that promote treatment engagement and reduce decision regret.

Methods

Participants and data collection

This study was conducted as part of a shared decision-making (SDM) program for children with myopia. In its first year, the program focused on parents of children aged 6 to 12 years who had been diagnosed with myopia and had received treatment within the past year. Data were collected on treatment modalities, parental knowledge of myopia control, decision regret, SDM, and vision-related quality of life, with the aim of developing a web-based SDM platform for children with myopia. Eligibility criteria were: (1) parents of children aged 6 to 12 diagnosed with myopia; (2) residence in Taiwan during the study period; (3) ability to read Chinese; (4) children with a spherical equivalent refractive error ranging from − 5.00 D to −0.50 D in both eyes; (5) children with astigmatism not exceeding − 1.50 D in both eyes or with an interocular refractive difference not exceeding − 1.50 D; (6) children with best-corrected visual acuity of 0.00 logMAR (equivalent to 20/20 Snellen); and (7) children without a diagnosed developmental ocular or optic nerve disorder.

In this equation, the critical value was set at Z = 1.96, corresponding to a 95% confidence interval. According to previous hospital-based surveys in Taiwan [25], 49.72% of participants under 12 years of age with myopia of more than one year’s duration had received optical correction, and 31.39% had received 0.125% atropine eye drops. Because this study focused on children under 12 years of age with myopia of less than one year’s duration, the rate of optical correction was expected to be lower than that reported by Cheng et al. [25], while initial treatment was predominantly atropine, as reported by Fang et al. [26]. Therefore, the anticipated proportion (p) for the sample size calculation was based on Cheng et al. [25], who reported approximately 31.39% uptake of atropine therapy among primary school children. The margin of error (E) was set at 0.05. Based on these parameters, the required sample size ranged from 308 to 330.

To ensure geographic diversity and include children with myopia from various ophthalmology clinics, school nurses were contacted to invite eligible parents to participate in an online survey. Data were collected between January 16 and August 22, 2023, using Google Forms (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA). The CHERRIES (Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys) guidelines were followed to ensure the integrity and quality of the data collection process. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (approval number: 202102324B0).

Measurements

Demographic and clinical information

Data were collected on children’s age and refractive error at diagnosis, parents’ education level, follow-up interval, whether the parents had high myopia (refractive error greater than − 5.0 D, diopters), and whether the parents obtained axial length data.

Parents’ knowledge of myopia control

Parents’ knowledge of myopia control was measured using the Myopia Control Knowledge Scale (Appendix 1), developed by our research team in earlier studies [22, 27]. The scale demonstrated a Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 coefficient of 0.80 and a content validity index of 0.85. It consists of 12 items covering physiological changes due to myopia (3 items), risk factors for myopia progression (3 items), and effective myopia treatments and precautions (6 items). The items were rated as “true,” “false,” or “don’t know.” Correct answers were given 1 point, while incorrect or “don’t know” answers were scored as 0. Higher scores indicated greater knowledge of myopia control. The scale has not been specifically validated in populations whose children were receiving orthokeratology treatment.

Myopia treatments

Parents reported the treatments their children had received three months after the initial diagnosis of myopia by selecting from a structured multiple-choice list. Options included single-vision spectacles, 0.125% atropine eye drops, short-acting mydriatics (for example, tropicamide), myopia control spectacles, myopia control soft contact lenses, and orthokeratology. Participants were allowed to select more than one option to reflect the possibility of combination therapy.

Decision regret

Decision regret was measured using the Decisional Regret Scale, a self-reported instrument comprising five items within a single dimension. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly agree”) to 5 (“strongly disagree”), with items 2 and 4 reverse-scored [28]. Sample statements included “It was the right decision” and “I regret the choice that was made.” The total score ranged from 0 to 100 and was calculated by subtracting 1 from each item and multiplying by 25. Higher scores indicated greater levels of regret. Xu et al. translated the Decisional Regret Scale into Chinese and reported acceptable psychometric properties [29].

SDM

The nine-item self-reported Shared Decision-Making Questionnaire (SDM-Q-9) was used to assess parents’ perceptions of their engagement in the SDM process [30]. Each item was rated on a 6-point scale ranging from 0 (“completely disagree”) to 5 (“completely agree”). Sample items included “My child’s doctor and I selected a treatment option together” and “My child’s doctor made it clear that a decision had to be made.” The raw total score was multiplied by 20/9 to yield a final score ranging from 0 to 100, where 0 indicated no SDM and 100 indicated a very high level of SDM. The SDM-Q-9 has been validated and is highly reliable, including in studies with parents in pediatric settings [31, 32]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.938, with item-total correlations ranging from 0.685 to 0.826 [30]. The Chinese version of the SDM-Q-9, translated and validated by Luo and Xiao [33], assesses parents’ views on SDM within clinical settings in China.

Data analysis

Results were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations (SDs). Univariate analyses, including t-tests, were performed to examine differences in SDM and decision regret by demographic and clinical variables, myopia treatments, and parents’ knowledge of myopia control.

Multiple regression analysis was then conducted with decision regret as the dependent variable. Variables that reached statistical significance in univariate analyses were included in the multiple regression analysis across three models. Collinearity diagnostics were performed for the regression models. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all independent variables, including treatment type and SDM, were below 2, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. In our analysis, treatment variables were coded as separate dummy variables, with “no use of that specific treatment” as the reference group. While 97.1% of participants received at least one form of treatment, the proportions varied by treatment type (e.g., 67.9% used atropine).

Model 1 included the child’s age, availability of axial length data, and follow-up interval as independent variables. Model 2 added treatment status, atropine eye drops, single-vision lenses, orthokeratology, and parents’ knowledge of myopia control. Model 3 included SDM. Regression coefficients (β) and 95% CIs were computed. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and statistical significance was set at p <.05.

Results

Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for all variables and reports significant differences in demographic and clinical variables, parents’ knowledge of myopia control, and myopia treatments based on SDM and decision regret. The study included 314 participants, of whom 51.91% (n = 163) were boys and 48.09% (n = 151) were girls. Most children were 8 years old or younger (64.00%, n = 201).

Among parents, 69.75% (n = 219) had a college-level education or higher, 37.46% (n = 106) had high myopia (refractive error > −5.0 D), and 44.27% (n = 139) obtained axial length data for their children during follow-up visits. In addition, 85.03% of parents reported a follow-up interval of ≤ 6 months, and 97.13% (n = 305) reported that their child received myopia treatment.

Tropicamide use was reported by 14.75% (n = 45) of parents, while 67.87% (n = 207) of children used atropine eye drops. Myopia control spectacles were reported in 4.92% (n = 15) of children, MiSight lenses in 1.31% (n = 4), and orthokeratology in 16.72% (n = 51). In addition, 57.01% (n = 179) of parents had knowledge scores ≥ 8, and 60.19% (n = 189) had SDM scores ≥ 78.

Children’s age, parents’ education level, availability of axial length data, and follow-up interval were significantly associated with both SDM and decision regret. Availability of axial length data was associated with higher SDM scores (t = −4.133, p <.001) and lower decision regret scores (t = 1.848, p <.05). Follow-up intervals of ≤ 6 months were associated with higher SDM scores (t = −2.231, p <.05) and lower decision regret scores (t = −3.370, p <.001), compared with longer intervals. Younger children (≤ 8 years) had higher decision regret scores (t = 1.826, p <.05) compared with older children (≥ 9 years). Parents with a high school education or lower had lower SDM scores (t = −2.080, p <.05) compared with those with a college-level education or higher. Similarly, parents of children with a refractive error ≥ −1.5 D had lower SDM scores than those of children with a refractive error < −1.5 D.

Among children receiving myopia treatment, parents of those who underwent orthokeratology had higher SDM scores than those who did not (t = −3.041, p <.001). Conversely, parents of children using atropine eye drops had lower SDM scores (t = 1.948, p <.05) and higher decision regret scores (t = 1.889, p <.05) than those whose children did not use atropine eye drops. In addition, the use of orthokeratology (t = 4.211, p <.001), higher knowledge scores (≥ 8) (t = 1.964, p <.05), and higher SDM scores (≥ 78) (t = 5.618, p <.001) were associated with lower decision regret scores (Table 2 ).

Table 3 presents the results of the multiple linear regression analysis. In Model 1, younger child age (≤ 8 years) and longer follow-up intervals (> 6 months) were associated with higher decision regret. In Model 2, longer follow-up intervals (≥ 6 months) were significantly associated with higher decision regret (B = 5.55, 95% CI = 2.56–8.54, t = 3.649, p <.001). Parents whose children received atropine treatment also reported greater regret compared with those receiving no treatment (B = 3.35, 95% CI = 0.49–6.22, t = 2.303, p <.05). In contrast, orthokeratology was negatively associated with regret (B = − 4.44, 95% CI = − 8.17 to − 0.72, t = − 2.345, p <.05), and higher parental knowledge (score ≥ 8) significantly reduced regret (B = − 1.22, 95% CI = − 1.92 to − 0.52, t = − 2.765, p <.01). In Model 3, follow-up intervals of ≥ 6 months remained a significant predictor of higher decision regret (B = 4.05, 95% CI = 1.30–6.81, t = 2.899, p <.01). Higher parental knowledge (score ≥ 8) was associated with lower regret (B = − 0.79, 95% CI = − 1.36 to − 0.23, t = − 2.749, p <.01). Importantly, SDM demonstrated a strong negative association with regret (B = − 0.37, 95% CI = − 0.46 to − 0.28, t = − 7.978, p <.001). The inclusion of SDM alone accounted for an additional 14.8% of the explained variance, increasing the total variance explained to 27.4%. Notably, the B values for all treatment variables decreased and became non-significant after SDM was included in the model. This suggests that while atropine eye drops and orthokeratology influence decision regret, higher SDM scores are associated with lower decision regret.

Discussion

This study examined the influence of parental knowledge of myopia, treatment type, follow-up behaviors, and shared decision-making (SDM) on decision regret among parents of children with myopia who had received treatment within the past year. The results of this study can help guide education for parents on myopia management in the early stages of treatment, promote SDM, and reduce decision regret. Notably, 97.13% of the children in this study had received myopia treatment, and 85% attended a follow-up session within six months. At follow-up, axial length measurements were available in 44.30% of cases. Compared with a previous study of children receiving orthokeratology, this cohort, which included a range of myopia control modalities, had a longer average follow-up interval [22].

Children newly diagnosed with myopia require regular monitoring of axial length progression, and follow-up visits every 3 to 6 months are valuable for monitoring adherence and early treatment response, although treatment adjustments are usually guided by 12-month outcomes [5]. In this study, 15% of participants returned for follow-up after more than six months, which is a point of concern. Nonetheless, under Taiwan’s school-based referral system, where school nurses monitor students with poor visual acuity and encourage follow-up care, the rate of follow-up within six months was higher than that reported by Tao et al. [34]. Axial length data play a critical role in myopia management and are closely tied to the quality of clinical monitoring. In this study, 44.30% of parents obtained axial length measurements at follow-up, indicating progress in Taiwan’s efforts to improve myopia control.

In this study, 16.72% of children underwent orthokeratology, while 67.87% were treated with atropine eye drops. The rate of atropine use was higher than that reported by Cheng et al. [25], who found atropine use among 31.39% of children in Taiwan. These findings reflect a prescribing pattern in Taiwan, where ophthalmologists often choose atropine eye drops as an initial intervention for myopia management [35–37]. The univariate analysis indicated higher regret among parents whose children received atropine, likely due to its side effects (such as photophobia and near-vision blurring), the need for supplementary optical correction, and uncertainty about long-term efficacy [38]. This may also explain the lower regret observed among parents whose children used orthokeratology, as it provides both therapeutic and corrective benefits [39]. Single-vision spectacles correct refractive error but do not control myopia progression [6]. While higher decision regret was observed in the univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA), this association did not remain significant in the multivariate regression model. Whether the use of single-vision spectacles alone is associated with greater regret requires further investigation with larger samples.

Furthermore, consistent with the findings of Hayes et al. [40], parents with higher education levels were more involved in SDM. Parents’ involvement in SDM was also greater when their children had lower magnitudes of myopia (e.g., less severe than − 1.50 D) compared with those with higher magnitudes (≥ − 1.50 D). When refractive error in children is < −1.50 D, preserving visual quality may require optical correction in addition to pharmacologic interventions such as atropine [41], which warrants more extensive discussion between clinicians and parents to support SDM. Hung et al. [22] reported that parents of children receiving orthokeratology who understood that axial length reflects myopia progression were more likely to adhere to 3-month follow-up visits. Additionally, axial length measurements are critical for monitoring myopia progression [6]. Discussing axial length data and myopia progression with parents during follow-up visits can enhance their sense of control over their child’s myopia management, thereby improving satisfaction and reducing decision regret. In line with this, we found that attending follow-up sessions within six months and obtaining axial length data were both associated with greater parental involvement in treatment decision-making.

In Taiwan, atropine is often the first-line treatment prescribed by ophthalmologists for newly diagnosed myopia [26, 42]. However, because of limited consultation time, explanations about atropine therapy may be insufficient [43]. Reported side effects of atropine eye drops are a common reason for discontinuation among children [38, 40]. These challenges, along with the compliance issues emphasized by Bullimore et al. [9], may partly explain why parents of children receiving atropine therapy had lower engagement in SDM and higher levels of decision regret. Given the limited availability of ophthalmologists, an important challenge is improving parents’ understanding of atropine eye drop use and its side effects, as well as encouraging more meaningful discussions about treatment decisions. The findings of this study highlight the importance of involving parents in treatment decision-making not only for public health considerations but also for the effectiveness of myopia control strategies.

In contrast, orthokeratology requires more thorough clinical assessment and parent education to ensure successful lens wear. This necessitates comprehensive communication with parents [44] and promotes greater involvement in treatment discussions [11]. Previous studies have reported high levels of satisfaction with orthokeratology [39], which may explain the lower levels of decision regret observed among the participants using this treatment.

Knowledge is a key factor in SDM. According to the guidelines of the International Myopia Institute, follow-up visits every 3 to 6 months allow for treatment adjustments based on myopia progression and visual quality [45]. Achieving this level of individualized care depends on effective communication between ophthalmologists and parents [46], which is central to fostering active parental involvement in treatment decision-making. Consistent with these findings, our results showed that parents of children who attended 3- to 6-month follow-ups and had higher levels of knowledge had greater involvement in SDM [47] and lower levels of decision regret [19].

Our findings suggest that higher levels of SDM are associated with lower levels of parental decision regret. This negative association is consistent with prior research demonstrating that active engagement in decision-making reduces uncertainty and fosters greater satisfaction with the chosen treatment [18]. The effect of treatment modality on decision regret diminished after adjusting for SDM, underscoring the pivotal role of SDM. Effective SDM can enhance parental understanding of treatment options, improve adherence to prescribed therapies, and reduce decisional conflict and regret [16]. However, the strong association between SDM and decision regret raises the possibility that SDM may partly mediate the relationship between treatment modality and regret. For example, parents who chose orthokeratology may have had more consultation time with ophthalmologists, which could enhance SDM and thereby reduce regret. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether SDM functions as a mediator of treatment effects on decision regret or serves as an independent predictor .

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about how SDM and decision regret evolve over time, and any causal inferences should therefore be interpreted with caution. Second, the sample size (N = 314) limited the ability to compare the effects of different treatment modalities on SDM and decision regret. In particular, the very small number of participants using soft contact lenses (such as Misight) prevented their inclusion in the regression analysis. Future studies with larger samples are recommended to enable more comprehensive analyses of SDM and decision regret across treatment options. Third, this study examined only parents’ perspectives on regret; future research should also include the views of older children (aged 10–12 years) to provide a fuller understanding of the treatment experience. Finally, participants were recruited through school nurses, and this convenience sampling method may have introduced selection bias. Parents who were more engaged in their child’s health, more proactive, or had stronger relationships with school staff may have been more likely to participate, which could have influenced the findings, particularly the reported levels of knowledge and involvement in SDM.

Based on these limitations, future studies should adopt a prospective longitudinal design to track changes in treatment decision-making and decision regret from the time of diagnosis. It is also important to develop decision aids and educational materials for parents with limited literacy or knowledge about myopia. Such resources may improve communication between healthcare professionals and families, strengthen the quality of decision-making, and improve treatment adherence and outcomes.

Conclusions

This study is the first to examine the relationships among myopia treatment in children, SDM, and decision regret by parents. It contributes to promoting collaboration between ophthalmologists and parents at the time of diagnosis, which may enhance treatment adherence and effectiveness. Although factors such as parental education level, refractive error, and treatment modality influenced parents’ involvement in treatment decisions, our findings showed that incorporating SDM reduced decision regret.
