Integrating haplotype-enhanced HIV surveillance with national databases improves the resolution of transmission networks, drug resistance, and risk assessment
Authors: Dongjun Cai, Yi Feng, Mengze Gan, Huan Li, Dong Wang, Jingjing Hao, Lu Lin, Yue Tang, Yicong Wang, Lingjie Liao, Zheng Wang, Hui Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSanger and other consensus-based HIV surveillance methods provide limited resolution for characterizing transmission dynamics. This study evaluated the added value of integrating haplotype sequences into the division of research on viral immunology (DRVI) pol database to improve analyses of viral diversity, transmission networks, and haplotype-level drug resistance. We deep sequenced the pol gene from 95 newly diagnosed individuals in the 2023 national survey, reconstructed haplotypes using CliqueSNV, and incorporated all haplotypes into the DRVI database for network construction. Principal component analysis and k-means clustering were used to identify subgroup structures based on viral diversity, selective pressures, and host factors, and these were linked with molecular network data. A total of 1,594 haplotypes were reconstructed, achieving a 91.1%sequence-level network inclusion rate (1452/1594 haplotypes), which was 2.4-fold higher than that of consensus sequences (37.9%, 36/95). Haplotype integration increased network connections by 2.14-fold (75 vs 35). Two subgroups were identified (p < 0.001), with high-diversity/low-selection individuals forming denser clusters (64 vs 6; p < 0.001). Among detected drug-resistant mutations, 69.7% were observed only at the haplotype level, and resistance patterns varied within the same genetic similarity-based cluster. These findings demonstrate that haplotype integration markedly enhances HIV molecular surveillance by resolving previously unrecognized links, clarifying diversity-associated network structure (which may partly reflect infection stage), and detecting low-frequency drug resistance missed by consensus methods.KEYWORDS: HIV haplotype analysis, transmission networks, drug resistance, viral diversity, next-generation sequencing

Introduction

HIV remains a significant public health challenge, and its extensive genetic diversity in China presents substantial obstacles for molecular epidemiological surveillance [1,2]. Molecular network analysis is widely used to characterize transmission patterns and guide targeted interventions. However, HIV’s rapid evolution generates heterogeneous viral populations within hosts [3]. Traditional molecular epidemiology and drug resistance studies have mainly relied on Sanger or consensus sequences derived from next-generation sequencing (NGS) [4], in which the most frequent nucleotide at each position is selected to construct a representative sequence. This approach averages within-host viral diversity, potentially missing minority or founder variants linked to transmission or resistance, thereby limiting the resolution of transmission dynamics [5].

With the increasing availability of NGS, reconstructing HIV quasispecies at the haplotype level is now possible and becoming increasingly practical. Haplotype analysis retains granular details of intrahost viral variants, offering substantial advantages for molecular network resolution and surveillance of drug resistance [6]. Previous studies have shown that haplotype analysis, even in small or regional datasets, significantly enhances clustering rates (from 2.9–12.8% to 35.8–82.1%) and identifies additional resistance mutations [7]. Large-scale NGS studies have also demonstrated a link between intrahost viral diversity and clinical indicators, including viral load. However, the high cost of NGS remains a major barrier to its widespread use in large-scale molecular network and drug resistance studies. Moreover, prior studies on viral population genetics have typically examined isolated measures, such as the dN/dS ratio, with few efforts to systematically integrate multiple diversity metrics with clinical variables [8]. Similarly, most studies of transmission dynamics have focused on behavioural factors, with limited attempts to link evolutionary metrics to transmission network characteristics [9].

Therefore, this study aimed to incorporate haplotype sequences from newly diagnosed HIV cases into the division of research on viral immunology (DRVI) database and conduct molecular network analysis within a comprehensive reference framework. Instead of providing a nationally representative analysis of transmission dynamics, this study focused on assessing the added value of haplotype-based analysis in identifying potential genetic linkages and resistance signals when compared with a large national reference database. Specifically, we aimed to evaluate the benefits of haplotype analysis in enhancing network connectivity and detecting low-frequency drug resistance mutations, as well as to explore the correlation between viral diversity metrics and the characteristics of the transmission network. We hypothesized that integrating these analyses could improve the resolution of HIV transmission networks and offer a practical framework for refining national surveillance and evaluating drug resistance transmission risk, ultimately supporting precision prevention and control strategies.

Materials and methods

Study population and data sources

We enrolled individuals newly diagnosed with HIV from the 2023 National Molecular Epidemiological Survey in China. Participants met the following inclusion criteria: they were antiretroviral therapy-naïve, had no history of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use prior to their HIV diagnosis, had sufficient plasma volume available for next-generation sequencing (NGS), and possessed complete clinical data. A total of 8,558 newly diagnosed cases were collected nationwide in 2023. Participants were preferentially selected from economically developed regions characterized by high population mobility, higher HIV prevalence, and designated prevention priorities. This recruitment strategy constituted a targeted, non-probability convenience sample, enriched for priority settings, and was not intended to be nationally representative. The primary objective was to assess whether haplotype-level resolution enhances the detection of potential genetic linkages when mapped onto the nationwide DRVI reference network. This focus differs from characterizing nationwide transmission cluster dynamics or drug-resistance distributions.

The collected clinical information included CD4+ lymphocyte count, age, transmission route, and other demographic characteristics. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Recent infection status was determined using the limiting antigen avidity enzyme immunoassay (LAg-Avidity EIA) as detailed in Supplementary Table S8 (appendix p8–9).

In addition, Sanger sequence data were retrieved from the DRVI database, encompassing HIV pol sequences collected nationwide between 2004 and 2023. This dataset consisted of two components: sequences obtained from the Los Alamos HIV Sequence Database and those generated by the DRVI at the NCAIDS, China CDC. The DRVI database primarily contains sequences covering the standard protease-reverse transcriptase (PR-RT) region of the HIV-1 pol gene, which are used for routine drug-resistance surveillance rather than full-length pol sequences. After rigorous quality control, a refined dataset of 95,775 unique PR-RT sequences was established to ensure one sequence per individual [10]. The increased sequence count reflects the inclusion of updated entries in the DRVI database. The curation workflow is depicted in Supplementary Figure S1 (appendix p9).

Viral sequencing and molecular analyses

Viral RNA was extracted from plasma samples using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen). This was followed by reverse transcription and PCR amplification to generate nucleic acid templates. The nearly full-length pol gene, approximately 3,000 bp, which includes the protease (PR), reverse transcriptase (RT), and integrase (IN) regions, was sequenced on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform at high depth, with a mean read coverage exceeding 10,000×. The sequencing quality was assessed using raw base quality metrics, demonstrating that the proportion of bases with Q scores of 30 or higher exceeded 90% across all samples (mean Q30 = 92.55%, SD = 0.87). This is consistent with a theoretical per-base sequencing error rate of approximately 10−³, based on Phred quality score definitions. Only anonymized samples approved by the ethics committee were included in the analysis. Raw reads underwent quality control to eliminate low-quality bases and remove adaptor or primer sequences. High-quality reads were then aligned to the HIV reference genome (HXB2), and metrics such as mapping efficiency, coverage, and duplication were assessed.

Viral haplotypes were reconstructed using CliqueSNV (version 2.0.3) in accurate mode with a maximum frequency difference threshold of 0.01 [11]. This threshold was selected by considering both the observed sequencing error rate and the algorithm’s frequency-based discrimination to provide a conservative margin for distinguishing true low-frequency variants from sequencing noise. Consensus and haplotype sequences were compared against sequences in the DRVI database using BLAST (version 2.13.0). A genetic distance threshold of 0.5% was applied to define potential epidemiological linkages, as previously described [12]. Transmission networks were constructed and visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.9.0), with network topology metrics used to characterize clustering patterns. The network inclusion rate was defined as the proportion of input sequences that formed at least one genetic linkage (genetic distance≤0.5%) with any other sequence in the constructed network. Network connectivity metrics were interpreted as indicators of genetic similarity-based linkage, rather than providing direct evidence of transmission direction, timing, or causality.

Statistical and computational analyses

For each sample, viral diversity was quantified from reconstructed haplotypes using four metrics: Shannon diversity index (H), evenness (H/H_max), nucleotide diversity (π), and Watterson’s θ [13]. Intrahost evolutionary dynamics and immune selection were assessed by constructing maximum-likelihood phylogenies in IQ-TREE (version 2.1.3) under the best-fit substitution model. Selective pressure was further estimated by calculating dN/dS ratios using HyPhy (version 2.5.2). Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to examine associations among viral diversity indices, dN/dS, CD4+ lymphocyte counts, and age. All variables incorporated into the PCA were summarized at the individual level, with each data point representing a single participant rather than individual haplotypes.

Data integrity was ensured by excluding samples with incomplete variables. Extreme outliers for each continuous variable were identified using the interquartile range (IQR) method, defined as values either below Q1−3 × IQR or above Q3 + 3 × IQR. To approximate normal distributions, log transformation was applied to Watterson’s θ, nucleotide diversity (π), dN/dS, and CD4+ lymphocyte counts; square-root transformation to age; and square transformation to evenness, while the Shannon index was analyzed in its original form. All variables were Z-score standardized before PCA (Supplementary Figure S2， S3; appendix p10–11). Sampling adequacy was evaluated using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) statistic and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (Supplementary Table S3, S4; appendix p5–6). Across all methods, a two-cluster solution (k = 2) consistently provided the best fit.

Latent subgroup structures were explored through unsupervised clustering of PCA scores using k-means, Gaussian mixture models, and hierarchical clustering. Cluster validity was examined using the silhouette coefficient, Calinski-Harabasz index, and Davies-Bouldin index, and between-group separation was evaluated with PERMANOVA and MANOVA (Supplementary Table S5; appendix p6).

All consensus and haplotype sequences were submitted to the Stanford HIV Drug Resistance Database for resistance interpretation, with resistance levels classified according to the Stanford HIV drug resistance level definitions. Drug resistance mutations were mapped onto molecular networks, with node colours indicating specific resistance variants. The distribution of resistance features across clusters was analyzed to assess the potential transmission risk of drug-resistant viruses.

Results

Comparison of haplotype and consensus networks

The study population was predominantly male (92.6%) and of Han ethnicity (91.6%), with men who have sex with men (MSM) identified as the main route of transmission (61.1%). Participants ranged in age from 16 to 78 years (mean 40.9 years). Overall, one-third (33.7%) had completed college-level education or higher, and nearly half (48.4%) were unmarried (Supplementary Table S1; appendix p2). Deep sequencing of the HIV pol gene using the DNBSEQ-G400 platform revealed CRF01_AE (23.2%) as the predominant subtype, followed by CRF07_BC (21.1%), with other subtypes accounting for 55.8% of sequences. Haplotype reconstruction with CliqueSNV yielded 1594 haplotypes across the 95 samples. To assess network performance, haplotypes and consensus sequences were compared with the DRVI database (genetic distance threshold 0.5%) and visualized in Cytoscape. The haplotype network incorporated 1452 sequences（haplotype sequences derived from 95 samples）and 75 sequences from the DRVI database, forming a densely connected network of 1527 nodes (Figure 1(A)). In contrast, the consensus sequence network contained only 36 sample-derived sequences and 35 DRVI database sequences (Figure 1(B and C)). The number of sequences connected to the database was higher in the haplotype network than in the consensus network (75 vs 35; 2.14-fold difference). To determine whether this difference was solely due to the increased number of input sequences, a rarefaction analysis was performed under matched input size conditions (K = 3, 5, and 7). After 500 iterations, analyses based on haplotypes consistently yielded more matches to the DRVI database than those based on consensus sequences (Supplementary Figure S6; appendix p.14).

Viral diversity and host clustering

Building on the network-level validation of the haplotype approach, we next examined within-sample haplotype characteristics to explore multidimensional relationships between viral diversity and host features. After quality control of NGS data from 95 individuals living with HIV, 79 patients (83.2%) retained complete datasets for analysis, whereas 16 were excluded (Supplementary Table S2; appendix p3). Samples were excluded either because fewer than four sequences were reconstructed or because sequences contained internal stop codons (TGA) and potential reading-frame disruptions that invalidated codon-based selection analysis. As a robustness check, we repeated PCA and k-means clustering (k = 2) after removing the dN/dS ratios within the analyzable samples (Supplementary Figure S5; appendix p.13). Variables analyzed included viral diversity metrics (Watterson’s θ, nucleotide diversity π, Shannon diversity index, and evenness), selective pressure (dN/dS ratio), and clinical parameters (age and CD4+ lymphocyte count). Spearman correlation analysis showed moderate to strong associations among these variables (Figure 2(A)).

Due to these intercorrelations, PCA is applied to reduce dimensionality and uncover latent patterns. PC1 was interpreted as a composite diversity axis reflecting the shared variation among correlated indices. The loading structure revealed that PC1 was mainly driven by viral diversity indices (Watterson’s θ, nucleotide diversity π, Shannon diversity index, and evenness; all loadings > 0.44), with an opposing contribution from dN/dS (−0.37), indicating that PC1 represented a “viral diversity-selection pressure” axis. PC2 was largely influenced by host factors, with positive loading for age (0.69) and negative loading for CD4+ lymphocyte count (−0.66), representing an “age-immune status” gradient (Figure 2(B)). Based on this loading pattern, four quadrants were delineated, each corresponding to a distinct combination of viral diversity, selective pressure, age, and immune status (Supplementary Table S6; appendix, p. 7).

Participant samples were then plotted on the PC1–PC2 plane to illustrate their distribution across these axes (Supplementary Figure S4, appendix p.12). PC1 scores ranged from −5.19 to 3.31 and PC2 scores from −3.27 to 2.43. Samples were distributed across the four quadrants (Q1: 19 [24.1%], Q2: 22 [27.8%], Q3: 16 [20.3%], Q4: 22 [27.8%]). Although the PCA plot suggested a continuous landscape, independent unsupervised clustering analyses were conducted to assess potential subgrouping. Among the tested algorithms and cluster numbers (Gaussian mixture, hierarchical, and k-means; k = 2–6) (Supplementary Table S5; appendix p6), the k-means model with k = 2 achieved the highest silhouette coefficient (0.451) and the most consistent performance across Calinski-Harabasz and Davies-Bouldin indices, supporting the presence of two moderately separated but statistically significant clusters (PERMANOVA p < 0.001).

The two clusters identified by the k-means model corresponded broadly to distinct regions on the PC1–PC2 plane (Figure 2(C)). Subtype distributions were comparable between clusters, with no significant differences observed, and no dominant subtype in either group (p = 0.399; Supplementary Table S9; appendix p9). Cluster 1 comprised individuals in quadrants I and IV, characterized by high viral diversity and low selection pressure, and these participants were connected to 64 DRVI database samples within the haplotype network. Cluster 2 included individuals located in quadrants II and III, defined by low viral diversity and high selection pressure. Participants in this group were connected to only six sequences in the haplotype network (64 vs 6; p = 2·44 × 10−¹³) (Figure 2(D); Supplementary Table S7; appendix p7). In the consensus network, Cluster 1 individuals also exhibited markedly higher connectivity compared with Cluster 2 (31 vs 6; p = 4·13 × 10−⁵) (Figure 2(E)). LAg-Avidity EIA results further suggested that Cluster 1 was enriched for long-term infections compared with Cluster 2 (35/41 [85.4%] vs 22/38 [57.9%]; Supplementary Table S8; appendix p8–9).

Haplotype-level resistance detection

Drug resistance was analyzed for both consensus (n = 95) and haplotype sequences (n = 1594) against four antiretroviral drug classes: protease inhibitors (PI), nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTI), non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTI), and integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTI). Consensus sequence analysis revealed only a few mutations sparsely distributed across the viral genome (Figure 3(A)). Haplotype-based analysis revealed a more intricate landscape of drug-resistance patterns (Figure 3(B)). For NNRTIs, consensus analysis detected three major mutations – V179D (9.5%), V179E (8.4%), and K103N (5.3%) – whereas haplotype analysis not only confirmed these findings but also identified additional rare mutations [14], such as F227L (1.1%) (Table 1).

Haplotype-driven resistance spread

The integration of resistance profiles into molecular transmission networks demonstrated the clustering of drug-resistance mutations. Within two major genetic similarity-based clusters, five NNRTI-associated mutations were identified: K103N, V179D, V108I, E138A, and V179E. While many samples within clusters shared similar resistance profiles, marked intra-host heterogeneity was observed, with multiple mutations coexisting within haplotypes from the same individual. For example, sample GZGY230091 carried V179E in its consensus sequence, but its haplotypes included both V179E and K103N; the K103N haplotype linked to nine sequences in the DRVI database. JH20233033 carried V179E in consensus but also harboured E138A haplotypes linked to two sequences in the DRVI database. GZGY230083 showed K103N as the dominant mutation, linking to three database sequences in the DRVI database, whereas all haplotypes from GZZY230003 consistently carried V179E (Figure 4(A)). Similarly, SCYB230013 and SCYB230067 harboured V179D in both consensus and haplotype sequences, whereas SCYB23012 and SCYB236014 displayed more complex haplotype diversity with multiple drug resistance combinations (Figure 4(B)).

Discussion

This study yielded three significant findings. First, haplotype-level sequencing demonstrated enhanced sensitivity in reconstructing transmission networks by identifying more closely related sequences within the DRVI database and uncovering potential transmission links that consensus-based methods had not detected. Second, analyses of host-virus interactions revealed that haplotype-level diversity indices are inversely related to selective pressure. Specifically, individuals exhibiting higher viral diversity and lower selective pressure demonstrated increased connectivity in haplotype-based molecular networks, suggesting a correlation between within-host evolutionary profiles and network positions. Third, in the realm of drug resistance monitoring, haplotype analysis not only corroborated the major resistance mutations detected by consensus sequences but also identified additional low-frequency or rare resistance variants, thereby revealing heterogeneity in resistance patterns within clusters based on genetic similarity. Collectively, these findings illustrate that haplotype-based integrative analysis within the DRVI database can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular characteristics of HIV transmission and evolution, offering novel insights for optimizing molecular surveillance strategies and drug resistance management in China.

In mainstream molecular epidemiology, analyses have traditionally relied on consensus sequences [15]. This approach has limitations regarding the proportion of sequences incorporated into molecular networks and their matching capacity with external databases; unless the sampling density is extremely high, only relationships of coarse resolution can be inferred [16]. Although a few studies employing deep sequencing or haplotype-based methods have demonstrated improved analytical resolution, most were limited by small sample sizes or regional datasets [6]. By contrast, the integration of haplotype sequences into the large-scale DRVI reference database has substantially enhanced network inclusion and the ability to resolve genetic linkages. Unlike earlier haplotype studies that focused on local outbreaks or single-centre cohorts, embedding haplotypes within a nationwide, heterogeneous reference background has enabled more reliable identification of high-similarity matches and strengthened the epidemiological relevance of network inference. Our analysis showed that haplotypes identified significantly more closely related sequences in the DRVI database than consensus sequences, and increased the number of samples incorporated into molecular networks, providing a more comprehensive view of potential transmission patterns. Under conditions of matched input size, rarefaction analysis indicated higher matching frequencies for haplotype sequences than for replicated consensus sequences. This advantage likely arises because consensus sequences average within-host diversity, potentially masking minority or recently transmitted founder variants [17]. In contrast, haplotypes preserve the fine-scale genetic structure, enabling high-similarity matches with other individuals’ consensus or haplotypes and increasing the detectability of edges and clusters within networks [11].

In previous studies, the dN/dS ratio has been frequently utilized as an indicator of selective pressure at the population or subtype level, primarily to characterize the overall evolutionary trajectory of HIV [18]. However, its utility in assessing transmission potential at the individual level remains constrained. Similarly, traditional diversity measures such as Watterson’s θ, nucleotide diversity π, the Shannon index, and evenness index have been extensively applied in population genetics and microbiome research but are seldom included in the analysis of HIV molecular epidemiology networks [19,20]. In this research, we applied haplotype reconstruction to create high-resolution sequence profiles and combined it with PCA to integrate viral diversity, selective pressure, and host clinical parameters into a cohesive framework. This framework was integrated with molecular transmission networks to develop a multidimensional approach. We identified a negative correlation between selective pressure and intrahost viral diversity. Additionally, we noted an association between higher viral diversity or weaker selective pressure and increased network connectivity; however, the causal interpretation of these findings is constrained by the cross-sectional study design and the indirect nature of genetic similarity networks [21]. The subtype distributions were similar across clusters, with no subtype showing preferential enrichment in either cluster, indicating that subtype composition did not significantly influence clustering. Our results underscore a previously overlooked conceptual link between a “viral diversity-host selection pressure axis” and transmission network topology, offering a systems-level perspective that enhances previous molecular epidemiological investigations. The underlying mechanisms of this phenomenon likely involve two main factors: firstly, substantial intrahost diversity can facilitate immune evasion, thereby aiding viral persistence and genetic diversification [22]; secondly, reduced host selective pressure may limit viral clearance, allowing variants to accumulate within clusters based on genetic similarity [23]. Notably, this pattern could also be partially explained by variations in the duration of infection, as within-host HIV diversity is known to increase over time [24]. Supporting this explanation, the LAg-Avidity EIA indicated a higher prevalence of long-term infections in Cluster 1 compared to Cluster 2, suggesting that the duration of infection likely influences the observed diversity/selection-connectivity association. These findings offer a molecular evolutionary perspective on network connectivity, yet they should not be construed as direct indicators of heterogeneous current transmission risks; further verification necessitates viral load data, longitudinal observations, and epidemiological validation. This perspective may complement prior research that focused on behavioural determinants by emphasizing the potential role of within-host evolutionary characteristics when integrated with haplotype-based molecular networks [9].

In resistance surveillance, conventional HIV genotyping relies mainly on Sanger sequencing of consensus genomes to identify resistance mutations; however, this approach cannot detect mutations present at low frequencies within viral populations [25]. Although NGS can detect numerous low-frequency resistance mutations, it remains limited by interpretive ambiguity and, critically, the lack of information on mutational linkage [26]. To address these limitations, we combined NGS with haplotype reconstruction for resistance analysis. By recovering near-complete within-host viral haplotypes from short-read data, this approach preserves linkage among mutations and clarifies whether multiple substitutions coexist on the same genome, thereby reducing ambiguity in interpreting minority variants [11]. Compared with consensus-based genotyping, haplotype-based resistance calling detected a larger number of putative resistance sites and allowed estimation of their within-host proportions. When used to assess resistance burden, haplotype analysis identified additional samples with resistant phenotypes and revealed a more heterogeneous, clustered pattern of resistance levels. Establishing haplotypes also enabled early recognition of pretreatment drug resistance (PDR), underscoring the importance of incorporating haplotype reconstruction into national HIV drug-resistance surveillance. Integrating resistance data with molecular transmission networks revealed pronounced clustering of transmissible drug resistance within specific clusters, providing new insights into resistance spread through HIV networks. Conventional molecular network analyses, which rely on consensus sequences, can describe population-level transmission characteristics but fail to capture low-frequency mutations and the within-host heterogeneity of viral quasispecies, leading to an incomplete understanding of individual resistance profiles [27]. By applying haplotype reconstruction [11], we recovered near-complete viral haplotypes and characterized resistance patterns at the individual level. In two large genetic similarity-based clusters, five different NNRTI resistance mutations (K103N, V179D, V108I, E138A, and V179E) were found. In contrast to single-sequence methods, haplotype comparisons revealed high levels of intrahost heterogeneity, indicating that multiple resistance mutations can coexist within a single individual’s viral population. Several haplotypes were directly linked to sequences in the DRVI database, suggesting ongoing dissemination of resistant variants across networks. These findings indicate that transmissible drug resistance operates through more complex dynamics than previously recognized.

This study presents several limitations. Firstly, the use of targeted convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of our results. Therefore, interpretations of network connectivity should primarily reflect the analytical performance of haplotype-based methods rather than national transmission dynamics. Secondly, genetic similarity networks are unable to determine the direction, timing, or level of infectiousness at the time of sampling, and certain additional links identified through haplotypes may represent background homologous matching noise, necessitating epidemiological validation. Thirdly, the observed diversity differences might be influenced by the stage of infection, and data on viral load were unavailable due to the insufficient remaining plasma volume post-sequencing. Finally, the diversity analysis was modest in size (n = 79) and excluded several samples due to limited haplotype reconstruction, potential biases in the estimation of dN/dS ratios, and the introduction of selection bias. Additionally, residual sequencing errors may have impacted the accuracy of low-frequency variant detection [28,29], and the analyses were confined to the pol gene.

Despite these limitations, our findings have important implications for HIV surveillance. The observation that integrating haplotypes substantially increased linkage to the national DRVI database suggests that broader or more representative sampling could facilitate the uncovering of additional genetic connections. This highlights the scalability and potential utility of this approach for future nationwide surveillance efforts. At the national level, we recommend progressively incorporating haplotype-based analyses into China’s molecular surveillance for drug resistance and tracking of genetic similarity-based clusters. Molecular profiles characterized by higher viral diversity and reduced selective pressure may provide valuable insights for surveillance, as they were associated with greater network connectivity in our haplotype-based genetic similarity networks and were more prevalent among long-term infections as identified by LAg-Avidity EIA. However, these characteristics, on their own, do not confirm active onward transmission. Public health strategies should instead be informed by evidence of recent transmission, such as recent infection status, viral load, clustering, and epidemiological investigation. Future research should broaden sampling to include more diverse populations and geographic regions to assess the generalizability of these findings. Where available, integrating epidemiological contact tracing or social network data with molecular analyses could enable multilayered inference and help clarify transmission dynamics.

In summary, integrating haplotype-level analysis with the national DRVI database has enhanced the resolution of HIV molecular surveillance by identifying finer-scale genetic linkages and low-frequency drug resistance mutations that were overlooked by consensus sequences. This integrated approach also provides evolutionary context for interpreting molecular network structures, as individuals with higher intrahost viral diversity and weaker selective pressure were more likely to occupy highly connected network regions. Nevertheless, these metrics should be evaluated in conjunction with recent infection status, epidemiological information, and clinical data, rather than being used in isolation to infer current transmission activity or determine public health priorities. This integrative framework highlights the benefits of combining intrahost haplotype resolution with national database resources for precise HIV surveillance and prevention.
