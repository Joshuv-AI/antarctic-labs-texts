Examining the impact of emotion-driven resource allocation on information-behavior-disease coupling dynamics
Authors: Yue Yu, Liang'an Huo, Nan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn public emergency management, both medical and informational resources play crucial roles in shaping disease spreading dynamics. To enrich the literature on resource allocation in the context of disease spread, this paper proposes a mUAU-DKD-rSIS model to investigate how emotion-driven resource allocation influences the coupled evolution of rumor dissemination, panic-buying behavior adoption, and disease spreading in multiplex networks. First, we examine the impact of emotional heterogeneity on resource allocation, hypothesizing that anxious individuals tend to provide more informational resources but fewer medical resources than calm individuals. Second, to capture the functional heterogeneity of resources across different individual states, informational resources are classified into learning and review types, while medical resources are divided into protective and curative categories. Third, the source of resources is extended from the individual level to the group level by incorporating common pool resources. Using the microscopic Markov chain approach (MMCA) and numerical simulations, our analysis shows that rational resource allocation is essential for effective disease control. Notably, learning informational resources give rise to a meta-critical point that modulates the coupling strength in multiplex networks. In contrast, curative medical resources exhibit a second-order phase transition and an optimal utilization rate. These findings provide theoretical insights that may inform resource allocation strategies in public health emergencies.Keywords: Resource allocation, Emotional heterogeneity, Epidemic transmission, Multiplex networks

Introduction

Disease spreading poses a significant threat to public health and social stability, propagating rapidly through interpersonal interactions and affecting large-scale populations (Adrian et al., 2025). The COVID-19 pandemic has vividly demonstrated the far-reaching consequences of highly transmissible infectious diseases, leading to global health crises, economic disruption, and social instability (Kacer et al., 2025).

To investigate disease spreading dynamics, extensive research has been devoted to mathematical modeling of epidemic processes. The SIS (Susceptible–Infectious–Susceptible) model, introduced in 1927, provides a foundational framework for describing diseases that do not confer lasting immunity after infection, such as the common cold and certain sexually transmitted infections (Kermack & McKendrick, 1927). Building upon the SIS model, numerous extensions have been proposed to capture more complex epidemiological features, thereby enriching the theoretical foundation of disease modeling (Hethcote, 2000). With advances in network science, populations can be represented as complex networks composed of nodes and edges, enabling the incorporation of heterogeneous contact patterns into classical epidemiological models (Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani, 2001). By integrating network structures and interaction mechanisms, the UAU (Unaware–Aware–Unaware)–SIS model has been developed to describe the interplay between information awareness and disease spreading. This framework reveals rich phase transitions and critical phenomena induced by information dissemination and provides an effective approach for extending epidemic modeling from single-layer networks to multiplex networks (Granell et al., 2013).

In modern society, information spreads rapidly through social media, exerting a significant influence on public health behaviors and epidemic dynamics (Chang et al., 2025). Accurate information dissemination can enhance awareness and motivate individuals to adopt preventive measures, such as mask-wearing and regular hand hygiene, thereby reducing disease spreading (Zhang, Zhu, & Wang, 2025). However, disease outbreaks are often accompanied by the widespread diffusion of rumors and misinformation (Liu et al., 2024). Previous studies have shown that individuals influenced by rumors may adopt inappropriate behaviors that increase their vulnerability to infection (Huang et al., 2021). Our earlier work further highlighted the importance of suppressing rumor-related behavioral adoption during epidemics (Yu & Huo, 2025). To model rumor dissemination dynamics, Li et al. (Li et al., 2025) proposed the SIRDR (Susceptible–Infective–Remover–Debunker–Remover) model to capture both rumor dissemination and debunking processes. Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2025) further incorporated higher-order interactions by developing a dynamic rumor dissemination model on simplicial complexes, accounting for heterogeneous groups and counter-rumor mechanisms.

Resource allocation has attracted increasing attention in the study of disease spreading dynamics, as different distribution strategies can substantially influence disease control outcomes. In general, infected individuals rely on medical resources for recovery, whereas susceptible individuals can use such resources to protect their health and contribute to the sustainable availability of medical resources (Ferguson et al., 2006). Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2021) highlighted the dual role of medical resources in disease prevention and treatment, demonstrating that both protective and curative medical resources significantly affect disease spreading dynamics. Zhu et al. (Zhu et al., 2022) further examined the impact of latent infections on resource allocation processes, particularly in relation to information dissemination. In addition, Zhang et al. (Zhang, Hong, et al., 2025) integrated resource allocation mechanisms into population networks, revealing that excessive emphasis on resource acquisition alone may be insufficient for disease control, especially under conditions of widespread panic.

Although existing studies have provided valuable insights into the role of medical resources, information transmission can also be regarded as a form of resource allocation. In information theory, information entropy, introduced by Claude Shannon, is used to quantify the richness and uncertainty of information (Shannon, 1948). A system with higher entropy contains more information content, reflecting a greater diversity of states (Jaynes, 1957). From this perspective, information dissemination can be interpreted as a process that increases information entropy and is functionally analogous to resource allocation. Based on this idea, Huo et al. (Huo & Zhao, 2025) introduced the concept of informational resources to investigate the joint effects of informational and medical resources on disease spreading. Their results showed that both types of resources contribute to raising the epidemic threshold and reducing infection rates, with medical resources generally exerting a stronger effect than informational resources. However, the functional heterogeneity of resources, particularly the distinction between different types of informational and medical resources, remains insufficiently explored.

Moreover, in public health emergencies, emotions play a crucial role in shaping rumor dissemination, behavioral decisions, and resource allocation (Erdős & Rényi, 1959). Anxious emotions can amplify rumor spreading, thereby increasing public panic and undermining the acceptance and implementation of public health measures. Panic-buying has emerged as a prominent behavioral response during disease outbreaks, driven by uncertainty regarding health risks and the availability of essential goods. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many individuals rushed to supermarkets and pharmacies to stockpile necessities, resulting in shortages of certain commodities and further exacerbating social anxiety (Chen, Li, & Li, 2024; Sarkar et al., 2025). In addition, under conditions of resource scarcity, anxious individuals are often reluctant to share medical resources with others due to self-protective instincts. Such behavior can intensify pressure on healthcare systems and reduce the overall effectiveness of public health interventions. Furthermore, growing attention has been paid to the role of mental health in shaping individual behavior, as emotional states influence not only decision-making processes but also information processing and social interactions (Keyes, 2006). Guo et al. (Guo et al., 2015) incorporated the herd effect into information dissemination models using a Heaviside step function to capture abrupt behavioral changes, while the Cognitive–Emotional Model (CEM) emphasizes the interaction between emotion and cognition in shaping individual decisions (Hasking et al., 2017).

Panic-buying behaviors fundamentally challenge the sustainability of common pool resources (CPR). The concept of CPR, developed by Elinor Ostrom, refers to shared resources that are open to common use but subject to competition due to their finite nature (Islam et al., 2021). From a group-level perspective, CPR are characterized by non-excludability and rivalrous consumption, meaning that excessive use by some individuals reduces availability for others (Shan & Pi, 2023). During disease outbreaks, essential medical supplies such as masks and disinfectants are typically accessible to individuals through shared markets or supply systems (Sim et al., 2020). However, panic-buying behavior can severely undermine such distribution mechanisms. Under conditions of uncertainty and perceived scarcity, individuals may hoard resources, leading to overconsumption by some and shortages for others. This irrational behavior not only exacerbates competition for shared resources but also destabilizes market mechanisms and undermines social fairness. Consequently, effective management of common pool resources during public health emergencies has become an urgent and challenging issue.

Based on the research background outlined above, modeling efforts on emotion-driven panic-buying behavior within the domain of resource allocation remain underexplored. While previous studies, such as Zhang et al. (Yu & Huo, 2025), incorporated resource allocation into epidemic networks and highlighted the disruptive role of panic behaviors, and Huo et al. (Huo & Zhao, 2025) introduced the concept of informational resources to examine their joint effects with medical resources, these approaches largely treated resources as functionally homogeneous and modeled behavioral responses as exogenous factors. First, in studies of rumor dissemination processes, most existing research has neglected how the distribution of informational and medical resources shapes the coupled propagation dynamics. Second, in behavioral decision-making, relatively few studies have considered the impact of panic-buying behavior on resource allocation, particularly under conditions of limited common pool resources. Finally, many studies have overlooked emotions as a key driver of individual decision-making and resource-sharing behaviors. To address these gaps, this paper introduces an emotion-driven resource allocation model to explore its impact on the coupled propagation dynamics of rumor dissemination, panic-buying behavior adoption, and disease spreading. The mUAU-DKD-rSIS model

Disease spreading is influenced not only by rumors and behavioral responses but also by the allocation of resources. In rumor dissemination, individuals exhibit heterogeneous abilities to spread and retain information due to differences in their knowledge levels. To capture this heterogeneity, we introduce the concept of rumor-related informational resources, which quantify the amount of rumor-related knowledge possessed by an individual. Similarly, in disease spreading processes, access to medical resources plays a crucial role in maintaining individual health. Importantly, the effects of resources are state dependent. For example, as rumor-related informational resources increase, unaware individuals become more susceptible to believing and spreading rumors, whereas aware individuals tend to reinforce their belief in the rumor. Analogously, greater medical resources enhance protection for susceptible individuals and facilitate recovery for infected individuals. In addition, emotional states further modulate individual decision-making: anxious individuals are generally more susceptible to rumors and panic behaviors, while calm individuals tend to make more rational choices. Due to the differences in social background, living habits, physiological conditions and psychological factors, individuals may exhibit heterogeneous emotional responses when facing diseases (Zhao et al., 2024). To capture such emotional heterogeneity, the population is categorized into two distinct emotional states: calm (c) and anxious (a). We assume that a proportion m of the population is anxious, while the remaining proportion 1−m is calm. Moreover, individuals’ emotional states are allowed to be influenced by their health status in the disease spreading layer. Informational resource allocation with emotional heterogeneity

In the rumor dissemination layer, we introduce the concept of informational resource, which quantify the cumulative influence of rumor received by individuals. Unlike medical resources, informational resources are virtual and non-depletable in that their transmission does not reduce the amount possessed by the disseminator.

At each time step, when an individual becomes aware of the rumor, one unit of informational resource is generated, representing the cognitive effort devoted to processing and spreading the rumor. According to the self-exhaustion theory (Liu & He, 2021), individuals have limited energy for rumor dissemination. Moreover, emotional arousal has been empirically shown to amplify risk communication and increase individuals’ tendency to share threat-related information (Welton-Mitchell et al., 2023). Motivated by this behavioral evidence, we contend that the impact of emotional heterogeneity among individuals affects the allocation of informational resources, based on the following assumptions:

(1)Anxious individuals actively disseminate informational resources to all their neighbors, reflecting heightened alertness and risk amplification.

Anxious individuals actively disseminate informational resources to all their neighbors, reflecting heightened alertness and risk amplification.

(2)Calm individuals selectively transmit informational resources only to neighbors who are unaware of the rumor, representing a more rational and restrained communication pattern.

Calm individuals selectively transmit informational resources only to neighbors who are unaware of the rumor, representing a more rational and restrained communication pattern.

For informational resources, utilization reflects cognitive engagement and information-processing processes. Medical resource allocation with emotional heterogeneity

In the medical resource allocation process, we assume that susceptible individuals produce one unit of medical resource at each time step t, whereas infected individuals do not produce resource. To mitigate disease spreading, each susceptible individual allocates a portion of the produced resource for sharing, distributing them among the individual and its neighbors. Empirical studies indicate that anxiety under perceived threat is often associated with self-protective decision-making and a greater tendency toward resource retention (Chen, Jin, et al., 2024). Motivated by this behavioral finding, we further assume that the amount of resources an individual is willing to share depends on both their emotional state and their awareness of rumors, leading to heterogeneous donation behaviors characterized as follows:

Coupled node states

Based on the above assumptions, each individual is characterized by the combination of emotional state, awareness state, behavioral state and infection state, leading to 16 possible coupled states in the mUAU–DKD–rSIS model: UaDS, UaDI, AaDS, AaDI, UaKS, UaKI, AaKS, AaKI, UcDS, UcDI, AcDS, AcDI, UcKS, UcKI, AcKS, AcKI.

For the informational response under infection, in real-world scenarios, infected individuals tend to actively seek disease-related information, which increases their exposure to unverified or misleading content and makes them more susceptible to rumor propagation (Paulus et al., 2024). To explain this mechanism, we draw on cognitive bias theory (Nickerson, 1998; Tversky & Kahneman, 1973), which states that individuals’ judgments systematically deviate from rational norms during information processing, often relying on heuristics that may lead to biased decisions. Therefore, we assume that once an individual becomes infected, they immediately become aware of the rumor, i.e., UaDI, UaKI are assumed to become AaDI and AaKI with probability 1; UcDI and UcKI are assumed to become AcDI and AcKI with probability 1.

For the behavioral response under infection and emotional heterogeneity, individual in anxiety state who knows the rumor will adopt panic-buying behavior with probability 1, i.e., AaDS will become AaKS with probability 1. In addition, infected individuals are typically subject to mobility limitations such as quarantine or self-isolation (Xiong et al., 2020). Thus, they are restricted from actively accessing common-pool resources, meaning that infected individuals cannot initiate resource acquisition behaviors, i.e., AaKI and AcKI are assumed to become AaDI and AcDI with probability 1.

For the emotional response under infection, infected individuals are more prone to emotional fluctuations and heightened anxiety due to perceived health risks (Chen, Jin, et al., 2024; Welton-Mitchell et al., 2023). To capture the dynamic interaction between disease spreading and emotional response, we introduce the emotion fluctuation intensity ξ, which quantifies the sensitivity of emotional states to infection. Specifically, when a susceptible individual becomes infected, the emotional state may transition from calm to anxious with probability ξ, while remaining unchanged with probability 1−ξ. Larger values of ξ indicate stronger emotional sensitivity to disease conditions, i.e., AcDI is assumed to become AaDI with probability ξ.

Because transitions above are deterministic (probability 1) or behaviorally constrained, several theoretical combinations cannot persist dynamically. After eliminating unreachable or transient configurations, the system reduces to 9 effective node states, namely UaDS, AaDI, UaKS, AaKS, UcDS, AcDS, AcDI, UcKS and AcKS.

Numerical simulations

Comparison of different network structures with real network dataset

To illustrate the correspondence between simulation results and real-world network conditions, Fig. 7 compares the outcomes across various network structures with an empirical dataset. This dataset, derived from the SocioPatterns infrastructure (Mastrandrea et al., 2015), captures contact and friendship relations among students at a French high school in Marseille during December 2013. At the information layer, interaction data is derived from Facebook friend lists. Similarly, at the behavioral layer, interaction data reflects real-life friendship relationships, as individuals often adapt their behavior based on friends' decisions. To construct the multiplex network model, we ensured one-to-one node correspondence across layers. Additional nodes were incorporated to preserve structural consistency. For disease layer, we incorporated heterogeneity by including Barabási-Albert (BA) networks (Barabási & Albert, 1999) (new nodes added 5 edges), Erdős-Rényi (ER) networks (Erdős & Rényi, 1959) (connection probability 0.002), and Watts-Strogatz (WS) networks (Watts & Strogatz, 1998) (each node initially connected to 4 neighbors, reconnection probability 1). As Fig. 7 shows, the BA network model demonstrates the closest fit to the real data. This alignment highlights the heavy-tailed effects inherent in real-world interactions, which correspond to the power-law rule used in BA network construction.

Due to the complexity and uncertainty of disease spreading within real network, it is challenging to obtain specific details of the transmission process during disease outbreak. To derive conclusions with broader applicability, we select the BA network, which exhibits a good fit with the actual network data in Fig. 7, for subsequent simulations. The network consists of 1000 nodes, and to ensure connectivity, the average network degree is set as <kV>=<kR>=6, based on the principle of six degrees of separation (Samoylenko et al., 2023). It is assumed that the topology of informational resources allocation is the same as that of the rumor dissemination layer, while the topology of medical resource allocation aligns with that of the disease spreading layer. Parameter robustness was examined by allowing the principal parameters to vary within the ranges commonly reported in the reference, as summarized in Table 1. The impact of each parameter on epidemic spreading is examined using a controlled-variable framework, in which one parameter is varied while the others remain fixed. The analysis focuses on two key outcomes: the disease outbreak threshold βc and the fraction of infected individuals ρI. In addition, as indicated by Eq. (41), the correlation between the disease infection rate and the quantity of medical resources μ significantly affects the disease outbreak threshold. Understanding the relationship between the disease recovery process and the availability of medical resources is a crucial factor that warrants further exploration in resource-related studies.

The effect of the resource allocation process

To investigate the different roles that the same type of resource can play for individuals in various states on the dynamics of disease spreading, we categorizes the purpose of informational resources into learning and review informational resources, while classifying the use of medical resources into protective and curative medical resources.

(1)The effect of the informational resources allocation process

The effect of the informational resources allocation process

First, we explore how different applications of informational resources influence disease spreading. Fig. 8 illustrates how learning informational resource utilization affects the fraction of infected individuals, while Fig. 9 focuses on the role of review informational resource utilization. Specifically, we consider not only the type of informational resource, but also how effectively individuals translate received information into cognitive or behavioral responses, which is quantified in the model by the informational resource utilization rate. In the case of learning informational resource acquisition, the situation illustrated shows that when an individual lacks awareness of disease-related rumor, their neighbor can supply the necessary informational resources. As the unaware individual gradually accumulates their own informational resources, the likelihood of them believing the rumor increases (as shown in Eq. (3)). The learning informational resource utilization rate represents the probability that an individual actively processes, evaluates, and internalizes newly obtained information. In contrast, review informational resources operate when an individual who is already aware of the disease-related rumor. In this case, when a neighbor donates informational resources, the aware individual's own resources gradually accumulate, enhancing their impression of the information (as shown in Eq. (4)). The review informational resource utilization rate measures the likelihood that individuals reinforce their prior beliefs through repeated confirmation, such as re-sharing, discussing, or reinterpreting the same information. In this case, the role of the informational resources is to reinforce and review information that is already known.

Fig. 8 highlights a meta-critical point concerning how informational resources impact disease spreading. This meta-critical point corresponds to a coupling threshold at which the rumor dissemination layer begins to exert a nonlinear influence on disease spreading dynamics. A lower learning informational resources utilization rate implies that individuals do not automatically internalize newly received rumors but instead exhibit selective cognitive processing before acceptance. Consequently, for individuals who are initially unaware, as this utilization rate ε11 decreases, their susceptibility to rumors decreases. This means that they become better equipped to recognize the harmful effects of rumors in a timely manner, thereby reducing the likelihood of being compelled by misleading information. Accordingly, a higher transmission intensity is required to trigger large-scale outbreaks, and the steady-state infection level decreases. In additional, the different subplots of Fig. 8 highlight that as the difficulty of disease recovery increases (i.e., as μ increases), the role of informational resources becomes increasingly crucial. This suggests that when medical recovery becomes less effective, cognitive resistance to misinformation plays a more dominant role in mitigating epidemic spread. More specifically, the model implies that informational interventions should aim to push the system across the identified coupling threshold. Early-stage policies should prioritize strengthening individuals’ capacity for critical information processing, whereas later-stage strategies should focus on reducing exposure to repetitive rumor amplification. Fig. 9 further explores the impact of review informational resource utilization rate on disease spreading. Unlike learning resources, review informational resources utilization rate represents the probability that individuals reinforce or reassess previously accepted information when repeatedly exposed to similar content. The figure indicates that as ε12 decreases, individuals' trust in rumors weakens, allowing them to more quickly realize the harm posed by rumors and consequently refrain from disseminating it. This has significant implications for raising the disease outbreak threshold and reducing the fraction of infected individuals. The results suggest that debunking campaigns are effective when targeted at limiting repeated exposure.

The effect of the medical resource allocation process

In this section, Fig. 10 illustrates how the utilization of protective medical resources affects the proportion of infected individuals across various levels of disease spreading. The utilization rate of medical resources represents the probability that individuals successfully translate available medical support into concrete health-related actions. Specifically, protective utilization corresponds to the likelihood that susceptible individuals adopt preventive measures, while curative utilization reflects the probability that infected individuals obtain timely and effective treatment. Fig. 10 illustrates the impact of protective medical resources on the phase transition of disease spreading. The observed phase transition underscores the importance of individuals utilizing resources for self-protection. As the utilization of protective medical resources ε21 increases, individuals' self-protection levels strengthen, thereby significantly reducing their probability of infection. These results suggest that preventive measures adopted before infection can play a meaningful role in limiting transmission and contribute to the overall control of disease spread.

Fig. 11 illustrates that as the utilization rate of curative medical resource ε22 increases, the disease outbreak threshold initially rises and then declines. This non-monotonic pattern indicates the presence of an intermediate utilization level at which disease control is most effective. Previous studies have reported the existence of an optimal level of medical resource utilization for suppressing epidemics (as illustrated in Fig. 7 of Ref (Huo & Zhao, 2025).). However, these studies generally did not distinguish between protective and curative components. The findings of Fig. 11 indicate that this optimal utilization rate of medical resources is driven by curative medical resources. When curative resources are underutilized, infected individuals may not receive timely and adequate treatment, prolong infectious periods and facilitate further disease spreading. For example, in the initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic, healthcare systems were under immense strain, facing shortages of hospital beds and ventilators, which resulted in critically ill patients being unable to receive timely treatment and consequently led to higher fatality rates. Conversely, excessive utilization of curative medical resources may also introduce risks, chiefly in the form of inefficient allocation and potential wastage, which can in turn limit the availability of care for patients with urgent needs. Therefore, epidemic management should not aim to maximize curative utilization indiscriminately, but rather to maintain it near the optimal operational range identified by the model.

The effect of the individuals' emotional heterogeneity

We hypothesize that individual emotional heterogeneity has two significant effects: the degree to which individuals are influenced by the herd behavior arising from their neighbors' environments and their willingness to donate resources. Compared with calm individuals, anxious individuals are assumed to be more responsive to their surroundings and therefore more likely to engage in panic-buying behavior (see Assumption 3). They are also more inclined to share informational resources (see Assumption 5), while tending to retain medical resources for self-protection rather than donating them rather than donate them (see Assumption 7). Fig. 12 illustrates the impact of individual emotional heterogeneity on disease spreading, where a higher m indicates a greater proportion of anxious individuals within the population. The phase transition shown in the figure indicates that higher levels of anxiety are associated with a lower outbreak threshold and more extensive disease spreading. On the one hand, anxious individuals are more likely to engage in panic-buying behaviors, which may lead to increased crowding and consequently elevate the risk of infection. On the other hand, anxiety can disrupt the rational use and distribution of healthcare resources, potentially delaying timely treatment for infected individuals and thereby worsening overall infection levels in society.

Conclusions

Main findings

A novel mUAU-DKD-rSIS model was developed to examine how emotion-driven resource allocation affects disease spreading in multiplex networks. The model hypothesized that anxiety promoted the allocation of informational resources while hindering the allocation of medical resources. Within the allocation framework, informational resources were classified into learning and review resources, whereas medical resources were divided into protective and curative types. In addition, panic-buying behaviors and common pool resources were incorporated to explore the interplay between individual-level optimization and group-level resource allocation during disease spreading. The principal findings are summarized as follows.

(1)Strengthening the clarification of informational resources in context of rumor dissemination and reducing their utilization can help to suppress the disease spreading. The phase transitions related to learning and review informational resources are both first-order transitions. Moreover, the presence of a meta-critical point influences the coupling of multiplex networks in the context of learning informational resources.

(2)Increasing the utilization of medical resources and promoting their optimal allocation contribute to improved disease control. Limitations and future research

Despite providing useful insights, this study also opens several avenues for further research. First, the model considered a static network structure. In reality, population mobility continuously reshapes contact patterns, which may substantially alter both spreading dynamics and resource allocation mechanisms. Second, interactions within each layer were assumed to follow homogeneous mixing. In practice, heterogeneities in resource availability, individual characteristics, and social connectivity can significantly influence interaction intensity and behavioral responses. Third, the present framework was built upon a baseline SIS epidemic process. Extending the model to more realistic epidemic dynamics (e.g., SIR and SEIR model) incorporating multiple information or interacting diseases would better approximate real-world scenarios. Moreover, with the advancement of higher-order networks, it is worthwhile to further investigate the differences in resource allocation mechanisms between pairwise interactions and higher-order interactions. In addition, empirical validation using real crisis data (e.g., records of COVID-19 resource distribution and documented panic-buying events) would help assess the model's applicability and refine its assumptions. These directions will contribute to a deeper understanding of the asymmetric roles played by informational resources and medical resources in shaping disease dynamics.
