Application of liver biopsy in etiological diagnosis of unexplained portal hypertension: Porto-sinusoidal vascular disease should not be ignored
Authors: Yu Zhang, Hongli Liu, Qingfang Xiong, Yandan Zhong, Duxian Liu, Wei Chen, Yongfeng Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The diagnostic value of liver biopsy has been confirmed in patients with abnormal liver test results; however, little data are available on its application in patients with portal hypertension. This study aimed to investigate the utility of liver biopsy for the etiological diagnosis of unexplained portal hypertension, and explore the clinical and pathological characteristics of each etiology. A retrospective observational analysis was conducted on 1367 patients who underwent liver biopsy at the Second Hospital of Nanjing from 2017 to 2019. Of these, 188 patients with unexplained portal hypertension were enrolled. The clinical and pathological characteristics were collected and reassessed in a multidisciplinary team meeting. Among these patients, 174 (92.6%, 174/188) had a definite etiological diagnosis through liver biopsy. The main etiologies were autoimmune hepatitis in 47 patients (25%, 47/188), autoimmune hepatitis-primary biliary cirrhosis overlap syndrome in 41 patients (21.8%, 41/188), and porto-sinusoidal vascular disease (PSVD) in 40 patients (21.3%, 40/188). Compared to liver cirrhosis, PSVD patients were younger and the liver function damage of which was subtler. The widths of portal vein diameter were widest in PSVD but the liver stiffness measurement were almost normal. Splenomegaly was common in PSVD, but ascites were less frequent than in autoimmune hepatitis (25.0% vs 51.1%, P = .013). Based on the histological patterns, we found that cholestatic liver diseases such as primary biliary cirrhosis, autoimmune hepatitis-primary biliary cirrhosis overlap syndrome, and progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis could lead to non-cirrhotic portal hypertension, while vascular liver diseases such as PSVD and Budd-Chiari syndrome could also show fibrous proliferation as the disease progresses. Liver biopsy is safe and valuable for etiological diagnosis of unexplained portal hypertension. Cirrhosis is the leading cause of portal hypertension, and porto-sinusoidal vascular diseases should also be considered. Clinical features may be helpful in suggesting the cause; however, pathological examination is still indispensable for disease diagnosis and progression assessment.

1. Introduction

Portal hypertension (PH) is a common and serious clinical syndrome characterized by hemodynamic abnormalities of the portal venous system. Esophagogastric variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, and ascites are serious complications of PH that often result in liver transplantation or death.[1] Liver cirrhosis is the leading cause of portal hypertension and end-stage chronic liver disease. Alcoholic steatohepatitis is the leading cause of cirrhosis in western countries and India, whereas chronic hepatitis B and C are the main causes of cirrhosis in Asia. Non-cirrhotic portal hypertension (NCPH) accounts for approximately 10% to 20% of portal hypertension.[2] Porto-sinusoidal vascular disease (PSVD) and extrahepatic portal vein thrombosis are the most common etiologies of NCPH and are characterized by obvious symptoms of portal hypertension, but almost normal liver function.[3,4]

Porto-sinusoidal vascular disease is a rare vascular liver disease of unknown etiology characterized by the absence of cirrhosis with or without typical pathological changes or portal hypertension.[5] Distinguishing PSVD from cirrhosis is difficult owing to their similar clinical presentations and imaging features. The incidence of PSVD is often underestimated owing to insufficient understanding and the absence of any suspicion among clinicians and radiologists. Studies have shown that 5.4% of cryptogenic cirrhosis patients are diagnosed with PSVD.[6] In recent years, with the gradual popularity of liver punctures and increased awareness of this disease, the diagnostic rate has also increased.[3]

Accurate etiological diagnosis is a prerequisite for the development of an effective treatment strategy. Although several invasive methods have been used to diagnose portal hypertension, some patients fail to obtain a definitive etiological diagnosis after thorough and complete physical and auxiliary examinations. There have been limited studies on the etiology of PH. In a previous study including 295 cases of esophageal varices, over 50% of the patients were diagnosed with viral hepatitis cirrhosis, alcoholic cirrhosis, and primary biliary cirrhosis (PBC), 12.5% with NCPH, and 26.1% with no clear etiological diagnosis.[2]

Liver biopsy is an invasive procedure with the risk of bleeding, infection, or perforation, which limits its clinical application. The value of liver biopsy in the diagnosis of abnormal liver test results has been demonstrated in previous clinical studies. In a study of 383 patients with abnormal liver test results, 87% had a clinical etiological diagnosis by liver biopsy, and severe complications occurred in 1.6% of patients.[7] Another study including 354 patients with unexplained abnormal liver biochemistry reported that liver biopsy led to a definitive diagnosis in 94% of patients, and altered patient management directly in 18% of patients.[8] However, the efficacy and safety of liver biopsy have not been evaluated in patients with portal hypertension.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed patients with unexplained portal hypertension who underwent liver biopsy. All patients included in this study had undergone complete serological examinations, such as liver function, hepatitis biomarkers, autoantibody series, immunoglobulins, iron metabolism, serum copper and ceruloplasmin, as well as imaging examinations such as ultrasound or CT. Patients who had obtained a definite diagnosis through noninvasive examinations, such as viral hepatitis, antimitochondrial antibody (AMA)-positive PBC, or portal vein thrombosis, were excluded from our study. At this point, histopathological examination is critical for accurate diagnosis. We aimed to evaluate the clinical utility and safety of liver biopsy in the diagnosis of portal hypertension and explore the etiological spectrum of unexplained PH. At the same time, we analyzed the clinical and pathological characteristics of each etiology to provide reference for clinical diagnosis.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Participants

This was a single-center, retrospective study. A total of 1367 consecutive patients who underwent liver biopsy at the Second Hospital of Nanjing between January 2017 and December 2019 were retrospectively evaluated. Complete medical records were collected, including demographics, clinical signs and symptoms, laboratory investigations (complete blood count, liver function, coagulation tests, hepatitis biomarkers, autoantibody series, immunoglobulin, iron metabolism, serum copper, and copper blue protein levels), imaging characteristics, and histopathological findings. A total of 279 patients who underwent a liver biopsy for portal hypertension were included [Fig. 1]. Although hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) is the gold standard for diagnosing portal hypertension, due to the small number of patients undergoing HVPG testing, we used clinical manifestations of portal hypertension as an alternative inclusion standard. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) imaging findings indicating splenomegaly, esophagogastric varices, or portovenous collaterals; (2) clinical evidence of nonmalignant ascites; and (3) a history of bleeding from esophageal or gastric varices. Of these patients, 91 were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) clear etiological diagnosis before biopsy, (2) incomplete clinical data, and (3) insufficient liver tissue. To evaluate the diagnostic utility of liver biopsy in patients with unknown causes, patients with a known etiology before biopsy, such as chronic viral hepatitis, PBC, extrahepatic portal venous obstruction, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, or Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS) were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Nanjing (no. 2021-LY-kt078).

Flow chart of patients included.

2.2. Diagnostic procedure

At our center, ultrasound-guided percutaneous liver biopsy was performed in most patients. Two liver tissues with a length of approximately 15 mm were obtained through 2 repeated punctures along different directions using a 16G needle. Hematoxylin and eosin, Masson trichrome, Periodic Acid-Schiff, periodic acid-Schiff diastase, and reticular fiber staining were routinely performed. Pathology sections were routinely reevaluated by expert pathologists and then officially assessed in a multidisciplinary team meeting by an expert panel comprising 4 experienced hepatologists, 1 hepatopathologist, and 1 imaging expert. The final clinical diagnosis was determined by an expert panel that combined the clinical characteristics and pathology reports.

2.3. Diagnostic criteria

Cirrhosis was defined as extensive liver fibrosis, and normal liver architecture was replaced by diffuse nodular regeneration. Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) was diagnosed according to the 2019 guidelines of the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases and characterized by pathological manifestations such as interface hepatitis and plasma cell infiltration. Serological features include elevated transaminase and Immunoglobulin G levels and positive antinuclear antibody.[9] The diagnosis of autoimmune hepatitis-primary biliary cirrhosis overlap syndrome (AIH-PBC OS) was considered when typical bile duct lesions were observed in liver sections simultaneously. Increased ALT and GGT levels and positive AMA results also supported this diagnosis.[9] The diagnosis of PSVD was based on the 2019 consensus of the Vascular Liver Disease Group.[5] Patients with at least one specific sign of portal hypertension, one histological lesion specific for PSVD, one atypical sign of portal hypertension, and one nonspecific histological sign of PSVD, excluding cirrhosis or other conditions causing PH, were diagnosed with PSVD.[10] Three PSVD-specific histological lesions were identified: obliterative portal venopathy, nodular regenerative hyperplasia, and incomplete septal fibrosis or cirrhosis. The diagnosis of PSVD could not be rejected for the finding of portal vein thrombosis because patients with PSVD may frequently develop thrombosis in a hypercoagulable state.[11] Nonalcoholic steatohepatitis cirrhosis was diagnosed according to the 2018 guidelines from the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases, and was characterized by the presence of cirrhosis with current or previous histological evidence of steatosis or steatohepatitis.[12]

2.4. Data procession

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 24.0. Continuous data are expressed as median or mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), whereas count data are shown as number and rate (%). Comparisons were performed by Student t test for normally distributed data, and Wilcoxon rank-sum test for nonnormal data. Chi-square test were performed for enumeration data. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3. Results

Among the 1367 patients who underwent liver biopsy, 188 with unexplained portal hypertension were enrolled in this study, with 68 males (36.2%) and 120 females (63.8%). The mean age of the patients was 51.3 years [Table 1]. 3.4. Demographic, laboratory and imaging features

Demographic data revealed that patients with autoimmune liver disease were predominantly female, while patients with PSVD were predominantly male. Patients with PSVD were significantly younger than those with chronic hepatitis and cirrhosis.

We also assessed the laboratory and imaging data collected at the time of liver biopsy. Albumin levels were lower in patients with AIH and AIH-PBC OS, which reflects a decline in synthetic liver function. At the same time, direct bilirubin and aminotransferase levels were significantly higher in these patients, and cholestasis enzyme levels were higher in patients with PBC and AIH-PBC. The indices of the liver and coagulation function were normal or slightly abnormal in patients with PSVD. On ultrasound examination, controlled attenuation parameter values were significantly increased in patients with NAFLD and were largely normal in other patients. The liver stiffness measurement increased significantly in AIH, AIH-PBC OS, and NAFLD patients, slightly increased in PBC patients, and almost normal in PSVD patients. The portal vein diameter was the widest in patients with PSVD, and the liver was longer in patients with BCS, AIH-PBC OS, PBC, and PSVD. Of the 47 biopsies of AIH, 41 were predominantly progressive, according to the P-I-R score, and all biopsies showed a pathological pattern dominated by portal fibrosis. Eight cases were complicated with steatohepatitis and lobular fibrosis. Characteristic pathological features such as interface hepatitis with rich lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, hepatocellular rosette formation, and emperipolesis were observed in the biopsies (Fig. 2A and B). One patient underwent a second liver puncture after 3 years of hormone treatment, and the fibrosis reversed from progressive to indeterminate.

(C) AIH-PBC overlap syndrome: moderate interface inflammation and damaged bile duct with a dense chronic inflammatory infiltrate(H&E, ×40). Eight patients with PBC were not diagnosed before biopsy for a negative AMA. Among the 8 cases of PBC, 6 cases showed biliary cirrhosis characterized by irregularly shaped nodules and “halos” sign,[14] and 2 cases showed no cirrhosis. Lesions mainly affected the bile ducts with a diameter <100 μm, which was manifested by an epithelioid noncaseating granuloma form, with a large infiltration of inflammatory cells in the portal tract, sometimes called florid duct lesions. Deletion of the bile duct in a part of the portal tract is also an essential diagnostic change.

Among the 41 AIH-PBC OS biopsies, 35 were predominantly progressive, 2 were indeterminate, and the other 4 biopsies showed no pseudolobule formation. Twenty-six cases were mainly characterized by portal fibrosis, and 11 patients had biliary cirrhosis. The biopsy specimens of these patients presented with the typical features of AIH and PBC (Fig. 2C and D). The second liver puncture in one case showed that interface inflammation was relieved after treatment. The P-I-R classification was reversed and the fibrosis pattern changed from portal fibrosis to biliary cirrhosis.

Of the 2 biopsies of progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC), one showed biliary cirrhosis and the other did not. Diseases involving the bile duct, such as PBC, AIH-PBC, OS, and PFIC, can cause portal hypertension without liver cirrhosis.

Forty PSVD biopsies were performed in this study. 36 biopsies showed no cirrhosis, while 4 biopsies presented fibrous hyperplasia and formed pseudolobules, of which one was mainly portal fibrosis, one was mainly lobular fibrosis, and 2 were ischemic fibrous hyperplasia. Ischemic fibrous hyperplasia is characterized by the formation of sparse and wide fibrous septa, with no significant infiltration of inflammatory cells in the septa.[15] Nodular regenerative hyperplasia was observed in 2 PSVD biopsies. Obliterative portal venopathy is the main histological change in PSVD and is characterized by irregular thickening of the vessel wall, eccentric narrowing of the vessel lumen, and the absence of the portal vein. Another portal vein (normal in caliber or dilated) can abut upon the liver parenchyma but be in contact with the portal tract, called the herniated PV. Portal tract multiplication, periportal vascular channels, artery dilation, and sinusoidal dilation were observed as secondary changes in portal sclerosis (Fig. 3A–D).

Among the 11 BCS biopsies, 8 showed no liver cirrhosis and 3 showed reversed lobulation cirrhosis, defined as venocentric or venoportal cirrhosis.[14] Vascular liver diseases such as PSVD and BCS usually present as non-cirrhotic portal hypertension. However, fibrous proliferation and pseudolobule formation are observed as the disease progresses.

3.6. Liver biopsy complications

Complications following liver biopsy included pain in 12 (6.4%) patients and nausea in 1 patient. One patient experienced hypotension without progressive bleeding on ultrasonography that improved after intravenous fluid therapy. Only one (0.5%) experienced significant complications of hepatic arteriolar biliary fistula. The patient underwent an interventional procedure and was discharged after the improvement.

4. Discussion

We investigated the role of a liver biopsy in the evaluation of unexplained portal hypertension. The results showed that 92.6% of the patients received a final definitive diagnosis after liver biopsy. Meanwhile, liver biopsy could not only provide useful information for the etiological diagnosis, but also be considered as the most important diagnostic tool for the inflammation grading and fibrosis staging. It helped evaluate the severity of disease, and assisted in formulating individualized treatment plans. A second liver biopsy for patients after treatment could also help evaluate the treatment effect. Pain was the most common complication of percutaneous liver puncture examination, and mostly resolved within 24 hours. The incidence of serious complications such as bleeding, infection, and perforation, was about 0.5% and could be effectively controlled. Patients with contraindications such as coagulation dysfunction and ascites would undergo transjugular liver biopsy. Transjugular liver biopsy was confirmed to be safer, but resulted in smaller tissues, which required more careful operation and microscopic observation. In summary, liver biopsy plays an important role in the diagnosis of portal hypertension, owing to its high diagnostic yield and manageable complications.

In our study, autoimmune hepatitis was the leading cause of unexplained portal hypertension, with an average age of 56.7 years old, and was more common in females. As the early clinical symptoms of AIH are not obvious and patients rarely undergo regular physical examinations, approximately half of patients with AIH develop ascites when diagnosed and miss the optimal treatment opportunity. The diagnosis of AIH is challenging, especially in patients with negative autoimmune testing (i.e., negative AIH-related autoimmune antibodies, such as antinuclear antibody, ASMA, LKM-1, and SLA). Studies have indicated that there are no significant differences in the risk of developing cirrhosis and that the prognosis is similar between asymptomatic and symptomatic autoimmune hepatitis patients and symptomatic patients.[16] Therefore, after excluding viral hepatitis, liver biopsy is recommended for patients with liver dysfunction, particularly those with hepatocellular injury.

AIH-PBC overlap syndrome was the second most common cause of liver cirrhosis in this study. Serological and pathological changes in AIH and PBC have also been observed in these patients. AIH-PBC OS should be considered in AIH patients with significant cholestasis or elevated serum IgM levels as well as in PBC patients with apparent increases in ALT, AST, and immunoglobulin G levels. In addition, typical pathological manifestations such as severe interfacial inflammation and nonsuppurative cholangitis are important for the diagnosis of AIH-PBC OS.

Unexpectedly, non-cirrhotic portal hypertension was diagnosed in 66 of 188 (35.1%) patients, and the ratio of cirrhosis to NCPH was approximately 2:1. However, NCPH is often misdiagnosed as cirrhosis, owing to the clinical features of splenomegaly, ascites, and variceal bleeding. The diagnosis of liver cirrhosis in previous studies was mainly based on clinical presentation and imaging examinations but without pathological confirmation. The imaging diagnosis of liver cirrhosis was established based on abnormal liver morphology and manifestations of portal hypertension such as splenomegaly, portal vein widening, and collateral circulation formation. However, some non-cirrhotic liver diseases can also show similar imaging findings, which are difficult to distinguish from those of cirrhosis. Therefore, liver cirrhosis diagnosed using imaging may only represent the presence of portal hypertension.

PSVD accounted for 21.3% of cases in this study and was more frequent in males. Clinical data analysis indicated that liver function damage caused by PSVD was subtle. Pathological examination showed that lesions of portal vein branches were the main pathological changes of PSVD, and would not cause significant hepatocyte inflammation. PH features such as splenomegaly and esophagogastric varices were common, but ascites were less common than cirrhosis. Previous studies have also indicated that compared with cirrhosis, although the symptoms of PH are more severe, the overall prognosis is better in NCPH due to preserved liver function.[17] Therefore, compared to liver injury, clinicians should pay more attention to the progression of portal hypertension and the occurrence of complications related to portal hypertension in patients with PSVD. Regular screening of liver function, blood routine, abdominal CT, and gastroscopy for esophagogastric varices was necessary.

The liver stiffness value of PSVD is significantly lower than that of cirrhosis, which is helpful in distinguishing PSVD from other causes. Liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis, especially in patients with significant portal hypertension but without obvious abnormal liver function. However, Patients with histological features compatible with PSVD, but without clinical portal hypertension, were not enrolled in this study because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Therefore, the actual incidence of PSVD may increase.

Further exploration of the pathological tissues showed that diseases involving the bile ducts, such as PBC, AIH-PBC OS, and PFIC, may lead to portal hypertension in the absence of cirrhosis, which may be related to endothelial damage caused by cholestasis. Simultaneously, PSVD, BCS, and other vascular liver diseases may also appear as fibrous septa and pseudo-lobule formation with disease progression. Whether the progression of fibrosis changes the prognosis of patients remains to be explored further. In addition, secondary liver puncture in patients with AIH and AIH-PBC OS showed that liver cirrhosis can be reversed by effective treatment. Early diagnosis and treatment are important to improve the prognosis of patients with PH.

Ultimately, 8 patients did not receive a definitive diagnosis. First, advanced nonalcoholic steatohepatitis steatosis might be the most common cause of cryptogenic cirrhosis.[18] With improvements in living standards and the control of viral hepatitis in recent years, the proportion of nonalcoholic fatty cirrhosis has gradually increased.[19] However, steatosis gradually decreases during the development of cirrhosis; therefore, fatty cirrhosis is under-diagnosed in patients with advanced liver cirrhosis. Studies have shown that NAFLD is a potential cause of 19% to 63% of cryptogenic cirrhosis cases. NAFLD should be considered in patients with cryptogenic cirrhosis, particularly those with obesity, type 2 diabetes, or metabolic syndrome.[6,20] The presence of residual pathological signs of steatohepatitis, including cellular ballooning, focus of macrosteatosis, and glycogenated nuclei, may suggest a diagnosis.[21] Second, owing to the uneven distribution of pathological changes and the limitations of liver puncture tissues, characteristic lesions may be missed. Sufficient liver tissue specimens are necessary for diagnosis. Thirdly, some elderly patients may present as “burn-out” autoimmune hepatitis, which shows differing degrees of fibrosis and no or slight inflammatory damage, thus hindering the definitive diagnosis.[22] The serological test results suggested AIH. Close observation and follow-up are recommended in these patients. Furthermore, occult viral infection caused by hepatitis B/C virus or non-hepatophagocytic virus and occult alcoholism may also be the etiology of cryptogenic cirrhosis.[22,23]

The present study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective study design may have induced potential selection bias and limited the generalizability of the results. Second, some patients with portal hypertension failed to undergo liver biopsy at our center because of critical illness, rejection of invasive examination, or existence of serious contraindications, resulting in loss of cases. Finally, the diagnosis of portal hypertension in this study was only supported by radiological data but not by the gold standard, such as HVPG or transient elastography, which may limit its quantification. In future research, we can collaborate with multiple centers to reduce selection bias caused by single center. For patients who have not undergone liver biopsy, follow-up can be conducted to understand the changes in their condition. We can also included patients with unexplained portal hypertension for prospective studies. HVPG and transient elastography can be detected in all patients, while liver biopsy can be conducted to explore the etiological composition. Nevertheless, we included all patients with unexplained portal hypertension who underwent liver biopsy in this study, and carefully evaluated the diagnostic efficiency and safety of this invasive examination. The results showed that liver biopsy is safe and has value in the etiological diagnosis of portal hypertension.

5. Conclusions

In summary, liver biopsy is safe and effective for the definitive diagnosis of unexplained portal hypertension. Although liver cirrhosis is the most common cause of portal hypertension, non-cirrhotic portal hypertension is also common in clinical practice. NCPH should be considered for patients with “cirrhosis” diagnosed by imaging, especially for those with severe portal hypertension but almost normal liver function. The diagnosis of PSVD can be considered when imaging suggests exclusion of common causes such as portal vein thrombosis or Budd-Chiari syndrome. Meanwhile, it should be noted that there is a lack of clear boundaries between cirrhosis and non-cirrhotic portal hypertension. Cholestatic liver disease can present with NCPH in the early stages of the disease, while vascular liver disease can also present with liver fibrosis as the disease progresses. Histological examination is the main basis for evaluation of cirrhosis. Cooperation between clinicians and pathologists is necessary to improve the diagnostic accuracy. The capacity of the different health systems for histological assessments can vary significantly, so we should strengthen our understanding of the pathological manifestations of various liver diseases.
