Macrophage exosome-engineered nanoplatform with pH-responsive ratiometric photoacoustic and NIR-II fluorescence imaging for guided photothermal immunotherapy of hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Wenya Li, Xingyang Zhao, Hongrui Qiu, Zhe Zhang, Feichao Xuan, Huili Wang, Yihuai Hu, Chun Yang, Yan Zhang, Wenwei Zhong, Tiantian Wu, Hsuan Lo, Chenyi Xie, Shiying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effectiveness of immunotherapy for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is severely compromised by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) dominated by M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), coupled with a lack of reliable strategies for real-time treatment monitoring. To address this, we developed a pH-responsive nanoimmunomodulator derived from macrophage exosomes for spatiotemporal TAM reprogramming combined with second near-infrared (NIR-II) theranostics. Our nanoplatform (anti-CD47 conjugated Croc@DMPC/Ag2Te/exosome, termed aCD47-CATE) is functionalized with anti-CD47 antibodies to block the CD47-“don't eat me” signal. For precise theranostics, it co-encapsulates silver telluride quantum dots for high-contrast NIR-II fluorescence imaging and croconaine J-aggregates that serve as efficient photothermal converters (emission >1100 nm) and acid-activated photoacoustic probes. The aCD47-CATE platform integrates acid-activated ratiometric photoacoustic imaging with NIR-II fluorescence for accurate tumor targeting and delineation. It effectively blocks CD47 to promote macrophage phagocytosis while exerting mild photothermal effects under NIR-II irradiation to induce immunogenic cell death. This combined action potently remodels the immunosuppressive TME, achieving significant antitumor efficacy. Transcriptomic profiling validated this mechanism, showing enhanced T-cell and macrophage activation alongside suppressed immunosuppressive signals. Collectively, aCD47-CATE represents a robust and multifaceted theranostic platform for image-guided photothermal immunotherapy in HCC.Keywords: NIR-II ratiometric PA imaging, NIR-II photothermal therapy, Macrophage-derived exosomes, CD47 blockade, Theranostic nanoimmunomodulator

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a major global health challenge, with high mortality rates often attributable to late diagnosis and limited efficacy of conventional therapies [1,2]. While the introduction of immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has transformed the treatment landscape for a subset of patients, its overall success in HCC has been inconsistent [3,4]. This limited responsiveness is largely due to the profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which actively disables anti-tumor immunity and facilitates disease progression [5,6]. Overcoming this immunosuppressive barrier, coupled with the need for strategies to monitor therapeutic responses in real time, represents a critical unmet need in advancing HCC treatment [7].

A network of cellular and molecular actors orchestrates the immunosuppressive landscape of HCC [8]. Chief among these are tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which are frequently polarized to an M2 phenotype [9]. These M2 TAMs act as central architects of immune evasion: they secrete inhibitory cytokines, suppress cytotoxic T-cell function, and promote angiogenesis [10]. Further complicating this picture is the ubiquitous overexpression of CD47, a “don't eat me” signal, on HCC cells [11,12]. By engaging its receptor signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) on macrophages, CD47 delivers a potent inhibitory signal that blunts phagocytic clearance and enables cancer cells to evade innate immune surveillance [13]. Together, the dominance of M2 TAMs and the robust CD47–SIRPα checkpoint establish an immunosuppressive milieu that most single-agent immunotherapies cannot effectively reverse [14].

Theranostic nanomedicine has emerged as a promising avenue for HCC, with combined photothermal immunotherapy offering a compelling strategy [15,16]. Here, mild photothermal therapy (PTT) induces immunogenic cell death and synergizes with immunomodulatory agents [17,18]. This approach is further enhanced by operating in the second near-infrared window (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm), which provides deeper tissue penetration and reduced scattering compared to NIR-I light, enabling more precise ablation of deep-seated tumors [[19], [20], [21]]. Notably, 1064 nm irradiation within the NIR-II spectrum exhibits lower tissue attenuation and higher photothermal conversion efficiency than 808 nm, resulting in superior deep-tissue heating performance [22]. As a result, NIR-II photothermal immunotherapy not only eradicates primary tumors but also activates innate and adaptive immunity, thereby reducing risks of recurrence and metastasis [23,24]. However, the clinical translation of this strategy is often limited by conventional nanocarriers, which typically lack sufficient biocompatibility and targeting specificity [25].

To overcome these limitations, exosomes offer an ideal solution as natural nanovesicles with inherent biocompatibility and parent-cell tropism [26]. Specifically, macrophage-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) inherit immune-interactive properties from their parental cells, providing a strategic foundation for infiltrating the TME and facilitating internalizing by resident TAMs [27,28]. Given that macrophages are highly heterogeneous, classically activated M1-like macrophages are particularly notable for their pro-inflammatory and tumoricidal activities [29]. Emerging evidence suggests that M1-derived sEVs partially inherit these immunomodulatory characteristics, enabling active participation in immune regulation rather than functioning solely as passive carriers [30,31]. In contrast to previously reported platforms that emphasize membrane camouflage or prolonged circulation, we deliberately employ M1 exosomes as immune-active components to serve a dual role by enhancing biocompatible tumor targeting and providing immune-contextual cues that synergize with CD47 blockade and photothermal-induced tumor stress. When combined with imaging modalities operating in the second near-infrared window (NIR-II, 1000-1700 nm), which offer superior in vivo performance, including minimized tissue scattering, enhanced penetration depth, and improved spatial resolution, this exosome-based strategy enables precise tumor delineation and guided therapeutic intervention [[32], [33], [34]].

Herein, capitalizing on this bio-inspired foundation, we report the rational design of a sophisticated pH-responsive nanoimmunomodulator engineered from macrophage exosomes for spatiotemporal TAM reprogramming and NIR-II theranostics. This platform, designated aCD47-CATE, is a multifaceted agent comprising anti-CD47 surface antibodies for blocking the “don't eat me” signal and priming phagocytosis, Ag2Te quantum dots for NIR-II fluorescence imaging, and croconaine-based J-aggregates (Croc) that function as dual NIR-II photothermal converters and acid-activated photoacoustic probes (Scheme 1). Notably, the incorporation of Croc J-aggregates addresses a critical gap in quantitative TME sensing. While photoacoustic imaging (PAI) combines optical excitation with ultrasonic detection to visualize tumor heterogeneity and vascular architecture [35,36], and recent NIR-II contrast agents have improved deep-tissue imaging with reduced scattering [37], the integration of NIR-II excitation with ratiometric PAI for accurate pH mapping remains challenging [38,39]. Croc J-aggregates exhibit strong absorption beyond 1100 nm and a ratiometric photoacoustic response across pH 5.8–8.0 [40], enabling high-resolution quantitative mapping of tumor acidity and treatment progression [41]. Thus, aCD47-CATE not only enables spatially and temporally guided photothermal immunotherapy but also provides a built-in ratiometric sensing capability for noninvasive monitoring of TME dynamics.

In summary, our integrated imaging system establishes a guided therapeutic workflow wherein NIR-II fluorescence enables deep-tissue tumor targeting, complemented by ratiometric photoacoustic imaging that verifies the acidic TME and monitors platform activation. Subsequent NIR-II irradiation initiates mild photothermal therapy precisely within the imaged region, inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) to generate an in situ vaccine without direct ablation. This process is synergistically augmented by CD47 blockade, which releases the brakes on phagocytosis, and ICD-driven antigen release that accelerates adaptive immunity, collectively reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME. In murine HCC models, aCD47-CATE demonstrated effective blockade of the CD47-SIRPα axis, enhanced macrophage phagocytosis, and triggered ICD, leading to significant antitumor efficacy. Transcriptomic analysis further confirmed the mechanism, showing enhanced T-cell and macrophage activation alongside suppression of immunosuppressive pathways. Together, this work establishes aCD47-CATE as an intelligent theranostic platform that integrates exosome-based biological targeting with precision NIR-II imaging and ratiometric sensing, offering a new paradigm for guided photothermal immunotherapy in HCC.

Result and discussion

Synthesis and photophysical characterization of the aCD47-CATE nanotheranostic platform

The aCD47-CATE nanoplatform was engineered for HCC theranostics by integrating NIR-II imaging with immune-photothermal therapy via a three-step assembly process (Fig. 1A). The foundation of this system is a croconaine (Croc) derivative, synthesized as previously reported [41] and characterized by mass spectrometry and 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figs. S1 and S2). This core photothermal agent exhibits a pH-dependent spectral shift, transitioning from an anionic J-aggregate (λmax = 660 nm) to a zwitterionic form (λmax = 980 nm) under acidic conditions (Fig. 1B). To leverage this pH-sensing property for targeting the tumor microenvironment, the Croc derivative was incorporated into 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DMPC) micelles using a film dispersion method, yielding Croc@DMPC nanoparticles. This formulation stabilized the J-aggregates, establishing a robust platform for ratiometric photoacoustic (PA) imaging and pH-activated theranostics.

While PA imaging offers valuable functional information, its spatial resolution for deep-tissue mapping is limited. To overcome this, we introduced silver telluride (Ag2Te) quantum dots (QDs) as a complementary NIR-II fluorescence imaging modality. The as-synthesized Ag2Te QDs displayed a well-defined morphology under transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. S3A), with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and HAADF-STEM elemental mapping confirming their homogeneous elemental composition of silver (Ag) and tellurium (Te) (Fig. S3B and C). These QDs were subsequently co-assembled with the Croc@DMPC micelles to form the CAT nanocomposite core. The UV-Vis-NIR absorption and NIR-II emission spectra of Ag2Te QDs were characterized to evaluate their optical properties. As shown in Fig. S3D, the QDs exhibit an absorption tail extending into the NIR region, providing a foundation for NIR-excited fluorescence. The NIR-II photoluminescence spectrum reveals a dominant primary peak centered at approximately 1300 nm, facilitating high-resolution NIR-II fluorescence imaging with minimized tissue autofluorescence and scattering. With the multimodal imaging and photothermal core established, we next focused on imparting immunotherapeutic activity. To this end, we leveraged the pro-inflammatory properties of M1 macrophages by inducing their polarization and subsequently harvesting their secreted exosomal (EXO) membranes. The high purity of the isolated EXOs and the engineered nanoplatforms were confirmed by the clear presence of exosomal markers (TSG101, CD63, CD9 and CD81) and the absence of the endoplasmic reticulum contaminant GRP94 (Fig. S5A). Finally, the purified M1-exosome membranes were fused with the CAT nanocomposite core to form CATE nanoparticles, which were subsequently conjugated with anti-CD47 antibodies (aCD47) to construct the final nanoplatform, aCD47-CATE. Notably, the consistent expression of these markers across EXO, CATE, and aCD47-CATE groups verified that the membrane protein composition was well-preserved throughout the fusion and conjugation processes. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) spectroscopy offered complementary molecular-level confirmation of co-assembly. Compared with free Croc and Croc@DMPC, Ag2Te/Croc@DMPC-Exo (CATE) exhibited pronounced lipid-associated C-H stretching vibrations at ∼2922 and ∼2851 cm−1 and an enhanced ester C=O stretching band at ∼1738 cm−1, indicating successful embedding of Croc within the phospholipid environment. In addition, characteristic protein (amide I/II) and fingerprint-region features, together with a broad band around ∼3500 cm−1 arising from overlapping N-H and O-H stretching vibrations, form a molecular signature consistent with exosomal membrane integration, collectively confirming the successful hybrid co-assembly of Ag2Te QDs, Croc, and lipid/exosomal components (Fig. S4A).

The morphology and composition of the aCD47-CATE nanoplatform were systematically characterized. TEM and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging revealed homogeneous, spherical nanoparticles with a discernible core-shell structure (Fig. 1C). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that the hydrodynamic diameter shifted from 131.5 nm (EXO) to 151.9 nm (CATE) and finally to 159.7 nm (aCD47-CATE) and a near-neutral zeta potential of −0.393 mV, consistent with the successful encapsulation of the core and subsequent antibody conjugation (Figs. S5B and 1D). Elemental mapping further confirmed the presence and distribution of the encapsulated Ag2Te QDs (Ag and Te signals) and the surrounding organic components (C, O, and N where detectable), supporting successful assembly of the composite nanostructure (Fig. 1E and Fig. S4B). Furthermore, the nanoplatform exhibited excellent colloidal stability, as evidenced by the maintenance of its size both upon laser irradiation and after 7-day incubation in 10% fetal bovine serum, underscoring its robustness for subsequent in vivo applications (Fig. S5C and S5D).

Next, to assess the potential of our nanoplatform for HCC photothermal therapy, we evaluated the photothermal properties of aCD47-CATE, which are derived from the Croc component. When exposed to a 1064 nm NIR-II laser, aqueous dispersions of the nanoparticles generated a robust heating effect that was contingent on both their concentration and the laser power density (Fig. 1F and G). A photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of 32.1% was determined, a value comparable to other high-performance photothermal agents [24]. Leveraging the pH-responsive Croc dye, the photothermal effect was enhanced in acidic environments, with temperatures increasing as the pH decreased from neutral to 5.8 (Fig. S6A). The uncoated CATE NPs showed a similar strong and tunable photothermal response (Fig. S6B). Furthermore, aCD47-CATE demonstrated exceptional photostability, as its photothermal performance remained consistent over five consecutive cycles of laser irradiation (Fig. 1H). Taken together, this evidence indicates that aCD47-CATE is a stable and efficient pH-responsive photothermal agent.

Validated dual-modal NIR-II imaging and optical penetration depth of the aCD47-CATE nanoplatform in tissue-mimicking phantom

Non-invasive detection of HCC requires significant tissue penetration, underscoring the critical importance of imaging depth. To evaluate this key parameter, the deep-tissue photoacoustic imaging capability of the nanoplatform was first investigated in tissue-mimicking phantoms (1% Intralipid), leveraging the excellent photothermal properties of the integrated Croc component. The ratiometric PA pH-sensing capability of CATE NPs was evaluated at depths from 0 to 4 mm under dual-wavelength excitation (532 nm and 1064 nm; Fig. 2A). Under acidic conditions (pH 5.8), the nanoplatform generated strong PA signals at 1064 nm, with intensity diminishing as a function of depth due to optical scattering and absorption. Cross-sectional analyses corroborated the pH-dependent imaging performance. At 1064 nm, well-defined structural features were clearly resolved at pH 5.8 and 6.5, which contrasted sharply with the progressively blurred profiles observed under neutral-to-basic conditions (pH 7.4-8.0), reflecting the pH-dependent aggregation behavior of Croc and the resulting NIR-II absorption enhancement. In comparison, under the biologically safe excitation power (0.5 W/cm2) used for in vitro and in vivo imaging, the PA signal at 532 nm exhibited negligible variation across the tested pH range and therefore functioned as a practical internal reference (Fig. 2B–E and Fig. S7). It is important to note that our current PA system is equipped with fixed 532 nm and 1064 nm excitation lasers and does not include a tunable Optical Parametric Oscillator (OPO) source, thus, 532 nm served as the only available secondary channel for ratiometric normalization. Future studies using multi-wavelength OPO-based systems may enable selection of an optimized, pH-inert NIR reference wavelength for deeper tissue applications. Quantitative analysis of the PA signal-to-background ratio (SBR) confirmed the superior performance of aCD47-CATE, with its SBR being approximately 8-fold and 30-fold higher than those of IR-26 and ICG, respectively (Fig. 2F). This distinct ratiometric response validates the ability of the aCD47-CATE platform to accurately sense subtle pH variations, a capability that was preserved even at greater tissue depths.

We subsequently evaluated the concentration-dependent NIR-II fluorescence intensity of the aCD47-CATE nanoplatform. The results confirmed its suitability for quantitative imaging and allowed us to identify the optimal concentration range for high signal output (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, aCD47-CATE demonstrated superior photostability under prolonged excitation, with minimal signal decay compared to the rapid quenching observed in the conventional fluorophores IR-26 and ICG (Fig. 2H). Having established its bright and stable emission, we investigated the critical parameter of penetration depth. Capillaries filled with CATE, IR-26, or ICG were embedded in a tissue-mimicking Intralipid phantom and imaged under 980 nm excitation. The emitted fluorescence was collected through a series of long-pass filters across the 1200-1500 nm spectral window (Fig. 2I). As penetration depth increased, all samples exhibited a progressive attenuation of fluorescence intensity. However, the CATE-based probes uniquely retained sharp, well-defined structural profiles at depths of up to 6 mm. Quantitative analysis of the SBR confirmed this superior performance, which was most pronounced at the 1300 nm emission window, a finding consistent with the probes’ emission spectra (Fig. 2J-M, S3D). The preservation of resolvable capillary edges under these conditions highlights the distinct advantage of CATE for deep-tissue imaging over conventional dyes.

NIR-II fluorescence/ratiometric photoacoustic imaging of aCD47-CATE nanoplatform in HCC cells

The HCC-targeting capability of aCD47-CATE is crucial for theranostic precision. Therefore, we next investigated the in vitro targeted imaging capabilities mediated by the nanoplatform. First, in vitro cellular uptake experiments demonstrated that the aCD47-CATE group exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity compared to the other two cohorts (Fig. 3A and B). aCD47-mediated endocytosis might be the reason for the enhanced cellular uptake ability of aCD47-CATE in Hepa1-6 cells. Next, we performed ratiometric PA imaging using dual-wavelength excitation (532 and 1064 nm), where 532 nm serves as a stable reference channel, including contributions from endogenous absorbers such as hemoglobin, and 1064 nm provides the pH-responsive probe signal and improved penetration, enabling robust evaluation of nanoplatform pH responsiveness via the PA1064/PA532 ratio. As shown in Fig. 3C, acidic environments yielded higher PA intensity, and the ratiometric PA1064/PA532 signal progressively decreased as the pH increased (Fig. 3D). In vitro fluorescence imaging studies using Hepa1-6 cells showed a clear concentration-dependent imaging performance of the nanoplatform. At high concentrations, cell edges remained sharp, whereas reduced concentrations led to blurred contours and diminished signals (Fig. 3E and F). These results validate the strong signal output and sensitivity of aCD47-CATE under biologically relevant conditions.

aCD47-CATE NPs induce apoptosis in Hepa1-6 cells and promote M1 macrophage polarization and infiltration

To investigate the cytotoxicity profile and therapeutic potential of aCD47-CATE nanoparticles (NPs), we evaluated their effects on both normal and malignant liver cell lines. Biosafety studies in normal hepatocytes (LO2) and macrophages (RAW264.7) demonstrated minimal toxicity, with cell viabilities consistently above 75% after 24 h of incubation at concentrations up to 600 μg/mL (Fig. S7A and B). To evaluate the direct antitumor efficacy and immunomodulatory potential of aCD47-CATE NPs in vitro, we treated Hepa1-6 murine hepatocellular carcinoma cells and assessed their subsequent impact on both cancer cells and macrophages. First, we investigated the direct cytotoxic effects of aCD47-CATE NPs on Hepa1-6 cells. The CCK-8 assay revealed that aCD47-CATE NPs significantly suppressed the viability of Hepa1-6 cells in a dose-dependent manner, exhibiting superior efficacy compared to the control or aCD47 alone groups (Fig. 4A, Fig. S8C). Consistent with CCK-8 assay results, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images using Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) staining demonstrated pronounced differences in cell viability among treatment groups (Fig. S9). To determine if the reduction in cell viability was attributable to apoptosis, TUNEL staining was performed, where a markedly higher number of TUNEL-positive (red) cells was observed in the aCD47-CATE NP-treated group (Fig. 4B), a finding that was quantitatively supported by statistical analysis (Fig. 4C). This induction of apoptosis was further validated by flow cytometry analysis. The results confirmed a substantial increase in the apoptotic rate of Hepa1-6 cells following treatment with aCD47-CATE NPs (Fig. 4D). Consistent with this interpretation, Western blot analysis revealed the depletion of pro-caspase-3 and significantly reduced Bcl-2 expression in treated Hepa1-6 cells, supporting the involvement of a caspase-dependent apoptotic mechanism (Fig. S10). Beyond direct apoptosis, we investigated whether the synergistic therapy could trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD) to prime the immune response. As shown in Fig. S11A–D, aCD47-CATE plus laser treatment significantly promoted the surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT) and the extracellular release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which serve as crucial “eat-me” and “find-me” signals for immune cells. We next examined whether the treatment of tumor cells could influence the phenotype of macrophages, a key component of the tumor immune microenvironment. RAW264.7 macrophages were incubated with conditioned media collected from the variously treated Hepa1-6 cells. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that conditioned media from the aCD47-CATE NP-treated group notably upregulated the expression of CD80, a characteristic M1 macrophage marker, indicating a shift towards a pro-inflammatory, antitumor phenotype (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, to assess the functional consequence of this M1 polarization, we conducted a tumor spheroid infiltration assay. CFDA-SE-labeled M1 macrophages were co-cultured with Hepa1-6 tumor spheroids. CLSM images clearly showed that M1 macrophages activated by aCD47-CATE NP-treated conditioned media exhibited a significantly enhanced ability to infiltrate into the tumor spheroids (Fig. 4F). The quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity unequivocally confirmed this increased infiltration capacity (Fig. 4G). Taken together, these results demonstrate that aCD47-CATE NPs mediate potent in vitro antitumor activity through direct induction of cancer cell apoptosis and concurrent promotion of M1 macrophage polarization and infiltration.

Active-targeting dual-modal NIR-II/photoacoustic imaging of HCC with aCD47-CATE NPs

Having demonstrated potent in vitro efficacy, we proceeded to evaluate the in vivo performance of aCD47-CATE in orthotopic HCC mouse models. To ensure its safe application in vivo, hemolysis assays were first conducted in vitro using red blood cells (RBCs) from C57 mice. The hemolysis rate of aCD47-CATE was only 3.9 %, exhibited negligible hemolysis (Fig. S12). These results demonstrate that the exosome membrane coating ensures excellent biocompatibility and hemocompatibility of the nanoplatform. To evaluate the in vivo active targeting efficacy and dual-modal imaging performance of aCD47-CATE, we established a murine HCC model. We first employed NIR-II fluorescence imaging, which unequivocally demonstrated the superior tumor-targeting capability of aCD47-CATE over CATE and free ICG. Notably, CATE alone exhibited significant tumor enrichment compared to free ICG, confirming the intrinsic tumor tropism of the M1 macrophage-derived exosome coating. Building on its in vitro selectivity, aCD47-CATE exhibited efficient uptake and retention in orthotopic HCC tumors, which is attributed to its exosome-mediated tumor tropism. As shown in Fig. 5A, the aCD47-CATE group exhibited a markedly stronger and more persistent fluorescence signal at the tumor site, achieving peak enrichment at 4 h post-injection and producing clear tumor delineation with high signal-to-background contrast. Quantitative analysis at this critical time point confirmed that aCD47-CATE achieved a significantly higher tumor-to-normal (T/N) ratio compared to the control groups, with values approaching 40 and a fluorescence intensity in tumors up to 32-fold higher than in surrounding tissues (Fig. 5B). Time-course NIR-II imaging demonstrates that tumor-associated signal increases from 0 to 2 h, reaches a clear maximum at ∼4 h, and then progressively decreases at 8-12 h, with minimal residual signal by 24 h, supporting 4 h post-injection as the optimal window for image-guided intervention (Fig. 5C and Fig. S13), producing clear tumor delineation with high signal-to-background contrast. Notably, while tumor accretion was dominant, the transient bladder-associated fluorescence observed likely reflects the renal excretion of dissociated fluorescent species rather than intact exosome-sized nanoparticles, which are primarily processed via hepatic pathways.

The photoacoustic imaging results provided high-resolution, cross-sectional evidence for the specific accumulation and functional utility of our nanoprobe. To overcome the limitations of conventional nanoprobes in the 400–900 nm window, such as strong light attenuation and background autofluorescence, we shifted to the NIR-II region for noninvasive pH-responsive photoacoustic imaging in HCC. We employed 1064 nm excitation for PAM, as a prior resolution test validated its superior penetration depth and spatial resolution over 532 nm, rendering it optimal for deep-tissue imaging (Fig. S14). Subsequently, we evaluated the capacity of aCD47-CATE for ratiometric PA imaging of tumor pH in vivo by recording the PA signals at dual wavelengths (1064/532 nm) over time (Fig. 5D). The 532 nm PAM clearly delineated the vascular architecture of the tumors, while the 1064 nm signal from aCD47-CATE provided greater contrast than the CATE group and clinically-approved ICG. Quantification at the key 4-h time point affirmed a significantly stronger signal for aCD47-CATE, achieving a relative PA1064 intensity of 11.5, which was 6-fold higher than CATE and 10-fold higher than free ICG, thus providing effective tumor demarcation (Fig. 5E). Finally, to unequivocally demonstrate the spatial resolution achievable with our nanoprobe and imaging system, we visualized the mouse hindlimb vasculature in vivo (Fig. 5F). The fluorescence intensity profile yielded a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 14.5 μm (Fig. 5G), confirming the exceptional resolution that enables precise delineation of microvasculature, a critical advantage for detailed tumor imaging and analysis.

Photothermal therapy with aCD47-CATE remodels the tumor immune microenvironment and promotes T cell infiltration

To investigate the antitumor efficacy and immunomodulatory effects of our novel agent, we established Hepa1-6 tumor-bearing mice and treated them according to the schematic timeline (Fig. 6A). We first confirmed the in vivo photothermal performance of aCD47-CATE. Upon 1064 nm laser irradiation, tumors in the aCD47-CATE + Laser group exhibited a rapid and significant temperature increase, reaching a plateau of approximately 50.6 °C within 5 min (Fig. 6B and C). This level of intratumoral heat was sufficient to induce necrosis, impair angiogenesis, and promote immunogenic cell death. Importantly, systemic body temperature normalized within 3-5 min post-irradiation, indicating a favorable safety profile. In contrast, control groups injected with PBS and exposed to the laser showed only a mild temperature rise to ∼30 °C, which was non-damaging. These results collectively demonstrate the potent and localized photothermal conversion capability of aCD47-CATE at the tumor site.

We next assessed whether the combination of photothermal ablation and CD47 blockade could reprogram the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Flow cytometric analysis revealed a profound repolarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward an immunostimulatory phenotype. Quantitative analysis showed the M1 macrophage population (CD86+CD206-) increased dramatically from 12.5% in the control group to 65.7% following combination therapy, while the M2 population (CD206+CD86−) was concurrently suppressed from 45.8% to 7.62% (Fig. 6D). This striking shift indicates a successful “re-education” of the innate immune landscape within the TME. This functional “re-education” is likely the result of a coordinated multi-pathway mechanism rather than a single ligand-receptor interaction. Specifically, while M1-derived exosomes (M1-Exo) provide an immune-active scaffold, their role in TAM reprogramming is multifaceted. The delivery of aCD47 successfully interrupts the SIRP α-CD47 “don't eat me” signaling axis, which is a prerequisite for overcoming macrophage tolerance. Besides, M1-Exo, as pro-inflammatory entities, carry unique molecular cargos, such as specific cytokines or miRNAs, that naturally bias recipient macrophages toward an M1-like state, a process distinct from the passive transport seen in M2-derived EVs. This M1-skewed polarization is further amplified by the photothermal-induced stress and the subsequent release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which collectively act as potent adjuvants to drive the gradual functional shift of heterogeneous TAM populations toward a tumoricidal phenotype.

T cells are central to the antitumor immune response, with CD4+ helper T cells acting indirectly by activating other immune cells and releasing cytokines, and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells directly recognizing and lysing malignant cells. The initiation of this adaptive immunity is critically dependent on innate immune activation. To investigate this, we first assessed dendritic cell (DC) maturation, a key bridge between innate and adaptive immunity. The results showed a marked expansion of mature DCs (CD80+CD86+) in the aCD47-CATE + Laser group, which increased by 8.2-fold from a baseline of 0.93% to 7.63% (Fig. 6E). This synergy can be explained by the photothermal effect releasing a burst of tumor antigens, while CD47 blockade removes the “don't eat me” signal, thereby potently enhancing antigen phagocytosis, processing, presentation, and subsequent T-cell priming.

Building on this enhanced DC maturation, we next quantified tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes to directly evaluate the adaptive immune response. Flow cytometric analyses demonstrated that the combination therapy led to a substantial augmentation in the infiltration of both CD4+ helper T cells (Fig. 6F) and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (Fig. 6G). This robust T-cell influx was visually confirmed by multiplex immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections, which clearly showed dense infiltrates of CD4+ (red) and CD8+ (green) T cells in the aCD47-CATE + Laser group (Fig. 6H). Together, these data illustrate a coherent immunologic cascade in which the therapy remodels the innate immune compartment to initiate a potent adaptive response, culminating in the recruitment and activation of critical T-cell populations for tumor cell killing.

To further investigate whether this synergistic treatment could elicit systemic immunological protection beyond local tumor control, we evaluated the induction of immune memory and the modulation of the systemic immunosuppressive environment. Splenocytes were harvested and analyzed via flow cytometry following the completion of the initial treatment cycle. Notably, the aCD47-CATE + laser treatment led to a significant expansion of both effector memory T cells (Tem, CD8+CD44+CD62L-, 30.4%) and central memory T cells (Tcm, CD8+CD44+CD62L+, 36.1%) in the spleen (Fig. S15A). This simultaneous increase in both memory T cell subsets indicates the successful establishment of a comprehensive and long-lasting immune surveillance network capable of systemic defense. Furthermore, we assessed the systemic immunosuppressive state by quantifying the regulatory T cell (Treg) population (CD4+CD25+Foxp3+) in the spleen. Compared to the control groups, the aCD47-CATE + laser group exhibited a marked reduction (0.81% vs 3.72% in PBS group) in Treg frequency (Fig. S15B), suggesting a successful shift from an immunosuppressive to an immunostimulatory systemic environment. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the synergistic coupling of CD47 blockade and NIR-II PTT reshapes the host immune landscape, potentially providing the cellular basis for long-term antitumor efficacy and the prevention of tumor recurrence.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that aCD47-CATE-mediated photothermal therapy not only effectively destroys tumors but also synergistically transforms the “cold” immunosuppressive TME into a “hot” immunologically active state. This transformation is achieved through reprogramming macrophage polarization, enhancing dendritic cell maturation, and ultimately fostering a potent and pervasive infiltration of effector T cells.

Elucidation of the transcriptomic mechanisms underlying synergistic antitumor immunity

To delineate the molecular underpinnings of the observed antitumor efficacy, we conducted transcriptomic profiling of tumor tissues. Analysis of the aCD47-CATE + Laser group versus the PBS control revealed 294 uniquely expressed genes and 88 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), indicative of a distinct transcriptional rewiring induced by the treatment (Fig. 7A and B). Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses demonstrated that these DEGs were significantly enriched in immune-related processes, including the IL-17, Toll-like receptor, and T-cell receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 7C and D). The coordinated interplay between key DEGs and these pathways was further visualized in a Circos plot, which illustrated a transcriptional network concurrently promoting immune activation and tumor suppression (Fig. 7E). These transcriptomic alterations culminated in a broad activation of both innate and adaptive antitumor immunity. GSEA of the comparison between the aCD47-CATE + laser and PBS control groups (Fig. 7F) demonstrated a significant upregulation of gene sets involved in macrophage migration, phagocytosis, and JAK–STAT/p38 MAPK signaling, alongside a downregulation of apoptosis-related pathways. This profile illustrates the overall immunostimulatory phenotype enabled by the combination therapy. Crucially, to delineate the specific contribution of CD47 blockade, GSEA of the aCD47-CATE + laser versus CATE + laser groups (Fig. 7G) revealed a distinct enrichment of gene sets associated with B cell receptor signaling, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, CD8+ T cell activation, MHC binding, and antigen presentation. This direct comparison underscores the pivotal, additive role of the aCD47 moiety in potently engaging both innate and adaptive immune arms. Concurrently, the downregulation of apoptotic genes in immune cells may contribute to sustaining a robust anti-tumor immune response.

Synergistic immuno-photothermal therapy achieves potent tumor growth control and survival advantage

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of aCD47-CATE NPs, we established an orthotopic HCC model in C57BL/6 mice. Tumor growth was longitudinally monitored via in vivo bioluminescence imaging. As shown in Fig. 8A, mice treated with aCD47-CATE NPs in combination with laser irradiation exhibited a profound suppression of tumor progression throughout the study period. In contrast, control groups (PBS, and aCD47-CATE only) showed rapid and unchecked tumor growth (Fig. 8B). Notably, while the CATE + Laser group also showed initial tumor reduction, the aCD47-CATE + Laser group maintained a more durable response with negligible recurrence. This potent antitumor effect directly translated into a significant survival benefit. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that the aCD47-CATE + Laser combination therapy markedly prolonged the median survival of tumor-bearing mice compared to all other groups (Fig. 8C). Consistently with the establishment of systemic immune memory and the reversal of immunosuppression observed earlier, underscoring the critical role of the combined photothermal and immunotherapeutic approach in achieving sustained remission.

Importantly, the potent antitumor efficacy of the combination therapy was accompanied by an excellent safety profile. Systemic toxicity was first ruled out, as evidenced by the stable body weight of mice throughout the treatment course in all groups (Fig. 8D). To further evaluate biosafety, histological and biochemical analyses were performed. Examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidneys) revealed no apparent lesions, inflammation, or significant histological alterations in any treatment group (Fig. S16), indicating minimal organ-level toxicity. Corroborating these findings, blood biochemical analyses showed no significant abnormalities in key markers of hepatic (ALT, AST, ALP) and renal (UA, UREA) function, or in albumin (ALB) levels (Fig. S17), collectively affirming the favorable biocompatibility of the aCD47-CATE platform. Given that CD47 is also expressed on normal circulating cells, such as, erythrocytes and neutrophils, we present anti-CD47 on an M1 macrophage-derived exosome-coated nanoscale construct, which differs from free antibody administration and reduces nonspecific exposure through pharmacokinetic retention and tumor pre-localization [42]. Biochemistry and histopathology results have shown no overt systemic toxicity under the administered regimen.

We next directly assessed the therapeutic efficacy at the tumor site. Histological examination using H&E staining revealed extensive tumor destruction in the combination group, characterized by classic morphological features of cell death such as nuclear condensation and fragmentation (Fig. 8E). This observation was quantitatively confirmed by TUNEL staining, which detected a massive increase in apoptotic cells within the treated tumors (Fig. 8F). Together, these data robustly demonstrate that the combination therapy effectively induces programmed cell death, establishing it as a primary mechanism underlying the observed tumor suppression.

In cancer theranostics, nanoplatforms that combine biomimetic delivery, immune checkpoint modulation, and photothermal treatment exhibit significant translational potential, with prior studies confirming their effective modular synergy. The liposome/macrophage-derived exosome hybrid system co-loaded with IR780, recently designed by Zhou et al., significantly enhances tumor delivery and microenvironment modulation, yet its imaging function remains primarily a distribution and navigation tool without incorporating a dedicated, quantitatively microenvironment-responsive ratiometric sensing module [43]. Li et al. introduced CD47 checkpoint modulation into a NIR-II photoacoustic-guided phototherapy system, but its imaging component still serves mainly for targeting guidance and fails to provide ratiometric functional readouts of the tumor microenvironmental state during therapy [44]. Recently, a novel NIR-II photothermal agents, quinoid conjugated polymer nanoparticles were developed, demonstrating robust photothermal efficacy under 1064 nm irradiation [45]. However, such photothermal-only materials typically lack biomimetic immune contextualization and checkpoint-coupled functional feedback mechanisms. We extend this paradigm by introducing dynamic environmental sensing and sequential immune-priming logic.Unlike “always-on” localization imaging, our croconaine-based module enables ratiometric photoacoustic sensing of acidity, transforming imaging from static navigation into functional monitoring of the tumor microenvironmental state during therapy. By choosing M1 macrophage-derived exosomes, we preserve the delivery benefits of exosomal membranes while including immune-contextual signals that skew the local milieu towards inflammatory activity. Moreover, instead of merely overlaying CD47 blockade with photothermal therapy, we advocate for a synergistic sequential approach, in which NIR-II photothermal therapy induces tumor stress and immunogenic cell death signals to enhance “eat-me” susceptibility, followed by CD47 blockade lowering the anti-phagocytic threshold to amplify macrophage-mediated clearance. This integration is conceptually distinct from earlier hybrid strategies combining exosomes, photothermal therapy, and checkpoint modulation.

Conclusion

In summary, to overcome the profound immunosuppressive microenvironment and therapeutic resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma, we developed aCD47 CATE, an intelligent macrophage exosome-derived nanotheranostic platform for imaging-guided photothermal immunotherapy. This bioinspired system seamlessly integrated a pH-responsive croconaine-based J aggregate for ratiometric photoacoustic imaging and NIR II photothermal conversion with Ag2Te quantum dots that provided stable deep tissue fluorescence visualization. By harnessing the innate tumor targeting capability and biological compatibility of macrophage exosomes, aCD47 CATE effectively delivered anti-CD47 antibodies and functional imaging components directly to tumor sites. Upon NIR II laser irradiation, the platform initiated a coordinated immunomodulatory response where mild photothermal therapy induced immunogenic cell death while concurrent CD47 blockade disrupted the dominant don't eat me signal to enhance macrophage-mediated phagocytosis. This dual mechanism successfully reversed immunosuppression by reprogramming tumor-associated macrophages from the protumor M2 phenotype to the antitumor M1 state, which subsequently improved antigen presentation and activated a potent tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cell response. Transcriptomic analysis further validated these findings, showing significant activation of immune pathways including Toll-like receptor signaling, IL-17 signaling, and T cell receptor signaling, alongside suppression of key immunosuppressive networks. Consequently, aCD47 CATE treatment achieved substantial tumor regression and markedly extended survival in an orthotopic HCC model, all while maintaining an excellent safety profile.

This work demonstrates a strategy that integrates precision NIR-II imaging, ratiometric microenvironment sensing, and multimodal immunotherapy within a single platform to break therapeutic resistance in HCC, thereby creating new avenues for dual-modality image-guided combination therapies.

Materials and methods

Chemical and reagents

Croconic acid was sourced from Aladdin Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Trioctylphosphine (TOP), tellurium (Te) powder, silver acetate (AgAc), dimyristoylphosphatidylcholine (DMPC), 1-octadecene (ODE), 1-octanethiol (OT), 1-butanol, and toluene were acquired from a biochemical technology company. Indole was purchased from Bidepharm Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from GIBCO (USA). Dialysis bags with a molecular weight cutoff of 12–14 kDa were supplied by Amicon Ultra (Merck Millipore, United States). Antibodies used for western blot analysis, including anti-GRP94, anti-TSG101, and anti-CD63, were obtained from BD Biosciences. CCK-8 kits were procured from MedChem Express (Princeton, NJ, USA). All chemicals and reagents were used directly without additional purification unless specified otherwise. A TUNEL assay kit was bought from Wuhan Servicebio Technology. Fluorescently labeled antibodies, such as anti-mouse CD11c, anti-mouse CD80, anti-mouse CD86, anti-mouse F4/80, anti-mouse CD206, anti-mouse CD3, anti-mouse CD4, and anti-mouse CD8, were obtained from commercial suppliers.

Instrumentations

1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Plus 400 Hz spectrometer using D2O as the solvent. Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted with a Bruker AmaZon Speed ESI-ion Trap-ETD Mass Spectrometer. UPLC-MS measurements were performed using an Agilent Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer located at the University Research Facility in Life Science. UV/Vis absorption spectra were obtained with an Agilent 8453 UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were measured using a Malvern Zeta sizer (Malvern Instruments, UK). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images were captured with a JEOL JEM-2100, and Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) images were obtained using a JEOL Model JSM-6490.

Synthesis and characterization of croconaine (Croc)

The synthesis of croconaine (Croc) followed a previously reported protocol [46]. Briefly, 2,3,3-trimethylindolenine and croconic acid were mixed in a reaction vessel, and the mixture was refluxed for 22 h. Water generated during the reaction was continuously removed via azeotropic distillation using a Dean-Stark apparatus. Throughout the reaction, the mixture gradually changed color from transparent red to dark green, and a dark brown-black solid precipitated. The resulting solid (Croc) was collected by filtration and washed three times with cold diethyl ether. To eliminate residual solvents, the solid was dried under reduced pressure overnight. Croc was obtained (200 mg, 53%) as a brown-black solid, and the spectral data were consistent with the reported values.

Preparation of Croc J-aggregates

Croc was first dissolved in methanol to prepare a stock solution with a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Then, 100 μL of this Croc stock solution was mixed with 80 mL of DMPC solution (25 mg/mL). The solvent was removed by rotary evaporation under vacuum to form a dry, dye-containing lipid film. The film was rehydrated with 2 mL of PBS buffer at 50 °C for 10 min, and the resulting solution was transferred to a 4-mL falcon tube. Probe sonication was performed at a low frequency (30 kHz) for 15 min with a cycle of 30 s on and 10 s off. Purification was achieved using a 100 kDa centrifugal filter at 3000 g for 20 min. The resulting J-aggregate solution was stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Synthesis of Ag2Te QDs

Tri-n-octylphosphine telluride (TOP-Te) was prepared by adding Te powder (76.6 mg) to tri-n-octylphosphine (9 mL) under a nitrogen atmosphere. The mixture was heated to 110 °C and maintained at this temperature for 30 min to obtain a pale yellow, transparent TOP-Te solution, which was then cooled naturally to room temperature for later use. To synthesize NIR-II Ag2Te QDs, 1-octadecene (ODE, 5 mL) was preheated to 50 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere, followed by the addition of oleylthiol (OT, 260 μL) and silver acetate (AgAc, 33.4 mg). The mixture was gradually heated to 170 °C, and 0.3 mL of the TOP-Te solution was injected rapidly. The temperature was then reduced and maintained at 120 °C for 60 min. The resulting products were isolated by centrifugation and washed with a mixture of ethanol and chloroform. Subsequently, PEG2000-SH was dissolved in chloroform at a mass ratio of 1:10 and heated to 50 °C for 6 h to functionalize the QDs. The PEG-functionalized Ag2Te QDs were collected for further use.

Preparation of CAT NPs

Croc was dissolved in dichloromethane (DCM) to prepare a 1 mg/mL stock solution, and DMPC was dissolved in methanol to prepare a 1.32 mg/mL stock solution. The Croc and DMPC stock solutions were mixed at a volume ratio of 1:5. The solvent was removed by rotary evaporation to form a lipid film, which was then rehydrated with 2 mL of PBS buffer (pH 7.4) containing 1 mg/mL Ag2Te QDs. The mixture was sonicated in a water bath at 50 °C for 10 min, followed by probe sonication for 15 min (30 kHz, 30 s on/10 s off cycle). The obtained sample was purified using a 100-kDa centrifugal filter and stored at 4 °C for further experiments.

Exosome (Exo) isolation

RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 24 h. The cell culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 2000×g to remove cell debris. The resulting supernatant was then centrifuged at 100,000×g for 90 min twice at 4 °C. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in PBS. The concentration of exosomes was quantified using a N30 Nano-flow analyzer (NANOFCM). Alternatively, exosomes could be isolated by adding 0.5 vol of total exosome isolation reagent (Invitrogen, 4478359) to the cell culture supernatant, vortexing thoroughly, incubating, and then centrifuging.

Preparation of CATE and aCD47-CATE NPs

For the assembly of CATE NPs, 1 mL of PBS containing 100 μg of CAT NPs was mixed with the prepared exosome vesicles. The mixture was incubated under appropriate conditions, then centrifuged at 1000×g to remove excess exosomes, yielding exosome-fused particles (CATE). For the preparation of aCD47-CATE NPs, a bifunctional linker Sulfo-SMCC (0.1 mg, 5 mg/mL) was first incubated with anti-CD47 antibody (10 μg/mL) for 60 min. This mixture was then added to 5 mL of CATE NPs (approximately 50 μg/mL) and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. Unbound antibodies were removed by centrifugation, and the resulting aCD47-CATE NPs were resuspended in PBS for subsequent use.

Laser-induced heat generation study

Aqueous solutions of CATE and aCD47-CATE NPs with different concentrations were prepared in 2.0 mL Eppendorf tubes, and the pH values of the solutions were adjusted to 5.8, 6.5, and 7.4. All samples were irradiated with a 1064 nm laser (Laser Shanghai) with a beam diameter of 0.5 cm, irradiation time of 5 min, and laser power densities set at 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 W/cm2. During irradiation, real-time near-infrared (NIR) images were recorded using a FLUKE camera and quantified with Smart View 4.3 software. A thermal camera was also used to continuously monitor temperature changes to evaluate the photothermal properties of the NPs. Additionally, the photothermal stability of aCD47-CATE NPs was assessed through 5 cycles of laser “on-off” treatments.

In vitro NIR-II fluorescence imaging

Glass capillaries containing CATE, ICG, and IR26 (each at 100 μg/mL in PBS) were covered with agar gels of different thicknesses (1% agar containing 1% Intralipid). Imaging was performed using a home-built NIR-II fluorescence imaging system with a 980 nm excitation laser and 1200 nm, 1300 nm, 1400 nm, and 1500 nm long-pass (LP) filters for image acquisition.

In vitro ratiometric PA imaging

Transparent silicone tubes filled with 100 μg/mL CATE in PBS (pH 5.8, 6.5, 7.4, 8.0) were used as in vitro phantoms. The phantoms were covered with agar gels of different thicknesses (1% agar containing 1% Intralipid) and scanned with a photoacoustic microscopy (PAM) system (PASONO-ANI, Guangdong Photoacoustic Technology Co., Ltd.) under 532 nm and 1064 nm pulsed lasers (532 nm: pulse width 1 ns; 1064 nm: AOML-150B).

Cell culture

LO2 cells (normal hepatocytes), RAW264.7 cells (macrophages), and Hepa1-6 cells (hepatocellular carcinoma cells) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% exosome-free fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell) and 2 mM Glutamax (Gibco). All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

NIR-II fluorescence and ratiometric PA imaging of cell phantoms

Hepa1-6 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 6 h. The medium was then replaced with DMEM containing different concentrations of aCD47-CATE NPs (0, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 μg/mL). After 24 h of incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and imaged using an NIR-II fluorescence imaging system (980 nm excitation, 1200 nm LP filter). For ratiometric PA imaging, Hepa1-6 cells were cultured in DMEM at different pH levels (5.8, 6.5, 7.4, 8.0) with 50 μg/mL aCD47-CATE NPs, then imaged using a PAM system at 1064 nm and 532 nm wavelengths.

Cell viability assay

Hepa1-6 cells, LO2 cells, or RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells per well) and cultured for 12 h to allow adherence. The culture medium was then replaced with medium containing different concentrations of CATE or aCD47-CATE NPs, and incubation continued for another 12 or 24 h. To assess the photothermal effect, Hepa1-6 cells were irradiated with a 1064 nm NIR laser (1.0 W/cm2) for 5 min and incubated in a CO2 incubator for 1 h. Cytotoxicity was evaluated using CCK-8 assays (Dojindo). A Spectra Max® M3 Microplate Reader was used to measure absorbance at 450 nm, and relative cell viability was calculated based on these measurements.

Cell uptake

Hepa1-6 cells were seeded on confocal plates and cultured for 24 h, then divided into three groups: control, CATE, and aCD47-CATE. After incubating with the corresponding Rhodamine B labeled nanoparticles for 6 h, the cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min, and washed again three times with PBS. Then, 1 mL of PBS containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin and 100 μL of diluted YF®488-Phalloidin solution (UElandy, 5 μL phalloidin stock solution in 200 μL PBS) was added, and the cells were incubated for 30 min. Nuclei were stained with DAPI solution for 5 min, and fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) Co-staining assay

Hepa1-6 cells were subjected to different treatments: control, aCD47-CATE, CATE + laser, and aCD47-CATE + laser. After treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and stained with Calcein-AM (10 μg/mL) and PI (5 μg/mL) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining (flow cytometry)

Hepa1-6 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated as follows: control, CATE, aCD47-CATE, laser alone, CATE + laser, and aCD47-CATE + laser. The supernatant was collected, and cells were harvested by trypsinization, followed by two cycles of centrifugation and washing. The cell pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of 1 × Annexin V binding buffer, and 5 μg/mL Annexin V-FITC and 3 μg/mL PI were added. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min in the dark, then analyzed immediately using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Excitation was performed with a 488 nm laser; FITC fluorescence was detected through a 525/40 nm filter (FL1 channel), and PI fluorescence was captured using a 610/20 nm filter (FL3 channel).

Penetration of 3D Hepa 1-6 cancer spheroids

Hepa1-6 cells were seeded in an ultra-low adsorption 96-well plate, and 20 μL of 4% methylcellulose solution was added to every 100 μL of medium. After 48 h of incubation, spheroids were formed and subjected to different treatments. RAW264.7 cells were labeled with 1 μM CFDA-SE (Medchemexpress), added to the 96-well plate, and co-cultured with the spheroids for 6 h. The cell pellet was aspirated, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with DAPI for 5 min, and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, 3D thunder).

Animal experiments and establishment of tumor model

Five-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Animal Center (Guangzhou, China) and housed in a specific pathogen-free environment. All animal care and handling procedures were approved by the Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital (Permit Number: KY2025-070-01). Hepa1-6-luc cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested and resuspended in culture medium to a concentration of 1 × 108 cells/mL. Mice were anesthetized, fixed in a supine position, and their upper abdomen was disinfected. A 1-1.5 cm longitudinal incision was made to expose the left liver lobe. A micro-syringe was used to aspirate 20 μL of the cell suspension (containing 2 × 106 cells), which was slowly injected into the liver parenchyma at a 30°angle with a penetration depth of 2-3 mm. After injection, the needle was withdrawn, and gentle pressure was applied to the injection site to stop bleeding. The left liver lobe was placed back into the abdominal cavity, and the wound was sutured layer by layer. At 2-3 weeks post-inoculation, the formation of orthotopic liver tumors was confirmed by in vivo fluorescence imaging.

In vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging

Orthotopic HCC-bearing mice were intravenously injected with ICG, CATE, or aCD47-CATE (5 mg/kg). NIR-II fluorescence images were collected using a 1024 × 1024 pixel 2D InGaAs NIR CCD camera (Pco.edge Instrument) under a 980 nm laser with a power density of 0.25 W/cm2. A 1200 nm filter was used for image acquisition.

In vivo PA imaging

Orthotopic HCC-bearing C57BL/6J mice were used for PA imaging experiments. The livers were exposed and adhered to glass slides with Vetbond tissue adhesive (3M). PAM images were taken before injection at 532 nm and 1064 nm. Mice were then intravenously injected with ICG, CATE, or aCD47-CATE (5 mg/kg), and PAM images were captured 4 h post-injection.

Monitoring of NPs-mediated photothermal or/and aCD47 immune therapy

Orthotopic HCC mouse models established with Hepa1-6-luc cells were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 5): control, CATE + laser, aCD47-CATE, and aCD47-CATE + laser. Mice in the control group received an intravenous injection of PBS; those in the CATE + laser group received CATE injection (10 mg/kg) followed by 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.2 W/cm2, 5 min); the aCD47-CATE group received aCD47-CATE injection (10 mg/kg); and the aCD47-CATE + laser group received aCD47-CATE injection (10 mg/kg) followed by 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.2 W/cm2, 5 min). Bioluminescence imaging (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer) was performed on days 1, 5, 7, 14, and 21 after treatment. To evaluate toxicity, serum biochemical indexes were measured, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) and tumors were resected for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and immunofluorescence observation.

Biosafety test and blood analysis

C57BL/6J mice were sacrificed, and blood was collected via cardiac puncture for biochemical analysis, including measurements of alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), uric acid (UA), urea, and albumin (ALB). Additionally, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were preserved in 10% formalin solution for histological analysis.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labelling (TUNEL) assay

Tumor tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, then washed twice with PBS for 15 min each. Sections were incubated with TUNEL staining mixture (In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, TMR red, Servicebio Technology CO LTD) at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by DAPI staining for 5 min. Images were obtained using a confocal fluorescence microscope, with TUNEL-positive nuclei showing red fluorescence and DAPI-stained nuclei showing blue fluorescence.

Flow cytometry

Tumor tissues were dissected and minced into small pieces, then digested in DMEM medium supplemented with collagenase at 37 °C for 1 h. After filtration through a 40 μm cell strainer, the cells were washed with 5 mL of washing buffer to prepare a single-cell suspension. For immunostaining, tumor-associated macrophages were labeled with F4/80, CD86, and CD206 antibodies; tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were labeled with CD3, CD4, and CD8a antibodies; and dendritic cells were labeled with CD11c, CD80, and CD86 antibodies. After staining at room temperature for 30 min, cells were washed with PBS containing 1% BSA and analyzed using a flow cytometer. Spleens were harvested and dissociated into single-cell suspensions using a 40 μm cell strainer. To evaluate immune memory, splenocytes were labeled with CD3, CD8a, CD44, and CD62L antibodies to identify effector memory T cells (Tem) and central memory T cells (Tcm). For regulatory T cell (Treg) analysis, cells were first labeled with CD3, CD4 and CD25 surface antibodies, followed by fixation, permeabilization, and staining with an anti-Foxp3 antibody.

Transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tumor tissues using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using standard bioinformatics methods. Statistical comparisons of in vitro and in vivo data were performed using ANOVA, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Peak intensity was determined and compared between groups using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Inc.).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least in triplicate, and quantitative results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to statistical analysis, the normality of data distribution and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Brown-Forsythe test, respectively. Statistical comparisons of in vitro and in vivo data was carried out in GraphPad Prism 10.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). For multiple-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons between groups. Significance levels are indicated as: ns (not significant), ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.
