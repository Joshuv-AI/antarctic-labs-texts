Artificial Intelligence–Enhanced Cardiac Point-of-Care Ultrasound: A Prospective Single-Arm Study
Authors: Lior Fisher, Michael Fiman, Yuval Yarkoni, Ella Segal, Boris Fishman, Kobi Faierstein, Noa Rubin, Adiel Am-Shalom, Howard Amital, Qiong Zhao, Smadar Kort, Praveen Mehrotra, Ehud Schwammenthal, Robert Klempfner, Eyal Zimlichman, Ehud Raanani, Elad Maor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical utility of combining artificial intelligence (AI) with handheld focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS) performed by noncardiologist physicians in clinical care settings.Patients and MethodsIn this prospective, single-arm study conducted from July 1, 2022, through December 31, 2023 (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05455541), 660 adult patients presenting to the emergency department or internal medicine wards were assessed with handheld ultrasound devices enhanced by AI algorithms. These algorithms provided automated analysis of ventricular function, valvular disease, pericardial effusion, and inferior vena cava size. Participating physicians received focused training and performed examinations either in response to clinical suspicion or as part of routine evaluation. The primary outcome was whether AI-guided FoCUS contributed to new diagnoses, treatment modifications, or additional procedures.ResultsArtificial intelligence–enhanced FoCUS identified clinically relevant cardiac findings in 193 patients (29%), including newly recognized valvular abnormalities and reduced left ventricular function. In 49 patients (7%), medical therapy was adjusted based on findings, and 9 patients (1.4%) underwent interventional procedures. Diagnostic performance analyses showed high sensitivity for detecting reduced left ventricular function and valvular disease, with lower sensitivity for right-sided abnormalities.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that integrating AI-enhanced FoCUS into routine workflows can provide clinically relevant information that may influence diagnostic assessment and management by noncardiology practitioners in acute care settings.

Cardiovascular diseases contribute substantially to morbidity and mortality.1,2 Although considered a fundamental diagnostic method, cardiac auscultation is limited by low sensitivity and specificity.3 In primary care settings, where comprehensive diagnostic resources may be scarce, reliance on auscultation by noncardiologists for detecting structural or functional cardiac abnormalities is problematic.4,5 Echocardiography, owing to its versatility, safety, and technological advancements, has become the gold standard for cardiovascular evaluation.6 However, its widespread use is constrained by high equipment costs, the need for specialized technicians, and limited access to board-certified cardiologists.7 These limitations can delay diagnosis in urgent and routine care alike, underscoring the need for accessible, rapid, and reliable diagnostic alternatives.

Focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS) is a readily accessible tool for noncardiology practitioners, enabling rapid preliminary assessment of cardiac structure and function.8, 9, 10 Implementation frameworks and professional societies emphasize training, device availability, and clinical context to support safe integration into practice.11, 12, 13

Previous studies have indicated that FoCUS is effective in assessing left ventricular (LV) function.14 However, evaluating other core cardiac findings, such as valvular pathology with FoCUS, requires additional training and expertise.15,16 Therefore, the integration of FoCUS in clinical routine has progressed slowly,17, 18, 19 primarily due to a shortage of experts available for bedside supervision and reliable interpretation of findings.20,21

These challenges may be mitigated by applying machine learning and artificial intelligence (AI) tools, which have shown strong performance in echocardiographic image recognition and disease detection.22 Integrating AI into FoCUS may help overcome barriers to its widespread use and enhance clinical decision making. However, evidence on the real-world impact of AI-enhanced FoCUS on patient management remains limited. This study, therefore, aimed to evaluate its effect on clinical assessment and decision making by noncardiology residents in routine care settings.

Patients And Methods

This prospective, single-center, single-arm study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05455541) was conducted at Sheba Medical Center, Israel’s largest tertiary hospital, which recorded more than 224,000 emergency department (ED) visits and 613,000 hospitalization days in 2022. Adults presenting to the ED or admitted to internal medicine (IM) wards were eligible.

Patients with a comprehensive transthoracic echocardiogram within 3 months, complex structural heart disease (eg, congenital disease, prior surgical or transcatheter valve intervention, and complex cardiomyopathies) or prosthetic valves were excluded following prospective electronic medical record (EMR) review. Limited or focused echocardiographic examinations were not exclusion criteria.

Participating physicians were IM and emergency medicine residents without formal echocardiography training beyond brief undergraduate workshops. All completed a 6-hour hands-on session on 2-dimensional and color Doppler image acquisition using human models. Examinations were performed using Philips Lumify S4-1 handheld point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) devices connected to Samsung S7 tablets (Suwon-si) integrated with AISAP.ai software (Ramat-Gan, Israel). Devices were institutionally or company purchased; the hardware manufacturers had no role in the study, although the manufacturer of the AI software contributed to algorithm development, as detailed in the disclosures.

The FoCUS protocol (Supplemental Figure 1, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/) included 4 standard views—parasternal long- and short-axis, apical 4-chamber, and subcostal views. Training emphasized 2-dimensional and color Doppler interpretation; advanced Doppler modalities, including spectral (pulsed and continuous wave) and tissue Doppler imaging, were excluded.

The AI-powered software integrated into the ultrasound system suggested potential diagnoses, their severity, and assessed the diagnostic-quality of each video clip. This capability provided real-time feedback on image quality and operator performance, allowing immediate technique correction and enabling diagnostic-quality imaging after limited training (Supplemental Figure 2A-C, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). Each resident’s first 20 scans were supervised by a cardiologist before independent scanning. Moreover, AI-enhanced FoCUS was not intended to serve as a comprehensive screening examination for all potentially life-threatening cardiac conditions. Residents were trained to identify grossly abnormal findings within the FoCUS scope, including severe ventricular dysfunction, large pericardial effusion, or suspected severe valvular disease. A cardiologist–sonographer support team was available for rapid consultation with a predefined escalation pathway, including expedited formal echocardiography.

AISAP.ai Algorithm

The AI algorithm used (Supplemental Figure 3, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/) in this study was developed from 120,127 labeled echocardiographic examinations performed at Sheba Medical Center between 2009 and 2020. The data set was randomly divided into training (76,342 cases; 62%), validation (22,825; 19%), and hold-out test (20,960; 17%) sets, with board-certified cardiologist readings based on American Society of Echocardiography guidelines serving as the reference standard. Using convolutional neural networks, the model provided continuous numerical estimates for LV ejection fraction (LVEF) and inferior vena cava (IVC) diameter and ordinal severity classification for valvular disease and right ventricular (RV) function. In a noninterventional validation study using 5203 echocardiographic examinations from 2021 (Supplemental Table, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/), reference interpretations were established by board-certified cardiologists using standard American Society of Echocardiography–based measurements and severity grading. Structural measurements such as LVEF and IVC diameter were evaluated as continuous quantitative outputs and compared with cardiologist measurements using root-mean-square error (RMSE), whereas valvular pathology detection was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic metrics. The model achieved sensitivities and specificities of 79.6% and 95.1% for mitral regurgitation (MR) greater than mild (≥moderate), 86.5% and 93.2% for tricuspid regurgitation (TR) greater than mild (≥moderate), 74.1% and 97.2% for aortic regurgitation (AR) greater than mild (≥moderate), 95.9% and 89.2% for aortic stenosis (AS) greater than mild (≥moderate), and 91.3% and 93.8% for RV dysfunction greater than mild (≥moderate), respectively. Furthermore, LVEF estimation had an RMSE of 4.68 percentage points (r=0.91), and IVC diameter estimation had an RMSE of 4.09 mm (r=0.78).

In a US multicenter Food and Drug Administration (FDA) study, AISAP CARDIO V1.0 system (FDA-cleared, 510(k) K234141, September 2024) achieved more than 93% sensitivity and specificity for significant valvular and ventricular abnormalities, with interreader agreement [κ] of 0.90 and RMSEs below expert cardiologist benchmarks (Mass General Brigham core lab).

FoCUS Acquisition

Following training, participating medical residents performed AI-enhanced FoCUS examinations as part of their clinical duties. Examinations were performed either when cardiac pathology was suspected or as a routine adjunct to physical examination. Verbal informed consent was obtained after explaining how potential findings could influence diagnostic or therapeutic decisions.

Residents were encouraged to incorporate AI-FoCUS findings into their patient assessments, but the extent of integration remained at their and their attending physician’s discretion. Minor management changes (eg, adjustment of fluids or diuretics) were often based on AI-FoCUS results alone, whereas major decisions involving suspected severe valvular disease or reduced LVEF required confirmation by formal echocardiography.

All AI-generated interpretations, images, and reports were stored in a designated cloud-based system, and summaries were entered into the EMR. Examination duration and total scan count per resident were tracked. A subgroup of patients underwent comprehensive echocardiography. For study analyses, investigators retrospectively compared AI-enhanced FoCUS findings with the corresponding echocardiography reports, which served as the reference standard. Decisions to obtain formal echocardiography were made by the treating team and reflected standard clinical practice. No echocardiographic studies were performed solely for research purposes. Formal echocardiograms were interpreted by cardiologists who were not involved in the study and were not provided with AI-enhanced FoCUS findings or study context at the time of reporting. After discharge, investigators reviewed EMRs and cloud-stored data, including hospitalization records, imaging, procedures, medications, demographics, and clinical history. Although the AI-enhanced FoCUS protocol was standardized, some examinations were abbreviated owing to clinical constraints (eg, patient instability, limited cooperation, and time sensitivity) or technical limitations in image acquisition.

Definition of the Primary Outcome

The primary study outcome was a change in patient management following the AI-enhanced FoCUS examination, defined using objective criteria and classified into the following 3 categories: (1) detection of previously undocumented significant echocardiographic findings—moderate or greater valvular disease, moderate or greater RV dysfunction, or LV dysfunction with LVEF of less than 40%; (2) modification of medication therapy following AI-FoCUS, particularly treatments affecting LV function or volume status interpreted in the clinical context; and (3) initiation of valvular or coronary interventions prompted by AI-FoCUS findings.

Although the AI algorithm outputs ordinal severity categories, clinically significant disease was defined a priori as moderate valvular disease or more or RV dysfunction; LVEF was generated as a continuous numerical estimate and categorized only for outcome analyses. Moderate abnormalities were included in outcome definitions when they informed diagnostic assessment, triage, or follow-up decisions.

A documented statement in the EMR, explicitly linking the AI-FoCUS findings to these management changes, was required for outcome confirmation. Cases with pre-existing diagnoses, such as acute myocardial infarction or known valvular disease already meeting criteria for intervention, were excluded from classification as AI-FoCUS–influenced changes. Only FoCUS findings that were not previously documented in the medical record were classified as new. Given the observational study design and concurrent availability of other clinical data, management changes were interpreted as temporally associated with AI-enhanced FoCUS findings rather than causally attributable to the AI system alone.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution are expressed as mean ± SD and compared using unpaired t tests. Nonnormally distributed variables are presented as median (range) and analyzed with the Mann–Whitney or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for dependent samples. Categorical variables are reported as percentages and compared using χ2 or Fisher exact tests. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp) and R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

For diagnostic performance subanalyzes, sensitivity and specificity were calculated by comparing AI-enhanced FoCUS results with formal echocardiography in the subset of patients who underwent confirmatory echocardiography during the same hospitalization, reflecting diagnostic performance within a clinically selected cohort. Based on previous studies reporting approximately 52% prevalence of management changes after cardiac POCUS, a sample size of at least 384 patients was required to achieve a 95% CI, with a 5% margin of error.

Results

From July 1, 2022, through December 31, 2023, 660 patients were enrolled by 13 participating physicians (Table 1, Supplemental Figure 4, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). Each resident performed between 8 and 158 examinations (median, 29; mean, 50). The average duration of each examination was 7 minutes and 20 seconds (range, 1.5-13 minutes). No operators were excluded for poor performance. Examinations of short duration (<4 minutes) were typically limited assessments of pericardial effusion, IVC diameter, or LV function. A valid LVEF estimate was generated in 635 of 660 patients (96%). Valvular assessment was feasible in 89% for AS, 92% for AR, 90% for MR, and 69% for TR; RV function was assessed in 595 patients (90%), IVC diameter in 452 (68%), and pericardial effusion in 504 (76%). Of 660 patients, 519 (79%) were hospitalized in IM wards, and 141 (21%) were evaluated in the ED. The mean age was 66 ± 17 years (range, 18-101 years), and 408 (62%) were men. Pre-existing conditions included heart failure in 102 (15%), ischemic heart disease in 216 (32%), and atrial fibrillation in 119 (18%). Cardiovascular risk factors were common: hypertension in 319 patients (48%), dyslipidemia in 287 (43%), diabetes in 188 (28%), and current or recent smoking in 141 (21%). Known valvular heart disease (VHD) was documented in 41 (6%). The median body mass index was 26.2 kg/m2 (IQR, 23.2-29.3 kg/m2). AI-Enhanced FoCUS Findings

Among 660 individual examinations conducted, 1 or more significant diagnoses were identified in 193 (29%) patients. Significant LV dysfunction (LVEF < 40%) was present in 71 (11%), and significant RV dysfunction was detected in 17 (3%). Significant valvular disease included AS in 63 (10%), AR in 24 (4%), significant MR in 96 (15%), and TR in 64 (10%). Additionally, 38 (5%) cases of actionable pericardial effusion detected, as well as dilated IVC (>20 mm) present in 112 (17%) cases. Overall, clinically significant AI-enhanced FoCUS findings (≥1 per patient) were identified in 117 of 408 men (28.7%) and 76 of 252 women (30.2%). Among significant echocardiographic findings, the majority were of moderate severity, whereas a smaller subset represented moderate to severe or severe abnormalities.

Discussion

The main findings of this study demonstrate that AI-supported FoCUS can be integrated into routine clinical workflows and provide real-time information that can be incorporated into bedside clinical assessment. When used either symptom driven or adjunctive to physical examination, it revealed at least 1 previously undocumented cardiac diagnosis in 22% of patients and prompted immediate therapeutic changes in 7%. Many moderate abnormalities did not mandate immediate intervention but may have contributed to diagnostic clarification, risk stratification, and follow-up decisions. The AI-enhanced FoCUS was used as an adjunct to standard clinical practice, and on retrospective chart review, we did not identify documented adverse events clearly attributable to AI-FoCUS–associated decisions.

Given the high prevalence of cardiac pathology in older adults,23,24 many cases likely remain undetected. Increasing referrals for inpatient echocardiography would be neither practical nor efficient, as such studies are time-consuming and resource intensive. Moreover, because valvular and ventricular abnormalities are common, some findings may be incidental; thus, unselective use of this technology should be avoided.

Although integrating AI-enhanced FoCUS into routine clinical workflows by noncardiologists is unique to this study, our findings partly corroborate previous research highlighting the feasibility and clinical value of FoCUS in acute care. Studies by Zanobetti et al25 and Ben-Baruch Golan et al26 demonstrated POCUS improved early diagnostic accuracy in patients with dyspnea or chest pain. Conversely, Cid-Serra et al,27 found no clear benefit—likely reflecting differences in timing, operator training, and the absence of AI assistance. These contrasts highlight the importance of appropriate implementation and the potential of AI to enhance FoCUS utility.

Applications of AI in medical diagnostics are expected to improve clinical medicine, detect high-risk patients, and improve patient outcomes.28, 29, 30 So far, most AI research in echocardiography has focused on measuring the size and function of cardiac structures rather than identifying pathology or predicting risk of outcomes.31, 32, 33 Additionally, many recently developed AI tools use high-end transthoracic echocardiography machines in dedicated imaging laboratories.34,35 In contrast, our study presents a novel, AI-enhanced FoCUS tool used in a real-world clinical setting as part of physicians’ initial patient assessment. The cloud-based AI platform used in this study connects seamlessly to an application installed on the POCUS device (eg, a transducer connected to a tablet), with results readily available to the physician immediately after scanning for review and approval, facilitating decision making within routine care. Although traditional FoCUS has proven effective in detecting cardiac abnormalities—including LV enlargement and hypertrophy, LV systolic dysfunction, left and right atrial enlargement, RV systolic function, pericardial effusion, and IVC size11—our study’s results suggest that AI-enhanced FoCUS may further enhance these capabilities. Specifically, adding AI could improve precision in identifying and assessing the extent of valvular dysfunction, even without advanced echocardiography protocols or extended training.

In our study, the participating residents could produce practical clinical insights using the AI-enhanced FoCUS despite limited training. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential positive effects of limited sonographic training, even among less qualified health care providers. For instance, Zisis et al36 reported that nurses with basic training could acquire diagnostic-quality cardiac and pulmonary ultrasound images and generate reports predictive of acute decompensated heart failure outcomes. We hypothesize that the diagnostic capabilities of the participating residents in this study were enhanced using AI-enhanced FoCUS. Notably, the integration of AI accelerated the learning process, even within the context of a relatively short training protocol.

Of note, previously undocumented significant VHD was suggested at the bedside in 95 patients (15%). Moreover, previously undocumented significant AS was detected in 39 cases and previously undocumented significant MR in 68 cases, disease conditions where there is ample evidence that close follow-up combined with timely intervention translates into survival benefits.37,38 A similar trend has been shown in the OxVALVE study, which included 2500 echocardiographically screened individuals aged 65 years or older; half of the patients had undiagnosed VHD (predominantly mild), whereas 6% of the cohort had clinically significant (moderate to severe) undiagnosed VHD, indicating substantial underdiagnosis at the population level, with the means of traditional physical examination.39,40

Clinical diagnosis of LV failure or dysfunction at the bedside has also been shown to be challenging in various clinical scenarios.41,42 Heart failure can occur with reduced or preserved LV ejection fraction (EF), so a complete diagnosis requires echocardiography or another cardiac imaging modality owing to the low accuracy of point-of-care workup and chest radiography.41,43 We detected 37 (5%) patients with a newly diagnosed significant reduction in LVEF. Our subanalysis (n=117) demonstrated a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 97% in accurately detecting significantly reduced LVEF. These findings are consistent with recent studies, including those by Olaisen et al,44 which used echocardiographic examinations and demonstrated that AI-based measurements are superior in interobserver variability and noninferior in intraobserver variability when assessing ejection fraction (EF) and LV size, compared with manual measurements. Furthermore, Kagiyama et al,45 in a real-world multicenter study, showed that AI-assisted POCUS provides accurate assessments of LVEF. In their study, reduced LVEF (<50%) was detected with a sensitivity of 85% (95% CI, 76%-91%) and a specificity of 81% (95% CI, 71%-89%). Although limited to the assessment of LVEF, a diagnostic accuracy study of POCUS with AI-assisted assessment by Motazedian et al46 corresponds with our study results in establishing a feasible sensitivity of 92.8%, with a specificity of 92.3% in identifying an abnormal LVEF (<50%).

Notably, a lower sensitivity was observed in assessing RV dysfunction (40%) and significant TR (60%). These latter results can be attributed to the low prevalence of these conditions in our cohort and the technical difficulties experienced by the participating physicians in accurately acquiring views of RV structures.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Although prospectively designed, it lacked a randomized control group, as the primary objective was to evaluate the routine implementation of AI-enhanced FoCUS in real-world practice. Importantly, the absence of a comparator arm precludes assessment of the diagnostic or clinical benefit attributable specifically to AI augmentation; some findings identified by AI-enhanced FoCUS may otherwise have been detected later through routine practice. Because ED and IM patients differ in characteristics and care priorities, separate analyses were performed (Tables 1 and 2). Although patient profiles varied between settings, the overall clinical impact was similar, with AI-FoCUS influencing management in both groups.

Most FoCUS examinations were symptom driven, potentially increasing diagnostic sensitivity through higher pretest probability. Diagnostic performance analyses were limited to a clinically selected subgroup undergoing echocardiography, introducing verification bias and limiting generalizability. In addition, diagnostic accuracy was not evaluated across subgroups defined by body mass index, cardiac rhythm, or image quality, and the study was not powered to assess subgroup-specific performance.

The AI-enhanced FoCUS algorithms were not designed for patients with complex structural heart disease or prosthetic valves. Because baseline echocardiography utilization rates were unavailable and no comparator group was included, the impact of AI-enhanced FoCUS on the use of formal echocardiography during hospitalization could not be assessed. No independent data monitoring committee was used, and residual interpretive bias cannot be fully excluded. Finally, Black and East Asian individuals represent less than 2% of Israel’s population, limiting generalizability and warranting further validation in these groups.47

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that integrating AI-enhanced FoCUS into routine clinical workflows can provide clinically relevant information that may influence diagnostic assessment and management by noncardiology practitioners in ED and IM settings.
