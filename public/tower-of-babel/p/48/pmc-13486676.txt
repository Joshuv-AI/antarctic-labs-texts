Biodiversity buffers forest ecosystems from compound climate extremes
Authors: Xuetao Qiao, Jakob Zscheischler, Michel Loreau, Peter B. Reich, Qingqing Chen, Michael Bahn, Neha Mohanbabu, Hannah J. White, Mingyu Luo, Shaopeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Forest ecosystems provide key ecological, social, and climate benefits, yet their functions are potentially threatened by rising compound extremes characterised by simultaneous heat and drought or moisture excess. Here, we explore how forests resist and recover from compound heat and moisture extremes and how tree diversity mediates these responses, using U.S. forest inventory data along with satellite-based stability estimates. We find that compound extremes result in lower forest resistance and recovery than individual extremes. Compound extremes tend to exhibit synergistic effects, causing greater negative impacts than the sum of individual extremes. Compound heat and drought have slightly stronger destabilising effects than compound heat and moisture excess. Higher tree diversity consistently improves resistance and recovery under both individual and compound extremes. Our findings suggest that focusing solely on individual climate extremes is likely to underestimate the impacts of compound extremes and to increase uncertainty in projections of terrestrial carbon-cycle feedbacks, while also highlighting the critical role of tree diversity in mitigating these threats under future climate conditions.

Introduction

Forests contribute about half of the gross primary productivity of terrestrial ecosystems and hold huge potential for future carbon sequestration1–3. However, how forests respond to anthropogenic climate change remains unclear and contributes one of the largest uncertainties in the projections of the terrestrial carbon cycle in the future4,5. Climate change is evident not only by shifts in average climate conditions but also by increasing occurrences of extreme climate events, including heat, drought, and excessive moisture. Such climate extremes can drastically alter key ecosystem processes and potentially trigger tipping points, which may destabilise the functioning of forest ecosystems4. Moreover, different types of climate extremes can occur simultaneously in the same region, referred to as compound extremes6,7. The effects of compound extremes are of increasing concern due to their potentially severe impacts on ecosystems6,8,9. In particular, several recent studies have highlighted the widespread occurrence of compound heat and moisture extremes (either drought or moisture excess) and discovered their negative impacts on ecosystem functions, including increased mortality rates of trees and reductions in forest carbon sequestration, as well as threats to global crop yields10–12. Understanding the stability of forest ecosystems in the face of compound heat and moisture extremes is critical for predicting forest responses to future climate change and reducing the uncertainty in projections of the terrestrial carbon cycle.

The capacity of forest ecosystems to withstand perturbations (e.g., climate extremes) and maintain their functioning is referred to as resistance, one of the most common measures of ecological stability13,14. Existing research on the resistance of forest ecosystems has mainly focused on individual extremes, such as heatwaves or extreme drought or moisture excess14–16. The resistance of ecosystems to compound extremes and how it compares with their resistance to individual extremes remains largely unknown. When occurring separately, heat and moisture extremes affect plant growth and survival in distinct ways4,17: heat extremes can impair photosynthesis and increase respiration rates18, while moisture extremes can lead to waterlogging or drought stress19. When occurring simultaneously, compound heat and moisture extremes may exhibit interactive effects on plant growth and ecosystem functioning11, wherein the presence of one extreme may exacerbate or mitigate the effects of the other. Such interactions can result in greater (synergistic) or smaller (antagonistic) effects of compound extremes compared with the additive effects of individual extremes20,21. Meta-analyses of multi-stressor experiments have shown that antagonistic and additive interactions are more common than synergistic ones22,23. Yet, there is still a lack of empirical studies in natural forest ecosystems comparing resistance in the face of compound vs. individual extremes and testing whether effects of different extreme events are synergistic or antagonistic.

Climate extremes not only affect ecosystem functions during the disturbance but also produce lagged effects on ecosystems after the disturbance has ended, resulting in an incomplete recovery of the ecosystem24,25. The latter phenomenon, often referred to as the ‘legacy effect’, has been demonstrated for various aspects of ecosystem structure and functioning25–27. Large legacy effects, indicated by an incomplete recovery after disturbances, add uncertainty to terrestrial carbon cycle feedbacks and hinders our ability to accurately predict future forest dynamics27. Despite the growing literature illustrating incomplete recovery from individual extremes due to legacy effects25–27, whether compound extremes lead to more severe recovery failure remains largely unexplored. In addition, the relationship between resistance and the completeness of recovery remains unclear, especially under compound extremes. Ecosystem resistance and recovery may be positively correlated, suggesting that systems better able to withstand disturbances may also recover more fully afterwards. Alternatively, resistance and recovery may exhibit a trade-off, such that ecosystems that resist initial impacts more strongly may recover less fully in post-disturbance phases28. Filling this knowledge gap is crucial for evaluating the overall stability of forest ecosystems during and after disturbance.

In the face of environmental change, species diversity may provide biological insurance to maintain and buffer ecosystem functioning29–31. A large number of studies have shown that diverse communities can be more resistant to climate extremes than depauperate communities14,32,33, though neutral and even negative effects of biodiversity have also been reported34. However, previous studies have mainly focused on the effect of biodiversity in the face of individual climate extremes, but whether species diversity continues to provide insurance effects under the increasing occurrence of compound extremes remains unclear. By imposing greater stress on ecosystems, compound extremes might overwhelm the buffering capacity of biodiversity and break up the diversity–stability relationship35. For instance, when most species are suppressed by the number or magnitude of stressors, there are few compensatory fluctuations among species, leading biodiversity to lose its stabilising effect36,37. Alternatively, the more severe stress from compound extremes might trigger more positive interactions between species and thereby enhance the benefits of species diversity on ecosystem stability38. Testing these hypotheses requires empirical analyses on the relationships between biodiversity and resistance or recovery under various scenarios of climate extremes.

Here, we combine forest plot data from the U.S. Forest Inventory and Analysis programme with satellite-derived estimates of annual productivity to quantify the stability of forest ecosystems at multiple response phases (i.e., disturbance and post-disturbance phases) under compound and individual extremes. For each forest plot, climate extremes are identified when climate anomalies in some year exceed a pre-assigned percentile threshold of the long-term distribution of climate conditions (i.e., during the period 1901–2020). Individual extremes refer to years with a single extreme event, while compound extremes involve extreme heat co-occurring with either extreme drought or excess moisture. Across all of the 88,116 forest plots, compound heat and moisture extremes were detected at least once between 2001 and 2020; in some plots, up to 6 events of compound heat and moisture extremes were detected (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1b). We exclude the case of compound cold and moisture extremes from our analysis due to their rarity during our study period (Supplementary Figs. 2–5). We assess the stability of forest ecosystems by their resistance during the climate extremes and their recovery. Resistance is calculated as the absolute value of the proportional deviation in forest productivity between normal and extreme years, and recovery is quantified as the degree to which forest productivity returns to normal levels in the year following a climate extreme (see “Methods”). To avoid confounding effects related to consecutive extreme events, we exclude from our analyses all years in which plots experience consecutive extremes. To test the robustness of our results with regard to the choice of recovery metrics, we repeat our analyses using an alternative approach, comparing observed productivity after the extreme with that predicted from productivity-climate relationships (see “Methods”). We employ multiple approaches to compare forest resistance and recovery in the face of compound versus individual extremes, and explore their relationships with biodiversity. Focusing on forest stability in multiple response phases under compound extremes, we address three questions: (1) Do forest ecosystems exhibit lower resistance and recovery under compound extremes than under individual extremes, and how are resistance and recovery related? (2) Do compound heat and moisture extremes exhibit antagonistic or synergistic effects, and does compound heat and drought or compound heat and moisture excess cause greater damage? and (3) How does tree species diversity influence forest resistance and recovery when forests are exposed to compound extremes? Our analyses reveal that compound extremes tend to exhibit synergistic effects, resulting in lower forest resistance and recovery than individual extremes. However, higher tree diversity consistently improves forest resistance and recovery under individual and compound extremes. This study enhances our understanding of how forests respond to compound extremes, underscoring the increased uncertainty compound extremes pose for projecting the terrestrial carbon cycle and the urgent need to conserve biodiversity under intensifying climate pressures.

Results

Reduced resistance and recovery of forest ecosystems to compound extremes

Our analyses revealed that resistance was lower under compound extremes than under the corresponding individual extremes (Fig. 2a, p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 6, t test: p < 0.001). Specifically, forest resistance to compound heat and moisture excess was lower than that to heat alone, and lower than that to moisture excess alone (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 7a, p < 0.001). Resistance to compound heat and drought was lower than that to either drought alone or heat alone (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 7b, p < 0.001). Similarly, forest recovery was also significantly lower under compound extremes than under individual extremes (Fig. 2b, p < 0.001). Specifically, forest recovery under compound heat and moisture excess was lower than that under moisture excess alone, and lower than that under heat alone (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 7c, p < 0.001). Recovery under compound heat and drought was lower than that under either drought alone or heat alone (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 7d, p < 0.001). These patterns were consistent across ecoregions (Supplementary Fig. 7). For recovery, results were also robust to different methods of quantification (Supplementary Fig. 8). To understand the interplay of co-occurring climate extremes, we compared ecosystem responses under compound extremes to those expected from an additive effect of individual extremes. We define heat and moisture extremes as having synergistic effects if ecosystem responses to compound extremes are greater than the sum of responses to each individual extreme, and antagonistic effects if ecosystem responses to compound extremes are weaker than the sum of responses to each individual extreme. We found that heat and moisture excess exhibited synergistic effects in 53% and 63% of plots, respectively, during the disturbance and post-disturbance phases. Heat and drought exhibited synergistic effects in 72% of plots during the disturbance and 69% of plots during post-disturbance phases (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 11). This suggests that the effects of compound extremes – particularly compound heat and drought – on forest ecosystem functioning were overall synergistic, both during and after the disturbance phases (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 11). We further found consistently positive correlations between resistance and recovery, under both individual and compound extremes (Supplementary Fig. 12). In other words, higher resistance during the disturbance phase was associated with greater recovery during the post-disturbance phase. Discussion

As compound climate extremes become increasingly frequent and severe, it is essential to assess their long-term impacts on natural forest communities and to identify the ecological factors that govern forest stability. By leveraging extensive forest survey data and satellite-derived productivity measures across the United States, our analysis shows that compound climate extremes result in lower ecosystem resistance and recovery than do individual extremes, but tree species diversity tends to mitigate this stress and enhance forest ecosystem stability.

Compared with individual extremes of heat, drought or moisture excess alone, compound heat and moisture extremes reduced the productivity of forest ecosystems more significantly. Ecosystem impacts may be amplified by multiple stressors due to the potential for mutually reinforcing effects between stressors4,17,19. Our study demonstrates that the combined effects of extremes tend to be synergistic, resulting in greater negative impacts than the sum of the impacts of individual extremes (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 11). For example, extreme heat can intensify extreme drought by increasing evapotranspiration and exacerbating water stress, while under waterlogged conditions, it exacerbates hypoxia and root damage10,11. This finding aligns with recent proposals to integrate extreme events into research on multiple stressors39. We further found that following the disturbance phase, recovery was notably less complete under compound heat and moisture extremes than under individual extremes, indicating that compound extremes impose more pronounced legacy effects on forest ecosystems after disturbances. These results indicate that studies focusing only on the disturbance phase can underestimate the impact of climate extremes on forest ecosystems7,11, particularly under compound extremes. This complexity increases uncertainty about terrestrial carbon cycle feedbacks, which hinders our ability to accurately predict future forest dynamics and their impacts on ecosystem functioning and terrestrial carbon balance25,26. Overall, we argue that the simultaneous occurrence of extreme heat and moisture anomalies amplifies stress on forests and undermines the ability of forests to resist and recover, exerting direct and long-term negative impacts on forest health and carbon sequestration.

Different types of compound extremes, however, can have different impacts on ecosystem functioning across multiple response phases. We found that compound heat and drought exert slightly stronger negative impacts on forest ecosystem stability during both the disturbance and post-disturbance phases than compound heat and moisture excess, as reflected by lower resistance, lower recovery and a higher proportion of synergistic effects (Figs. 3, 4 and Supplementary Fig. 11). The reason could be that extreme drought has a direct impact on ecosystem functions by reducing water supply, inhibiting photosynthesis and growth, and causing oxidative stress and plant death, making it more disruptive than the indirect effects of extreme moisture excess, which primarily acts through oxygen deficiency4,18,19. Together with previous studies illustrating the negative impacts of compound heat and drought on critical ecosystem functions including elevated tree mortality, reduced forest carbon sequestration, and potential threats to global crop productivity10–12, our findings emphasise that such events require special attention from managers in order to mitigate the risk of serious consequences.

Tree species diversity consistently enhances forest ecosystem resistance to various types of climate extremes, with more diverse communities generally experiencing smaller proportional productivity changes under both individual and compound extremes. This finding aligns with earlier results from controlled plant diversity experiments under moisture anomalies alone14,33, as well as recent global observations of forests experiencing extreme droughts alone15. We further found that higher tree species diversity enhances the completeness of ecosystem recovery after climate extremes, thereby mitigating their legacy effects on forest ecosystems. These results suggest that biodiversity benefits forest ecosystems facing climate extremes across multiple response phases. Biodiversity enhances the buffering capacity of forests, reduces damage during disturbances, and decreases uncertainties about terrestrial carbon cycle feedbacks by mitigating the legacy effect of climate extremes.

Building on previous findings that biodiversity enhances ecosystem stability under individual extremes, our study provides large-scale empirical evidence that this stabilising effect extends to compound extremes14,32,33. Although compound extremes did not overwhelm the buffering capacity of biodiversity to alter the direction of its effects, they modulated its strength, suggesting that multiple climatic stressors can influence the benefits of biodiversity on ecosystem stability. Specifically, tree species richness consistently enhanced resistance and recovery across both individual and compound extremes, yet the strength of these biodiversity effects varied considerably among types of climate extremes. Notably, stronger biodiversity effects on resistance were found under drought alone and compound heat and drought, whereas compound heat and moisture excess and heat alone resulted in relatively weaker effects. Similarly, recovery exhibited stronger biodiversity effects under compound heat and drought and drought alone, but weaker effects under compound heat and moisture excess and moisture excess alone. These findings emphasise that the stabilising effects of tree species richness are not uniform but context-dependent, particularly under compounding climatic stressors. A recent meta-analysis of controlled experiments showed that biodiversity enhances ecosystem productivity to a greater extent in the presence of individual environmental stressors30. Our results corroborate these findings and extend them to natural ecosystems in a multi-stressor context. This highlights the crucial importance of conserving forest biodiversity to mitigate the negative impacts of future compound extremes on the functioning and stability of forest ecosystems.

Our analyses show that tree species diversity had low explanatory power for both ecosystem resistance and recovery, a pattern consistent across ecoregions and within individual ecoregions (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Fig. 13). This result aligns with recent studies reporting a positive, yet weak, association between diversity and other measures of stability (e.g., temporal invariability)40,41. These low R² values indicate that tree species richness alone is not sufficient to predict forest ecosystem resistance or recovery, and that environmental factors such as precipitation, temperature, and soil properties must also be taken into account. However, from a management perspective, broad-scale environmental factors are difficult to manipulate3,33,41, whereas tree species diversity can be actively managed through silvicultural and conservation practices. Therefore, despite its limited explanatory power across sites, tree biodiversity remains an important practical strategic objective for enhancing forest stability at each site.

Resistance and recovery characterise the responses of ecosystems during and after climate extremes, respectively. The observed positive relationship between resistance and recovery indicates that higher resistance not only mitigates the negative impacts of climate extremes but also facilitates more complete ecosystem recovery. Our findings reveal that community attributes such as stand age and tree species diversity significantly influence the magnitude of recovery. In this study, stand age for each plot represents the approximate age of the dominant age class and thus captures broad contrasts between young and older forests. Our results thus suggest that older forest communities have greater resistance to and recovery from climate extremes. Remarkably, the effect size of diversity on both resistance and recovery was larger than that of stand age, suggesting the important role of preserving tree species diversity in stabilising ecosystem functioning.

This study focused on legacy effects one year after climate extremes. Our results proved robust when validated using an alternative approach that compared observed post- extreme productivity with climate-predicted values. In line with these findings, recent data from 76 eddy covariance sites have shown that drought legacy effects typically persist for up to one year, and are comparable in magnitude to the effect of the drought itself 42. However, future work could extend the present study to investigate legacy effects that last longer than one year. This would help link short-term responses to climate extremes to long-term changes in ecosystem functioning. In addition, our study focused on isolated extreme years, i.e., one extreme followed by a recovery period, in order to avoid confounding the legacy effect of the first event with the direct impact of subsequent events. When a new drought occurs before the system has fully recovered from a previous drought, legacy effects may accumulate and reduce productivity more strongly than in the case of a single event42–44. As a consequence, our estimates of drought legacy effects should be viewed as conservative lower bounds on the potential impact of recurrent extremes on forest productivity and stability. Future work that explicitly examines sequences of extreme years and incomplete recovery will be important to quantify the full risk that compound climate extremes pose to forests.

In a changing world where temperature and moisture anomalies become increasingly common, forest ecosystems will suffer from compound climate extremes, which often exert stronger negative impacts than individual extremes. Confronted with more harmful compound extremes, tree species diversity may continue to provide biological insurance effects that buffer ecosystems against the adverse impacts of extreme events. Our work underlines the crucial role of biodiversity in maintaining ecosystem resistance, highlighting the fact that diverse forests are better equipped to withstand and recover from the increasing severity of compound extremes under future climate scenarios. Projections of terrestrial carbon dynamics and the role of forests as nature-based climate solutions should take into account the responses of forest ecosystems to compound climate extremes.

Methods

Forest plot data and filtering

Our study covers the continental United States, utilising data from the Forest Inventory and Analysis programme of the U.S. Department of Agriculture Forest Service (FIA), which is a systematic and standardised survey of forested areas throughout the United States45. These fixed-grid permanent plots are systematically distributed across the forested areas of the 48 contiguous states, with a spatial sampling intensity of one plot per ~ 2400 ha46. Each plot consists of four subplots with a radius of 7.32 m: one located at the centre and the other three arranged in an equilateral triangle, spaced 36.6 m apart. Following the FIA sampling protocol, trees with a diameter at breast height (DBH) of at least 12.7 cm were measured on subplots with a radius of 7.32 m. Smaller trees, defined as saplings with DBH from 2.54 to 12.7 cm, were measured on associated microplots with a radius of 2.07 m47. Because the sampling areas differ between subplots and microplots, we normalised tree counts to a per-unit-area basis using FIA tree-level expansion factors. Beginning in the late 1990s, the FIA initiated a nationally standardised annual inventory. Under this programme, approximately 10 percent of plots in the western United States and about 20 percent of plots in the eastern United States are remeasured each year. The interval between remeasurements is roughly 10 years for the western plots and about 5 years for the eastern plots.

Before our analyses, we strictly filtered forest plots based on several criteria47,48. We only used data collected in forested areas identified as natural stands, excluding those classified as artificial regeneration. We further restricted our analyses to conditions with no observable treatment and excluded conditions with recorded treatments or recorded disturbances (e.g., harvesting, grazing, insects/disease, or fire) according to FIA disturbance and treatment codes. We filtered plots using FIA metadata (e.g., sample kind and plot design codes) to retain observations collected under the standard annualised inventory protocol with consistent subplot design. Any plots containing missing data, whether measurements or variables, were excluded from the analysis. Ultimately, our analysis encompassed 88,116 plots spread across 22 distinct ecoregions (Supplementary Fig. 1). Each ecoregion is characterised by certain geological features, soil types, and climatic conditions, which collectively sustain similar potential natural vegetation types49. We extracted and quantified geographical factors and plot attributes for each selected plot, including latitude, longitude, elevation, stand age and species richness. Stand age for each plot represents the approximate age of the dominant age class, which should be interpreted as an index of the development stage of that age class rather than the exact age of all trees in the plot. Based on the geographical coordinates of each selected plot, we extracted climatic conditions such as long-term mean annual temperature and total annual precipitation (1970–2000 climatologies) with a resolution of 1 × 1 km from the WorldClim version 2.1 dataset50. Soil properties such as pH, bulk density and total nitrogen content were extracted at 250 m resolution from the SoilGrids dataset51.

Identifying extreme and compound events

We used global climate observation data to assess compound and individual climate extremes in the study area. For this purpose, we employed the CRU TS dataset (Climatic Research Unit Time Series, version 4.07), which provides long-term, gridded monthly temperature and precipitation data for global land areas from 1901 to 2022 at a spatial resolution of 0.5°52. The CRU TS dataset is widely used in climate research because it interpolates station-based observational anomalies to continuous spatial grids using the angular distance weighting method, thereby enabling robust analyses of climate variability and extremes at regional to global scales53. For each plot, we extracted mean temperature and total precipitation during a fixed growing-season window (May-September) for each year from 1901 to 2020. This window broadly captures the main growing season for many forest types across the conterminous United States and provides a consistent basis for interannual comparisons. We used the 1901–2020 record as a historical reference period to characterise long-term variability and to estimate distribution-based percentile thresholds for each plot. For each year between 2001 and 2020, climate extremes were identified when growing-season temperature or precipitation exceeded the upper (90th) percentile or fell below the lower (10th) percentile of the plot-specific 1901–2020 distribution54–56. These cutoffs correspond to approximately the upper or lower 10% of years (≈ once every ~ 10 years on average) and are commonly used to identify climate extremes14,55,56. Percentile thresholds are appropriate in this context because forest ecosystems are adapted to local climatic conditions and variability. For each plot, extreme heat was defined as years with growing-season temperature at or above the 90th percentile, extreme drought as years with growing-season precipitation at or below the 10th percentile, and moisture excess as years with growing-season precipitation at or above the 90th percentile. Individual extremes were defined as years in which only one type of extreme occurred (extreme heat, drought, or moisture excess). Compound extremes were defined as years in which extreme heat co-occurred with either extreme drought or extreme moisture excess7,8,12. This percentile-based definition allows for the comparison of extreme thresholds across different climates, regions, and periods, enabling the identification and quantification of the co-occurrence intensity of climate extremes at different locations53. Because of the long-term increase in global mean temperature, extreme cold events occurred rarely during the study period, as did concurrent cold and moisture excess events (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). Therefore, our analyses focused on compound events involving extreme heat.

To assess future trends in the extent and frequency of compound heat and moisture extremes under different emission scenarios, we extracted projected temperature and precipitation values from five Earth System Models (CESM2, E3SM-1-1, GFDL-ESM4, EC-Earth3, and BCC-ESM1) archived in the sixth phase of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6) and calculated the ensemble mean. CMIP6 models simulate future climate changes under various scenarios. We selected three Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs), namely SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5, which represent the sustainable development, medium emissions, and high emissions pathways, respectively, and cover the period from 2015 to 210057. To avoid inconsistencies arising from mixing different climate datasets, we identified future climate extremes using thresholds derived exclusively from the CMIP6 data. In CMIP6, the period 1900–2014 corresponds to historical simulations, whereas 2015–2100 corresponds to scenario simulations. To align with our historical baseline (1901–2020), we combined historical simulations from 1901 to 2014 with SSP1-2.6 data from 2015 to 2020 to form a continuous reference period. Taking data during 1901–2020 as the historical baseline, we assessed whether climate extremes would occur within each year during the period 2021–2100. This step is solely intended to estimate the general trends in the future frequency and extent of compound extremes, and this approach does not materially affect the results.

Defining resistance and recovery measures

We used the Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) from the MODIS MOD13Q1 product as our proxy of ecosystem function58. The NDVI offers a global measure of vegetation “greenness” across landscapes over a specified period58, which has been demonstrated to have a positive correlation with biomass and annual aboveground net primary productivity across various geographic regions and ecosystems59. As a result, NDVI has been widely utilised to quantify the functioning and stability of grassland and forest ecosystems15,58,59. The MOD13Q1 product provides NDVI data 23 times per year (every 16 days), which has been corrected for geometric and atmospheric conditions. Based on the geographic coordinates of each plot, we extracted NDVI data from 2000 to 2022 and calculated the mean NDVI for each growing season at 250 m resolution.

Statistical analyses

To test the differences between compound heat and moisture extremes and their individual extremes in terms of resistance and recovery, we performed paired comparisons. We identified and selected plots that experienced compound heat and moisture extremes (either drought or excessive moisture), heat alone, drought alone and moisture excess alone during the period from 2001 to 2020. Because consecutive climate extremes make it difficult to separate the legacy effect of the first climate extreme from the direct effect of later climate extremes, we filtered out and excluded years with consecutive climate extremes detected in each plot42–44. We first identified forest plots that experienced at least one compound heat and moisture extreme and at least one occurrence of either heat alone or moisture extreme alone. We then conducted paired comparisons of resistance and recovery by comparing values under the compound extreme with those under heat alone, drought alone and/or moisture excess alone (Fig. 2). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used because it is more robust to outliers and non-normal distributions, and a two-sided t-test was included as a complementary method to confirm the robustness of the results. This procedure allowed us to assess the overall differences between compound extremes and their individual components. As a sensitivity analysis, the comparative analyses of stability responses were repeated after excluding plots with stability values below the 5th percentile or above the 95th percentile under both compound and individual extremes.

We further focused on plots that experienced at least one compound heat and moisture extreme and at least one occurrence of each corresponding individual extreme, i.e., at least one heat alone and at least one moisture extreme alone. For each plot, we compared resistance and recovery under compound heat and moisture excess with those under heat alone and moisture excess alone, and under compound heat and drought with those under heat alone and drought alone (Fig. 3). When the value of the compound extreme was greater than both values of the individual extremes, it was recorded as the compound extreme being greater. Otherwise, it was recorded as the individual extreme being greater. This reflected the differences between different types of compound heat and moisture extremes and their individual extremes.
