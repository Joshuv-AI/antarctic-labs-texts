Development of a General Composite Scale (GENCOMS) for Progressive Neurodegenerative Diseases and Implications for the Assessment of Disease-Modifying Therapies
Authors: Samuel P Dickson, Craig H Mallinckrodt, Basia Rogula, Lauren C Powell, Michele H Potashman, Vladimir Coric, Gilbert J L’Italien, Suzanne B Hendrix
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe reliable assessment of treatment outcomes for disease-modifying therapies (DMT) in neurodegenerative disease is challenging. The objective of this paper is to describe a generalized framework for developing composite scales that can be applied in diverse, degenerative conditions, termed “GENCOMS.” Composite scales optimize the sensitivity for detecting clinically meaningful effects that slow disease progression.MethodsThe GENCOMS method relies on robust natural history data and/or placebo arm data from DMT trials. Validated scales that are core to the disease process have been identified, and item level data obtained to standardize the response outcomes from 0 (best possible score) to 1 (worst possible score). A partial least squares regression analysis was conducted with temporal change as the dependent variable and change scores in standardized items as the explanatory variables. The derived model coefficients constitute a weighted sum of items that most effectively measure disease progression.ResultsThe resultant composite scale was optimized to detect disease progression and can be examined in a range of slow or fast progressing populations. The scale can be used in studies with comparable patient populations as an endpoint optimized to measure disease progression and therefore ideally suited to assess treatment effects in DMTs.ConclusionThe methodology presented here provides a generalizable framework for developing composite scales in the assessment of neurodegenerative disease progression and evaluation of DMT effects. By objectively selecting and weighting items from previously validated measures based solely on their sensitivity to disease progression, this methodology allows for the creation of a more responsive measurement of clinical decline. This heightened sensitivity to clinical decline can be utilized to detect modest yet meaningful treatment effects in the early stages of neurogenerative diseases, when it is optimal to begin a DMT.Keywords: Composite measure, Neurodegenerative disease, Disease progression, Functional impairment, Partial least squares regression

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?In the neurogenerative disease space, it is difficult to reliably assess disease progression and thus the treatment effects of disease-modifying therapies (DMT).This study sought to describe a generalized framework for developing composite scales with heightened sensitivity using partial least squares regression.There is interest across a number of disease areas to develop better measures of disease progression, and this approach can be considered when derivatives of well-established scales may be of interest to explore.What was learned from this study?The resultant composite scale is optimized to detect disease progression and can be used to detect modest yet meaningful treatment effects in the early stages of neurogenerative diseases, when it is optimal to begin a DMT.Composite measures developed using this methodology have the potential to expedite the development of DMTs—an area that is notably challenging given the heterogeneity of disease progression.The GENCOMS approach described in this paper forms the foundation from which further work can be based to develop composite endpoints tailored to the specific needs of various stakeholders in a wide variety of neurologic disorders.

Introduction

Disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) slow or halt disease progression by changing the underlying pathophysiological process [1]. Such treatments fulfill an important unmet need in progressive neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease, frontal temporal dementia, and Huntington’s disease by prolonging higher levels of function for longer time periods, particularly in early stages of disease. Admittedly, there are several conceptual, analytic, and practical challenges to the development of DMTs. The signs and symptoms of progressive neurodegenerative conditions may progress at different times in the disease course [2–5]. Therefore, scales that comprehensively evaluate the condition may have limited sensitivity to change early in disease because of floor/ceiling effects induced by disease aspects that do not change [6].

In early disease, progression is often slow and effect sizes for even highly effective drugs will be small For example, in the CLARIY AD trial, lecanemab slowed progression on the primary endpoint by nearly identical percentages in earlier-stage patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and in patients later in the disease with mild AD (28% and 27%, respectively) [7]. However, mean change on placebo was half the magnitude in patients with MCI. Therefore, the treatment group difference in mean change was also half the magnitude in the mild AD cohort (− 0.35 vs. − 0.62) [7].

Traditional outcome assessments for neurodegenerative disease are not designed to measure disease progression, and usually focus on one specific aspect of the disease. For instance, there are three domains for AD assessment: cognition, function, and global. Traditional AD assessments like ADAS-Cog, ADCS-ADL, and CDR-sb only emphasize one domain each, providing only partial measurement of disease progression. While many may expect that achieving evidence of an effect on one of these outcomes would necessarily provide a high chance of success on an outcome in a second domain, the correlation between outcomes is often 40% or lower [8].

Composite scales that focus on the most progressive aspects of the disease are well-suited to detect changes in early stages of progressive diseases. These can favorably impact sample size estimation and thus contribute to more efficient trial design. Recent examples of composite endpoints include the composite United Huntington Disease Rating Scale, the Alzheimer’s Disease Composite Score, the Alzheimer's Prevention Initiative Preclinical Composite Cognitive test (APCC), and the Spinocerebellar Ataxia Composite Scale [9–13]. Composite scales are developed using objective selection and weighting of the scale items based on their responsiveness to disease progression, resulting in a more sensitive measure of disease progression.

The purpose of this paper is to describe a general methodology for the development and psychometric validation of composite scales. Based on prior experience, these methods are posited to be applicable across a broad range of neurodegenerative diseases.

Data Selection

Data selection is the process to select the patient population and data which best represent a study population. Placebo arm data from previous trials are ideal for predicting what will happen in future clinical trials with similar populations. In the absence of placebo arm data, natural history data can also capture natural progression. For GENCOMS development, the NH1 and NH2 studies do not need to exactly match the clinical trial scenario if the datasets are large enough for extracting a subsample of interest. For example, NH1 and NH2 may include patients with all levels of severity, but CT1 includes patients with mild or moderate severity. Therefore, within NH1 and NH2, the subset of patients with mild or moderate severity is extracted by matching on the key inclusion and exclusion criteria from CT1. It is recommended that all inclusion/exclusion criteria that are contained in NH1, NH2, and CTI be identified and matched; examples include age, time since diagnosis, disease severity, genetic information, and key comorbidities.

In addition to patient selection based on all available inclusion/selection criteria, endpoints and assessment times must be ascertained from the data source. The NH1 and NH2 trials should include the same (validated) scales that are sufficiently broad to capture aspects of function, activities of daily living, and/or quality of life that are relevant to patients and clinicians. Provisions can be made to use scales that differ somewhat between studies, if particular items are convergent between two scales. Assessments from NH1 and NH2 should include those which constitute the primary, secondary, and/or exploratory endpoints of the trial.

With the availability of two data sources, NH1 can be designated as the primary data source development analysis of the composite endpoint to be utilized in CT1, and the scale derived from NH2 can be utilized as a validation dataset.

Item Selection

Item selection can be complex due to the number and variety of candidate predictor items from multiple scales, and because some items will be correlated. Partial least squares (PLS) regression is a multiple regression method based on covariance. It is recommended in situations where the number of explanatory variables is high, and the variables are correlated. Therefore, PLS is ideally suited to the development of a composite scale since the dependent variable is defined as temporal change and PLS coefficients are selected (and weighted) from explanatory variables (items) that are deemed most responsive to temporal change. Further, correlation matrices can be developed from the candidate composite scale and correlated items can be removed [14]. PLS regression is convergent with principal components analysis in that it yields a parsimonious set of predictor variables.

Linear decline is a valid assumption for disease progression if the population and time course of evaluation are constrained to the linear phase of decline, even if the overall disease course is a sigmoidal decline (see Fig. 1) [15]. Hence, linearity is often reasonable for the relatively short durations of clinical trials, and can be represented in three phases as (1) slow initial decline, (2) rapid decline, and (3) slowing decline at the late stages of the disease. Time is a direct measure of disease progression during the period of linear decline.

Alzheimer's disease progression over time

An underlying assumption of PLS regression is that the items and scales are optimally responsive to change in natural disease progression (or placebo progression if the data source is a clinical trial) and thus would be responsive to changes in disease trajectory that results from treatment with DMTs [7, 10].

The PLS regression model should be derived from longitudinal data sources that encompass a time course analogue to that required for clinical trials. If available, longer-term data can be included to improve model stability if the underlying assumption of linear change is tenable. Acknowledging that PLS regression is statistical in nature, the selection of candidate scale must be supplemented by clinical judgement and regulatory assessment. Issues such as content validity of the original measures, and patient relevant factors (i.e., function, activities of daily living, quality of life) must be given serious consideration [16]. Collaboration at this stage of scale development is thus critical (Fig. 2).

Data transformation for each item and at each visit is transformed to a 0–1 scale, and reversed, if necessary, so that 0 represents the best possible score and 1 represents the worst possible score. This transformation standardizes item scoring across measures and ensures that each predictor in the PLS regression has an equal contribution prior to running the regression model. Another element of PLS regression includes estimation of the variable of importance (VIP) score., which is a measure of a variable's importance in the PLS model and is calculated as the sum of the squared correlations between the PLS components and the dependent variable. Negative VIP values can result from lack of change on an item, and such items are not included in the composite endpoint. Items are assessed for collinearity using statistical thresholds and, as stated previously, clinical judgement is inherent to variable selection. In the analysis, if two items are correlated, the more progressive item will be retained, while the less progressive item is removed.

Wold’s criterion is applied in concert with VIP scores as a threshold for item inclusion, to objectively remove variables with the lowest contribution to clinical decline, yielding a more succinct set of predictors items in the final version of the scale [17]. Wold’s criteria of 0.5 and 0.8 have both been used in item selection, depending on the clinical scenario. For example, if many scale items are being considered (e.g., more than 10), a VIP of ≥ 0.8 may be helpful to discriminate signal from noise. A VIP ≥ 0.5 may be more appropriate for a model with fewer items, to be more inclusive. Ultimately, a sensitivity analysis framework is recommended wherein different VIP cutoffs are used to assess the consistency of item weights and composite score MSDRs.

Item Weighting

PLS regression coefficients from the final model define the weightings for each included item, and the composite total score is calculated as the weighted sum of the items. The percentage contribution from each item to the total weighted composite score is calculated by dividing the weight of the individual item by the sum of all weights. As a final step in composite scale development, the mean to standard deviation ratio (MSDR) derived from each item’s temporal change, and the variance of that change, are estimated and compared between the original scale and the composite scale. If the composite scale exhibits a greater MSDR than the original scale, its sensitivity to temporal change is deemed greater than the latter. MSDRs are also referred to as standardized response means and are a vital component of scale development.

Cross-validation

Cross-validation is critical to the assessment of composite scale reliability. Two validation techniques are applicable: X-fold cross-validation and cross-validation by exchanging weights between NH1 and NH2.

X-fold cross-validation is performed by randomly sectioning the natural history dataset into n = X subgroups. Assuming a subgroup of 5, each individual subgroup is excluded from analysis and the remaining 80% of the data are used to create the model (training set) which is tested on the remaining 20% (test set). This validation step is repeated for each of the 5 subgroups, and randomly selected n of 5 subgroups are similarly run a total of 40–100 times. MSDR values are then compared between the training set and the test set to estimate the bias of the MSDR in the total population. In addition, the percentage of training samples for which each item was selected is calculated, and the average of the item weights from the 5 training samples can be compared.

The second technique involves comparing the MSDRs for composite scores after exchanging the weights between the two datasets (i.e., comparing scale items and weights from NH2 in analyses of NH1 and vice-versa). If derived MSDRs are similar between the two datasets, this is evidence that the composite score is robust.

Treatment Effect Estimation

In the CT1 data, each item score at each visit is scaled from 0 to 1 (as described for NH1 and NH2) and the change from baseline to each time point is calculated using the scaled data. The composite score for baseline and study follow-up timepoints on the scaled data is computed using the PLS weights. A standard model for longitudinal data, such as mixed models repeated measures, can be used to estimate within- and between-group mean changes. Responsiveness of the new scale to treatment effects can then be assessed in the CT1 data by comparing Cohen’s D values obtained from the original scales with the Cohen’s D value from the composite. The Cohen’s D value is the mean difference between treatments divided by standard deviation; hence, Cohen’s D is often referred to as a standardized treatment effect [18]. In addition to comparing Cohen’s D values, a useful way to summarize the benefits of an optimized composite scale is to compare sample sizes required for specific levels of power from the composite with existing scales.

The psychometric properties of GENCOMS are derived from the properties of the candidate measures, which should be validated as a requisite for use in clinical research. In addition, it is assumed that the clinical relevance of the original scales has been assessed previously via qualitative scale validation including the determination of content and concept validity and clinical relevance (via cognitive debrief) to patients and practitioners.

It is critical that some aspects of qualitative validation be re-assessed for GENCOMS. Although concepts are likely to be consistent with those defined for the original scale, the clinical relevance of changes to the composite scale (with disease progression or treatment) must be assessed via qualitative survey [19]. Furthermore, these change metrics can be utilized to derive a responder analysis which can provide an additional estimate of treatment benefit [20].

Discussion

This paper outlines a methodology from which optimized, disease specific composite scales for progressive neurodegenerative diseases can be developed [10, 21].

Many neurodegenerative conditions impact survival within 5–20 years of diagnosis [22, 27], and many such have minimal treatment options. Thus, composite scales that are more responsive to disease trajectory and to therapies that modify disease trajectory might lead to more efficient trials with cogent sample sizes and reasonable follow-up, given the rapidity of disease progression in certain neurodegenerative conditions. Further, evidence of a delay in disease progression that derives from early treatment might offer considerable benefit to patients and to the medical entities that treat them. Composite scales with sufficient sensitivity to detect treatment benefits at the early stages of disease might illustrate this early benefit, not merely for clinically relevant measures but also with regard to prognostic and predictive biomarker validation.

Precedent exists for generating composite endpoints in neurodegenerative diseases: the widely accepted cUHDRS was developed using similar methods to the current analysis (specifically, signal to noise ratios and principal components analysis) [11]. Additional composites include the Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Impairment Multidomain Scale, the integrated Alzheimer’s disease rating scale, and the Parkinson’s disease composite scale [11, 28, 31]. However, these were primarily based on expert opinion. Using the GENCOMS method to optimize these accepted composite scales is likely to achieve higher sensitivity and tobenefit future clinical trials.

Strengths of the GENCOMS methodology include the use of established clinical scales and accepted statistical approaches, specifically, PLS regression [9, 10]. Objective selection and weighting of items from validated scales based on sensitivity to change in the population of interest ensures the resultant composite scale is sensitive to disease progression, and therefore also likely sensitive in detecting effects of DMTs that slow progression. A key strength of this method is the augmentation of the findings via clinician and patient perspectives. In addition, use of multiple data sources for scale development allows for cross-validation to assess the reliability of item selection and weightings. Consistency of results across data sources also increases confidence in the findings.

Limitations of this methodology include the assumption of linear decline, which can be mitigated by selecting an appropriate treatment duration. Scale development may be limited by data availability because derivation is reliant on robust natural history data or placebo data from clinical trials. As with any novel scale, clinicians, regulators, and health technology assessors may require additional research to provide objective evidence of the scale’s validity and clinical responsiveness. This limitation can be mitigated by engaging key stakeholders early in the process of developing the composite scale, addressing their concerns, identifying relevant outcomes and datasets, and maintaining transparency. Additionally, the application of qualitative research (e.g., concept elicitation from both patients and providers, and cognitive debrief) could provide an objective independent assessment of the composite.

The practical use of newly derived composite scales for clinical trial research might benefit from parallel utilization (i.e., inclusion) in the data sources from which they were derived, thus permitting prospective evaluation. All the described elements that constitute composite scale development and validation, including evidence of clinical benefit and evidence of temporal delay in disease progression, can and should be vetted with regulators prior to use in clinical trials. Composite scales should also be made readily available and amenable to independent validation research.

With regard to their application, health technology assessors, doctors, patients, and caregivers may find the composite scale more useful than existing scales if the composite is a better measure of disease progression, patient burden, and/or well-being. Clinical trial sponsors may favor the composite scale, especially for earlier decision-making in a Phase 2 trial or in an interim analysis, given that the greater sensitivity of the composite will yield more reliable estimates of treatment effects for a given sample size, or equal reliability with a smaller sample size, compared to existing scales. Composite scales comprised of items from multiple measures also provide an opportunity for holistic evaluation of the patient beyond what any single measure can provide.

Conclusions

The methodology presented in this paper provides a general framework for the development and validation of composite scales in the assessment of neurodegenerative disease progression and DMT effects. GENCOMS allows for more sensitive measurement of clinical decline on aspects core to the condition, yielding more efficient and reliable results, especially in earlier stages and over shorter time periods when effect sizes are likely to be small. The GENCOMS framework will be helpful for complex diseases because combining all aspects of disease into a single composite makes both the effectiveness of good treatments and the ineffectiveness of bad treatments clear. It may also be particularly valuable in rare diseases with various potential outcomes that are less well understood, therefore mitigating the risk of missing key aspects of the disease.
