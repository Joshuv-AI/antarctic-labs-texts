Rational design of caffeic acid amides as novel antioxidants in lipid systems: Experimental and theoretical studies
Authors: Zhiheng Yuan, Qicheng Sun, Yaoyao Zhang, Sichang Ti, Meimei Shan, Yang Xiao, Shangde Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaffeic acid (CA), known as a natural phenolic compound, is widely recognized for its antioxidant properties and potential applications in food preservation. However, its practical antioxidant efficacy and potential applications have been limited due to its poor lipophilicity. To overcome this issue, based on the hydrophobic residue structure and antioxidant activity of amino acids, three amino acids (methionine, tyrosine, and tryptophan) were screened for amidation with CA to both improve the lipophilicity of CA and enhance its antioxidant performance. The antioxidant efficacy of CA amides was evaluated by experimental and theoretical approach. The experimental results showed that CA amides with tryptophan exhibited the excellent antioxidant efficacy, which increased by 49 % and 47 % compared to CA in DPPH and FRAP assays, respectively. The induction period of lard with CA amides in the Rancimat test was increased by 45–55 % compared to CA. Density Functional Theory (DFT) analysis demonstrated that tryptophan conjugation improved the electron-donating ability (EHOMO from −7.306 to −6.863 eV) and reduced the O-H bond dissociation energy (from 79 to 78 kcal/mol) of CA amide. Moreover, the 4-OH site was identified as the primary active site for radical scavenging. These results clarified how specific amino acid residues modulate the antioxidant behavior at the molecular level and indicated that CA amides with tryptophan were more effective in scavenging free radicals. These findings offer valuable insights into the structure-antioxidant activity relationship of CA amides, highlighting their potential as antioxidants and application in inhibiting lipid oxidation.Keywords: Density functional theory, Antioxidant, Caffeic acid amide, Structure-activity relationship

Introduction

Lipid oxidation is a great challenge in the food system, leading to the production of toxic substances such as aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones (Khan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). These potentially toxic compounds have harmful effects on human health, resulting in cancer, atherosclerosis, aging, and other diseases (Abeyrathne et al., 2021). Thus, it is critical to prevent lipid oxidation for maintaining food quality and ensuring consumer safety. The addition of antioxidants has been proven to be the most effective strategy for preventing lipid oxidation (Yildiz et al., 2025). However, most used synthetic antioxidants, such as butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) and tert-butylhydroquinone (TBHQ), have raised growing safety concerns due to their potential toxic and carcinogenic effects. In this context, researchers have shown increasing interest in developing natural antioxidants.

Caffeic acid (CA), a hydroxycinnamic acid widely found in nature, has gained more attention due to its strong antioxidant properties. Its catechol structure facilitates radical scavenging through H-atom transfer and other electron-related mechanisms (Khan et al., 2016; Spiegel et al., 2023). CA has been demonstrated to improve the oxidative stability of some fats and oils in several studies, with performance frequently surpassing that of BHT (Laguerre et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2018). Moreover, CA also exhibited antibacterial properties against bacteria and fungi (such as Staphylococcus aureus) and anti-inflammatory properties in vivo and in vitro (Pavlíková, 2022). However, its practical application in food systems is limited due to its low lipophilicity, which reduce its antioxidant efficacy in nonpolar matrices. To overcome this limitation, structural modification of CA has emerged as an effective strategy to enhance its functional performance, such as esterification and amidation (Liu et al., 2024; Rajan et al., 2001; Alrosan et al., 2024). Among these, amide compounds exhibit superior stability compared to esters. Meanwhile, recent studies demonstrated that amide derivatives of cinnamic acids exhibit significantly superior antioxidant activity and the tyrosinase inhibitory activities compared to their parent acids or its esters (Elkamhawy et al., 2023; Halpani and Mishra, 2024; Song et al., 2022). However, most studies have focused on conjugating cinnamic acid with amines such as benzylamine and benzamide. The potential health risks might be found with these organic amines. Amino acids, as natural amino compounds, have not been sufficiently studied in relation to the amidation of cinnamic acid.

Therefore, to enhance the lipophilicity of CA and improve its antioxidant activity, three amino acids (methionine, tyrosine, and tryptophan) were screened for amidation with CA based on the hydrophobic residue structure and antioxidant activity of amino acids. The antioxidant properties of CA amides were investigated using the DPPH, FRAP, ABTS and Rancimat tests. Additionally, DFT calculations were employed to analyze intramolecular weak interaction, front molecular orbitals (FMOs), and thermodynamic parameters under three different antioxidant mechanisms. By integrating experimental and theoretical approaches, this study also investigates that how does amino acid residues modulate the antioxidant behavior of CA amides. The findings further reveal critical patterns in their structure-activity relationship. The findings offer a theoretical foundation for the rational design of potent antioxidant compounds to improve the efficacy and bioavailability.

Materials and methods

Materials

All reagents, including caffeic acid (CA, purity ≥99 %), L-tyrosine ethyl ester hydrochloride (Tyrs, purity ≥98 %), L-methionine ethyl ester hydrochloride (Mets, purity ≥98 %), L-tryptophan ethyl ester hydrochloride (Trps, purity ≥98 %), 1-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt), were all bought from Aladdin Industrial Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Other chemical reagents were both of analytical grade.

Experimental methodology

Synthesis of CA amides

Three CA amides were synthesized by EDC and HOBt, according to Wei et al. (2012) with minor modifications (Fig. 1). Briefly, CA (8.25 mM), amino acid (8.25 mM), and HOBt (8.5 mM) were all dissolved in DMF (25 mL) and stirred at 0 °C for 10 min. Then, EDC (8.35 mM) and triethylamine (24.75 mM) were both added and mixed. The reaction was performed at 0 °C for 10 min and then mixed at room temperature for 20 h. After the reaction was completed, 100 mL 5 % NaHCO3 was added, and the solution was extracted with ethyl acetate (4 × 100 mL). Then, the organic phase was washed with water (2 × 50 mL) and brine (2 × 50 mL) before drying with anhydrous Na2SO4. Following solvent removal, column chromatography (silica gel, hexane/ethyl acetate) was employed to purify the residues. Three CA amides were obtained and named as CA-Mets, CA-Trps, and CA-Tyrs, respectively.

Synthesis of three caffeic acid amides (CA-Mets: Caffeic acid conjugated with methionine ethyl ester; CA-Trps: Caffeic acid conjugated with tryptophan ethyl ester; CA-Trps: Caffeic acid conjugated with tyrosine ethyl ester).

Products identification analysis

The products were identified and analyzed by HPLC-MS, NMR, and FT-IR, respectively. The MS analysis was carried out using an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC-Q Exactive (Thermo Scientific, USA). The electrospray ionization source was utilized in positive ion mode. The 13C NMR and 1H NMR spectroscopy analyses were performed using a Bruker Avance III 500 MHz spectrometer fitted. Each sample was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide-d6. FT-IR spectroscopy analysis was performed using the Nicolet Nexus 470 instrument in the 4000-400 cm−1 range at room temperature.

Thermal stability analysis

The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was completed using a Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC3 with a thermo-gravimetric analyzer. The analysis was carried out under N2 protection at a heating rate of 10 °C/min from 30 to 600 °C.

DPPH assay

The DPPH radical scavenging activities of CA-Mets, CA-Trps, and CA-Tyrs were analyzed following the method reported by Peng and Shahidi (2022) with minor modifications. Briefly, 2 mL of DPPH (0.1 mM) ethanolic solution was mixed with 0.5 mL of the sample. After dark incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The Trolox standards at varying concentrations were measured under identical conditions. The result was represented using mole efficacy equivalents of Trolox per mole of CA amides (n/n, %).

FRAP assay

Firstly, FRAP working solution was obtained according to the previous study (Xiao et al., 2023). 0.5 mL of amidated CA methanol solution and 2.0 mL of FRAP working solution were diluted to 10 mL with deionized water, and then the absorbance was measured at 593 nm after incubation in the dark for 20 min. The result was represented using mole efficacy equivalents of Trolox per mole of CA amides (n/n, %).

ABTS radical scavenging assay

2 mL of ABTS•+ working solution was mixed with 0.5 mL of the sample. After dark incubation for 20 min, the absorbance was measured at 734 nm. The result was represented using mole efficacy equivalents of Trolox per mole of CA amides (n/n, %).

Rancimat test

An accelerated oxidation test (Metrohm 892, China) was used to investigate the antioxidant activities of CA amides in lard. Lard samples (3.00 ± 0.05 g) with different antioxidants were subjected to a fast oxidation process under a 20 L/h air flow rate at 120 °C. The results are described as the induction periods (IP, in hours) of lard samples containing antioxidants. The IP is defined as the time at which the rate of oxidation changes most rapidly (the inflection point on the conductivity curve).

Computational method

The effect of structural variation on the antioxidant activities of CA amide were analyzed by DFT calculations (Lund et al., 2013). Gaussian 16 software was used for all calculations. The B3LYP/6-311G(d,p) level of theory was used for frequency calculation and geometry optimization, while the M06-2X/6–311++G(d,p) was used for single-point energy calculations (Tošović et al., 2017). A density-based solvation model (SMD) was chosen to simulate aqueous and lipid (benzene) milieus (Shang et al., 2022). The FMOs and intramolecular interactions were analyzed by the Multiwfn program (Lu and Chen, 2012). Those results were graphically studied by the VMD visualization tool according to the interaction region indicator (IRI) method (Lu and Chen, 2021, 2024).

Statistical analysis

All tests were performed in triplicate determinations. The data were displayed as the average value ± standard error.

Results and discussion

Identification of caffeic acid amides

Three CA amides were obtained by the amidation with CA and three amino acid ethyl esters, and the reaction scheme was shown in Fig. 1. The yields of CA-Mets, CA-Trps, and CA-Tyrs were 66.2 %, 76.8 %, and 67.1 %, respectively. The chemical structures of target products were identified using MS, NMR, and FT-IR, respectively. Initially, the products were preliminarily characterized by MS analysis. As seen in Fig. 2, in the positive ion mode, the detected m/z values of 340.12, 395.16, and 372.14 correspond to the theoretical molecular mass of the synthesized CA amides (MW: 339.11, 394.15, and 371.14), respectively.

NMR spectroscopy was also used to provide further insights into the molecular structure of the CA amides. The 1H NMR spectra of CA-Mets, CA-Trps, and CA-Tyrs were shown in Fig. 2D. As expected, the appearance of the amide (NH proton) peaks around 8.34 ppm showed the formation of amide bonds between CA and amino acid ethyl ester. The chemical shift of NH proton at 8.34 ppm could be also found in other phenolamides (Wang et al., 2019). Additionally, the phenolic hydroxyl protons of CA in CA amides were observed at 9.14 and 9.39 ppm. The aromatic hydroxyl protons of Tyrs in CA-Tyrs appeared within the same chemical shift range (9.14–9.39 ppm). The methyl protons in CA amides were assigned to 1.02 and 1.19 ppm. The NH proton signal in the indole group of CA-Trps was observed at 10.86 ppm. Moreover, in the 13C NMR spectra (Fig. 2E) of CA amides, the ester-bonded carbon peak and amide-bonded carbon peak were observed at 172.3 ppm and 165.8 ppm, respectively. The carbon peaks at 146.0 and 147.9 ppm were attributed to two aromatic carbons bearing OH in CA. In the 13C NMR of CA-Tyrs, an additional signal at 165.4 ppm was assigned to the aromatic carbon bearing an OH group from the tyrosine moiety. These results were corresponding to the results of the 1H NMR. In summary, the combined results of MS, NMR, and FTIR provided a comprehensive structural characterization of CA-Mets, CA-Trps, and CA-Tyrs, which confirmed three CA amides were obtained.

Meanwhile, the target products were further confirmed by FT-IR. As seen in Fig. 3, a peak at 1644 cm−1 was found in the CA, which be designated as the carboxylic acid (C=O) band (Nagarjuna et al., 2017). After amidation, the peak at 1644 cm−1 was not observed in CA amides, which indicated that the -COOH group of CA has been consumed. Therefore, these findings further confirmed the synthesis of the CA amides.

Thermal stability analysis

Thermal stability is a critical determinant for antioxidants in food processing applications. Maintaining structural integrity during thermal exposure ensures sustained antioxidant efficacy. The thermal stability of the CA amide was systematically investigated using TGA. The TGA curves of CA, amides and TBHQ were presented in Fig. 4, highlighting the onset decomposition temperature (Tonset) and the peak decomposition temperature (Tpeak) of each compound. It could be observed that CA-Trps and CA-Trys begins to degrade significantly at relatively low temperatures. However, it was important to point out that CA-Trps and CA-Trys start to show a sharp mass loss at 321.37 and 330.13 °C, respectively, higher than CA. In contrast, TBHQ, while widely used as a synthetic antioxidant, demonstrated relatively poor thermal resistance with a sharp mass loss peaking only at 257.32 °C. Therefore, in comparison, CA-Trps and CA-Trys could persistently exert antioxidant activity at persistent high temperatures. Additionally, CA amides showed a lower migration tendency than CA in thermogravimetric analysis, which might be due to their larger molecular mass (Lu et al., 2022).

TGA curves of CA, amides and TBHQ.

DPPH assay

DPPH radical scavenging is one of the most commonly used chemical models to evaluate antioxidant efficacy. The DPPH scavenging efficacy of CA amides is shown in Fig. 5A. Specifically, Trolox equivalent values of CA-Mets, CA-Trps, and CA-Trys were 96.7 %, 100.7 %, and 75.0 %, respectively, which increased by 14–52 % compared to the CA (65.8 %). In contrast, the two traditional synthetic antioxidants, TBHQ and BHT, demonstrated lower scavenging activities, at approximately 61.0 % and 20.4 % of Trolox equivalents, respectively. This could be attributed to the steric hindrance effect caused by the tert-butyl groups in the chemical structures of TBHQ and BHT, which reduced their binding power with DPPH compared to CA amides. Moreover, the enhanced DPPH radical-scavenging activity of CA amides could be attributed to two primary factors: (1) the preservation of the dihydroxyl functionality and (2) the amidation, which optimizes the electronic structure of the molecule and enhances the hydrophobic interactions between reactants (Fig. S1). For CA-Trps, the indole ring, an electron-donating group with an extensive conjugated π-electron system, facilitates the unpaired electron delocalization after hydrogen donation. This can stabilize the radical intermediate more efficiently, which results in the superior antioxidant capacity compared to CA. In contrast, methionine and tyrosine residues have a weaker electron-donating capacity, while weaker antioxidant activity was observed in CA-Mets and CA-Tyrs compared to CA-Trps. Consequently, the antioxidant activity of CA amides exhibited dependence on the electron-donating/withdrawing effects of amino acid residues. This electronic dependence establishes a molecular-level foundation for the observed structure-activity relationship of CA amides. These results can be further supported by the following results of DFT calculation (Fig. 7). This structure-activity relationship of the substituent-dependent was first observed in CA amides, extending a phenomenon previously observed only in synthetic antioxidant (Kumar et al., 2020). Ethenyls with electron-withdrawing groups showed no or weak antioxidant activity. While ethenyls with electron-donating substituents exhibited antioxidant activities comparable to those of vitamin E. Furthermore, the electron-donating ability of benzene ring substituents was additionally noted by Chen et al. (2020) as a factor influencing the antioxidant properties of phenolic acids. On the other hand, the lipophilicity of the molecule was increased by the indole structure (Fig. S1), which improved hydrophobic interaction between reactants. Overall, balancing electronic effects in molecular design is demonstrated to be critical by these results for developing phenolic antioxidants. The markedly improved DPPH scavenging activity of CA-Trps highlights the potential of such molecular modifications in the advancement of antioxidant compounds.

FRAP assay

FRAP is used to evaluate the SET ability or reduction ability of antioxidants. Among the compounds tested (Fig. 5C), the parent compound CA showed a moderate reduction capacity (119 %). Interestingly, amidation of CA with tryptophan substantially increased the reducing ability to 176 %, which indicated a significant enhancement in SET efficiency. This might be attributed to the superior electron-donating and stabilizing capacity of the indole ring in the tryptophan residue. In contrast, because of the relatively weak electron-donating ability of methionine and tyrosine, CA-Mets (125 %) and CA-Tys (109 %) exhibited comparable or even lower FRAP activity than CA. Furthermore, it could be observed that the FRAP activities of the conventional synthetic antioxidants (TBHQ and BHT) were significantly lower than those of CA amides. The low reduction ability of BHT and TBHQ can also be found in previous study (Peng and Shahidi, 2022).

ABTS radical scavenging assay

The ABTS•+ assay is used to evaluates the radical scavenging ability of antioxidants against a hydrophilic radical cation. As shown in Fig. 5D, CA-Mets demonstrated the higher ABTS•+ scavenging activity (127 %), whereas the CA-Trps (103 %) exhibited activity comparable to CA (100 %). The superior performance of CA-Mets might be due to its moderate lipophilicity (Fig. S1). However, cinnamic acid amide derivatives exhibit the lower radical scavenging activity than their parent compounds in previous study (Song et al., 2022). Notably, the activities of the three CA derivatives were superior to that of TBHQ (90 %) and BHT (81 %). The preserved catechol structure in all CA amides provided a fundamental advantage over the BHT and TBHQ.

Rancimat test

The Rancimat test, a commonly used accelerated oxidation method, is used to evaluate the performance of antioxidants in lipid systems. The antioxidant activities of CA amides were investigated in oil using the Rancimat method. Lard was used to avoid the effects of endogenous antioxidants in vegetable oils (Olajide et al., 2020). The IP (Fig. 5B), which reflects the oxidative stability of different systems, was recorded. It could be seen that the lard with CA amides had a long induction period. Compared to BHT, the IP values of lards with CA-Mets, CA-Trps, and CA-Tyrs were increased by 366.9 %, 405.6 %, and 413.9 %, respectively. These results showed that CA amides had greater antioxidant activity than BHT. This might be because CA amides have one catechol structure, while BHT has just one active hydroxyl group. In addition, the lard with amide compounds also had better stability than CA, which was the increased lipophilicity of CA amides by amidation (Fig. S1). These resulted in the better dispersion of CA amides in the oil than CA. Thus, these Rancimat results further indicated that the amidation modification could increase the antioxidant activity of CA amides.

Computational results and discussion

Intramolecular interactions and geometrical features

In this study, the structure-antioxidant activity relationship and the antioxidant mechanisms of CA amides were elucidated by the DFT. Initially, the stable conformer of CA amides was determined by the conformational searching. Structural optimization and subsequent computations were implemented using these initial geometry configurations as the primary input parameters. The optimized structures and weak intramolecular interactions of the CA and its amides were illustrated in Fig. 6. The CA molecule adopts a geometrical planar structure. In contrast, in the CA amides, the -CH2CH2NH group tends to adopt a near-vertical orientation relative to the phenolic acid skeleton, while the CA portion retains a planar conformation. This configuration was consistent with the hydroxycinnamic acid amides in previous study (Kabanda, 2015). Furthermore, the geometrical structure of these compounds is stabilized by intramolecular weak interactions, such as steric effects, van der Waals forces, and hydrogen bonds (Yang et al., 2024). In this study, these interactions within CA and its amides were analyzed in detail using the IRI analysis method. The IRI iso-surfaces, with the value of 1, are colored according to the sign(λ2)ρ function, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Red, green, and blue regions represent steric effects, weaker van der Waals forces, and strong attractive intermolecular forces (such as hydrogen bonds) within the molecule, respectively. Additionally, the scatter plot provides an intuitive representation of the different types of weak interactions.

The intramolecular weak interactions maps of the studied compounds: Isosurface map (A2-D2) of IRI = 1.0 and Scatter map (A1-D1) between IRI and sign (λ2)ρ. (A. CA, B. CA-Tyrs, C. CA-Trps, D. CA-Mets).

The IRI results revealed that IHB could be observed between the oxygen atom of the 3-OH group and the 4-OH group in CA and its amides, which corresponded to a common structural pattern observed in related catechol derivatives (Li et al., 2023). Furthermore, the red region was observed between 4-OH and 3-OH, which suggests a spatial site barrier. These two weak interactions played an important role in the antioxidant activity of CA and its amides. Specifically, when minimal spatial hindrance was experienced by the hydroxyl group of CA amides, a hydrogen atom could be quickly donated to the ROO ·. Subsequently, the more stable phenoxy radical could be formed through hydrogen bonding with the neighboring hydroxyl group (Scheme 1). For CA-Mets, CA-Trps, and CA-Tyrs, the IRI isosurface of green was observed between the amino acid residues and the amide bond, which indicated the presence of van der Waals interactions between them. Those interactions played a crucial role in stabilizing the overall molecular conformation (Lu and Chen, 2024).

Frontier molecular orbitals

At the electronic distribution and energy theory level, FMOs are commonly used to evaluate the antioxidant potential of phenolics, with particular emphasis on LUMO and HOMO (Costa et al., 2021; Shang et al., 2022). The electron-accepting/donating properties of phenolic compounds are represented using HOMO and LUMO energy (EHOMO, ELUMO), respectively. It has been established that the molecules with high EHOMO exhibit great ability for electron-donation, which suggests that the molecule has better antioxidant potential (Anouar et al., 2009). The ease of electronic transitions can be indicated by the HOMO-LUMO energy gap (ΔE), where a low ΔE suggests high reactivity of the molecules. A similar pattern was observed in the HOMO and LUMO distributions of CA, CA-Mets, and CA-Tyrs, with electron density predominantly localized on the catechol ring and conjugated double bond of the CA, as illustrated in Fig. 7. This suggests that the electron density distribution of CA was not significantly altered by the modifications with Mets and Tyrs. However, in the case of CA-Trps, the HOMOs are mainly distributed within the indolic amine region. This might be due to the presence of the indole group with an electron-donating group. The similar HOMO distribution could also be found in other molecules with indole groups (Li et al., 2023). The indole group is a strong electron-donating moiety, which facilitates the delocalization of unpaired electrons after hydrogen donation. Consequently, this enhances the antioxidant potential of CA amides.

For LUMOs, CA and its amides display high electron density at the 4-OH hydroxyl groups, which indicated that the 4-OH hydroxyl group is a potential reaction site (Shang et al., 2022). Additionally, it should be noted that all CA amides exhibited higher HOMO energies (from −7.111 to −6.863 eV) compared to CA (−7.306 eV), which suggested that the amino acid modification increased the electron-donating ability of CA amides. Among them, CA-Trps exhibited the highest EHOMO (−6.863 eV), which suggested its superior ability to donate electrons. The ΔE of CA and its amides were decreased in the order CA-Tyrs (6.466 eV) > CA-Mets (6.447 eV) > CA (6.373 eV) > CA-Trps (6.321 eV), which indicated that CA-Trps exhibited the highest antioxidant activity. This result also indicated that the CA-Trps molecule exhibited a greater propensity for electron transfer in chemical reactions, which meant that CA-Trps has a high chemical reactivity. Meanwhile, the CA-Trps also showed high antioxidant activity. These computational findings were consistent with the DPPH radical scavenging results (Fig. 5A), in which CA-Trps demonstrated the stronger DPPH scavenging activity. At the molecular level, the antioxidant activity was further validated.

The HAT mechanism is an essential pathway for the antioxidant properties of phenolic compounds. It involves the abstraction of an H-atom from the phenolic hydroxyl group to neutralize a free radical (Scheme 1). The BDE is the key parameter of the HAT mechanism. A lower BDE value indicates a higher likelihood of hydrogen atom transfer, thus enhancing the antioxidant activity (Chen et al., 2020).

In the gas phase, the BDE values of 3-OH and 4-OH in CA were 81.0 and 79.0 kcal/mol, respectively, as shown in Table 1. When CA was modified using Mets, Trps, and Tyrs, the BDEs at the same positions were decreased. Among them, CA-Trps exhibited the most significant reduction, with a decrease of 1 kcal/mol. Benzene and water were found to have a similar effect. Furthermore, the lower BDE of the 4-OH group compared to the 3-OH group in CA amides indicated a thermodynamic preference for H-atom transfer at the 4-OH position. These results indicated that the modification with Trps could enhance the hydrogen-donating capacity of phenolic hydroxyl groups on the CA-Trps, corresponding to the DPPH results (Fig. 5). Moreover, the BDE values of CA amides in water were significantly higher compared to those in benzene and the gas phase. The formation of IHB between the phenolic hydroxyl group and water molecule might cause the observed increase in BDE. This leads to the energy barrier for hydrogen abstraction being increased (Foti et al., 2002).

The BDE values (>110 kcal/mol) of 10-NH were significantly higher than the phenolic hydroxyl groups in the three CA amides, which indicated that H-atom abstraction from the 10-NH position was thermodynamically unfavorable. This result suggested that 10-NH was not an antioxidant site in CA amides. Meanwhile, the high BDE energy was also observed in the 1-NH of CA-Trps (91.5 kcal/mol) and 4-OH of CA-Tyrs (87.3 kcal/mol) compared to the phenolic hydroxyl groups in CA amides. Thus, the HAT antioxidant mechanism of the CA amides, similar to CA, primarily results from abstracting H-atoms from phenolic hydroxyl groups. The structural modifications (e.g., amidation with Trps) reduced the BDE of phenolic hydroxyl groups in CA amides, which further confirmed the enhanced antioxidant activity of CA amides.

SET-PT mechanism: IP and PDE parameters

The IP and PDE parameters represent the properties of the electron and proton transfer in the SET mechanism, respectively (Zheng et al., 2018). The IP and PDE values for CA amides are summarized in Table 1. In the gas phase and solvents, the IP values were decreased in the order of CA-Trps < CA-Tyrs < CA-Mets < CA. Among them, CA-Trps exhibited the highest electron-donating ability, which corresponds to the FRAP assay (Fig. 5C) and FMO results (Fig. 7). These results suggested that the introduction of Trps, Tyrs, and Mets could enhance the electron-donating ability of CA. This phenomenon can result from the extension of molecular conjugation facilitated by these substituents, which delocalizes electron density and lowers the IP (Xue et al., 2021). Moreover, compared to the gas phase, the calculations revealed that the IP values for CA amides in water and benzene were significantly decreased by ∼70 and ∼30 kcal/mol, respectively. This indicated that water could enhance the electron-donating ability of CA amides. The IP of pinobanksin and its ester were both significantly decreased with the increasing solvent polarity in previous study (Zheng et al., 2018). These results confirmed that the polar solvent played an essential role in the ability of CA amides to donate electrons.

The PDE is used to describe the second step of SET-PT. The results were presented in Table 1. A decrease in the PDE values of CA and its amides in polar solvents could be observed, which indicated that proton transfer became more favorable in these environments. The sequence of the PDE values was CA < CA-Mets < CA-Tyrs < CA-Trps in the solvent and gas phase, which indicated that CA more easily donates protons after losing an electron. However, the high IP values in all solvents indicated that the electron transfer step was a significant barrier to the SET-PT process. Overall, the HAT mechanism was shown to be more thermodynamically favorable than the SET-PT pathway for CA and its amides. Similarly, the SET-PT mechanism also exhibited the energetic disadvantages in flavonoid systems (Dimitrić Marković et al., 2017).

SPLET mechanism: PA and ETE parameters

The feasibility of the SPLET mechanism is evaluated using PA and ETE as the key parameters, which represent the proton and electron transfer properties in the SPLET pathway, respectively. As shown in Table 1, environmental factors, particularly solvent polarity, significantly affected the thermodynamics of the SPLET pathway. The PA values of CA amides in water (39.3–54.6 kcal/mol) and benzene (90.3–115.4 kcal/mol) were significantly lower than those in the gas phase (324.3–351.6 kcal/mol). This indicated that deprotonation of CA amides was more easily occurring in the solvents, especially for the polar solvents. Moreover, the sequence of the PA values was CA < CA-Mets < CA-Tyrs < CA-Trps. This result suggested that a negative effect on the deprotonation ability of the phenolic hydroxyl group of CA was caused by the introduction of the three amino acids. While CA-Trps had the lowest ETE values, which indicated that the introduction of Trps did not weaken the electron abstraction from the CA-Trps. The results were similar to the previous IP trends (Table 1). Additionally, the 4-OH of all compounds had lower PA and ETE values than that of the 3-OH. This further suggested that the 4-OH was a potential active site, which was consistent with the result of the BDE (Table 1).

A comparative analysis was performed on the thermodynamic parameters of three mechanisms. The PA values of CA amides in the aqueous phase were lower compared to the IP and BDE values. However, the total energy required for the whole SET process (PA + ETE) was higher than the BDE. Thus, comparative analysis revealed HAT as the thermodynamically preferred mechanism among SET-PT, SPLET, and HAT pathways. This conclusion was supported by its significantly lower BDE values than the PA + ETE and IP + PDE. Additionally, the specific hydroxyl group for CA amides also was critical for the antioxidant activity. For example, the most favorable site for antioxidant activity was found to be the 4-OH group of CA amides. These results provided valuable insights into the relationship between CA amides and antioxidant activity at the molecular level.

Conclusions

In this study, three CA amides (CA-Mets, CA-Trps, and CA-Tyrs) were successfully synthesized by conjugating CA with amino acid to improve the lipophilicity and antioxidant activity of CA. After amidation, the LogP value of CA was increased from 0.98 to 2.98, which showed an increase in lipophilicity. Meanwhile, comprehensive experimental results revealed that the CA amides, especially CA-Trps, exhibited excellent antioxidant efficacy in chemical models and Rancimat test, which increased by 14–55 % compared to CA. This indicates the application potential of CA amides in developing food preservatives. Moreover, by integrating experimental data with the results of theoretical calculations, the structure-activity relationship of CA amides was elucidated. The introduction of amino acid residues, especially the indole group of tryptophan, enhanced the electron-donating capacity (EHOMO from −7.306 to −6.863 eV) and reduced the O-H BDE (from 79 to 78 kcal/mol), which promoted efficient hydrogen atom transfer. Moreover, the HAT mechanism was the most probable process in comparison to the SET-PT and SPLET mechanisms for radical scavenging. The 4-OH group of CA amides was the preferred site for scavenging free radicals reaction. These results bridge the gap between experimental findings and molecular mechanisms, which demonstrated that amino acid conjugation effectively modulates the electronic and steric properties of phenolic antioxidants. It should be noted that the current research findings were obtained in various chemical model system. Future work will require further validation in complex food matrices to fully assess the practical applicability of CA amides.
