Objective

Ralstonia syzygii subsp. celebesensis (Rsc) is the causal agent of Blood Disease of Banana (BDB) which significantly impacts Banana (Musa sp.) [1, 2]. As a nation with a high diversity of bananas, BDB is the top-level important disease of banana in Indonesia with a yield loss up to 90%. The symptoms of BDB are wilting, red-brown vascular staining starting from the fruit peduncle to the true stem, discolored and rotten fruits, and dying of the infected plant [3, 4]. The disease is spread through the inoculum-contaminated animals including pollinators visiting the male buds and influorescents, stem or corm borer pests, agricultural tools, and surface running water from the residue of infected materials [5]. The first incidence of BDB was reported in the islands in South Sulawesi (Celebes), Indonesia in the 1900s. Recently BDB has been reported in all main Islands of Indonesia and Malaysia [6, 7]. Effective control measures are urgently needed to prevent further spread of this pathogen. While it is well established that Rsc spreads via human-mediated mechanisms particularly through the movement of infected plant materials the extent to which geographic or ecological barriers shape the genetic structure of Rsc populations remains unclear. Compared to its close relative Ralstonia solanacearum, relatively little is known about the molecular basis of Rsc pathogenicity and its interaction with host plants. To contribute to this knowledge gap, this study aims to explore the genomic characteristics of an Rsc isolate from Bali and compare it to a published genome from Yogyakarta (Java) [8]. These two regions are situated on opposite sides of the Wallace Line, a well-known biogeographic boundary. This comparative genomic approach is intended to assess whether regional divergence exists between Rsc populations, which could reflect historical separation, ecological differentiation, or limited gene flow despite the potential for long-distance human-mediated dispersal.

Data description

Genome sequence of Ralstonia syzygii subsp. celebesensis isolated from Musa balbisiana cultivar Kepok in Bali consists of the genome structure and G-C percentage, genome was de novo assembled and analyzed to gain information about genome length, CDS pattern, and also annotation. All the data was collected and processed individually based on the Genome Analysis Pipeline supporting sequence produced from Oxford Nanopore sequencing with its protocol described below.

Rsc isolate UGMITBRKI_24001 was isolated from a Musa balbisiana cultivar Kepok with blood disease of banana-specified symptom located in Buleleng, Bali, Indonesia (8.089265 S 115.234645 E). The isolate was grown on casein peptone glucose (CPG) medium and identified as Ralstonia syzygii subsp. celebesensis based on a series of tests: it was Gram-negative, positive in Kovac’s oxidation test, induced a hypersensitive reaction on tobacco leaves, and caused typical blood disease symptoms on banana plants at 21 days post-inoculation with 10⁵ CFU. Molecular confirmation was performed using PCR with the Rsc-specific primer 121 F/R [1, 8]. Three days confirmed isolated was extracted to gain genomic DNA using Bacteria Genomic DNA Kit (Geneaid, Taiwan). DNA concentration and purification were assessed using NanoDrop spectrophotometers resulting in gDNA concentration > 200 ng µl− 1 with a DNA purity ratio of absorbance at 260/280 nm of 1.72 then proceed to library preparation step using SQK-RBK114 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) following with its protocols. Genome was sequenced using MinION (Oxford nanopore Tech., UK) with the MinKNOW software 23.04.6 (Oxford Nanopore Tech, UK) also for Basecalling the raw sequence [8]. Genome analysis was done using Galaxy Cloud Program (https://usegalaxy.org.au/) and fastQC (Galaxy Version 0.74 + galaxy1) was performed to know reads quality of the sequence. Sequencing reads were filtered using Filtlong (Galaxy Version 0.2.1 + galaxy0) with a minimum length threshold of 1000 bp and an output threshold targeting approximately 130× genome coverage, based on an estimated genome size of 5.2 Mb. Read quality was visualized using NanoPlot, and Flye (Galaxy Version 2.9.5 + galaxy0) was used for de novo assembly in Nanopore-corrected mode, with reduced coverage applied during the initial disjointing assembly step [9–12]. Annotation was performed using NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP) followed by CheckM analysis to know genome completeness with the Ralstonia CheckM marker set [13]. Annotation result shown in Data file 1 and visualized in Data file 2. The raw sequence reads were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive under accession SRX28572606 and the incomplete genome assembly was deposited in GenBank under BioProject PRJNA580360, BioSample accession SAMN45032525 and genome sequence assembly accession on GCA_046245135.1. Genome comparison was performed using OAT: OrthoANI Tool v0.93.1 to know the different between Rsc genome from west Indonesia UGMSS_Db01 and the first genome of Ralstonia solanacearum GMI1000 [8, 14] with the result presented on Data file 4. Limitations

The pathogen is Ralstonia syzygii subsp. celebesensis which is isolated, identified and stored in Department of Plant Protection, Universitas Gadjah Mada under the isolate name UGMITBRKI_24001. The isolate was specified in exact location mention in the paper (Buleleng, Bali, Indonesia from Musa balbisiana cultivar Kepok). The genome assembly of isolate UGMITBRKI_24001 is incomplete. The assembly has a completeness of 67.46% based on CheckM analysis, a total genome size of approximately 5.2 Mb, and an N50 of 3.8 Mb. The assembly consists of 2 contigs with a genome coverage of 130×. These metrics indicate that while the depth of sequencing was sufficient, the assembly is fragmented and lacks full completeness.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Samples, including fruit and pseudostem tissues from symptomatic plants, were collected from a local farmer’s plantation in Bali, Indonesia. The farmer is part of a community outreach program supported by our institution, and the collection was conducted within the scope of ongoing collaborative activities. The landowner provided informed verbal consent after being fully briefed on the purpose of the study, which involved diagnosis and identification of the causal agent of the disease. In accordance with common practice in Indonesia, such sampling for plant disease diagnostics is generally welcomed by farmers. The study involved non-destructive sampling on non-protected land and did not require special permits under current Indonesian regulations. Sampling and transport of plant material were inspected and approved by a plant quarantine office, ensuring compliance with national biosecurity and plant health regulations. All procedures followed institutional, national, and international ethical standards for plant research.
