MicroRNA-21 is a potential therapeutic agent targeting Tgfbi and mitigating high-fat-diet-induced liver disease and cancer
Authors: Urmila Jagtap, Anan Quan, Yuho Ono, Jonathan D. Lee, Alice Rodrigues, Kylie A. Shen, Sergei Manakov, Gyongyi Szabo, Imad Nasser, Frank J. Slack
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Liver disease, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), is a major global health concern, claiming approximately 2 million lives worldwide annually, yet curative treatments remain elusive. In this study, we aimed to investigate the role of microRNA-21-5p (miR-21) in metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), and HCC within the context of a Western choline-deficient (CD) high-fat diet (HFD) and offer potential therapeutic insights. We found that reduced miR-21 levels correlated with liver-disease progression in wild-type (WT) mice fed on CD-HFD, while miR-21-knockout mice showed exacerbated metabolic dysfunction, including obesity, hepatomegaly, hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, steatosis, fibrosis, and HCC. Our study reveals that miR-21 plays a protective role in metabolic syndrome and in the progression of liver disease to cancer. miR-21 directly targets Transforming growth factor beta-induced (Tgfbi), a gene also known to be significantly upregulated and a potential oncogene in HCC. Further, our study showed that intervention with the administration of an miR-21 mimic in WT livers effectively improves insulin sensitivity, steatosis, fibrosis, Tgfbi expression, and tumor burden in CD-HFD conditions. These findings indicate that miR-21 could serve as an effective strategy to delay or prevent liver disease in HFD environments.

Introduction

Liver disease is one of the greatest contributors to the global health burden,1 with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) being the fourth most prevalent cancer.2 Liver disease manifests as metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD; formerly, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease or NAFLD) or MASLD with alcohol (Met-ALD; formerly, alcoholic steatohepatitis or NASH).3 Its pathophysiology includes a gradual progression from simple fatty liver (i.e., steatosis) to steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even HCC.4,5 In addition to genetic predisposition, hepatic insults in the form of a fatty diet, alcohol, drug intake, and viral infections are largely responsible for hepatic dysfunctions.6 In addition to affecting liver pathophysiology, these factors are also correlated with rising rates of metabolic disorders,7 like obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and insulin resistance,8 together resulting in significant disease morbidity and mortality. Indeed, the Western diet consisting of high caloric value has claimed more than 100 million individuals in the United States alone.9

It is well documented that consumption of a high-fat diet (HFD) (or so-called Western diet) leads to liver damage10,11 and can be described by a multiple-hit model.12 The primary insult originating from genetic predisposition sensitizes the liver to injury. The secondary hits by subsequent insults like inflammatory cytokines, adipokines, endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and hepatocellular death then lead to impaired fatty acid metabolism and insulin resistance, triggering steatosis, steatohepatitis, and fibrosis.11

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of small non-coding RNA molecules that play various roles in metabolic functions. One such miRNA is microRNA-21 (miR-21), which has been shown to function in glucose metabolism, fatty acid uptake, metabolism, inflammation, etc.13,14 The dysregulation of miR-21 promotes insulin resistance, hepatitis, and fibrosis in various cell lines and mouse models.15 In addition, increased hepatic expression in animal models and patients with MASLD/MASH and HCC has been reported13,14; however, the serum levels of miR-21 vary significantly in different studies.15 Contradictory reports exist correlating liver disease with both increased16,17 and decreased18 expression of miR-21 in human serum. However, despite the possible correlation of miR-21 levels with liver disease, efforts to utilize it as a diagnostic marker or therapeutic intervention for liver disease and HCC have failed so far.16,17,18 One of the most likely reasons is the high variability of miR-21 in the serum and tissues of patient samples. Studies over more than a decade have hinted at the possible context-dependent functions of miR-21 in various liver diseases like HCC, probably based on the nature of the insult.19,20,21

The function of miR-21 as an oncogene and hepatotoxic agent is a long-standing dogma. It was proposed that miR-21-5p promotes NASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis via PPARα22 or through miR-21 interaction with the Hbp1/p53/Srebp1c pathway, highlighting that miR-21-ASO could be of potential therapeutic value23 or that miR-21 promotes migration and invasion in HCC through the miR-21/PDCD4/AP-1 feedback loop.24 At the same time, several reports have highlighted that liver miR-21 progressively increases with NAFLD/MASLD progression, acting upon specific pathogenic pathways during the disease stages.25 For example, it has been shown that PPARɣ attenuates inflammation and oxidative stress in NASH by inhibiting the miR-21-5p/SFRP5 pathway26; that miR-21 abrogation, decreasing PPARα, significantly improves whole-body metabolic parameters in NASH27; that miR-21 closely associates with fibrosis in a rat model of NASH28; hepatic miR-21/miR-21∗ deficiency prevents glucose intolerance and steatosis in mice fed on obesogenic diet29; and inhibiting or suppressing liver miR-21 expression reduces liver cell injury, inflammation, and fibrogenesis in distinct NASH models by targeting PPARα expression.30 However, recently emerging reports have challenged this belief, since some show no dependency on miR-21 for liver fibrosis and other phenotypes.15 Contradictory to reports correlating liver disease with increased levels of miR-21 in patient serum samples, Sun et al. report reduced levels of miR-21 in sera of NAFLD patients compared with healthy controls.18 Furthermore, they demonstrate that miR-21 negatively regulates the levels of triglycerides, free cholesterol, and total cholesterol in PA/OA-treated HepG2 by attenuating its direct target 3-hydroxy-e-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR).18

Recently, two independent reports showed results in contrast to the expected oncogenic role of miR-21. Using two different models of HCC, i.e., genetic and carcinogen (diethylnitrosamine [DEN]) based, Correia de Sousa et al. showed that the total or hepatocyte-specific genetic depletion of miR-21 fosters HCC development.31 Another study by Lhamyani et al. demonstrated that miR-21 activates a thermogenic program in brown adipose tissue and browning in different white adipose tissue depots, suggesting an increase in adipose energy expenditure.32 These studies reveal the importance of in vivo experiments in revealing functions for miR-21.

Considering the high prevalence of liver disease and HCC worldwide and the lack of preclinical alternatives, animal models that can recapitulate the pathophysiology of this progression are essential. In this study, we have created a mouse model of liver disease and cancer that displays all classical metabolic and pathological features associated with various stages of human liver disease. Over the course of 32 weeks following a one-time DEN administration and a HFD-based protocol, mice sequentially developed metabolic phenotypes starting from obesity, hyperglycemia, and insulin resistance to the entire spectrum of liver disease (i.e., steatosis, fibrosis, MASH, and ultimately HCC). Using bulk RNA sequencing of the liver representing various stages of liver disease, we propose pathways that could contribute to transitioning the healthy liver to the progressive disease condition upon HFD insult. Since we found that miR-21 levels decrease over time in this model, we investigated the role of miR-21 in the progression of liver disease using this protocol with a whole-body knockout (KO) of miR-21.

Our results indicate that the loss of miR-21 is pathogenic to the liver and causes metabolic dysfunction in mice. The severity of liver disease increased significantly when the mice were fed a choline-deficient (CD) HFD, representing multiple insults to the liver, resulting in a more severe diseased condition. In our transcriptomics analysis, we identified the molecular pathways regulated by miR-21 and HFD independently and in combination. Through various analyses, we propose that one key mechanism of miR-21 action is via directly targeting the Transforming growth factor beta-induced (Tgfbi) transcript. Further, we investigated whether administration of miR-21 in the form of mimic in HFD-induced liver disease in the wild-type (WT) mice can protect from or delay liver-disease pathogenesis and HCC. We found that the miR-21 mimic administration increased insulin sensitivity, reduced steatosis and fibrotic phenotypes, and reduced tumor burden as compared to the livers from the negative control (NC) mimic-injected mice. Together, our study has revealed a protective role of miR-21 in liver disease and HCC in the HFD setting and advocates it as a potential therapeutic agent to delay liver-disease progression.

Results

WT mice on HFD develop obesity, liver injury, hyperglycemia, and insulin resistance

Generating an accurate model of human liver-disease progression to HCC was one of the aims of this study. B6/129SF2/J (WT) male mice were administered a one-time dose of 25 mg/kg of diethylnitrosamine (DEN) intraperitoneally at 3 weeks of age. Following injections, all mice were randomly divided into two feeding categories: chow or CD-HFD (Figure 1A). All the mice were weighed weekly over a 32-week period, and the body weight was graphed over time. As expected, the HFD-fed mice showed significantly higher body weights (Figures 1B and S1A) as well as increased body-fat accumulation (Figure S1C) over time than chow-fed mice, indicating obesity in this group. The obesity phenotype is usually associated with hepatomegaly and liver injury. Interestingly, the liver-to-body-mass ratio did not show any increase upon HFD feeding, indicating that these mice did not develop hepatomegaly (Figures 1C and S1B). However, the measurement of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels in serum showed a significant increase, highlighting liver injury (Figures 1D, S1D, and S1E). Metabolic dysfunctions that are known to be associated with HFD consumption were also tested. The increased concentrations of blood glucose indicated that mice fed on an HFD suffered from hyperglycemia compared to chow-fed counterparts (Figures 1E and S1F). WT mice on HFD sequentially develop steatosis, steatohepatitis, advanced fibrosis, and liver tumors

A set of mice were euthanized at specific time points (i.e., weeks 16, 24, and 32) and their livers were analyzed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), oil red O, and Sirius red stains to assess liver histopathology, inflammation, fat accumulation, and fibrosis, respectively. Histopathological analysis indicated that, starting from week 16, as compared to chow-fed counterparts, all WT mice fed on HFD showed the presence of mixed micro-vesicular and macro-vesicular fat droplets within hepatocytes throughout the liver, as also confirmed by oil red O staining (WT panel in Figures 1G and 3A). The severity of this phenotype increased, as indicated by the accumulation of stage 3 phenotypes at week 24 (Figure 1G, WT panel of Figure 3A). As assessed by Sirius red staining, the presence of stage 1 and 2 fibrosis was apparent in week 16 (Figures 1H and 3A), indicating that the fibrotic phenotype was initiated before week 16. Notably, high scoring of hypertrophy (Figure 1I, WT panel of Figure 3A) and lobular-inflammation phenotypes (Figure 1J, WT panel of Figure 3A) were also observed in these mice, consistent with MASH. Along with MASH and increased fibrosis in these livers, the HFD-fed mouse livers developed tumor nodules by week 24 (Figure 1K), which were virtually absent in the chow group (Figure 1L, left graph). By week 32 (Figure 1L, right graph), the tumors progressed to adenomas/dysplastic nodules (∼40%) and fatty adenomas (∼45%). Some of the tumors (∼15%) also progressed to early and/or advanced HCC (WT panel of Figure 4E).

WT mice on HFD show gene expression changes related to metabolic response early in hepatic injury

To better understand the pathways and factors involved in liver-disease progression, we isolated RNA from livers from each sub-group at each stage of the protocol and performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). T-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) analysis showed distinct clusters in WT-chow and HFD groups, highlighting the robust effect of HFD on the hepatic transcriptome (Figure S2G). RNA-seq analysis of liver samples showed a significant number of genes to be differentially regulated at each stage (Figures S2A, S2C, and S3A). Analysis of these genes using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) software showed enrichment of pathways involved in liver injury (Figure S2F), fatty acid synthesis and metabolism (Figures S2H, S3B, and S3D), and inflammation (Figures S3B and S3C) in the livers of WT-HFD group as compared to the WT-chow group. This correlates with the steatosis and MASH phenotypes observed in the tissue sections, especially at week 16—the first time point tested. In addition, the highest activation of immune-function, inflammation, and lipid-metabolism genes was also observed in week-16 livers, suggesting the early response of the liver to injury and its adaptive shift (Figures S2H and S3). However, the extent of the metabolic and immune response reduced over time (i.e., in weeks 24 and 32) (Figure S2H), suggesting that the chronic insult possibly led to the decline of liver function over time.

miR-21 KO causes metabolic dysfunction and progressive liver disease from fatty liver to advanced fibrosis

As mentioned earlier, miR-21 has multiple functions in liver metabolism, and its modulation impacts pathways central to inflammation, fibrosis, and hepatocellular injury. Elevated levels of miR-21 in circulation16,33 and liver tissue17,34 have been correlated with liver disease in humans. Importantly, miR-21 is one of the few miRNAs with a well-characterized role across multiple stages of liver disease, ranging from steatosis to fibrosis and even HCC, making it a compelling candidate for molecular and therapeutic evaluation. We, therefore, measured miR-21 levels in the liver tissue of our mice by RT-qPCR. Interestingly, in our model, as liver disease progressed from simple steatosis to HCC in response to CD-HFD feeding (i.e., from week 16 to 32), miR-21 expression progressively declined, reaching nearly undetectable levels in the week-32 livers (Figure 2A). Interestingly, we also found several miR-21 targets to be differentially regulated in these livers (Figures S4A–S4C). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of these genes showed that they are involved in critical metabolic liver functions (Figures S4D–S4G), including enrichment in lipid metabolism and peroxisome-related processes (Figures S4D and S4E). Based on these results, we investigated the role of miR-21 in liver-disease progression and performed similar assays to those described in Figures 1 and 2 on mice with whole-body miR-21 KO. The body-weight analysis of miR-21-KO mice fed on the chow diet showed no significant change as compared to the WT-chow group (Figure 2B, light red line, and Figure S5A), indicating no obvious obesity phenotype. Similarly, no significant changes in serum ALT levels were observed at week 4 (Figures 2C, S5D, and S5E), indicating that these mice had healthy livers at the commencement of the experiment. The enzyme levels showed no change until week 16; however, a significant increase was observed in mice from miR-21-KO-chow group as compared to the WT counterparts in week 24 (Figures S5D and S5E), indicating liver injury. Interestingly, unlike the WT-HFD group, miR-21-KO mice on chow diet exhibited a higher liver-to-body-mass ratio when compared to their WT counterparts, indicating hepatomegaly in miR-21-deficient livers (Figures 2D and S5A–S5C). Additionally, miR-21 KO was associated with elevated blood glucose levels, suggesting a hyperglycemic state (Figures 2E and S5F). miR-21-KO mice on HFD show exacerbated signs of metabolic syndrome, fatty liver, steatohepatitis, advanced fibrosis, and tumor development. Our results demonstrate that the combination of miR-21 KO and HFD resulted in the most severe manifestation of the metabolic dysfunction-related phenotypes we assessed. The body weights of miR-21 KO-HFD showed a dramatic increase, with some mice reaching up to 57 g (Figures 2B and S5A). Serum levels of ALT and AST were nearly double compared to those of their WT counterparts (Figures 2C, S5D, and S5E). The liver-to-body-weight ratio was also highest in the miR-21-KO-HFD group, indicating pronounced hepatomegaly (Figures 2D and S5C). Interestingly, blood glucose concentrations did not increase beyond the levels observed in the miR-21-KO-chow group (Figure 2E). However, ITT assay showed almost complete insensitivity at week 16 (Figure 2F), suggesting that insulin resistance emerged early in the disease course. This was further supported by RT-qPCR analysis, which showed elevated expression of genes associated with insulin resistance (Figure 2G), corroborating the ITT results.

The histological analysis revealed that the miR-21-KO mice fed HFD exhibited the most severe pathological phenotypes among all groups. H&E, oil red O, and Sirius red staining, along with histological scoring, demonstrated that both steatotic (Figures 3A, 3D, and 3F) and fibrotic (Figures 3A, 3E, and 3G) features in miR-21 KO-HFD were initiated earlier and progressed rapidly, reaching grade 3 severity, compared to other groups. While histology scoring did not show severe lobular inflammation (Figure 3C), RT-qPCR analysis showed markedly elevated expression of inflammation- (Figures 3H and S5H), fatty acid uptake- (Figure 3J), and fibrosis-related (Figures 3I and S5I) genes. Consistent with their pronounced fat accumulation, the miR-21-KO-HFD group also exhibited the highest serum cholesterol levels (Figure 3H); however, triglyceride levels remained unchanged across all groups (Figure 3I).

miR-21 KO and HFD have additive effects on the progression of liver disease from steatosis to the development of HCC

Across all metabolic and pathological stages of liver-disease progression examined, the miR-21-KO-HFD group exhibited the highest disease severity and the fastest rate of disease progression (Figures 1, 2, and 3). This was also evident from the t-SNE analysis performed in the miR-21-KO liver samples, which revealed clear separation from WT samples for both chow and HFD groups, indicating genotype-driven molecular differences. Within each genotype, chow and HFD samples formed distinct subclusters, emphasizing the combined effects of diet and miR-21 deletion (Figure S2G). Interestingly, tumors from WT-HFD mice clustered closely with miR-21-KO samples, reinforcing the similarity of molecular signatures between HFD-induced tumorigenesis in WT and the miR-21-KO-HFD condition (Figure S2G). In terms of tumor burden, we observed drastic differences when comparing the effects of HFD feeding, miR-21 KO, and their combination. As noted previously, HFD feeding of WT mice developed tumor burden by week 24 (Figure 1L). Interestingly, in contrast to the WT-chow group, mice from the miR-21-KO-chow group developed more tumors, indicating the independent role of miR-21 in driving liver-disease progression from fibrosis to tumor development (Figures 4A and 4B). In consistent with the trend observed across all stages of liver disease, the miR-21-KO-HFD group displayed the highest tumor burden among all groups, with tumors that were significantly larger (Figures 4A and 4B). By week 32, some tumors exceeded 1 cm in diameter (Figure 4A). This may also explain the relatively low histological scores for hypertrophy and lobular inflammation in the HFD-fed groups, particularly miR-21 KO-HFD, where the normal liver parenchyma was engulfed in the tumor growth (Figures 3B and 3C). We did not observe obvious metastasis to lungs or bones in any of the groups. Histopathological assessment of liver sections (Figure 4C) corroborated these findings, revealing a significantly greater tumor burden (Figure 4D) with a significant number of neoplastic lesions in the WT-HFD and miR-21-KO-chow groups compared to the WT-chow group, with the miR-21-KO-HFD group showing the highest incidence (Figure 4D). Tumor profiling in the miR-21-KO-chow group revealed a mixture of adenomas/dysplastic nodules (∼36%), fatty adenomas (∼7%), and early HCCs (∼57%). Interestingly, this group exhibited more advanced tumors as compared to the WT-HFD group, with ∼57% early HCC in the miR-21-KO group vs. ∼11% in the WT-HFD group (Figure 4E). The combination of both factors (i.e., miR-21 deficiency and HFD) in the miR-21-KO-HFD group, led to the most severe tumor pathology, with nearly 80% of the tumors already progressed to early HCC, while only ∼20% were fatty adenomas (Figures 4C and 4E). A small subset of tumors also showed early HCCs arising within adenomas (Figure 4E). These findings indicate that both miR-21 loss and CD-HFD independently contribute to tumor development, with a severe additive effect when combined. To elucidate the molecular contribution of miR-21 to tumorigenesis, we performed a comparative RNA-seq analysis of tumors isolated from miR-21-KO and WT mice fed on HFD. A total of 102 genes were upregulated, and 187 genes were downregulated (Figure 4F) in the miR-21-KO tumors. CancerMine analysis35 identified 27 upregulated and 19 downregulated genes. Among the upregulated genes, 33% are associated with cancer-promoting driver mutations, 70.3% with oncogenic functions, 59.2% with tumor-suppressor roles, and 29.6% with dual functions (Figure 4G; Table S2).

IPA analysis revealed significant enrichment of genes associated with advanced malignant tumor features in miR-21-KO liver tumors compared to WT tumors at week 32 (Figures 4H and 4I). Interestingly, many of these gene expression changes were evident in the surrounding tissue but not within the tumors themselves (Figures 4H and 4I), suggesting that miR-21 loss may promote tumorigenesis through broader metabolic alterations in the liver microenvironment.

miR-21 directly regulates Tgfbi

Our results demonstrated that miR-21-KO mice are predisposed to metabolic syndrome and liver disease upon HFD consumption. In order to understand the susceptibility of miR-21-KO mice to liver disease, we compared the gene expression changes in miR-21-KO and WT mice fed on a chow diet. As previously observed (Figure 4), the gene signatures corroborated the disease phenotypes observed in the miR-21-KO mice (Figures S6A and S6B). Notably, significant upregulation was observed in the genes involved in hepatic steatosis, liver toxicity, and inflammation (Figure S6). Additionally, genes associated with retinol biosynthesis, triglyceride degradation, gustatory pathways, and protein kinase signaling were significantly upregulated (Figure S6C).

Retinol biosynthesis plays a pivotal role in liver physiology, particularly in regeneration, fibrosis, and tumorigenesis. Retinol, primarily stored as retinyl esters in hepatic stellate cells, is converted to retinoic acid, which modulates immune responses, enhances liver regeneration, and exhibits anti-tumor responses in certain hepatic malignancies.36,37,38 Triglyceride degradation represents a critical hepatic mechanism to prevent lipid accumulation and sustain systemic energy homeostasis.39 Gustatory signaling, although classically associated with taste perception, may influence nutrient sensing, appetite regulation, and feeding behavior, thereby indirectly impacting metabolic health.40,41 Protein kinase signaling is central to liver functions, encompassing a wide array of kinases involved in the regulation of hepatic metabolism, inflammatory responses, and cell-survival pathways.42,43 The observed upregulation of these pathways at the 16- and 24-week time points suggests an active hepatic response aimed at mitigating injury and preserving metabolic function. However, despite the compensatory mechanisms, concurrent increases in markers of hepatic steatosis, liver injury, and inflammation indicate increasing disease manifestation. The observed upregulation of these pathways at the 16- and 24-week time points suggests an active hepatic response aimed at mitigating ongoing liver injury and preserving metabolic function (Figure S6C). However, the marked downregulation of these compensatory pathways by week 32 (Figure S6C) suggests the loss of hepatic plasticity and transition to an irreversible severe disease stage, consistent with the histopathological evidence of advanced liver damage observed at this stage. These observations support our hypothesis that loss of miR-21 alone is sufficient to initiate liver-disease pathogenesis.

To identify direct targets of miR-21-5p in mouse liver, we performed chimeric enhanced cross-linking and immunoprecipitation (eCLIP) on liver tissue isolated from 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice, followed by probe capture for miR-21-5p, as described in the preprint by Manakov et al.44 This analysis, across three biological replicates, yielded 484,776 chimeric reads corresponding to 8,618 genes (Table S3). We compared this dataset with genes dysregulated in miR-21-KO livers, WT-HFD livers, hepatic tumors in HFD-fed mice, and predicted miR-21 targets from TargetScan, v8.0,45 finding considerable overlap46 (Figure 5A). The direct target of miR-21 commonly identified in this analysis was the gene Tgfbi (Figure 5A). Tgfbi encodes an extracellular matrix protein that plays a role in various physiological and pathological conditions, including diabetes, corneal dystrophy, and tumorigenesis.47 To assess its clinical relevance, we extracted gene expression data for human HCC patients from OncoDB,48 which revealed a significant upregulation in TGFBI levels in these patients (Figure 5B). As predicted by TargetScan (Figure 5C), we observed enrichment in the miR-21 peaks on the 3′ UTR of Tgfbi (mm10) in our chimeric eCLIP data (Figure S7A), precisely aligning with the miR-21 reverse seed sequence (Figure S7B). Regulation of Tgfbi by miR-21 has also been established previously. We observed that, in our model, Tgfbi levels increased in WT mouse livers as liver disease progressed (Figure 5D). Reinforcing the inverse relationship between miR-21 and Tgfbi, miR-21 KO displayed significantly higher expression of Tgfbi mRNA levels at all stages compared to the WT-chow controls (Figure 5D). We also validated this regulation at the protein level by western blotting (Figure 5E). The miR-21-KO-HFD group showed the highest Tgfbi levels in week-32 samples, correlating its expression with the most severe liver-disease phenotypes (Figures 5D and 5E). Thus, all these findings, together with the literature, strongly support Tgfbi as a direct, functional target of miR-21. To further investigate the regulation of Tgfbi by miR-21, we employed three human HCC patient-derived cell lines (i.e., JHH-1, SNU387, and SNU423). Based on the data from the Human Protein Atlas, JHH-1 cells exhibited low TGFBI expression, while SNU387 and SNU423 cell lines showed elevated levels. We validated these expression patterns through RT-PCR analysis (Figure 5F). We also observed that this varying expression of TGFBI is inversely correlated with miR-21 levels in these cells (Figures 5F and 5G). Furthermore, we observed TGFBI expression to be positively correlated with the expression of fibrosis-associated genes (Figure S7C), consistent with the previously established relationship between the two.50

To further explore this regulatory relationship, we performed functional experiments using miR-21 mimics/inhibitors and TGFBI-targeting small interfering RNAs (siRNAs). In JHH-1 cells, which express high levels of miR-21, transfection with a miR-21 inhibitor (20 nM) led to increased expression of TGFBI as well as fibrosis-related genes, including TIMP1, ACTA2, and COL1A1 (Figure 5H). This upregulation was significantly diminished upon co-treatment with TGFBI siRNA (Figure 5H). Conversely, in SNU387 and SNU423 cells, which are characterized by lower miR-21 and higher TGFBI expression compared to JHH-1 cells (Figures 5F and 5G), treatment with miR-21 mimic was able to reduce both TGFBI and fibrosis-associated gene expression (Figures 5I and 5J). These experiments allowed us to demonstrate that miR-21 regulates TGFBI expression, which in turn modulates the expression of fibrosis-related genes. By manipulating TGFBI expression through siRNA-mediated knockdown and miR-21 mimic transfection, we established that miR-21 acts upstream to control fibrogenic gene expression. Together, these results highlight the pivotal role of TGFBI in mediating the downstream effects of miR-21 in this context, deepening our understanding of the molecular mechanisms at play.

Administration of miR-21 mimic slows the progression of liver-disease pathogenesis in WT mice on HFD

As our previous results indicated an inverse relation between disease pathogenesis and miR-21 mRNA levels in WT mice, we next investigated whether supplementation of miR-21 after the disease manifestation could attenuate further disease progression in WT mice fed on HFD. To address this, we injected WT mice with 75 mg/kg of DEN intraperitoneally at 3 weeks of age and continually fed them on HFD as before. This 3-fold increase in DEN dosage compared to previous experiments was intended to accelerate liver pathogenesis, thereby shortening the experimental time frame. Based on the prior findings, we allowed disease progression until week 16, by which point we anticipated the development of metabolic defects, including obesity, insulin resistance, and steatosis. At week 16, we randomly assigned the mice to two groups and injected lipid nanoparticles (LNOPs) encapsulating either an NC or miR-21 mimic intravenously via the tail-vein route. Each group was injected twice weekly, administering a total of 1.8 mg/kg of mimic per week for 6 weeks. At week 22, mice were sacrificed and liver tissue and serum were collected for further analysis (Figure 6A). Since Tgfbi is a direct target of miR-21, we assessed its expression levels in response to miR-21 mimic treatment. We then analyzed the metabolic phenotypes of the treated mice. Body-weight measurements revealed that the mice receiving miR-21 mimic were significantly protected from further weight gain over the 6-week treatment period (Figure 6C). An insulin-tolerance assay revealed comparable glucose levels in the serum of both groups after 5 h of starvation. Although the genes involved in insulin signaling did not show significant alteration in their levels (Figure S8B), insulin-tolerance assay performed at both week 18 and week 22 time points in the miR-21 mimic-injected mice exhibited more sustained glucose-lowering response than the NC mimic group, indicating heightened insulin sensitivity (Figure 6D). Further, we performed histological analyses on liver sections using H&E, oil red O, and Sirius red staining to assess tissue architecture, steatosis, and fibrosis, respectively. Notably, the livers of miR-21 mimic-treated mice displayed improved tissue architecture with reduced hypertrophy, fat accumulation, and fibrosis (Figure 6E). Blinded histology scoring of these samples supported these findings, although a slight inflammation was noted in miR-21 mimic-injected mice (Figure 6F). qPCR analysis of genes involved in steatosis, lipid metabolism, and inflammation also validated these observations (Figures S8C, S8D, and S8E). Finally, tumor-burden analysis revealed that the miR-21 mimic-injected mice exhibited reduced tumor penetrance (Figure 6H) and lower tumor burden as compared to the NC mimic group (Figures 6G and 6I).

Together, these results indicate that administration of miR-21 mimic mitigates the CD-HFD-induced pathological features of liver disease. These results support a protective function of miR-21 in liver-disease pathogenesis and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target to prevent or slow down the disease progression in the context of HFD exposure.

Discussion

In this study, we have developed a comprehensive, HFD-based model of liver disease in mice that recapitulates the full spectrum of clinical stages observed in human patients, ranging from metabolic syndrome to MASLD to HCC. We further investigated the role of miR-21 in this disease progression. Among the most compelling findings of our study is the heightened susceptibility of miR-21-KO mice to systemic insults, including exposure to a low dose of DEN and a chronic HFD feeding. The accelerated disease progression and the increased severity of all metabolic and pathological stages of liver disease in the miR-21-KO-HFD combination underscore the protective role of miR-21 in liver-disease progression in vivo under these metabolic conditions. In addition, our studies suggest that this protection by miR-21 is mediated, at least in part, through its direct miR-21 target, Tgfbi.

To establish our liver-disease model, we used mice of B6/129F2J background. It is well known that different strains exhibit substantial variations in MASH pathology and liver fibrosis. For example, analysis of clinical and molecular traits in 100 unique inbred mouse strains showed a >30-fold variation in hepatic TG accumulation51 or various degrees of NAFLD.52 Recent studies analyzing different genetic backgrounds fed on varying dietary regimes (i.e., A/J, BALB/c, C3H/HeJ, C57BL/6J, CBA/CaH, DBA/2J, FVB/N, and NOD/ShiLtJ53 and C57BL/6J, DBA/2J, A/J, 129S1/SvlmJ, WSB/EiJ, CAST/EiJ, and PWK/PhJ53) pointed to the FVB/N mouse strain as the most adequate diet-induced mouse model for the recapitulation of MASLD54 and the PWK/PhJ mice as exhibiting the highest overlap with the human NASH signatures.53 Reportedly, 129Sv mice exhibit the most consistent developemnt of diet-induced non-alcoholic/alcoholic fatty liver disease relative to C57BL/6 and CD-1 mice, as indicated by alternations in their hepatic lipid profiles55 and in the diet-induced animal model of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (DIAMOND), B6/129, a cross between two mouse strains 129S1/SvImJ and C57Bl/6J, recapitulated non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and hepatic cancers signatures, features absent in the parental strains.56 While all the mentioned studies report that the extent of phenotypic changes triggered by environmental factors vary greatly based on the genetic background of the animal model, except the DIAMOND model, which studied the first filial generation of B6/129, no prior study, to the best of our understanding, has investigated liver-disease progression using the B6/129F2J strain, a second filial generation related to B6/129, which we employed in this study.

It is well established that an HFD causes obesity and hepatic steatosis. Various HFD-based mouse models of liver disease exist57,58,59; however, many fail to reflect a complete spectrum of human liver pathology or require long periods to develop advanced disease stages such as HCC, hindering their acceptance. Conversely, models that achieve late-stage disease either utilize high doses of carcinogens or genetic modifications.60,61,62 In this study, we aimed to create a model that constitutes the entire liver-disease spectrum while using an obesogenic diet that represents US dietary habits. To this end, we adopted a diet consisting of 58% kcals originating from fat. To accelerate the development of MASH-associated pathologies such as HCC while also minimizing the impact of the carcinogen, we limited the dose of DEN to 25 mg/kg using only a one-time injection.

Although miR-21 has been widely implicated in the pathogenesis of HCC,22,23,24 the literature does not provide a clear consensus regarding its precise role. Several studies have reported oncogenic functions of miR-21, while others suggest tumor-suppressive or neutral effects, highlighting that its actions are highly context dependent.18,22,23,24,31,32 Such discrepancies likely arise from differences in experimental models, cellular contexts, environments, and regulatory networks in which miR-21 is embedded.23,28,29 These observations underscore the pleiotropic nature of miR-21, which can modulate multiple signaling pathways and exert diverse biological effects depending on the cellular environment.15,31,32 In support of previous studies reporting a protective role for miR-21 in HCC,31,32 our study provides additional evidence that challenges the conventional oncogenic role of miR-21. By utilizing the whole-body miR-21-KO mouse model, we demonstrated that the loss of miR-21 leads to exacerbated liver pathology, including increased liver injury, hepatomegaly, hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, steatosis, fibrosis, and ultimately HCC in mice. Our results unambiguously affirm the protective function of miR-21 during liver-disease progression. While we were able to demonstrate the full spectrum of metabolic (i.e., obesity, hepatomegaly, hyperglycemia, and insulin resistance) as well as pathophysiological phenotypes (i.e., steatosis, fibrosis, MASH, and progressive HCC) induced by HFD and DEN exposure, we also showed that these effects were much more severe in the miR-21-KO background. This aligns with the multiple-hit hypothesis of liver pathogenesis, wherein the loss of miR-21 serves as the primary hit, rendering the liver more vulnerable to subsequent secondary insults such as DEN, choline deficiency, and high fat, and ensuing metabolic dysfunctions. Our study thus offers insights into the multifaceted interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental factors in liver-disease progression to HCC. Mechanistically, we observed an inverse relationship between miR-21 levels and liver-disease severity. Notably, the direct miR-21 target, Tgfbi, exhibited increased expression in the absence of miR-21, regardless of HFD exposure. Previous literature has linked elevated Tgfbi levels in the serum of patients diagnosed with various gastrointestinal tract carcinomas63 with reductions observed following therapeutic interventions,64 emphasizing its role in cancer pathogenesis. In mouse models, Tgfbi KO in mice conferred resistance to adipose tissue hypertrophy, liver steatosis, and insulin resistance.65 In a separate study, the overexpression of Tgfbi showed an increase in the incidence of carcinogen-induced liver pre-neoplasia and tumors, whereas the KO showed protection,64 demonstrating that it is causal in tumor formation.

In our study, as expected from a direct miR-21 target gene, liver Tgfbi showed significant upregulation in response to miR-21 KO with and without HFD exposure, especially at week 32, while the tumor incidence was highest. In contrast, Tgfbi levels were repressed by exogenous miR-21 administration. We further tested this correlation in HCC patients’ liver tissues and found it to be consistent with our data and previous literature.48 Furthermore, using human HCC cell lines with varying TGFBI expressions, we showed that TGFBI levels affect the expression of genes involved in fibrosis, such as Col1a1, Timp1, and Timp2. Manipulation of TGFBI levels using the miR-21 mimic or inhibitor confirmed a direct regulatory functional relationship between miR-21 and TGFBI. Our results strongly suggest that Tgfbi is a pivotal downstream target that is responsible for promoting liver disease in the context of miR-21 loss, with or without concurrent HFD exposure. Coupled with previous literature showing its role in liver disease and cancer, this study makes Tgfbi a potential causal miR-21 downstream target promoting liver disease in the HFD setting.

MiRNA-based therapeutics hold considerable promise as a strategy for restoring depleted miRNA levels. Owing to the observed reduced levels of miR-21 under severe liver-disease conditions, we speculated that restoring miR-21 levels might have a beneficial impact on disease progression. Our hypothesis was also supported by a study by Lhamyani et al., where the authors demonstrated that miR-21 mimic administration delayed weight gain in HFD-fed mice, highlighting miR-21 mimic as a potential therapeutic tool to modulate adipose function and obesity.32 To investigate our hypothesis, we employed LNPs to encapsulate the miR-21 mimic and administered them by tail-vein injections to WT mice with pre-manifested features of metabolic syndrome, steatosis, and fibrosis. Our 6-week injection regimen of weekly injections demonstrated a significant improvement in various parameters, including body weight, insulin sensitivity, and levels of steatosis and fibrosis. Our study underscores the potential of miR-21 as a viable miRNA therapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment of liver disease.

Clinical significance

We propose that the potential mechanism of toxicity of the combination of miR-21-KO DEN exposure and CD-HFD feeding is mediated through elevated expression of the miR-21 target protein TGFBI. Similar upregulation in TGFBI was observed in HCC clinical samples, correlating with HCC phenotype. Thus, we propose that, in addition to targeting miR-21, TGFBI itself represents a promising therapeutic target and could be pursued to tackle liver-disease progression. Our study has important implications for understanding the mechanisms underlying liver-disease progression, especially in patients with low expression of miR-21 and a lifestyle with fatty, obesogenic diets and for the design of future studies directed at the prevention and treatment of liver disease.

Study limitations

This study used a whole-body miR-21-KO model, preventing us from pinpointing liver- or cell-type-specific effects of miR-21 loss. However, previous work has revealed that miR-21 KO in just hepatocytes was associated with increased liver cancer in a tissue-specific KO mouse model.31 In addition, our study was performed using only male mice due to the higher incidence of HCC observed in the male population in humans. Given the well-documented regulation of miR-21 by female sex hormones (estrogen and progesterone66,67), additional studies in female mice are warranted to assess the translatability of this model across sexes. Lastly, miR-21 mimics were administered intravenously in mice during this study. Although no obvious side effects were observed at the tested dose, potential unwanted or off-target effects in hepatic and non-hepatic tissues at higher doses may occur and need to be considered.

Taken together, our study presents a comprehensive murine model of liver disease, based on an HFD and a small amount of carcinogen, that captures the spectrum of human phenotypes and highlights the protective role of miR-21. Using whole-body miR-21 KO, our findings showed that the animals lacking the miR-21 expression are predisposed to upcoming liver insults and that this loss is sufficient to cause notable liver-disease pathogenesis. In addition, we show that the combination of miR-21 KO and HFD is a recipe for severely accelerated liver-disease progression from simple steatosis to HCC, thus highlighting the protective role of miR-21 in prevailing Western-diet metabolic settings. We further suggest that this protection by miR-21 is mediated, at least in part, by regulating its direct target, Tgfbi. Finally, we propose that utilizing miR-21 as a therapeutic might be an effective strategy to prevent or delay liver disease, including HCC.

While acknowledging the limitations of our study, we anticipate that our findings will stimulate further investigation into intricate mechanisms governing liver disease and pave the way for innovative therapeutic strategies. Our study has far-reaching implications, not only in advancing the understanding of liver-disease pathogenesis but also in guiding future research and therapeutic interventions aimed at preventing and treating these debilitating conditions.

Materials and methods

Sex as a biological variable

Our study examined male mice as male animals are more likely to be affected by severe liver disease.

Animals

The B6/129SF2/J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory and were used as WT controls. miR-21-KO (B6;129S6-Mir21atm1Yoli/J) mice with the same background were used for the experiment. The complete KO of miR-21 was assessed by the genotyping assay of the WT and miR-21-KO mice, as suggested by the Jackson Laboratory. All mice were housed in a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle in a 21-23°C pathogen-free barrier facility with free access to water. All procedures were performed according to protocols approved by the Animal Care facility of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) and Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (protocol number: 010-2021).

Dietary interventions

Male mice were used for this study. At 3 weeks of age, all mice were injected with 25 mg/kg of DEN (catalog [Cat.] #N0756) by intraperitoneal injections. All mice were maintained ad libitum on of either a normal chow diet (LabDiet, Formulab Diet, Irradiated 5008C33. %kcal: 26.644% protein, 56.844% carbohydrate, 16.512% fat) or CD-HFD (Research Diets, D17022005i. %kcal: 17% protein, 26% carbohydrate, 58% fat). The full composition was as follows: casein, 80 mesh, 228 g; DL-methionine, 2 g; maltodextrin 10, 170 g; sucrose, 175 g; soyabean oil, 25 g; coconut oil, hydrogenated, 333.5 g; mineral mix S10001, 40 g; sodium bicarbonate, 10.5 g; potassium citrate, 1 H2O, 4 g; vitamin mix V10001, 10 g; and food, drug, and color (FD&C) Red Dye #40, 0.1 g. The mice were euthanized at their respective time points for further analysis.

Biochemical analysis

Blood was collected at regular intervals for biochemical analysis using the submandibular blood collection method. Glucose measurement was done using the Accu-Chek Guide. ALT (Teco diagnostics, Anaheim, USA), AST (843 3815), triglyceride (133 6544), and cholesterol (166 9829) measurements were done using the instrument VITROS350 from Ortho-Clinical Diagnostics. Whole-body fat was measured using the EchoMRI 2009 3- in-1 composition analyzer (version 130718).

Insulin-tolerance assay

All the mice were fasted for 5 h before performing the insulin-tolerance assay (ITT). The mice were injected with an insulin solution (1.5 U/kg) via intraperitoneal injections of Humulin R (NDC 0002-8215-17). A minimum of five mice per group were used for the assay. During the assay, mice were measured for glucose at 0 (baseline), 15, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90, 105, and 120 min post insulin administration by tail snip.

Histological analysis

For liver histological analysis, mouse liver tissues from weeks 16, 24, and 32 were dissected and fixed in either 10% neutral buffered formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin or placed in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) medicum and frozen on dry ice. Liver histology was assessed using H&E staining in paraffin-embedded sections at the BIDMC institutional core facility. Fibrosis was assessed using Sirius red staining in paraffin-embedded sections. The presence of steatosis was confirmed with oil red O staining in frozen sections. An expert pathologist evaluated the liver histology in a blinded manner. The histology-scoring systems from Liang et al.68 and Kleiner et al.69 were adapted to assess the phenotypes. The liver sections were evaluated for the presence and degree of steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. The grading system for hypertrophy and steatosis is defined as follows: 0 (<5%), 1 (5%–33%), 2 (34%–66%), and 3 (>66%). Hypertrophy was defined as cellular enlargement more than 1.5 times the normal hepatocyte diameter and was scored based on the percentage area affected. Steatosis was scored and graded based on whether the vacuoles displaced the nucleus to the side (macro-vesicular) or not (micro-vesicular). Inflammation was scored as the number of inflammatory foci per field using 100× magnification (view size of 3.1 mm2), while a focus is defined as ≥5 inflammatory cells. Five different fields were scored, and the average was categorized as: 0 (<0.5 foci), 1 (0.5–1.0 foci), 2 (1.0–2.0 foci), and 3 (>2.0 foci). Fibrosis was evaluated at 40× magnification by scoring collagen staining as 0 (only in vessels) or observed within the hepatic parenchyma as 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), or massive (3). In addition, the surface area covered by collagen staining was measured using ImageJ.

The macroscopic and microscopic tumor nodules were elevated and classified according to their malignant potential. A panel of immunohistochemistry (IHC) stains used to study malignant potential included glypican 3 (Invitrogen PA5-89561, polyclonal, 1:300 and 1:600 dilutions), glutamine synthetase 6 (Abcam AB176562, clone EPR13022, 1:500 dilution), β-catenin (Abcam AB223075, clone IGX4794R-3, 1:4,000 dilution), heat shock protein 70 (Abcam AB194360, clone EPR16893, 1:4,000 dilution), and serum amyloid A (Bio SBBSB2806, clone EP335, 1:100 dilution). All IHC staining was performed on the Leica Bond III platform.

RNA extraction, cDNA preparation, and RT-qPCR

Total RNA extraction was performed using Trizol (Invitrogen, Cat. #15596018), and RT-qPCR was performed using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche, Cat. #04887352001) on the Roche LightCycler 480 II System. The primers were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies, USA. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. For miR-21 quantification, the pre-designed miRCURY LNA miRNA PCR assay kit from Qiagen was used (GeneGlobe ID YP00204230, Cat. #339306).

Protein quantification and western blotting

Total protein was determined using the Pierce BCA protein kit (Thermo Scientific). Then, 10 μg of total protein was mixed with 4× Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad), boiled for 5 min at 95°C, and loaded in a Novex gradient 4%–12% Bis-Tris polyacrylamide gel (Thermo Scientific) for electrophoresis. After the electrophoresis run, proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane, 0.45 μm (Thermo Scientific), using the iBlot2 system (Thermo Scientific). Anti-TGFBI (Proteintech, Cat. #10188) and anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. #2118) were diluted 1/500 and 1/1,000, respectively, in 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T; 20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5, 0.1% Tween 20) supplemented with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) or 5% non-fat milk according to the manufacturer’s instructions and visualized by blotting with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary anti-rabbit antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. #7074) 1:3,000 dilution in 5% non-fat milk. Chemiluminescence was detected using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific) in a GE Amersham Imager 600 Imager (GE Healthcare, UK). Western bands were analyzed using Image Studio, version 5.2.5 (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA).

Library preparation and sequencing

Libraries were prepared from the extracted RNA using the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit with RiboErase (HMR) KK8561. To understand the effects caused by HFD exposure, and miR-21 KO independently and in combination, we performed a bulk RNA-seq analysis. Livers from all four groups, i.e., WT-chow, WT-HFD, miR-21 KO-chow, and miR-21 KO-HFD at weeks 16, 24, and 32, were extracted and were used to isolate RNA. In addition, tumors extracted from WT-HFD and miR-21 KO-HFD at week 32 were also used to isolate RNA and perform bulk RNA sequencing.

RNA sequencing analysis

Raw sequencing reads were aligned to a reference transcriptome generated from the Ensembl v104 GRCm38 database with salmon v1.4.0 using options “–seqBias – useVBOpt –gcBias –posBias –numBootstraps 30 –validate Mappings.” Length-scaled transcripts per million were acquired using tximport v1.18.0, and log2 fold changes and false discovery rates were determined by DESeq2 in R. t-SNE was performed with counts transformed by the variance stabilizing transformation function from DESeq2.

Differential expression analysis

Qiagen Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (94302991) software was used to perform gene enrichment and comparative analyses for all samples. A part of the analysis was also performed using the online tool Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) software.

LNP formulation

mirVana mimics purchased from Thermo Fisher were used in the synthesis of miRNA s LNPs. Mimics for miR-21a-5p (5′-UAGCUUAUCAGACUGAUGUUGA-3′) (MC10206), and NC #1 (CM00102) were generated with a 5′-Carboxyfluorescein (FAM) modification. The miRNA mimics were dissolved in PNI formulation buffer (NWW0043) at a 0.24-mg/mL working concentration for an N/P ratio of 3. Encapsulation was performed using the NanoAssemblr Benchtop system (Precision NanoSystems). Diluted RNA was mixed with Genvoy-ILM-DiD (NWW0039), a fluorescently labeled, off-the-shelf lipid mix (molar ratios of 50:10:37.5:2.5 for PNI-lipid, DSPC, cholesterol, and PNI stabilizer). Components were mixed via a microfluidic cartridge (NIT0062) at a flow rate ratio of 3:1 (RNA in PNI buffer:Genvoy-ILM) at a total flow rate of 12 mL/min. After encapsulation, particles were dialyzed against PBS and concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filters (EMD Millipore).

Ribogreen assay for RNA encapsulation efficiency

A modified Ribogreen assay (Invitrogen, Quant-iT Ribogreen RNA assay kit) was performed to determine the encapsulation efficiency. In a 96-well clear microplate, miRNA-LNP samples were added in a 1:1 ratio to either 1× Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer or 2% Triton buffer (Triton in TE buffer) in duplicates with Ribogreen reagent. After a 10-min incubation at 37°C, the plate was read using a fluorescent plate reader with an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 528 nm. Encapsulation efficiency and mRNA concentration were calculated using the standard curve and sample values.

LNP characterization

Mean diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using ZetaSizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical). Samples were diluted to ∼18 ng/μL of total RNA and added to a quartz cuvette (ZEN0040), and measurements were made using a 173° backscatter angle. Measurement parameters were as follows: the material refractive index was 1.45 m and absorption was 0.001; for the dispersant, viscosity was 1.0200 cP, RI 1.335, and dielectric constant 80.4. Ten runs of 10-s duration were performed for each of the measurements. Data were processed using a general-purpose model with normal resolution.

LNP injections in mice

For the therapeutic-intervention experiment, the WT mice were injected with 75 mg/kg of DEN at 3 weeks of age and were fed on HFD throughout the experimental duration. At week 16, the mice were divided into two categories: NC and miR-21 mimic. LNPs encapsulating either the NC or miR-21 mimic were injected via the intravenous tail vein in each mouse twice weekly, totaling 1.8 mg/kg of mimic per week. The injections were performed for 6 weeks, and, at the end of week 22, all mice were euthanized to collect livers.

Cell culture

Human HCC cell lines JHH2, SNU387, and SNU423 were a gift from Dr. Christopher Ott from the Center for Molecular Therapeutics, Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston. The JHH2 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12634010) with 10% FBS (F2442) and 2% Pen/Strep (Gibco 15140-122). The SNU387 and SNU423 were cultured in RMPI-1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11875119) with the additional 10% FBS, 2% Pen/Strep, 1% sodium pyruvate (Gibco 11360070), and 1% 100× glucose (Gibco A2494001).

In vitro assays

SNU-387 and SNU-423 cells were seeded in six-well plates and, at 70% confluency, were transfected with NC (Qiagen, GeneGlobe ID: YM00479902-ADA) or miR-21 (Qiagen, GeneGlobe ID: YM00473093-ADA) miRCURY LNA miRNA mimic using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, 13778150). After 48 h, the cells were collected and fixed in Trizol for RNA extraction. The mRNA expression of TGFBI and fibrosis genes was evaluated using the RT-qPCR method.

JHH-1 cells were seeded in six-well plates. At 70% confluency, the wells were divided into three treatment groups: (1) NC inhibitor (Qiagen, GeneGlobe ID: YM00479902-ADA) + NC siRNA (IDT, 51-01-14-03), (2) NC siRNA + miR-21 inhibitor (Qiagen, GeneGlobe ID: YI04100689-DDA), and (3) miR-21 inhibitor + TGFBI siRNA (IDT, 483790144, hs.Ri.TGFBI.13.1). These were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. After 48 h of treatment, cells were collected and fixed in Trizol for RNA extraction. The mRNA expression of TGFBI and fibrosis genes was evaluated using the RT-qPCR method.

Statistical analysis

All data represent at least three independent experiments. p < 0.05 was considered significant and indicated by ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Analyses performed included two-way analysis of variance, Student’s t test, and unpaired t test where appropriate.
