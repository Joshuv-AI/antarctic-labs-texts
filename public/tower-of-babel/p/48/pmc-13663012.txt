Incremental diagnostic value of HSP-70, presepsin, and HO-1 for differentiating acute appendicitis from nonsurgical abdominal pain in children: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Sarper Sahin, Husam Ibrahimoglu, Fatmagul Can, Yasemin Unal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Acute appendicitis is the most common surgical emergency in childhood and one of the leading indications for urgent abdominal surgery in paediatric emergency departments [1, 2]. Despite advances in imaging and laboratory evaluation, diagnosis remains challenging in children with nonspecific abdominal pain, atypical symptom localisation, early-stage disease, or equivocal physical examination findings [1–4]. Delayed diagnosis may increase the risk of disease progression and complications, whereas overdiagnosis may lead to unnecessary imaging, negative appendectomy, and avoidable surgical intervention [1–4].

Current evaluation relies on clinical assessment, routine inflammatory markers, clinical scoring systems, and imaging [1, 2, 5–8]. White blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and C-reactive protein are widely available and may support clinical suspicion; however, they reflect nonspecific inflammation and may also be elevated in nonsurgical abdominal conditions [5–8]. Their diagnostic performance is therefore insufficient for stand-alone decision-making, and laboratory findings should be interpreted together with physical examination, clinical risk assessment, and appropriate imaging. Ultrasonography remains the preferred first-line imaging modality in children because it avoids ionising radiation; however, nondiagnostic examinations are common, and diagnostic uncertainty may persist after the initial evaluation [9–12].

These diagnostic limitations have led to the evaluation of additional biomarkers beyond conventional inflammatory markers [8, 13–21]. Heat shock protein-70 (HSP-70) is upregulated in response to cellular stress, ischaemia, tissue injury, and inflammatory activation, making it a biologically plausible marker in localised appendiceal inflammation [13–15]. Presepsin reflects monocyte–macrophage activation following bacterial stimulation, whereas heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) represents an inducible oxidative stress-response pathway with cytoprotective and immunomodulatory properties [16–21]. Together, these markers represent complementary biological domains, including tissue stress, innate immune activation, and oxidative injury response.

However, a difference in biomarker concentration alone does not establish clinical usefulness. For a novel biomarker to justify additional cost and testing, it should demonstrate diagnostic value beyond routinely available laboratory variables. Evidence regarding the incremental diagnostic value of inflammatory and tissue-stress biomarkers beyond conventional markers remains limited in children with suspected acute appendicitis [8, 16, 20]. This prospective cohort study therefore evaluated the individual diagnostic performance of serum HSP-70, presepsin, and HO-1 and explored whether these biomarkers improved discrimination beyond a conventional laboratory-marker model comprising white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and C-reactive protein.

Methods

Study design, setting, and ethics

This prospective, single-centre diagnostic biomarker cohort study included consecutive children aged 3–17 years presenting to a tertiary paediatric emergency department with clinically suspected acute appendicitis and access to integrated paediatric surgical consultation. The primary diagnostic comparison was histopathologically confirmed appendicitis versus nonsurgical abdominal pain within the symptomatic cohort.

The study protocol was approved by the Kütahya Health Sciences University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (decision no. 2025/07 − 03; 29 May 2025) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians before enrolment, and assent was obtained from age-appropriate children in accordance with institutional policy.

Study objectives

The primary objective was to determine whether serum HSP-70, presepsin, and HO-1 concentrations differed between children with histopathologically confirmed appendicitis and those with nonsurgical abdominal pain within the symptomatic cohort. Secondary objectives were to assess the individual diagnostic performance of the investigated biomarkers and to explore whether their addition improved diagnostic discrimination beyond routinely available laboratory variables, including white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and C-reactive protein.

Participants and cohort structure

The symptomatic study population comprised 68 consecutive children aged 3–17 years presenting with clinically suspected acute appendicitis. Consecutive enrolment was used to reduce selection bias and preserve the clinical spectrum of children evaluated for suspected appendicitis in routine paediatric emergency practice.

For the principal diagnostic comparison, children in the symptomatic cohort were classified according to final diagnostic status as having either histopathologically confirmed appendicitis or nonsurgical abdominal pain. Biomarker-specific and exploratory incremental-value analyses were performed in the complete-case cohort of children with available biomarker measurements.

Clinical evaluation and final diagnostic classification

All symptomatic children underwent routine clinical evaluation, including history-taking, physical examination, laboratory testing, and imaging when clinically indicated. Ultrasonography was the preferred first-line imaging modality, whereas computed tomography was reserved for nondiagnostic ultrasonography, discordance between clinical and ultrasonographic findings, or persistent clinical concern [9–12]. Imaging decisions were made by the treating clinicians and were not mandated by the study protocol, thereby reflecting routine clinical practice.

Final diagnostic status was assigned using a prespecified reference standard. In children who underwent appendectomy, histopathology served as the reference standard. Children managed nonoperatively were classified as having nonsurgical abdominal pain if symptoms resolved and no diagnosis of appendicitis was established during structured follow-up assessments on days 7 and 30, including symptom review and physical examination. One child who underwent appendectomy but had no histopathological evidence of appendicitis was classified as having a negative appendectomy and included in the nonsurgical abdominal pain group.

Biomarker sampling and laboratory procedures

Blood samples were obtained at initial presentation as part of routine clinical evaluation, before imaging escalation or therapeutic intervention whenever feasible. No additional venepuncture or research-specific intervention was performed. Residual serum samples were processed according to institutional laboratory procedures and stored at − 80 °C until batch analysis.

Serum HSP-70, presepsin, and HO-1 concentrations were measured in duplicate using sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits from BT Lab, China, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay details were as follows: HSP-70 (catalogue no. E1813Hu; analytical range, 0.3–90 ng/mL; lower limit of detection, 0.12 ng/mL), presepsin (catalogue no. E3754Hu; analytical range, 5–1000 pg/mL; lower limit of detection, 2.39 pg/mL), and HO-1 (catalogue no. R0932Hu; analytical range, 0.1–9 ng/mL; lower limit of detection, 0.05 ng/mL). Manufacturer-reported intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were < 8% and < 10%, respectively, for all assays.

Laboratory personnel performing the assays were blinded to final clinical classification, and biomarker results were not available to treating clinicians during patient management.

Biomarker availability

Biomarker measurements were available for 63 of 68 symptomatic children (92.6%). Five children had missing biomarker measurements because of insufficient residual serum volume, and all were in the nonsurgical abdominal pain group. Therefore, biomarker-specific and exploratory incremental-value analyses were restricted to the complete-case cohort, comprising 34 children with histopathologically confirmed appendicitis and 29 with nonsurgical abdominal pain. No imputation was performed.

Incremental diagnostic value analysis

Exploratory multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed in the complete-case cohort to assess whether the investigated biomarkers provided incremental diagnostic value beyond routinely available laboratory variables. The dependent variable was final diagnostic status, defined as histopathologically confirmed appendicitis versus nonsurgical abdominal pain.

The conventional laboratory-marker model included log-transformed white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and log-transformed C-reactive protein measured at initial presentation. HSP-70, presepsin, and HO-1 were also log-transformed before regression modelling because of right-skewed distributions. Each investigated biomarker was added separately to the conventional laboratory-marker model. To limit model complexity in this small cohort, no model including more than one novel biomarker was fitted.

Model discrimination was assessed using the apparent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve with 95% confidence intervals. Changes in discrimination after addition of each biomarker were evaluated by comparison of correlated receiver operating characteristic curves using the DeLong method. Nested models were additionally compared using likelihood-ratio tests. These analyses were exploratory and were intended to assess incremental discrimination rather than to develop or validate a clinical prediction rule.

Use of artificial intelligence tools

During manuscript preparation, a large language model was used only for English language editing and formatting assistance. All scientific content, data analysis, interpretation, and final manuscript decisions were performed and verified by the authors.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarised as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to assess the individual diagnostic discrimination of the investigated biomarkers. Areas under the curve were reported with 95% confidence intervals calculated using the DeLong method [22]. Optimal cut-off values were identified using the Youden index, and sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated at these cohort-derived thresholds. These thresholds were not considered externally validated clinical decision limits.

Because three primary biomarkers were evaluated, P values for between-group biomarker comparisons were adjusted using the Holm sequential procedure. Holm adjustment was also applied to the three comparisons of incremental model discrimination. Both nominal and Holm-adjusted P values are reported. All tests were two-sided, and adjusted P values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Primary symptomatic comparison: appendicitis versus nonsurgical abdominal pain

In the complete-case biomarker cohort, HSP-70 concentrations were nominally higher in children with histopathologically confirmed appendicitis than in those with nonsurgical abdominal pain (median, 26.30 [IQR, 25.55–27.98] ng/mL vs. 25.70 [IQR, 24.10–27.30] ng/mL; unadjusted P = 0.044). However, this difference did not remain statistically significant after Holm adjustment for the three primary biomarker comparisons (adjusted P = 0.132).

Presepsin concentrations were numerically higher in the appendicitis group but did not differ significantly between groups (181.85 [IQR, 171.25–206.58] pg/mL vs. 174.00 [IQR, 156.00–189.30] pg/mL; unadjusted P = 0.117; adjusted P = 0.234). HO-1 concentrations were similar between groups (1.75 [IQR, 1.67–1.79] ng/mL vs. 1.71 [IQR, 1.59–1.81] ng/mL; unadjusted and adjusted P = 0.557). Therefore, none of the evaluated biomarkers showed a statistically significant between-group difference after adjustment for multiple comparisons (Table 2).

Diagnostic performance of individual biomarkers

HSP-70 had the highest numerical AUC among the investigated biomarkers, with a value of 0.649 (95% CI, 0.510–0.788). Presepsin showed a slightly lower AUC of 0.616 (95% CI, 0.473–0.758), whereas HO-1 showed limited discrimination, with an AUC of 0.544 (95% CI, 0.396–0.691). Pairwise DeLong comparisons did not demonstrate statistically significant differences between HSP-70 and presepsin (P = 0.662) or between HSP-70 and HO-1 (P = 0.149).

At the cohort-derived Youden thresholds, HSP-70 and presepsin showed identical sensitivity and specificity, whereas HO-1 showed lower sensitivity. Overall, the individual diagnostic discrimination of all three investigated biomarkers was modest, with limited specificity for HSP-70 and presepsin (Fig. 2, Table 3).

Incremental diagnostic value beyond conventional laboratory markers

The conventional laboratory-marker model, comprising log-transformed white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and log-transformed C-reactive protein, had an apparent AUC of 0.749 (95% CI, 0.627–0.872). Addition of HSP-70 increased the apparent AUC to 0.779 (95% CI, 0.664–0.894; ΔAUC, 0.029), whereas addition of presepsin and HO-1 resulted in AUCs of 0.764 (95% CI, 0.647–0.881; ΔAUC, 0.014) and 0.745 (95% CI, 0.622–0.869; ΔAUC, − 0.004), respectively.

Although HSP-70 and presepsin produced small numerical improvements in apparent model performance, none of the investigated biomarkers significantly improved discrimination beyond the conventional laboratory-marker model after Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons. HO-1 provided no incremental diagnostic value. These findings indicate that the evaluated biomarkers did not demonstrate statistically robust incremental diagnostic value beyond routinely available laboratory markers (Table 4).

Discussion

In emergency departments, the diagnosis of acute appendicitis relies on a combination of clinical assessment, laboratory findings, and imaging. Despite this approach, missed appendicitis, negative appendectomy, and reliance on computed tomography remain clinically relevant concerns in children [1–4, 9–12]. These challenges have prompted evaluation of additional biomarkers; however, a novel biomarker should demonstrate value beyond routinely available laboratory variables before it can justify additional testing in clinical practice [8].

In this prospective cohort of children with suspected acute appendicitis, HSP-70 had the highest numerical AUC among the investigated biomarkers. Although HSP-70 concentrations showed a nominal unadjusted between-group difference, this finding did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for multiple biomarker comparisons. Its modest individual discrimination and low specificity further limit its utility as a stand-alone diagnostic test. Nevertheless, the observed numerical difference is biologically plausible because HSP-70 may be induced during cellular stress, tissue injury, and inflammatory activation accompanying localised appendiceal inflammation [13–15].

Presepsin showed a slightly lower individual AUC and did not differ significantly between groups. Its Youden-derived threshold yielded sensitivity and specificity identical to those of HSP-70, but specificity remained limited. This pattern may reflect the role of presepsin as a marker of monocyte–macrophage activation and broader host-response activity rather than appendicitis-specific pathology [16–18].

HO-1 showed the weakest individual diagnostic performance and did not provide incremental value beyond conventional laboratory markers. HO-1 is more closely related to oxidative stress and cytoprotective responses, which may become more prominent in the presence of ischaemic or necrotic tissue injury [19–21]. Because acute appendicitis represents a spectrum from uncomplicated inflammation to gangrenous or perforated disease, these pathways may not be uniformly activated across all affected children [1, 2].

Importantly, the conventional laboratory-marker model comprising white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and C-reactive protein had moderate apparent discrimination, but it was not intended as a stand-alone clinical decision rule. Adding HSP-70 or presepsin resulted in small numerical improvements in apparent model performance, whereas HO-1 provided no improvement. However, none of these changes was statistically robust after adjustment for multiple comparisons. Thus, the evaluated biomarkers did not demonstrate sufficient incremental diagnostic value beyond routinely available laboratory markers to support routine clinical implementation. As with conventional laboratory markers, they should not replace clinical assessment, serial physical examination, or appropriate imaging in children with suspected appendicitis.

HSP-70 and presepsin showed relatively high sensitivity but limited specificity at the cohort-derived thresholds. This pattern is clinically understandable because these biomarkers, similar to conventional inflammatory markers, are not appendicitis-specific; rather, they may reflect inflammatory activity, cellular stress, or broader host-response pathways [13–18]. Children with nonsurgical abdominal pain may also have inflammatory or infectious conditions, such as gastroenteritis, mesenteric lymphadenitis, or urinary tract infection, which may contribute to biomarker elevation and reduce specificity [23]. Therefore, HSP-70 and presepsin should be interpreted as exploratory indicators of inflammatory activity rather than confirmatory diagnostic tests.

Taken together, these findings reinforce that the diagnostic assessment of children presenting with acute abdominal pain and suspected appendicitis should remain integrated. Routine laboratory and novel biomarker results may support risk assessment, but they should be interpreted alongside clinical assessment, serial physical examination, clinical risk stratification, and appropriate imaging, particularly ultrasonography in equivocal presentations [24, 25].

Several limitations should be considered. First, this was a single-centre study with a modest sample size, which limits generalisability and increases uncertainty around diagnostic-performance estimates. Second, the exploratory multivariable analyses were performed in a small complete-case cohort and evaluated apparent model performance only; therefore, the incremental-value findings may be unstable and should not be interpreted as a validated clinical prediction model. External validation in larger, independent cohorts is required.

Third, the Youden-derived cut-off values were obtained from the present cohort and should be interpreted as exploratory, cohort-specific thresholds rather than externally validated clinical decision limits. Fourth, biomarker measurements were missing in five symptomatic children, all in the nonsurgical abdominal pain group, because of insufficient residual serum volume. This differential missingness may have introduced selection bias and may have influenced estimates of specificity. Fifth, symptom duration and disease stage were not explicitly analysed, although biomarker expression may vary during the course of appendiceal inflammation. Sixth, the incremental value of the investigated biomarkers beyond established clinical scoring systems, including the Pediatric Appendicitis Score and Alvarado score, was not evaluated; therefore, their potential role within score-based diagnostic pathways remains uncertain. Finally, although children managed nonoperatively underwent structured follow-up on days 7 and 30 with no evidence of missed appendicitis, final classification in this group relied on clinical resolution rather than histopathology.

Conclusions

In this prospective cohort of children with suspected acute appendicitis, HSP-70 had the highest numerical individual AUC among the investigated biomarkers; however, its between-group difference did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons. Presepsin and HO-1 showed limited individual diagnostic performance.

When added to a conventional laboratory-marker model comprising white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and C-reactive protein, HSP-70 and presepsin produced small numerical improvements in apparent model performance, whereas HO-1 showed no additional contribution. However, these changes were not statistically robust in this cohort.

Overall, the evaluated biomarkers may reflect inflammatory or tissue-stress activity, but their incremental clinical value beyond routinely available laboratory markers remains uncertain. They should be interpreted, where available, together with clinical assessment, serial physical examination, and appropriate imaging. Larger multicentre studies are needed to clarify their potential role in the diagnostic evaluation of paediatric appendicitis.
