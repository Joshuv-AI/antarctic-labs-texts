APOE ε4‐related blood–brain barrier disruption and APOE ε4‐independent microstructural abnormalities in white matter hyperintensities
Authors: Yuki Oi, Qian Shen, Seraphina K. Solders, Charlotte S. Rivera, McKenna E. Williams, Emilie T. Reas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhile the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele promotes blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability and microstructural disruption, its specific contribution to white matter hyperintensity (WMH) pathological heterogeneity remains unknown.MethodsWe measured BBB permeability (K trans) in 31 and microstructure in 59 cognitively normal older adults with WMHs and normal‐appearing white matter (NAWM) using dynamic contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging and restriction spectrum imaging (RSI). Linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs) examined differences between WMHs and NAWM by APOE ε4 status.ResultsIn APOE ε4 carriers, K trans was elevated and restricted isotropic diffusion (RI) was reduced within WMHs compared to NAWM. This effect was absent in non‐carriers. In contrast, reduced restricted directional diffusion and elevated isotropic free water within WMHs was observed in both genotypes. K trans did not correlate with brain microstructure.DiscussionOur data suggest that WMHs comprise both APOE ε4‐related and APOE ε4‐independent pathological components. These findings implicate neurovascular and non‐vascular mechanisms through which APOE ε4 may contribute to WMH pathological heterogeneity.

BACKGROUND

White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are common radiological findings in older adults, 1 , 2 a surrogate marker of cerebral small vessel disease. 2 , 3 However, WMHs encompass various pathologies including abnormal vascular permeability from blood–brain barrier (BBB) breakdown, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 and microstructural damage such as axonal loss, demyelination, and gliosis. 2 , 3 , 5 , 9 , 10 , 11 This pathological heterogeneity presents a key challenge in interpreting WMHs and to understanding how risk factors affect brain health through WMHs.

The apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele, a well‐established genetic risk factor for sporadic Alzheimer's disease (AD), 12 , 13 is also an important risk factor for the development of WMHs. 14 , 15 , 16 Indeed, WMHs are more severe in APOE ε4 carriers than in non‐carriers, 16 , 17 , 18 suggesting that the underlying pathology of WMHs may differ according to APOE genotype. Although the link between APOE ε4 and WMHs may offer insight as to how APOE ε4 promotes cognitive decline, the pathological processes by which APOE ε4 drives WMHs remain unclear. 19 , 20

APOE ε4 is implicated in cognitive decline both by promoting amyloid beta (Aβ) deposition 21 , 22 and through amyloid‐independent pathways, 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 including vascular endothelial dysfunction and BBB breakdown. 27 Considering that BBB disruption may contribute to the development of WMHs, the higher susceptibility of APOE ε4 carriers to BBB dysfunction may partially account for the more severe WMHs in this population. This hypothesis is further strengthened by recent studies reporting that vascular permeability within WMHs may be a more sensitive predictor of cognitive impairment than total WMH volume, 28 highlighting the value of quantifying vascular permeability to disentangle the functional physiology of WMHs. Studies have also implicated microstructural damage, including a recent animal study suggesting that APOE ε4 reduces fractional anisotropy, axial diffusivity, and neurite density, findings that were histologically linked to demyelination. 9 Human studies in cognitively normal older adults have similarly linked APOE ε4 to microstructural damage 29 in widespread white matter tracts relevant to AD. Together, these findings establish both vascular and microstructural pathways as plausible targets of APOE ε4, raising the critical question as to whether APOE ε4 primarily contributes to WMH pathology via vascular dysfunction, microstructural damage, or both.

Characterizing such pathways may offer crucial insight into how APOE ε4 promotes AD risk through WMH burden. Focusing on cognitively normal individuals is especially critical because detecting APOE ε4‐driven pathology in preclinical disease stages may both inform future biomarker development and provide an opportunity for early precision‐medicine therapeutic intervention among individuals at elevated risk for AD.

A multi‐modal imaging approach permits simultaneous assessment of WMH burden, BBB permeability, and tissue microstructure. Dynamic contrast‐enhanced MRI (DCE‐MRI) enables direct and region‐specific quantification of BBB permeability 30 that is not afforded by biochemical proxies of BBB integrity measured from biofluid samples. Multi‐compartment diffusion MRI models, such as restriction spectrum imaging (RSI), provide detailed, in vivo characterization of cellular architecture, enabling the highly sensitive detection of subtle microstructural properties. 31 Combined with fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) MRI to measure WMHs, integrating these complementary techniques provides a powerful framework to simultaneously evaluate vascular and microstructural contributions to WMHs.

Thus, this study aimed, first, to determine whether APOE ε4 is associated with vascular permeability or microstructural integrity within WMHs, and second, to evaluate whether these complementary pathways are independent or interrelated. To this end, we used DCE‐MRI and RSI to compare vascular permeability and microstructure in WMH between APOE ε4 carriers and non‐carriers, focusing on cognitively normal older adults, who may experience preclinical pathophysiological changes that emerge prior to overt cognitive impairment.

METHODS

Participants

In this study, 59 cognitively normal participants were recruited from the University of California (UC) San Diego Shiley‐Marcos Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC; n = 50) and the San Diego community (n = 9), between April 2013 and December 2025. All 59 participants completed a diffusion MRI scan for RSI analysis, whereas a subset of 31 also completed DCE‐MRI for K trans analysis. ADRC participants were classified as cognitively normal based on consensus diagnosis from clinical and neuropsychological evaluations, in accordance with the standardized ADRC protocol. 32 Community participants underwent an overlapping neuropsychological assessment and were categorized as cognitively normal if they reported no subjective cognitive complaints and showed no objective impairment on the Mini‐Mental State Examination.

Exclusion criteria included contraindications to MRI, history of stroke or other neurological condition, treatment for a substance use disorder, kidney disease (glomerular filtration rate [GFR] < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2), or a known allergy to gadolinium‐based contrast agents. Renal function was confirmed by GFR from blood tests completed within 6 weeks before the MRI.

The initial sample eligible for analysis included 60 participants who completed T1‐weighted (T1w), FLAIR, DCE‐MRI, and RSI sequences, and met all other safety and clinical inclusion criteria. One participant was excluded due to the presence of a chronic, asymptomatic infarct on MRI. Thus, the final sample comprised 31 participants (age: mean ± standard deviation [SD], 74.2 ± 9.8, range 51–91 years, 58% female) for DCE‐MRI analyses and 59 participants (age: mean ± SD, 76.4 ± 8.3, range 51–91 years, 63% female) for RSI analyses. All procedures were approved by the UC San Diego Human Research Protections Program, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

APOE genotyping

APOE genotype data for ADRC participants (n = 50) were provided by the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center. For community participants (n = 9), saliva samples were processed by Diagnomics, Inc. using the Illumina V2.2 genotyping array, and APOE genotype was imputed from single‐nucleotide polymorphisms rs429358 and rs7412. Twenty individuals were classified as APOE ε4 carriers (19 ε3/ε4, 1 ε4/ε4), and 39 as non‐carriers (34 ε3/ε3, 6 ε2/ε3).

Measurement of vascular risk

Information on clinical comorbidities and smoking history was obtained from participant self‐report using a standard questionnaire. Clinical comorbidities of interest included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia, defined as a reported diagnosis and/or ongoing treatment for the respective condition at the time of MRI. Smoking status was categorized as never or ever (former or current). A vascular risk score was calculated as the unweighted sum of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and smoking history (range 0–4), with higher scores indicating a greater burden of vascular risk factors. These variables were selected a priori as they represent major vascular risk factors known to impact cerebrovascular health and are consistent with findings from large‐scale international studies and recommendations from major prevention guidelines. 33

Aβ biomarker assessment

Aβ biomarkers were available on 39 participants. Aβ positivity was determined using [1 8F] florbetapir positron emission tomography (n = 4), cerebrospinal fluid Aβ42/40 ratio (n = 25), plasma phosphorylated tau 217 (n = 10), selected according to prespecified temporal eligibility criteria relative to MRI. Detailed acquisition, processing, and thresholding procedures for each modality are provided in Methods S1 in supporting information.

MRI acquisition

MRI was conducted using three 3T Discovery 750 scanners (GE Healthcare) at the UC San Diego Center for Translational Imaging and at the UC San Diego Center for Functional MRI. The MRI protocol included a sagittal 3D fast spoiled gradient echo (FSPGR) T1w structural image; an axial 2D single‐shot spin‐echo echo planar imaging multishell diffusion‐weighted image; a sagittal 3D FLAIR image; and in 31 participants, DCE T1 imaging with gadobutrol injection. Because of a scanner software upgrade affecting the computation of RSI metrics, analyses for RSI included a binary covariate reflecting software version. Complete acquisition parameters are detailed in Methods S2 in supporting information.

Image preprocessing

Figure 1 presents an overview of the MRI processing and analysis workflow. All data were visually inspected for noise and artifacts prior to analysis. Structural and diffusion‐weighted images were processed using the Multi‐Modal Processing Stream (MMPS), 34 an in‐house automated pipeline combining tools from FreeSurfer (version 6.0) and the FMRIB Software Library (FSL, version 5.0.2).

Overview of imaging processing pipeline. Using the MMPS, T1w images underwent preprocessing and segmentation, which then served as a reference for co‐registration of fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery and diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) data. Dynamic contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE‐MRI) data were co‐registered to the T1w images using SPM12. The WMHs were segmented using pgs and refined to generate cerebral WMH, NAWM, and corrected WM masks. DCE‐MRI data were processed with the ROCKETSHIP package using the Patlak model to compute K trans for BBB permeability. DWI data were fitted using the RSI model to derive metrics reflecting WM microstructure: RI, RD, and IF. K trans, RI, RD, and IF were extracted from each cerebral mask (WMH, NAWM, WM) for analysis. T1w and FLAIR images underwent gradient non‐linearity, bias field correction, and resampling to 1 mm3 resolution. Diffusion data were corrected for motion, eddy current, and B0 inhomogeneities, and gradient warp distortions. DCE‐MRI data were motion corrected and co‐registered to the processed T1w structural image using SPM12 (https://fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). Full preprocessing details are provided in Methods S3 in supporting information.

Computation of K trans and RSI metrics

Pharmacokinetic modeling was performed on DCE‐MRI using the Patlak model with ROCKETSHIP 35 to yield K trans, a measure of BBB permeability, using a vascular input function derived from the superior sagittal sinus. RSI 31 metrics were computed using MMPS to derive three indices: restricted isotropic diffusion (RI, non‐directional diffusion at short length scales, likely reflecting diffusion within cell bodies or other large intracellular spaces), restricted directional diffusion (RD, anisotropic diffusion at short length scales, consistent with neurites or other long slender cellular processes), and isotropic free water (IF, non‐directional unrestricted diffusion reflecting extracellular fluid and inflammation). Detailed methods are provided in Methods S4 in supporting information.

Segmentation of WMH and NAWM

WMHs were automatically segmented from T1w and FLAIR using an artificial intelligence–based deep learning software, pgs 36 (ensemble U‐Net architecture), and refined by removing non‐cerebral regions by using an exclusion mask derived from the FreeSurfer‐derived anatomical segmentation. To account for WMHs misclassified as cortical gray matter, 37 the WMH mask was added back into the FreeSurfer‐defined cerebral white matter mask to generate a corrected WM mask. The NAWM mask was derived by subtracting the WMH mask from the corrected white matter mask. All segmentation outputs were visually inspected by an experienced neurologist with neuroimaging expertise, and manual corrections were applied when necessary to ensure anatomical accuracy.

Extraction of imaging metrics

For each participant, quantitative imaging maps (K trans, RI, RD, and IF) were co‐registered to their native T1w space. To minimize the influence of outliers and potential noise within the regions of interest, the median value within each mask (WMH and NAWM) was calculated and used as the representative metric for that region in all subsequent statistical analyses.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.1). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For analysis involving multiple RSI metrics (RI, RD, and IF), multiple comparisons across these three measures were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between APOE ε4 carriers and non‐carriers using a Welch t test for continuous variables and Fisher exact test for categorical variables.

Linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs) were used to evaluate intra‐individual differences between WMH and NAWM, inter‐individual differences by APOE4 status, and the interaction between tissue type and APOE ε4, on BBB permeability and white matter microstructure. For each imaging metric, an interaction model was constructed with tissue type (WMH vs. NAWM), APOE ε4 status (carrier vs. non‐carrier), and their interaction (tissue type × APOE ε4 status) as fixed effects. Age, sex, and scanner software version (RSI analyses only) were included as covariates and allowed to interact with tissue type. To further probe potential differences by APOE ε4, we subsequently conducted LMMs stratified by APOE ε4 status, testing the main effect of tissue type within each group. Three models with differing covariate adjustments were evaluated: (1) basic model adjusted for age, sex, and scanner software version (for RSI models); (2) a vascular model additionally adjusted for the vascular risk score; and (3) an amyloid model adding amyloid positivity to the basic model. To account for the non‐independence of repeated measures from the same individual, a random intercept was included for each participant. Effect sizes were reported as approximated partial eta squared (ηp 2), derived from the F statistic and its degrees of freedom. For data visualization, adjusted values were calculated from the basic model, by controlling for the main effects of age, sex, and scanner (only for RSI metrics), while preserving the interaction terms with tissue type, subtracting their estimated contributions from each observed value.

Finally, to investigate the relationship between vascular permeability and microstructure, we performed Spearman rank correlation analyses using the adjusted values derived from the basic model. Correlations of K trans with each RSI metric (RI, RD, and IF) separately within WMH and NAWM were conducted across all participants and stratified by APOE ε4 status. Software and statistical details are provided in Methods S5 in supporting information.

Vascular permeability within WMHs

Across all participants, there was a non‐significant trend for higher K trans in WMHs than in NAWM (p = 0.09, ηp 2 = 0.11). In basic models (adjusted for age and sex), K trans was higher in WMH compared to NAWM among APOE ε4 carriers (p = 0.006, ηp 2 = 0.69), but not among non‐carriers (p = 0.33, ηp 2 = 0.05; Figure 2A and Table S1 in supporting information). The difference among APOE ε4 carriers remained significant after additional adjustment for the vascular risk composite (p = 0.02, ηp 2 = 0.61), while no difference was observed among APOE ε4 non‐carriers (p = 0.32, ηp 2 = 0.06).

Microstructural damage in WMHs

Across all participants, WMHs exhibited lower RI and RD and higher IF than NAWM (Figure 2B–D). In basic models, RI was lower within WMHs than NAWM among APOE ε4 carriers (corrected p = 0.048, ηp 2 = 0.22), but not among non‐carriers (corrected p = 0.13, ηp 2 = 0.06). In contrast, reduced RD and elevated IF within WMHs were observed in both APOE ε4 carriers and non‐carriers (all corrected p < 0.03). Effect sizes for differences in RSI metrics between tissue types were not materially changed after additional adjustment for vascular risk factors or for amyloid positivity (Table S2 in supporting information).

There were no significant interactions between tissue type and APOE ε4 for any RSI metric (all corrected p = 0.42). RSI metrics did not differ between APOE ε4 carriers and non‐carriers within either NAWM or WMH (all corrected p > 0.17).

Associations between vascular permeability and microstructure

K trans did not correlate with any RSI metric within WMH or NAWM for either APOE ε4 carriers or non‐carriers (Table S3 in supporting information).

DISCUSSION

Using a multi‐modal approach, this study aimed to determine how APOE ε4 relates to the pathological heterogeneity of WMHs. Elevated vascular permeability and reduced RI within WMHs relative to NAWM were observed only in APOE ε4 carriers, whereas reduced RD and increased IF within WMHs were observed in both genotypes. The general pattern of these findings persisted after accounting for vascular risk factors and amyloid positivity, and the lack of association between vascular permeability and microstructural damage suggests that they may represent partially dissociable pathological processes. These results indicate that APOE ε4 may promote WMH pathology in part through vascular dysfunction, warranting deeper examination of the mechanisms by which vascular disruption is genetically regulated.

Our finding of increased vascular permeability within WMHs of APOE ε4 carriers extends the existing literature, which has consistently reported elevated BBB permeability in cortical gray matter of APOE ε4 carriers, 27 , 38 , 39 , 40 but which has not been observed in white matter. 27 , 39 , 41 The previous lack of salient findings in white matter may be explained by methodological challenges, such as the pathological heterogeneity of WMHs. 3 , 5 , 10 and high inter‐individual variability of permeability metrics. 39 , 42 Several mechanistic pathways provide a biological basis for our finding suggesting that APOE ε4 carriers may be vulnerable to BBB dysfunction within WMHs. APOE ε4 has been shown to activate the cyclophilin A‐matrix metalloproteinase‐9 cascade in pericytes, 27 leading to degradation of tight junction proteins and basement membrane components 43 and thereby compromising BBB integrity. While these processes have been characterized predominantly in cortical regions, they provide a potential framework within which to interpret our findings in WMH. BBB disruption allows plasma proteins, complement factors, and immunoglobulins to leak into the white matter parenchyma 4 triggering chronic edema, demyelination, and microglial phagocytic activation, 8 which together may accelerate lesion progression. Given these molecular and cellular mechanisms, our observation of elevated WMH BBB permeability in cognitively normal older adults at increased genetic risk for AD suggests that APOE ε4‐related vascular dysfunction may be detectable in vivo at an early stage before the onset of clinical symptoms. Recent neuroimaging studies further suggest that APOE ε4‐associated BBB dysfunction may contribute to downstream AD‐related pathology, including impaired brain waste clearance and increased amyloid burden, 44 reinforcing the potential clinical relevance of early BBB dysfunction in APOE ε4 carriers. While our study does not directly assess the underlying molecular or histopathological substrates, our imaging findings are consistent with previous histological findings linking APOE ε4 and BBB disruption, 40 , 45 , 46 , 47 and support the possibility that such neurovascular alterations within WMHs represent a very early event in the neurodegenerative process. Thus, our results support the hypothesis that in healthy older adults, APOE ε4 is associated with BBB disruption not only in cortical gray matter, but also in WMHs.

Reduced RD and elevated IF within WMHs were observed regardless of APOE status. While microstructural alterations in WMHs have been reported using diffusion tensor imaging, and RSI abnormalities have been described in multiple sclerosis white matter lesions, 48 we observed abnormal microstructure in WMHs relative to NAWM among older adults free of diagnosed neurological disease. Consistent with reports in multiple sclerosis lesions, 48 we observed decreased restricted anisotropic diffusion and increased free water, which may reflect degenerative pathological processes underlying WMHs, such as reduced axon density, demyelination, or cell shrinkage. 31

Mirroring the genotype‐dependent pattern observed for BBB permeability, reduced RI within WMHs relative to NAWM was also observed only among APOE ε4 carriers. While RD and IF abnormalities appear to represent features common to WMHs regardless of APOE ε4 status, RI and BBB disruption may capture pathological characteristics that are more closely associated with APOE ε4‐related WMH pathology. Thus, these findings support the concept that APOE ε4 regulates distinct pathological processes within WMHs, contributing to their biological heterogeneity, with K trans and microstructure providing complementary information regarding WMH composition.

The observed imaging patterns were not explained by demographic characteristics or common vascular risk factors. The higher vascular permeability and lower RI in WMHs among APOE ε4 carriers and the alterations in RD and IF in WMHs were all independent of age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and smoking history. Although the smaller subset with available amyloid data had limited statistical power, effect sizes after adjustment for amyloid positivity in these subsamples were generally preserved and no substantial change in the overall pattern occurred. This suggests that the observed imaging features in WMHs compared to NAWM carriers are unlikely to represent a downstream consequence of the vascular risk factors examined here, or amyloid burden.

No significant correlation was detected between K trans and any RSI metric within WMH, in either APOE ε4 carriers or non‐carriers, suggesting that within the pathological environment of WMHs, these two processes may reflect distinct but potentially interacting pathological mechanisms or operate on different timelines. As RSI metrics represent established tissue damage resulting from cumulative pathological processes in WMH over time, whereas K trans captures a snapshot of current BBB leakage, the extent of microstructural damage may not reliably be related to current BBB permeability. Although no direct linear relationship was observed, the coexistence of APOE ε4‐associated BBB abnormalities and microstructural alterations within WMHs raises the possibility that vascular and tissue‐related pathological processes may interact over longer timescales or spatially extended distributions.

This study has several limitations. First, because of its cross‐sectional design, this study can neither assess how BBB permeability and microstructure fluctuate over time, nor their associations with future cognitive decline or WMH progression. Longitudinal follow‐up studies are essential to clarify any causal relationships and long‐term outcomes. Second, the relatively modest sample size may have limited power to detect interactions or to assess the influence of amyloid status, given the smaller number of participants with amyloid biomarkers, although our intra‐individual design partially offset this limitation. Therefore, results should be viewed as hypothesis generating. Validation in larger, longitudinal cohorts incorporating spatially resolved analyses across WMHs and NAWM will be a critical next step to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, this multi‐modal imaging study revealed that WMHs consist of both APOE ε4‐related and APOE ε4‐independent pathological components. These findings provide novel evidence that APOE ε4 influences specific pathological processes contributing to WMHs, and highlight the importance of considering WMH heterogeneity when investigating mechanisms linking cerebrovascular dysfunction and AD risk. An APOE ε4‐specific phenotype of WMHs holds significant clinical potential, as it could serve as a more sensitive biomarker for APOE ε4‐related pathology. Furthermore, the possibility that APOE ε4 contributes to cognitive risk via BBB disruption has important personalized medicine implications, and suggests that targeting cerebrovascular stabilization could be a novel therapeutic strategy to prevent or slow cognitive decline. Such a biomarker could be used to stratify high‐risk individuals for clinical trials, identifying those most likely to benefit from BBB‐restoring therapies, monitoring therapeutic responses, and complementing current amyloid‐lowering therapeutic agents.
