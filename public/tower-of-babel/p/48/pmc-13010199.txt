Relationship Between Heart Rate Variability and Sleep Among Girls and Boys With Abdominal Pain‐Related Disorders of Gut‐Brain Interaction
Authors: Kendra J Kamp, Robert L Burr, Camden E Matherne, Emily Simonds, Tasha Murphy, Margaret M Heitkemper, Rona L Levy, Robert J Shulman, Miranda A L van Tilburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChildren with abdominal pain‐related disorders of gut‐brain interaction (AP‐DGBI) often experience sleep disturbances which may be linked to the autonomic nervous system. Heart rate variability (HRV) is a non‐invasive measure of autonomic nervous system activity. Our aim was to examine if HRV was associated with characteristics of disturbed sleep in children with AP‐DGBI in order to elucidate the underlying physiological mechanisms of disturbed sleep.MethodsThis analysis used baseline data from a randomized control trial of 156 children ages 7–12 years with AP‐DGBI. HRV was measured after dinner using a Polar monitor. Sleep disturbances were evaluated using the Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire. Associations between HRV indices and sleep characteristics were determined using partial correlations controlling for age and stratified by sex.Key ResultsAmong girls, higher parasympathetic activity (Ln RMSSD and Ln pNN50) was associated with longer sleep onset delay while lower heart rate was correlated with increased sleep anxiety. Among boys, lower autonomic balance (Ln LF/HF ratio) was associated with increased daytime sleepiness. In both sexes, lower low frequency HRV was associated with greater sleep‐disordered breathing. Post hoc analysis of multiple indices revealed that children with sleep‐disordered breathing had significantly lower HRV vs. those without disordered breathing.Conclusions & InferencesThe autonomic nervous system may be a potential mechanism which impacts sleep among children with AP‐DGBI. Future longitudinal studies are needed to confirm this relationship.Keywords: abdominal pain‐related disorders of gut‐brain interaction, autonomic nervous system, children, heart rate variability, sleep disturbances, sleep‐disordered breathing

Introduction

Abdominal Pain‐Related Disorders of Gut Brain Interaction (AP‐DGBI, formerly referred to as functional abdominal pain disorders) are common, affecting up to 20% of children worldwide [1]. Disturbed sleep is more common in children with AP‐DGBI compared to healthy controls [2, 3]. Across chronic pain conditions, sleep disturbances in children and adolescents are associated with increased pain sensitivity, greater disability, and poorer quality of life [4, 5, 6]. Physiological hyperarousal of the autonomic nervous system (ANS) may contribute to both disturbed sleep as well as pain [7]. Thus, examining sleep in relationship to the ANS among children with AP‐DGBI is an important, yet often overlooked, aspect of care.

Autonomic nervous system (ANS) function can be measured by heart rate variability (HRV) which is the beat‐to‐beat alteration in heart rate. Research on HRV among children with AP‐DGBI is limited. Two studies found no differences between children with AP‐DGBI and healthy controls on various HRV indices at baseline [8, 9]. However, the HRV response to stress may be different in children with AP‐DGBI. Puzanovova and colleagues found that children with chronic abdominal pain who experienced success on a task (a stressor) showed increases in both sympathetic and parasympathetic HRV components (total power, low frequency, and high frequency) [8]. Using 24‐ambulatory monitoring, our team found no difference in vagal activity (24 h, day [1 PM‐5 PM], or night [2 AM‐6 AM]) comparing children (ages 7 to 10) with AP‐DGBI and healthy children [9]. We did find that vagal activity indices and psychological distress were negatively correlated among girls with AP‐DGBI but not boys, [9] suggesting potential sex differences in the relation between HRV and AP‐DGBI in children. However, to our knowledge, studies have not examined HRV and sleep within a pedatric population with AP‐DGBI. Studies in adult women with irritable bowel syndrome, a common AP‐DGBI, provide evidence of the link between HRV and sleep characteristics as measured by polysomnography and concurrent heart rate monitoring at night in a sleep laboratory [9, 10, 11]. These relationships were reported to be influenced by psychological distress levels and bowel pattern characteristics [9, 10, 11].

In sum, additional studies exploring the relationship of sleep and HRV in pediatric AP‐DGBI populations are needed. Therefore, the aim of this exploratory study was to examine if HRV measures were associated with characteristics of disturbed sleep in children with AP‐DGBI. We examined girls and boys separately to examine potential sex differences.

Materials & Methods

Study Design

This study is a secondary data analysis of baseline data from a randomized controlled trial (Clinicaltrials.gov NCT03823742) in which the primary caregiver (parents, stepparents, or grandparents who had the primary caregiver role) of children with AP‐DGBI received either Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, focused on how to best respond to the child's pain, or dietary therapy, focused on implementing a low fermentable oligosaccharides disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols (FODMAPs) diet (LFD) in their child [12]. The current study used data collected at baseline before randomization into either treatment group. The study was approved by the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board. The parent or guardian provided consent and the child assent.

Participants

Children with a diagnosis of an AP‐DGBI and their primary caregiver were identified through the Texas Children's Hospital medical records and invited by letter to be part of the study. Interested families were screened by phone using a modified pediatric Rome III questionnaire [13]. If the children passed screening, the child and primary caregiver filled out the Rome IV Diagnostic Questionnaire for Pediatric Functional GI Disorders to insure they had an AP‐DGBI. Additional inclusion criteria included: ages 7–12 years and able to understand and speak English. Exclusion criteria were: (1) pain < 1 time a week, (2) organic reason for the pain, (3) past bowel surgery, (4) other documented GI disorders (e.g., Crohn's disease), chronic conditions with GI symptoms (e.g., cystic fibrosis), or serious chronic medical condition (e.g., diabetes), (5) child weight and/or height > or < 2 SD for age, (6) currently or previously having received CBT or LFD treatment, (7) unable to be randomized into the LFD due to food allergies/sensitivities, (8) autism spectrum disorder or other significant developmental delay, and/or (9) psychosis or bipolar disorder.

Methods

Families received written, oral, and video instructions on how to carry out the HRV measurement at home. HRV was evaluated using a Polar V800 heart rate monitor (Polar Electro Oy, Finland) and the Polar H10 cardiac transducer to collect the times between successive heart beats, as measured by the time in milliseconds between R waves. As 24 h HRV recordings are challenging to obtain in children, we used a short‐term collection of resting HRV. Short‐term collection has been used previously in children with obstructive sleep apnea [14]. In our study, children wore a HRV monitor for 20 min while in a supine position. Data collection occurred in the evening, after dinner and before bedtime. Data from the Polar heart monitors were downloaded and processed using Kubios HRV premium analysis software (version 3.5.0) [15, 16]. Data were analyzed separately in 5 min blocks and the blocks are averaged. Both time domain (time periods between successive heartbeats) and frequency domain (power represented in frequency bands) were obtained. A table of HRV measures included and their definitions is presented in Table 1 [17].

Questionnaires

Baseline surveys were completed through REDCap [18]. Primary caregivers completed demographic questionnaires about themselves and their children, including age, race, ethnicity, and sex.

Sleep problems were measured with the Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ) [19]. The CSHQ was completed by the primary caregiver. The primary caregiver scored the frequency of the child's sleep problems in a typical week on 35 items using a 3‐point scale ranging from “usually” (sleep problem is occurring 5–7 times a week), “sometimes” (2‐4 times a week) to “rarely” (0–1 times a week). An example item is: “Afraid of sleeping in the dark.” Items are summed for a total sleep problem score. In addition, 9 subscales are calculated: Bedtime Resistance (6 items), Sleep Onset Delay (1 item), Sleep Duration (3 items), Sleep Anxiety (4 items), Night Wakings (3 items), Parasomnias (7 items), Sleep Disordered Breathing (3 items), and Daytime Sleepiness (8 items). The CSHQ distinguishes between clinical and control groups with a sensitivity of 0.80 and a specificity of 0.72 [19].

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). To be included, participants needed complete data for HRV and CSHQ sleep variables. All HRV metrics, except for heart rate, were transformed on the natural log scale. Descriptive statistics were computed for child and primary caregiver demographics as well as HRV indices and sleep variables. Partial correlations were used to examine the relationship between HRV and sleep controlling for child age. As this study was an exploratory analysis, we used a p < 0.05 significance level and did not adjust for multiple testing. We conducted post hoc analysis to further explore the relationship between disordered breathing and HRV indices. We dichotomized the CSHQ disordered breathing subscale into those with a score greater than 4 (problems with disordered breathing) and a score of 3 or 4 (no or minimal problems) and conducted a t‐test. Data are provided as mean (SD).

Sleep‐Disordered Breathing

As sleep disordered breathing was significantly associated with LF in both the girls and boys, we conducted a post hoc analysis to explore the disordered breathing subscale which includes items of “snores loudly”, “stops breathing”, and “snorts and gasps” with HRV. Twelve percent of the sample (n = 18; n = 10 girls and n = 8 boys) had primary caregiver‐reported problems with disordered breathing (defined as a score greater than 4). Those with scores greater than 4 had significantly lower SD5min, total power, LF, and HF than those with a score of 3 or 4 (Table S1).

Discussion

Altered ANS functioning appears to be associated with disturbed sleep among girls and boys with AP‐DGBI. Both girls and boys showed relationships between ANS variables and caregiver‐reported sleep‐disordered breathing. Among girls but not boys, lower heart rate was associated with increased sleep anxiety. Higher Ln RMSSD and Ln pNN50, both measures reflecting parasympathetic activity, were associated with longer sleep onset delay. Our findings point to a potential relationship between ANS functioning and sleep in children with AP‐DGBI, although larger longitudinal studies are needed.

Consensus guidelines recommend that children ages 6 to 12 years should sleep between 9 to 12 h to promote optimal health, including improved attention, emotional regulation, memory, and mental and physical health [20]. Children in our sample had greater primary caregiver‐reported sleep disturbances across all sleep subscales than published norms of healthy school‐aged children [2, 19]. Among children ages 11–18 years, those with AP‐DGBI had poorer sleep quality, greater insomnia, and greater sleepiness compared to age‐ and gender‐matched controls as reported by parents [21]. Poor sleep and insomnia were associated with worse abdominal pain [21]. These results are consistent with those from a prior study done by our team [2]. Thus, sleep may serve as a modifiable factor which can impact abdominal pain. Indeed, a recent systematic review recommends routine screening of sleep disorders among children with AP‐DGBI [22]. As sleep disorders are associated with poorer academic achievement, cognitive abilities (such as attention and memory), mood, and behavioral problems, facilitating better sleep is an important target.

Among girls, but not boys, we found lower heart rate was associated with increased sleep anxiety. Ln RMSSD and Ln pNN50, typically markers of parasympathetic activity, were positively associated with sleep onset delay. Typically, decreased HRV has been associated with increased internalizing symptoms, particularly among girls [23]. Our findings differ from the previous pediatric sleep literature in which lower parasympathetic activity was associated with poorer sleep quality including longer sleep onset delay, based on actigraphy recordings [24]. This difference could be due to our measurement of heart rate and HRV during the pre‐bedtime period instead of nighttime. This opens the door for future research to examine longitudinal relationships between HRV and sleep, with a more in‐depth consideration for how sleep anxiety and sleep onset delay impact health outcomes in girls with AP‐DGBI. Ln LF, a measure reflecting a mix of sympathetic and parasympathetic activity, was negatively correlated with disordered breathing in both girls and boys. Post hoc analysis indicated that those with disordered breathing had significantly lower HRV (including SD5min, total power, LF, and HF). The primary caregiver‐reported sleep‐disordered breathing questionnaire includes problems with snoring and gasping. HRV disturbances have been described in adults and children with sleep‐disordered breathing [25]. Previous research also indicates that adult patients with irritable bowel syndrome have greater airway responsiveness to inhaled methacholine compared to healthy controls [26] and that asthma and abdominal pain disorders may be linked [27, 28]. Additional research is needed to examine the relationship between sleep‐disordered breathing and HRV in children.

This study addressed an overlooked area of research–sleep disturbances and HRV among children with AP‐DGBI–and sought to advance the literature by examining the relationship between HRV and sleep. To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date on HRV in children with AP‐DGBI. Strengths of the study include the large sample size consisting of a diverse racial and ethnic group. In addition, the children were well screened to ensure they met the AP‐DGBI diagnosis.

Several study limitations should be considered. The measures were obtained at home and the child's position during HRV data collection was not verified. However, written, oral, and video instructions were provided in advance and the research coordinator contacted the family on the day HRV was measured. The lack of a control group prevents us from examining if the relationship between sleep and HRV is different in children with and without an AP‐DGBI. However, that was not an aim of the current study. Our cross‐sectional study does not enable us to examine causality. It is possible that both pain and sleep are driven by changes in ANS activity. Future longitudinal studies are needed to examine the temporality of the relationship. As this was an exploratory analysis, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons.

In conclusion, this study suggests that ANS disruption may be a potential mechanism impacting sleep in children with AP‐DGBI. Our results provide impetus for a future study examining sleep and HRV incorporating longitudinal analyses. Given the well‐established relationship between pain and sleep in children with AP‐DGBI, sleep interventions may serve as an important treatment pathway.
