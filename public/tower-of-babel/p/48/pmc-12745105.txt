Reply: Response to concerns regarding single-anesthesia robotic bronchoscopy for lung nodules
Authors: Harmik J Soukiasian, Claire Perez, Raffaele Rocco, Philicia Moonsamy, Andrew R Brownlee
Published: 2025

Reply to the Editor:

We appreciate the thoughtful comments regarding our manuscript on single-anesthetic bronchoscopic biopsy to resection (SABBR).1 We welcome the opportunity to address the points raised and to clarify the context of our findings.

It is correct that our cohort represents a subset of carefully selected patients—approximately 40% of our lung resection cohort was diagnosed with ION. This reflects both patient preference and careful multidisciplinary selection, rather than a systematic exclusion of technically challenging nodules. Although lesion size in our study averaged 2.2 cm, this distribution reflects the clinical population referred for SABBR rather than an intentional bias. Importantly, we demonstrated high agreement (Cohen κ = 0.94) between frozen and final pathology even in subcentimeter and ground-glass dominant lesions, suggesting the workflow remains robust across morphologies when applied in an appropriate clinical environment.2

The VERITAS trial compared transthoracic needle biopsy (TTNB) and robotic navigational bronchoscopy and showed no difference in the diagnostic yield of small lung nodules.3 Furthermore, the choice of diagnostic modality must be balanced against its greater pneumothorax and chest tube rates of TTNB, which are reported in 15% to 25% and up to 6% of cases, respectively.4 Robotic navigational bronchoscopy, in contrast, consistently demonstrates a more favorable safety profile with a pneumothorax rate of 1% to 2%, while also allowing for the routine biopsy of multiple pulmonary nodules and the mediastinum in a single procedure.

In a small subset of patients in this series, frozen section revealed atypical adenomatous hyperplasia or atypia—a diagnosis that, although technically not invasive, carried sufficient concern in the multidisciplinary tumor board to justify resection. There is always a concern that these findings may represent partial sampling of an invasive malignancy. We acknowledge that management of pure ground-glass nodules remains nuanced; however, the ability to obtain intraoperative diagnosis while preserving the option to abort resection, in our view, represents a strength of SABBR, not a limitation.

We agree that outcomes from high-volume centers with dedicated thoracic pathology support may not be immediately reproducible everywhere. However, robotic bronchoscopy adoption is accelerating nationally (projected to overtake TTNB as the predominant modality in 2025), and structured training pathways, standardized cytopathology support, and collaborative trials (eg, VERITAS) are establishing frameworks to extend this workflow beyond specialized centers.

We have disclosed all financial relationships per journal guidelines, and we agree that independent, multicenter validation is essential. For this reason, we support ongoing trials and encourage collaboration across institutions to rigorously evaluate SABBR not only for yield but also for cost-effectiveness, quality of life, and oncologic outcomes.

We maintain that SABBR provides a safe, reliable, and efficient diagnostic–therapeutic pathway that reduces time-to-treatment and may prevent loss to follow-up in patients with suspected lung cancer. Although not intended to supplant existing diagnostic algorithms at this stage, SABBR represents a promising strategy to align biopsy and resection in a single patient-centered encounter.
