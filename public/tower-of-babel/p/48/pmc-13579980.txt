Traumatic brachial artery reconstruction using an in-wound no-touch superficial vein graft with 20-month imaging follow-up: a case report
Authors: Heng Tian, Yang Liu, Rui Li
Published: 2026

Background

Segmental arterial loss after limb trauma often precludes tension-free primary repair and necessitates interposition grafting. In contaminated or complex extremity wounds, autologous vein grafts are generally preferred because prosthetic conduits may be more vulnerable to infection and may show inferior long-term behavior in hostile wound beds [1–3]. Extremity vascular reconstruction literature, although limited and heterogeneous, repeatedly supports the practical value of autologous venous conduits in both traumatic and oncologic limb salvage settings [1, 2].

Conventionally harvested vein grafts are often stripped of surrounding tissue and manually distended, which may damage the intima, media, adventitia, and vasa vasorum and may contribute to spasm, wall injury, intimal hyperplasia, progressive dilatation, and late graft failure [4–8]. In coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), the no-touch technique was introduced to preserve a pedicle of surrounding tissue and avoid uncontrolled manual distension [4]. Morphologic and histologic studies have shown that no-touch harvest better preserves endothelial integrity, adventitial structure, and vasa vasorum than conventional preparation [4, 5, 9, 10], and randomized coronary studies have reported improved long-term patency of no-touch saphenous vein grafts compared with conventional vein grafts [11–14].

However, those data arise from coronary surgery and should not be directly extrapolated to traumatic limb reconstruction. In upper-extremity trauma, the role of no-touch harvest remains uncertain, and evidence is limited to technical rationale rather than proven clinical advantage. In addition, postoperative antithrombotic practice and imaging surveillance are not standardized for traumatic brachial artery interposition grafting [15–18]. We therefore report a patient with traumatic brachial artery loss who underwent reconstruction using a diameter-matched superficial vein found within the wound and harvested with a no-touch technique, with objective duplex ultrasonography and CTA follow-up at 20 months.

Case presentation

A 63-year-old man sustained an open lacerating injury to the right elbow caused by a fan blade. He had no known chronic medical illness, no history of infectious disease, and no relevant family history. He was an occasional smoker and drinker. Emergency exploration demonstrated injury to the superficial elbow veins, the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, and part of the superficial brachioradialis muscle. The brachial artery was transected, and both arterial ends showed contusion and thrombosis. Only a small vascular branch maintained tenuous continuity across an intervening damaged segment (Fig. 1A).

Intraoperative photographs showing (A) traumatic brachial artery segmental loss, (B) harvest of the in-wound median cubital vein with surrounding tissue preserved, (C) the harvested conduit with short fascial cuffs at both ends, and (D) completion of reversed interposition graft reconstruction

After debridement under the operating microscope, the gap between healthy arterial ends was approximately 5.5 cm. A median cubital vein within the wound was considered for grafting because it was relatively large and closely matched the brachial artery in diameter. Importantly, the selected venous segment was outside the most severely crushed part of the wound, remained grossly continuous over a usable length, and showed no obvious mural contusion, segmental collapse, or visible intraluminal thrombosis after trimming of the injured ends. The wall appeared elastic and intact on gentle handling, and the vein could be harvested without extending dissection into a visibly devitalized zone. Because this conduit was already available in the operative field and met these practical criteria, we used it to avoid a second donor-site incision [19].

The vein was harvested under loupe magnification with a cuff of surrounding tissue, following a no-touch concept (Fig. 1B). Approximately 3 mm of perivascular tissue was preserved circumferentially, and short cuff-like fascial tissue of approximately 5 mm was maintained at both ends (Fig. 1C). Small tributaries were ligated externally with 5 − 0 sutures placed outside the venous wall. The harvested vein was gently irrigated with heparinized saline from the distal end until clear effluent emerged from the proximal end. Topical papaverine was applied locally during conduit preparation to reduce vasospasm, but the conduit was not mechanically or pressure-distended.

The vein graft was reversed to negate the effect of venous valves. Graft length and tension were assessed with the elbow in a neutral, slightly flexed position across the mobile segment. Under the operating microscope, the graft ends were trimmed and end-to-end anastomoses between the reversed vein graft and the distal and proximal brachial artery stumps were completed using 8 − 0 and 9 − 0 polypropylene sutures. After release of the vascular clamps, the graft filled promptly and demonstrated good pulsation, a positive milking test, and no leakage, kinking, torsion, or focal narrowing (Fig. 1D). The preserved fascial cuffs at both graft ends were then sutured to the peri-adventitial tissue of the recipient artery ends to provide local stabilization and protection at the anastomotic sites. The wound was subsequently closed in layers.

The patient received a single intraoperative intravenous dose of heparin sodium (6250 U), followed by low-molecular-weight heparin (4100 IU once daily) during hospitalization. Because this was an open traumatic wound with recent microsurgical repair, no standardized evidence-based protocol was available for long-term antithrombotic treatment in this exact setting. The decision not to prescribe oral antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy at discharge was individualized on the basis of satisfactory limb perfusion, early graft patency on postoperative assessment, wound status, and concern about unnecessary bleeding in the absence of limb-specific supporting evidence. This favorable course should not be interpreted as evidence that long-term antithrombotic therapy is unnecessary in similar patients. At just over 1 month, local ultrasonography confirmed graft patency, although standardized diameter measurements suitable for interval comparison were not recorded.

At 20-month follow-up, duplex ultrasonography and CTA demonstrated sustained patency of the reconstructed arterial segment. Duplex ultrasonography showed that the bilateral axillary, brachial, radial, and ulnar arteries had no intima-media thickening or plaque formation, and color Doppler demonstrated patent luminal flow with generally normal spectral findings (Fig. 2). On the reconstructed right side, the distal brachial artery measured 4.0 mm with a flow velocity of 96 cm/s, the proximal anastomotic region measured 4.2 mm with a velocity of 86 cm/s, and the distal brachial bifurcation region measured 4.0 mm with a velocity of 31 cm/s. Distal runoff was preserved, with a distal radial artery diameter of 2.0 mm and velocity of 54 cm/s, and a distal ulnar artery diameter of 1.5 mm and velocity of 48 cm/s. On the contralateral side, the distal brachial artery measured 3.3 mm with a velocity of 77 cm/s, the distal radial artery 1.7 mm with a velocity of 47 cm/s, and the distal ulnar artery 1.9 mm with a velocity of 54 cm/s. These findings indicated continued graft patency with mild ectatic remodeling of the reconstructed brachial segment compared with the contralateral side, without evidence of hemodynamically significant stenosis. CTA with three-dimensional reconstruction confirmed uninterrupted opacification of the reconstructed brachial artery, preserved distal runoff, and no occlusion or pseudoaneurysm on the available images (Fig. 3).

Clinically, the limb remained viable without recurrent ischemic symptoms or graft-related hemorrhagic complications during follow-up. The accompanying lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury was identified intraoperatively. However, this report focuses on vascular reconstruction, and detailed data on neurologic repair and long-term sensory outcomes were not available for structured reporting.

Discussion and conclusions

This case shows a practical option for reconstruction of traumatic brachial artery segmental loss when primary end-to-end repair is not possible. In this patient, a superficial vein of appropriate caliber was already available within the wound and could be used as an interposition conduit. This avoided an additional donor-site incision and simplified the reconstructive plan. In traumatic limb surgery, that may be advantageous because separate vein harvest can increase operative trauma, prolong the procedure, and consume future conduit options [21]. At the same time, use of a local venous conduit should be selective. In our case, the chosen segment lay outside the most severely damaged zone and showed no gross contusion or thrombosis after preparation, which was central to the decision to use it.

A second noteworthy aspect is the application of a no-touch harvest concept in traumatic upper-extremity arterial reconstruction. Existing evidence supporting no-touch harvesting comes predominantly from coronary surgery, where randomized studies have shown better graft preservation and long-term patency than with conventional preparation [11–14]. However, those data are only indirectly relevant here. The present case should therefore be interpreted as documenting technical feasibility in a limb-trauma setting, not as evidence that no-touch harvesting improves peripheral outcomes.

The imaging follow-up is a strength of this report. At 20 months, both duplex ultrasonography and CTA showed continued graft patency, preserved distal runoff, and no pseudoaneurysm or occlusion. The reconstructed segment was mildly larger than the contralateral brachial artery, but there was no focal high-velocity signal or other imaging feature suggesting hemodynamically significant stenosis. Because standardized graft-diameter measurements were not available from the early postoperative ultrasound study, we cannot determine whether the mild ectatic change progressed over time. It is therefore reported here as an observational finding in one functioning graft, with uncertain long-term significance.

This case also highlights an unresolved postoperative issue, namely antithrombotic management. Current peripheral revascularization guidelines support antiplatelet therapy after bypass procedures, and vitamin K antagonists may be considered in selected autologous vein bypass settings [15–17]. However, these recommendations are derived mainly from lower-extremity atherosclerotic disease rather than traumatic brachial artery repair. Published experience with traumatic brachial artery repair is largely composed of retrospective case series, which further limits procedure-specific recommendations [22]. In the present patient, long-term graft patency was achieved after perioperative anticoagulation without prolonged oral antiplatelet or anticoagulant treatment, but this favorable course should not be interpreted to mean that long-term therapy is unnecessary in similar cases, and trauma-specific studies are needed.

From a technical standpoint, this report also supports a pragmatic combination of loupe-assisted conduit harvest and microscope-assisted arterial anastomosis. Loupe magnification was sufficient for careful dissection of the in-wound vein with preservation of surrounding tissue, whereas the definitive anastomoses were completed under the operating microscope. Attention to graft length with the elbow in a functional position was also important because the reconstruction crossed a mobile segment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This is a single case without a comparator, and the favorable outcome may reflect patient selection, local injury characteristics, conduit quality, or technical execution rather than the harvest technique itself. In addition, a superficial vein within the wound will not always be available; indeed, in many traumatic cases the local vein is too damaged or too close to the zone of trauma to be used safely, so the suitability of the median cubital vein in this patient was somewhat fortunate. The postoperative antithrombotic regimen was individualized, which further limits generalizability. Exact ischemia-time data were not recorded in a form suitable for retrospective reporting. Preservation of surrounding tissue was intentionally limited to a thin cuff rather than a bulky pedicle in order to reduce unnecessary soft-tissue and possible lymphatic disruption within the antecubital region, but this issue deserves attention whenever a pedicled venous harvest is contemplated. Finally, although 20-month surveillance is a strength of this report, longer follow-up is still needed to exclude late stenosis or aneurysmal degeneration [23].

In conclusion, reconstruction of traumatic brachial artery loss with a reversed superficial vein graft harvested from within the wound using a no-touch technique was technically feasible in this patient and remained patent on duplex ultrasonography and CTA at 20 months. When a local vein is available, of appropriate caliber, and outside the most severely injured zone, this strategy may help avoid additional donor-site morbidity. Nevertheless, this report should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Further well-documented limb cases with standardized imaging follow-up are needed before broader clinical recommendations can be made.
