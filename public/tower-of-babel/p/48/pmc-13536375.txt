Comparative in vivo study of 3D-printed composite bioceramic scaffolds fabricated by distinct pre-processing methods
Authors: Yu-Ting Li, Ju-Hsuan Yang, Yu-Hsin Chang, Hao-Hueng Chang, Kuo-Yang Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeThis study aimed to evaluate the in vivo osteoinductive potential and biocompatibility of three-dimensional (3D) printed bone substitutes composed of polylactic acid (PLA) and PLA-based bioceramic composites of hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP), fabricated using distinct pre-processing methods.Materials and methodsPure PLA, thermally blended PLA/HA/β-TCP, and tetrahydrofuran (THF) solvent-cast PLA/HA/β-TCP (7:3w/w) were first processed into 1.75 mm filaments and subsequently fabricated into scaffolds using fused deposition modeling (FDM). These scaffolds were implanted subcutaneously in 8-week-old Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses were performed at 2 and 4 weeks, as per ISO 10993-6, to evaluate inflammation, fibrous capsule formation, and osteogenic potential.ResultsSemi-quantitative scoring revealed that T-MIX (tetrahydrofuran solvent-cast composite) had the lowest inflammatory scores at both 2 and 4 weeks, which were significantly lower than those of H-MIX (heat-blended composite) (P < 0.05). At 2 weeks, BSP (bone sialoprotein) expression in both H-MIX and T-MIX was significantly higher than in PLA (P < 0.05 and P < 0.001, respectively), with T-MIX also exceeding H-MIX (P < 0.05). Overall, T-MIX demonstrated superior performance in reducing inflammation and promoting osteogenesis.ConclusionThe solvent-cast T-MIX scaffold demonstrated significantly reduced inflammatory responses and enhanced osteogenic marker expression compared to both pure PLA and heat-blended composites, positioning it as a promising and standardized candidate for 3D-printed, patient-specific bone grafts in dental and craniofacial applications.Keywords: 3D printing, Bone regeneration, Composite bioceramic filaments, Polylactic acid, Tetrahydrofuran, Animal experiment

Introduction

Alveolar bone augmentation is a common surgical procedure in clinical dentistry, and its outcome is largely influenced by the properties of the implanted material and the patient’s biological adaptability.1–3

Current graft substitutes frequently present limitations: particulate bone powders exhibit insufficient compressive strength and tend to collapse. In contrast, block-type substitutes may provide adequate mechanical support but lack appropriate resorption and remodeling capacity. Therefore, an ideal bone substitute should maintain a stable space for bone regeneration, allow convenient shaping, resist compressive forces, and demonstrate favorable biocompatibility—features considered essential for successful alveolar bone reconstruction.4

In recent years, three-dimensional (3D) printing has emerged as a promising approach for treating bone defects. Various techniques, including fused deposition modeling (FDM), selective laser sintering, and inkjet-based 3D printing, have been integrated with different biomedical materials to fabricate scaffolds and substitutes for clinical applications.5,6 Among these, FDM has become the most widely adopted due to its accessibility and versatility. In studies on alveolar bone defect repair, polymeric materials combined with bone substitutes, such as hydroxyapatite (HA) and tricalcium phosphate (TCP), are commonly used to produce porous HA/TCP composite scaffolds.7 Polylactic acid (PLA) is a biodegradable polymer derived from corn starch, characterized by a controllable degradation rate. Using established processing techniques, PLA has been successfully applied in medical devices, including biodegradable sutures and tissue-engineering scaffolds. Importantly, it has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration as a safe and biocompatible material for human implantation.8

Despite recent advances, the fabrication of 3D printing filaments still lacks a standardized protocol applicable to various composite formulations. In particular, the pre-processing procedures required to produce uniform and printable composite filaments have not been systematically investigated or optimized, even though they play a critical role in ceramic dispersion, material homogeneity, and the resulting biological performance. Building on our previous study,9 which compared direct heating and tetrahydrofuran (THF) solvent-casting methods as pre-processing techniques for filament fabrication, the present work extends these findings to an in vivo evaluation. In that study, THF-processed composites (T-MIX) exhibited improved physical integrity and compressive strength exceeding the ISO 5833 threshold.

Moreover, T-MIX showed significantly higher alkaline phosphatase activity and calcium deposition than other groups, indicating enhanced early and late osteogenic differentiation. These results suggest that the THF-assisted solvent-casting method promotes more homogeneous ceramic–polymer integration and improved cellular responses compared with the direct-heating approach. Accordingly, this in vivo investigation was designed to validate whether these processing-related advantages translate into superior biocompatibility and host tissue responses. Specifically, composite materials were prepared using direct heating and solvent casting, extruded into 1.75 mm filaments, and printed into scaffolds via FDM. The scaffolds were implanted into the dorsal subcutaneous tissue of Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats to evaluate biocompatibility and host tissue reactions. This study aims to provide further evidence supporting the feasibility of standardized filament pre-processing strategies for future applications in alveolar bone regeneration.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of composite filaments and 3d-printed scaffolds

PLA, HA, and β-TCP powders (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were used as base materials. Three experimental groups were established: pure PLA, a heat-blended PLA/HA/β-TCP composite (H-MIX), and a solvent-cast PLA/HA/β-TCP composite (T-MIX) prepared using tetrahydrofuran (THF, anhydrous, lot AL-401757-1; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The polymer-to-ceramic weight ratio (PLA: HA+β-TCP) was maintained at 7:3. Within the ceramic phase, hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) were mixed at a 3:7 weight ratio, corresponding to overall mass fractions of 70 wt% PLA, 9 wt% HA, and 21 wt% β-TCP. Fabrication of H-MIX and T-MIX was performed according to our previously published protocol.9

Composite filaments (ø 1.75 mm) were extruded (Filabot, Barre, VT, USA) and printed into porous cube-shaped scaffolds (2 × 2 × 1 mm3) with an orthogonal, fully interconnected pore architecture (internal pore size 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm3) using a FDM printer (QTS Technology Co., Zhengzhou, China). The designed pore geometry corresponds to an open-lattice structure that allows cell infiltration, nutrient diffusion, and vascular ingrowth in bone tissue engineering applications.

Animal experiments

A total of 18 male SD rats (8 weeks old) were used in this study. Animals were anesthetized with Zoletil 50 (1 mL/100 g; Virbac, Carros, France), and the dorsal hair was shaved. Under sterile conditions, ethylene oxide-sterilized scaffolds were implanted subcutaneously into the dorsal region of each rat. Animals were sacrificed at 2 and 4 weeks post-implantation using a carbon dioxide euthanasia chamber. Harvested tissue samples were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) and bone sialoprotein (BSP).

Inflammatory responses and osteoinductive activity were evaluated by semi-quantitative scoring of IHC signals and inflammatory cell infiltration. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the National Taiwan University Laboratory Animal Center (Protocol No. IACUC 20250042).

Histological evaluation and semi-quantitative scoring

H&E staining was performed using standard protocols to assess tissue morphology and inflammatory responses. Paraffin-embedded sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, stained with hematoxylin for 5 min and eosin for 1 min, then dehydrated and mounted. The stained slides were digitized using a PANNORAMIC Flash digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) at 400 × magnification.

Qualitative assessment focused on overall tissue architecture, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibrous encapsulation. Semi-quantitative scoring was performed at 400× high-power fields (HPFs) according to the ISO 10993-6 Annex E criteria. In each specimen, polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs), lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages, and multinucleated giant cells were scored on a 0–4 scale. Necrosis, neovascularization, fibrosis, and fatty infiltration were also graded on the same 0–4 scale.

The inflammatory subtotal comprised PMNs, lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages, giant cells, and necrosis, whereas the remodeling subtotal comprised neovascularization, fibrosis, and fatty infiltration. The average score per animal (8 HPFs per sample; n = 3 per group) was used to compare the PLA, H-MIX, and T-MIX groups at 2 and 4 weeks. In addition, the ratios of neovascularization to acute (PMN) and chronic (lymphocyte) inflammatory cells were calculated as indices of tissue repair efficiency. Statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro 8.5 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Masson’s trichrome staining

Masson’s trichrome staining was used to assess collagen fiber deposition and fibrosis. Paraffin-embedded sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through graded alcohols, and stained with: Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for nuclei (black), Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin for cytoplasm and muscle fibers (red), and aniline blue for collagen fibers (blue), with differentiation in phosphotungstic/phosphomolybdic acid. Sections were dehydrated, mounted, and scanned using a digital slide scanner at 400 × magnification.

Immunohistochemical staining and semi-quantitative analysis

IHC staining was performed to evaluate the expression of osteogenic markers, including RUNX2 and BSP, which represent early and late stages of osteogenic differentiation, respectively. Paraffin-embedded sections were processed using an automated staining system (BOND-MAX, Leica Biosystems GmbH, Nussloch, Germany) with a standard chromogenic detection protocol. Antigen retrieval was performed using appropriate pH-based buffers according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Primary antibodies included RUNX2 (ab236639, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and BSP (PA5-79424, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), both diluted 1:250. Hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining. Whole-slide digital scanning was conducted using the PANNORAMIC Flash scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd.) at 400× magnification.

Semi-quantitative evaluation of IHC staining was performed under 400 × HPFs. Eight representative fields per sample were assessed, and staining intensity was scored as follows: 0 = negative; 1 = weak; 2 = moderate; and 3 = strong. The average score per animal (n = 3 per group) was calculated for statistical comparison. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to determine statistical significance, with P-values ≤0.05 considered significant.10–12

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. After confirming normality and homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to determine statistical differences among groups. The same statistical procedures were applied to the semi-quantitative ISO 10993-6 tissue-reaction scores and to the immunohistochemical quantification of RUNX2 and BSP. All statistical analyses were carried out using OriginPro 8.5 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA) (see Fig. 1).

Results

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)

At 2 weeks, all groups exhibited inflammatory responses on H&E-stained sections (Fig. 2A). The PLA group showed dense cellular infiltration and a pronounced inflammatory reaction. In contrast, both H-MIX and T-MIX displayed more organized tissue alignment with evident neovascularization. By 4 weeks (Fig. 3A), fibrous tissue deposition increased in all groups, and the composite groups showed fewer inflammatory cells than PLA.

Masson’s trichrome staining

Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated keratin, muscle fibers, and cytoplasm in red, while collagen and bone matrix appeared blue, and nuclei were stained dark brown to black. At the 2-week time-point, all three groups exhibited active vascular growth in the lower region (red staining), with blue collagen fibers forming a circumferential encapsulation at the margins (Fig. 2B). By 4 weeks, the collagen fibers had become more finely organized, indicating progressive tissue repair and maturation (Fig. 3B).

Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2)

RUNX2-positive nuclei were detected in all groups at 2 weeks (Fig. 2C), with staining intensity following the order T-MIX > H-MIX > PLA. At 4 weeks (Fig. 3C), T-MIX exhibited the strongest and most extensive RUNX2 expression, suggesting that early osteogenic activity persisted longer in the composite groups.

Bone sialoprotein (BSP) immunohistochemical staining (Late osteogenic marker)

At 2 weeks (Fig. 2D), BSP-positive areas were observed in HMIX and T-MIX but were almost absent in PLA, indicating delayed mineralization in the control. At 4 weeks (Fig. 3D), BSP staining further intensified in T-MIX, surpassing both HMIX and PLA, which suggests more advanced matrix mineralization (Fig. 4C).

Quantitative evaluation

Semi-quantitative ISO 10993-6 scoring (Fig. 4A–C) showed that both composite groups had lower total tissue reactivity than PLA at 4 weeks (P < 0.05), confirming attenuated inflammation. RUNX2 scores showed no significant group differences at either point, though T-MIX presented slightly higher values at 4 weeks. For BSP, both H-MIX and T-MIX scored higher than PLA at 2 weeks (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively), with T-MIX also exceeding H-MIX (P < 0.05). The neovascularization-to-lymphocyte ratio increased 6 % in T-MIX at 2 weeks, and inflammatory scores decreased 31 % in H-MIX and T-MIX at 4 weeks relative to PLA, indicating accelerated healing and reduced chronic inflammation.13

Overall summary

The histological and immunohistochemical results (Figs. 2–4) demonstrate a time-dependent transition from inflammation to tissue remodeling and osteogenesis. Both composite scaffolds, particularly T-MIX, promoted more organized fibrous encapsulation, greater osteogenic marker expression, and lower tissue reactivity than PLA.

Discussion

The composition and fabrication strategy of polymer–bioceramic filaments are central determinants of their physical, chemical, and biological performance. Filaments produced from different combinations of biopolymers and ceramic fillers—such as HA and β-TCP—exhibit tunable mechanical strength, degradation kinetics, ion-release profiles, and immunomodulatory effects, all of which are critical for bone-regenerative applications.14–16 These tunable characteristics provide a versatile platform for designing clinically adaptable scaffolds capable of supporting various stages of bone healing, ranging from early immune regulation to later matrix mineralization. In particular, HA/β-TCP offers a unique opportunity to adjust bioactivity through compositional ratio, enabling controlled alkalinization, osteoconduction, and sustained bioactive ion release.17,18,19

In this study, the biological response to pure PLA was markedly inferior to that of the composite groups, as reflected by the significantly higher ISO 10993-6 inflammation scores observed in the PLA group. This finding is consistent with the well-documented degradation behavior of PLA, which generates lactic acid monomers and oligomers that lower the local pH and create an acidic microenvironment. Such an environment is known to activate macrophages, amplify pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling, and prolong the inflammatory phase, ultimately delaying early tissue integration and wound healing.20,21,22 Previous studies have further demonstrated that acidic degradation not only disrupts early osteogenic differentiation but also compromises the stability of newly formed extracellular matrix, thereby reinforcing the heightened inflammatory response observed in the pure PLA group in the present work.23–25 Therefore, the pronounced inflammatory response observed in the PLA group is consistent with existing literature describing the immunoreactive nature of PLA alone.

In contrast, both composite groups demonstrated a substantial reduction in the inflammatory response over time, underscoring HA/β-TCP’s buffering capacity.

Beyond pH stabilization, the incorporation of HA/β-TCP provides a biomimetic advantage, as its chemical composition closely resembles that of native bone mineral, enabling more favorable cell–material interactions. In this study, the addition of HA/β-TCP clearly enhanced osteogenic responses at the cellular level, as reflected by improved cell viability and subsequent mineralization compared with pure PLA. Similar osteoconductive and osteoinductive behaviors have also been consistently reported in in vivo models.26,27 This modulation of the immune microenvironment is essential because macrophage polarization strongly influences osteogenesis: acidic environments promote M1-like inflammatory phenotypes, whereas buffered or alkaline conditions favor M2-like tissue- repair phenotypes.28 In the present study, the significantly lower inflammatory score of T-MIX compared with PLA (P < 0.05) suggests that solvent-assisted fabrication improved mixing uniformity, thereby increasing the exposure of ceramic surfaces and enhancing neutralization efficiency. Osteogenic marker expressions further highlight the benefits of composite integration.

The early RUNX2 positivity in the PLA group at 2 weeks may have been induced indirectly by inflammatory stress rather than true osteoinduction, as inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 can transiently upregulate RUNX2 expression without contributing to stable osteoblast maturation.29,30 Conversely, HA/β-TCP-containing filaments supported genuine osteogenic differentiation. Both HA and β-TCP are well documented to enhance osteoblast activity by releasing Ca2+ and PO43- ions, promoting integrin- mediated signaling, and upregulating osteogenic transcription factors, including RUNX2, Osterix, and ALP.31,32

Notably, T-MIX maintained elevated RUNX2 expression at 4 weeks, indicating prolonged osteogenic stimulation. This result is consistent with reports that ceramics with higher β-TCP content release ions at a rate conducive to continuous osteogenic signaling. At the same time, HA provides structural stability and prolonged bioactivity.17,18 The synergy between buffering capacity, mechanical support, and biochemical signaling reinforces the notion that composite ratio and interfacial engineering are critical drivers of osteogenesis.

BSP expressions further support the superior osteogenic potential of composite groups. At 2 weeks, both H-MIX and T-MIX showed significantly higher BSP levels than PLA, with T-MIX outperforming H-MIX (P < 0.05). By 4 weeks, the differences widened, reflecting enhanced mineral deposition and maturation of the extracellular matrix. BSP is particularly relevant because of its role in nucleating hydroxyapatite crystals and in mediating matrix-mineral interactions. 33 The increased BSP levels in composite groups are consistent with their ion-release profiles, as sustained Ca2+ and PO43- release can promote hydroxyapatite nucleation and accelerate mineralization.34,29 The fabrication method also contributed to intergroup differences. Thermally blended filaments are known for their mechanical stability and solvent-free composition; however, the higher processing temperatures may reduce polymer chain mobility and limit ceramic–polymer interfacial bonding.35 In contrast, solvent-mediated processes such as THF casting enhance polymer flow, improve ceramic dispersion, and increase ceramic exposure on the filament surface, which may facilitate earlier biological interaction and more effective buffering of acidic degradation products. Recent studies comparing melt blending and solvent casting in bioceramic composites report that solvent-based methods often generate composites with more uniform microstructures and superior biological activity.36,37 The superior early performance of T-MIX in the present study aligns with these observations.

From a degradation–remodeling perspective, composite filaments containing HA/β-TCP not only mitigate acidity but also actively participate in the dynamic process of bone regeneration. β-TCP supports faster ion release and biodegradation, while HA provides slower remodeling and greater mechanical stability. This complementary behavior mirrors natural bone turnover processes and provides a favorable scaffold–host interface. The literature consistently shows that BCP-containing scaffolds outperform single-phase ceramics or polymer-only materials in supporting both early osteoblastic differentiation and later mineral maturation.31,32

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that although PLA provides a biodegradable platform, its biological performance is limited by acidic degradation and insufficient bioactivity. Incorporating HA/β-TCP offers dual benefits by stabilizing the inflammatory microenvironment and promoting osteogenic signaling.

Both H-MIX and T-MIX significantly improved biological outcomes compared with PLA, with T-MIX demonstrating superior early performance, likely due to more efficient ceramic exposure, enhanced interfacial integration, and sustained biochemical stimulation. These results underscore the importance of optimizing both composite composition and fabrication processes to develop clinically adaptable, biologically effective bone regeneration filaments.
