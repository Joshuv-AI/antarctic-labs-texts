Mapping ICU nursing intervention notes to clinical care classification using a large language model: an evaluation study
Authors: Yeonju Kim, Jiin Kim, Yunseong Cho, Mona Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntensive care unit (ICU) nurses often document their clinical concerns as free-text formats. The free-text documents pose significant challenges for transformation into quantifiable and standardized nursing data. Although large language models (LLMs) show promises in standardizing such documents, evidence supporting their application in nursing domain-specific tasks remains limited. This study aims to evaluate the extent to which an LLM can map ICU nursing intervention notes to Clinical Care Classification (CCC) terminologies and to assess the quality of the mapping results.MethodsThis evaluation study utilized the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) IV database. Nursing notes documented during ICU stays were included, and those without records of interventions or actions were excluded. These selected notes were defined as intervention notes and were analyzed using a four-stage process: (1) expert-driven development of CCC mapping references; (2) LLM-driven generation of CCC mappings; (3) quantitative, and (4) qualitative evaluation of LLM-driven CCC mappings against expert-driven references. The LLM-driven mapping was conducted using prompt engineering techniques and employed the GPT-4o mini model deployed on Azure OpenAI to ensure data security.ResultsA total of 9,614,214 intervention note entries from 39,711 ICU admissions were extracted from MIMIC-IV. Among these, duplicates were excluded, and 269 unique intervention notes were selected. Of these 269 intervention notes, 107 were identified as reflecting nurses’ concerns. Based on these 107 intervention notes, CCC mapping references were established by ICU nurses and subject matter experts, comprising 17 care components, 31 interventions, and 26 sub-interventions. Approximately two-thirds of the LLM-driven CCC mappings achieved a performance comparable to those of expert-driven mappings. Quantitative and qualitative evaluations showed that the LLM captured underlying meaning embedded in nursing intervention notes relatively well, with inconsistency due to reasoning failure as the primary source of mapping errors.ConclusionsThis study identified the feasibility of LLM-driven automated nursing terminology mapping from ICU nursing intervention notes to CCC terms, potentially reducing the burden of terminology mapping. Future research should aim to improve the model’s contextual understanding by utilizing larger, multiple, narrative data-rich datasets, thereby enhancing its generalizability.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04949-6.

Introduction

Intensive care unit (ICU) nurses monitor a patient’s condition about which they are concerned, assess the necessity for high-acuity intensive care, and document their clinical observations and interventions during the care process [1, 2]. This study applies the Healthcare Process Modeling Framework to Phenotype Clinician Behaviors for Exploiting the Signal Gain of Clinical Expertise (HPM-ExpertSignals), which interprets nurses’ documentation patterns such as frequencies, as proxies of nurses’ knowledge, decision-making, and expertise [3]. These patterns can be used to detect and predict patient deterioration before physiological signs of decline become apparent, thereby reducing adverse outcomes such as mortality, cardiac arrest, and length of stay [3, 4]. Because nurses’ concerns are embedded in narrative documentation, transforming these abstract concerns into quantifiable and structured data is essential for operationalizing this framework [5]. Nurses often document their clinical judgments in unstructured formats, such as notes and narratives when structured formats cannot adequately capture or express the information [2]. However, interpreting the conceptual meaning of these unstructured texts and mapping them to appropriate standardized terminology systems demands substantial effort [5, 6]. Traditionally, this mapping process has relied on domain experts, which is time-consuming and susceptible to human error [5]. Therefore, more efficient approaches are needed to map unstructured nursing documentation into interoperable standardized terminology.

In medical and nursing fields, large language models (LLMs) have emerged as promising technologies for transforming unstructured records into structured formats [7–9]. These models are valuable tools for generating instruction-oriented outputs based on foundation models pre-trained on extensive natural language data [10]. Through learning contextual patterns within unstructured text [11], automated mapping using LLMs can be considered [7]. To the best of our knowledge, there is a scarcity of studies demonstrating the feasibility of mapping unstructured nursing records to standardized terminology using LLMs. Although LLMs can be a useful approach for analyzing unstructured documentation, evaluating the quality of LLM-generated outputs is pivotal for patient safety [12]. Regarding physician-generated records, LLMs exhibit enhanced knowledge retrieval and reasoning capabilities through instructional prompt tuning; however, studies have ascertained the necessity of rigorous quality evaluation by domain experts [8]. Studies have suggested that the quality evaluation of LLM-driven results should comprehensively incorporate both natural language-based quantitative metrics and expert-based qualitative assessments [12]. Integrating LLMs into clinical settings requires robust evidence-based approaches that ensure patient safety and address ethical and privacy concerns including confidentiality risks and algorithmic bias [13, 14].

This study aims to evaluate the extent to which an LLM can match expert-driven references in mapping ICU nursing intervention notes to standardized terminology and to assess the quality of its mapping results.

Methods

Study design and setting

This evaluation study utilized secondary data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) IV version 2.2 [15]. This database contains publicly available deidentified patient data from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center from 2008 to 2019 and is accessible through PhysioNet. This study utilized the MIMIC-IV, which primarily comprises ICU environments and patient admissions. While the majority of nursing documentation in MIMIC-IV consists of structured data, including physiological indicators and assessment data such as the Glasgow Coma Scale scores, nursing care plans provide narrative descriptions of patients’ primary problems, including goals, plans, interventions, and expected outcomes. In this study, nursing care plans identified using specific keys in the MIMIC-IV dictionary table were defined as “nursing notes”. Only notes documented during ICU stays that contained specific interventions or actions were included, and these selected notes were defined as “intervention notes”.

Figure 1 illustrates the extraction process of unique ICU intervention notes. From 73,181 ICU admissions, 39,711 ICU admissions with 9,614,214 intervention note entries were identified after applying exclusion criteria. These notes were categorized into predefined elements, such as goals, plans, interventions, expected outcomes, and others, using keys from the MIMIC-IV dictionary table. Because nursing notes in MIMIC-IV contain highly repetitive content (e.g., repeated goal or expected outcome phrases across the same admission, with an average of 236 repeated intervention entries per admission, deduplication was performed. Three subject matter experts with extensive ICU clinical experience and expertise in nursing informatics and standardized terminologies reviewed the 469 unique notes that remained (goals = 22, plans = 4, interventions = 269, expected outcomes = 85, others = 89). Guided by the HPM-ExpertSignals framework [3], we selected 269 unique intervention notes that represented specific actions, activities, or treatments best reflecting ICU nurses’ clinical concerns for analysis.

Clinical care classification system and mapping

The Clinical Care Classification (CCC) system version 2.5 provides a standardized hierarchical framework for documenting nursing practices in EMRs [16]. As illustrated in Fig. 2 (a), the hierarchy is derived from four healthcare patterns—health behaviors, psychological, functional, and physiological—and is structured across multiple levels of granularity. These include 21 care component classes, 77 intervention categories, and 124 sub-intervention categories, with four action-type qualifiers (monitor/assess, perform/care, teach/instruct/supervise, and manage/refer). For this study, we mapped nursing notes to the core hierarchical levels of the CCC system, including care component classes, interventions, and sub-interventions, through both expert review and LLM–based classification.

Step 1) Expert-driven development of CCC mapping references from nursing intervention notes

To develop expert-driven CCC mapping references that integrate evidence-based knowledge and clinical background, we conducted three phases: (1) subject matter experts’ review of unique intervention notes; (2) validation of matching between unique intervention notes and nurses’ concerns; and (3) mapping of validated unique intervention notes to CCC terms [17]. First, 269 unique intervention notes were reviewed by subject matter experts with intensive care and nursing informatics expertise to select those reflecting nurses’ concerns. Second, 34 ICU nurses evaluated how well expert-selected intervention notes matched 15 nurses’ concern categories. To ensure contextual relevance to the ICU setting, the categories were established by experts and grounded in a comprehensive literature review [1, 3, 16] encompassing abnormal respiratory state, fluid volume alteration, abdominal discomfort, monitoring, fall risk, pain, deficit of circulation, infection, general concern, abnormal heart rhythm, improper renal function, mood disorder, violence gesture, abnormal mental state, and abnormal temperature.

Subject matter experts reviewed 269 unique intervention notes and selected 117 notes reflecting nurses’ concerns; 107 of these intervention notes were reviewed by 34 ICU nurses and then mapped by experts to CCC care components, interventions, and sub-interventions. During mapping, experts considered both the higher-level CCC structure linked to each note in the MIMIC-IV database and qualitative feedback from ICU nurses. For example, regarding ‘Administer blood products as indicated to correct fluid imbalance’, ICU nurses noted that transfusions may occur in various clinical situations, such as bleeding or laboratory deviations during routine monitoring. Given the clinical diversity, the note was mapped to the higher-level intervention terminology, ‘Fluid Therapy’, rather than a specific sub-intervention to better capture the clinical context. For notes addressing multiple deteriorating conditions, subject matter experts developed the final mapping based on the evaluation results of ICU nurses, incorporating their clinical judgment to identify the highest-priority decisions in the ICU context.

Step 2) LLM-driven generation of CCC mappings from nursing intervention notes

We preprocessed 107 clinically validated intervention notes, each consisting of a single sentence, as units of analysis to enable the LLM to follow the expert-driven reasoning process. Examples of these refinements are presented in Additional file 1. Two researchers with critical care nursing experience independently reviewed the refined intervention notes and reached consensus, which was subsequently validated by a third researcher.

We employed the GPT-4o mini model deployed in Azure Open AI service to comply with the responsible use statements for MIMIC data [18]. This model was selected based on three key domains: (1) sufficient reasoning performance for accurate mapping of clinical nursing documentation to standardized terminologies; (2) optimal cost-efficiency balancing computational requirements and performance; and (3) comprehensive capabilities for interpreting nursing interventions in clinical documentation [19].

We analyzed 107 pre-processed intervention notes using the LLM to map them to the CCC care components, interventions, and sub-interventions. All prompts of this analysis were outlined using templates included by relevant literature [20–22] to maintain analytical rigor. We refined prompts through multiple collaborative meetings among researchers with clinical experience in critical care settings and expertise in nursing informatics and then implemented a six-phase prompting strategies, including zero-shot learning [20], few-shot learning [20], the CCC context [16] add-up, tree-of-thought (ToT) prompting [21] add-up, reasoning and action prompting (ReAct) [22] add-up, and nurses’ concern context add-up. Figure 4 and Additional file 2 presents the refinement points and explanations for each phase, along with prompt examples.

As shown in Fig. 4, at each stage of analysis, the LLM was instructed through specific prompts to interpret the meaning of each note and generate three CCC mappings considered most appropriate. Mapping was considered successful when at least one of the three LLM-driven results exactly matched the expert-driven mapping reference. The instruction and selection of successful mappings were based on previous literature that evaluated the matching between nursing flowsheet records and CCC concepts by mapping each data template–measure pair to three ranks, and that defined cases in which the CCC names used for providing the clinician the most relevant information in the institution matched as exact matching [23]. The most appropriate CCC mappings were identified as either the highest priority interventions based on nurses’ clinical concerns for deteriorating ICU patients or those that comprehensively encompassed multiple documented activities.

We configured parameters to maintain consistent and accurate mapping of ICU nursing notes to standardized terminology while allowing the language model access to its complete vocabulary range. The parameters included a deterministic temperature setting (0.1), response length limitation (1,000 tokens), complete vocabulary access (top_p = 1), and no repetition penalties (frequency_penalty = 0; presence_penalty = 0). The process of parameter optimization was not included during analysis phases.

For each CCC care component, intervention, and sub-intervention, we calculated metrics by comparing LLM -driven with expert-driven mappings.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were expressed as frequencies and proportions. Match rates comparing expert-driven with LLM-driven CCC mappings were presented with 95% confidence intervals. We conducted Cochran’s Q test and pairwise McNemar’s test with Bonferroni corrections as post-hoc analysis to evaluate statistical differences in mapping performance across analysis phases [29]. Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed tests. The mapping evaluation results based on quantitative metrics were presented as the mean and standard deviation. The extraction process of nursing notes from the MIMIC-IV database was performed using Python version 3.11.3. All statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio version 3.6.0.

Results

Study dataset

Among 73,181 ICU admissions in MIMIC-IV, we identified 9,614,214 intervention note entries recorded to 39,711 admissions. The characteristics of this dataset are presented in prior research related to this study [17]. Among the 9,614,214 intervention note entries, duplicates were excluded. Consequently, 269 unique intervention notes were identified.

Study steps

Step 1) Expert-driven development of CCC mapping references from nursing intervention notes

Subject matter experts developed 107 unique intervention notes into CCC mapping reference, including 17 care components, 31 interventions, and 26 sub-interventions. Regarding the nurses’ concern categories matched with the 107 notes, abnormal respiratory state (17.8%) and fluid volume alteration (16.8%) were the most prevalent, followed by abdominal discomfort (10.3%), monitoring (7.5%), and pain (7.5%) [17].

Step 2) LLM-driven generation of CCC mappings from nursing intervention notes

Figure 5 and Table 3 depict the LLM-driven CCC mapping performance compared with expert-driven mapping references across six prompting phases. The mapping performance of the LLM-driven analysis progressively increased across all CCC structures (care components, interventions, and sub-interventions) from zero-shot learning to the nurses’ concern context add-ups. Mapping performance between analysis phases showed statistically significant differences (Care components: Q-value = 188.89, p-value: <0.001; interventions: Q-value = 164.73, p-value: <0.001; sub-interventions: Q-value = 81.06, p-value: <0.001). Regarding post-hoc tests, Phase 1 (Zero-shot) and Phase 2 (Few-shot) had significantly lower match rates than other phases across all CCC structures (p-value: <0.001). For care components, Phase 3 (CCC context add-up) showed significantly lower mapping performance when compared to Phase 5 (ReAct add-up) and Phase 6 (Nurses’ concern context add-up) (p-value: <0.001), but no statistically significant difference was observed when compared to Phase 4 (ToT add-up). For interventions, Phase 3 showed significantly lower mapping performance compared to Phase 6 (p-value: <0.001), but no statistically significant differences were found when compared to Phase 4 and Phase 5. For sub-interventions, the integration of additional contexts or prompting techniques beyond Phase 2 did not result in statistically significant improvements in mapping performance.

Categories including fluid volume alteration, abdominal discomfort, pain, fall risk, abnormal mental state, improper renal function, and violent gestures showed successful mapping performance above two-thirds across all CCC structures. Conversely, categories of mood disorder and abnormal temperature showed poorest mapping performance. Regarding nursing notes in the mood disorder category that demonstrated low mapping performance (e.g., assess patient’s experience, feelings and fears related to violence to ensure a safe environment), experts primarily mapped detailed interventions aimed at assessing and alleviating anxiety and fear experienced by ICU patients, whereas the LLM interpreted and mapped these as nursing interventions for effective emotional coping strategies.

Step 3) Quantitative evaluation of LLM-driven CCC mappings against expert-driven mapping references

Table 5 presents the results of the LLM-driven CCC mappings compared with expert-driven references across multiple metrics. The LLM-driven mappings were evaluated based on Phase 6, which showed the highest mapping performance in Step 2. In the CCC structure, care components showed relatively lower performance across the ROUGE and BLEU metrics, whereas sub-interventions demonstrated the highest ROUGE-1 score (0.420), indicating better unigram overlap with expert-driven mapping references. Cosine similarity scores were generally high, ranging from 0.647 to 0.730, suggesting a relatively high semantic alignment between the LLM- and expert-driven mappings across all CCC structures. Norm-LD showed the highest scores in care components (0.653) and the lowest scores in interventions (0.574).

Approximately half of the mapping errors showed inconsistencies due to reasoning failure, including misunderstanding of CCC hierarchical structure (62.5%; e.g., mapping a sub-intervention such as Positioning Therapy to a higher-level intervention category) and misaligned reasoning priorities regarding nursing concerns in the ICU environment (37.5%; e.g., mapping ‘increase ventilator-supported pressure or mode’ to Oxygen Therapy Care, whereas experts, considering the ICU setting, mapped it to Ventilator Care.). Discussion

This study evaluated the match rates and the quantitative and qualitative aspects of the LLM’s mappings by comparing them with an expert-driven reference established through reviews by subject matter experts and assessments by ICU nurses. The LLM-driven analysis achieved the highest mapping performance by incorporating nurses’ concern contexts, resulting in concordance with two-thirds of expert-driven mapping. Two-thirds of nurses’ concern categories were successfully mapped across all CCC structures.

LLM-driven analysis markedly improved performance through refined prompting strategies, as supported by a survey [11]. Our prompt strategies correspond to the two approaches: (1) providing the CCC systems through tabulated demonstration examples to facilitate consistent learning patterns in the LLM, and (2) matching nurses’ concern categories with intervention notes to enhance the model’s understanding of underlying clinical meanings during the inference process. By leveraging these strategies, incorporating nurses’ concern contexts resulted in the highest mapping performance. Unlike care components and interventions within the CCC structures, sub-interventions did not show statistically significant improvement in mapping performance when additional prompting techniques or contexts beyond few-shot were applied. This suggests that as one moves toward the most descendant level in the CCC hierarchical structure, specialized knowledge injections may be required to optimize the CCC mapping task [7]. The systematic approach of this study could be extended to other classification systems with varying granularity levels, such as the International Classification for Nursing Practice [30].

The LLM demonstrated relatively concordant mapping with experts for concern categories related to patients’ physiological conditions and safety, compared to those involving psychological conditions. The difference of mapping performance may emerge from the intersection of pre-trained knowledge distribution and the unique characteristics of the ICU environment. Human–model interactions may prioritize clinically urgent patient problems over psychological manifestation, including patient feelings, fears, and suicidal ideation [31]. For the mood disorder category, which showed low mapping performance, the model tended to interpret nurses’ assessments and interventions on negative emotions in a more patient-centered positive direction (e.g., as effective coping). This aligns with previous research suggesting that LLMs tend to interpret patient situations more positively and empathically in healthcare contexts [32]. Further research needs to explore strategies to mitigate hallucination issues arising from LLMs by considering these mapping differences [28].

Similar to a recent study [12], our study revealed that cosine similarity demonstrated higher values compared to other quantitative metrics. These findings suggest that the model captures the underlying meaning embedded in nursing notes relatively well, rather than relying on lexical expressions or structures during mapping. In the qualitative evaluation, inconsistencies due to reasoning failure emerged as the predominant type of mapping error. These inconsistencies can be categorized as inference hallucinations, which are primarily instances observed when reasoning about situations involving complex, multiple associations [28]. This can be linked to the CCC terminology with hierarchical structures and nurses’ concerns that should consider the complex clinical situations in ICU settings. The integration of quantitative and qualitative assessments suggests that, while LLM-driven CCC mappings offer a novel, resource-efficient approach to nursing terminology standardization, its clinical implementation requires a human-in-the-loop approach to maintain safety and accuracy standards [7, 9]. To responsibly integrate LLMs into real-world hospital workflows, all stakeholders, including patients, healthcare professionals, and system developers, will need to adhere to the principles of biomedical ethics: beneficence, non-maleficence, respect for autonomy, and justice [33]. Patients and healthcare professionals should act as human safeguards to ensure ethical and safe use of LLMs in practice [7, 9], while system developers should protect data privacy and promote secure model development through approaches such as federated learning [33–35]. Building on these ethical and safety foundations, the use of LLM-driven mappings in clinical practice may enable more transparent data sharing and support the generation of data-driven insights. At the policy level, fostering global collaboration and establishing equitable data-sharing infrastructures are essential to accelerate the responsible adoption of LLMs in clinical practice [33].

This study has several limitations. First, this study utilized a single LLM and a relatively small dataset, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research needs to validate these findings using multiple LLMs and larger, more diverse datasets including narrative nursing documentation. Second, the evaluation of the LLM’s performance and quality of its mapping was based on an expert-driven reference established through the interpretations of nursing intervention notes by subject matter experts and ICU nurses with specific clinical backgrounds and expertise. Future studies should include experts from diverse institutions, countries, and clinical settings to develop a more representative reference. Third, both expert- and LLM-driven mapping results may not have fully captured the interconnected nature of nursing activities as the mapping prioritized nurses’ concerns regarding patient deterioration in ICU settings. Fourth, the prompt-based approach lacked fine-tuning, highlighting the need for ICU context-aware model optimization. Fifth, this study utilized MIMIC-IV version 2.2, which aligns with the expert reference but preceded the release of version 3.1.

Conclusions

This study showed the feasibility of using an LLM to automatically map ICU nursing notes to the CCC standardized terminology based on nurses’ concerns. By incorporating both quantitative and qualitative evaluations of LLM-driven mappings, this study provides insights for future research and recommendations for the safe application of LLM technology in nursing documentation and nursing terminology standardization. While further validation with diverse models and larger datasets is needed, this research provides foundational insights for integrating artificial intelligence technology safely into nursing research and practice and advancing automated terminology mapping in healthcare.
