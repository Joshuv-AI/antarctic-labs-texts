Dynamic “Cap”‐abilities of P‐bodies and the XRN1‐EDC4 axis
Authors: Lauren E Malsick, Jeffrey Wilusz
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
RNA turnover regulates the quality and quantity of cellular gene expression through a coordinated cavalcade of enzymes, factors, and phase transitions. In this issue, Brothers et al reveal the importance of balanced communication between the Xrn1 exonuclease and the EDC4 decapping factor to coordinate P‐body dynamics and maintain cellular fitness.

Far from being a mere garbage removal service, RNA turnover in human cells is a carefully integrated, multipathway process using protein–RNA and protein–protein interactions, post‐translational modifications, and enigmatic liquid–liquid phase separation/granule formation to regulate mRNA homeostasis. This coordination effectively integrates the quantity and quality control of cellular gene expression. A major pathway of mRNA decay involves sequential removal of the poly(A) tail (deadenylation), removal of the 5′ m7G cap (decapping), and 5′‐3′ exonucleolytic degradation. These enzymatic activities interface with P‐bodies, RNA granules that largely contain deadenylated mRNAs and several core protein factors, some of which are directly involved in mRNA decay (Łabno et al, 2016). Understanding how the cell simultaneously accomplishes the feat of coordinating mRNA decay and responding to the changing cellular gene expression needs represents a key knowledge gap in molecular biology.

In the latest issue of the EMBO Journal, Brothers et al (2023) investigated the role of the 5′‐3′ exonuclease XRN1, which degrades decapped mRNAs, and thereby provided important insights into the function of P‐bodies and the coordination of mRNA turnover activities (Fig 1). Using HeLa cells that only express a catalytically dead Xrn1 protein, the authors expanded on earlier observations and demonstrated that P‐body size does not linearly correspond to the amount of residual deadenylated mRNA. In other words, P‐bodies are not merely a temporary dumping ground for unwanted mRNAs awaiting degradation. The authors then used a series of mutations in selective protein domains to demonstrate that XRN1‐EDC4 interactions play a major role in determining P‐body size. For example, excess free cellular EDC4 protein by overexpression or prevention of EDC4‐Xrn1 interaction by mutations resulted in a dramatic increase in P‐body size. These observations clearly demonstrate the functional connection between the Xrn1 exonuclease (Nagarajan et al, 2013), the EDC4 decapping factor (Chang et al, 2014), and P‐bodies (Riggs et al, 2020). While P‐bodies are not necessarily considered to be major sites of mRNA decay in all cases, these data nicely set the stage for demonstrating how P‐bodies majorly influence the mRNA “trash” clean‐up process. Through a series of complementary experiments, the authors convincingly demonstrate that P‐bodies contribute to decapping initiation of deadenylated mRNAs and that mRNA decapping efficiency (and thus the overall decay rate) is clearly connected with P‐body expansion. Finally, the paper addresses the key question of whether decay factor‐P‐body coordination is even necessary for cell viability. The answer is a resounding “yes”! Increasing P‐body size is vital to manage disruptions in gene expression that result from major changes in mRNA decay rates (e.g., deletion of the Xrn1 exonuclease). If P‐bodies are not available to deal with this disruption in mRNA levels, the resulting inherent stresses may result in cell death.

P‐body dynamics is influenced by the ratio of Xrn1 and EDC4 proteins and is important for cell fitness

The formation of membraneless organelles/liquid–liquid phase separations called P‐bodies in human cells is influenced by the ratio of two interacting proteins—the Xrn1 exonuclease and the regulatory factor EDC4. Changing this ratio by reducing Xrn1 protein levels in the cell causes enlargement of P‐bodies and translational repression. However, if P‐bodies are prevented from forming in response to altered protein ratios or stress (e.g., by adding the micropeptide NBDY which blocks P‐body formation), cells cannot effectively regulate cytoplasmic gene expression, cell fitness is significantly compromised, and cell death can result. In the context of the extensive body of work already available in the RNA decay and P‐body fields, these new results significantly advance several important aspects of mRNA decapping/decay regulation. First, the study makes it very clear that P‐bodies contribute to mRNA stabilization in cells. This nicely extends work that indicated that P‐bodies are not necessarily major sites of mRNA decay (Luo et al, 2018). Second, the study shows that protein–protein interactions between the major RNA 5′‐3′ exonuclease and the EDC4 decapping factor (Bloch et al, 2023) are vital for effective integration of P‐bodies in the regulation of aspects of mRNA turnover in human cells. Next, P‐bodies are shown to play a vital role in maintaining cellular fitness in the face of internal stress caused by fluctuations in RNA metabolism. Finally, the study shows that P‐body size matters in terms of its ability to regulate decapping. Overall, the study also has major implications for the decision between mRNA translational repression and mRNA turnover—particularly for miRNA targets. The study emphasizes the extensive cross talk between mRNA turnover and translation in the process as well as how P‐bodies play a major role in determining the fate of miRNA‐targeted mRNAs.

Like any interesting set of observations, the paper by Brothers et al stimulates a plethora of questions for future studies. For instance, since all the mRNAs in the observed P‐body‐associated regulation were deadenylated, does poly(A) binding protein (PABP) play a mechanistic role in preventing mRNAs from entering P‐bodies? Along these same lines, since the study clearly shows a mechanism for coordination of decapping and 5′‐3′ exonuclease decay, how might the deadenylation process (Passmore & Coller, 2022) also be co‐coordinated (or is it)? It might be interesting to assess ways of targeting the EDC4‐Xrn1 interface with small molecules or peptide mimics to provide an additional experimental tool to further assess the role this decay factor coordination plays in overall mRNA decay. The authors also point out the need to understand how P‐bodies repress decapping, how EDC4 interactions with itself and other decay factors might provide an additional layer of regulation onto the process, how translation of P‐body associated mRNAs may be eventually reactivated, and the underlying mechanism for how P‐bodies ultimately support cell viability and suppress stress granule formation.

Along with important remaining questions specific to RNA turnover, this work raises some more broad questions as well. For instance, is the Xrn1‐decapping factor‐P‐body interface conserved among all eukaryotes? Xrn1 is known to interact with Dcp1 (not EDC4) in yeast and Drosophila (Vidya & Duchaine, 2022). Could this difference be important for regulating RNA decay in organisms with faster life cycles or lower ambient temperatures? The study provides evidence that subcellular localization can influence mRNA half‐life. Could this suggest that assessing the turnover rate of an entire population of a given mRNA may be in some ways misleading, without assessing compartmentalization? Another area that might be particularly insightful to pursue is how RNA viruses interface and potentially manipulate the EDC4‐Xrn1 interaction to remodel P‐bodies, disrupt typical cellular regulation of mRNA turnover, and promote efficient viral infection. It may not, for example, be a coincidence that CRISPR KO screens have revealed EDC4 and Xrn1 as key factors in SARS‐CoV‐2 infections (Grodzki et al, 2022).

Ultimately, coordinating the activity of decay factors along with P‐bodies implies that mRNA decay in human cells might be more like the “reduce‐reuse‐recycle” model of careful waste management rather than a simple garbage removal service.
