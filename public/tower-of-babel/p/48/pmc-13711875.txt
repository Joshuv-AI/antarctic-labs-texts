“Drug-Delivering-Drug” nanocomplexes reinforce cytosolic nucleic acid sensing for broad-spectrum antiviral activity in fish
Authors: Baofu Ma, Wenjuan Yao, Xiaozhe Fu, Yinjie Niu, Ouqin Chang, Kaiguang Yang, Hongru Liang, Xia Luo, Wenwen Xiao, Qiang Lin, Ningqiu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractViral diseases pose a persistent global threat, necessitating next-generation antiviral strategies that overcome the limited antiviral spectrum, rapid resistance, and insufficient immune engagement of conventional therapeutics. Herein, a carrier-free, “drug-delivering-drug” antiviral nanocomplex, termed BBTS25, is developed through directed self-assembly of baicalin and the bioactive peptide TS25. BBTS25 exhibits potent broad-spectrum antiviral efficacy. Notably, in a representative viral model, it strongly suppresses viral replication, reducing intracellular and supernatant viral loads by approximately 70% and 80%, respectively, while concurrently compromising virion structural integrity. Mechanistically, atomistic molecular dynamics simulations reveal that BBTS25 efficiently penetrates cellular membranes, with its peptide component forming strong interactions with intracellular nucleic acids. The interaction shields nucleic acids from nuclease-mediated degradation, thereby sustaining the activation of the type I interferon pathway. Consistently, transcriptomic profiling of treated cells shows robust induction of interferon-stimulated gene networks and activation of innate antiviral responses. In vivo, BBTS25 administration in a lower vertebrate (teleost) model enhances survival by approximately 47% and demonstrates excellent biocompatibility. Furthermore, spleen transcriptomic analysis and quantitative PCR indicate that BBTS25 activates dendritic cells and macrophages, and promotes type I interferon-associated immune responses. Introduction

Viral infections remain a persistent and increasingly severe threat to global public health. The recurrent outbreaks of seasonal influenza and the emergence of novel epidemics such as (COVID-19) illustrate that viral pathogens not only cause acute morbidity and mortality but can also lead to chronic infection, immune dysregulation, and long-term sequelae [1, 2]. Beyond human health, viral diseases also exert profound impacts on livestock production, aquaculture, and crop production, leading to substantial economic losses across global food systems [3–5]. Traditional antiviral strategies, including antiviral small-molecule drugs and prophylactic vaccines, have markedly advanced the management of infectious diseases; however, they continue to face significant limitations [6–8]. Many direct-acting antivirals exhibit high target specificity and therefore possess a narrow activity spectrum, limiting their effectiveness across diverse viral species [9]; the high mutation rates of many viruses further facilitate the rapid development of drug resistance [10]; effective vaccines are still unavailable for numerous viral pathogens [11]; moreover, many existing antiviral agents fail to adequately engage or potentiate host immune responses. Consequently, there remains an urgent scientific and clinical need to develop next-generation antiviral approaches capable of simultaneously enhancing innate and adaptive immunity while offering broad-spectrum antiviral potential.

Baicalin, a major flavonoid glycoside isolated from Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi (Huang-Qin), represents one of the most extensively studied natural small molecules with reported antiviral and anti-inflammatory properties [12, 13]. Baicalin has demonstrated measurable but modest antiviral activity against several viruses, including influenza A virus (H3N2), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2(SARS-CoV-2), coxsackievirus B3(CVB3), and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) [14–17]. More recently, its effects have also been investigated in aquaculture-related pathogens. In our laboratory, baicalin exhibited detectable inhibitory activity against largemouth bass ranavirus (LMBV), though the antiviral effect remained weak and required relatively high concentrations to achieve partial suppression of viral replication [18].

Structurally, baicalin is composed of the flavone baicalein conjugated to a glucuronic acid moiety at the 7-position (baicalein-7-O-β-D-glucuronide), a structure that confers strong biological activity but also results in poor aqueous solubility and suboptimal pharmacokinetic behavior [19]. Moreover, the antiviral effects of baicalin rely predominantly on its anti-inflammatory activity, which is intrinsically insufficient to counteract viruses that undergo rapid intracellular replication [20, 21]. These intrinsic drawbacks severely restrict its systemic exposure and thereby attenuate its in vivo antiviral potency. Although baicalin shows measurable antiviral activity and an excellent safety profile, its native form remains far from sufficient for practical antiviral applications [13, 22]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for advanced engineering strategies that can enhance its solubility, improve delivery efficiency, and potentiate its antiviral efficacy, thereby unlocking its full potential as a next-generation broad-spectrum antiviral agent.

Bioactive peptides are functional polypeptides typically composed of fewer than 50 amino acid residues, generated through enzymatic hydrolysis of proteins or produced by chemical synthesis and recombinant expression [23]. Owing to their diverse origins, intrinsic safety, and high biological selectivity, bioactive peptides have emerged as an attractive molecular class distinct from traditional small-molecule antivirals or macromolecular biologics [23, 24]. These peptides participate in numerous physiological regulatory processes and display broad functional diversity, including anti-microbial, anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, immunomodulatory, anti-tumor, and endocrine-regulatory activities [25]. Notably, anti-microbial peptides has been shown to exert potent antiviral effects [26]. Such peptides inhibit infection by disrupting viral envelopes, blocking receptor engagement, or interfering with early entry steps [27]. Consequently, antiviral peptides (AVPs) are rapidly gaining attention as promising candidates for the development of next-generation broad-spectrum antiviral agents [28].

In our previous work, we rationally designed and screened a tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI)-derived peptide, TS25, which exhibited strong antiviral activity [29]. Mechanistic studies revealed that TS25 effectively blocks viral attachment to host cell membranes and disrupts virion structural integrity, thereby rapidly suppressing viral entry and early-stage replication to provide direct antiviral protection. Despite its promising antiviral potential, TS25 faces intrinsic physicochemical limitations that hinder its translational application. Like many bioactive peptides, TS25 suffers from poor structural stability, susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, and a short in vivo half-life [23]. In addition, TS25 primarily functions through direct virucidal and entry-blocking mechanisms, with limited capacity to modulate host innate immunity, making it difficult to establish sustained and systemic antiviral protection. Enhancing the stability, biocompatibility, and immunomodulatory capacity of TS25 is therefore essential for advancing it toward practical antiviral use.

Given the limited antiviral efficacy of baicalin and TS25 in their native forms, which is associated with their unfavorable physicochemical and pharmacological properties, we developed a para-boronophenylalanine (BPA)-mediated fusion strategy to integrate baicalin with TS25, thereby constructing a “drug-delivering-drug” nanomaterial. This system achieved pronounced therapeutic efficacy, and its underlying mechanism was elucidated from both chemical and biological perspectives.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of BBTS25

Inspired by the limitations of conventional combination therapies, where simple co-administration of drugs often fails to achieve synergistic efficacy, we conceived BBTS25 as a “drug-delivering-drug” system, in which two pharmacologically active molecules simultaneously act as therapeutic agents and mutually serve as delivery modules for each other. Guided by this concept, the central design principle of BBTS25 is the BPA-mediated molecular fusion of baicalin and TS25, integrating the two bioactive molecules into a single, self-delivering therapeutic entity. This design is chemically enabled by the dynamic nature of boronic acids, as the boronic-acid group readily forms reversible boronate esters with cis-diols under alkaline conditions while undergoing hydrolysis in acidic environments, thereby imparting BBTS25 with both structural integrity and environmental responsiveness. Notably, this reciprocal fusion strategy is fundamentally distinct from physical mixing or carrier-assisted delivery, representing a new paradigm for drug integration.

Based on the structural features of baicalin and TS25, the most reactive sites toward para-boronophenylalanine (BPA) can be clearly identified (Fig. 1A). The β-D-glucuronide moiety of baicalin contains a C2-C3 vicinal diol, which readily forms a boronate ester with the boronic acid group of BPA. In TS25, the most nucleophilic and sterically accessible site is the N-terminal α-amino group. Therefore, BBTS25 was constructed through BPA-mediated directed self-assembly. Specifically, baicalin reacts with BPA to form a stable boronate ester via the C2-C3 diol on the glucuronide ring. Owing to the hydrophobic flavonoid scaffold of baicalin, this complex readily assembles into nanoscale aggregates (Fig. S1). To verify the formation of the baicalin@BPA intermediate, 1H NMR and 11B NMR analyses were performed (Fig. S2). Compared with free baicalin and BPA, characteristic changes in both proton and boron chemical environments were observed after assembly, supporting the formation of boronate ester linkages between baicalin and BPA. Subsequently, an amide bond is formed between the carboxyl group of BPA and the N-terminal amino group of the cationic antiviral peptide TS25, ultimately yielding the nanocomplex (Fig. 1A and B, and S3). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that baicalin@BPA assemblies formed well-defined spherical nanoparticles with an average diameter of approximately 100 nm (Fig. 1C). Upon incorporation of the antiviral peptide TS25, the nanoparticles transformed into larger BBTS25 nanospheres (≈ 200 nm, Fig. 1D), indicating successful peptide integration into the nanoassembly framework. Dynamic light scattering measurements further corroborated this structural evolution, showing a clear increase in hydrodynamic diameter following peptide conjugation (Fig. 1E and F).

Zeta potential measurements showed that the baicalin@BPA assemblies carried a negative surface charge of approximately − 14 mV (Fig. 1G). Upon incorporation of TS25, the potential shifted to around − 6 mV, indicating successful incorporation of TS25 into the assembled nanocomplex (Fig. 1G). Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) and circular dichroism (CD) absorption spectra further verified the coexistence of baicalin and TS25 within BBTS25. As baicalin exhibits a characteristic absorption band at 280 nm due to its flavone backbone, the retention of this feature in the BBTS25 spectrum indicates successful integration of baicalin into the hybrid assemblies (Fig. 1H). CD spectra analysis revealed that incorporation of TS25 induced pronounced changes in the far-UV region, particularly the emergence of a strong positive signal around 203 nm, suggesting conformational rearrangement of the peptide within the assembled nanocomplex. Meanwhile, minimal changes were observed in the near-UV region, indicating that incorporation of TS25 did not substantially disrupt the overall chiral organization of the baicalin@BPA assembly (Fig. 1I and S4). Elemental mapping further corroborated these observations, evidencing the presence of boron from baicalin@BPA and sulfur from the cysteine residues in TS25 (Fig. S5). The encapsulation efficiency of baicalin in BBTS25 was further evaluated and reached approximately 90%, highlighting the effectiveness of the BPA-mediated assembly strategy(Fig. S6A and S6B). In addition, BBTS25 displayed pH-dependent baicalin release behavior, with enhanced release under acidic conditions(Fig. S6C). Therefore, these results confirm the successful construction of the nanocomplex and validate its suitability for “drug-delivering-drug” applications.

The boronic-acid group readily forms reversible boronate esters with cis-diols under alkaline conditions, which are hydrolyzed in acidic environments [30]. The pH-responsive dynamic covalent interaction has been widely applied in drug delivery, biosensing, and tumor-cell enrichment [31–33]. Therapeutic peptides are prone to rapid degradation by lysosomal or extracellular proteases, shortening their half-life and reducing antiviral efficacy [23]. Strategies like PEGylation, cyclization, stapling, backbone modification, lipidation, and glycosylation have been developed to enhance their stability [34, 35]. In this work, BPA was introduced to covalently link baicalin with the antiviral peptide TS25. This design enables acid-triggered, controllable baicalin release in the lysosomal microenvironment through reversible boronate-ester cleavage, while simultaneously improving the proteolytic stability of TS25 due to BPA-mediated chemical modification. Furthermore, baicalin@BPA exhibit a strong negative surface potential (≈ -14 mV), which limits their interaction with negatively charged cellular membranes and consequently restricts cellular uptake [36]. In contrast, cationic antiviral peptides typically display high charge density-dependent cytotoxicity and also suffer from insufficient intracellular delivery [37]. The nanostructure BBTS25, formed via BPA-directed self-assembly, effectively overcomes these limitations: baicalin and TS25 are co-organized into a single and uniform nanostructure, significantly reducing the intrinsic cytotoxicity of TS25 while enhancing cellular uptake and overall structural stability.

Together, these findings demonstrate that a BPA-driven self-assembly strategy enables synergistic co-delivery of baicalin and TS25, and provides a promising platform for harnessing their potential cooperative antiviral activity. Importantly, this assembly mechanism is not inherently restricted to baicalin or TS25. In principle, other antiviral small molecules containing suitable diol or polyphenolic structures could potentially be integrated with bioactive peptides through similar boronate-mediated interactions. Therefore, this drug-delivering-drug concept establishes a robust and expandable foundation for future in vitro and in vivo studies.

In vitro antiviral activity

To assess the antiviral potential of individual components and the assembled nanostructure, we first examined the cytotoxicity of baicalin, TS25, and BBTS25. As shown in Fig. 2A-C and S7, baicalin exhibited excellent cellular safety, while TS25 caused marked dose-dependent toxicity at higher concentrations, consistent with its known membrane-disruptive nature. Notably, BBTS25 largely eliminated this intrinsic cytotoxicity, demonstrating a safety profile comparable to baicalin. These results suggest that the nanoassembly organization significantly mitigates the free-peptide–associated toxicity. We next evaluated the antiviral activity of these drugs against LMBV. Neither baicalin, TS25, nor their physical mixture produced meaningful inhibition of viral infection (Fig. 2D and E), indicating that simple co-administration is insufficient to achieve synergistic antiviral effects. In sharp contrast, BBTS25 robustly suppressed viral replication, reducing intracellular and supernatant viral loads by approximately 70% and 80%, respectively (Fig. 2F and S8). In addition, BBTS25 exhibited inhibitory activity against Infectious spleen and kidney necrosis virus (ISKNV) and Siniperca chuatsi rhabdovirus (SCRV) in vitro, which represent DNA and RNA viruses, respectively (Fig. S9).

To further examine whether BBTS25 directly interacts with virions, we performed negative-stain TEM. The images clearly showed that BBTS25 disrupted virion morphology, causing membrane collapse and overall structural compromise (Fig. 2G). These observations reveal that BBTS25 is capable of inactivating virus particles even before cellular entry. At the intracellular level, Western blot analysis confirmed a dramatic reduction in LMBV major capsid protein (MCP) following BBTS25 treatment (Fig. 2H and S10), supporting its potent suppression of viral replication inside host cells. To elucidate the intracellular fate of BBTS25, co-localization and time-dependent assays were performed. Co-localization studies showed that BBTS25 predominantly accumulated within lysosomes after cellular uptake (Fig. 2I). Time-dependent uptake experiments further revealed rapid internalization within 1 h, followed by a gradual decrease, suggesting controlled release of active components in lysosomes (Fig. 2J and Fig. S11). Although BBTS25 initially traffics through lysosomal compartments, the diffuse intracellular fluorescence observed at later time points suggest progressive intracellular release of the nanocomplex, which may facilitate subsequent interactions with cytosolic nucleic acids (Fig. S12). Finally, viral burden was evaluated using a cell-sectioning technique, which revealed that the number of intracellular virions was markedly reduced in the BBTS25-treated group compared with the control group (Fig. 2K and S13). Together, these results demonstrate that BBTS25 exerts potent dual-site antiviral activity-both extracellularly through direct virion disruption and intracellularly via inhibition of viral protein expression and replication.

Baicalin possesses a flavonoid backbone and exhibits poor aqueous solubility, which limits its interaction with cells [19]. Consequently, it shows low cytotoxicity but also suffers from poor bioavailability, making it difficult to exert potent intracellular antiviral activity [18]. In contrast, TS25 carries a positively charged surface that strongly interacts with negatively charged cell membranes, resulting in noticeable cytotoxicity. The same cationic characteristic also facilitates strong binding to viral envelopes, enabling TS25 to block viral attachment and disrupt virion integrity [29]. Therefore, free TS25 primarily functions as a preventive antiviral agent and is unable to effectively inhibit viral replication once infection has occurred. After assembly, the resulting nanomaterial BBTS25 is taken up by cells via endocytosis without damaging the cell membrane. This design markedly reduces the intrinsic cytotoxicity of TS25 while enabling two complementary antiviral mechanisms. Cationic nanoparticles easily escape from the negatively charged lysosomal membrane due to electrostatic attraction, thus failing to perform their function within lysosomes [38]. BBTS25, however, carries a partially negative surface charge, allowing it to remain within lysosomes after entering the cell. Therefore, it slowly and controllably releases baicalin and its BPA-TS25 complex within the lysosome. This intracellular release enables baicalin to exert its antiviral effect in the cytoplasm, while the TS25 component continues to exert its antiviral effect.

This dual-site antiviral action highlights the advantage of the drug-delivery-drug design: it improves molecular stability, reduces toxicity, and enhances synergistic antiviral efficacy. Together, these results demonstrate that BBTS25 acts as a potent antiviral agent capable of functioning both inside and outside cells, offering substantial promise for translational applications.

Mechanistic insights via molecular dynamics

To verify whether the BBTS25 nano-assemblies possess an enhanced ability to penetrate cell membranes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to compare the interactions between dispersed TS25 and assembled TS25 with a lipid bilayer. As shown in Fig. 3A, in the system containing 15 dispersed peptides, the peptides gradually moved apart during the simulation. Some peptides interacted with the membrane, whereas others detached from the membrane region. In contrast, the 15-peptide assembly continuously approached the membrane surface and eventually established tight contact (Fig. 3B). The time-dependent changes in the distance between the peptide mass center and the membrane further confirmed this behavior. The distance increased over time in the dispersed system but steadily decreased in the assembled system, indicating that the assembly had a stronger tendency to associate with the membrane (Fig. 3C and S14A). Analysis of hydrogen bond formation revealed that both systems showed increased hydrogen bonding during the simulation, but the assembled system exhibited a much faster growth, yielding approximately threefold more hydrogen bonds at the end of the simulation (Fig. 3D). The mean square displacement (MSD) of lipid headgroups also increased in both systems, while the assembled peptides induced considerably larger MSD values (Fig. S14B), suggesting stronger perturbation of membrane surface morphology that favors peptide entry. Interaction energy calculations further showed that both electrostatic and van der Waals interactions between peptides and the membrane were weaker in the assembled state than in the dispersed state, with electrostatic energy showing the most pronounced reduction. The electrostatic attraction between dispersed peptides and the membrane was roughly three times stronger than that of the assembly (Fig. S14C), implying that the assembly weakens excessive electrostatic adsorption.

After entering the cell, the interaction between TS25 and nucleic acids was investigated through molecular docking. TS25 formed stable interactions with both dsRNA and dsDNA via hydrogen bonds and salt bridges (Fig. 3E and F, S15A and Tables S1, S2). MD simulations were then used to evaluate the effect of TS25 on nucleic acid stability. In the absence of TS25, the nuclease gradually approached and contacted the DNA during the simulation (Fig. 3G and Movie S1). However, in the presence of TS25, the nuclease was unable to approach the DNA (Fig. 3H and Movie S2). Distance and hydrogen-bond analyses revealed that the nuclease-DNA distance increased over time and hydrogen bonds dropped to zero when TS25 was present (Fig. 3I and J). TS25 also markedly weakened the electrostatic interactions between the nuclease and DNA (Fig. S16). Collectively, these results demonstrate that assembled TS25 is more efficiently captured by the membrane and more readily internalized. Once inside the cell, TS25 binds strongly to nucleic acids and prevents nuclease access, thereby stabilizing intracellular nucleic acids (Fig. S15B).

The MD simulations provide mechanistic insights into why the assembled state of TS25 facilitates cellular entry. Specifically, this nanoassembly can be more efficiently captured by the membrane surface and induce stronger lipid lateral mobility, collectively lowering the energetic barrier for membrane penetration. Once internalized, TS25 also may contribute to antiviral activity by stabilizing intracellular nucleic acids. These nucleic acids may originate from virus-derived dsRNA or dsDNA produced during replication, or from mitochondrial stress-released nucleic acids (Fig. S17). By forming strong hydrogen-bond and salt-bridge interactions with dsRNA or dsDNA, TS25 effectively prevents nuclease access and preserves nucleic acid integrity. Similar peptide-nucleic-acid stabilization effects have been reported in previous studies on cationic or amphipathic peptides [39]. The stabilization of intracellular nucleic acids may have important immunological consequences. Cytosolic dsRNA and dsDNA are potent activators of RIG-I/MDA5-IRF3/7 and cGAS-STING pathways [40–42]. By preventing nucleic acid degradation, TS25 may prolong and amplify these innate immune signals, leading to sustained interferon (IFN) and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) induction. This mechanism aligns with the observed reduction in intracellular viral activity in BBTS25-treated cells and suggests that the superior antiviral efficacy arises from both enhanced cellular entry and the ability of TS25 to maintain immunostimulatory nucleic acid ligands within the cell.

Together, these findings highlight a antiviral mechanism in which the nanoassembly promotes efficient cell entry, while TS25 stabilizes intracellular nucleic acids, thereby potentially enhancing the downstream innate antiviral response.

In vitro mechanistic analysis

To elucidate the intracellular antiviral mechanism of BBTS25, we performed transcriptomic profiling using four experimental conditions: control, BBTS25 treatment, LMBV infection, and LMBV + BBTS25 co-treatment, each with three biological replicates. Principal component analysis (PCA) and sample correlation heatmaps revealed clear separation among the treatment groups while maintaining excellent intra-group consistency, confirming robust data quality and significant transcriptional alterations induced by the treatments (Fig. 4A and S18). Qualitative analysis further identified approximately 16,000 genes in each of the three comparison groups, providing a solid foundation for subsequent quantitative comparisons (Fig. 4B and S19). Differential expression analysis showed that BBTS25 vs. control resulted in 149 upregulated and 112 downregulated genes; LMBV vs. control produced 268 upregulated and 365 downregulated genes; and BBTS25 + LMBV vs. LMBV yielded 72 upregulated and 56 downregulated genes (Fig. 4C and S20). These significantly regulated genes were subsequently subjected to Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) enrichment analyses. For BBTS25 vs. control, GO analysis indicated that BBTS25 strongly activated “cellular defense response” and “innate immune response,” whereas transcription-related pathways were broadly suppressed (Fig. 4D). KEGG enrichment revealed marked activation of several canonical antiviral innate immune pathways, including the RIG-I-like receptor, Toll-like receptor, NOD-like receptor, and cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways (Fig. S21A). GSEA further demonstrated a pronounced induction of the type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling cascade (Fig. 4E). The genes contributing to these changes were enriched for IFN and ISGs production (Fig. 4F and S21B), and STRING analysis showed that many of them formed a coherent interaction network (Fig. 4G). Together, these data suggest that BBTS25 elicits a robust antiviral state that is associated with activation of the IFN-I axis and its downstream effector programs (Fig. 4H).

For LMBV vs. control, viral infection triggered extensive transcriptional reprogramming, enhancing multiple transcription-associated pathways while suppressing kinase activity (Fig. 4I). Notably, in BBTS25 + LMBV vs. LMBV, BBTS25 not only attenuated virus-induced transcriptional activation but also reinstated IFN-mediated innate immune responses (Fig. S22), suggesting that BBTS25 is capable of counteracting viral manipulation and restoring cellular immune homeostasis. Finally, qPCR validation confirmed these findings: key upstream IFN-I regulators (Dhx58, Sting, Stat1a, and Stat2) were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4J), and downstream ISGs (Mx, Lyg, Hamp, and Leap2) exhibited strong induction as well (Fig. 4K), consistent with the transcriptomic results. In addition, Western blot analysis revealed that BBTS25 markedly attenuated LMBV-induced NF-κB activation, as evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of p65 at Ser536 (Fig. S23), suggesting a potential role in limiting excessive inflammatory responses.

Although baicalin has been reported to activate components of the IFN-I pathway and exert antiviral effects [43], our quantitative data indicate that its activation potency is substantially weaker than that of TS25. Together with our molecular dynamics simulations, these findings suggest that BBTS25 primarily relies on TS25 to trigger the antiviral response. TS25 stabilizes cellular nucleic acids and may contribute to enhanced activation of IFN-I-associated antiviral programs and upregulation of downstream antiviral genes. In contrast, the predominant antiviral activity of baicalin is attributed to its anti-inflammatory properties, particularly the suppression of the NF-κB pathway [21]. TS25 appears to promote IFN-I-associated antiviral responses and may also contribute to NF-κB activation, thereby enhancing inflammatory signaling. Hyperinflammation can exacerbate tissue damage during viral infection, ultimately leading to worsening of the condition and even death [44, 45]. Some studies have shown that LMBV infection is associated with significant increases in various inflammatory cytokines [46, 47]. Within the BBTS25 nanocomplex, baicalin likely mitigates TS25- or LMBV-induced hyperinflammation, helping to restore immune balance and thereby enabling a more favorable antiviral outcome.

Importantly, the enhanced antiviral efficacy of BBTS25 should not be interpreted as the emergence of a new biological activity resulting from nanocomplex formation. Rather, BBTS25 functions as an assembly that integrates and potentiates the intrinsic biological activities of TS25 and baicalin through improved co-delivery, intracellular retention, and coordinated immunomodulation. Together, the integrated design leverages the immunomodulatory advantages of baicalin, and the nucleic-acid-binding properties of TS25, providing a promising platform for broad-spectrum antiviral interventions with balanced immune activation and low cytotoxicity.

In vivo antiviral efficacy and safety

To validate whether the strong antiviral activity observed in vitro translates into therapeutic benefit in vivo, we further evaluated the antiviral efficacy and biosafety of BBTS25 in a fish viral challenge model (Fig. 5A). An LMBV infection model was first established in largemouth bass juveniles, and an appropriate challenge dose was determined (Fig. 5B). Time-course and tissue-distribution analyses revealed that viral replication peaked in the liver on day 3 post-infection (Fig. S24), which was therefore selected as the key time point for subsequent efficacy studies. Assessment of antiviral activity showed that, compared with baicalin or TS25 alone, BBTS25 markedly reduced hepatic viral loads at day 3 (Fig. S25). Furthermore, BBTS25 administration improved survival by approximately 47% in the LMBV challenge model (Fig. 5C). After 14 days of treatment, BBTS25 reduced the whole-body viral burden by nearly 89% relative to the infected group (Fig. 5D). Given that BBTS25 activates the IFN-I axis, we examined its influence on the IFN pathway in vivo. Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, the key upstream regulator Irf3 and the downstream antiviral effector Hamp were significantly upregulated following BBTS25 treatment (Fig. 5E and F), supporting the conclusion that BBTS25 enhances antiviral immunity through IFN-I activation.

The biosafety of BBTS25 was systematically evaluated. No mortality was observed in either the BBTS25-treated or control groups during the 14-day observation period (Fig. 5G). Histological examination of the liver, spleen, brain, and intestine showed no detectable tissue damage, and intestinal villi at the administration site remained intact (Fig. 5H and I). In addition, the results of inflammatory factor detection showed that BBTS25 induced very low inflammatory changes in the liver compared to TS25(Fig. S26). Together, these results demonstrate that BBTS25 exhibits potent in vivo antiviral efficacy while maintaining an excellent safety profile.

Following LMBV infection, viral accumulation reaches its highest level in the liver, followed by the spleen. After intraperitoneal administration, drugs are primarily absorbed through the peritoneal villi and then enter the portal circulation, finally reaching the liver first before being distributed systemically to other organs [48]. Thus, intraperitoneal delivery provides an appropriate route to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of BBTS25 in the major target tissues of infection. In vivo data demonstrate that BBTS25 markedly reduces hepatic viral loads while inducing minimal inflammatory responses in liver tissue, highlighting both its potent antiviral activity and excellent biocompatibility. In contrast, TS25 elicits a significant inflammatory response. These results further demonstrate that baicalin in BBTS25 can effectively reduce the inflammatory response induced by TS25, thereby enhancing its antiviral effect. Therefore, these findings indicate that BBTS25 exhibits robust in vivo antiviral efficacy, and favorable biosafety, underscoring its strong potential for further development as a broad-spectrum antiviral therapeutic.

In vivo mechanistic insights

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the antiviral activity of BBTS25, we performed transcriptomic profiling of the spleen. PCA revealed clear separation among the treatment groups relative to the control, with tight clustering within replicates, indicating distinct transcriptional reprogramming induced by each treatment (Fig. 6A). Venn analysis identified approximately 20,000 genes shared across all comparisons, providing a robust foundation for subsequent quantitative analyses (Fig. S27). In the three different comparison groups, each group showed hundreds of significantly altered genes, with approximately 1000 genes showing significant variations in the BBTS25 vs. Control (Fig. 6B and Fig. S28). These DEG sets were subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. In the BBTS25 vs. Control comparison, GO terms associated with immune activation were significantly enriched (Fig. 6C). KEGG analysis revealed pronounced activation of key antiviral innate immune pathways, including the intestinal immune network for IgA production, the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, and the RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway (Fig. S29). Some key genes were associated with IFN-I production and immune-cell activation (Fig. 6D), supporting a model in which BBTS25 stimulates the IFN-I axis and mobilizes immune responses (Fig. 6E). To validate these transcriptomic findings, qPCR analysis of liver and spleen tissues revealed significant upregulation of IL-6R, MHC-II, and IFN-γ following BBTS25 administration, consistent with the transcriptomic trends(Fig. 6F, S30 and S31). In the LMBV vs. Control comparison, viral challenge induced sharp transcriptional shifts, broadly enhancing gene expression programs associated with transcriptional machinery while concurrently suppressing host immune pathways (Fig. S32). Strikingly, in the LMBV + BBTS25 vs. LMBV comparison, BBTS25 reversed this immune suppression, downregulating several ion-channel-related pathways while robustly reactivating host defense and immune-response pathways (Fig. S33).

Finally, to evaluate the cross-species conservation of BBTS25-induced immune activation, we examined transcript responses in murine dendritic cells (DC2.4) and macrophages (Ana-1). In DC2.4 cells, BBTS25 treatment significantly upregulated CD40, CD86, IL-6, and MHC-II (Fig. 6G). Similarly, BBTS25 strongly induced CD40, CD86, CD163, and MHC-II in Ana-1 macrophages (Fig. 6H). These results are consistent with the immune activation characteristics observed in fish tissues. Accordingly, these in vivo and cross-species data highlight BBTS25 as a potent activator of the interferon axis and immune-cell engagement, providing mechanistic insight into its broad-spectrum antiviral efficacy.

In normal cells, BBTS25 can activate the IFN-I pathway, thereby inducing the expression of antiviral ISGs. In immune cells, BBTS25 appears to activate dendritic cells and macrophages, and then promote the transcription of cytokines and maturation-related markers in association with IFN-I signaling, which may contribute to immune responses. Because bioactive peptides typically lack intrinsic immunogenicity [49], it was important to determine whether TS25 itself might be processed and presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). MHC-II epitope prediction demonstrated that TS25 contains no high-affinity binding epitopes (Tables S3-S5), suggesting that APCs do not present TS25. Instead, BBTS25 appears to activate these immune cells at the transcriptional level rather than through antigen presentation. Activated APCs can enhance immune responses by promoting type I IFN dependent T-cell activation or BAFF-mediated B-cell stimulation, ultimately supporting the expansion and functional priming of these lymphocyte populations [50–52]. In parallel, increased IFN-γ expression may further reinforce APC maturation and enhance the expression of key immunological markers [53].

Accordingly, these findings indicate that BBTS25 not only induces intrinsic antiviral programs but also enhances antiviral immunity by activating multiple immune cell populations in vivo, thereby contributing to its strong therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed a “drug-delivering-drug” antiviral nanocomplex, termed BBTS25, constructed through a directed self-assembly strategy between baicalin and TS25. The “drug-delivering-drug” nanocomplex markedly improves the cooperative antiviral performance of baicalin and TS25. In vitro, BBTS25 strongly suppresses viral replication, decreasing intracellular and supernatant viral loads by ~ 70% and ~ 80%, respectively, while impairing virion structural integrity. Mechanistically, BBTS25 promotes efficient entry into host cells, where TS25 stabilizes intracellular nucleic acids and is associated with enhanced IFN-I-related antiviral signaling and induction of downstream ISGs. Meanwhile, baicalin restores immune balance and promotes a more favorable antiviral response by mitigating the hyperinflammation caused by TS25 or the virus. In vivo, BBTS25 administration in a fish viral challenge model enhances survival by approximately 47% and demonstrates excellent biocompatibility. Furthermore, in vivo mechanistic analyses revealed that BBTS25 activates intrinsic antiviral programs and engages multiple immune cell populations, including dendritic cells and macrophages.

Overall, these findings highlight BBTS25 as a robust and broad-spectrum antiviral candidate whose efficacy derives from the integrated advantages of baicalin-mediated anti-inflammation and TS25-mediated nucleic acid stabilization, together with IFN-driven immune activation. This strategy provides important implications for future antiviral therapeutic development.
