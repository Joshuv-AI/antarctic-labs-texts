Addressing inequalities in the primary and secondary outpatient interface: A policy-focused evidence brief
Authors: Amy Dehn Lunn, Heidi Lynch, Bilaal Adam, Jenny Blythe, Emily Clark, Serge Amba'a Engamba, Payam Torabi, Adnaan Ghanchi, John Ford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Structured abstractThe policy challengeIn the UK, an estimated 15 million primary care appointments are used every year dealing with issues between primary and secondary care. A dysfunctional primary-secondary care interface impacts on patient experience, safety and staff workload, and is likely to widen health inequalities by disproportionately impacting underserved populations. Health care systems globally have implemented numerous initiatives to improve this interface. While the aim is often to increase efficiency, short-term efficiency gains may be outweighed by populations missing out on care and subsequently presenting late and acutely. Here we explore what works to address inequalities in the primary-secondary care interface.Key evidence to inform policyMost evidence describes the problem of inequalities at the primary-secondary care interface, rather than identifying solutions. We identified five points at which inequalities can arise, and action should be considered: 1) referral from primary care, 2) navigating the health and care system, 3) integration of care, 4) follow-up after initial appointment and 5) policies when patients miss appointments. Evidence exists for the use of care coordinators, co-location of services, culturally tailored services, and appointment reminder systems. Patient-initiated follow-up should be used with caution.Further considerations and implicationsPolicymakers should consider programmes that include support mechanisms to help attendance at specialist appointments, care coordinators, inclusive and culturally competent communication, flexibility, co-location where possible and using patient-initiated follow up on a case-by-case basis. Improved evaluation and monitoring are also needed to understand the current and evolving impact of health care interface policy across different communities.

Current challenges

The medical interface is described as “the point of interaction between different systems … where a patient journey crosses from one area of care into another such as moving between primary and secondary care, between health and social care and between scheduled and unscheduled care” [1]. The primary and secondary care outpatient interface is a two-way relationship that encompasses referrals (and re-referrals) from primary care to secondary and community care specialists; communication relating to patient care, screening and investigations; management of did-not-attends (DNAs); and medication management. Problems can arise at any point along the pathway.

Issues at the primary and secondary care outpatient interface have been recognised as an important problem for many years in countries where primary care providers are usually the first point of patient contact. These issues impact on patient experience of care, safety, and staff workload and morale. In the UK, managing issues regarding the interface between primary and secondary care is estimated to consume 15 million GP appointments annually [2]. Healthwatch England data suggests that one in five patients seeking specialist care found themselves in a ‘referral black hole’; referred to the wrong service, removed from a waiting list, had an appointment cancelled or lacking referral follow-up [2]. There are high levels of professional dissatisfaction amongst UK GPs around interface issues between primary and secondary care [3]. Inability to access timely specialist care can lead to worsening health and avoidable admissions and requests from secondary care for GPs to refer to other specialists can add delays and increase administrative burden [4]. Issues around the primary-secondary care interface are reported in Ireland, Australia and Canada, with communication challenges between primary and secondary care, and increasing waiting times for specialist provision.

Attempts to address some of the issues around the primary-secondary care interface have been implemented in the UK and internationally [5,6]. In many areas of the UK, Advice and Guidance (A&G) services have been introduced to help GPs access rapid advice from a specialist, however the A&G process can add barriers to referral or delays if specialists’ capacity is limited.

For disadvantaged population groups delayed specialist care increases rates of hospital presentation disproportionately compared to the general population, perpetuating health inequalities [7]. However, guidance to support improvements at the primary-secondary care interface generally has little reference to health inequalities. In this rapid review, we focus on how to address inequalities in the primary and secondary outpatient interface.

Approach to collating evidence

To develop evidence-informed recommendations for policymakers and practitioners, we identified the most relevant and robust evidence which explored what works to address inequalities at the interface between primary and secondary care. Based on an initial scope of the published evidence, we anticipated that there were unlikely to be any interventional studies examining specific programmes or interventions to reduce inequalities at the interface. Therefore, to generate useful evidence for policy makers, we identified studies which explored how and why inequalities arise at the interface between primary and secondary care and then used transferable, evidence-based principles to make policy and practice recommendations. Transferable evidence involves logically applying evidence-informed principles, where appropriate, from one area to another. It helps policy makers and practitioners make evidence-informed decisions and avoid evidence reviews with an absence of research.

We used Living Evidence Maps developed by the Health Equity Evidence Centre to identify relevant studies. We then undertook snowballing to identify related articles and a grey literature search. We focused on evidence which explored inequalities across socio-economic groups and drew upon transferable principles identified through existing reviews. In total, we prioritised 23 studies drawn from published and grey literature.

Key evidence to inform policy

Evidence assessing how interventions at the interface between primary and secondary care impact inequalities is limited. However, there is a body of evidence which describes the way in which inequalities arise across the interface and evidence-informed principles which can guide policymakers to address these inequalities. Equity action point 1: Referral from primary care

Patients from the most deprived areas have a higher rate of outpatient appointments than patients from the least deprived areas [8]; reflecting a higher burden of ill health amongst patients from lower socioeconomic backgrounds. Programmes to improve referral patterns from primary care include the use of locally designed pathways, decision-support tools and advice and guidance from secondary care clinicians without formal referral. There is relatively little evidence on the impact of these schemes on health inequalities.

A service development project aimed to improve the quality and effectiveness of secondary care referrals made by GPs in Wales [9]. GPs from ten of thirteen practices with high rates of multi-morbidity and deprivation participated in regular discussions with local hospital specialists to assess the appropriateness of referrals and compatibility with local guidelines. Overall referral rates fell from 5.5 to 4.3 (per 1000 patients per quarter) and variation in individual GP referral rates also narrowed (from 2.6 to 7.7 to 3.0-6.5 per 1000 patients per quarter). GPs’ self-assessment of referral quality also improved on average from 89% to 95%.

A before-and-after study in Brazil assessed the impact of introducing E-consultations between the referring GP and an external medical attendant auditing the referral [10]. Conversations aimed to define the need, triage category and most appropriate destination of the referral or support clinicians to manage cases in primary care. The integration of telehealth into referral management systems reduced the number of cases waiting for specialised care between January 2019 and February 2020 across three socioeconomically disadvantaged regions by 89%, 77% and 55%. The median waiting time was also reduced.

Equity action point 2: Navigating the system

As a result of the pandemic, access to planned care fell more drastically in the most deprived areas of the UK compared to the least deprived [11]. The King's Fund identified that a waiting time of 12 months or more was twice as common for patients living in the most deprived parts of England compared to those in the least deprived areas [12]. Length of time spent on an outpatient waiting list was linked with worse outcomes. Kulkarni et al. evaluated the impact of deprivation for patients on lengthy waiting lists, identifying that those patients in the most deprived areas reported longer symptom duration, more severe pain, lower quality of life, poorer overall health and lower activity levels [11].

Care coordinators support people to navigate the health and care system, providing a point of continuity and support for patients across services, particularly for patients with multiple long-term conditions, and those living with frailty [13]. They have been found to have positive impacts on health outcomes, including improved mental health symptoms, reduced nursing home admissions, reduced rehospitalisation rates and improved quality of life measures [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. There is little evidence examining targeted use of care coordination to support people experiencing health inequalities. A scoping review exploring access to health care for people experiencing homelessness in the UK and Ireland highlighted barriers in the referral process, discharge planning, fragmentation of services and poor communication with the use of care coordinators, such as specialist nurses, showing positive impacts [18].

Equity action point 3: Co-location and integration of care

Co-location of primary and specialist care may improve integration of health care. A systematic review and meta-analysis found mixed results for the impact of placing specialty care alongside primary care [19]. Meta-analysis of three randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comprising over 2000 patients demonstrated significant improvements in patient satisfaction with co-location of behavioural and mental health services with primary care (OR 3.16, 95% CI 1.49 to 6.68). Meta-analysis results of RCTs found mixed results for the impact of co-location on depression outcomes. A single cross-sectional study involving 813 patients with HIV attending integrated infectious disease and primary care services did not demonstrate any increase in service uptake associated with co-location [20].

A systematic review examining the efficacy and cultural acceptability of integrated care for health care delivery to indigenous Americans found increased continuity of care, improved cultural acceptability of care, easier access to mental health services and reduced rates of depression [21]. An Australian systematic review evaluated integrated specialist and primary care services, including 10 studies [22]. Of the total 7697 patients included in the review, those described as being from disadvantaged population groups were 2495 patients in a semi-rural region and 40 Aboriginal Australians. The authors found that integrated primary-secondary care, compared to usual care, can improve elements of disease control and service delivery at a modestly increased cost, although the reported impact on clinical outcomes was limited. A further review of an integrated diabetes service involving specialist outreach for 124 Aboriginal Australians across three remote communities demonstrated improvement in baseline HbA1c and total cholesterol [23].

Equity action point 4: Follow-up

Patient-initiated follow-up (PIFU) has been proposed as a mechanism of matching available services with patient need [24]. By allowing patients to schedule their care as and when they deem necessary, PIFU intends to reduce the incidence of missed, cancelled and unnecessary appointments. Health care systems are implementing PIFU to increase capacity in outpatients [25]. A rapid evidence review conducted by the Nuffield Trust revealed that there is very little existing research on how PIFU may impact health inequalities [24]. Of the 15 studies which examined PIFU, most focused on low-risk patients with higher levels of agency to initiate contact. Eight showed a statistically significant reduction in outpatient appointments compared to fixed follow-up and seven showed no difference. There was little or no effect on clinical outcomes. Previous research suggests that services which require more patient effort are more likely to increase inequalities as those with high health literacy and more resources are more likely to benefit [26].

Equity action point 5: Missed appointments

Did-not-attend (DNA) rates are disproportionately high in lower socioeconomic status groups, with patients in the lowest two deprivation quintiles being the most likely to DNA appointments [[27], [28], [29]]. Elderly patients, those with mental illness and minority ethnic groups also demonstrate higher rates of DNAs [27]. Data from Scotland shows that patients who miss GP appointments are more likely to miss hospital appointments and be higher users of inpatient and outpatient hospital care, but not emergency department care [30].

A community mental health team in Enfield, the 64th most deprived local authority district in the UK, implemented the use of text message reminders to reduce DNA rates [31]. Overall clinic DNA rates reduced from 11.4% to 10.6% during a six-month period with non-attendance in medical specialities improving most significantly from 17.7% to 11.8%. While multiple studies support the use of text message reminders to mitigate non-attendance rates across health care, most do not disaggregate results by population groups [27]. A large retrospective and prospective study examined the implementation of text message reminders at a new patient breast clinic in northwest England and found reduced DNA rates from 8.2% to 4.1% (p < 0.001) during a 15 month trial period [32]. However, median age of non-attending patients rose between the pre-intervention and post-intervention cycle from 38 years-old to 47 years-old (p < 0.001), indicating the intervention was less effective in older patients.

In their literature review of the causes, impacts and methods to mitigate DNA rates, Wilson et al. conclude that reducing non-attendance rates equitably will not be achieved by a single standard approach [27]. Letter prompts to improve clinic attendance were trialled by a community mental health clinic in Leeds covering 64,000 patients from predominantly deprived backgrounds [33]. 1433 brief paper prompts were distributed to all patients with scheduled appointments over six months. Non-attendance dropped to 17% during the trial period, a statistically significant reduction in comparison to clinic DNA rates of 26%. Reminder calls proved effective at reducing DNA rates in a randomised controlled trial involving 393 patients, however DNAs from patients from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds remained higher than their affluent counterparts (61% versus 46%) [34].

Further considerations and implications

Limitations

There is a lack of research examining interventions to reduce inequalities at the primary-secondary care interface. While a body of literature exists describing the problem of inequalities at the interface, there is a lack of research of what to do about these inequalities. We have not focussed on the interface between public and private healthcare provision as there is even less published on this topic, however it is likely to widen inequalities and an area for further research. The rapid nature of this review and the breadth of the topic area means that it is possible that relevant studies were missed, however the aim of this review was to produce evidence-informed recommendations for policy makers rather than undertaking a comprehensive review of all studies published.

Key recommendations

Interface inequalities are particularly important in countries where primary care is the first point of contact for patients and referral from primary care is necessary to access specialist services. We have developed recommendations based on the prioritised data, in addition to evidence-based guiding principles to address health and care inequalities. Given the relative lack of evidence-based interventions to reduce inequalities at the primary-secondary care interface, we have also drawn on the broader principles of the EQUALISE study to inform these recommendations [26].•Improving equity of referrals from primary care: At the point of referral from primary care, clinicians should discuss with patients barriers to attendance including signposting to potential financial support. Referrals should highlight patients' communication needs (e.g. language, digital exclusion) and potential barriers to attendance, such as caring responsibilities, lack of flexibility around work patterns, transport issues.•Helping patients from disadvantaged backgrounds navigate the health care system: Targeted care coordination should be considered to support patients and carers. When patients are waiting for elective care, providers should deliver inclusive, culturally-competent communication to keep patients up-to-date.•Integrating care to address inequalities: Patients facing challenging social circumstances should be offered flexibility around appointment modes (e.g. face-to-face/remote) and timings (e.g. evenings/weekends). Where possible, health systems should co-locate services and diagnostics to reduce travel barriers with better integration of care prioritised to areas of high socioeconomic deprivation.•Reducing inequalities in follow-up: Patient-initiated follow-up should be implemented with caution because it is likely that people from higher socioeconomic groups will use it more. Where used, it should be on a case-by-case basis involving shared decision-making between clinician and patient.•Avoiding inequalities when patients miss appointments: Health care providers should use targeted appointment reminders, such as text message, letter and phone call reminders. Patients who miss appointments should be contacted to explore barriers and offer support, rather than asking their primary care practice to re-refer.
