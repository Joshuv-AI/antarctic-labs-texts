Association between prenatal vitamin D supplementation and respiratory diseases in children: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jieru Shen, Zhonghui Zhou, Fao Feng, Yu Zhang, Sayu Bao, Yangrong Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmunomodulation and respiratory health have a critical role in the offspring well-being. Maternal status of vitamin D during pregnancy seems to have immunomodulatory and respiratory protective effects in children. However, evidence regarding respiratory and allergy-related outcomes remains conflicting. Therefore, this study aimed to synthesize the available data in this regard to provide a firm direction.MethodsComprehensive searches were made in the databases of PubMed, Scopus, the Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library until August 2025. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to evaluate the effects of maternal intake of vitamin D during pregnancy on outcomes in children with respiratory and allergy conditions. Also, the quality of evidence was evaluated using GRADE method and the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool was used to evaluate study quality.ResultsSeven RCTs were included. Prenatal supplementation of vitamin D did not affect respiratory outcomes in the children who followed: RTI (RR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.99, 1.09; P = 0.122, I2 = 0.0%), asthma (RR: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.67, 1.31; P = 0.701, I2 = 64.9%), and wheezing (RR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.62, 1.09; P = 0.176, I2 = 0.0%). Similarly, vitamin D supplementation in pregnant women was associated with non-significant effects on eczema (RR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.83, 1.10; P = 0.524, I2 = 0.0%), allergic condition (RR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.85, 1.06; P = 0.324, I2 = 0.0%), and immunoglobulin E (IgE) (RR: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.73, 1.21; P = 0.639, I2 = 52.6%) in children.ConclusionOverall, the vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy has no significant effect on respiratory and allergy-related outcomes in children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06268-2.

Introduction

Childhood respiratory and allergy conditions, affects approximately 30% of children in the worldwide [1]. Respiratory and allergy conditions such as respiratory tract infection (RTI), asthma, wheezing, eczema, allergy positivity and immunoglobulin E (IgE) pose a global challenge [2, 3]. The very rapid upward trend requires effective strategies in early childhood. Evidence highlights the importance of the intrauterine period in the development of respiratory and immune systems [4, 5]. Accordingly, prenatal nutrition as a modifiable factor seems to have critical role in predicting health outcomes [6]. In this context, vitamin D has emerged as a nutrient of particular scientific interest.

In the past, vitamin D had been known for its beneficial role in the calcium balance and bone health [7–9]. While, now it is known to have a broad range of immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects [10–12]. Previous studies indicate that vitamin D may have immunomodulatory properties in the development of lungs too [13, 14]. Vitamin D insufficiency seems to be implicated in the immune reaction.

Children born to mothers with vitamin D deficiency are more susceptible to developing respiratory infections, wheezing, allergic sensitization [15]. However, the exact causal relationship between respiratory disorders and vitamin D are unknown. To this end several randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have been conducted to determine the efficacy of prenatal vitamin D to improve respiratory and allergy-associated outcomes [16–18]. Accordingly, several studies with various population, supplementation dosage, and duration make difficult to interpret the results. Likewise, some studies address favorable effects for vitamin D, while some reported null-effects, and it is difficult to determine the exact direction of the prenatal vitamin D in this regard.

The conflicting results led us to adopt a more robust methodological approach to investigate the sources of heterogeneity. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is therefore to provide a comprehensive and robust evaluation of the available evidence to determine whether prenatal vitamin D supplementation has a beneficial effect on respiratory and allergy outcomes in children.

Methods

This research adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) standards [19]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Registry of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) as CRD420251132286.

Eligibility criteria

In this study we included RCTs studies evaluating the effect of prenatal vitamin D supplementation vs. placebo, or no intervention group on respiratory and allergy-related outcomes in children. Studies involving pregnant women across all age groups that evaluating the effect of vitamin D supplementation in offspring were included. The outcomes assessed in this study included RTI, asthma, wheezing, eczema, allergy and IgE. While, we excluded observational, quasi-experimental, animal studies, as well as, studies without placebo group and relevant outcomes.

Study selection

A comprehensive systematic search was done in PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library and Web of Science databases from inception to August 2025. We also manually screened references of included studies and relevant reviews. No language limitation was applied. EndNote 21 was used as a reference manager to remove duplicate and screening process. Two researchers screened the title and abstracts and full-texts for evaluation of study eligibility independently. Any disagreements were resolved with third researcher. The study selection process was performed using a PRISMA flow diagram. Full search strategies are detailed in the Supplementary Table 1.

Data extraction

Accordingly, two independent researchers extracted the following data from each study: first author, publication year, study design, sample size, gestational age, vitamin D supplementation dosage, and follow-up duration. Also, mean and standard deviation (SD) for pre and post intervention measurements in both treatment and placebo groups were extracted for RTI, asthma, wheezing, eczema, allergy, and IgE values.

Risk of bias and Meta-Evidence assessment

Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool was used to assess the publication bias including randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. Each domain was rated as having high risk, some concerns, or low risk. In addition, the certainty of evidence was evaluated using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach involving risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The quality assessment process was completed by two independent researchers and any conflicts were consult with third researcher.

Statistical analysis

Pooled effect estimates were obtained random-effect model and the pooled estimates were expressed as risk ratios (RRs). Heterogeneity of studies were assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. In studies including multiple control groups with different doses of vitamin D, each group was treated as a separate trial to allow a more comprehensive evaluation of the overall effect. Subgroup analyses were done based on type of treatment. Also, sensitivity analyses were carried out for all study outcomes using leave-one-out method. In addition, publication bias was examined using Egger’s and Begg’s tests, and funnel plot was generated for RTI outcome which included more than 10 studies.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The comprehensive literature searching identified 836 studies form predefined databases. After removing 451 duplicates, 418 studies were excluded based on title and abstract screening. Likewise, 43 studies were evaluated by full-text, of which 36 publications were excluded by reasons such as: failure to meet the specified inclusion criteria, incompatible study design, or data duplication. Finally, 7 studies were aligned with our study inclusion criteria and included in the present meta-analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1The screening flowchart

Vitamin D on RTI

Seven studies comprising 13 arms evaluated the effects of maternal vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy period on RTI incidence in children. The pooled analyses showed that prenatal vitamin D supplementation did not have a significant effect on the incidence of RTI in children (RR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.99, 1.09; P = 0.122, I2 = 0.0%, P-heterogeneity = 0.562) (Fig. 2). Subgroup analyses based on the type of control group indicated that vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy, when compared specifically to placebo, was associated with a higher incidence of RTI (P = 0.023) (Table 4). Sensitivity analyses using leave-one-out approach demonstrated that no single study had a significant effect on pooled estimate (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, Egger’s and Begg’s tests found no evidence of publication bias (P = 0.161, 0.078 respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 2).Fig. 2The forest plot for RTI

Vitamin D on asthma

Two studies evaluated the effect of vitamin D supplementation in pregnant women on the incidence of asthma in children compared with control group, showed no significant association between prenatal vitamin D supplementation and asthma in children (RR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.52, 1.99; P = 0.971, I2 = 0.0%, P-heterogeneity = 0.478). Also, three studies compared vitamin D supplementation with basic treatment plus placebo yielded a non-significant effect on asthma incidence (RR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.60, 1.40; P = 0.695, I2 = 81.5%, P-heterogeneity = 0.004) (Fig. 3). Robustness of results were confirmed by sensitivity analyses, suggesting that no single study affected the pooled findings.Fig. 3The forest plot for asthma

Vitamin D on wheezing

Two studies evaluated the effect of vitamin D supplementation in pregnant women on the incidence of wheezing in children compared with control group, showed no significant association between prenatal vitamin D supplementation and wheezing in children (RR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.55, 1.38; P = 0.550, I2 = 0.0%, P-heterogeneity = 0.413). In addition, one study compared vitamin D supplementation with basic treatment plus placebo indicated a non-significant effect on RTI incidence (RR: 0.80; 95% CI: 0.56, 1.14; P = 0.213 (Fig. 4). Vitamin D on eczema

The combined effect of two studies compared the vitamin D supplementation versus control group in offspring and reported no significant effect for vitamin D supplementation on eczema in children (RR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.63, 1.47; P = 0.864, I2 = 0.0%, P-heterogeneity = 0.792). Similarly, three studies assessed the effect of vitamin D vs. basic treatment with placebo and found no significant association for eczema incidence (RR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.82, 1.11; P = 0.538, I2 = 0.0%, P-heterogeneity = 0.842) (Fig. 5). Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that prenatal vitamin D was unsuccessful to affect the respiratory and allergy related outcomes in children including RTI, asthma, wheezing, Eczema, allergy, IgE significantly. In addition, subgroup analyses further indicated that vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy, when compared specifically to placebo, was associated with a higher incidence of RTI.

Pooled analyses of 13 RCTs demonstrated that prenatal vitamin D was not associated with statistically significant changes in the risk of RTI in children and the CI does not include thresholds considered clinically meaningful, suggesting any true effect is likely small. Likewise, available body of evidence does not support the protective role of prenatal vitamin D supplementation against childhood RTIs. It is worth noting that, the results of publication bias using Egger’s and Begg’s tests were non-significant which suggests the robustness of results. In line with our meta-analyses results which included three high quality studies [17–22], Ganmaa et al. demonstrated that vitamin D supplementation had no significant effects on the risk of acute respiratory infections and tuberculosis in children [25]. However, a systematic review demonstrated that baseline status of vitamin D affecting the infection risk [26]. Experimental and clinical work has proposed that vitamin D may influence respiratory outcomes by supporting alveolar maturation, promoting vascular growth, and modulating inflammatory pathways, potentially lowering the risk of bronchopulmonary dysplasia and respiratory distress syndrome [27]. In addition, it has been shown that reduced expression of surfactant-associated proteins and increased synthesis of collagen in vitamin D deficient animal models are associated with respiratory function inversely [28, 29]. Accordingly, vitamin D deficiency may contribute to reduced alveolar density, thickened airway muscle, and narrowed tracheal diameter, which may reduce lung compliance, and elevate the risk of respiratory diseases [30, 31]. Also, vitamin D deficiency may trigger immune developments through elevated neutrophil and eosinophil activity in the respiratory system [32]. In addition, prenatal vitamin D may not provide effective immunomodulatory benefits alone to cause a decline in RTI incidence in offspring. Although prenatal vitamin D supplementation can usually maintain adequate maternal levels, current evidence suggests it offers little protection against RTI in children. However, the variability in supplementation dosage and duration of vitamin D status may have contributed to the inconsistency in the results across studies. Moreover, RTI incidence may be affected by several confounding factors such as genetic susceptibility, and environmental exposures which could overshadows any effect of prenatal vitamin D level.

Also, prenatal exposure to vitamin D was not effective to reduce the risk of asthma significantly. However, it seems that clinically important effect cannot be ruled out. Moreover, I2 indicates substantial heterogeneity, confirmed by the wide CI and likely opposite effect directions across studies. It seems that prediction interval would span both benefit and harm. This finding is consistent with previous studies. Vitamin D has been known for immunomodulatory effects through regulating T-helper (Th1/Th2) balance, promoting regulatory T-cell development. However, the asthma is a multifactorial health condition, and is affected by several factors. In this context, postnatal vitamin D may play a crucial role to exert beneficial effects on the risk of asthma. However, previous studies have demonstrated that maternal vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy contribute to increased level of vitamin D in cord blood. While it decreases within first year postpartum [18]. The probable mechanism for elevated maternal vitamin D levels is attributed to increased CYP27B1 activity. This enzyme and vitamin D receptor are expressed by both decidual and trophoblast cells which suggesting the immunomodulatory properties of vitamin D [33]. Likewise, it seems that post term vitamin D supplementation may have regulatory effects on wheezing and asthma prevention [33, 34]. Nonetheless, the baseline maternal level of vitamin D appears to have an important effect on the observed outcomes related to asthma. Evidence suggests that vitamin D supplementation may have more pronounced effect in asthma among vitamin D deficient population, while it may be less evident in mothers with normal level of vitamin D.

Moreover, wheezing as a respiratory-related outcome was not associated with the prenatal vitamin D in our pooled analyses in the children. Although the findings are inconclusive, the CIs still allows for the possibility of a clinically meaningful benefit. The limited number of studies should be taken into consideration when evaluating publication bias. Wheezing is a frequent feature of early asthma and may present as either a temporary or persistent condition. Its expression is strongly shaped by environmental factors such as tobacco smoke, air pollution, and viral infections, which may obscure any potential effect of prenatal vitamin D. In addition to respiratory outcomes, allergic values such as eczema, allergy, and IgE were not affected by prenatal vitamin D exposure among offspring. These findings indicate that prenatal exposure of vitamin D may not be considered as a preventive strategy on the development of atopic diseases in childhood. Nevertheless, in clinical view, the substantial clinically meaningful effects are unlikely too. Allergen exposure may induce immune responses and inflammatory signaling pathways which affect the respiratory health. It has been suggested that allergic responses are mediated by a type 2 helper T cell (Th2)-driven immune reaction [35, 36].

Overall, the results of RTI, eczema, and allergic conditions, were consistent, with narrow CIs near the null effect with lower I², suggesting a little heterogeneity across included studies. For asthma and IgE positivity, the wide CIs, substantial I² level, and opposing study effects indicated inconsistency; prediction intervals would likely span both benefit and harm, which reduce certainty.

The strengths of this study are: (1) a systematic review and meta-analysis of the relationship between vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy and different outcomes, providing a reliable assessment based on the available data; (2) the inclusion of multiple RTI studies, which improves the robustness of the findings, which reduces bias and makes them more generalizable. However, there are limitations to this study. First, the generalizability of the results may be limited by the high variability of some results, which may be due to differences in the characteristics of the subjects, the vitamin D baseline level, and the dose of supplementation in the different studies. Secondly, the short follow-up periods included in the studies may not adequately reflect the long-term effects of the supplementation. Furthermore, for results with a relatively low incidence, e.g., asthma and some allergic conditions, the statistical power of the pooled data (seven studies) may be limited. Likewise, the results should be interpreted with caution. In addition, lower number of included studies for asthma, wheezing, eczema, allergy, and Ig E, limited the publication bias analyses using egger’s and Begg’s tests. Moreover, the timing of vitamin D supplementation in pregnancy was not clearly specified in the included studies which represents a limitation affecting the interpretation of the results.

Conclusion

Based on the results of this systematic review of clinical studies, vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy did not effectively reduce the incidence of respiratory infections and allergy-related outcomes in children.
