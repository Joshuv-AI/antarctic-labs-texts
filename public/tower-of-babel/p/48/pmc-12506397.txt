Multi-clade co-evolution and differential replication efficiencies of subgroup A avian leukosis virus in Chinese guinea fowl
Authors: Jian Chen, Xiyue Li, Qi Liu, Minghui Li, Zuhua Yu, Songbiao Chen, Lei He, Ke Ding, Ying Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvian leukosis virus (ALV) has a wide range of hosts and is Susceptible to commercial chickens, local chickens and rare birds. To assess the presence of ALV-A infection in guinea fowl and characterize its viral genetics, PCR testing of guinea fowl embryosfrom four provinces revealed an ALV-A positive rate of 68.1% (32/47) in China. Furthermore, phylogenetic analysis of the 15 ALV-A gp85 sequences obtained from guinea fowl-fertilized chicken embryos revealed four evolutionary clades (1, 2, 3, 4). Notably, no clade 1 isolates were detected in this study. Comparative sequence analysis demonstrated that amino acid variations among different clades primarily localized to host range determinant regions 1 and 2 (hr1 and hr2) of the gp85 protein. To systematically investigate the replicative potential of ALV-A across distinct evolutionary clades, envelope genes from three representative strains: HN23A16 (clade2), HN24Axx (clade4), and HN23A09 (clade3) were cloned into the RCASBP (replication-competent avian sarcoma-leukosis virus) retroviral vector to generate recombinant ALV-A viruses. Binding affinity between ALV-A gp85 (surface glycoprotein) and the chicken TVA receptor (the receptor of ALV-A, a protein of the low-density lipoprotein receptor family) was subsequently validated through co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays. Comparative analysis of replication kinetics revealed marked differences among clades. Both HN23A16 (clade2) and HN23A09 (clade3) exhibited robust replication efficiency in vitro and in vivo, whereas HN24Axx (clade4) showed significantly reduced replicative capacity. Co-IP quantification demonstrated that the HN24Axx gp85 variant bound to TVA with 3.3-fold lower affinity compared to clade 2 and 3 counterparts. Here, we report for the first time the viral genetic characteristics of 15 ALV-A strains from Chinese guinea fowl, and the proposed classification method will facilitate future studies of ALV-A epidemiology and the comparison of sequences obtained.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05043-w.Keywords: ALV-A, Gp85, TVA, Replication efficiencies, Binding affinity

Introduction

Avian leukosis virus (ALV) causes not only tumours and death but also subclinical symptoms in chickens, including immune suppression and growth inhibition, that are responsible for massive losses in poultry industries worldwide [1, 2]. Similar to other retroviruses, ALV is composed of 5’LTR (Long Terminal Repeat)−5’UTR (Untranslated Regions)-gag (Group-specific Antigen)-pol (Polymerase)-env (Envelope)−3’UTR-3’LTR, where gag, pol and env are the three structural proteins of ALV. Env is a combination of surface glycoprotein (SU, gp85) and transmembrane glycoprotein (TM, gp37) proteins; gp85 is exposed to the cell or virus surface, whereas gp37 is anchored in the cell or viral membrane in ALV infected cells or in viral particles. ALV consists of 11 subgroups (A ~ K), distinguished by viral glycoprotein gp85s, whose sequence could be divided into three variable regions (vr1, vr2, and vr3) and two host range regions (hr1 and hr2); ALVs recognize cell receptors through the interaction of gp85 hr1 and hr2 with the host cell surface receptor [3]. In addition, gp85 vr3 plays an important role in coordinating host range regions binding to the host receptors [4], and may participate in the binding of ALV gp85 [5, 6].

Both coding and non-coding regions of ALV genome affect its replication ability. The promoter activity of LTR has a significant effect on the replication ability of ALV. The LTR from ALV-J can enhance the replication ability of ALV-K, and LTR from ALV-K can reduce the replication ability of ALV-J [7, 8]. 3’UTRs directly affect ALV tissue preference and can promote virus replication as an enhancer, and ALV-J containing 3’UTR of △-r-TM proliferated fastest in primary cells [9]. In the coding area of ALV, an 8-nt deletion (positions 32–39) in the pol gene enhanced reverse transcriptase activity, which conferred greater replication ability, shedding, and vertical transmission to ALV-K [10]. The gp85 affects ALV replication mainly through its binding affinity to cell receptors. Mutations in key amino acids of the same subgroup of ALV gp85 proteins, especially hr1 and hr2, may have a key impact on viral replication [11, 12]. In addition, C terminus and the tyrosine motifs [Tyrosine-X-X-Methionine motif (YxxM), Immunoreceptor Tyrosine-based Inhibition Motif (ITIM), and Immunoreceptor Tyrosine-based Activation Motif-like (ITAM-like)] in the C-terminal cytoplasmic domain (CTD) of gp37 of ALV-J could affect the pathogenicity of ALV-J in vitro and in vivo [13]. However, the molecular evolution of coding and non-coding regions in ALV-A and its impact on viral replication capacity remain uncharacterized.

Of all subgroups, ALV-J remains the most common in chicken flocks in China [14, 15], although ALV-K is also widespread in local chicken breeds in China [16], and the strong recombinant strain is worthy of veterinary attention [17]. By contrast, ALV-A, the earliest subgroup identified, is not widely detected in chicken flocks in China, seems to occur only sporadically in poultry in the country, and is primarily reported in wild birds and live vaccines[18 ~ 20]. With the purification of AL in poultry-breeding farms in China, the prevalence of ALV in commercial chickens has been controlled to a certain extent, but the increasingly severe phenomenon of co-infection with multiple pathogens and the prevalence of ALV in local chickens and rare birds in China should also be examined [21]. Research on ALV in China’s rare avian species remains limited. This study aims to investigate ALV occurrence in rare birds, including pheasants and guinea fowl, by detecting the virus in fertilized embryos. We will characterize the molecular epidemiology (viral subtypes, genetic features) and assess the pathogenicity of isolated strains in commercial laying hens. These findings will elucidate the genetic evolution and pathogenic potential of ALV within China’s rare bird populations, providing a scientific foundation for developing AL control strategies.

Materials and methods

Extraction of whole genome DNA from fertilized chicken embryo of guinea fowl

To understand the prevalence of ALV in guinea fowl, in our study, we prepared fibroblast cell Lines from guinea fowl embryos fertilised for 9 d from Jiangsu, Hebei, Guangdong and Henan provinces. The prepared fibroblast cell Lines were cultured until Day 7, at which point the cells were harvested for whole-genome DNA (Omega Bio-Tek, Doraville, GA, USA) to be extracted, and the cell supernatant was collected and stored at −20 ℃ for future use.

Env sequencing, homology analysis, multiple sequence alignment and spatial structure analysis

The primers shown in Table 1 were used to detect ALV and amplify env fragments. 25 µL of 2×Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 1 µL each of the forward primers and reverse primers, 1 µL of total DNA from guinea fowl embryos cells, and ddH2O up to 50 µL. The PCR amplification procedure of env gene is as follows: 95 °C for 5 min, then to 95 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, in 35 cycles, followed by 8 min at 72 °C. Amplicons of the env gene were recovered using a StarPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (GenStar, Beijing, China) and ligated into the pMD-19T vector (Takara, Tokyo, Japan), and individual clones were selected for gene sequencing (Table 1). To explore the evolutionary branches of ALV-A, a Bayesian phylogenetic approach was adopted. All sequences were aligned using MEGA 11. Bayesian inference through a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) framework was implemented in MrBayes version 3.2.7. The appropriate nucleotide Substitution model was selected using the IQ-TREE 3.0.1 software according to the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Each MCMC run consisted of 2 million steps, with sampling conducted every 5,000 steps. The phylogenetic tree was visualized in iTOL v7 (https://itol.embl.de/). The MUSCLE multiple alignment and Neighbor-joining trees were constructed with the MEGA11. The online software TVBOT (https://www.chiplot.online/tvbot.html) was used to modify the phylogenetic tree of ALV gp85 [22]. The gp85 sequences of 94 ALV-A isolates were analysed using multiple sequence alignment in CLUSTALW (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw) and presented via Jalview (version 2.11.3). SWISS-MODEL online website (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive) was used to build HN23A16 (clade2), HN24Axx (clade4), and HN23A09 (clade3) gp85 space structure of homologous MODEL.

Construction of recombinant virus and analysis of virus replication ability in vitro

In order to study the effects of different gp85 amino acid sequences on ALV-A replication, a RCASBP vector was used as the skeleton and ALV-A isolates with different clades (HN23A09, HN23A16, HN24Axx, whose gp37 were in the same cluster) were selected to construct recombinant RCASBP(A)-EGFP plasmids carrying different ALV-A env genes, as previously described [6, 23]. Then the RCASBP(A)-EGFP vectors were transfected into DF-1 cells, the virus was saved and performed three successive generations, and the cell supernatant was collected and stored at −80℃. The Successfully rescued recombinant ALV-A was uniformly quantified to a virus titer of 103.8 TCID50, and the DF-1 cells were re-infected to compare the effect of gp85 gene on the replication ability of ALV-A in different isolates according p27 ELISA (Guangdongbiaoyun, Xinxing, China) and qPCR (Table 1).

Analysis of virus replication ability in vivo

To further analyze the effect of gp85 gene on the replication ability of different ALV-A isolates in vivo, Twenty-eight 2-day-old Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) chickens were used for animal experiments (Animal Welfare and Ethics Code: HAUST-025-P0310017) in the Experimental Animal Center, Henan University of Science and Technology. Twenty-eight chickens were assigned to four groups (n = 7 per group). Three experimental groups received intraperitoneal injections of 0.2 mL recombinant ALV-A strains (103.8 TCID50/mL) HN23A09, HN23A16 or HN24Axx, respectively. The remaining group served as PBS-injected controls. At 63 dpi, total RNA was extracted from homogenized tissues (thymus, bursa of Fabricius, spleen, liver, and brain) of SPF chickens that were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by exsanguination. Following RNA extraction, reverse transcription (500 ng RNA) and qPCR targeting ALV-A gp85 were performed with 10 µL total volume contained: 5 µL of 2×ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech), 0.5 µL primers (10 µM each), 1 µL cDNA, 3 µL ddH2O. Cycling: 95 °C 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 °C 5 s, 60 °C 30 s, with viral loads were quantified via standard curve.

Analysis of binding affinity of ALV-A gp85 variants to chicken TVA receptor

In order to analyze the reasons for the differences in the replication ability of the 3 ALV-A isolates, the gp85 sequences of the 3 ALV-A isolates were synthesized with a signal peptide added to the N-terminal and a flag tag added to the C-terminal. Since chicken TVA (the receptor of ALV-A, a protein of the low-density lipoprotein receptor family) has its own signal peptide, only the HA label is added at the C-end. Gp85 and tva were each connected to their own pCAGGS vectors to construct eukaryotic expression plasmids for achieving soluble expression of gp85 and TVA. Five µg of pCAGGS-s-gp85-flag or pCAGGS-TVA-HA-FC were transfected into 6-well plates with 293 T cells. Twenty-four hours later, the cells transfected with different plasmids were cleaved with NP-40, and then mixed with protein A/G agarose beads for 6 ~ 8 h at 4℃ to perform co-IP tests to directly evaluate the binding affinity of ALV-A gp85s to chicken TVA. Precooled PBS washed protein A/G agarose beads for 5 times, and western blotting detected the expression of flag and HA (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) labeled proteins.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0.2). Two-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests (Dunnett’s test for comparisons against a control group; Tukey’s test for multiple pairwise comparisons) were used as needed. All p-values were adjusted for double or multiple comparisons. Results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. The levels of significance were indicated as follows: P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001, P < 0.0001, and ns (not significant).

Results

Detection of ALV-A positive rate

The results of PCR showed that DNA extracted from guinea fowl fibroblasts tested positive for ALV-A by common exogenous A, B, J and K tests (Table 1) and a high mean positive rate of ALV-A in guinea fowl fibroblasts collected from four provinces (68.1%), with positive rates of 100% (10/10) in Jiangsu, 40% (6/15) in Hebei, 60% (9/15) in Guangdong, and 100% (7/7) in Henan (Fig. 1A). Random samples of 3 ~ 4 ALV-A positive DNA from each province were amplificated again by PCR via env-F/R (Table 1), a primer that targets the end of pol gene and the R region of the LTR for amplification of the env gene and the 3’UTR region (Fig. 1B). Sequence homology analysis showed that gp85 sequence of 15 ALV-A isolates showed obvious cladistic trend (Fig. 1C), and MEGA11 was used to classify the cladistic sequences of ALV-A gp85 (Fig. 1E). The sequence homology analysis of gp37 showed that the homology of other 14 ALV-A isolates was above 93.6% except HN24AZ3D (Fig. 1D).

Phylogenetic tree analysis and amino acid multiple sequence alignment analysis of ALV-A gp85 and gp37

As a membrane glycoprotein that recognizes host receptors in ALV, gp85 is the most mutable protein in ALV and as an ALV membrane glycoprotein that recognizes host receptors. A total of 94 ALV-A isolates were used for phylogenetic tree analysis, of which 79 were collected from GenBank and 15 were isolated in this study (GenBank: PQ685145-PQ685159), from commercial chickens, layer chickens, wild birds, Chinese local chicken breeds and guinea fowls (Table.S1). The phylogenetic tree revealed that ALV-A gp85s were divided into four evolutionary clades using MEGA11 (Fig. 1E)—clade1 (24), clade2 (26), clade3 (9), and clade4 (35)—among which 15 strains of ALV-A gp85 from guinea fowl were prevalent in clade2, clade3, and clade4 (Fig. 2). In addition, all ALV-A strains were isolated from 1986 to 2024 and came from multiple countries, including 63 in China, 15 in USA, 6 in Canada, 3 in Japan, and 1 each in France, the United Kingdom, India and location was not provided for 4 isolates.

Phylogenetic analysis of the 94 sequences of ALV-A gp85 from 1980–2024

The hr1 and hr2 domains emerged as primary hotspots of amino acid divergence in ALV-A gp85 across evolutionary lineages, with secondary variability regions in vr1-vr3 regions, as revealed by sequence alignments (Fig. 3A). SWISS-MODEL search showed that the three strains of ALV-A gp85 protein had the highest homology (about 80%) with R4RB95 (R4RB95_CHICK), and the spatial structure of this protein was AlphaFold DB model. Further, the spatial structure of gp85 proteins modeled by HN23A09, HN23A16 and HN23Axx showed some differences, especially in hr1 and hr2 regions (Fig. 3B, green and red). Phylogenetic and amino acid sequence analyses of ALV-A gp37 demonstrated that all isolates except for HN24AZ3D clustered on recent evolutionary branches and shared high amino acid homology (Fig. 4), a finding consistent with the results of nucleotide homology analysis (Fig. 1D).

Discussion

To date, no effective drug or vaccine is available to combat AL. As an alternative, strategies of preventing and controlling the disease can involve purifying the population by continuously removing chickens from the flock that test positive. In China, after more than 10 years of purification, some progress has been made in eliminating AL in commercial chickens in the country, and numerous AL purification demonstration fields have been established. However, ALV has a wide host range [18, 24], and though AL purification in commercial chickens and laying hens has shown incremental progress, ALV’s prevalence in non-commercial chickens remains undeniable.

In our study, we collected fertilised guinea fowl embryos from different regions of China to prepare fibroblast cell lines for the detection of ALV-A. Our results demonstrate a high prevalence of ALV-A in Chinese guinea fowl populations (68.1%, 32/47), confirmed through env gene amplification and sequencing (Fig. 1). This study represents the first report of ALV-A detection in guinea fowl within China. As we all konw, ALV-A is primarily transmitted through the vertical transmission of imported ancestral chickens and the inoculation of attenuated vaccines contaminated with ALV-A. With attention paid to purifying China’s poultry-breeding farms of ALV, the prevalence of ALV-A has been controlled, and reports of ALV-A in commercial meat and laying hens are sporadic. However, the results of our survey on the prevalence of ALV-A in guinea fowl show that ALV-A has a very high positive rate in the population, which suggests that more attention should be paid to preventing and controlling ALV-A, especially in free-range farms that host guinea fowl. The widespread existence of ALV-A among guinea fowl may complicate the eradication of ALV and thus lead to the failure of purification of AL.

Phylogenetic tree results showed that 94 ALV-A gp85 reference isolates were distributed in multiple clades (clade1, clade2, clade3 and clade4) by Bayesian methods (Fig. 1E), covering almost all strains from 1986 to 2024, which is consistent with the evolutionary trend of other subgroups of ALV [14, 16, 25]. Of the 24 isolates in clade1, 13 were from the USA and 7 from China, but ALV-A of clade1 was not isolated in this study (Fig. 2). The clade2 isolate was first isolated from the USA in 1986, and of the 26 isolates in this clade, 5 were from this study. In addition, the isolate SDAU09C1, which came from imported breeder chickens [26], was also located in this evolutionary clade. Only 9 isolates were located in clade3, of which 7 were from this study and only 2 were matched from GenBank, suggesting that clade3 was the dominant clade in guinea fowls. clade4 is the main endemic clades of ALV-A in China, and 25 of the 35 reference strains were from China. The clade1 ALV-A isolates from guinea fowls were not isolated. Possible reasons include: (1) Most clade 1 isolates are historical/international, suggesting adaptive mutations during transmission; (2) Guinea fowls are susceptible to ALV-A but resistant to ALV-K, linked to the critical TVA residue W33 [24, 27], suggest that susceptibility to ALV-A isolates from distinct clades (including Clade 1) may also vary in guinea fowls, a hypothesis requiring further experimental validation. It is worth noting that 4 isolates are from the Live vaccines 2011NDVP4 [20], ALV-NDV2006, ALV-FPV2007 and ALV-IBDV2009 [19] and were all located in the evolutionary clade4 (Fig. 2). It is speculated that the prevalence of ALV-A clade4 in China may be related to live vaccines contaminated with ALV-A, which emphasizes that qualified chicken embryos should be used in the process of vaccine preparation and supervision and management should be strengthened.

The results of comparing multiple sequences of ALV-A’s gp85 protein showed obvious differences in the amino acid sequences of ALV-A’s gp85 in different clades, especially in hr2, where amino acid sequence deletion or insertion was observed (Fig. 3A). It may cause the mutant strain to use multiple cellular proteins as receptors, such that if one infection channel is blocked, the virus can still use other channels to invade host cells [28]. Gp37 is usually conserved in different subgroups of ALVs, but the difference in the C terminal of ALV-J gp37 affects the replication of the virus in vitro and in vivo [13]. There are a low homology of isolates HN24AZ3D with other guinea fowl isolates (Fig. 4), and whether this difference would affect the replication ability of HN24AZ3D needs to be further investigated.

The results of different ALV-A isolates showed that HN23A16 and HN23A09 demonstrated higher infection titer or viral load, possessed strong replication ability, while HN24Axx showed weak replication in vitro (Fig. 5A, B). No clinical symptoms or tumor lesions were observed during the trial period or at necropsy. The viral load of different ALV-A isolates in various organs of SPF chickens was significantly different (P < 0.05), with the viral load of the three ALV-A isolated from the brain being the highest, suggesting that ALV-A can breach the blood-brain barrier to infect chicken’s brain. HN23A16 and HN23A09 showed higher replication levels or viral loads, possessed strong replication ability, while HN24Axx showed weak replication in vitro and in vivo, which may be due to the 3.3-fold weaker binding affinity of HN24Axx gp85 to the TVA receptor (Fig. 6). The results of comparing multiple sequences of ALV-A’s gp85 protein showed obvious differences in the amino acid sequences for different clades, especially in hr2, where amino acid sequence deletion or insertion was observed (Fig. 3). Amino acid mutations in the receptor binding region may cause the mutant strain to use multiple cellular proteins as receptors, such that if one infection channel is blocked, the virus can still use other channels to invade host cells. Previous studies have shown that ALV-A can induce avian glioma in chickens, characterized by the so-called fowl glioma and cerebellar hypoplasia [29, 30]. Vitamin B12 (VB12) and ALV-A interfere reciprocally in vitro [31]. Therefore, ALV-A potentially induces chicken glioma not only through direct brain infection but also indirectly by impairing neural VB12 uptake. These synergistic effects likely contribute to gliomagenesis. Recent research shows that insertion/deletion mutations within the TVA receptor gene confer chicken resistance to infection by ALV-A and K [32]. However, the impaired VB12 metabolism was compensated for by increasing the expression level of VB12 carrier-related genes (AMN, GIF, TCN2) in chickens [33]. The effect of ALV-A infection on VB12 uptake needs further investigation.

In addition, due to the competition between ALV-A and ALV-K for TVA receptors invasion of host cells [24], coupled with the high positive rate of ALV-K in local chickens in China [16, 25] and the purification of AL in commercial chickens, ALV-A is not widespread in China. Nevertheless, ALV-A still exists in non-commercial chickens and in chickens where ALV-K is not prevalent, which should be paid attention to.

In conclusion, there was a high prevalence of ALV-A in guinea fowl in China, and the sequence comparison between 15 ALV-A isolates from guinea fowl and that from GeneBank showed that there was multiple evolutionary trends of ALV-A gp85s. The sequence differences between different ALV-A isolates are mainly concentrated in the hr2 of gp85. HN23A16 and HN23A09 showed higher replication level or viral load, possessed strong replication ability, while HN24Axx showed weak replication in vitro and in vivo, which may be due to the 3.3-fold weaker binding affinity of HN24Axx gp85 to the TVA receptor. This study provides insights into ALV-A’s evolutionary trajectory and informs future epidemic containment strategies.
