Effects of orienteering exercise on spatial visualization ability in preschool children aged 4–5 years: Behavioral and fNIRS evidence
Authors: Jiajia Yu, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), this study examined cortical activation differences during a mental rotation task in 4–5-year-old children with varying levels of spatial visualization ability, and further compared the effects of Orienteering and Aerobic exercise in children with low spatial visualization ability. In Experiment 1, 186 children aged 4–5 years were divided into high and low spatial visualization groups based on median performance. Cortical activation and functional connectivity (FC) during a mental rotation task were compared. In Experiment 2, children with low spatial visualization ability were randomly assigned to an orienteering group or an aerobic exercise group and completed a 12-week intervention, with pre- and post-intervention behavioral and fNIRS assessments. Results from Experiment 1 showed that, during the task, the high spatial visualization group exhibited significantly lower activation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (L-DLPFC) but higher activation in the left primary motor cortex (L-M1), along with stronger FC within the prefrontal–somatosensory–visual association pathway, compared with the low spatial visualization group. In Experiment 2, linear mixed model (LMM) analyses revealed that, compared with the aerobic exercise group, the orienteering group showed greater improvements in mental rotation accuracy and higher neural processing efficiency, as reflected by reduced activation in the L-DLPFC and visual association cortex (VAC), as well as enhanced FC within the prefrontal cortex and between prefrontal, somatosensory, and visual association regions. Mediation analysis further indicated that changes in L-DLPFC activation significantly mediated the relationship between intervention type and improvements in mental rotation performance, whereas the mediating effect of L-DLPFC–VAC FC was not significant. In summary, children with high spatial visualization ability exhibit greater neural efficiency and more optimized patterns of inter-regional brain coordination, while Orienteering exercise enhances neural efficiency and functional coordination in children with low spatial visualization ability, with changes in L-DLPFC activation playing a key mediating role in performance improvement.

Spatial cognition refers to the core cognitive ability to perceive, represent, and manipulate the positions, orientations, distances, and relationships of objects in the environment, forming a critical foundation for adapting to surroundings, navigating, and learning (Newcombe and Huttenlocher, 2000). Among its subcomponents, spatial visualization represents the most fundamental ability, reflecting an individual’s capacity to mentally represent, manipulate, and predict dynamic changes in spatial information (McGee, 1979, Lyon et al., 2008; Van Garderen et al., 2006). Early childhood is a critical sensitive period for the development of spatial visualization, which is strongly associated with general intelligence (IQ) and serves as an important predictor of later achievement in mathematics, science, and engineering (Ehrlich et al., 2006, Stieff and Uttal, 2015, Judd and Klingberg, 2021). Moreover, strong spatial visualization skills support preschool children in completing daily tasks more independently, such as organizing and locating objects and navigating within the home environment (Verdine et al., 2014). In contrast, preschool children with lower spatial visualization abilities require greater working memory resources to process visuospatial information, leading to higher information loss, cognitive overload during perspective-taking tasks, frequent reliance on visual backtracking, and reduced learning efficiency (Mohler, 2008). Therefore, investigating the developmental characteristics and neural mechanisms of spatial visualization in preschool children, as well as exploring effective strategies to enhance this ability, holds significant theoretical value and practical implications for cognitive health and future academic and functional outcomes.

Mental rotation (MR) tasks, as a classic paradigm for assessing spatial visualization ability, require individuals to mentally rotate object representations and serve as a key indicator of spatial visualization capacity (Wang et al., 2005). Neuroscientific studies indicate that mental rotation involves coordinated processing across multiple brain regions: the primary visual cortex (V1) supports the initial processing of the physical properties of stimuli (Shepard and Cooper, 1982), the visual association cortex (VAC) contributes to higher-level integration and representation, and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) plays a critical role in task control and cognitive resource allocation (Tomasino and Gremese, 2016). Simultaneously, the sensorimotor cortex is also engaged, reflecting the embodied nature of cognition (Bennet and Reiner, 2022, Doganci et al., 2023). Embodied cognition theory posits that cognition is grounded in bodily activity, emphasizing that the environment, body, and mind form a dynamically interacting, coupled system (Wilson, 2002), wherein bodily movements and perceptual experiences directly participate in higher-order cognitive processes such as reasoning (Feng et al., 2017). Within this complex multi-brain coordination, individuals with different abilities may allocate neural resources differently. Evidence shows that high-ability individuals typically exhibit lower prefrontal activation and more optimized brain network configurations when performing cognitive tasks, completing the same or even superior performance with fewer neural resources (Neubauer et al., 2005), a phenomenon also observed in expert–novice comparisons (Liu et al., 2024). However, most existing research has focused on adults or expert–novice contrasts, and direct evidence is lacking on whether preschool children at different levels of spatial visualization exhibit similar neural efficiency patterns. Notably, prior studies have often been limited to localized brain analyses, with few systematically examining task-related neural activation patterns and interregional functional coordination at the whole-brain level, which constrains a comprehensive understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying spatial visualization.

Given that early childhood represents a period of maximal central nervous system plasticity, identifying effective interventions to promote spatial visualization development is crucial. A large body of research indicates that physical exercise can enhance cognitive abilities by modulating brain structure and functional activity (Chen et al., 2021, Xia et al., 2018, Zhou and Jin, 2021, Zhu and Zhang, 2026). Among these, aerobic exercise has been shown to improve prefrontal cortex function (Erickson et al., 2014, Chaddock-Heyman et al., 2013), yet such activities primarily impose physiological load and lack specific cognitive challenges targeting spatial information processing. In contrast, combined motor–cognitive dual-task interventions, which integrate cognitive demands into physical activity, may produce stronger facilitative effects (Vazou et al., 2019, Liu, 2022). Neuroimaging studies further reveal that performing motor–cognitive dual tasks increases prefrontal activation in young adults (Saraiva et al., 2021). Orienteering, as a prototypical motor–cognitive dual-task activity, exhibits distinct advantages in the spatial visualization domain (Zheng and Lin, 2012). This activity requires participants to perform real-time map recognition, spatial orientation, and route planning in unfamiliar environments (Liu, 2017). The continuous visual–spatial transformations and orientation judgments involved make orienteering a deeply embodied cognitive training method, compelling participants to dynamically integrate map symbols, compass directions, and environmental sensory cues while moving, thereby establishing a coupled system linking bodily actions, perceptual input, and spatial representations (Guo et al., 2023, Liu and Yang, 2018). Preliminary evidence suggests that orienteering positively impacts spatial abilities (Liu and Liu, 2019, Su, 2021), yet the neural mechanisms underlying these facilitative effects remain unclear. In particular, whether orienteering can specifically enhance cognitive performance in preschool children with low spatial visualization ability by modulating patterns of brain activity remains to be investigated using neuroimaging approaches.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), as a non-invasive optical imaging technique, offers advantages including portability, high temporal resolution, and strong ecological validity, making it particularly suitable for investigating cognitive neural activity in preschool children under naturalistic task conditions (Xie et al., 2021). This technique enables real-time monitoring of cortical hemodynamic changes, providing a novel approach for elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying cognitive processes. Given that mental rotation involves complex visuospatial transformations (Yang et al., 2023) and requires coordinated engagement of multiple brain regions, integrating task-related activation and functional connectivity at the whole-brain level is essential for a systematic understanding of the neural mechanisms of spatial visualization in preschool children.

Building on this, the present study combined behavioral measures with fNIRS to investigate the neural mechanisms of spatial visualization in preschool children and the effects of intervention across two studies. Study 1 compared cortical activation patterns and functional connectivity characteristics during a mental rotation task between children with High and Low spatial visualization, aiming to reveal differences in neural processing across ability levels. We proposed the following hypotheses: H1: compared with children with Low spatial visualization, those with High spatial visualization would exhibit lower prefrontal activation during task performance; H2: children with High spatial visualization would show stronger functional connectivity (FC) among brain regions involved in spatial processing. On this basis, Study 2 employed a randomized controlled design to compare the effects of Orienteering and Aerobic exercise interventions. We hypothesized that H3: compared with the aerobic exercise group, the orienteering group would show greater behavioral improvement after the intervention, accompanied by reduced prefrontal activation and enhanced inter-regional functional connectivity, reflecting neural activity patterns approaching those of the High spatial visualization group; H4: changes in prefrontal activation would mediate the relationship between intervention type and behavioral improvement.

Experiment 1: neural activation differences in preschool children with different levels of spatial visualization

Research objectives

Based on a mental rotation task paradigm, this study combined behavioral measures and fNIRS to systematically examine differences in neural activity during task performance between preschool children with High and Low spatial visualization. By comparing group differences in regional cortical activation and functional connectivity, the study aimed to elucidate the neural basis underlying variability in spatial visualization ability, thereby providing empirical support for subsequent intervention research.

Participants and methods

Participants

An a priori sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009) based on an independent-samples t-test, with α = 0.05, statistical power = 0.80, and a medium effect size (d = 0.5), yielding a required total sample size of 128 participants (at least 64 per group). To account for potential data loss, a total of 186 preschool children aged 4–5 years (98 boys, 88 girls) were recruited from a kindergarten in Xi’an, China. Written informed consent was obtained from all guardians, and verbal assent was obtained from the children. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shaanxi Normal University (Approval No. 202516090). Inclusion criteria were: (1) no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders; (2) right-handedness; and (3) no prior participation in similar psychological experiments. Prior to the experiment, the procedures were explained to both children and their guardians, and children were instructed to maintain adequate sleep, avoid vigorous physical activity, and keep their scalp clean before testing. Following data acquisition, fNIRS data were preprocessed, and six participants were excluded due to excessive motion artifacts. The final sample consisted of 180 preschool children (mean age = 5.05 ± 0.45 years). Based on a median split of mental rotation accuracy, participants were classified into High (n = 90) and Low (n = 90) spatial visualization groups, with no significant age differences between groups (p > 0.05).

Experimental design

A between-subjects design with one factor and two levels was employed, with spatial visualization level (High vs. Low) as the independent variable. Dependent variables included mental rotation task accuracy (MRT-ACC), oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) concentrations in regions of interest (ROIs), and changes in functional connectivity (FC).

Procedure

A two-dimensional mental rotation task (Windows Test, WT1) was used to assess mental rotation ability (Tzuriel and Egozi, 2010). The stimuli consisted of a 3 × 3 grid “house-with-windows” figure (nine windows in total), with some windows presented as closed (filled in black). Children were required to observe a target figure and then identify the corresponding closed windows on rotated versions (45°, 90°, or 180°). The experiment was conducted in a quiet room in the kindergarten and consisted of three phases: practice, resting-state recording, and formal testing. During the practice phase, the experimenter explained and demonstrated the task, and children completed several trials to become familiar with the procedure. This was followed by a 3-minute resting-state recording, during which children were instructed to sit still, look forward, and minimize movement; these data served as the baseline. The formal test employed a block design consisting of three blocks, each lasting 30 s and including five trials (6 s per trial). Trials varied in rotation angle (45°, 90°, 180°) and task complexity (2, 3, or 4 closed windows), presented in a randomized order, with a 20 s rest interval between blocks. In each trial, children indicated target windows by placing small black dots (rather than shading) on the answer sheet within 6 s using a marker, thereby minimizing motor output. Responses were scored dichotomously: a trial was scored as correct (1) only if all target windows were correctly marked with no incorrect markings; otherwise, it was scored as incorrect (0). No feedback was provided during the formal test. Throughout the experiment, cortical hemodynamic signals were continuously recorded using fNIRS, and behavioral performance was indexed by overall task accuracy.

fNIRS detection procedure

Cortical hemodynamic responses during the mental rotation task were recorded using a multi-channel NirSmart fNIRS system (Danyang Huichuang, China). The system consisted of 24 sources and 16 detectors forming 48 channels, sampled at 11 Hz. Source wavelengths were 730 and 850 nm, with a source–detector distance of 3 cm (range 2.7–3.3 cm), and probes were fixed in child-sized caps. According to the international 10–20 system, the cap covered bilateral prefrontal cortices, sensorimotor areas, and visual cortices. To accurately record the anatomical positions of sources and detectors in preschool children, 3D coordinates were acquired for child before the experiment. Individual coordinates were registered to standard MNI space to ensure precise brain region localization. Channel-to-region mapping was referenced to the adult Brodmann–Talairach atlas and adjusted for the head size of 4–5-year-old children (Peng et al., 2025). Data processing and analysis

fNIRS data were processed using NirSpark 1.8.8 (Danyang Huichuang). During data acquisition, children’s head movements were monitored in real time, and participants were reminded to remain still to minimize motion artifacts. (1) Quality control: The coefficient of variation (CV) was used to evaluate the quality of all 48 channels, and channels with CV > 20% were excluded (Lloyd-Fox et al., 2019). (2) Preprocessing: To remove transient spikes caused by hand or head movements, motion artifacts were automatically detected using the moving standard deviation method and corrected with spline interpolation. Signals were then band-pass filtered (0.01–0.20 Hz) to reduce noise, global trends, and physiological signals. Filtered optical density data were converted to relative concentrations of oxy-hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxy-hemoglobin (HbR) using the modified Beer–Lambert law. HbO was selected as the primary measure due to its higher sensitivity to task-induced hemodynamic changes. The differential path-length factor (DPF) was calculated using an age-dependent equation (Kamran et al., 2016), resulting in DPF = 5 for 4–5-year-old children. (3) Block averaging: Task-related hemodynamic responses were extracted using block averaging. A time window from 20 s before task onset to 30 s after task completion was extracted, and the −20–0 s period served as baseline. HbO time series were then averaged across trials to obtain mean hemodynamic responses per channel. Signals within each ROI were further averaged to generate representative ROI responses. (4) Functional connectivity (FC) calculation: To quantify task-induced coordination among brain regions, HbO time series from each ROI were extracted and Pearson correlation coefficients were computed for all ROI pairs. Correlation coefficients were then Fisher-Z transformed and averaged across ROI pairs for subsequent statistical analyses.

Statistical Analysis: Preprocessed fNIRS data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05), and outliers were assessed via boxplots. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare HbO activation and FC differences between high- and low-spatial-visualization groups. Multiple linear regression was applied to test the robustness of median-split grouping results. For analyses involving multiple comparisons, p-values were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Multiple linear regression

To avoid information loss and reduced statistical power associated with median-split grouping, MRT-ACC was further treated as a continuous variable in a multiple linear regression model. After controlling for age, sex, and BMI, its predictive effects on cortical activation and functional connectivity were examined. Results showed that MRT-ACC significantly negatively predicted L-DLPFC activation (β = −0.324, p < 0.001) and significantly positively predicted L-M1 activation (β = 0.182, p = 0.016) in preschool children. Regarding functional connectivity, MRT-ACC positively predicted CH20–CH24 (β = 0.201, p = 0.009), CH24–CH29 (β = 0.166, p = 0.030), CH29–CH42 (β = 0.192, p = 0.013), CH42–CH48 (β = 0.183, p = 0.017), and CH47–CH48 (β = 0.185, p = 0.014). The results from the continuous-variable analysis were consistent with the group comparisons, indicating good robustness of the findings.

Summary of Experiment 1

Experiment 1 employed fNIRS to investigate neural processing differences during a mental rotation task in preschool children with varying levels of spatial visualization ability. Behavioral and fNIRS results revealed significant differences between the high spatial visualization group (HSVG) and the low spatial visualization group (LSVG) in information processing strategies and brain network coordination. Regarding cortical activation patterns, compared to the LSVG, children in the HSVG exhibited significantly lower activation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (L-DLPFC), suggesting higher automatization in cognitive control and the ability to complete mental rotation tasks with fewer neural resources, consistent with the neural efficiency hypothesis. Conversely, HSVG children showed significantly higher activation in the left primary motor cortex (L-M1), indicating a greater reliance on motor simulation-based embodied processing strategies. In terms of functional connectivity, the HSVG demonstrated significantly stronger connectivity along the prefrontal–primary somatosensory–visual association cortex pathway than the LSVG, reflecting more efficient cross-regional information integration and the formation of an optimized cognitive processing network. In summary, preschool children with lower spatial visualization ability exhibited lower neural efficiency and less coordinated engagement of motor simulation during the mental rotation task. These findings highlight the need to address the difficulties LSVG children face in spatial information processing and cognitive transformation, and to enhance their spatial representation, mental simulation, and cognitive control through systematic training. Recently, orienteering, as a dual-task intervention combining physical activity and cognitive challenges, has shown notable improvements in spatial visualization ability and its underlying neural mechanisms (Liu et al., 2024). However, empirical research on preschool populations remains limited. Based on this, Experiment 2 aims to systematically examine the effects of orienteering on neural efficiency and behavioral performance in LSVG children.

Experiment 2: intervention effects of orienteering exercise on preschool children with low spatial visualization ability and its neural plasticity mechanisms

Research objective

Building on Experiment 1, this study employed a randomized controlled design to compare the effects of orienteering and aerobic exercise on behavioral performance and neural activity in preschool children with low spatial visualization ability. The focus was to investigate whether orienteering leads to greater improvements in mental rotation task accuracy and whether such improvements are accompanied by enhanced neural efficiency and optimized functional connectivity in prefrontal, motor, and visual-related brain regions, thereby revealing potential neural plasticity mechanisms.

Participants and methods

Participants

Ninety preschool children with low spatial visualization ability, identified from Experiment 1, were included (47 boys, 43 girls). Using a random number table, children were assigned to an orienteering group (n = 45, 24 boys, 21 girls, age 5.07 ± 0.33 years) or an aerobic exercise group (n = 45, 23 boys, 22 girls, age 4.98 ± 0.34 years). Ethical approval, trial registration, and experimental precautions were consistent with Experiment 1.

Experimental design

A 2 (group: orienteering vs. aerobic exercise) × 2 (time: pre-test vs. post-test) mixed-design was employed. Dependent variables included mental rotation task accuracy (MRT-ACC), HbO concentrations in each ROI, and changes in functional connectivity strength.

Intervention protocol

The orienteering intervention was developed based on the characteristics of specialized orienteering and the developmental stage of spatial visualization in 4–5-year-old children. Guided by domestic orienteering experts, a course appropriate to preschoolers’ cognitive level was designed. Intervention plan for orienteering.

During each session, children completed approximately 2–3 orienteering routes of varying difficulty. Tasks were classified based on map complexity and checkpoint number: beginner (≤5 points, simple terrain), intermediate (6–8 points, moderate terrain), and advanced (≥9 points, complex terrain). Children progressed sequentially from beginner to advanced. Instructors used supportive and guiding strategies to encourage active observation of environmental features, identification of map symbols, and practical navigation, promoting spatial exploration and problem-solving skills.

To control exercise load, the intensity of orienteering sessions was maintained at a moderate level (60–69% HRmax, HRmax = 220 − age) (American College of Sports Medicine, 2010). Heart rate was monitored in real time using Domor wristbands, and both average session heart rate and target heart rate adherence were recorded to assess compliance. To ensure intervention consistency, the research team developed standardized teaching protocols and trained all instructors. Attendance and task completion were recorded, and only children with attendance above 90% were included in subsequent analyses.

The aerobic exercise group participated in traditional aerobic activities (e.g., running, jump rope) with frequency, duration, and intensity matched to the orienteering group. No spatial visualization elements such as maps or navigation tasks were included, controlling for potential confounding factors.

Experimental materials, task procedures, and fNIRS data acquisition parameters were consistent with those used in Experiment 1. No adjustments were made to the experimental conditions, except for the inclusion of the intervention. Data preprocessing and statistical analysis

fNIRS data preprocessing followed the same procedures as in Experiment 1. Linear mixed-effects models (LMM) were employed to evaluate the effects of different interventions on spatial visualization performance, with group (orienteering vs. aerobic exercise), time (pre-test vs. post-test), and their interaction as fixed effects, and gender, age, height, weight, and baseline values of the dependent variable as covariates. Subjects were treated as random effects. Dependent variables included HbO concentration changes within each ROI and functional connectivity (FC) strength between ROIs. LMM effect sizes are reported as regression coefficients (β). Model validity was assessed via Q–Q plots and Shapiro–Wilk tests for normality of outcome variables. For analyses involving multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied for FDR correction, with a corrected p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To investigate whether specific cortical activity (i.e., task-evoked HbO changes and task-related FC) mediated the orienteering-induced improvement in spatial visualization performance (i.e., changes in accuracy), significant variables were first identified through partial correlation analysis. Mediation models were then constructed, controlling for gender, age, height, and weight. Bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations) was used to assess the mediation effects, with a bias-corrected 95% confidence interval not containing zero indicating a significant mediating effect. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.4.1.

Summary of Experiment 2

Experiment 2 focused on preschool children with low spatial visualization ability and employed a pre–post design comparing an orienteering group and an aerobic exercise group. By integrating behavioral measures and fNIRS indicators, the study examined the differential effects of the two exercise interventions and their underlying neural mechanisms. The results revealed that, following the intervention, children in the orienteering group showed a significant improvement in mental rotation task accuracy (MRT-ACC), which was also significantly higher than that of the aerobic exercise group, indicating a stronger facilitative effect of orienteering on spatial visualization ability.

fNIRS results further revealed that, after the intervention, children in the orienteering group exhibited significantly reduced activation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (L-DLPFC) and the visual association cortex (VAC), with activation levels lower than those in the aerobic exercise group. This pattern is consistent with the neural efficiency hypothesis, suggesting more economical resource utilization and higher processing efficiency during cognitive tasks. At the same time, orienteering significantly enhanced functional connectivity between the prefrontal cortex and somatosensory and visual-related brain regions, indicating improved inter-regional coordination. Mediation analysis revealed that changes in L-DLPFC activation played a significant mediating role in the relationship between intervention type and improvements in mental rotation performance, whereas the mediating effect of L-DLPFC–VAC functional connectivity was not significant. This suggests that reduced L-DLPFC activation may serve as a key neural pathway through which orienteering facilitates cognitive improvement. In summary, from the perspectives of behavioral performance, neural activation, and functional connectivity, the present study revealed that orienteering is more effective than aerobic exercise in enhancing spatial visualization ability in preschool children with low baseline levels. The core mechanism appears to involve optimization of neural efficiency in spatial processing through modulation of L-DLPFC activation.

Discussion

Neural mechanism differences between preschool children with different levels of spatial visualization ability

The present study revealed significant differences in the neural mechanisms underlying mental rotation performance between preschool children with different levels of spatial visualization ability. These differences were primarily reflected in differential activation of the prefrontal and motor cortices, as well as distinct functional connectivity patterns along the prefrontal–somatosensory–visual pathways. In terms of cortical activation, children in the high spatial visualization group (HSVG) exhibited significantly lower activation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (L-DLPFC) and significantly higher activation in the left primary motor cortex (L-M1) compared to those in the low spatial visualization group (LSVG), suggesting higher neural efficiency and the use of distinct cognitive strategies during spatial information processing and mental manipulation. Mental rotation is a complex spatial cognitive process that relies on the coordinated involvement of multiple brain regions, including prefrontal executive control systems, visual processing areas, and sensorimotor simulation networks (Liu et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2023). Visuospatial memory serves as a fundamental basis for mental rotation (Ebert et al., 2024), with the DLPFC supporting working memory and executive control by maintaining and comparing spatial representations before and after rotation (Mutlu et al., 2020, Shelton and Pippitt, 2006). The primary visual cortex (V1) is involved in the extraction of basic visual features such as orientation and contours (Ou et al., 2025), while the visual association cortex (VAC) acts as a bridge between early visual areas and higher-order cognitive regions, enabling more complex visual processing, including shape recognition, object perception, and the integration of visual features, thereby providing structured representations for subsequent spatial operations and motor simulation (Gazzaley et al., 2007). Meanwhile, activation of the L-M1 supports the notion that mental rotation involves an implicit embodied simulation of physical object rotation (Lotze and Halsband, 2006), indicating that the brain does not rely solely on abstract spatial transformations but also engages motor system representations to simulate action plans associated with object manipulation (Porro et al., 1996; Vingerhoets et al., 2001). The efficient coordination among these regions forms the neural basis for spatial representation and cognitive manipulation during mental rotation.

The reduced DLPFC activation observed in the HSVG suggests an advantage in automatic processing and relatively lower demands for cognitive control, consistent with the neural efficiency hypothesis (Bilalić, 2018). This finding indicates that individuals with better cognitive performance tend to exhibit lower levels of cortical activation, particularly in task-relevant regions (Haier et al., 1992). Similar patterns have been reported in expert–novice comparisons, where experts typically show reduced neural activation during task performance (Hu et al., 2025, Yin et al., 2024a). Such reductions may reflect more focused cortical activation and reduced recruitment of neural circuits, indicating enhanced neural efficiency in cognitive processing (Wei, 2019). In contrast, individuals with lower spatial visualization ability may recruit additional neural resources to compensate for reduced processing efficiency, reflecting a more effortful and less efficient cognitive strategy.The increased activation in L-M1 observed in the HSVG further suggests greater engagement of motor simulation strategies during mental rotation (Yang et al., 2023), indicating that these children more effectively utilize embodied cognition by leveraging motor experiences to facilitate spatial problem-solving. This interpretation is supported by studies on neural resource sharing, which suggest that even simple motor planning tasks (e.g., finger movements) and mental rotation tasks share common neural substrates despite apparent task differences (Doganci et al., 2023). In terms of functional connectivity, the present study further revealed that the HSVG exhibited significantly stronger FC in CH20–CH24, CH24–CH29, CH29–CH42, CH42–CH48, and CH47–CH48 compared to the LSVG. These connections primarily spanned pathways from the prefrontal cortex to the primary somatosensory cortex and extended to the visual association cortex. The enhanced connectivity within this network suggests that the HSVG achieved more efficient functional coordination among prefrontal executive control regions, sensorimotor areas, and visual association cortices during task performance (Cortes et al., 2023, Ecker et al., 2008). This strengthened neural coordination reflects more efficient large-scale network integration, enabling effective cross-modal processing among spatial representation, cognitive control, and motor simulation, and ultimately forming an optimized and resource-efficient information processing loop. In summary, the cognitive advantages observed in preschool children with high spatial visualization ability are not only reflected in region-specific activation patterns but also rely on the efficient coordination of core brain networks.

Effects of orienteering compared to aerobic exercise on spatial visualization ability and related brain regions in preschool children

Building on the neural mechanism differences identified in Experiment 1, Experiment 2 further revealed that, compared to aerobic exercise, orienteering—as a typical motor–cognitive combined intervention—more effectively reshapes neural function in preschool children with low spatial visualization ability and significantly promotes the development of their spatial visualization skills. Behavioral results revealed that, following the intervention, children in the orienteering group showed a significant improvement in mental rotation task accuracy, which was also markedly greater than that observed in the aerobic exercise group. This finding suggests that the 12-week orienteering intervention produced superior effects in enhancing spatial visualization ability in preschool children. These results are consistent with previous studies indicating that aerobic exercise combined with cognitive engagement yields greater benefits for cognitive function in children (Fisher et al., 2011, Pesce et al., 2013, Diamond and Ling, 2016). From the perspective of embodied cognition theory (Wilson, 2002), the advantages of orienteering do not merely stem from increased cognitive load but rather from the construction of a highly coupled “environment–body–cognition” dynamic system. During orienteering, individuals are required to continuously perform tasks such as map reading, spatial localization, orientation judgment, and route decision-making in real-world environments, embedding spatial cognitive processing within ongoing bodily movement and environmental interaction. Compared to decontextualized cognitive training or repetitive physical activity, this dynamic interaction in real contexts transforms cognitive processing from abstract symbolic manipulation into an online construction process grounded in bodily experience. In the present intervention design, initial training focused on compass use and orientation recognition, enabling children to integrate environmental cues with map symbols to accurately determine direction and spatial orientation, thereby establishing fundamental spatial reference abilities. In addition, representational training involving map–environment matching and scene recognition was implemented to help children build accurate mappings between map symbols and real-world features, strengthening the transformation between two-dimensional and three-dimensional spatial representations and facilitating the construction of cognitive maps. Following 12 weeks of systematic training, children’s abilities in spatial perception and orientation judgment were effectively enhanced, which in turn promoted the development of spatial visualization ability. Previous studies have shown that outdoor physical activities contribute to the development of overall cognitive abilities in preschool children (Qin, 2012). For example, a 12-week spatial orientation exercise program significantly improved preschool children’s ability to transform spatial information (Boccia et al., 2017). Similarly, research on orienteering in children and adolescents has revealed significant improvements in spatial orientation and navigation abilities (Bao et al., 2021). Moreover, studies involving children with attention deficit disorders have indicated that orienteering interventions can effectively enhance cognitive function (Liu and Yang, 2018, Tang and Liu, 2021). Orienteering requires individuals to continuously engage in a sequence of tasks—map reading, spatial judgment, route planning, and rapid movement—in unfamiliar environments, a process that more strongly activates spatial mapping and mental rotation abilities than simple repetitive physical movements (Yi et al., 2022). Therefore, by strengthening the integrated coordination of body, environment, and cognition, orienteering provides a unique and effective training pathway for enhancing spatial visualization ability in preschool children.

The fNIRS results revealed that, following the 12-week orienteering intervention, the orienteering group exhibited significantly reduced activation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (L-DLPFC) and visual association cortex (VAC) during the mental rotation task, with activation levels significantly lower than those observed in the aerobic exercise group. This finding is consistent with previous research suggesting that orienteering exercise reduces HbO concentration in the DLPFC (Yin et al., 2024b). Importantly, such reductions in activation do not indicate diminished neural function; rather, they suggest that the brain is able to achieve more efficient information processing with fewer neural resources when performing the same task, in line with the neural efficiency hypothesis. From the perspective of brain plasticity theory, physical activity promotes both structural and functional plasticity, thereby enhancing cognitive function (Hillman et al., 2020, El-Sayes et al., 2019). Previous studies have suggested that aerobic exercise can improve spatial cognition by increasing cerebral blood flow, oxygenation levels, and neurotransmitter release; however, these neurochemical effects may be relatively transient and could diminish in the absence of sustained reinforcement (McMorris, 2016). In contrast, orienteering continuously imposes spatial information processing demands during physical activity, requiring children to repeatedly encode, retrieve, and transform spatial information within real-world, complex, and dynamically changing three-dimensional environments. This process may consolidate and amplify the short-term physiological benefits induced by aerobic exercise through multiple cognitive mechanisms, ultimately leading to more robust and sustained improvements in spatial memory. For preschool children, whose brains are undergoing rapid development and exhibit high plasticity (Tau and Peterson 2010), orienteering requires continuous engagement in higher-order cognitive processes, including visuospatial transformation, orientation judgment, map recognition, and route planning. Such demands systematically strengthen the functional coordination between the prefrontal cortex and visual regions. Specifically, the DLPFC exerts top-down control over the VAC, prioritizing task-relevant visual information and thereby enhancing the efficiency of spatial representation and reasoning (Gazzaley et al., 2007). As a result, reduced activation during task performance may reflect a shift from effortful, high-control processing toward more automated and efficient neural strategies. In addition, the present study revealed that, compared to aerobic exercise, the orienteering intervention led to significantly stronger functional connectivity in L-DLPFC–R-DLPFC, L-DLPFC–R-S1, and L-DLPFC–VAC pathways. Previous studies have suggested that various forms of physical exercise can enhance prefrontal-related functional connectivity (Bao et al., 2022, Xu et al., 2026), indicating a general facilitative effect of exercise on cognitive brain networks. Furthermore, aerobic exercise has been shown to induce broad and fundamental changes in large-scale brain networks, such as the default mode network and executive control network (Voss et al., 2010). Building on this, the present findings further suggest that orienteering, conducted in real-world three-dimensional environments, continuously requires the extraction of spatial representations, maintenance of spatial memory, and real-time decision-making. This “environment–body–cognition” coupling enforces the integration of multimodal information, including visual, spatial, and motor inputs, transforming spatial processing from abstract symbolic manipulation into an embodied, experience-dependent online construction process. Through 12 weeks of repeated reinforcement, this integrative process progressively optimizes inter-regional coordination efficiency, resulting in enhanced functional integration across brain networks, which reflects improved neural efficiency at the network level. Mediation analysis further revealed that changes in L-DLPFC activation played a significant mediating role in the relationship between intervention type and improvements in mental rotation performance. This finding directly links behavioral gains to region-specific neural activation, suggesting that, compared to aerobic exercise alone, orienteering—by incorporating spatial cognitive demands—may enhance visuospatial information processing efficiency through modulation of L-DLPFC activation. Specifically, orienteering continuously introduces cognitive demands such as spatial judgment and route planning during physical activity, thereby increasing engagement of prefrontal regulatory processes and, over time, promoting more efficient neural processing strategies, as reflected by reduced activation during task execution (Bao et al., 2022). Taken together, the L-DLPFC emerges as a key target region of the orienteering intervention and a critical neural bridge linking intervention type to cognitive performance improvements. The remodeling of its activation pattern partially explains the superiority of orienteering over traditional aerobic exercise. Overall, as an embodied intervention integrating physical activity with spatial cognitive challenges, orienteering more effectively shapes the neural foundations of spatial cognition in preschool children through task-specific activation patterns and enhanced brain network coordination, thereby providing a more robust neuroplastic basis for long-term cognitive improvement (Mehren et al., 2019).

Limitations and future prospects

This study, through two experiments, systematically revealed the neural mechanisms underlying differences in spatial visualization ability among preschool children and showed the facilitating effects of orienteering intervention, providing important insights into the neural basis of exercise-induced enhancement of spatial visualization development. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, children performed tasks by hand drawing, which may have been accompanied by subtle head or body movements, potentially introducing motion artifacts into the fNIRS signals. Future studies could adopt key-press responses combined with eye-tracking techniques to minimize motion-related confounds. Second, fNIRS cannot detect activity in deep brain structures such as the hippocampus; future research could integrate multimodal neuroimaging approaches, including fMRI, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying neural mechanisms. Third, only a 0.01–0.20 Hz band-pass filter was applied during preprocessing, which may not fully separate cortical signals from systemic physiological noise. Future studies could employ short-channel regression or similar techniques to improve signal purity. Fourth, due to anatomical differences between children and adults and the current lack of standardized brain atlases for this age group, spatial localization remains somewhat uncertain. Future studies could develop age-appropriate standardized brain templates would enhance the precision of brain region identification. Fifth, functional connectivity analysis based on task-evoked HbO time series cannot completely rule out the influence of task-related coactivation. Future studies could employ psychophysiological interaction (PPI) analysis or residual time-series-based methods to isolate intrinsic connectivity patterns more accurately.

Conclusions

Based on fNIRS technology, this study conducted two experiments to examine the developmental characteristics of spatial visualization ability in preschool children and to compare the effects of orienteering and aerobic exercise interventions on children with low spatial visualization ability and their underlying neural mechanisms. The results revealed that children with high spatial visualization ability exhibited higher neural efficiency during the mental rotation task, characterized by lower prefrontal activation, stronger activation in the left primary motor cortex (L-M1), and enhanced functional connectivity, indicating reliance on a more optimized pattern of brain network coordination. Further investigation showed that a 12-week orienteering intervention, compared with general aerobic exercise, not only significantly improved mental rotation accuracy in low-ability children but also promoted a shift in cortical activation patterns toward greater efficiency and enhanced coordination within key neural networks, approaching the neural activity patterns observed in the high-ability group. Mediation analysis indicated that changes in L-DLPFC activation significantly mediated the relationship between intervention type and improvements in mental rotation performance, suggesting that, relative to aerobic exercise alone, orienteering—which incorporates spatial cognitive components—may enhance visuospatial information processing efficiency by modulating L-DLPFC activation. In summary, orienteering, as a dual-task intervention combining physical activity with spatial cognitive challenges, can effectively enhance preschool children’s spatial visualization ability by regulating L-DLPFC activation. This study provides novel empirical evidence for the neural mechanisms underlying exercise-induced improvements in spatial cognition and offers a scientific basis for designing early childhood education programs.
