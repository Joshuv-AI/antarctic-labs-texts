A lightweight TcrLM model predicts T cell receptor and epitope binding specificity
Authors: Chenpeng Yu, Xing Fang, Shiye Tian, Judong Luo, Hui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmune responses depend on specific interactions between T cell receptors (TCRs) and peptides presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). The vast diversity of the TCR repertoire makes accurate prediction of TCR-antigen binding specificity highly challenging. Here, we present a lightweight, masked language model, tcrLM, to address this problem. We pretrain tcrLM on a large-scale TCR CDR3 sequence dataset and use the pretrained encoder to extract informative features for pTCR binding prediction. tcrLM achieves competitive performance on hold-out and external test sets and shows comparatively robust zero-shot generalization relative to several baselines on a large, unseen-COVID-19 peptide set. The model effectively captures biochemical properties and positional preferences of amino acids within TCR sequences. In an exploratory melanoma cohort, the predicted TCR-neoantigen binding scores correlate with immunotherapy response and clinical outcomes. These results highlight the potential of tcrLM for advancing immunotherapy and personalized medicine.Keywords: T cell receptor, large language model, Transformer, virtual adversarial training, peptide-TCR binding, tumor neoantigen

Introduction

The recognition of antigens is a crucial step in initiating T cell-mediated immune responses against cancers.1,2 This process is orchestrated by the interactions between T cells and antigen-presenting cells (APCs),3 followed by the clonal expansion and differentiation of naive T cells into effector T cells and their migration to the tumor site to eliminate cancer cells.4 T cell receptors (TCRs) specifically recognize antigens presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules on the surface of APCs. This recognition occurs through binding to the antigen-MHC complex, triggering a series of intracellular signaling events that result in T cell activation. Upon activation, T cells undergo clonal expansion and differentiate into various effector subsets, most notably cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs),5,6,7 which play a central role in the anti-tumor immune response by directly targeting and eliminating tumor cells. Consequently, the peptide-TCR (pTCR) binding represents a pivotal step in the immune response process.8,9

The TCR comprises α and β chains that are generated through genetic recombination, leading to an expansive TCR repertoire. Previous studies have documented that humans have the potential to generate approximately 1015–1020 distinct TCR sequences. This diversity predominantly manifests in the complementarity determining region 3 (CDR3),10 which engages directly with the peptide-MHC complex, thereby dictating the binding specificity of TCRs.11,12 Quite a few computational methods have been developed to predict the binding specificity of TCRs to peptide-MHC complexes (pMHCs). These methods roughly fall into three main categories: 1) Clustering-based methods, which measure the similarities between TCRs and provide understanding of the underlying patterns in binding to antigens. Representative methods include TCRdist,13 DeepTCR,14 GIANA,15 iSMART,16 GLIPH,1 and ELATE17 models. 2) Peptide-specific models, which focus on predicting the binding of specific peptide to TCRs, for example, TCRGP,18 TCRex,19 and NetTCR-2.20 3) Generic prediction models, which are not limited to specific peptides but need training on known pTCR bindings, for example, PanPep,21 pMTnet,22 DLpTCR,23 ERGO2,24 and TITAN.25 Although these methods have demonstrated promising accuracy in specific scenarios, they remain limited in generalizing to unseen peptides, which is crucial for identifying the binding specificity of tumor neoantigens or exogenous antigens. Therefore, accurately identifying the pTCR bindings remains a challenging task.

Essentially, both the TCR CDR3 and antigens can be expressed as a sequence of amino acids, exhibiting striking similarity to human natural language. Large language models have gained increasing attention in the field of protein modeling in recent years26,27,28 and yielded new insights into the structures and functions of proteins. Most protein language models are built upon the Transformer encoder,29 which can encode protein sequences or structures into predefined-length latent representations. The representations have been proven to boost the performance of downstream tasks related to proteins, such as the prediction of structures, functions, and protein-drug interactions. The masked language models (MLMs), such as CVC,30 TCR-BERT,31 ESM1b,32 ESM-1v,33 ProtFlash,34 and ProtTrans,35 aim to reconstruct masked tokens based on surrounding sequences. Their success has inspired taking advantages of MLMs to accommodate the diversity of TCR sequences. For example, TULIP36 exploits the Transformer-based unsupervised language model to predict the interactions between peptides and TCRs. TAPIR37 is also inspired by language models and involves a two-tower architecture composed of independent CNN-based encoders for TCR and target sequences. ProtLM.TCR38 is a RoBERTa-style Transformer-based language model designed to predict binding between TCR and HLA class I epitope sequences. CVC30 uses a lightly modified BERT architecture with tweaked pretraining objectives trained on TCR CDR3 sequences. TCR-BERT31 adopts self-supervised learning to learn latent representations of TCR CDR3 sequences, thereby enhancing the performance of downstream tasks, such as TCR-antigen binding prediction, sequence clustering, and engineered TCR design.

In this study, we introduce tcrLM, a lightweight MLM designed to predict TCR-antigen binding specificity. By randomly masking segments of TCR sequences and training the model to infer masked regions, we develop a pretrained encoder that can extract informative and expressive features from TCR sequences. To enhance robustness, we also incorporate virtual adversarial training into the tcrLM architecture. For pretraining, we curated the largest set of TCR CDR3 sequences to date (113,888,692 distinct sequences). Next, we fine-tune it for TCR-antigen binding prediction. The model was evaluated on three test datasets—independent, external, and COVID-19—and the results verified that tcrLM outperformed existing TCR prediction methods as well as mainstream protein language models. Furthermore, we validated the correlation between the tcrLM’s predicted TCR-neoantigen binding scores and immunotherapy outcomes in a melanoma cohort. Interestingly, tcrLM was able to effectively capture the biochemical properties and positional preference of amino acids within the TCR CDR3 sequences. These findings underscore the potential of tcrLM in advancing immunotherapy and personalized medicine.

Results

TCR-antigen binding specificity prediction framework

We utilize the BERT-based language model26 and its training methodology to effectively process and analyze TCR sequences (Figure 1). Our model employs an MLM to learn bidirectional contextual information by reconstructing masked tokens from their surrounding context. The encoder, composed of stacked attention mechanism modules, is trained to capture meaningful representations of TCR sequences. Unlike other protein language models trained on generic protein sequences, such as UniRef50 or UniRef90 sets, we compiled a dataset comprising over 100 million TCR CDR3 distinct sequences obtained from diverse sources (Table S1) to pretrain our model. During the fine-tuning stage, the pretrained encoder was used to derive the embeddings of TCR and antigen sequences, which were subsequently concatenated and passed through a projection head to predict pTCR binding specificity. We believe that this study makes at least four significant contributions: 1) We develop a lightweight MLM to learn the representations of TCR CDR3 sequence. Also, virtual adversarial training is introduced to reduce the model’s sensitivity to slight input variations, thereby enhance its generalizability. 2) For pretraining, we curated more than 100 million distinct TCR CDR3 sequences from more than ten databases and publications. To our best knowledge, this is the largest set of TCR CDR3 sequence to date. Also, we established three large-scale pTCR binding test sets for performance evaluation. 3) Our extensive experiments demonstrated that tcrLM outperforms current state-of-the-art pTCR binding prediction methods and also exhibits superior performance compared with other protein language models, even those with significantly larger parameter sizes. 4) We demonstrated that the predicted pTCR binding scores closely correlated with the immunotherapy outcomes in a melanoma cohort. Moreover, the attention scores enabled us to capture residual biochemical properties and positional preference.

Performance evaluation on diverse test sets

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of tcrLM, we conducted a comparative analysis against a suite of current state-of-the-art models, including PanPep,21 ERGO2,24 DLpTCR,23 ATM-TCR,39 TEIM,40 TEPCAM,41 and TULIP.36 To ensure a rigorous and fair comparison, all these competing models were retrained using their published source codes on our constructed benchmark training set. Two other methods, pMTnet∗22 (no available training code) and TAPIR∗37 (web server only), were included but not retrained. Especially, because each competing method involves distinct model configurations and hyperparameters, we conducted grid search-based hyperparameter tuning for all these retrainable methods, starting from their default settings. The optimized hyperparameters (Table S2) reduce model-specific biases and ensure a fair and objective comparison. Performance comparison was conducted on three different test sets—hold-out, external, and COVID-19—which exhibit different degrees of antigenic peptide overlap ratios with the training data (Table S3). The hold-out test set contains only a small fraction of unseen peptides, the external test set contains approximately 65% unseen peptides, while the COVID-19 test set consists entirely of previously unseen peptides. These test sets with varying overlap ratios enable a comprehensive assessment of model generalization performance under increasingly challenging settings.

The experimental results are summarized in Figure 2. It can be found that tcrLM achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.937 and an area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR) of 0.933 on the hold-out test set. Its performance is on par with current state-of-the-art models, such as TEPCAM (AUROC = 0.956) and TEIM (AUROC = 0.955), and it significantly outperforms other methods like DLpTCR (AUROC = 0.823) and ATM-TCR (AUROC = 0.750). When evaluated on the external test set, tcrLM maintained robust performance, achieving an AUROC of 0.887 and an AUPR of 0.894 (Figure 2B). In contrast, other competing models that excelled on the hold-out set exhibited dramatic performance degradation. For instance, the AUROC of ERGO2 declined to 0.854, TEIM to 0.863, while PanPep experienced a substantial drop to 0.504. To validate tcrLM’s generalization capability in response to emerging novel pathogens, we conducted an evaluation on the large-scale COVID-19 test set. This dataset comprises unique peptides that were never seen during the training phase, thereby fully simulating real-world prediction scenarios targeting novel viruses. Evaluation results indicated that tcrLM exhibited advantageous zero-shot generalizability over competing methods, achieving an AUROC of 0.596 and an AUPR of 0.603 (Figure 2C). In contrast, the AUROC values for ERGO, DLpTCR, and PanPep were merely 0.515, 0.528, and 0.500, respectively, suggesting that these models degraded to near-random guessing when tested on unseen antigenic peptides. TEIM, which performed relatively well (AUROC = 0.591), demonstrated performance slightly inferior to that of tcrLM overall. Although the performance metrics are constrained by the inherent difficulty of the task, the advantage of tcrLM over baseline models is remarkable.

To further examine our model’s ability to prioritize pTCR bindings, we calculated the positive predictive value (PPV) for the top-ranked predicted pTCR samples. Specifically, we computed the PPV for the top 100, top 1,000, and top 5,000 predictions. The results showed that tcrLM achieved PPV values of 99%, 98.7%, and 96.74% for the top 100, top 1,000, and top 5,000 predictions on the hold-out test set (Figure ??d), respectively. In comparison, other methods demonstrated inferior prioritization capacity compared with tcrLM. On the external and COVID-19 test sets, tcrLM again achieved best or at least comparable PPV values (Figures 2E and 2F). These results indicated that tcrLM can accurately identify high-probability binding pairs from a vast pool of candidates, thereby providing robust support for its application in vaccine design and immunotherapy against future emerging infectious diseases.

To verify the effectiveness of the pretrained encoder, we conducted ablation experiments to evaluate its impact on pTCR prediction performance. Instead of utilizing the pretrained encoder to extract features from input sequences, we directly fed sequence embeddings into the projection head for binding prediction. This approach allowed us to independently assess the impact of removing the TCR encoder, the antigen encoder, or both simultaneously. The performance of the three ablated models revealed that the removal of the pretrained encoder consistently led to a performance decline (Figure 2G). In particular, the removal of the TCR encoder resulted in a significant drop in AUC from 0.937 to 0.86, highlighting the critical role of the pretrained encoder in extracting features from TCR sequences. We also tested the effect of virtual adversarial training and found that its removal resulted in an approximately 1% reduction in performance.

TcrLM outperforms other protein language models

To explore the influence of parameter size on performance, we built three variants of the tcrLM model: tcrLM-S, tcrL-M, and tcrLM-L, corresponding to small, medium, and large parameter sizes, respectively. Detailed configurations of the tcrLM variants are summarized in Table S4. We pretrained them on our collected 100M-scale TCR CDR3 sequences, and the three variants spent approximate 3, 7, and 16 days on our workstation, respectively. We found that tcrLM-L achieved the lowest perplexity (Figure 3A), suggesting that a large number of parameters improved the model’s ability to fit sequence data effectively.

We further fine-tuned four other protein language models pretrained on generic protein sequence sets (e.g., UniRef50 and UniRef90) to predict pTCR bindings. These language models include proteinBERT,42 ProtFlash,34 ProstT5,43 ESM2,44 CVC,30 and TCR-BERT.31 We compared their parameter sizes (Figure 3B, log2 scale) and found that ProstT5 had the largest parameter size (3 billion), while tcrLM-S and ProteinBERT had much smaller parameter sizes. We used tcrLM-M in performance evaluation experiments because it had slightly more parameters than ProteinBERT but much fewer than the other protein language models.

For pTCR binding prediction, we fed the TCR and antigen embeddings derived from these pretrained language models into the projection head. During the fine-tuning stage, the parameters of the pretrained encoders were kept frozen. To evaluate the degree of fitness to the pTCR training samples, we plotted their loss curves over training epochs (Figure 3C). The results demonstrated that all models converged, with CVC achieving the lowest loss value, followed by TCR-BERT and ProstT5. Next, we evaluated their performance on the independent, external, and COVID-19 test sets, respectively. The results showed that tcrLM achieved superior performance and generalization capability on most metrics across all three test sets (Figure 3D). Although ProstT5 surpassed tcrLM on certain performance metrics, this came at the cost of a parameter size approximately 40 times larger than that of tcrLM. CVC and TCR-BERT demonstrated comparable performance to tcrLM on independent and external test sets. However, their performance on the COVID-19 test set fell short of tcrLM, indicating slightly inferior generalization capabilities. Other models exhibited performance inferior to that of tcrLM, underscoring tcrLM’s ability to outperform previously published protein language models in TCR-specific tasks.

Predicted TCR-antigen bindings indicate immunotherapy outcomes

To further explore the predictive capacity of tcrLM, we conducted an exploratory analysis of the predicted pTCR binding scores on a cohort of patients with advanced melanoma.45 This cohort consists of 29 patients who underwent treatment by immune checkpoint inhibitors. On the basis of the TCR-seq and genomic sequencing data, we took each missense mutation as an anchor and generated all possible 9-mer peptides covering this anchor. After extracting CDR3 sequences from the TCR-seq data, we created all conceivable peptide-CDR3 pairs for each patient, yielding a total of 81,851,486 pTCR candidates.

We scored these candidate pairs by using fine-tuned tcrLM and selected the top 25,000 highest-scored pTCRs for each patient. By stratifying the patients into benefit, non-benefit, and long-term survival groups, we observed that patients in the long-term survival group had higher scored pTCRs than those in the other groups (Figure 4A). Subsequently, we categorized the patients into four categories according to RECIST criteria: complete remission (CR, n = 2), partial remission (PR, n = 5), stable disease (SD, n = 9), and progressive disease (PD, n = 12) groups; we found that the predicted binding scores of the four groups differed significantly from each other (Figure 4B). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) verified the statistically significant differences among these groups (F test, p value = 0.01). These findings validated the correlation between higher pTCR predicted scores and better immunotherapy outcomes, offering a new perspective and a possible clinical indicator for tumor immunotherapy.

Finally, to confirm the correlation between highly scored pTCR pairs by tcrLM and improved clinical outcomes, we conducted survival analysis on this cohort. We considered the top 2% highly scored pTCR pairs as high-confidence bindings and stratified patients with such pairs into the high-confidence group, while the remaining patients were placed in the low-confidence group. The survival analysis showed that the high-confidence group exhibited significantly higher overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) than the low-confidence group (Figures 4C and 4D). Although the difference in PFS did not exhibit statistical significance, it may be attributed to the limited sample size of the cohort. These findings suggest that the patients with highly scored pTCR bindings benefited more from immunotherapy and yielded favorable clinical outcomes.

TcrLM captures residual biochemical properties and positional preference

To investigate whether tcrLM has learned the physicochemical properties of amino acids, we applied t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE)46 to project the high-dimensional embeddings of 20 amino acids obtained from the attention weight matrices into a two-dimensional space. Similar to the observations in other protein language models such as ESM-1b32 and ProtFlash,34 the t-SNE plots revealed distinct clustering patterns of amino acids within the embedding space (Figure 5A). Specifically, amino acids with similar physicochemical properties formed well-defined clusters: hydrophobic and polar residues formed a distinct group, and aromatic residues concentrated in another cluster. Furthermore, amino acids were also grouped according to molecular weight and charge. These results highlight the tcrLM’s capability to learn and encode fundamental amino acid properties in the latent space. To further evaluate the model’s ability to capture relationships between different amino acids, we calculated the cosine similarity for each residue pair based on their embeddings from the final projection layer, and the results are displayed in a heatmap (Figure 5B). For comparison, we present the heatmap of the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix, a well-established benchmark derived from expert-curated sequence alignments. Notably, a strong concordance was observed between the cosine similarity scores and the BLOSUM62 substitution scores. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient and mean-squared error (MSE) between the two heatmaps were 0.692 and 0.0027, respectively. These findings indicate that tcrLM effectively captures the likelihood of similar amino acids substituting for one another, aligning with biologically “tolerant” substitutions that minimally affect protein structure and function.

To elucidate the importance and positional preferences of amino acids within TCR CDR3 sequences, we examined the cumulative attention weights at each position across sequences of varying lengths (5–20 residues) and visualized the results using a heatmap (Figure 5C). For shorter CDR3 sequences (length < 9 residues), amino acids located at the first position and the third-to-last position were found to be critical for pTCR binding. In contrast, for relatively long CDR3 sequences (length > 10 residues), the 10th position exhibited a markedly higher contribution, underscoring its significance in binding to antigens. We further examined the importance of amino acids at each position by using cumulative attention weights (Figure 5D), which revealed that the amino acid A at the first position and S at the second and third positions are highly significant. Moreover, F and Y showed predominant importance in the tail regions of the CDR3 sequence, highlighting their critical role in antigen binding.

Discussion

This study proposes a BERT-based large language model, named tcrLM, to enhance the accuracy of predicting pTCR binding specificity by utilizing the strong capability in feature extraction of large language models. We pretrained the model on more than 100 million TCR sequences and verified its performance on downstream tasks. Our results show that tcrLM achieved equivalent or improved performance relative to several state-of-the-art methods on independent, external, and COVID-19 test sets, with the degree of improvement varying by dataset. Moreover, tcrLM’s predicted TCR-neoantigen binding scores correlated with immunotherapy responses and clinical outcomes in a preliminary melanoma cohort.

We assembled a large-scale dataset (>100 million CDR3 sequences), which may support richer representations and improved downstream performance. While several studies have proposed TCR-specific language models for predicting pTCR binding, these models exhibited variable performance on our curated test set. We also compared our lightweight tcrLM with larger protein language models; while some larger models achieved strong performance in certain settings, it was found that tcrLM nonetheless offers substantial gains in parameter efficiency. These results suggest that a compact model pretrained on TCR CDR3 sequences can achieve competitive predictive capabilities for pTCR binding and, in our evaluations, presented advantages in zero-shot settings.

When utilizing the predicted pTCR binding scores to study immunotherapy responses, the absence of patient-specific HLA alleles makes us to consider all possible neoantigens and match them with TCRs to generate candidate pTCR pairs. In fact, most neoantigens cannot specifically bind to MHC class I molecules for presentation on the cell surface and, consequently, cannot be recognized by TCRs. Despite this discrepancy from actual biological processes, the predicted pTCR binding scores exhibited a significant correlation with immunotherapy responses and patients’ OS. Overall, our findings point to potential utility in vaccine design and immunotherapy, subject to further validation.

Limitations of the study

While tcrLM demonstrates competitive performance across multiple benchmarks, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, tcrLM framework is based solely on primary sequence representations and does not explicitly incorporate three-dimensional structural information of TCR-pMHC complexes. Because antigen recognition is fundamentally determined by spatial complementarity, electrostatic interactions, and conformational dynamics at the binding interface, sequence-only modeling may be insufficient to fully resolve structural determinants of specificity, particularly for peptides with similar sequences but divergent conformations. Second, although tcrLM exhibits improved performance relative to prior approaches, predictive accuracy declines substantially in fully zero-shot settings involving entirely unseen peptides. This highlights the persistent challenge of out-of-distribution generalization and suggests that learned sequence representations alone may not fully capture the transferable binding principles across diverse antigen landscapes. Finally, negative samples were generated through random TCR-peptide mismatching, a common but imperfect strategy that may introduce artificial biases and produce overly simplistic non-binding examples. Such construction schemes could inflate performance estimates and may not faithfully reflect biologically realistic non-interacting pairs.

Method details

Data source and preprocessing

To train a masked language model, we set about to establish the largest set of TCR CDR3 sequences. For this purpose, we collected 113,888,692 distinct TCR CDR3 sequences from more than ten databases and publications (Table S1). With over 100 million sequences, our model is able to capture underlying feature from TCR sequences. The sequence frequency distribution with respect to lengths is illustrated in Figure S1A, which reveals that most CDR3 sequences range between 10 and 20 amino acids in length. The frequency distribution of associated antigen sequences is shown in Figure S1B, indicating that most antigens 9-mer in length.

For fine-tuning, we built a benchmark dataset of pTCR bindings by collecting a substantial amount of pTCR binding data from various databases. We consider both the α and β chains of TCR and treat them as independent CDR3 sequences, as previous studies have demonstrated both of them play a critical role in antigen recognition. Specifically, we define a distinct sample as a unique combination of an antigen and a TCR sequence. The pTCR training dataset consists of 258,362 bindings, covering 890 unique antigens and 96,675 unique TCR CDR3 sequences. To our best knowledge, this is the largest pTCR binding dataset to date.

For performance evaluation, we establish an external test set comprising 97,043 pTCR bindings across 1,239 unique peptides and 59,738 CDR3 sequences collected from numbers of various publications. In addition, a large-scale test set is sourced from ImmuneCODE database,48 consisting of 520,000 pTCR bindings between COVID-19 virus-derived antigens and human TCRs. As shown in the Unseen Peptide column of Table S3, the hold-out test set contains only a small fraction of unseen peptides, the external test set contains approximately 65% unseen peptides, while the COVID-19 test set consists entirely of previously unseen peptides. These test sets with varying overlap ratios enable a comprehensive assessment of model generalization performance under increasingly challenging settings.

The negative pTCR samples were created by randomly mismatching TCR and peptide sequences, till the same number of negative samples were generated. During the fine-tuning stage, we randomly select 10% of the pTCR binding dataset as an independent test set, while the remaining 90% is used as the training set. The 5-fold cross-validation is used to optimize the model hyperparameters.

Retraining comparative models

To ensure fairness, transparency, and reproducibility in performance comparison, we provide a detailed description of the training and configuration procedures for all competing methods. All competing methods were evaluated using exactly the same training and test data splits, thereby eliminating potential biases arising from data partitioning. We retrained seven representative TCR–peptide binding prediction methods— TEIM, PanPep, TEPCAM, ERGO2, TULIP, DLpTCR, and ATM-TCR—using source code obtained from their respective official open-source repositories. Two methods, pMTnet∗ (training code unavailable) and TAPIR∗ (web-server only), could not be retrained and were therefore compared directly. All these models were retrained and then evaluated on the same computational platform to ensure consistency. In addition, considering that different methods rely on distinct hyperparameter configurations, we conducted grid search–based hyperparameter optimization for all competing methods, starting from their default settings. To ensure full transparency, we have added a comprehensive summary table (Table S2) that explicitly reports the model architectures, hyperparameter choices, and training configurations used for each competing method (excluding pMTnet and TAPIR). These measures collectively ensure that our benchmarking results are fully reproducible and that the reported performance reflects a rigorous and unbiased comparison.

TcrLM architecture

The tcrLM model begins with pretraining a lightweight BERT-based masked language model on large-scale TCR CDR3 sequences to extract expressive features. It also incorporates virtual adversarial training for robustness, subsequently fine-tuning the pretrained encoder to predict TCR-antigen binding specificity (Figure 1A. The masked language model is trained to recover the masked segment using the surrounding sequences on the collected more than 100 million TCR sequences (Figure 1B). Each TCR CDR3 sequence is padded to a fixed length of 20, since all CDR3 sequences do not exceed 20. We randomly generate a mask of length between 3 and 5 to cover a continuous segment in the TCR sequence. The masked sequence passes through an embedding layer that converts each token into a high-dimensional embedding. The embeddings are then taken as input to the encoder and ultimately mapped into a 20 × 512 matrix, which is transformed to 20∗21 matrix via a linear layer. We introduced the rotary positional embedding (RoPE)49 into tcrLM. This method encodes absolute positions using rotation matrices and integrates explicit relative positional dependencies into self-attention calculations. RoPE offers the flexibility in sequence length, decays the dependencies between tokens as their relative distance increases, and provides the capability to enhance linear self-attention with relative positional encoding. Furthermore, we incorporated the mixed chunk attention (MCA)50 to combine the advantages of both local attention and linear attention mechanisms.

In our practice, we applied different learning rates to distinct components of the model. For the encoder, the learning rate was set to 1e-6, while for the subsequent fully connected layers, it was adjusted to 1e-4. The optimizer was set to Adam. The pretraining was conducted on a CentOS Linux 8.2.2004 (Core) system, equipped with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Silver 4210R CPU running at 2.40GHz, along with a GeForce RTX 4090 GPU and 128GB of RAM. The model was implemented using the PyTorch 2.2.1 framework.

Predicting pTCR binding

The pretrained encoder is subsequently used for the prediction task of pTCR binding. As shown in Figures 1C and 1D, the antigen sequences and TCR sequences are padded to the maximum length of 15 and 20, respectively. After passing through an embedding layer, these two sequences are taken as input of the pretrained encoder, and transformed into matrices of dimensions 15 × 512 and 20 × 512. Subsequently, these two matrices are concatenated along the feature dimension to form a 35 × 512 matrix, which is then flattened into a 17,920-dimensional vector. This vector passes through a fully-connected layer and is projected to a 2-dimensional vector, followed by softmax layer to obtain the final predicted probabilities. Virtual adversarial training

Given the vast diversity of the TCR repertoire, the training data currently available is still limited and even biased. This poses a tough challenge on the development of robust prediction models for pTCR binding. To address this challenge, we employ virtual adversarial training to enhance the model’s generalizability. Since protein sequences are discrete, we introduce perturbations in the embedding layer of the sequences.51 For this purpose, we create adversarial examples designed to maximize the loss function by applying adversarial perturbations to the sequence embeddings, namely, the adversaries are generated in the direction of gradient ascent and constrained by L2 norm. This approach helps reduce the model’s sensitivity to slight input variations, thereby enhancing its generalizability. By incorporating virtual adversarial training, we aim to build a more robust and reliable prediction model, capable of handling the vast diversity of the TCR repertoire even with limited and potentially biased training data.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The performance of the predictive models was evaluated using multiple comprehensive classification metrics, including Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPR), Accuracy, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), F1 Score, Precision, Recall, and Specificity. Additionally, Positive Predictive Value (PPV) was calculated for the top-ranked predictions to evaluate the models’ capacity to prioritize high-confidence pTCR bindings.

For the exploratory clinical cohort analysis, statistical differences in the predicted pTCR binding scores across varying immunotherapy response groups (CR, PR, SD, PD) were evaluated using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Survival analysis, including Overall Survival (OS) and Progression-Free Survival (PFS), was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and statistical significance between the high-confidence and low-confidence groups was determined via the log rank test. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value <0.05. All statistical analyses and performance metrics were calculated using SciPy Python package.
