Molecular Epidemiology of Candidozyma auris Within a Case Cluster in North-Central Florida: Diverse Origins and Long-term Persistence of Strains
Authors: J Glenn Morris, Massimiliano S Tagliamonte, Rebeccah L Messcher, Tamara Revazishvili, Brian A Bourgeois, Zachary D Kaplan, Emil A Furat, Nicole M Iovine, Kartikeya Cherabuddi, John A Lednicky, Marco Salemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Candidozyma auris, first recognized in 2009, has emerged as a pathogen of major global concern, particularly in association with health care–associated infections in long-term care facilities.MethodsIn a study of infected and colonized patients conducted between 1/17/2023 and 11/30/2023 at our medical center in North-Central Florida, whole-genome sequence data were obtained for 43 C. auris isolates from 36 patients.ResultsThirteen (30%) of the 43 isolates were from cultures collected as part of an investigation of a possible infection, with blood (7 isolates) being the most common source; 8 (62%) of the 13 patients with clinical infections died. Isolates were within either a Clade I monophyletic subclade associated with European and Middle Eastern strains (n = 27) or were from Florida subclades within C. auris Clade III (n = 16). In 7 instances, multiple isolates with virtually identical genetic profiles were isolated from the same patient at time intervals that ranged from 3 weeks to 7 months, with, in some instances, intervening negative cultures. All isolates were resistant to triazoles, albeit with resistance mutations at different nucleotide positions and within different genes for Clade I and Clade III isolates. One Clade I isolate was resistant to echinocandins.ConclusionsData are consistent with a point-source C. auris outbreak involving a Clade I subclade of possible European origin, combined with multiple introductions and transmission of Clade III isolates from Florida. Strains were able to persist for extended periods of time in colonized/infected patients.Keywords: Candidozyma auris, fungus infections, fungus colonization, fungus persistence, resistance to anti-fungal agents

Candidozyma auris (previously known as Candida auris [1]) was first identified in 2009, isolated from the ear canal of a patient in Tokyo; retrospective studies of fungal isolates from clinical cases suggest that infections were present as early as 1996 in South Korea [2–4]. In the intervening years, C. auris has spread rapidly across multiple countries and continents and has emerged as a major cause of health care–associated infections, particularly within long-term care facilities [5–7]. The first documented US case was reported to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and Prevention (CDC) in 2016; case numbers have increased rapidly since that time, with 4514 new clinical cases reported to the CDC in 2023 [8]. In its fungal pathogens priority list, the World Health Organization (WHO) placed C. auris in its “critical” group, reflecting its rapid global dissemination and its resistance to major classes of antifungal drugs.

At a molecular level, 6 major C. auris clades have been identified/designated, with names based on the location of the first reported isolate: Clade I (South Asia); Clade II (East Asia); Clade III (South Africa); Clade IV (South America); Clade V (Iran); and Clade VI (Bangladesh/Singapore) [2, 4, 9]. Based on molecular clock analysis, the earliest strains were within Clade II, with a time to most recent common ancestor (TMRCA) of 317–400 years; Clades I and III appear to have emerged in the 19th century, while Clade IV emerged in the 20th century [10]. These major clades differ from each other by thousands of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [9–11]. In contrast, there has tended to be limited diversity within the major clades themselves (generally <70 SNPs), although smaller clusters/subclades are starting to be identified. There are also differences in patterns of antimicrobial resistance among and within clades, correlating with specific mutations and/or acquisition of resistance genes. Clades I–IV have been isolated in the United States, although there is evidence of geographic variability, with case clusters within specific geographic areas tending to be restricted to a single clade [6].

Based on CDC reporting, the 5 states with the highest total number of reported clinical C. auris cases between 2016 and 2023 were Florida, California, New York, Illinois, and Nevada [8]. C. auris was first identified in our medical center (UFHealth), which has an embedded long-term acute care floor, in North-Central Florida in June 2018, with recurring isolation of the microorganism from both infected and colonized patients since that time. To facilitate development of control programs, we were interested in knowing if we were dealing with a single “local” clonal group or if isolates were coming from diverse sources, if patients were persistently infected/colonized with a single strain or clone, and how resistance patterns correlated with strain/clone.

METHODS

Collection of data from patients from whom C. auris was isolated from a clinical site as part of evaluation of an active infection (“infected patients”) or during screening for C. auris colonization (“colonized patients”) was initiated in 2018; numbers of infected and colonized patients by month are shown in Figure 1. Screening criteria for colonization were based on CDC recommendations and review of available literature at the time and included any patient with a medically complex history such as multiple admissions to health care facilities and needing various devices such as gastronomy tubes and tracheostomy. Samples were collected by taking a dry media culture swab (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and vigorously rubbing the swab on bilateral axilla and groin sites. In the UFHealth microbiology lab, swab samples were plated on CHROMagar Candida (Becton Dickinson, NJ, USA), and the identity of any organism that grew was confirmed by mass spectroscopy (BioMérieux, Marcy-l′Etoile, France). Screening criteria were modified in the summer of 2024 to focus on patients who had admission of 5 or more days within the past year to the following facility types: long-term acute care hospital (LTACH), skilled nursing facility (SNF), acute care hospital, inpatient rehab hospital, and prison hospital.

Number of C. auris–infected and –colonized cases, UFHealth, by month, 2018–2025.

Isolates from 36 randomly selected patients were selected for sequencing; this included 7 patients for whom 2 isolates, collected at different time points, were available, resulting in a total of 43 isolates. Thirteen of these isolates were from clinical sites that had been cultured during active infections, with the remainder isolated during screening for colonization. Isolates were collected between 1/17/2023 and 11/30/2023. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for antifungal agents were determined at the UFHealth clinical laboratory using a Thermo Scientific Y04 Panel; clinical isolates were also sent to the Florida Department of Health laboratories and from there to the Tennessee Department of Health (Nashville, TN, USA) for confirmatory susceptibility testing. Testing included screening for resistance to the echinocandin class drugs caspofungin and micafungin and triazole class drugs fluconazole, itraconazole, and voriconazole, with more limited MIC data available for isuvuconazole, anidulaconazole, and posaconazole; breakpoints followed CDC recommendations [12].

For sequencing, de-identified C. auris isolates were received from the UFHealth clinical laboratory as trypticase soy agar slant tube (Trypticase Soy Agar, Becton Dickinson BBL, Thermo Scientific, Bothell, WA, USA) cultures. Cultures were re-streaked onto HardyCHROM Candida + auris chromogenic agar plates (Hardy Diagnostics, Santa Maria, CA, USA), forming teal-colored colonies. Individual colonies were picked and liquid cultures prepared in Sabouraud Dextrose Broth (Becton Dickinson DIFCO, Thermo Scientific, USA) incubated at 37°C. Yeast cells were collected by centrifugation and sphereoplasts prepared with lyticase enzyme (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), after which gDNA was extracted for sequencing using a Qiagen (Germantown, MD, USA) DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit, as recommended by the manufacturer. Whole-genome sequencing of the gDNA was performed by Novogene Corporation, Inc. (Sacramento, CA, USA); SRA accession numbers are included in Supplementary Table 1.

Bioinformatic Methods

Isolates were mapped to isolate B11205 (NCBI assembly accession No. GCA_016772135.1) and genotyped using CDCgov/mycosnp-nf pipeline, version 1.5 [13], run using Nextflow, version 24.04.2 [14, 15]. Output alignment and variant call format (VCF) files were visualized with IGV, version 2.19.7 [16], to visually examine drug resistance mutations (Supplementary Table 2).

Our 43 samples were analyzed together with published C. auris isolate raw reads (Supplementary Table 3), for a total of 399 genomes analyzed. Parsimony-informative sites were extracted from the SNP alignment, and a multiclade maximum likelihood (ML) tree was calculated using IQ-TREE, version 2.1.3 [17]; the best fitting evolutionary model was calculated with jModelTest [18, 19], plus ascertainment bias correction for variant sites only [20]. Branch support was calculated by running 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates [21].

For phylodynamics purposes, variant codon alignments for Clade I and Clade III were created with custom Python scripts starting from binary alignment mapped files, and isolates missing the sampling date were removed from the alignments. The clock signal was assessed by correlation between root to tip divergence and sampling dates using TempEst [22]. The time-scaled tree was calculated with BEAST X [23] using a relaxed clock and Bayesian SkyGrid tree prior to the codon model SRD06 [24], which unlinks substitution rate parameters and rate heterogeneity models across codon position, with 1 partition for the first 2 codon positions, and 1 for the third position. The Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) was run for 1 000 000 000 iterations, with the first 10% discarded as burn-in.

Tree plots were drawn with GGTREE R package, version 4.0.1 [25], using RStudio, version 2025.09.01 build 401 [26], and R, version 4.5.1 [27].

RESULTS

Thirteen (30%) of the 43 isolates sequenced were from infections, with blood (7 isolates) being the most common source. These 13 isolates were from 13 patients, 8 (62%) of whom died. Based on a retrospective review of medical records, in at least 5 of these 8 fatal cases (all 5 of which included a report of fungemia), there was a reasonable likelihood that C. auris was a major contributor to mortality based on the temporal association of infection with mortality and the lack of another potentially fatal diagnosis at the time of death. Thirty isolates, from 28 patients, were identified during screening for colonization; 9 (32%) of those patients died. Based on analysis of sequence data, 27 isolates from 23 patients were in Clade I, with 16 isolates from 13 patients in Clade III. Rates of infection and death did not differ significantly between clades.

The mean SNP distance between UFHealth Clade I and Clade III isolates was 43 609; the mean intraclade SNP distances were 16.4 and 27.9 for Clades I and III, respectively. As shown in the maximum likelihood (ML) (Figure 2A and B) and maximum clade credibility (MCC) (Figure 3A and B) trees, Clade I isolates were monophyletic and would appear to represent a single introduction. Patients from whom a Clade I strain was isolated did not have a known contact outside of the primary UFHealth hospital (Shands Hospital) and associated long-term care facility, or from the local prison (4 patients), which utilizes these facilities. In the ML tree, the closest relatives to the Clade I strains were from France, then Germany and the United Arab Emirates.

In contrast to Clade I isolates, the UFHealth Clade III isolates did not fall into a single monophyletic clade, but, rather, were interspersed with other Floridian strains falling into 2 primary subclades, within a larger subclade containing Kenyan and South African isolates. This implies multiple sources of infection from lineages already known to be circulating in Florida. Five of the 11 patients with Clade III isolates were known to have had exposure to medical facilities in Florida outside of the UFHealth network.

In 7 instances, we had 2 isolates from the same patient, separated by time periods ranging from 3 weeks to 7 months; this includes 1 instance in which there were intervening negative screening cultures between the 2 positive cultures. In 5 instances, the initial culture was a screening culture, followed by a subsequent culture from an active infection; 3 of these second cultures were from blood (1 of which was obtained 7 months after the initial positive screening culture), and in 2 of these cases the patient died. On genetic analysis, Clade I paired samples were 2–6 SNPs apart, while Clade III paired samples were virtually identical (Figure 4). Clade I paired samples seemed to have higher variability than Clade III paired samples, but all still within the wide ranges of reported C. auris mutation rates [28, 29].

SNP distance violin plots. Paired samples are samples derived from the same patient at different times; unpaired samples are samples only sequenced once. The information above the plots summarizes the mean distances between groups of samples. Abbreviation: SNP, single nucleotide polymorphism.

MICs for antifungal susceptibility testing are reported in Supplementary Table 4; data were available for 30 isolates. Based on CDC breakpoints [12], all strains were resistant to triazole antifungals. On genetic analysis, all Clade 1 strains had both a Tyr132Phe mutation in the ERG11 gene and an Ala583Ser mutation in the TAC1B gene (Supplementary Table 1). These mutations were not seen in the Clade III strains resistant to triazoles; instead, strains had a Val125Ala mutation in the ERG11 gene and a Asn647Thr mutation in the MRR1 gene. One Clade 1 strain (resistant to triazoles) was also resistant to echinocandins, with a Ser639Pro mutation in the FKS1 gene. This was a strain from a colonized patient who, to our knowledge, had not received antifungal therapy.

DISCUSSION

Despite sustained implementation of enhanced infection prevention procedures within our medical system, we continue to observe periodic colonization and infection with C. auris. The work presented in this report focuses on a relatively small group of sequenced isolates from 36 patients identified over an 11-month period in 2023. Our data underscore the potential clinical impact of C. auris infection, with mortality exceeding 60% among patients from whom C. auris was isolated from clinical sites during what appeared to be active infections. Directly attributing death in these patients to C. auris is difficult, as patients often had multiple underlying medical problems. Nonetheless, for at least 5 of the 8 fatalities in infected patients, findings were consistent with the hypothesis that C. auris was a major contributor to death, based on identification of fungemia, combined with an observed temporal association between infection and death and the lack of another major potentially fatal diagnosis at the time of death.

Unlike many hospital-based outbreaks that involve a single clonal strain [3–6, 30], our data suggest a more complex strain transmission dynamic. Our Clade I isolates, recovered almost exclusively from patients who had minimal contact with patients and facilities outside of our medical center, are monophyletic, consistent with a single strain introduction into the hospital and affiliated medical facilities (including the local prison [31]). The close phylogenetic relationship of these isolates to earlier European strains raises the possibility of a European origin, although in light of incomplete sampling and the time gap among samples there may well be missing links between these earlier strains and our isolates, precluding definitive conclusions regarding geographic source. In contrast, Clade III isolates did not form a monophyletic group but appeared to be distributed across multiple Florida-associated subclades, consistent with repetitive introductions through transfers into our tertiary referral center. Epidemiologic data indicate prior exposure to outside health care facilities in several cases, supporting this interpretation.

Our data from patients with serial positive cultures for C. auris are consistent with the hypothesis that C. auris colonization can persist for extended periods of time. Importantly, progression from colonization to infection (including bloodstream infections with mortality) was observed in 5 patients, reinforcing the clinical relevance of persistent colonization. We observed recovery of the same strain across extended periods of time despite intervening negative cultures, suggesting that “clearing” patients with 1 or more negative screening cultures is of uncertain value. Skin surfaces and intertriginous areas are the primary areas associated with C. auris colonization, with a high fungal burden correlated with environmental contamination, transmission, and occurrence of outbreaks [4, 32]. Effective patient “decolonization” methods are lacking, and decontaminating environments is challenging due to the resistance of C. auris to common hospital disinfectants such as quaternary ammonium compounds [4]. Although modifying the skin microbiome has been suggested to reduce colonization [33], further research on approaches to decolonization is urgently needed, particularly in long-term care settings.

Consistent with other studies and national surveillance data by the US Antimicrobial Resistance Laboratory Network [4, 9, 10, 34], all of our isolates were resistant to triazole antifungals, with resistance associated with previously described genetic mutations in ERG11, TAC1B, and MRR1. Echinocandin resistance was observed in 1 Clade I isolate in the setting of an FKS1 mutation [4, 35, 36]. Given that our Clade I isolates likely arose from a single introduction, the minor variability in resistance pattern may reflect point mutations within the local monophyletic cluster.

Our genomic analysis indicated that C. auris transmission reflected both local persistence and repeated importation, highlighting the need for integrated genomic surveillance and infection prevention strategies across health care networks. Although rapid expansion of resistant clones was not observed, selective pressure from antifungal therapy may promote the emergence and dissemination of resistant strains in this setting. Heightened and sustained infection prevention measures to limit health care–associated transmission of local strains, as well as robust screening of patients transferred from high-risk facilities (particularly those caring for long-term care patients) to minimize introduction of new strains [7], is clearly needed, together with effective antifungal stewardship.
