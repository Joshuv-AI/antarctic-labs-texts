Factors influencing the employed caregiver burden for patients with dementia residing in nursing homes: A cross-sectional study from China
Authors: Caixia Liu, Huiling Zheng, Baiyu Li, Shijia He, Rongrong Hu, Feifei Li, Yao Zheng, Weimin Lou, Biyan Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe institutionalization of care for patients with dementia is becoming a trend. Understanding the burden on employed caregivers and exploring associated factors are of great importance in practice. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the relationship between basic attributes, caring ability, and caregiver burden in employed caregivers practicing in nursing homes.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 541 employed caregivers in 11 four-star nursing homes in Zhejiang Province from April to December 2022. Caregiver burden was assessed using the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI). Demographic characteristics of participants, characteristics of the older patients with dementia, caring characteristics, training in dementia care, and caring abilities were collected for analysis of influencing factors. A hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted to explore the factors influencing the burden on employed caregivers in nursing homes.ResultsThe ZBI score of employed caregivers in nursing homes was 40.42 ± 10.18, representing a moderate caregiver burden. Factors such as age (U = 27.82, P < 0.001), residence (U = 7.89, P < 0.001), educational level (H = 55.81, P < 0.001), self-care of older patients with dementia (H = 85.21, P < 0.001), daily care hours (H = 73.25, P < 0.001), number of older people with dementia cared for (H = 14.56, P = 0.012) and training in dementia care (U = −9.43, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with caregiver burden.Caring ability was negatively associated with caregiver burden (r = −0.22, P < 0.01). Furthermore, after controlling for demographic characteristics, the characteristics of older people with dementia, caring characteristics, training in dementia care, and caring ability explained 8.5%, 5.8%, and 4.8% of the caregiver burden, respectively.ConclusionThe burden of employed caregivers on patients with dementia in nursing homes can be attributed to various factors. We recommend tailored interventions, such as dementia care training and reviewing the number and duration of hours worked to reduce the burden experienced by caregivers.

Introduction

Dementia is an acquired intellectual disability with cognitive dysfunction as its main symptom. According to the data published by the World Health Organization, more than 55 million people worldwide are diagnosed with dementia, and the prevalence is expected to increase to 152.8 million by 2050 [1]. The onset of dementia is closely related to age, and the prevalence of dementia has been gradually increasing with population aging. Notably, individuals aged 60 years and above experience significantly higher prevalence and mortality rates from dementia [2]. This dramatic rise is responsible for the serious economic and disease burden borne by affected families and society at large [3]. Its tremendous impact on the healthcare system inevitably makes dementia a global public health problem.

China is one of the fastest-aging countries in the world. At the end of 2022, China was estimated to have approximately 280 million people aged 60 years and above [4], with an overall dementia prevalence of 6.0%. This implies that roughly 15.07 million people aged 60 years and above in China have dementia, accounting for a quarter of the global population of patients with dementia [5]. As a progressively chronic disease, dementia is characterized by a decline in one or more cognitive domains, including learning and memory, language, executive functioning, complex attention, and perceptual-motor functioning. This leads to severe impairment and results in high dependence and interference with activities of daily living [6]. The high dependency associated with dementia makes the provision of care by family members a daunting task, creating a heavy caregiver burden. Additionally, the lack of professional competence, the heavy psychophysical burden associated with long-term care, and the lack of social support and assistance reduce the effectiveness of the care provided by family members [7]. Consequently, an increasing number of Chinese families send older people with dementia to nursing homes compared to a few decades ago [7]. Nursing homes employ caregivers to provide extensive care to patients with dementia, including round-the-clock nursing care, daily health monitoring, and daily life support, thus generating a significant caregiver burden. The high burden of employed caregivers has a negative impact on the quality of care provided and the prognosis of patients with dementia [8].

Previous research on caregiver burden primarily focused on family caregivers, with only a few studies examining the factors affecting the burden of caregivers employed in nursing homes [9]. Based on past studies [10,11], the concept of caregiver burden requires careful consideration of the general demographics of caregivers, including gender and literacy, training experience, caring ability, daily duration of care provided, and characteristics of patients being cared for, such as the presence of behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) [10,11]. In this preliminary study of caregivers in nursing homes, we aimed to comprehensively examine the important variables that were found in previous studies of family caregivers. Furthermore, we investigated the factors that had a greater impact on caregiver burden in nursing homes through hierarchical multiple regression.

Our overarching goal is to bring more attention to this important group, potentially increase supportive and helpful interventions, reduce caregiver burden, improve caring ability, and enhance the prognosis and quality of life for patients with dementia, thereby benefiting both patients with dementia and their caregivers.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this cross-sectional study, participants were recruited through convenience sampling from 11 four-star nursing homes in different cities across Zhejiang Province. These nursing homes were selected by random sampling from a pool of 21 four-star nursing homes in Zhejiang Province [12]. Random numbers were generated by a computer for each of the 21 four-star nursing homes and sorted according to size. Eleven nursing homes with odd numbers were selected for this study. Due to the growing trend of population aging in China, the state has implemented a star rating system for nursing homes. Nursing homes in Zhejiang Province are classified into five categories, ranging from one-star to five-star. This classification is based on essential, basic, and innovative indicators, and four-star nursing homes are required to score at least 780 points out of a total of 1,000 to maintain their rating [13]. At the time of our study, there were no five-star nursing homes in Zhejiang Province, and four-star homes were the highest.

For the regression analysis, the sample size was required to be at least 20 times the number of independent variables to verify validity. The study tested 13 independent variables, so the minimum sample size was 286, accounting for a 10% missed visit rate. The sample size for this study was adequate. Eleven nursing homes were included in the study, and we selected as many samples as possible to ensure the representativeness of the data. The inclusion criteria were employed caregivers in nursing homes who were: 1) ≥18 years old; 2) engaged in the continuous care of older people with dementia for ≥1 month; 3) providing care to older people (> 60 years) with dementia who met any of the 2011 National Institute of Aging-Alzheimer’s Association diagnostic criteria [14]; 4) voluntary participation in the study. The exclusion criteria included non-employed caregivers such as family members, friends, and social workers. Overall, 541 caregivers participated in the study.

Data collection and ethical consideration

This study was conducted in Zhejiang Province, China, between April and December 2022. Before data collection, the study manager contacted managers from the 11 nursing homes, explained the details of the study, and asked for their assistance. Five equally trained researchers collected data physically from caregivers using uniform instructions based on a full understanding of the meaning of each variable and proficiency in the assessment method. The purpose and methods of the study were fully explained to the employed caregivers, and informed consent forms were signed before commencing the assessment. Data was anonymized and used solely for research, and no personally identifiable information was obtained. To ensure the correctness and completeness of the data, queries raised by the caregivers were patiently and non-suggestively explained, all the items of the assessment were checked for completeness, and omissions were rectified. The study was approved by Ethical Review Committee of Zhejiang Hospital (2022 Clinical Audit No. (61K)-X1). The study was reported following the STROBE statement [15].

Measurement

The variables included in the study were demographic data, characteristics of older patients with dementia, caring characteristics, training in dementia care and caring ability, and caregiver burden.

Data collected on caregivers’ demographics included gender, age, residence, marital status, and educational level. The characteristics of older people with dementia included the level of self-care and the presence of BPSD. Caring characteristics included duration of work, daily care hours, and the number of older people with dementia cared for. Training in dementia care and caring ability included the use of a ‘yes-no’ question and the Nursing Ability Assessment Scale for Dementia Caregivers. The scale was compiled by a Chinese researcher in 2018 based on the 6 elements of the Farran Caregivers Skills Model [16] and was used to assess the ability of caregivers to provide quality care for patients with dementia. It contained 36 items divided into five factors divided into five dimensions, dementia knowledge with 13 items, care skills with 13 items, personal qualities with 4 items, caregivers’ health with 3 items, and relationship with 3 items. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with a total score ranging from 36 to 180. Higher scores indicated a higher level of caring ability. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale is 0.936 [17].

The Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI) [18], developed by Zarit, is a 22-item scale designed to assess the perceived burden experienced by caregivers of individuals with dementia. This scale is organized into four domains: Demands of care and social impact on caregiver (nine items); Confidence or control over the situation (five items); Psychological impact on caregiver (six items); and Worry about caregiving performance (two items). Data on each item was collected using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0, which implies “never,” to 4, which implies “nearly always.” The total score ranges from 0 to 88, with higher scores indicating a higher caregiver burden. A total score of 0–19 signified no or extremely mild caregiver burden; a score of 20–39 implied mild caregiver burden; a score of 40–59 indicated moderate caregiver burden; a score of more than 60 was considered a severe caregiver burden. In 2006, Lu et al. translated ZBI into Chinese, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient is 0.87, with good internal consistency reliability [19].

Data analysis

Bivariate and multivariate analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics [Mean ± SD, n (%), Median (P25, P75)] were used to describe the basic attributes, caring abilities, and caregiver burden of employed caregivers in nursing homes. The association between caregiver burden and basic attributes was examined using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test for post-hoc analysis due to the non-normal distribution of the data. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to test the correlation between caring ability and caregiver burden.

To identify the key variables assessed in this study that were important predictors of caregiver burden, a hierarchical multiple regression analysis was performed. The independent variables (P < 0.10) in the bivariate analysis of variance and correlation were included in a hierarchical multiple regression analysis, with caregiver burden as the dependent variable. The independent variables were entered into four models: the first model included control variables such as age group, residence, and educational level; characteristics of older patients with dementia were added to the second model; caring characteristics were added to the third model; and details on training in dementia care and caring ability were added to the fourth model. All differences were tested using a two-tailed test, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Basic attributes and univariate analysis of caregivers in nursing homes

Of the 541 employed caregivers in the 11 nursing homes, most were female (84.5%), living in rural areas (70.4%), and married (90.9%). The age and duration of work of the participants were (50.21 ± 5.50) years and (19.29 ± 10.42) months, respectively. A total of 196 participants (36.2%) had an educational level of at least elementary school, 33.7% had a junior high school education, and 30.1% had a high school education or above. Most older people with dementia were capable of partially self-care (76.4%) and had BPSD (72.6%). Most caregivers worked in nursing homes for at least 12 h per day (96.0%) and cared for more than one person with dementia (98.3%). More than half had no training in dementia care (53.6%). Discussion

Currently, nursing homes are the second most common place where older people with dementia receive effective care. However, the burden on employed caregivers, who are the main caregivers, has rarely been studied. In this study, the ZBI scores of employed caregivers for patients with dementia averaged 40.42 ± 10.18. Notably, 97.0% (525/541) of employed caregivers reported experiencing caregiver burden, which is higher than the approximately 80% of primary care caregivers and 90.3% of nursing home caregivers reported in other studies [11,20]. This disparity may be because our participants come from four-star nursing homes, where employed caregivers have higher care requirements and provide more sophisticated care, making them more prone to caregiver burden. Another explanation for this disparity is that patients with dementia in these nursing homes have poor self-care skills and require full-time care. They are also prone to neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as agitation, irritability, abnormal motor behaviors, depression, and hallucinations, which complicate care and contribute to caregiver burden and feelings of helplessness and sadness [21,22]. Overall, we found that 42.3% of caregivers experienced mild caregiver burden, while 53.8% experienced moderate burden, a higher rate than the 58.5% of family caregivers who reported moderate burden in a different study [23]. Employed caregivers typically care for approximately four patients with dementia on average, compared to a family member who usually cares for one relative [24]. As discussed in the introduction, caregivers of patients with dementia perform difficult tasks. Caregiver burden negatively impacts patients with dementia and their caregivers. Under the strain of caregiver burden, care deficits and accelerated progression of patients with dementia are common, resulting from caregivers’ insensitivity or indifference to the needs of care recipients [25]. There is also a link between the severity of dementia and negative changes in caregivers’ physical and mental health, leading to increased morbidity and mortality among caregivers [26]. Therefore, it is important to explore the factors affecting the burden of employed caregivers for patients with dementia in nursing homes to develop effective coping strategies, highlighting the need for our study.

By systematically examining the association between caregiver burden and the demographic characteristics of employed caregivers in nursing homes, we found that older caregivers experienced a greater burden, a finding consistent with other studies [24]. Similarly, this correlation has been observed among family caregivers of patients with dementia [27]. Employed caregivers from rural areas expressed a higher caregiver burden than those from urban areas, likely due to contextual effects in rural areas. In China, there is a general urban-rural disparity. People living in rural areas have poorer physical health, lower education levels, and more depressive symptoms than those living in urban areas. These factors necessitate greater effort in completing care tasks and acquiring care knowledge [28]. Additionally, economic issues are a secondary stressor of caregiver burden. Caregivers from rural areas want to take on more care tasks to earn higher pay but often lack the personal capacity to do so, resulting in an inability to offset the caregiver burden caused by economic issues [29]. Employed caregivers from rural areas have inherent limitations that lead to a higher caregiver burden. Furthermore, the education level of employed caregivers was also an influential factor in caregiver burden. Those with only an elementary school education experienced the highest caregiver burden, likely due to their limited knowledge about dementia, which makes them more susceptible to mood swings in response to disease-related events (e.g., the prognosis for recovery and disease deterioration), leading to heavier psychological burdens [30]. Notably, the relationship between educational level and caregiver burden was not linear. Caregivers with junior high school education experienced a lower burden than those with both higher and lower levels of education. Few specific studies have provided insights into the differences in burden relative to the educational level of employed caregivers. While some studies have concluded that family caregivers of patients with dementia who have low education bear a higher caregiver burden, others have not considered education as an influencing factor in caregiver burden [31,32]. This contradiction suggests that caregiver education for patients with dementia might play a specific role. However, the evidence is not conclusive.

Regarding the characteristics of older people with dementia, extensive research on the burden of family caregivers has established that behavioral disorders of dementia and psychiatric symptoms are key factors that adversely affect the health and burden of caregivers [9,33,34]. We found that self-care abilities and behavioral and psychological symptoms of older people with dementia were associated with caregiver burden, explaining 8.5% of caregiver burden after controlling for other factors. The challenges with self-care in patients with dementia require caregivers to invest more time and energy in general basic and life care [35]. Treatment could be impeded when older people with dementia have BPSD; caregivers often feel sadness and helplessness due to increased caring difficulties, thus increasing their burden [21,36]. This phenomenon has been reported among both employed caregivers and family caregivers of patients with dementia [32,37,38].

The model of caregiver burden and caring characteristics suggests that the number of older people with dementia cared for by caregivers and the amount of time spent providing care are important factors in caregiver burden. An increase in the number of people with dementia results in a significant increase in the intensity of care tasks in the same timeframe or a reduction in the intensity of care by extending the length of care; however, it also directly increases the length of care. Continuous heavy care consumes the energy of employed caregivers and, at the same time, leads to persistent burnout [39]. Long-term stress and lack of effective responses lead to physical and mental fatigue in caregivers, and over time, these transform into abuse and harm to the caregivers’ physical and mental health [40,41]. In addition, multiple comparisons showed a nonlinear U-shaped relationship between caring hours and caregiver burden. For caregivers employed for patients with dementia, fewer care of work may result from caring for patients with severe physical and mental illness; therefore, it is understandable that there is a higher level of burden associated with it, and the same effect may be seen with longer hours of care. Although no specific studies clearly indicate the cause of this effect, a similar relationship between daily caring hours and caregiver burden has been reported among family caregivers [42]. In one study, patients with dementia showed long sleep latency (> 40 min), short sleep duration, habitual sleep inefficiency, and daytime dysfunction. Their average total sleep duration was less than 6 h per day (74.3% were “short sleepers”), which is clearly not conducive to the maintenance of adults’ health [26]. Frequent nocturnal awakenings also indirectly increase caregivers’ care time and prevent adequate sleep [43]. Adding helpers or reducing caring hours could effectively reduce caregiver burden.

Currently, employed caregivers in nursing homes have not been institutionalized, with less than half (46.4%) reporting training in dementia care. The caregiver burden is significantly higher among untrained caregivers. It is well known that caregiver training reduces caregiver burden by increasing awareness of illness and encouraging good caring skills that ultimately improve caring ability [44,45]. Notably, we found that the caring ability score of caregivers who received training was 130.02 ± 10.96, which was higher than those untrained (127.90 ± 11.93), further illustrating the correlation between training and caring ability. Moreover, the caring ability score is negatively correlated with caregiver burden. Also, personal quality of caring ability was positively associated with caregiver burden. Personal qualities explore whether the caregivers put themselves in patients’ shoes and carry out self-reflection and self-learning in the process of caring. A high score means that the caregivers have higher caring and ethical expectations from themselves and may invest more time and energy in caring, causing problems such as sleep disorders and lack of concentration, which can increase caring ability but also inadvertently increase their own burden [46].

In general, although the demographic characteristics of employed caregivers are difficult to change through interventions, our findings can prompt managers to pay attention to employed caregivers who are older, have low literacy, and are from rural areas because of their increased risk of caregiver burden. Patients with BPSD are an important influencing factor, and focusing on symptom management in patients with dementia can reduce the burden on employed caregivers. Among the many factors influencing the burden of employed caregivers in nursing homes, care characteristics (number of older people with dementia cared for and hours of care) and caring ability can be improved through targeted interventions. Policymakers should improve the supervision mechanisms of nursing homes, protect the rights and interests of employed caregivers, and increase salaries. Institutional managers should reasonably arrange labor intensity, create a good organizational atmosphere, and provide more opportunities for employees with strong professional abilities [47]. Previous research has demonstrated the importance of dementia training courses in improving the understanding of the disease, nursing skills, and coping strategies for BPSD [48]. Employed caregivers for patients with dementia who are older and less knowledgeable may struggle with online training and have difficulty comprehending the content of the training manuals. Managers should provide regular face-to-face courses, scenario-based training, and case experience sharing. Theoretical training should cover daily living care, safety management, emergency response, and practical skills, such as nursing practice skills for patients with dementia, BPSD coping skills, and daily life care skills [47]. In addition, employed caregivers should be given the flexibility to choose training times, courses, and methods based on their individual needs [49].

This study is the first to examine the effects of basic attributes and caring ability on the caregiver burden for patients with dementia in nursing homes in China. After controlling for several difficult-to-modify demographic characteristics and certain characteristics of the patients with dementia, caring characteristics, and caring ability emerged as significant predictors of caregiver burden among employed caregivers. This provides a direction for other countries, especially those with healthcare systems similar to China, intending to reduce the burden on employed caregivers in nursing homes.

Limitation

The limitations of this study must be considered. First, although study participants were drawn from 11 different nursing homes, it was limited to Zhejiang Province, China. Therefore, the representativeness of the sample is limited, and it is difficult to represent the overall situation of employed caregivers of patients with dementia in all nursing homes in China. Given that the current management models of nursing homes vary from region to region in China, a multicenter study should be conducted across all regions of the country. Second, although we included four models that explained only 36.5% of caregiver burden, some studies confirmed the correlation between psychological characteristics and caregiver burden [50,51], which was not considered in our study.

Conclusions

With the tendency to institutionalize long-term care for dementia, preventing and reducing the burden on employed caregivers have become important components of human resource management in nursing homes. Therefore, we first sought to identify factors affecting the burden of employed caregivers in nursing homes. Characteristics of older people with dementia play an important role in caregiver burden, which suggests that reducing caregiver burden is a systemic project that requires consideration of not only the caregivers themselves but also the patients with dementia being cared for. The associations between various factors and caring characteristics (number of patients with dementia cared for and daily care hours), as well as caring ability and caregiver burden, were emphasized, and the effect of training on caring ability was further analyzed. Therefore, we suggest that nursing home managers systematically review the number of older people with dementia cared for and the daily care hours of employed caregivers, reduce the caregiver burden, and improve the quality of life of older people with dementia through ensuring training in dementia care.
