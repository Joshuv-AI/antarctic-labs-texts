The effectiveness of the Braun anastomosis in the management of severe hypoalbuminaemia occurring after a single anastomosis sleeve ileal bypass
Authors: Sükrü Salih Toprak, Yücel Gültekin, Ceyhun Pekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The global escalation of morbid obesity has expanded the scope of bariatric and metabolic surgery in addressing obesity and its associated comorbidities. This pilot study retrospectively evaluates the efficacy of Braun anastomosis in the management of nausea, vomiting and hypoalbuminaemia following single anastomosis sleeve ileal (SASI) bypass surgery.Patients and Methods:Nineteen patients who underwent SASI bypass for obesity and Type 2 diabetes mellitus between June 2020 and June 2023 and subsequently developed nausea, vomiting and hypoalbuminaemia were included. Braun anastomosis was performed as a revision procedure. Pre-operative and post-operative assessments at 1, 6 and 12 months included serum albumin, haemoglobin A1c and body mass index (BMI).Results:Pre-operative mean serum albumin levels of 1.86 ± 0.24 g/dL increased significantly to 3.38 ± 0.15 g/dL by the 6th post-operative month (P < 0.001). BMI decreased from a pre-operative mean of 32.58 ± 2.26 kg/m2 to 27.60 ± 1.53 kg/m2 at 6 months postoperatively (P < 0.001). Haemoglobin A1c showed a non-significant reduction from 6.40% ±0.60% preoperatively to 6.20% ±0.37% at 6 months postoperatively (P = 0.250). Bile reflux, present preoperatively, resolved by the 6th post-operative month. A marked reduction in nausea and vomiting was also noted following the revision surgery.Conclusions:Braun anastomosis was effective in managing the complications following SASI bypass, leading to significant improvements in serum albumin and BMI. Further studies are required to assess the long-term safety of SASI and the role of Braun anastomosis in the management of post-SASI complications. These results suggest that Braun anastomosis may play a valuable role in optimising the outcomes following SASI bypass.

INTRODUCTION

The global rise in morbid obesity has heightened the importance of bariatric and metabolic surgery for the treatment of obesity and its associated morbidities. These surgical interventions are considered the most effective means to combat obesity.[1] Amongst the newer procedures, single anastomosis sleeve ileal (SASI) bypass is a modified version of the Santoro procedure, combining the elements of the duodenal switch and biliopancreatic diversion (BPD) but with fewer malabsorptive components.

The reduction in appetite following SASI bypass is achieved through two primary mechanisms. The first mechanism involves a decrease in ghrelin levels after sleeve gastrectomy. The second mechanism is mediated by peptide YY (PYY) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), hormones secreted from the L-cells of the terminal ileum and colon when food rapidly reaches the distal small intestine. These hormones promote satiety and delay gastric emptying by reducing proximal bowel motility.[23]

As a novel procedure, SASI bypass exhibits both restrictive and malabsorptive properties. However, its malabsorptive component is less pronounced compared to procedures such as BPD and One Anastomosis Gastric Bypass, as the duodenum and proximal jejunum remain the part of the digestive process.[3]

The SASI bypass is a simple modification of the Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. Like other single anastomosis procedures, SASI bypass carries a potential risk of bile reflux. Braun anastomosis has been suggested as an effective solution to mitigate bile reflux in single anastomosis procedures.[4]

Anatomical restrictions following bariatric surgery can lead to the complications such as dysphagia, vomiting and serious metabolic disturbances. Malabsorption can also result in hypoproteinaemia, hypoalbuminaemia and excessive weight loss.[5] In this study, we evaluated patients who had previously undergone SASI bypass and subsequently required revision surgery with Braun anastomosis due to persistent nausea, vomiting and hypoalbuminaemia.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design and patient selection

In this pilot study, patients who underwent laparoscopic SASI bypass surgery for obesity and Type 2 diabetes mellitus at our clinic or affiliated centres between June 2020 and June 2023 were retrospectively evaluated. Nineteen patients who developed nausea, vomiting and hypoalbuminaemia and subsequently underwent revision surgery with Braun anastomosis were included. These patients commonly presented with periodic episodes of bilious vomiting occurring every 3–4 days and hypoalbuminaemia refractory to treatment.

Pre-operative evaluation for revision surgery

Before revision surgery, each patient underwent an endoscopic evaluation of the upper gastrointestinal tract. Albumin, haemoglobin A1c, body mass index (BMI) values and Modified Rhodes Index of Nausea and Vomiting (MAT) scores were recorded preoperatively, as well as at the 1st, 3rd and 6th months postoperatively. In the pre-operative period, albumin levels were increased above 2.5 g/dL through parenteral nutrition and human albumin therapy.

Surgical method

Revision surgery was performed laparoscopically in all patients. First of all, gastroileal anastomosis was found proximal to the pylorus. Then, 40 cm distal to this anastomosis, afferent and efferent ileal passages were found, and ileoileal (Braun) anastomosis was performed with a 45 mm linear endostapler. Drain was placed in the anastomosis line. There were no complications during the operation. Oral food intake was started on the 2nd post-operative day, and the patients were discharged on the 3rd day.

Follow-up

Patients were followed up at the 1st, 6th and 12th months postoperatively, during which albumin, haemoglobin A1c, BMI values and MAT scores were re-evaluated and recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normal distribution of variables was assessed through histograms, probability plots and Kolmogorov–Smirnov/Shapiro–Wilk tests. A one-way ANOVA test was used to compare albumin, haemoglobin A1c and BMI values across time points, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For nausea and vomiting, changes in MAT scores over time were evaluated using the Friedman test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Post hoc pairwise comparisons for albumin and BMI were performed using Tukey’s test, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for post hoc analysis of MAT scores.

RESULTS

This study evaluated patients who underwent revision surgery due to nausea, vomiting and hypoalbuminaemia. A total of 19 patients were included, and all underwent Braun anastomosis as the revision surgery.

Demographic data

The mean age of the patients was 44.16 ± 6.50 years, and 63.2% (n = 12) of the participants were women.

Albumin levels

The mean albumin levels were 1.86 ± 0.24 g/dL preoperatively, 2.68 ± 0.19 g/dL at the 1st post-operative month, 3.08 ± 0.19 g/dL at the 6th month and 3.38 ± 0.15 g/dL at the 12th month. The changes in albumin levels between the pre-operative period and the 1st, 6th and 12th post-operative months were statistically significant (P < 0.001). Notably, a significant increase was observed in albumin levels by the 12th post-operative month (P < 0.001) [Figure 1].

Body mass index

The mean BMI values were 32.58 ± 2.26 kg/m2 preoperatively, 30.99 ± 1.99 kg/m2 at the 1st post-operative month, 29.20 ± 1.58 kg/m2 at the 3rd month and 27.60 ± 1.53 kg/m2 at the 12th month. Significant differences in BMI were observed at all time points (P < 0.001) [Figure 2].

BMI change after braun anostomosis

Haemoglobin A1c levels

The mean haemoglobin A1c levels were 6.40% ± 0.60% preoperatively, 6.30% ± 0.53% at the 1st post-operative month, 6.20% ± 0.46% at the 3rd month and 6.20% ± 0.37% at the 12th month. No statistically significant differences were detected between the repeated measurements of haemoglobin A1c (P = 0.250).

Gastrointestinal findings

Although 19 patients presented with bile reflux and gastritis in the pre-operative period, no bile reflux was detected during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at the 12th post-operative month. The median (min–max) Modified Rhodes Index of Nausea and Vomiting (MAT) scores at the pre-operative and post-operative 1st, 6th and 12th months were 12 (6–18), 2 (0–6), 2 (0–4) and 2 (0–3), respectively. The difference between pre-operative MAT scores and post-operative measurements was statistically significant. However, no significant differences were observed between the 6th and 12th post-operative months (P = 0.37). No complications were detected postoperatively.

Based on these findings, significant improvements in albumin and BMI levels, but non-significant changes in haemoglobin A1c levels, were observed following revision surgery. Braun anastomosis proved effective in resolving bile reflux as evaluated by upper gastrointestinal endoscopy in the pre-operative and post-operative periods. Clinically, significant improvements in nausea and vomiting were also observed, as indicated by the MAT score, following revision surgery.

DISCUSSION

This study examined the effects of Braun anastomosis in managing the complications such as nausea, vomiting and hypoalbuminaemia following SASI bypass surgery. The findings indicate that while SASI bypass is an effective and safe method in bariatric surgery, it can cause serious complications in some patients.[6] In a multicentre study conducted by Mahawar et al. with 551 patients (2017), it was reported that SASI bypass should no longer be considered an experimental procedure. The study demonstrated that SASI was highly effective in short-term weight loss and contributed significantly to the remission of Type 2 diabetes mellitus.[7] The SASI technique was standardised by Bhandari et al. under the International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders in 2019.[2]

Single anastomosis stimulates the secretion of GLP-1 and PYY from the ileum by undigested nutrients passing through the ileal bypass, supporting weight loss through increased insulin secretion and reduced calorie intake. This makes SASI superior to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy.[489] In a study by Santoro et al., hypoalbuminaemia and malabsorption were not observed following bipartition surgeries.[3] However, for the first time in 2015, Chevallier et al. reported excessive weight loss and hypoalbuminaemia in patients who underwent single anastomosis surgery.[10] Similarly, Kermansaravi et al. detected excessive weight loss and hypoalbuminaemia in patients receiving high protein and nutritional support, raising the concerns about the malabsorptive component of SASI.[6] In the study by Mahdy et al., some patients required re-operation due to excessive weight loss after SASI, with the rate of bilious vomiting reported at 5.8%.[411] Salama et al. reported no significant changes in albumin levels or gastrointestinal complaints such as nausea and vomiting in their SASI patients.[12]

Bile reflux may trigger the vomiting reflex by irritating the stomach and oesophageal mucosa. It has been suggested that serotonin release from the gastric mucosa may increase after inflammation caused by bile reflux, stimulating the vomiting centre in the brainstem. Inflammation in the gastric mucosa due to bile reflux, along with the neutralization of stomach acid, can negatively affect protein digestion and absorption.[13] Braun anastomosis is recommended to reduce bile reflux in SASI and other single anastomosis procedures. Olmi et al. reported that the Braun anastomosis effectively corrected bile reflux in these procedures.[14] Similarly, our study demonstrated significant improvement in patients’ nausea and vomiting following revision surgery.

Another mechanism we believe contributes to malabsorption in SASI is the possibility that food passing through the pylorus re-enters the stomach due to the gastroileal anastomosis, allowing already digested food with reduced nutritional value to be re-digested, leading to malabsorption. Braun anastomosis prevents this by stopping food from re-entering the stomach, allowing it to move towards the caecum. This prevents the repeated digestion of nutritionally depleted food and thus helps reduce malabsorption. The results of this pilot study support the notion that Braun anastomosis may provide a solution to the malabsorption issue in SASI. In this context, future clinical and experimental studies will better elucidate the long-term effects of the SASI procedure and how the Braun anastomosis prevents malabsorption.

Chen et al. suggested that lengthening the common channel could prevent hypoalbuminaemia and excessive weight loss.[15] However, in our study, significant improvements in albumin levels were achieved without lengthening the common duct, solely through Braun anastomosis.

These results demonstrate that while SASI is effective for weight loss and Type 2 diabetes mellitus remission, malabsorptive complications may occur in some patients. These complications can be managed with revision surgery, but a long-term follow-up and nutritional support are crucial. Future studies should provide more data on the long-term safety and effectiveness of SASI and determine optimal patient selection criteria. In addition, the long-term effects of the Braun anastomosis and its role in addressing other potential complications should be further investigated. This study also highlights the potential benefits of Braun anastomosis in managing the complications following SASI bypass, suggesting that it may improve the safety of the procedure.

There are some limitations to this study. First, the study included only 19 patients, which may limit the generalisability of the results. The retrospective nature of the study may introduce bias due to the retrospective collection of data. In addition, the study was conducted in a single centre, and the results need to be confirmed with similar studies conducted in multiple centres. The post-operative nutritional and dietary management of patients was not standardised, potentially affecting the reliability of the results. The demographic characteristics of the patients (e.g., age, gender and comorbidities) were limited, and a wider demographic range would provide more generalisable results. Finally, only certain complications (i.e., nausea, vomiting and hypoalbuminaemia) were evaluated, and other possible side effects and complications were not considered.

CONCLUSIONS

Based on the current literature and this study’s findings, the integration of Braun anastomosis into SASI bypass procedures may reduce post-operative complications and improve the surgical outcomes. For this reason, Braun anastomosis may be recommended as a standard practice in bariatric surgery procedures.
