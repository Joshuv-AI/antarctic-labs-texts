Student perceptions of technology-enhanced simulation resources in laparoscopic training: a scoping review and implications for surgical instrumentation
Authors: Arley Denisse Vega Ochoa, Hannys Liseth Pinto Bautista, Wendis Johana Weber Ipuana, María de los Ángeles González Cuello, Anderson Díaz Pérez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLaparoscopic training increasingly uses technology-enhanced simulation resources, including virtual reality, augmented reality, box trainers, portable trainers and digital surgical curricula. In this review, these resources are analysed as technology-enhanced simulation resources rather than as uniformly “emerging” technologies, because some tools, particularly box trainers, are established components of laparoscopic education. Direct evidence for Surgical Instrumentation students remains limited, making transferability from other health-professions learners a central interpretive issue.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following PRISMA-ScR and JBI guidance. The review question was structured using the PCC framework: population, health sciences and surgical training students; concept, perceptions, attitudes, satisfaction, confidence, self-efficacy, acceptance and perceived usefulness; and context, laparoscopic surgery education. PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Redalyc and Google Scholar were searched for English- or Spanish-language publications from January 2020 to March 2025. Findings were charted and synthesized narratively rather than pooled statistically.ResultsA total of 1,800 records were identified in the retained review flow. Fifty-seven full-text reports were assessed, and 11 studies met the eligibility criteria. Most learner-facing studies that evaluated perception, satisfaction, acceptance, confidence or self-efficacy reported favorable responses, mainly related to confidence, satisfaction, perceived safety, self-efficacy and opportunities for repeated practice. The included evidence was heterogeneous and relied substantially on subjective measures. Only one study directly addressed Surgical Instrumentation students.ConclusionsTechnology-enhanced simulation resources appear acceptable to learners in laparoscopic training, but positive perception should not be equated with competence, clinical readiness or transfer of learning. The educational value of these resources depends on structured pedagogy, feedback, faculty development, access, sustainability and objective assessment. Because direct evidence in Surgical Instrumentation is limited to one included study, the implications should be interpreted as transferability hypotheses and priorities for future research rather than definitive curriculum recommendations.

Background

Laparoscopic surgery has transformed contemporary surgical practice by enabling minimally invasive procedures associated with relevant clinical benefits, including shorter hospital stay, reduced postoperative pain and faster functional recovery in selected conditions [1]. Nevertheless, learning laparoscopic surgery differs substantially from learning open surgical techniques because trainees must coordinate long instruments, indirect vision, depth perception, counterintuitive movements and restricted operative spaces [2–6]. These demands have encouraged surgical educators to use simulation and technology-enhanced learning environments before learners participate in procedures involving patients [7–15].

Simulation-based education has become a central strategy in health professions education because it enables deliberate practice, feedback, progressive task difficulty and error correction in controlled environments [16–21]. In laparoscopic training, box trainers, virtual reality simulators, augmented reality platforms, portable endo-trainers and online surgical curricula have been used to support psychomotor skill acquisition, procedural familiarity and learner confidence [22–40]. However, educational outcomes are not determined by technology alone. Effective learning depends on curricular alignment, faculty facilitation, assessment tools, feedback and the institutional capacity to maintain simulation-based training over time [41–49].

For clarity, this review uses the term technology-enhanced simulation resources rather than assuming that all included tools are emerging technologies. The review includes newer immersive tools such as VR and AR, while also including established resources such as box trainers when they are evaluated as part of contemporary technology-supported, portable, low-cost, digital or structured simulation approaches. This operational definition avoids equating novelty with educational value.

Students’ perceptions are important because perceived usefulness, ease of use, motivation and confidence influence acceptance and sustained engagement with educational technologies [50, 51]. A learner may report satisfaction with a virtual platform without necessarily achieving procedural competence; therefore, perceptions should be interpreted alongside observable performance, assessment criteria and patient-safety goals [16–21, 46–49]. This distinction is especially relevant in laparoscopic training, where technical enthusiasm can be confused with educational effectiveness if implementation is not accompanied by structured learning objectives.

The added value of this review is not merely to confirm that learners often like simulation or VR. Its contribution is to map how perceptions, implementation conditions and transferability intersect, and to distinguish direct evidence for Surgical Instrumentation from indirect evidence drawn from medical students, surgical residents, nursing students or mixed surgical trainees. This distinction is important because surgical instrumentation professionals participate in instrument preparation, sterile-field organization, anticipation of surgical needs, technology handling and intraoperative teamwork [52–56]. Their role-specific competencies are not identical to those of surgeons or residents, and findings from other populations should therefore be transferred cautiously.

The objective of this scoping review was to map evidence on health sciences students’ perceptions of technology-enhanced simulation resources in laparoscopic training and to identify cautious implications for Surgical Instrumentation education.

Methods

This scoping review followed the PRISMA-ScR checklist, JBI methodological guidance, and foundational recommendations for scoping reviews [57–63]. A scoping review was considered appropriate because the purpose was to map the extent, characteristics and conceptual domains of available evidence rather than to estimate a pooled intervention effect. The heterogeneity of designs, learners, technologies and outcomes also made meta-analysis inappropriate.

No protocol was registered or published a priori. An internal protocol and eligibility framework guided the review process; this is acknowledged as a limitation because external protocol registration would have strengthened transparency.

The review question was: What evidence exists on health sciences students’ perceptions of technology-enhanced simulation resources in laparoscopic training, and what cautious implications can be identified for Surgical Instrumentation education? The PCC framework is presented in Table 1.

Eligibility criteria for study selection

Study selection and data charting

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts. Potentially relevant records were assessed in full text, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. Duplicates were checked using DOI, PMID, title, year and author matching during initial cleaning and title/abstract screening. The original screening log did not retain duplicate records as a separate numeric category; this limitation is reported transparently rather than replaced with unverifiable reconstructed counts.

A data-charting matrix captured author, year, country, design, population, technology or educational context, analytic domain, key findings and relevance for Surgical Instrumentation. Because studies differed in design, population and outcome measures, findings were synthesized narratively across four domains: perception and satisfaction; technical performance and learning; implementation barriers; and implications for Surgical Instrumentation.

Critical appraisal

A formal risk-of-bias assessment was not conducted because the aim was evidence mapping rather than estimation of intervention effectiveness, which is consistent with scoping-review methodology [57–63]. However, methodological heterogeneity, reliance on subjective or self-reported measures, and the distinction between perception and competence were considered when interpreting the findings.

Synthesis method

Findings were summarized narratively rather than aggregated statistically. The review does not present a pooled effect estimate or a vote-counting synthesis. Descriptive statements about favorable perceptions are therefore interpreted as mapped patterns across heterogeneous studies, not as a quantitative measure of effectiveness or prevalence.

Included studies and evidence characteristics

Perceptions and satisfaction

Across the included evidence, most learner-facing studies that measured perception, satisfaction, acceptance, confidence or self-efficacy reported favorable responses to technology-enhanced simulation resources [30–36, 38–40]. Favorable responses were expressed through satisfaction, confidence, perceived safety, motivation, self-efficacy and willingness to engage in repeated practice. Wilkinson et al. [37] did not contribute a favorable learner-perception finding; it was included because it mapped implementation barriers in low- and middle-income laparoscopic training contexts.

These findings are consistent with technology-acceptance theories, which explain adoption through perceived usefulness, ease of use and facilitating conditions [50, 51]. However, the studies used heterogeneous instruments and many relied on subjective or self-perceived outcomes; therefore, favorable perception should be interpreted as learner acceptance, not as proof of competence or clinical transfer.

Technical performance and learning

Experimental and quasi-experimental studies described improvements in time, precision, movement economy, task quality or perceived competence after simulation or VR exposure [30–33, 38, 39]. These results are compatible with broader evidence that simulation can support learning when combined with deliberate practice, mastery learning and structured feedback [16–21, 41–49]. However, the heterogeneity of tasks, learner populations and assessment methods prevents causal generalization across learner groups, and performance findings should not be interpreted as pooled evidence of effectiveness.

Implementation barriers

Implementation barriers included cost, equipment availability, maintenance, faculty training, lack of standardized protocols and institutional inequalities [36, 37, 53–56]. These barriers are particularly relevant for Latin American and other resource-constrained contexts, where low-cost simulators and portable trainers may increase access but still require curricular planning, maintenance strategies and faculty development [32, 36, 37].

Direct and indirect evidence for Surgical Instrumentation

Only one included study directly addressed Surgical Instrumentation students [38]. Therefore, most findings should be considered indirect evidence derived from medical students, residents, nursing students or mixed surgical trainees [30–37, 39, 40]. Discussion

This scoping review suggests that technology-enhanced simulation resources are commonly perceived favorably by learners in laparoscopic training, particularly when they enable repeated practice, feedback, confidence building and a safer transition toward clinical environments [16–40]. Nevertheless, the main contribution of this review is not simply to confirm positive perceptions. Its contribution is to show that perceptions must be interpreted through pedagogy, assessment, implementation conditions and professional specificity, especially when evidence is transferred to Surgical Instrumentation education.

A technology-centered interpretation would be insufficient. VR, AR, box trainers and portable simulators are not educationally effective by default; their value depends on how they are integrated into objectives, sequencing, faculty facilitation, feedback and assessment [16–21, 41–49]. A student may perceive a simulator as attractive or useful, but satisfaction alone does not demonstrate psychomotor competence or safe clinical transfer. This distinction is important because it prevents overclaiming results from heterogeneous evidence.

The positive direction of many findings should also be interpreted in light of potential positivity and publication biases. Studies evaluating educational technologies may be more likely to report satisfaction, acceptance or perceived usefulness than neutral or negative experiences. Furthermore, subjective outcomes are often easier to collect than objective metrics such as retention, supervised transfer or patient-safety effects. These features limit the strength of conclusions that can be drawn from perception studies alone.

The limited direct evidence in Surgical Instrumentation students is a major gap. Surgical Instrumentation involves specific competencies that include preparation and organization of laparoscopic instruments, anticipation of intraoperative needs, support for sterile-field safety, knowledge of energy devices and coordination with the surgical team [52–56]. Evidence from residents or medical students can inform educational design, but it cannot replace studies conducted in Surgical Instrumentation populations. For this reason, the findings should be framed as transferable hypotheses and research priorities rather than definitive curriculum recommendations.

Several uncertainties remain for Surgical Instrumentation education. It is not yet clear which simulation modalities best support instrument recognition, sterile-field management, anticipation of procedural steps, device handling, team communication or safety-oriented behaviors in this population. It is also unclear whether favorable perceptions translate into retention, supervised clinical transfer or improved intraoperative performance. Future studies should therefore test these outcomes directly using role-specific competencies and objective assessment criteria.

From an international medical-education perspective, this review also highlights equity and implementation issues. High-fidelity simulators and VR platforms may provide metrics and repeatability, but their cost, maintenance requirements and faculty-training demands may limit sustainability in low-resource settings [36, 37]. Low-cost trainers and portable devices may offer a pragmatic alternative when linked to structured tasks, competency rubrics and progressive supervision [32, 36]. Implications for education and research

For Surgical Instrumentation programs, the evidence supports a cautious, competency-based exploration of laparoscopic simulation. Educational designs should define learning outcomes, ensure faculty training, combine low-cost and technology-enhanced resources when appropriate, use checklists or objective metrics, and evaluate both perception and performance. These recommendations are not presented as a finalized curriculum guideline; they are implementation hypotheses derived from indirect evidence and one direct study in Surgical Instrumentation students.

For research, priorities include multicenter studies in Surgical Instrumentation students, validated perception instruments, comparative studies between low-cost and high-fidelity simulators, assessment of skill retention, and evaluation of supervised transfer to clinical practice. Such evidence would help determine not only whether learners value these resources, but also how, when and under what institutional conditions they contribute to safe, progressive and competency-based laparoscopic training.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, only 11 studies met inclusion criteria, and only one directly studied Surgical Instrumentation students. This limits direct transferability to the target professional field and requires cautious interpretation of educational implications.

Second, included studies were heterogeneous in design, population, technology, outcome measures and educational context. This heterogeneity prevented meta-analysis and required narrative synthesis.

Third, many studies relied on subjective or self-reported outcomes such as satisfaction, confidence or perceived competence. These outcomes are important for technology acceptance but do not demonstrate procedural competence, clinical readiness or transfer of learning.

Fourth, a formal risk-of-bias assessment was not conducted because the purpose of the review was evidence mapping rather than effectiveness estimation. Nonetheless, the methodological heterogeneity and varied credibility of source studies require cautious interpretation, especially when studies reported performance improvements in time, precision or movement economy.

Sixth, the review included English and Spanish sources only, which may have excluded relevant studies in other languages.

Fifth, exact source-specific retrieval counts and a separate duplicate count were not preserved in the manuscript file available for this revision. To avoid reconstructing unverifiable numbers, the revised report provides full search strings, the aggregate PRISMA-ScR flow and an explicit note on when duplicates were considered. Future updates should archive database exports and a complete source-level search log.

Eighth, the protocol was not registered or published a priori. Finally, the literature may be affected by positivity or publication bias toward favorable perceptions of educational technologies.

Conclusions

Technology-enhanced simulation resources in laparoscopic training appear to be acceptable to health sciences learners, particularly when they provide repeated practice, structured feedback, increased confidence and safer learning environments before exposure to real clinical settings. Simulation-based resources, VR, AR and box trainers may be valued by students; however, positive perception should not be equated with competence, clinical readiness or transfer of learning.

The educational value of these resources depends largely on their pedagogical integration. Simulation and immersive tools are more likely to support learning when they are embedded in structured curricula, facilitated by trained faculty, supported by deliberate practice and assessed through competency-based criteria. Technology should therefore be considered an educational mediator rather than a stand-alone solution.

The evidence base for Surgical Instrumentation remains limited. Most included studies involved medical students, surgical residents, nurses or mixed health professional groups, which restricts direct transferability to Surgical Instrumentation education. Given the specific responsibilities of surgical instrumentation professionals in instrument preparation, sterile-field management, intraoperative anticipation, team coordination and patient safety, further discipline-specific evidence is required.

This review supports the need for multicenter, competency-oriented studies focused on Surgical Instrumentation students. Future research should evaluate perceptions, technology acceptance, psychomotor outcomes, skill retention, supervised clinical transfer, faculty readiness and implementation feasibility. Such evidence would help determine not only whether learners value technology-enhanced simulation resources, but also how these tools can contribute to safe, progressive and competency-based laparoscopic training.
