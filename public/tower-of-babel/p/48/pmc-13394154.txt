Ketogenic diet for infantile epileptic spasms
Authors: Morris H. Scantlebury, Anamika Choudhary, Andy Cheuk‐Him Ng, Cezar Gavrilovici
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractApproximately half of all cases of Infantile Epileptic Spasms Syndrome (IESS) do not respond to vigabatrin and hormonal therapies. There is no clear consensus as to the second‐line therapy for IESS. Ketogenic diet (KD) has emerged as an effective treatment for certain drug‐resistant epilepsies and in many cases of IESS. Understanding the mechanism of action of the KD in IESS will allow for harnessing the power of the KD and discovering novel therapeutics for IESS. In this review, we will summarize the current state of knowledge of the action of the KD in IESS derived from animal models. We emphasize the importance of the KD in altering respiration to cause brain acidosis. In addition, we review recent data implicating altered gut microbiome and the tryptophan–serotonin–kynurenine pathway in KD animals with infantile epileptic spasms syndrome.Plain Language SummaryInfantile Epileptic Spasms Syndrome is a serious seizure condition in babies, often resistant to standard drugs, failing in half of cases. Animal studies helped unravel multiple mechanisms through which a high‐fat, low‐carb ketogenic diet can control seizures, including altering gut bacteria, reducing inflammation, balancing brain chemicals, boosting mitochondrial function, or adjusting breathing to slightly acidify the brain. These findings could lead to new, targeted therapies that are simpler to use and more accessible for families facing this challenging condition.

INTRODUCTION

Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome

Infantile Epileptic Spasms Syndrome (IESS) is a severe condition and the prototypical developmental and epileptic encephalopathy syndrome of infancy. It was first described in 1841 by Dr. William James West in his own, previously normally developing son, James, as a cry for help. West's clinical description of IESS has weathered well the test of time with very few improvements. We now know that the epileptic spasms in IESS can be flexor, as originally described, extensor, mixed, or subtle types (the trick as described by West). 1

Epidemiological studies show that IESS typically manifests between 3 and 12 months of age, and 90% of patients present with epileptic spasms within the first year of life. 2 , 3 , 4 The incidence of IESS is 2.5–6 per 10,000 live births, with a slight male predominance, and accounts for 10% of epilepsies with onset before 3 years of age. 5 IESS is age‐specific and will likely spontaneously remit by early childhood. 4 Gibbs and Gibbs first demonstrated that IESS is also associated with a severe and chaotic interictal EEG pattern termed hypsarrhythmia 6 , 7 and several forms of this chaotic EEG pattern have been described. 8 Electrodecremental responses have been recorded time‐locked with epileptic spasms, which has confirmed their nature as epileptic. 9

IESS is due to diverse etiologies that include structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, and unknown causes. 4 Classification of IESS is currently based on etiology, although historically, IESS was classified into symptomatic, cryptogenic, or idiopathic. Approximately 60% of patients with IESS have a known underlying etiology. 10 , 11 The consequences of IESS are devastating as 75%–80% of affected infants will sustain neuromotor deficits, and IESS‐associated behavioral abnormalities, including autism, are common. 12 Other drug‐resistant seizure types frequently ensue, and tragically, the mortality is high. 12 However, early control of the epileptic spasms and hypsarrhythmia is a prognostic factor for survival. 13 Prognosis in children with IESS can also be heavily influenced based on etiology. 12

Thus, it is paramount to successfully treat both the epileptic spasms and hypsarrhythmia in children with IESS to improve outcomes. Hormones such as adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), prednisolone, and vigabatrin have long been used as first‐line treatments but are frequently ineffective. 12 , 14 , 15 , 16 A slightly higher response rate can be achieved in the short term when ACTH and vigabatrin are used in combination. 17 The side effects of ACTH (hypertension, hyperglycemia, obesity, irritability, increased susceptibility to infections, gastric ulcers, metabolic derangements, pericarditis/myocarditis, cerebral atrophy, and/or death) 12 , 18 , 19 and vigabatrin (encephalopathy, retinopathy, myelin edema of deep brain structures, movement abnormalities, and/or hypotonia) 20 , 21 , 22 are serious and common, thereby limiting their long term use 12 , 17 , 18 , 23 and may contribute to high recurrence rates, which range upwards of 30%, depending on etiology. 24 , 25 , 26

Despite the significant side effects associated with ACTH and vigabatrin, the risks do not outweigh the benefits, as if left untreated, IESS will undoubtedly lead to severe, debilitating psychomotor outcomes and high rates of mortality.

The question remains, even to this day: what should be done next for patients with IESS when first‐line treatments fail? This is not a trivial question, as there is little evidence to support that conventional antiseizure medications (ASMs) are effective in these patients, leaving physicians often helpless in the face of this progressively debilitating condition. In this review, we will examine the studies supporting—and sometimes refuting—the remarkable ability of the ketogenic diet (KD) to control IESS. We will also discuss mechanistic data derived from animal models, which have provided a framework for the development of targeted new treatments for this devastating condition.

KD in IESS—Is it effective?

The KD—a high‐fat, low‐carbohydrate, adequate‐protein diet—has been reported to be an effective nonpharmacological alternative for treatment‐resistant cases of IESS. It was developed to mimic fasting following the discovery of Dr. Rollin Woodyatt, an endocrinologist, that acetone, β‐hydroxybutyrate, and acetoacetate (ketone bodies) were produced by the liver of healthy individuals in a prolonged fasted state or when consuming a high‐fat diet. 27 It is astonishing to note that in 1977, around the time vigabatrin was discovered, an extensive report on KD's efficacy in pediatric epilepsy was first published by Livingston et al., in the largest single‐center case study in the diet's history. 28 Written in part to rebut the assertion that the KD is ineffective as a treatment for childhood myoclonic epilepsy, the authors reported seizure freedom in 53% of patients and a significant improvement in 26% of patients with myoclonic epilepsy, the majority of whom had IESS. 28 Then, in 1998, Dr. John Freeman published the first prospective study, which demonstrated that the KD is effective in treating pediatric epilepsies, including IESS. In this study, 13 patients with IESS were reported, and at 6 months on KD, 8% of patients with IESS became seizure‐free, 31% had >90%, 23% had 50%–90%, and 39% had <50% reduction in their seizures. 29

In 2018, Prezioso et al. published a meta‐analysis that pulled together data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies published between 1980 and 2017, encompassing 341 children with IESS across 13 studies, of which only four were prospective. 30 In this study, 57% of children with IESS experienced a >50% reduction in epileptic spasms frequency within 6 months of initiating KD treatment. When looking at treatment response in prospective studies alone, this number rose to 66%. Within 6 months, seizure freedom was achieved in 34% of children with IESS overall and in 29% of patients in the prospective studies subset. In this subset, KD was rarely used as a standalone first‐line treatment. Only a minority of patients in the Hong et al. (2010) 31 study (17.3%) were purely on KD at initiation, while the majority, including all patients in Kayyali et al. (2014), 32 Li et al. (2013), 33 and Pires et al. (2013), 34 had the KD introduced as an add‐on therapy following or in combination with other antiseizure medications. Long‐term outcomes were limited to only four studies, which showed that 24% of patients remained seizure‐free at 12 months. 30 Notably, clinical improvements are often observed within days of starting the diet, 35 although the improvement of hypsarrhythmia may lag by several months. 36

Following the 2018 systematic review, five notable clinical studies have further examined the efficacy of KD in children with IESS. In 2019, Dressler et al. published the first head‐to‐head RCT comparing the efficacy and safety of KD to high‐dose ACTH. 37 This prospective, single‐center study also included a nonrandomized parallel cohort (PC). The study enrolled 101 children with IESS and evaluated electroclinical remission after 28 days. Patients achieving electroclinical remission were similar between KD and ACTH in both the RCT (62% vs. 69%) and the PC arms (41% vs. 38%). However, the frequency of relapse was similar between the groups in the RCT but significantly lower in patients treated with the KD (0%) vs. ACTH (50%) in the PC study. Long‐term seizure freedom was comparable in the RCT arm but doubled for KD compared to ACTH in the PC arm, although this did not achieve statistical significance. Adverse events requiring medical intervention were strikingly lower with KD (30%) compared to ACTH (94%), with a death attributed to cardiomyopathy reported in the ACTH group. These findings highlight the potential advantages of KD in long‐term seizure control in IESS with comparable efficacy to ACTH but with better tolerability. 37 Unfortunately, there were no differences observed in long‐term neurodevelopmental outcomes between the groups. Furthermore, in this study, 18% of the infants were drug‐naive, and in these patients, the KD was significantly less effective than ACTH; however, higher relapse rates were observed in patients treated with ACTH, leading to comparable long‐term outcomes. The converse was true in patients pretreated with vigabatrin, which suggests that a combination of vigabatrin and KD may represent an effective approach for treatment‐resistant cases, though further research is needed to confirm this.

In 2021, Zhang et al. published a prospective, multicenter‐controlled study across 10 tertiary pediatric medical centers that examined the effectiveness of the KD in the control of epileptic spasms and hypsarrhythmia in patients with IESS who failed ACTH. The study enrolled 227 infants with ACTH‐ or corticosteroid‐resistant IESS, who were assigned to a KD or control ASM group. Following a 16‐week trial, a ≥50% spasm reduction and/or hypsarrhythmia remission was seen in 40.7% of patients treated with the KD versus 20.7% of controls, which was statistically significant. Electroclinical remission was similar between the KD (13.4%) and control (10.9%) groups. Importantly, no correlation was found between KD efficacy and IESS etiology. However, uneven group sizes may have introduced bias into the findings. 38

Then, in 2022, Ye et al. published a multicenter, retrospective cohort study analyzing clinical data from 481 children with IESS treated with classical KD across 17 tertiary centers. With KD treatment, seizure freedom rates progressively increased from 6.9% at 1 month, 16% at 6 months, to 16.8% at 12 months, while ≥50% seizure reduction was achieved in 49.1% of patients at 6 months, although this declined to 32% at 12 months. There was no effect between the number of ASMs that failed before KD initiation and KD's effectiveness in reducing seizures. 39

Conversely, a retrospective cohort study of highly treatment‐resistant children with IESS found no complete seizure cessation in any patient after 1 month on the KD, despite a slight reduction in epileptic spasms compared to baseline. This reduction, however, was not sustained beyond 3–12 months of treatment. 40

Finally, a multicenter, open‐label, randomized clinical trial comparing the classic KD with antiseizure medications in children with drug‐resistant epilepsy, 41 which included a significant proportion (~50%) of children with infantile spasms, found no significant difference in efficacy and tolerability between the KD and antiseizure medications for managing seizures in this population. The lack of a clear advantage for dietary intervention over traditional antiseizure medications underscores the complexity of treating IESS (Table 1).

Only a few studies have reported on the impact of the KD on hypsarrhythmia. 31 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 42 , 43 Overall, the effect appears to be positive 34 , 35 , 37 , 43 , 44 but can lag the impact on seizures by several months. 36 , 42 Some studies show limited to no impact. 40 The KD has been shown to have an impact on behavioral outcomes in IESS, but these findings are inconsistent. 45 , 46 , 47 The variable patient outcomes observed across some studies highlight the challenges of assessing the diet's true efficacy, particularly given the complexity and varying clinical presentations of IESS. The large variation in the diet's treatment efficacy may be attributed to factors such as the KD ratio used, duration of treatment/adherence, and age of patients at treatment initiation. Thus, more high‐quality, rigorously controlled prospective studies on the use of KD in IESS are needed to either confirm or refute its effectiveness in treating IESS and to explore mechanisms of action.

A significant hindrance to establishing the widespread use of the KD in the treatment of IESS is its tolerability. Studies support that a more tolerable Modified Atkins Diet (MAD) is at least as effective as the KD in the control of seizures in IESS. In 2023, Dou et al. published a retrospective case–control study on 56 children with IESS comparing the efficacy of KD vs. MAD, which they showed was similarly effective. This study showed escalating and high rates of effectiveness of the KD and MAD in patients with IESS. The KD and MAD led to 50% spasm reduction at 3, 6, and 12 months in 75%, 82.6%, and 90.9% versus 66.7%, 75%, and 84.6%; respectively. Spasm freedom was seen in ~1/3 of patients treated with either the KD or MAD at 3 months, which dropped to ~10% in the KD group, but the difference was not statistically significant. Patients remained on concurrent ASMs, which were adjusted at some point up or down during the study in all patients and limited the ability to attribute clinical and electrographic improvements to the diet alone. 48 The same year, Sharma et al. also published an open‐labeled RCT comparing the efficacy of KD vs. MAD in 40 drug‐resistant children with IESS and similarly showed comparable effectiveness between the groups at 3 months, although the number of patients achieving 50% seizure reduction (KD, 25% vs 15% MAD) was much lower than reported in the Dressler study. Comparable rates of spasm freedom were observed in both groups at 3 months: KD, 15% versus MAD, 25%. 49

These results suggest that the KD is likely effective for the treatment of refractory IESS depending on the clinical context, but more research is required to support or refute this possibility.

The KD and epilepsy: A mechanistic overview

The exact mechanisms responsible for the diet's effects on epileptic spasms and hypsarrhythmia remain poorly understood. 50 , 51 Mechanistic studies conducted primarily in adult rodent models of epilepsy suggest that multiple mechanisms may contribute to the seizure‐suppressing effects of the KD (Figure 1). These include the direct anticonvulsant properties of ketone bodies, modulation of GABAergic circuits, alterations in noradrenergic tone, anti‐inflammatory effects, mitochondrial function modulation (e.g., glycolytic restriction, increased ATP production, activation of mitochondrial K‐ATP channels, enhanced antioxidant capacity, inhibition of reactive oxygen species), the anticonvulsant effects of adenosine, protective effects against postictal hypoxia, gut–microbiome alterations, and intracerebral acidosis. 51 , 52 , 53 From these data, it is unclear whether all these mechanisms act synergistically or if there is a principal mechanism that forms the basis for the anticonvulsant effects of the KD, which may be age, sex, or epilepsy syndrome dependent.

A schematic outlining the major proposed mechanisms of action underlying the effects of the ketogenic diet. The diagram illustrates central pathways including ketone bodies and glycolysis influencing ATP and energy metabolism; altered neurotransmitter dynamics (e.g., GABAergic modulation, glutamate, norepinephrine); neuroprotection through postictal hypoxia protection, mitochondrial biogenesis, and modulation of mitochondrial function (e.g., glycolytic restriction, increased ATP production, activation of K‐ATP channels, enhanced antioxidant capacity, inhibition of reactive oxygen species); anti‐inflammatory effects via cytokine reduction; gut microbiome and tryptophan metabolism alterations; intracerebral acidosis; and anticonvulsant effects of adenosine via A1 receptors, alongside leptin and insulin regulation.

Additionally, the precise mechanism of action of the KD in the immature brain remains largely unexplored. Why so? This is predominantly due to the significant technical challenges surrounding delivering tightly controlled diets to neonatal animals, which are altricial at birth and rely on maternal feeding until approximately postnatal day (P) 21. Another issue is the relatively limited availability of immature rodent models of epilepsy.

MECHANISMS OF THE KD IN IESS

Animal models of IESS

Several etiology‐specific IESS animal models (Table 2) have been developed in an effort to gain a better understanding of the condition and to develop novel treatments. 54 , 55 Currently, the mechanism of action of the KD or its metabolites in IESS has been examined in only two pediatric models: the betamethasone‐N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate (NMDA) model 56 and the triple‐hit model. 57 The prenatal betamethasone‐postnatal NMDA model is an acute, cryptogenic model of IESS with responsiveness to high‐dose ACTH as well as vigabatrin. 56 The triple‐hit model of IESS is a widely accepted, well established, and rigorously validated chronic lesional model of IESS.

This model is of critical importance given that the vast majority of patients with IESS have epileptic spasms due to brain lesions. 2 In the model, structural brain abnormalities are induced in neonatal rats with an injection of lipopolysaccharide and doxorubicin into the brain at P4, followed by a systemic injection of p‐chlorophenylalanine at P5. These injections result in the age‐specific expression of epileptic spasms between P4 and P12–13 that do not respond to ACTH and partially to vigabatrin, serving as a drug‐resistant model of IESS. 57 The antiseizure effects of the KD have only been tested in this triple‐hit model of IESS using rigorous artificial rearing techniques—where neonatal rat pups are raised outside the natural dam environment using a specialized feeding system, typically with milk formula and the ‘pup‐in‐cup’ method, in which individual pups are housed in cups and fed via cannula to control nutrition, coupled with 24‐h EEG monitoring. 52 , 77 , 78 With this method, the KD has been shown to reduce seizures, interictal spiking, behavioral abnormalities, mortality, and lesion size in the triple‐hit model of IESS. 52 The mechanisms underlying the beneficial impact of the KD in the triple‐hit model are discussed below.

Ketone bodies

The KD results in the production of acidic ketone bodies—β‐hydroxybutyrate (BHB), acetoacetate, and acetone—primarily via β‐oxidation of long‐chain fatty acids in the liver's mitochondrial matrix. Although to a lesser extent, astrocytes are also capable of oxidizing fatty acids to generate ketone bodies, which are then utilized by neighboring neurons in the brain. 79 , 80

In the relative absence of glucose, ketone bodies become the brain's primary energy source, crossing the blood–brain barrier via monocarboxylate transporters (β‐hydroxybutyrate or acetoacetate) or free diffusion (acetone) and providing a readily available and efficient energy substrate. 81 The greater effectiveness of the KD in children compared to adults may be attributed to the decline in monocarboxylic acid transporters as the brain matures. 82 , 83 However, BHB levels measured in the blood or urine do not necessarily correlate with KD‐induced seizure control, as some studies are supportive and others are not. 84 , 85 Together, these studies suggest that the direct action of BHB on brain excitability may not be the principal mechanism by which the KD acts to control seizures.

Systemic injections of BHB three times daily for 3 days in the betamethasone‐NMDA model led to a reduction and delayed the latency of NMDA‐triggered epileptic spasms. 86 NMDA exposure led to increased GABA, glutamate, choline, N‐acetylaspartate, creatine, and macromolecule levels. BHB significantly blunted these NMDA‐induced neurometabolic derangements. These data suggest that repeated, prolonged administration of ketone bodies may be sufficient in reducing epileptic spasms in the cryptogenic model of IESS.

The antiseizure effects of the low carbohydrate component of the KD and ketone bodies were tested in the betamethasone‐NMDA model by another group using acute treatments of 2‐deoxyglucose (2‐DG) and BHB during NMDA induction of epileptic spasms. No reduction in epileptic spasms frequency and onset latency was observed with 2‐DG and BHB when given during NMDA induction of epileptic spasms. 87

Therefore, the observation that repeated dosing of BHB, rather than acute exposure, is required for its antiseizure effects in vivo suggests the need for long‐term inhibition and remodeling of epileptogenic networks conferred by the KD. These findings correlate with in vitro studies testing the effects of BHB on the excitability in hippocampal brain slices. Acute exposure of BHB or acetoacetate had no effect on evoked field potentials, population spikes, long‐term potentiation, or low magnesium‐induced seizure‐like events. 88 , 89 In contrast, repeated exposure of BHB in organotypic hippocampal slice cultures reduced oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD)‐reperfusion‐induced seizure‐like activity. In slice cultures from Kcna1‐null hippocampal slice cultures, 2‐week exposure of acetoacetate and BHB suppressed seizure‐like events, 90 effects thought to be mediated through improvement in synaptic integrity and raising the threshold of mitochondrial permeability transition.

Brain acidosis

In the human body, acid–base equilibrium (pH ~7.36–7.44) is tightly regulated through the coordinated actions of chemical buffers, the respiratory system, and the renal system. 91 The carbonic acid‐bicarbonate system is the most important extracellular buffer. In this tightly regulated system, CO2 levels are controlled by alveolar ventilation, whereas H+ and HCO3 − levels are regulated by the kidneys. Protein buffering varies by tissue, age, and can be impacted by various conditions, including hypoalbuminemia. 91 The respiratory system rapidly regulates pH by controlling arterial CO2 through modulation of tidal volume and respiratory rate. Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies) and central brainstem chemosensors detect changes in pH and PCO2 and drive ventilatory adjustments. 92 In response to high PCO2 levels, the respiratory system will increase the depth and the rate of breathing (hyperventilation) to remove excess CO2 and excess H+. The engagement of central chemoreceptors across the blood–brain barrier is important but usually slower. 91 , 92 In contrast, the renal system provides slower but more powerful, long‐term regulation through the reabsorption or excretion of filtered bicarbonate via phosphate and ammonium buffers to maintain a stable plasma pH.

Systemic pH and brain pH are interlinked, as CO2 freely crosses the blood–brain barrier. Increases in arterial CO2 rapidly translate to shifts in interstitial pH. An increase in blood CO2 will lower cerebrospinal fluid pH, whereas a decrease in CO2 (e.g., during hyperventilation) makes the brain environment more alkaline. These shifts can strongly modulate neuronal excitability, underscoring the intimate link between systemic acid–base balance and brain function. 91 , 93 The association between brain pH, specifically brain acidosis and seizure suppression, was first made in 1929 by Dr. William Lennox, who reported that respiratory acidosis (hypercapnia) reduced seizures in epileptic patients. 94 Another clinical study reported that an increased pH in the cerebral cortex resulted in increased excitability, whereas a lowered pH resulted in reduced cerebral excitability. 95 This was supported by in vitro rodent studies, where exposing hippocampal brain slices to low pH decreased epileptiform activity. 96

Does the KD lead to intracerebral acidosis? Adult rats fed 5 weeks of KD did not demonstrate any changes in cerebral pH. 97 However, in these KD‐fed adult rats, the plasma BHB achieved was below 0.5 mM, suggesting insufficient ketosis. Choudhary et al. similarly showed no change in cerebral pH (pH ~7.15) in KD‐fed, postnatal day (P) 7 rat pups. However, cerebral pH was decreased to pH ~6.9 in P7 KD‐fed rats exposed to brain lesioning as part of the triple hit infantile spasms model 52 (Figure 2). This suggests an interaction between the injured epileptic brain and the KD to induce intracerebral acidosis and its concomitant beneficial effects to reduce cerebral hyperexcitability. KD in brain‐lesioned rat pups led to intracerebral acidosis through changes in breathing. Specifically, KD led to slower and deeper breathing, resulting in decreased expired CO2. These findings were in line with humans exposed to 20 days of KD. 98 We speculate that KD in lesioned rats showed changes in respiration through an acute increase in leptin levels, as leptin is a powerful stimulator of breathing 99 and the KD has been shown to increase leptin levels acutely following the initiation of the KD in a prior study. 100

How acidosis mediates its antiseizure effects in the model of IESS is unknown (see Figure 3 for the proposed mechanism of action). Current research indicates that the anticonvulsant effects of acidosis are primarily mediated through two mechanisms: modulation of acid sensing ion channels (ASICs) and/or gap junctions, 101 , 102 although shifts in pH can impact the expression and function of other excitatory and inhibitory ionic channels. 103 , 104 Interestingly, the immature brain is highly susceptible to fluctuations in pH and all physiologically relevant acid‐evoked currents are dependent on ASICs. 105 , 106 ASICs are pH‐sensitive ion channels that are widely expressed in the brain and have been implicated in pathologic disease states, including epilepsy. 105 Studies have implicated the role of ASICs in the endogenous control of seizures. 107 , 108 , 109 , 110 Further studies should evaluate the role of ASICs in gating the KD‐induced antiseizure effects seen in the triple‐hit model. Increased gap junctional communication between neurons is thought to play a critical role in promoting neuronal synchrony, driving seizure activity. The developing brain exhibits high expression levels of gap junctions, which, when coupled, enable direct electrical communication between neurons. Acidosis is a potent uncoupler of gap junctions and may represent an additional mechanism underlying the therapeutic action of KD. 101 In addition to these mechanisms, changes in pH can influence the activity of glycolytic enzymes and those involved in the synthesis of GABA and glutamate, which play crucial roles in seizure regulation. A decrease in pH, as induced by KD through intracerebral acidosis, could enhance the activity of glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), the enzyme responsible for GABA synthesis, while reducing the activity of γ‐aminobutyric acid transaminase (GABA‐T), which degrades GABA. 111 These results could lead to increased levels of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA, potentially inhibiting seizure activity. 111

Potential mechanisms of acidosis‐induced antiseizure effects in infantile spasms. The ketogenic diet may acutely modulate leptin levels, potentially stimulating respiration and leading to respiratory acidosis. This acidosis could mediate antiseizure activity through (1) modulation of acid‐sensing ion channels (ASICs), which are highly expressed in the immature brain and implicated in seizure control; (2) uncoupling of neuronal gap junctions to disrupt neuronal synchrony; and (3) pH‐dependent changes in the expression and function of other ion channels, as well as enhanced GABA synthesis via activation of glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) and inhibition of GABA transaminase (GABA‐T). Further investigation is needed to confirm these pathways in the triple‐hit model of IESS.

Gut microbiome, tryptophan metabolism, and IDO1

Recent literature has reported gut microbiome alterations in patients with IESS, with several bacterial taxa increased and others decreased. For example, a study following 23 children with IESS found decreased levels of Lactobacillus, as well as upregulated lipA and lipB genes, which are associated with lipoic acid synthesis. Lipoic acid, a crucial cofactor in mitochondrial metabolism, plays an essential role in supporting key enzymatic reactions leading to the oxidation of amino acids and carbohydrates and energy production. 112 Additionally, its potent antioxidant properties enable it to neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby preserving cellular redox balance and mitigating oxidative stress. 113 Further, metabolic pathway analysis revealed potential dysfunction in the lipoic acid metabolism in these patients. 114 Interestingly, the disruption of the lipoic acid pathway has been associated with status epilepticus. 115 Indeed, dysregulation of lipoic acid metabolism can lead to a cascade of cellular events, including excessive ROS production, increased oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which can have significant implications for neurological health and epilepsy pathophysiology 116 , 117 , 118 , 119 through mechanisms such as neuronal injury, 120 , 121 altered neuronal excitability, and disrupted synaptic transmission. 122 When looking at the subset of children that responded to ACTH vs. non‐responders, the authors reported a reduction in the following bacteria: Anaerotruncus, Odoribacter, Phascolarctobacterium, Mitsuakella, and Robinsoniella. 114 Notably, increased levels of bacteria such as Anaerotruncus have been reported to lead to overactivation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. 123 Since ACTH is regulated by the HPA axis, its dysfunction might lead to ACTH and/or cortisol resistance over time; in other words, the system may simply become less sensitive to ACTH. However, despite these observations, it remains unclear how these microbial shifts influence brain excitability or contribute to disease progression. Remarkably, few studies have directly examined the functional consequences of gut microbiome changes in IESS and their impact on seizure outcomes, leaving a critical gap in our knowledge.

Mu and colleagues addressed this gap by studying the effects of KD on the gut microbiome using the triple‐hit IESS model, using 16S ribosomal sequencing. Alongside the seizure reduction, Mu et al. also reported that KD enhanced gut microbial α‐diversity and species richness, as shown by higher operational taxonomic units, Chao1, and elevated abundance‐based Coverage Estimator (ACE) indices, which estimate total species richness by accounting for rare taxa. On the other hand, studies done in KD‐fed adult mice show a reduction in gut microbial α‐diversity. 124 The contrasting data between microbial expansion and restriction in neonatal and adult animals, respectively, shed light on the critical importance of conducting studies in appropriately aged animal models for KD and microbiome research.

Mu et al. further showed KD selectively enriched certain microbial species; specifically, it led to an overgrowth of Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactococcus lactis, which are known to be powerful metabolizers of dietary tryptophan (TRP), capable of converting TRP into bioactive metabolites such as indoles and serotonin. 124 Conversely, other proinflammatory microbes, such as Lactobacillus johnsonii and E. coli, were suppressed in IESS rats on the KD, and inflammation is thought to be important to the pathogenesis of IESS given their responsiveness to ACTH in some patients. 124

There is ample evidence supporting a role in altered tryptophan–serotonin–kynurenine metabolism in IESS. Tryptophan, an essential aromatic amino acid primarily obtained through dietary sources, serves as a critical precursor in numerous physiological pathways, predominantly metabolized via two distinct routes: the serotonin and kynurenine pathways. Approximately 90–95% of tryptophan undergoes catabolism through the kynurenine pathway to kynurenine, catalyzed initially by key enzymes indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase (IDO1) and tryptophan 2,3‐dioxygenase (TDO). Kynurenine is then converted to kynurenic acid and quinolinic acid via the kynurenine aminotransferase and kynurenine 3‐monooxygenase (also known as kynurenine hydroxylase) pathways, respectively (Figure 4). Since KA inhibits, whereas QA enhances neurotransmission, perturbations in IDO1 and KAT activity can have profound effects on cerebral excitability. 125 The kynurenine pathway is primarily regulated by IDO1, which can be activated by stress hormones; notably, IDO1 is also directly stimulated by pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1, IL‐16, and TNF‐α, and inflammation is believed to play a key role in the pathogenesis of IESS. 125 Conversely, the serotonin pathway, accounting for only a minor fraction, converts tryptophan into serotonin and subsequently melatonin, thus influencing mood regulation, circadian rhythm, and neurological function.

Schematic representation of tryptophan metabolism via the serotonin and kynurenine pathways. Tryptophan is metabolized via two main branches: The serotonin pathway (left, green), leading to 5‐hydroxytryptophan (via TPH1/TPH2, Tryptophan Hydroxylase 1/2), serotonin (via DDC, Dopa Decarboxylase), which can then be converted to N‐acetylserotonin (via AANAT, arylalkylamine N acetyltransferase) and subsequently to melatonin (via ASMT, acetylserotonin O‐methyltransferase), or degraded to 5‐hydroxyindoleacetic acid (via MAO A/B, monoamine oxidase A/B). The kynurenine pathway (right, blue), initiated by IDO1/IDO2 (Indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase 1/2) or TDO2 (Tryptophan 2,3‐dioxygenase) under pro‐inflammatory cytokine influence, producing N‐formylkynurenine, kynurenine (via formamidase), and downstream metabolites including kynurenic acid (via KATs, kynurenine aminotransferases), 3‐hydroxykynurenine (via KMO, kynurenine 3‐monooxygenase), 3‐hydroxyanthranilic acid (via KYNU, kynureninase), 2‐amino‐3‐carboxymuconic‐6‐semialdehyde (via HAO, 3‐ hydroxyanthranilate dioxygenase), quinolinic acid (via non‐enzymatic spontaneous cyclization), 2‐aminomuconic‐6‐semialdehyde (via ACMSD, 2‐amino‐3‐carboxymuconate‐6‐semialdehyde decarboxylase), and picolinic acid (via nonenzymatic spontaneous cyclization). Quinolinic acid further leads to NAD+ (via QPRT, Quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase), a crucial coenzyme in cellular energy metabolism.

Patients with IESS have been reported to have abnormal tryptophan load test results that improved in those patients who responded to ACTH. 126 Lower levels of kynurenines and serotonin have subsequently been observed in patients with IESS, indicating a potential connection between disrupted tryptophan metabolism and the development of the condition. 127 The serotonin metabolite 5‐hydroxyindoleacetic acid was also lower in the CSF of children with IESS. 128 Interestingly, kynurenine has been shown to have proconvulsant effects in rodents; thus, lower levels seen in patients with IESS should be protective. However, to fully understand the impact of kynurenine levels on cerebral excitability in the context of IESS, it is also important to know the activity levels of IDO1, KAT, and other downstream enzymes along the kynurenine pathway (see Figure 4), which were not reported in this study. For instance, low kynurenine levels in the presence of low KAT activity likely mean the kynurenine is metabolized to quinolinic acid, which is proconvulsant. Interestingly, subsequent studies have shown decreased KAT activity (KA/KYN ratio) in patients with IESS, with a greater reduction in the KA/KYN ratio in the drug‐resistant patients. 129 , 130 , 131 In the triple‐hit model of IESS, the KD resulted in an elevated KA/KYN ratio in line with clinical studies. However, KAT activity in rodents with IESS compared to nonlesioned controls was not assessed. As previously indicated, increased KAT activity will result in elevated KA levels, which have anticonvulsant effects. KD‐treated pups also had a lowered kynurenine‐to‐tryptophan (KYN/TRP) ratio, indicating reduced IDO1 activity, in contrast with elevated IDO1 activity present in IESS rats. 124 Given that IDO1 activity is a major driver of kynurenine production, the lowered KYN/TRP ratio implied that overactivation of IDO1 might contribute to seizure susceptibility in IESS. Importantly, IDO1 inhibitors, 1‐methyltryptophan and minocycline, not only augmented the KD's antiseizure effects but also reduced spasm frequency in IESS rats fed a control diet. These treatments also raised hippocampal KA levels to match those seen with KD‐fed rats. These experiments establish that lowering KYN/TRP via IDO1 inhibition is sufficient to protect against epileptic spasms, highlighting IDO1 as a critical downstream target of KD.

Fecal transplant, antibiotics and probiotics

Does the altered gut microbiome mediate the antiseizure effect of the KD? Rat pups on a control diet that received fecal microbiota from KD‐fed counterparts showed a significant drop in seizures. Additionally, the KYN/TRP ratio was lower in the microbiota‐transplanted rats, suggesting that microbiota modulation may offer a promising avenue for anticonvulsant therapies. 124 This “transfer” of seizure protection via gut bacteria highlights the sufficiency of KD‐induced microbial changes in mediating antiseizure effects.

Further, broad‐spectrum antibiotics were administered to IESS rats to deplete the gut microbiota. Surprisingly, this antibiotic treatment led to a reduction in spasm frequency, and when given in combination with KD, it resulted in an even greater reduction of epileptic spasms. 124 Antibiotics increased Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactococcus lactis in both KD‐fed and control milk‐fed IESS rats. Antibiotic treatment also influenced tryptophan metabolism; through microbial functional prediction analysis, Mu et al. reported an increase in TRP 2‐3‐dioxygenase and TRP 2‐monooxygenase. Kynurenic acid and the KA/KYN ratio were increased in the serum of KD‐fed IESS rats treated with antibiotics compared to control milk‐fed IESS rats. However, direct effects of antibiotics on brain excitability are not ruled out. 132 , 133 , 134

Excitingly, the manipulation of gut bacteria through probiotics offers a potential nonpharmacological intervention. A targeted probiotic mix of S. thermophilus (HA‐110) and L. lactis (HA‐136) was tested in the IESS model. 135 This probiotic blend led to an increase in species expansion and produced antiseizure effects in IESS. Furthermore, KD‐fed IESS rats developed severe hepatic steatosis, but cotreatment with the probiotic blend completely prevented this. Mechanistically, probiotics restored activation of AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) in the liver as probiotic‐treated KD rats showed increased pAMPK and phosphorylated form of acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (pACC) on western blots, consistent with enhanced fat oxidation. 135

Inflammation and IDO1 suppression

KD‐fed and probiotic‐treated rats showed reduced levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNFα. 135 These findings are important because inflammation is a powerful inducer of IDO1 expression. 136 Thus, KD, through its anti‐inflammatory effects, reduces IDO1 activity, and this may explain the reduction of epileptic spasms. ACTH elevates cortisol and downregulates the corticotropin‐releasing hormone (CRH)–stress axis, which likely suppresses brain cytokines and may decrease IDO1 activity. In other words, ACTH may reduce microglial activation and IDO1 just as KD does, albeit through endocrine pathways. 137

The aforementioned microbiome manipulations (KD, probiotics, antibiotics, or fecal transplant) appear to converge on IDO1 suppression as their common pathway, lowering KYN/TRP ratios and improving seizure control. 124 Thus, the gut–IDO1 axis may be a unifying mechanism by which dietary and microbial therapies exert antiseizure effects. Given this compelling preclinical data, the development of clinical IDO1 inhibitors should be more rigorously explored. Small‐molecule IDO1 inhibitors have been developed (primarily for cancer immunotherapy) 138 but some concerns have been raised due to off‐target effects and the lack of tissue specificity of some IDO1 inhibitors. 139

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Although numerous mechanistic insights have emerged from preclinical studies of KD in animal models of IESS, more research is needed to evaluate whether these findings can be generalized across different rodent models and ultimately to the human disease state. It is our opinion that the field would be greatly advanced with the following future directions: There is a pressing need for more high‐quality, multicenter RCTs to rigorously evaluate the efficacy of the KD in IESS. These studies should incorporate detailed analyses of outcome measures such as underlying etiology, treatment duration, and long‐term outcomes. The long‐term use of the KD requires meticulous and regular monitoring of several clinical parameters, including glucose and insulin levels, concentrations of ketone bodies, and acid–base balance. CONCLUSIONS

KD causes profound shifts in cellular intermediary metabolism and alters whole body physiology. It is thus not surprising that many mechanisms are implicated in its anticonvulsant effects. Recent advances using rodent models of IESS provided strong support for the role of altered respiration and cerebral acidosis in mediating the effects of the KD in IESS. Recent research into the impact of the KD on the gut microbiome and tryptophan–serotonin–kynurenine pathway is yielding promising results. Further elucidation of the mechanistic underpinnings of KD in IESS will undoubtedly lead to the development of novel therapies for drug‐resistant IESS.
