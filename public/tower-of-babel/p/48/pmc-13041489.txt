Facilitators and barriers to adherence to severe malaria treatment guidelines in children at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital, Uganda: a health workers’ perspective
Authors: Sarah C Chelangat, Ashley Nakawuki, Sam Orech, Paul Oboth, Rebecca Nekaka, Jacob S Iramiot, Immaculate Mbwali, Rose Nabirye Chalo, Joshua Epuitai, Pamella R Adongo, Lydia V N Ssenyonga, Samuel Olowo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria is a significant public health concern, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where it causes considerable morbidity and mortality. Severe malaria is a major cause of mortality among children under five, contributing to Uganda’s high malaria mortality burden. Effective case management of severe malaria is crucial which should be guided by the Uganda clinical guidelines adopted from the World Health Organization. This study explored the barriers and facilitators to healthcare providers’ adherence to severe malaria treatment guidelines in children at Mbale Regional Referral hospital, Uganda.Materials and methodsA qualitative descriptive approach was carried out at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital, eastern Uganda. A total of 21 healthcare providers (doctors, nurses, pharmacists; Laboratory technicians; Blood Bank health workers; and clinical officers) were purposively recruited during the study period. Data was collected through key informant interviews, audio recorded, and analyzed using thematic analysis using Braun and Clark’s approach. Adherence to the Lincoln and Gubba principles of credibility, conformability, transferability, and dependability was ensured. Data was presented in tables and narratives. Avis Donabedian model was employed to explore the facilitators and barriers to adherence to the severe malaria management guideline.ResultsThe study identified key facilitators under three themes: Structure (availability of resources, drugs, and training); Process (optimal working conditions, manageable workloads, and use of monitoring tools like yellow stickers); and Patient-related factors (caretaker cooperation, effective communication, and trust-building). Barriers included structure resource constraints, staffing and work load issues; process, delayed referrals, delays in the lab, limited working hours in the lab and pharmacy, low CMEs attendance among nurses; patient related factors like patient financial limitations and poor patient cooperation.ConclusionThis study identified facilitators and barriers to adherence to severe malaria management guidelines in children under five. The barriers and facilitators emerged in terms of structural, processes, patient characteristics and outcome. Addressing resource shortages and improving staffing and workload conditions are crucial for better adherence. Enhancing patient education and strengthening health system infrastructure are also essential for ensuring timely and effective care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05834-y.Keywords: Facilitators, Barriers, Adherence, Guideline, Severe malaria, Children, Uganda

Background

Malaria poses a significant threat to nearly half of the global population with 86 countries in the tropical and subtropical regions struggling with the disease [1]. According to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) estimates, the number of malaria cases worldwide increased from 58.6 cases per 1000 population at risk in 2022 to 263 million cases in 2023, which is an incidence of 60.4 cases per 1000 population at risk [2]. According to world malaria report 2024, Africa accounted for 94% of all cases worldwide in 2023, with just five African nations Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda, Ethiopia, and Mozambique sharing 52% of the global burden [2] Similarly, the WHO African Region accounted for 95% of the anticipated 597 000 malaria fatalities worldwide in 2023 [2].

About 99% of these cases are attributed to the Plasmodium falciparum strain which is responsible for the most severe and life-threatening form of the disease [3].The burden of malaria disproportionately affects children with 80% of the deaths occurring among children under 5 years [3–6]. Additionally, the prevalence of severe malaria among children in hospital-based studies reaches 39% [3].

In Uganda, malaria is a significant public health concern affecting 95% of the country and causing a considerable degree in morbidity and mortality [7]. Alarmingly, 80% of the deaths occur among children under the age of 5 years with the highest prevalence rates found in the Karamoja (34%), West Nile (22%), and Busoga (21%) regions [7]. At Mbale regional referral hospital, available evidence demonstrate that the prevalence of malaria among children to be extremely high estimated to be 45.6% [8]. The Ugandan government has set ambitious targets to reduce malaria cases by 80% and deaths to near zero or 1 death per 100,000 population per year by 2030 [9] In Uganda, severe malaria accounts for 15–20% of hospital admissions and is the major cause of mortality among children under 5 years, contributing to the country’s high malaria mortality burden which is 8th in position worldwide [10]. In February 2022, a high number of deaths among children were reported to the ministry of health from Namutumba district with the cause confirmed later as severe malaria [7]. Data from Apac district in Eastern Uganda reported the prevalence of severe malaria to be 40% among children under five [11]. Evidence from Mbale regional referral Hospital demonstrate that children under five contributed to the greatest proportion 757/1379 (54.9%) of severe Malaria [12]. Available evidence demonstrated that about one in 6 children with malaria will develop severe malaria [13].

To reduce this burden of disease, Uganda has implemented various interventions, which include distribution of treated mosquito nets, effective antimalarial medications, indoor residual spraying, strengthened test and treat policies, intensified malaria case management, targeted measures in high-transmission areas [14, 15]. Despite these efforts, some of the malaria mitigation measures have been received with resistance especially in Mbale Regional referral hospital catchment area. Available evidence from Mbale Regional referral Hospital show that uptake of insecticide treated bed net is low found at 57.9% among parturient women [16]. The poor uptake of Malaria preventive measures makes malaria to remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Uganda, with about 19,600 deaths reported yearly [17].

Effective case management of severe malaria is crucial, this is guided by the Uganda clinical guidelines which was adopted from the World Health Organization guideline [18].

The World Health Organization established guidelines in the management of severe malaria among children under 5 years [18, 19] include among others; giving complete doses of effective parenteral antimalarial specifically Artesunate either intravenously or intramuscularly or intramuscular Artemether for at least 24 h and until the patient can tolerate oral therapy. In case Artesunate or Artemether are unavailable the alternative second line option is quinine either intravenous or intramuscular. The parenteral agents are followed with a 3 day course of oral Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT),, ensuring clinical observations as frequently as possible including vital signs monitoring, coma score, blood glucose level and urine output and supportive care [18, 19]. In addition, according to these guidelines, all patients who present with a fever must be tested by microscopy or Malaria-Rapid Diagnostic test (mRDT) to confirm Diagnosis before they are started on antimalarials, artesunate is the drug of choice which should be given IM or IV until the patient can take oral provide supportive treatment accordingly [18].

Adherence to the severe-malaria management guideline has been demonstrated to have several advantages including; improved patient outcome, decreased malaria specific mortality and mobility, reduced risk of over diagnosis of malaria, reduced hospital delays, and lowered treatment costs [20]. Conversely non-adherence to the severe malaria management guidelines has been associated with poor patient outcome, increased malaria specific mortality and mobility, increased hospital delays, and increased treatment costs as a results of increased need for care escalation like need for intensive care unit admissions, dialysis for the severe malaria associated acute kidney injury. Despite reported benefits and dangers from the adherence and non-adherence to the national guidelines respectively, studies indicate varying levels of health care workers adherence to the new malaria management guidelines which is way below the optimum. Several factors have been found to hinder adherence to the management guidelines of severe malaria including lack of laboratory and diagnostic facilities, inconsistency in the supply of rapid diagnostic test (RDT) kits, limited human resources [21] Lack of job aides like RDT kits stock out [21].

Conversely the facilitators to the adherence to the severe malaria guideline include among others; availability of treatment job aides [22], availability of functional laboratories [22, 23], availability of microscopes and malaria Rapid diagnostic test kits, presence of parenteral drugs [24], thermometers [25].

In East Africa, adoption of the WHO guidelines’ in the real world clinical setting is alarmingly low [23, 26] whereby a “parenteral antimalarial and an ACT are administered to 2.7% (28/1051) of admitted children in Nigeria, 44.5% (1211/2724) in Uganda, and 50.3% (2117/4208) in DRC” [27], there is a gap in the implementation of this guidelines in everyday clinical practice. Available evidence from Uganda showed adherence to the severe-malaria management guideline to be at 3.1% in Kaliro and Buyende districts [28]. Findings from Mbale Regional Referral Hospital show that adherence to the severe malaria management guideline to be low at 3% [29]. These missed opportunities could be contributing to the high in-hospital mortality among the children under five with severe malaria estimated between 3.0% [12] to 9.5% overall, 33.3% and 19.5% among children with cerebral malaria and severe anemia respectively at the same hospital [30].

The reason for the inconsistence in adherence to the severe malaria management guideline is not known. Establishing the root cause of these inconstancies could help address the existing gaps.

Therefore, this study explored the facilitators and barriers to adherence to severe-malaria treatment guideline at Mbale regional referral hospital.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study was a qualitative descriptive research approach.

Study setting

The study was carried out at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH) which is a government funded hospital. Mbale regional referral hospital started serving the population in 1924, when it first opened as a health center, Mbale regional referral hospital has undergone changes evolving into a 450-bed Regional Referral hospital that serves a large population of over 4.6 million individuals across 16 districts. MRRH has over 10 specialists and more than 359 staff. The hospital is situated 250 km east of Kampala in Mbale City. The 16 districts include Bulambuli, Busia, Budaka, Bududa, Butebo, Butaleja, Bukwo, Kapchorwa, Kibuku, Kween, Manafwa, Mbale, Namisindwa, Pallisa, Sironko, and Tororo [31]. The pediatric ward at this hospital handles a high number of patients, with an average of 100–200 children receiving care daily [29]. Mbale Regional Referral Hospital catchment districts/areas Mbale including Bugisu sub-region of Eastern Uganda. The catchment area features both highland and lowland areas with two rainy seasons with long rains from March to July and short rains from September to November. The average annual rainfall typically ranges from about 1000 to 1500 mm. This creates conditions that allow for high, constant malaria transmission in lower areas and seasonal transmission in the higher regions. The sub-region is mostly rural, with smallholder farming as the main source of livelihood. Common crops include tea, coffee, and bananas. Many communities live near irrigated farms, wetlands, and streams that provide good breeding grounds for mosquitoes. Socio-economic conditions in parts of the sub-region include high poverty rates [32, 33], limited access to timely healthcare, and many homes with open eaves that let mosquitoes enter. These factors increase the risk of mosquito bites and delay treatment for feverish children. The geography and socio-economic issues contribute to higher malaria rates and a greater chance of severe disease in children in Uganda [33]. This situation is reflected in the high number of malaria cases and severe admissions reported at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital [12].

Study population

The study population comprised of Doctors, Clinical Officers, ward Nurses, pharmacists, dispenser, laboratory personnel, and blood bank specialists; who are involved in the management of pediatrics at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital who have been working in Peadiatric ward for the past 3 months. The pediatric ward is staffed by a diverse team of medical professionals, including clinical officers, intern physicians, and resident and attending pediatric physicians, qualified nurses, and medical and nursing students. Physicians are present on the acute care section of the ward from 7:00 am to 11:00 pm, and are available on call if needed outside of these hours. During the night shift, from 11:00 pm to 7:00 am, the acute care section is typically staffed by a single qualified nurse [29].

Sample size determination

Purposive sampling was employed to determine the sample size of the study, data collection process was terminated when no new information emerges from the participants. However, available evidence demonstrates that full saturation can be achieved with 16–20 participants [34].Therefore, we recruited 21 participants for this study. The study final sample size comprised of 5 Doctors, 1 Clinical Officers, 9 ward Nurses, 1 pharmacists, 1 dispenser, 2 laboratory personnel, and 2 blood bank specialists.

Inclusion criteria

The study included Doctors, Clinical Officers, Nurses; pharmacists; dispenser; laboratory personnel, blood bank specialists; who are involved in the management of pediatrics at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital for at least 3 months.

Exclusion criteria

The study excluded prospective participants who decline informed consent to participate in the study.

Sampling procedure

A maximum variation purposive sampling technique was used to select Doctors, Clinical Officers, Nine Nurses; pharmacists; laboratory personnel, blood bank specialists who are involved in the management of pediatric patients at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital. The aim was to recruit as many different key players involved in the management of severe malaria within the hospital.

Data collection tool

Key-informant interview guide was developed after a comprehensive literature review which was used to guide the data collection. The Key Informant Interview guide comprised of open-ended questions which was used to gather information on the facilitators and barriers in adherence to the management guidelines of severe malaria in children under 5 years in Mbale Regional referral hospital. The interview guide also had probing questions for follow-up where necessary. For quality and reliability, the Key Informant interview guide was pre-tested among 3 health workers at Namatala health Centre IV after informed consent to enable the researcher to assess its clarity, and relevancy and thereafter made necessary adjustments before applying it in the study area.

Data collection procedure

Approval letter was obtained from Busitema University Faculty of Health Sciences Research and Ethics committee (BUFHSREC) under approval number BUFHS 2024-196 on the 17th/Sept/2024, the research permission to conduct the study was obtained from the Dean faculty of Health Sciences Busitema University, and administrative clearance from Mbale Regional Referral Hospital director for permission to collect data. The researchers introduced herself to the participants and explain to them the topic and objectives of the research. Doctors, clinicians and nurses who consented to participate in the study were required to read and sign an informed consent form. This was done in private room at the hospital.

Data was collected through Key Informant interviews using an interview guide. The key informant interview lasted between 30 and 45-min. The notes were taken by an assistant for the non-verbal cues. An audio recorder was used to record the interview after permission from the participants. The participants ask the researcher what they did not understand. Auditory data was verbatim transcribed.

Rigors for the study

The study rigor was achieved by maintaining trustworthiness; making the study suitable, reliable and acceptable hence believability of the study. This was based on the four criteria of evaluating trustworthiness of data and interpretation as described by Lincoln and Guba [35] which includes; credibility, confirmability, dependability, and transferability.

Credibility

The researcher ensured credibility through prolonged engagement with the participants, to gain a deeper understanding. Each interview with a participant in this research was 30–45 min. This allowed the participant to gain trust and open up to the researcher. Lastly, member checking, sharing findings with participants to validate interpretations, ensure their voices are represented and to verify the accuracy of the conclusions made by the researcher.

Transferability

Transferability was ensured through a thick description of the study, which involves providing adequate details on the site, participants and methods used to collect data during the study. This will help other people to replicate this study to other places.

Dependability

To ensure dependability, the following was employed during the research time, Peer debriefing this means getting a second opinion from colleagues and the supervisor to ensure that am on the right track. Separating facts from opinions, keep observations and interpretations separate to avoid mixing facts with personal bias. Being transparent knowing that the work will be reviewed by others and lastly being honest about own biases and how they may affect the research process [36, 37].

Confirmability

The researcher ensured confirmability by Involving other researchers to verify and validate findings, minimizing own influence during data collection, use of observations, recorders, building rapport and trust to ensure accuracy of data collected.

Data analysis

The recorded data from the key informant interviews was transcribed verbatim, the data was then analyzed using thematic analysis by Braun and Clark’s approach [38]. The thematic analysis was used to interpret the interview texts to uncover underlying themes, concepts and patterns by paying attention to every detail in the data, recurring ideas were identified, topics and meanings that come up often. An inductive approach was employed in the data to generate the themes. The following are the steps which were taken during the thematic analysis [38, 39]. Becoming familiar with the data collected: This was achieved in this study by transcribing the audios and reading the transcripts again and again to get a deeper understanding of the data. The labels or codes were assigned to text phrases or sentences to categorize content. The codes were analyzed to identify relationships and patterns, grouping related codes into comprehensive themes and this helps us group data into meaningful clusters. The themes were reviewed to ensure that there is an accurate representation of the data. The themes were defined, by explaining what exactly we meant by each theme and identify how each theme contributes to a better understanding of the data set from which they were developed. The findings were written narratively and summarized in tables [37–39].

Definitions

Severe Malaria was conceptualized in this study as: the presence of asexual forms of P. falciparum in the blood on microscopy and or, a positive rapid diagnostic test (RDT) for malaria, accompanied by one or more of the following complications;

Unresponsive coma characterized by inability to react to pain stimuli, severe anemia with a hemoglobin level below 5 g/dl, Respiratory distress characterized by rapid breathing and nasal flaring, Dangerously low blood sugar which is below 2.2 mmol/l, Cardiovascular collapse with a systolic blood pressure below 50 mmHg in children, Kidney failure with a urine output below 12 ml/kg in 24 h, Unexplained bleeding without an apparent cause, Recurrent seizures with two or more episodes in 24 h, Metabolic acidosis characterized by labored breathing and a plasma bicarbonate level below 15 mmol/hemoglobinuria characterized dark colored urine, Pulmonary edema characterized by rapid, labored breathing and nasal flaring.

Additional indicators include: Impaired consciousness with confusion, drowsiness, or inability to respond to pain, Yellowing of the skin and eyes without an apparent cause, Inability to sit or drink, with severe weakness, Severe vomiting with inability to retain fluids, Severe dehydration with sunken eyes and lethargy Extremely high fever above 39.5 °C, Extremely high parasite count above 250,000/microliter [18, 19].

Facilitators to adherence to the management guidelines of severe malaria

Adequate buildings

Adequate beds

Theme one: structure

Structure is described as the environment where care is being offered which include organizational physical features, equipment used, human resources, organization structure, supplies, physical guideline and funding [40]. This theme emerged from four subthemes which are, Physical environment, resource availability, Organizational policies, and knowledge and training.

Sub theme one: physical environment

Physical environment was derived from three code, including adequate buildings.

Adequate beds and Availability of power. The participants narrated that having adequate building equipped with adequate beds provided space for admission of children with severe malaria right from pediatric acute care unit which is an entry point to the wards. In addition they pointed the availability of reliable power supply to the hospital to be a facilitator in the adherence to the malaria guidelines, right from running of the oxygen plant, electronic laboratory equipment which facilitate timely diagnosis among others.

………Our buildings infrastructure is up to date because that is a lab that is meeting the international standards. For sure, we have everything in place………. [Participant 18 lab technician MRRH-Male]

Subtheme two: resource availability

Resource availability was derived from two codes namely drug and equipment availability. Drug availability is a key facilitator of adherence to the management guidelines for severe malaria. According to the participants, when essential anti-malarial medications are readily available, healthcare providers are able to promptly administer the correct treatments to patients, ensuring timely and effective management of the disease. This reduces the risk of complications and improves patient outcomes. For healthcare workers, having access to the right drugs means they can follow established treatment protocols, which are crucial for managing severe malaria. Adherence to these guidelines is critical, as it ensures that patients receive the appropriate medications in the right doses and at the right time. Additionally, for patients, the availability of drugs helps foster confidence in the treatment process, making them more likely to complete their prescribed course of medication. In turn, this improves overall adherence to treatment and contributes to better clinical outcomes, including a reduced risk of treatment failure, relapse, and mortality.

……………………..Then another enabler, when we have artesunate on the ward, we reduce the turnaround time for looking for the artesunate, we immediately give first dose as they look for the rest……………… [Participant 10, Nurse Female]

Diagnostic tools help ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment decisions. Equipment for monitoring vital signs, such as pulse oximeters allows for effective management of complications.

Subtheme three: favorable organizational policies

Existence of favorable policies within the hospital such as the 24 h full operation of the blood bank, polices allowing the volunteers and apprentices to practice in the hospital especially in the blood bank emerged from the participants as an important enabler to the adherence to severe malaria guideline:

Theme one: structure

The theme emerged from 2 subthemes: resource constraints and staffing and workload issues.

Subtheme one: resource constraints

Resource constraints drug stock outs, blood supply shortages, limited access to oxygen, poor physical infrastructure. Participants frequently mentioned the unavailability of artesunate, rapid diagnostic test (RDT) strips, glucometers, and vacutainers. These shortages lead to delays in diagnosis and treatment, placing patients at higher risk of complications. Similarly, stock outs of emergency medications, such as rectal paracetamol and anticonvulsants, further exacerbate treatment delays, particularly during night shifts or in emergencies. More so infrastructure deficiencies also impede effective care. Overcrowded wards, insufficient beds, poor-quality mattresses, and a lack of isolation rooms are recurring issues. Healthcare workers mention that congested spaces make it challenging to maneuver between patients, observe critical conditions like convulsions, and maintain hygiene standards. Furthermore, participants stressed the point of stock outs of specific blood groups like blood group O positive which is the commonest blood type meaning its demand is highest and also rhesus negative blood groups which are very rare in the population so getting them is also hard.

…………………….Blood groups like O+, O-, these are very hard blood to get. They come once in a while. So, stock out gives you a blow………………….. [Participant 11, Nurse Male]

………………….Sometimes, in fact, like now, we are supposed to have MRDT at hand, but you find that they come for one week, they get finished. Now you spend like one month so we have that challenge of equipment used in the management of malaria………………. [Participant 9 Male nurse].

Subtheme two: staffing and workload issues

The sub-theme emerged from two codes, inadequate staffing and high patient load. Most participants persistently reported inadequate staffing levels, which result in overwhelming workloads for the available healthcare workers. Discussion

Adherence to the severe malaria treatment guideline among children at Mbale regional referral hospital has been reported to be very low at 3% [29]. This study aimed to explore the facilitators and barriers to adherence to severe malaria management guideline in children under 5 years from the perspective of health workers at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital.

Facilitators to adherence to severe malaria management guidelines in children

Several facilitators were identified from the study, firstly, the availability of resources emerged as key facilitator. Participants consistently reported resources such as access to diagnostic tools including rapid diagnostic test for Malaria (RDTs), glucometers, and pulse-oximeters enables timely diagnosis, monitoring, and initiation of treatment. This aligns with finding from previous related study by Nalikka [24] at Kayunga Regional referral Hospital Uganda. Severe malaria is associated with severe anemia as a result of hemolysis of the red blood cells [42, 43]. This lowers the blood oxygen carrying capacity with resultant ventilation-perfusion mismatch and a consequent respiratory failure [44, 45]. This associated hypoxemia necessitates the need for continuous monitoring of the oxygen saturation in children with severe malaria [46]. Therefore, the availability of pulse-oximeters and stable oxygen supply allows for proper monitoring of patient’s oxygen saturation and pulse-rate [47]. This ensures that complications like respiratory failure are promptly addressed. Pulse-oximetry has been used in the diagnosis, management and prediction of outcomes among patients with febrile illnesses including severe malaria [45, 48]. The findings of this study therefore suggests that adherence to severe malaria guideline is not solely dependent on individual healthcare worker competence, but rather on the functionality of the health system in providing the minimum inputs required for evidence-based care. Additionally the findings demonstrate that even highly knowledgeable healthcare workers are unable to adhere to guidelines when system resources are inconsistent, highlighting the critical role of structural readiness in severe malaria management guideline implementation.

Similarly, availability of essential medicines for the management of severe malaria for example intravenous artesunate and blood products allows healthcare workers to implement severe malaria guideline recommended care without delay. Timely initiation and administration of malaria treatment has been associated with reduced morbidity and mortality among patients with s malaria in Uganda [49]. As compared with quinine, use of artesunate has been associated with a 22% reduction in case fatality rates in children with malaria [50]. Blood products facilitate the treatment of severe anemia which is a hemolytic form of anemia calminating from breakdown of red blood cells [51, 52].

Healthcare workers’ prior knowledge of malaria management guidelines, as well as access to informational emerged as major facilitators to the adherence to severe malaria management guideline. Knowledge of the guidelines helps healthcare providers make timely and accurate treatment decisions and so guideline adherence. Healthcare workers’ prior training and access to clinical guidelines further facilitates adherence by enhancing confidence in decision-making and ensuring standardized care. Easy access to the severe malaria management guideline has been reported in related studies done in Uganda where easy access to the severe malaria guideline was a major facilitator to adherence to the guideline [53]. Unlike previous studies that focused largely on training coverage [54, 55], this study demonstrates that ready access to physical guideline materials and peer-supported learning environments plays an important role in sustaining adherence during routine clinical practice. The findings of this study on the role of training on adherence to severe malaria management guideline was reported from previous from Nigeria by Amachree and Eleke [56], among nurses in Port Harcourt, where participants trained on the Integrated management of childhood illness (IMCI) guidelines demonstrated significantly higher adherence (83% higher) compared to those who were not trained, highlighting the positive impact of training on guideline adherence. The authors recommended that providing regular training sessions for nurses on the IMCI guidelines could facilitate better adherence to the guidelines and improve the quality of healthcare provided to children in primary health centers [56]. These findings are in agreement with findings from other related studies elsewhere [21, 56]. However, our findings are inconsistent with the findings from some other studies. For example a study carried out in Karamoja, Uganda in 2022 showed that there is no significant association between training and malaria case management [57]. More Contradicting findings from Uganda by Mpimbaza et al. [22] demonstrated that health care workers who had received a training in malaria management showed a lower likelihood of appropriate case management [22] and no association between the latest training on malaria case management and appropriate management [58].

The possible explanation for this inconsistent with our findings could be due to differences in study setting and study design in that our study was purely qualitative and the later by Zalwango et al. [57] in Karamoja Uganda was quantitative. Therefore, the lack of association between training and appropriate malaria management points that training alone cannot guarantee malaria guideline adherence but a complex interaction between several factors. The relevancy of hard copy clinical treatment guideline has been cited in other related studies in Uganda [22] and Nigeria [59].

From a process-of-care perspective, manageable patient load, presence of students and interns, continuous blood bank services, and the use of yellow stickers to identify severe malaria cases were identified as important facilitators to the severe malaria treatment guideline adherence. Notably, the use of yellow stickers emerged as a context-specific innovation that improved prioritization of critically ill children. This represents a novel operational insight that has not been widely reported in the literature and offers a low-cost strategy for improving adherence in busy hospital settings. Expounding more on patient loads, when patient loads are within manageable limits, healthcare workers are able to provide focused care, administer treatments on time, and monitor patients more effectively, this happens mostly in the morning hours before admitting in of new cases. The presence of student nurses and other interns helped reduce the workload of healthcare workers by assisting with tasks such as patient monitoring and administration of treatments as part of their skills acquisition. Access to the blood bank 24/7 allowed healthcare workers to quickly secure necessary blood supplies, ensuring timely transfusions for children with severe malaria that is if there is blood in the laboratory. Finally, the introduction of yellow stickers to identify patients with severe malaria helped healthcare workers maintain focus on these critical cases, ensuring timely and accurate treatment. Color coding has demonstrated invaluable input in triaging of patients from time immemorial [60].

The third theme that emerged from the participants was the patient related factors that enable health workers adhere to the severe malaria management guideline: Cooperative patients and their caregivers played a crucial role in supporting adherence to malaria management guidelines. When caregivers are engaged in the treatment process, they help in ensuring that medications are administered on time and necessary interventions, such as nasogastric tube feeding or oxygen therapy, are implemented appropriately.

Effective communication and counselling also facilitates adherence, as caregivers who understood the importance of nasogastric tube usage, blood transfusion or oxygen therapy are more likely to follow the instructions by the health worker. Patient education and empowerment, particularly around the timing of artesunate administration, were key factors in improving adherence and achieving better clinical outcome, Healthcare providers reported to often gain the trust of caregivers by reassuring them, explaining the procedure, and offering consistent support, even if it requires multiple discussions. In cases where caregivers refuse a treatment, like an NG tube or oxygen, they reported that it is essential to provide clear explanations and, if needed, gain written consent for refusal. Over time, most caregivers accept necessary interventions once they understand their critical role in the child’s survival and recovery.

Additionally, the empowerment of caretakers through health education significantly enhances adherence to the severe malaria treatment guideline. By teaching caregivers about medication schedules, such as when to administer the next dose, and ensuring they understand the importance of consistent treatment, healthcare providers can foster better cooperation. Caregivers also serve as important reminders, helping to ensure treatments are given on time, especially when nurses or students are unavailable. In essence, a supportive and communicative approach, combined with educating and empowering caregivers, is essential in ensuring adherence to management guidelines and improving outcomes for children with severe malaria.

Availability of resources and finances, this enables care givers to purchase medications and other hospital demands in case of stock-outs or emergencies and the line is long in the pharmacy, similar findings were also reported by Atukunda et al. [20]. The findings indicate that when caregivers understand the rationale for interventions such as nasogastric feeding, oxygen therapy, or blood transfusion, they actively support timely care and remind healthcare workers about medication schedules. This study therefore contributes new evidence that caregivers can function as active partners in severe malaria treatment guideline adherence, particularly in settings with staff shortages.

Barriers to adherence to severe malaria management guidelines in children

Resource constraints emerged as a significant barrier to the adherence to severe malaria management guidelines. Shortages in critical supplies like medications, diagnostic tools and equipment were narrated by the participants, this directly impacts the timely diagnosis and treatment of patients. The frequent stock-outs of key drugs such as artesunate, and essential diagnostic tools like Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT) strips, glucometers strips, and vacutainers, contributed to delays in treatment initiation. For example, participants reported that when emergency medications such as rectal paracetamol and anticonvulsants were unavailable, treatment was often delayed, particularly during night shifts. The findings are in line with those from study done in Uganda where a major barrier was the inconsistent availability of artemether-Lumefantrine (AL) stock, especially in government-owned facilities, which hindered appropriate malaria treatment [22]. In addition, a study carried out by Kolekang et al. [61] in Ghana, the authors identified several barriers to adherence to the ‘test, treat, and track’ (T3) malaria case management guideline among prescribers in Ghana. These barriers included irregular supply of malaria rapid diagnostic test kits (RDT), mistrust of RDT and microscopy negative results, absence of lab technicians, Inadequate staff numbers and high patient numbers were reported to negatively affect adherence to the management guideline. Similar results were reported in other studies elsewhere in Uganda, Sierra Leone and Sudan [21, 62, 63] respectively.

Additionally, blood supply shortages, particularly for blood groups like O positive and rare groups like rhesus-negative blood was also highlighted as a challenge, this was narrated by most of the participants. Blood group O positive is one of the commonest blood types and can be safely transfused to most other blood groups making its demand high and so gets out of stock quickly and rhesus negative blood is also rare since the donors are few in the population. The hospital relies on regional blood banks and this coupled with irregular availability of certain blood groups, significantly impacted the ability to deliver timely blood transfusions, which are a critical aspect of severe malaria management.

Physical infrastructure limitations also hindered effective care delivery. Overcrowded wards, insufficient beds, and warn out mattresses makes it difficult to maintain hygiene and closely monitor critical conditions, such as seizures.

Staffing shortages and overwhelming workloads emerged as another critical barrier to guideline adherence. With insufficient staffing, healthcare workers were often required to manage high patient loads, resulting in delayed interventions and reduced capacity for close patient monitoring. During shifts with high patient volumes, essential treatments, such as artesunate administration, are reported to be delayed. Nurses described how, under such conditions, there is little time for investigations or even continuous monitoring, which is crucial in the management of severe malaria. These excessive workloads leads to staff exhaustion, which further compromises the quality of care provided. The findings on high patient loads as a major barrier to adherence to severe malaria management guidelines is consistent with findings from a study in Northern Uganda [53]. High patient loads as a barrier to severe malaria management guideline adherence have similar findings were reported in related studies elsewhere [25, 64, 65]. This universal reportage of workload-related challenges among participants underscores the structural nature of this barrier and suggests that adherence failures are more reflective of system strain than individual negligence. However, the studies from Senegal argued that even at times when workload is high, there is no evidence that provider effort or quality of care are significantly reduced. They further stated that the providers operate below their production possibility frontier and have sufficient capacity to attend more patients without compromising quality [66]. This inconsistency in the findings with that of the present study could be due to differences in study setting and study design.

Theme 2 comprised of barriers associated with the process of giving care to the patients, this included Systemic and Organizational Issues. Systemic and organizational issues were also significant barriers to severe malaria management guideline adherence. Delays in patient referrals from lower-level health facilities was reported by all the participants, particularly for patients with severe malaria, often the hospital receive critically ill children who need urgent and close monitoring, because of this late arrival. less time is left for the health worker who is probably only one on duty to even do certain tests because of delays in the laboratory, clinician’s immediately start on medication as they wait for the results to come back. The participants narrated that following the guidelines in such a situation is not possible. Delays in laboratory was similarly reported as a barrier by Nalikka [24] in Uganda. Limited working hours in the laboratory and pharmacy was reported by all the participants. The participants narrated that long queues at the pharmacy contributes to treatment delays. The laboratory, for example, operates from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., Monday to Friday, but is closed on weekends, limiting the availability of necessary diagnostic tests outside of regular working hours. Participants also noted that sometimes samples were lost in the laboratory or results were not available on time, leading to clinical decisions being made without laboratory results. The closure of the pharmacy during off-hours was particularly a problem for urgent medication needs, especially in emergency situations when patients arrived after pharmacy working hours. In addition, the challenge of long waiting times at the pharmacy during working hours made it difficult to quickly access needed medications. Therefore, this study provides new insight into how healthcare workers are forced to initiate treatment empirically and bypass guideline steps due to these operational limitations, highlighting a gap between guideline design and real-world feasibility.

Finally, low attendance at Continuing Medical Education sessions (CMEs) was another organizational barrier. Despite the availability of CMEs to enhance healthcare workers’ knowledge and skills in management of children with severe malaria sing the established guidelines, many nurses were either too busy or uninterested in attending. This gap in training hinderes the uptake of updated guidelines and practices, ultimately impacting adherence to evidence-based protocols for severe malaria management. Findings from this study on limited CME attendance and limited training on the severe malaria management guideline has been reported in several studies across the globe. A study carried out in Buyende and Kaliro districts in Busoga Region-Eastern Uganda showed that lack of staff training on national malaria treatment guidelines in the past 3 years, this contributed to the low adherence level [28]. Another Ugandan study on adherence to malaria guidelines by Nalikka [24] at Kayunga Regional referral Hospital, health workers reported no interests and limited engagement in trainings on guideline updates and malaria management this in return results in limited knowledge with a consequent poor guideline adherence. Relatedly a study among prescribers in Ghana by Kolekang et al. [61] showed that lack of regular training for some categories of staff in guideline use makes it hard for the people to understand the ambiguity of the guidelines [61]. Additionally, a cross sectional descriptive analytical study carried out at health centers at port Harcourt Nigeria in 2022 showed that there is No training of staff on updates on the Integrated Management of Childhood Illness Guidelines among nurses, this makes it hard for the nurse to apply the new updates or they may not be aware of these guidelines. According to Lambiris et al. [67], inadequate referral facility worker training was one of the key barriers to successful implementation of rectal artesunate as pre-referral treatment for severe malaria in Sub-Saharan Africa [67]. More findings from Sub-Saharan Africa by Baraka et al. [68] demonstrated that there is lack of continuous medical education of the updates made in the malaria guidelines leading to low adherence to this treatment guidelines [69].

Regarding the third theme which is patient-related factors that hinder adherence to the severe malaria management guidelines, financial limitations and caregiver poor cooperation, emerged from the participants as significant barriers to adherence to the severe malaria treatment guideline. Families often struggled to afford medications or diagnostic tools, particularly during stock-outs when supplies were unavailable in the hospital. This financial burden leads to delays in treatment initiation, as caregivers were forced to seek alternative sources for necessary medications. Furthermore, issues of cooperation between caregivers and healthcare workers also emerged as a major contributor to the suboptimal adherence severe malaria treatment guideline. Delays in the administration of artesunate doses were often attributed to caregivers not adhering to prescribed schedules or failing to inform nurses when it’s time for the next dose.

Misconceptions and myths, particularly about the use of oxygen therapy or nasogastric tubes, also played a role in hindering adherence to the severe malaria treatment guideline. Some caregivers believed that oxygen therapy indicates that the patient is soon dying, leading them to resist its use. Additionally, cultural and religious beliefs sometimes led to refusal of blood transfusions despite adequate information, which is a critical component of malaria treatment. Some religions and cultures do not accept blood transfusions as they believed their patient would still recover without blood.

Poor health seeking behaviors among caretakers was another significant challenge the severe malaria treatment guideline adherence. The participants narrated that many patients arrive at the hospital in critical conditions after delays at home on self-medication from drug outlets at their own reach. These late arrivals, often with advanced complications like severe anemia or unresponsiveness, placed a considerable strain on healthcare workers and reduced the likelihood of positive outcomes. The findings show that socioeconomic and cultural factors directly interfere with the severe malaria clinical guideline adherence, an area that is often underemphasized in guideline evaluations.

Implications of the study findings

The findings suggest that improving adherence to severe malaria management guidelines requires health system strengthening, rather than focusing solely on healthcare worker training. Ensuring consistent availability of essential medicines, diagnostics, blood products, and staffing is critical. In addition, simple context-specific interventions such as patient prioritization tools, caregiver education, and strengthening student supervision can meaningfully improve adherence to the severe malaria management guideline.

At the policy level, aligning guideline recommendations with operational realities, extending laboratory and pharmacy services, and strengthening referral systems may enhance adherence to the severe malaria management guideline.

Future studies should incorporate the perspectives of caregivers and patients to better understand their role in facilitating or hindering adherence to the severe malaria management guideline.

Multicenter studies across different levels of health facilities would also help determine the transferability of the findings of the present study.

Additionally, interventional research evaluating low-cost strategies such as patient-labeling systems, caregiver engagement models, and task-shifting approaches could provide practical solutions for improving adherence to the severe malaria management guideline in resource-constrained settings.

Strength and limitations of the study

The major strength of this present study is the qualitative nature employing key informant interviews and maximum variation purposive sampling. This enabled in-depth exploration and consequently in-depth and comprehensive understanding of the facilitators and barriers to the adherence to the severe malaria treatment guideline in the hospital. This is because though previous studies for example Moffitt et al. [29] investigated the adherence to the severe malaria guideline quantitatively and also determined the possible areas of need for improvement. Their quantitative nature does not comprehensively provide areas that need improvement in regards to the adherence to the severe malaria guideline. Therefore this present adds into their body of knowledge enabling and intensive and extensive understanding of the facilitators and barriers to the adherence to the severe malaria treatment guideline in this regional referral hospital.

While this study provides valuable insights into the facilitators and barriers to adherence to treatment guidelines for severe malaria in children at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital, there are several limitations which should be considered while interpreting the results; the research was conducted at a single hospital, Mbale Regional Referral Hospital. While the findings offer important insights for this specific setting, they may not fully represent the challenges faced in other hospitals or regions with different resource levels, patient populations, or healthcare systems. The results may therefore not be entirely generalizable to other contexts. However, Mbale regional referral hospital has a large catchment area covering a large population of over 4.6 million people across over 16 districts in the region, the results therefore represents the status of guideline adherence in the region and therefore increases the transferability of the findings to other regional referral hospitals.

Secondly the data collected from healthcare workers relied on self-reports, which may be subject to bias. Participants may have provided responses influenced by social desirability, especially regarding sensitive issues such as workload, resource shortages, or systemic challenges. However, to minimize this limitation, data were collected anonymously, confidentiality was emphasized, and standardized key informant interview guide was used. Additionally, triangulation across cadres of staff helped to enhance credibility.

Finally, the study primarily focused on the perspectives of healthcare workers and did not incorporate the views of patients or caregivers. While healthcare workers play a central role in severe malaria management, understanding the experiences and challenges faced by patients and caregivers could provide a more comprehensive view of adherence barriers and facilitators to the adherence to the severe malaria treatment guideline.

Conclusion

This study has highlighted the key facilitators and barriers to adherence to management guidelines for severe malaria in children under five at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital. The facilitators included; the availability of essential resources like medications and diagnostic tools, as well as healthcare workers’ knowledge and training in malaria management. Favorable work conditions, such as manageable patient loads and access to student assistance, were also key contributors to improved guideline adherence. Furthermore, supportive patient and caregiver involvement, through education and engagement, was found to positively influence adherence and treatment outcomes. Despite these facilitators, several barriers were identified that can hinder adherence to the management guidelines. The barriers identified included resource constraints, understaffing and workload issues, patient-related challenges, and systemic and organizational issues, all of which significantly hinder timely and effective adherence to malaria treatment protocols. Resource constraints, such as stock-outs of essential medications and diagnostic tools, alongside infrastructural challenges, emerged as critical factors that delay diagnosis and treatment initiation. Additionally, the strain caused by insufficient staffing, excessive workloads, and delays in patient referrals and laboratory results further exacerbates the difficulty in adhering to guidelines. On the patient side, financial constraints, cultural beliefs, and delayed health-seeking behaviors contributed to delayed treatment and suboptimal care.

Addressing resource shortages, improving staffing and workload conditions, enhancing patient education, and strengthening health system infrastructure are crucial steps toward ensuring that children under five receive timely and effective care in line with current malaria management protocols.

Recommendations

The Hospital administration needs to adopt evidenced-based targeted interventions for example workflow changes, such as obtaining patient blood samples for diagnostic testing by the admitting healthcare provider as well as utilizing patient caregivers to assist nursing staff in timing medications.

The current staff-to-patient ratio is extremely strained, leading to healthcare workers becoming overwhelmed and unable to provide optimal care. More staff, especially in specialized areas like pediatric and malaria wards, are urgently needed to distribute the workload and ensure that patients receive adequate attention. The Ministry of Health, in collaboration with hospital administration, must recruit and deploy additional healthcare workers. They should focus on hiring more staff particularly for high-demand areas like pediatric care, emergency rooms, and malaria treatment wards. In addition, a shift system should be implemented to ensure that there are always sufficient personnel on duty at least 4 or 5 nurses in one shift.

The current hospital spaces are overcrowded and not conducive to providing quality care. Emergency situations, such as resuscitation of patients, are difficult to manage due to the limited space, leading to delays in treatment and a higher risk of complications. Therefore, the Ministry of Health, in collaboration with hospital administration, should prioritize infrastructure development by either expanding existing wards or constructing new units. Hospitals should be provided with additional space for patient isolation, emergency treatment areas, and sufficient room for patient beds.

Frequent stock outs of essential drugs, such as artesunate for malaria treatment, create significant gaps in treatment and patient care. This issue needs to be addressed by ensuring a consistent supply of medications to avoid delays in patient care. The government, through the Ministry of Health, must work closely with national medical supply chains to ensure that hospitals receive timely and sufficient deliveries of essential medicines. This ensures that the hospital is well-stocked with necessary drugs, including emergency medications for conditions like malaria and severe anemia.

Prevention of malaria is just as important as its treatment. There is a need for health education campaigns to raise awareness about how to prevent malaria, particularly in rural areas. Communities should be informed about the importance of sleeping under mosquito nets, eliminating mosquito breeding grounds, and seeking prompt medical attention when malaria symptoms appear. Local health authorities, including community health workers and Village Health Teams (VHTs), should lead health education initiatives in collaboration with government health agencies. The government should fund and support these outreach programs, using various media channels such as radio, social media, and public gatherings to disseminate information about malaria prevention. Additionally, schools, churches, and local leaders should be mobilized to share prevention messages regularly.

Healthcare workers, particularly those in frontline malaria and pediatric care, need regular training to stay updated on the latest guidelines and best practices for managing malaria and other diseases. This includes training on proper diagnosis, treatment protocols, and how to handle emergency cases. The Ministry of Health should facilitate and mandate regular CMEs for all healthcare workers in malaria-endemic regions. Hospitals should be provided with guidelines and teaching materials for training sessions. Senior healthcare professionals should be tasked with conducting these training sessions and ensuring that all staff, including new recruits, are well-prepared to handle malaria cases.

Malaria testing kits should be readily available in all wards, especially in acute pediatric ward where timely diagnosis is crucial. Diagnostic tools like malaria rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) and machines for blood tests, including hemoglobin estimations, should be provided at the point of care to reduce delays and improve treatment outcomes. The Ministry of Health, in collaboration with hospital administration, should ensure that sufficient diagnostic tools are available in all relevant wards. The government should allocate funds for the purchase and regular supply of testing kits and diagnostic machines, ensuring that they are accessible, especially in emergencies. Local health departments should monitor and manage the stock levels of these essential items.

Caregivers, especially mothers, need to be educated about the importance of timely malaria treatment. Many patients, particularly children, miss out on treatment schedules due to delays in receiving medications. Health workers should actively involve caregivers in managing treatment schedules and ensure they understand when to administer medications like artesunate. Nurses and doctors should educate caregivers during consultations and hospital admissions. This can be done through effective communication and sensitization sessions, as well as through printed materials like treatment schedules and instructions. Hospitals should ensure that caregivers are aware of the treatment plan, including specific times for administering medication, and should encourage caregivers to remind nurses when it’s time for a dose.

Recognizing and rewarding health workers who go above and beyond can boost morale, encourage adherence to guidelines, and improve overall care. A system of regular evaluation and rewards should be implemented. Hospital administrations, in partnership with the Ministry of Health, should implement performance-based recognition programs. This could include quarterly or annual awards for outstanding healthcare workers.

By addressing these critical areas staffing, infrastructure, drug availability, education, training and diagnostics, the government and Mbale regional referral hospital can significantly improve the quality of care for malaria patients, reduce the burden on healthcare workers, and ultimately lower the incidence of malaria-related complications and deaths.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical Approval was sought from Busitema University Faculty of Health Sciences Research and Ethics committee (BUFHSREC) reference number BUFH-2024-196. Administrative clearance was sought from Mbale Regional Referral Hospital Director to be allowed to conduct the study. Permission was obtained from the unit officers in charge before reaching the participants. Informed consent was obtained from respondents after explanation prior the data collection and will be free to terminate their participation at any point during the study. All data collection was done confidentially and anonymously. No personal identifiers like names, identification cards will be collected during the course of the interview. To maintain anonymity of the study participants, unique numbers will be used.
