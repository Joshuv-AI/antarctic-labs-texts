Bedding’ dry sanitization with aluminosilicates affects its chemical composition, growth performance, meat quality, and footpad dermatitis incidence in broiler chickens
Authors: Sebastian Wlaźlak, Jakub Biesek, Mirosław Banaszak, Szymon Różański, Marek Adamski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of aluminosilicates used as a litter additive on production performance, carcass composition, litter chemical composition, and footpad dermatitis (FPD) in broiler chickens. A total of 240 Ross 308 broiler chicks were randomly assigned to one control group (CON) and three experimental groups. Zeolite and halloysite (80:20) were applied to the litter at 125 g/m² either once (DSA1), twice (DSA2), or six times (DSA6) during the 42-day rearing period. Production parameters, including viability, body weight (BW), body weight gain (BWG), growth rate (GR), feed intake (FI), feed conversion ratio (FCR), and the European production efficiency factor, were monitored throughout rearing. Post slaughter, carcass yield, carcass composition, and physicochemical traits of breast and leg muscles (pH, color, water-holding capacity, and drip loss) were determined. The chemical composition of the litter and the incidence of FPD were also assessed. Birds from the DSA6 group showed significantly lower FI and FCR compared with the other groups (P<0.001; P=0.032). Chickens from the DSA2 group exhibited significantly greater water loss in leg muscles compared with CON and DSA6 (P=0.011). Litter from the experimental groups contained significantly more dry matter than that from the CON group (P<0.001). In addition, magnesium (P=0.001) and phosphorus (P=0.004) contents were higher in the litter of the DSA2 and DSA6 groups, and iron content was higher in DSA6 (P<0.001). The incidence of FPD was significantly reduced in the DSA6 group (P<0.001). Overall, frequent application of aluminosilicates in litter improved FI and FCR while lowering the incidence of FPD, indicating beneficial effects on both production efficiency and animal welfare. Furthermore, the used litter contained increased concentrations of selected minerals, which may enhance its potential value for agricultural use. Based on the present findings, weekly dry sanitization with zeolite and halloysite can be recommended as an effective strategy in broiler production systems.

Introduction

The welfare, health, and production efficiency of broiler chickens depend on both genetic and environmental factors (Meluzzi and Siri, 2009; Baracho et al., 2018). Key environmental factors influencing the microclimate include air temperature, humidity (Baracho et al., 2019), microbiological air quality, concentrations of harmful gases, and litter quality (Ritz et al., 2009; Ishchenko et al., 2019). Bedding material should be inexpensive, uncontaminated, highly absorbent, and resistant to caking (Diarra et al., 2021; Biesek et al., 2024).

Appropriate nutrition (e.g., protein, biotin, sodium, and potassium levels) and the use of litter additives, including propionic acid, monobasic calcium phosphate, aluminum chloride (III), alum, clay, or sodium bisulfate, can reduce litter moisture and beneficially influence pH and microbial activity (Nagaraj et al., 2007; Świątkiewicz et al., 2017). Among these, zeolite and halloysite are natural aluminosilicate minerals that differ in structure but share several important properties. Zeolite has a three-dimensional framework, whereas halloysite has a nanotubular, two-dimensional layered structure (Bish, 2013; Burmańczuk et al., 2015; Atyaksheva and Kasyanov, 2021). Both are characterized by high porosity, non-toxicity, ion-exchange capacity, and a large specific surface area (Maj and Matus, 2023). Their strong water absorption capacity is considered a key property supporting their application in poultry production (Gilani et al., 2016).

Several studies have shown beneficial effects of aluminosilicates on litter quality and broiler performance. Schneider et al. (2016) reported that 100 g of zeolite per 1 kg of sawdust litter reduced moisture content and ammonia volatilization during rearing of Cobb broilers. Eleroğlu and Yalçın (2005) found that 25 % zeolite in wood shavings reduced litter moisture (36.2 % vs. 25.2 % in control and zeolite groups, respectively) and improved feed conversion ratio (FCR). Banaszak et al. (2022a) demonstrated that supplementing feed with 0.5 % halloysite and adding 0.5 kg/m² to peat litter reduced the incidence of footpad dermatitis (FPD) and improved protein and intramuscular fat content in breast and leg muscles. Other studies also confirmed that halloysite supports meat quality and bird growth in broiler production (Banaszak et al., 2021; Banaszak et al., 2022b).

Poultry litter is a mixture of feces, feathers, feed, and bedding material. Its quality changes as birds grow. Moisture content is particularly important, as excess water promotes the proliferation of microorganisms, including those responsible for harmful ammonia production (Torok et al., 2009; Crippen et al., 2021). High litter moisture is strongly associated with FPD, a common welfare issue in broilers. This condition ranges from superficial lesions to deep ulcerations on the footpads and toes, facilitating pathogen penetration and negatively affecting health and performance (Shepherd et al., 2017). Birds affected by FPD often show impaired mobility, reduced feed intake, and lower body weight gain. Monitoring FPD is therefore an important element of flock management in Europe and the United States, both for welfare and economic reasons (Shepherd and Fairchild, 2010).

The use of natural minerals has been shown to improve litter quality by reducing moisture (Nikolakakis et al., 2013; Schneider et al., 2016). Other studies have examined litter pH, ammonia concentration, Kjeldahl nitrogen, ammoniacal nitrogen, and total solids (Karamanlis et al., 2008; Nikolakakis et al., 2013; Schneider et al., 2016). Moreover, the addition of aluminosilicates to litter may indirectly influence meat quality by stabilizing the rearing environment, reducing stress, and supporting more efficient nutrient utilization. Improved conditions have been linked to favorable changes in carcass characteristics, changes in chemical compostion of meat, and altered physicochemical traits, including water-holding capacity and intramuscular fat content (Banaszak et al., 2021; Banaszak et al., 2022a).

However, few studies have examined the elemental composition of litter following the application of zeolite and halloysite. This gap highlights the relevance of investigating how aluminosilicate supplementation affects both litter chemistry and bird welfare, while also considering implications for meat quality.

The study aimed to evaluate the effect of zeolite and halloysite in wheat litter on production results, carcass characteristics, meat quality, the litter's chemical composition, and the occurrence of footpad dermatitis during the rearing of Ross 308 broiler chickens.

Material and methods

Ethics

The study received approval from the Committee for the Care of Animals at Bydgoszcz University of Science and Technology, Poland (approval no. 1/24) and complied with applicable regulations. All procedures conformed to the ARRIVE guidelines (Percie du Sert et al., 2020).

Chickens rearing

The experiment was performed in the summer period (June – July), and 240 one-day-old Ross 308 broiler chicks were purchased from a commercial hatchery in the Kuyavian-Pomeranian Voivodeship (Poland). The birds were divided into 4 equal groups, 60 birds each in 6 replications (10 birds per pen). Rearing lasted 42 days. In the control group (CON), the birds were kept on chopped wheat litter. In the experimental groups, dry sanitization with aluminosilicates was applied, respectively: once (DSA1) on the 1st day of rearing, twice (DSA2, at the beginning of rearing and on day 21), and six times (DSA6, every week) during rearing. Each time, the pens were filled with a zeolite and halloysite at a ratio of 80:20. The total weight of the mixture was 125 g per 1 m2 of the pen surface.

Pens with an area corresponding to a stocking density of 33 kg/m2 were used. The litter was regularly filled (4 times) during rearing. The temperature on day 1 was 33°C, and an additional heat source in the form of a heating lamp was installed in each pen. Then, the temperature was gradually reduced to 24°C in week 4 of rearing. The light intensity was 20 lux, and the duration of lightness was 8 hours on average. The concentration of harmful gases did not exceed the permissible values, i.e., 3000 ppm - carbon dioxide (CO2), 20 ppm - ammonia (NH3), and 5 ppm - hydrogen sulfide (H2S). Movement and relative air humidity were maintained at 0.5 m/s and 65 %, respectively. Two nipple drinkers were placed in each pen. Rearing was divided into three feeding periods using a commercial diet: from day 1 to day 14, starter feed was given; from day 15 to 35 – grower feed; and from day 36 to 42 – finisher feed. The feed was in the form of crumbles (starter) and granules (grower and finisher) (Golpasz, De Heus Animal Nutrition; De Heus Ltd., Golub Dorzyń, Poland). Feed and water were given ad libitum.

Aluminosilicates

The aluminosilicates used in the experiment were in powder form. Zeolite came from a Slovak zeolite mine located east of the country (Južná trieda, Slovakia). It was characterized by the following properties: specific surface area: 30 – 60 m2/g, bulk density: 1.60 – 1.80 kg/m3, weight 2.20 – 2.44 kg/m3. The chemical composition of zeolite includes: SiO2 (71.30 %), Al2O3 (13.10 %), CaO (5.20 %), K2O (3.40), Fe2O3 (1.90 %), MgO (1.20 %), Na2O (1.30 %), TiO2 (0.30), Si/Al (5.40 %), clinoptilolite (84.00 %), cristobalite (8.00 %), mica clay (4.00 %), plagioclases (3.50 %), rutile (0.20 %). Halloysite came from a mine in southwestern Poland (Krotoszyce, Poland). According to the manufacturer’s declaration, it was characterized by the following properties: specific surface area: 65 – 85 m2/g, bulk density: 0.70 – 0.85 g/cm3 and composition: Al (13.00 %), Si (12.00 %), Ca (0.40 %), Mg (0.30 %), Na (0.10 %), K (0.08 %), P (0.30 %), Fe (9.00 %), Ti (1.00 %), Mn (0.20 %).

Growth performance

During rearing, the number of dead birds and feed intake (FI) were recorded daily. Birds were weighed on days 1, 14, 35, and 42 of rearing (BW). Analytical composition of a commercial diet, aluminosilicates, and chopped wheat straw

The following were collected for chemical analyses: 3 samples of starter, grower, and finisher diets (9 in total), 3 samples of zeolite and halloysite (6 in total), 3 samples of fresh and rearing litter from each group (24 in total) weighing approximately 500 grams. The following analyses were performed using methods approved by the Polish Committee for Standardization (www.pkn.pl): dry matter (PN-ISO 6496:2002) using POL-EKO dryer (Wodzisław Śląski, Poland), crude ash (Commission Regulation (EC) No. 152/2009 of 27/01/2009, Annex III M), total protein (Kjeldahl method PN-EN ISO 5983-1:2006) using Kjeltec 8400 Analyzer Unit and Kjeltec Sampler 8420 (FOSS, Hilleroed, Denmark), crude fat (PN-EN ISO 6492:2005) using SOXTEC SYSTEM HIT 1043, Gemini BV, Apeldoorn, Netherlands), crude fiber (PN-ISO 6865:2002), gross energy (PN-EN ISO 9831:2005) - KL-21 PLUS isoparabolic calorimeter (PrecyzjaBit PPHU Sp. z o.o. Bydgoszcz, Poland). All analyses were performed in duplicate.

In the case of the content of elements (calcium, copper, iron, magnesium, manganese, potassium, sodium, zinc) in litter (fresh and after rearing), zeolite and halloysite, the analytical samples were burnt in a muffle furnace at a temperature of 550 ± 15°C. Then, they were dissolved in hydrochloric acid, transferred to a measuring wheel to 50 ml, and topped up with water to the line. The solution was diluted to the desired volume and sucked into the air-acetylene flame of an atomic absorption spectrometer (PN-EN ISO 6869:2002). Regarding phosphorus content, the analytical samples were burnt in a muffle furnace at 550 ± 15°C and dissolved in hydrochloric acid. They were transferred to a measuring wheel to 50 ml and topped up with water to the line. The solution was diluted to the desired volume and then evaluated with the addition of a stopper and a coloring agent. The analysis was performed using a UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at a specific wavelength (Commission (EC) No 152/2009). The analyses were performed in duplicate. Carcass composition and meat quality

After 42 days of rearing, 12 birds were selected for slaughter from each group (2 from each pen), with a body weight similar to the average body weight of the birds in each pen. The birds were stunned by a percussive blow to the head (EUR-Lex, 2009) and then slaughtered by severing the spinal cord between the first cervical vertebra and the occipital condyle. After bleeding, the carcasses were scalded in water at 65°C for 3 seconds. Then, they were plucked in a mechanical plucker while being doused with cold water for about 10 seconds and eviscerated. The gizzard, heart, and liver were collected. The carcasses and giblets were chilled at 4°C for 24 hours (Hendi, Poznań, Poland). They were then weighed to calculate the slaughter yield of carcass and yield with giblets (Radwag, Radom, Poland).

The pH of the breast muscle was measured using an electronic pH meter (Elmetron, Zabrze, Poland). An electrode was inserted into the right breast muscle, and the pH was measured. The carcass was dissected (Ziołecki and Doruchowski, 1989). The following elements were separated: neck, neck skin, breast muscles, leg muscles, wings, skin with subcutaneous fat, abdominal fat, and carcass remains (trunk, leg bones).

A colorimeter measured the color of the breast muscles from the inner side (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). In order to obtain reliable results, a piece of meat without any connective tissue or fat was selected. Before analysis, the apparatus was calibrated on a white calibration plate. A 3-level color scale was used: L* - lightness, a* - redness, and b* - yellowness (CIE, 1986). Then, measurements of the color of the leg muscles were made in the same way.

For the analysis of drip loss, one breast muscle from each carcass was weighed and placed in a notched string bag (150×200 mm) and then in a larger string bag (200×300 mm). The samples were placed in a refrigerator (temperature 4°C) for 24 h (Hendi, Poznań, Poland) (Honikiel, 1987). After this time, the muscles were re-weighed, and the drip loss was calculated using the following formula:Driploss=100−(M2M1)x100where, M1 – initial pectoral muscle weight, M2 – final pectoral muscle weight after 24 h.

Breast muscles were ground in a meat grinder (Hendi, Poznań, Poland). Six samples from each group, weighing 0.295–0.305 g, were placed between two pieces of Whatman paper and subjected to a 2-kg load for 5 min, according to Grau and Hamm (1953). After compression, the samples were re-weighed to calculate water-holding capacity. The same procedure was performed for leg muscles, with six samples of 0.295–0.305 g taken from each group. The formula was used:WHC=100−(M2M1)x100where, M1 – initial pectoral muscle weight, M2 – final pectoral muscle weight after.

Footpad dermatitis occurrence

On the last rearing day, all birds used in the experiment were assessed for FPD. The soles of the feet were thoroughly cleaned. A three-level Swedish assessment scale was used: 0—no footpad lesions, 1—minor footpad lesions, and 2—severe footpad lesions. Based on the results obtained, the percentage occurrence of FPD was calculated for each group (Sorin et al., 2013).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using the Statistica program, ver. 13.3.0. ((TIBCO Statistica, 2017), Kraków, Poland). Mean values and pooled standard error of the mean (SEM) were calculated. The homogeneity of samples and group distribution were verified. One-way analysis of variance was performed using Tukey's test. The significance of differences between groups was assessed at P < 0.05. The following statistical model was used: Ya = µ + Da + ea, where Ya = the dependent variable; µ, the overall mean; Da, the effect of aluminosilicate (a = CON, DSA1, DSA2, DSA6); ea, residual error.

Results

Growth performance

Significantly higher GR was demonstrated in the DSA6 group compared to the DSA2 and DSA1 groups in the period of feeding the finisher feed (P=0.007). BWG was also significantly higher in the DSA6 group compared to DSA2 (P=0.029). Significantly, the lowest FI was characteristic of the birds in the DSA2 group in the second (P=0.001) and third (P<0.001) feeding periods, as well as throughout the rearing period (P<0.001). Discussion

Birds in the DSA6 group showed significantly higher GR compared with DSA1 and DSA2, and higher BWG compared with DSA2 in the last week of rearing. However, this did not affect total GR or BWG. Previous studies have confirmed the positive effects of using minerals in feed and litter on BWG (Basha et al., 2016; Nadziakiewicz et al., 2021; Farghly et al., 2024). The beneficial effects of aluminosilicates on BWG may result from their high capacity to bind toxins from feed (Ghazalah et al., 2021), their positive influence on gastrointestinal function (Banaszak et al., 2020), or immunomodulatory activity in birds (Dunislawska et al., 2022). In our study, applying a zeolite–halloysite mixture to litter once a week significantly reduced FI and FCR throughout rearing compared with the other groups.

Eleroğlu and Yalçın (2005) also reported improved FCR when aluminosilicates were added to litter at 25, 50, and 75 % of the volume of wood shavings, compared with the control group. A significant increase in FI of Ross 308 chickens in the last week of rearing was shown by Banaszak et al. (2021), who applied zeolite and halloysite to litter (0.325/0.325 kg/m²) and to feed (0.5–2.0 %; 75:25 ratio). Similar results were found when a higher proportion of zeolite and lower proportion of halloysite (0.200/0.600 kg/m²) were applied (Banaszak et al., 2022b).

The physical properties of minerals depend strongly on their structure, with zeolite having a three-dimensional framework and halloysite a two-dimensional layered structure. Their chemical composition also varies with origin, influencing activity and environmental effects (Bish, 2006; Murray, 2006; Brigatti et al., 2013; Belghazdis and Hachem, 2022). In our study, zeolite contained more than 50 times more calcium and twice as much magnesium, whereas halloysite contained 7 times more sodium, 10 times more phosphorus, and 29 times more manganese (Table 2).

During rearing, birds show natural behavior by scratching and pecking in the litter. It is known that they can also eat small amounts of litter, but this depends on its structure (Petek et al., 2014). Due to the high frequency of using aluminosilicates in the litter in the DSA6 group, birds were likely to eat minerals, which could have impacted significantly lower FI and a lower FCR. Moreover, aluminosilicates in the digestive tract of chickens promote slowing down the flow of intestinal contents, which results from the sorption capacity for water. This was confirmed by the study of Damiri et al. (2012), where chickens were fed with different levels of sodium bentonite feed (0.75, 1.50, 2.25, 3.00, and 3.75 %).

Significantly higher WHC of leg muscles was found in the DSA2 group compared to the CON and DSA6 groups. As reported by Biesek et al. (2021), significantly lower WHC of breast muscles was shown in the group of chickens kept on litter with the addition of halloysite (0.650 kg/m2) and fed a mixture of halloysite with zeolite (0.5-2 %; 1:1 ratio), compared to the control group and with zeolite. Similar results were presented by Banaszak et al. (2020), where leg muscles from Ross 308 chickens, kept in the group that was treated with zeolite in feed and litter, were characterized by significantly higher WHC (39.2 %) compared to the control group (34.6 %) and with halloysite (33.1 %). Changes in WHC in meat are also determined by post-mortem factors, including the rate of glycolysis, which leads to a decrease in meat pH (Warner, 2023), but no changes in pH were observed in our study. Additionally, changes in WHC may result from the oxidation of lipids and muscle proteins, contributing to higher drip loss from meat (Safaei et al., 2016).

Poultry litter is a multi-component mixture, which determines its chemical composition (Omeira et al., 2006). The frequency of using dry sanitization of litter with aluminosilicates also affected the content of individual components. In all experimental groups, the moisture content of the litter expressed as the dry matter content in 1 kg of bedding was significantly reduced. A significantly higher protein content was found in the CON and DSA2 groups compared to DSA6, and ash content in CON, DSA2, and DSA6 compared to DSA1. Higher frequencies of using aluminosilicates (2 or 6 times) increased the content of calcium, phosphorus, and iron. Similar to our research, Park et al. (2023) found a positive effect of using zeolite and illite for rice husk litter on its moisture content during the rearing of Ross 308 broiler chickens.

Pappas et al. (2010) found no changes in litter quality after palygorskite use to broiler chicken feed at 1 or 10 %. Other results were presented by Dim et al. (2021), where 30 g bentonite per 1 kg of feed reduced litter moisture on days 14 and 28 of rearing and litter nitrogen content on days 35 and 42 of rearing (P<0.05). Lemos et al. (2015) also showed that with increasing kaolin content in the feed (0.75 and 1.5 %), litter moisture decreased from 33.98 % in the control group to 24.79 % in the 1.5 % kaolin group. The calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium content in the litter after 42 days of rearing is similar to the values ​​reported by Bolan et al. (2010). After rearing broiler chickens, it is possible to obtain litter fertilizer with good chemical composition and high suitability for use in agriculture (Chastain et al., 2012). Based on our research, it can be suggested that aluminosilicates may be a potential additive enriching poultry litter as a fertilizer for further use.

Weekly dry litter sanitation significantly reduced the incidence of FPD compared with the other groups. Sujiwo and Ariyadi (2023) also confirmed the practical effect of natural litter additives, including zeolite (10 %), on reducing FPD. These findings are consistent with Hussein and Areaaer (2024), who reported improved welfare through reductions in FPD, hock inflammation, and improved feather cleanliness. Positive effects of halloysite on FPD were also demonstrated (Banaszak et al., 2022a; Biesek et al., 2022). Park et al. (2023) showed that 1 % illite applied to rice husks reduced FPD more effectively than 1 % zeolite or a 0.5 % zeolite + 0.5 % illite mixture. Marchioro et al. (2022) reported that a litter additive consisting of 98 % kaolin and 2 % cinnamon essential oil (200 g/m², applied weekly) reduced footpad damage and the presence of Salmonella spp.

The lesions on the soles of the feet may be associated with infections with Gram-positive cocci (Thøfner et al., 2019) or E. Coli bacteria (Freeman et al., 2020). Frequent use of aluminosilicates promoted more intensive litter drying and reduced moisture content. High litter moisture content is one of the main factors contributing to the occurrence of FPD in birds, which may translate into other production parameters (Shepherd and Fairchild, 2010; Taira et al., 2014). According to De Jong et al. (2014), the high frequency of FPD in Ross 308 broiler chickens may promote lower BWG, increased FCR, hock burns, and lesions of the pectoral muscle area. However, as the authors (De Jong et al., 2014) reported, in the experimental group (lesion induction), all birds had an FPD score of 2 on day 36 of rearing. According to our results, no statistically significant differences between groups were found in the occurrence of FPD at level 2. However, the control group had the highest quantity of cases (12.78 %). Despite this, over 74 % of birds in the CON group had FPD at level 1, which did not negatively affect the presented production results. The low occurrence of FPD at level 2 in all groups may also indicate the good quality of the litter and its high absorbent properties, which were used in the experiment.

In summary, weekly dry sanitation (DSA6) with a zeolite–halloysite mixture (80:20; 125 g/m² of litter) throughout rearing improved FI and FCR in broiler chickens. The chemical composition of the minerals also enriched the litter, increasing its potential as a phosphorus-, magnesium-, and iron-rich fertilizer. In the DSA6 group, FPD occurrence was markedly reduced. This strategy can be considered a practical approach to flock management, improving both production performance and bird welfare.

It could be considered from the third week of rearing to reduce the costs of dry aluminosilicate sanitization when the quality of the litter decreases dynamically with the birds' growth. However, this variant was not analyzed in the presented research, and it is only a hypothetical suggestion encouraging further steps and implementation of recommendations.
