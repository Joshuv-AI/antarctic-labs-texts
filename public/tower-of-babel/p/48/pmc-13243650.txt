Steric hindrance-mediated extracellular vesicle size fractionation for rapid prehospital diagnosis of intracerebral hemorrhage
Authors: Xingjie Wu, Shasha Xiong, Litao Zhang, Yueyue Zhao, Zhilu Sun, Likun Wang, Weifeng Long, Qianqian Guo, Yu-E Wang, Ying Chen, Ling Tao, Wei Li, Xiangchun Shen, Guofeng Wu, Haitao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnosing intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) in prehospital settings remains challenging due to unavailability of immediate neuroimaging, clinical overlap with ischemic stroke, and absence of validated circulating biomarkers for time-critical settings. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), subcellular structures capable of transporting biomolecular payloads (e.g., proteins, nucleic acids) across the blood-brain barrier, have emerged as compelling diagnostic candidates for ICH. Nevertheless, their clinical translation has been impeded by inherent biophysical heterogeneity, particularly polydisperse size distributions. To address this limitation, we engineer a steric hindrance-mediated EV analysis and size fractionation (SHEAF) platform, integrating steric hindrance-based size fractionation with membrane protein profiling to stratify EVs into three size subtypes within a 45-min workflow. Systematic evaluation using the SHEAF platform reveals that the 90 − 180 nm EV subtype exhibits superior discriminative capacity in differentiating ICH from ischemic stroke plasma specimens. This technology not only advances rapid prehospital ICH diagnostics but also establishes a method for elucidating size-dependent EV functionalities across neurological pathologies.Subject terms: Diagnostic markers, Biosensors, Nanobiotechnology

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and ischemic stroke (IS), the two primary stroke subtypes, differ fundamentally in pathophysiology and clinical management. ICH involves acute bleeding into the brain parenchyma, often leading to neurological deterioration due to mass effect and elevated intracranial pressure; management focuses on hemorrhage control, intensive neurocritical care, and potential surgical evacuation1,2. In contrast, IS results from cerebral artery occlusion, causing rapid neuronal death from ischemia, with treatment centered on timely reperfusion via intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy3,4. Critically, these therapeutic approaches are mutually exclusive: thrombolytic agents, essential in IS, are strictly contraindicated in ICH due to the risk of exacerbating hemorrhage. Moreover, each minute of untreated ischemia leads to the loss of approximately 1.9 million neurons5, while delays in ICH intervention worsen mass effect and outcomes6. Therefore, rapid and accurate differentiation between ICH and IS from symptom onset is crucial. Although neuroimaging modalities, such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) serve as the diagnostic gold standard7–9, their limited availability to hospital settings hinders prehospital diagnosis and delays subtype identification. This diagnostic gap underscores the urgent need for biomarker-based diagnostic strategies. However, many candidate biomarkers, such as glial fibrillary acidic protein10,11, may lack sufficient specificity and accuracy alone, highlighting the necessity of identifying more reliable molecular markers or panels to improve diagnostic precision.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are bi-layered nanoparticles that encapsulate diverse molecular cargo—including nucleic acids, proteins, and saccharides. The surrounding lipid bilayer shields this cargo from degradation, which better preserves biomarker integrity compared to free-floating molecules12. This protective feature makes EVs highly sensitive and reliable circulating biomarkers for various diseases, particularly in early stages13–16. Furthermore, EVs can cross the blood-brain barrier via transcytosis, enabling non-invasive detection of brain-derived pathological signals in peripheral blood17,18. This capability is especially relevant in conditions like ICH, where elevated levels of EV biomarkers have been consistently observed19,20. However, a significant challenge in EV-based diagnostics is their high heterogeneity in native biofluids, evident in size, origin, and cargo composition. While this heterogeneity complicates analysis, it also unveils a layer of biological specificity: different EV subpopulations are now understood to carry distinct biomarker profiles and functions. For instance, emerging evidence confirms that specific size-defined EV subtypes are selectively enriched with disease-specific molecular signatures, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy for cancer, neurodegenerative, and cardiovascular disease21–25. Nevertheless, existing technologies for EV size subtyping remain constrained by high costs, low throughput, and risks of damaging EV integrity during isolation26–29. Addressing these limitations is essential to unlocking the full clinical diagnostic potential of EVs.

Steric hindrance at nano-interfaces reduces the activity of ligands immobilized on nanoparticle surfaces. This effect intensifies as nanoparticle size increases. For example, reduced binding efficiencies were observed for duramycin, cinnamycin, α-synuclein, and β-amyloid when interacting with phospholipids on larger EV membranes30–32. Moreover, ligand activity on nanoparticle surfaces can be modulated by tuning the grafting density and the length of inert protective chains33. Building upon these insights, we developed a steric hindrance-mediated EV analysis and size fractionation (SHEAF) platform, which integrated size-selective separation with EV membrane protein profiling into a microfluidic system. This platform revealed that the distinct protein signatures in 90–180 nm EVs can effectively differentiate ICH and IS plasma samples. Our work established a rapid, accurate, and user-friendly system for EV size fractionation and molecular characterization, underscoring the diagnostic significance of specific EV size subtypes in ICH assessment.

Results

Design of SHEAF platform

To accomplish size-selective EV isolation through precisely controlled surface interactions, aptamers specifically targeting the EV transmembrane protein CD63 (APTCD63) were chemically co-immobilized with polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymer chains on magnetic nanoparticle (MNP) surface. Within this dual-functional interface, the APTCD63 ligands served as molecular recognition ligands for CD63-mediated EV binding, while the PEG created an adjustable steric barrier through their flexible polymer chains to spatially regulate ligand accessibility (Fig. 1a). Through iterative experimental optimization of two critical surface parameters—the APTCD63 grafting ratio and the PEG molecular weight (ranging from 2k to 30 kDa)—three distinct MNP formulations were developed for capturing EVs with diameters below 90 nm diameter, below 180 nm, and exceeding 180 nm.

A three-stage fractionation protocol was subsequently established using these engineered MNPs in serial processing steps: first applying <90 nm-capture MNPs to remove the smallest EVs from biofluids, followed by <180 nm-capture MNPs to isolate medium-sized EVs, and concluding with >180 nm-capture MNPs to harvest large EVs, thereby achieving precise EV size fractionation (Fig. 1b). Each isolated EV subpopulation then underwent quantitative membrane protein analysis using a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-based enzymatic signal amplification system, where HRP generated chemiluminescent (CL) signals proportional to specific biomarker on EV surfaces (Fig. 1c). The complete workflow was subsequently miniaturized into an integrated microfluidic system combining three essential components: (1) programmable peristaltic pumps for automated fluid handling, (2) microchannels implementing the sequential MNP processing steps, and (3) smartphone-based optical detectors for biomarker quantification, collectively enabling point-of-care clinical plasma analysis with a total processing time under 45 min (Fig. 1d). When applied to ICH diagnostics, this platform effectively discriminated size-subtype-specific EV protein signatures between ICH and IS patients through machine learning-assisted pattern recognition, demonstrating superior diagnostic performance compared to conventional approaches (Fig. 1e).

Steric hindrance-mediated size selective capture

Quantitative assessment of APTCD63 binding efficiency was performed through analysis of colorimetric ELISA signal variations under different experimental conditions (Fig. 2a). To minimize interference from MNP size heterogeneity, monodisperse MNPs with a controlled diameter of 37.8 ± 1.7 nm were employed throughout the study (Supplementary Fig. 1). APTCD63 and PEG were anchored at MNP surface through sequential aza-Michael addition (Supplementary Fig. 2) to give MNP decorated solely with APTCD63 (MNP-APT), solely with PEG (MNP-PEG), or with both APTCD63 and PEG (MNP-APT-PEG). The APTCD63 grafting ratio and PEG molecular weight of MNP-APT-PEG were designated as MNP-APT20%-PEG20K, where 20% denoted the APTCD63 grafting ratio and 20K denoted the PEG molecular weight (Mw = 20 kDa). Mesoporous silica nanoparticle (MSN), characterized by tunable nanoarchitectures, narrow size distributions, and low buoyant density34,35, served as synthetic EV analogs. Considering the established biological significance of 30—180 nm EV subpopulations in disease biomarker transport36,37, six distinct MSN size variants (37.5 nm, 61.1 nm, 93.1 nm, 124.1 nm, 152.7 nm, 182.9 nm) were functionalized with complementary sequence of APTCD63 (cAPT) and HRP (MSN-cAPT-HRP, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Fig. 4). The hybridization efficiency between surface-immobilized APTCD63 and cAPT served as the primary metric for binding efficiency quantification, as both DNA duplex formation and aptamer-protein recognition require complete spatial accessibility of aptamer sequences. PEG’s inert chain effect was validated through statistically indistinguishable ELISA signals observed in MSN-cAPT-HRP solutions treated with PBS, MNP-PEG8K, MNP-PEG14K, and MNP-PEG20K (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Kinetic analysis demonstrated complete MSN capture within 30 min, as shown by time-dependent attenuation of ELISA signal difference before and after incubation with MNP-APT20%-PEG20K and MNP-APT40%-PEG20K (Supplementary Fig. 5b). High-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS) analyses of C 1 s, N 1 s, and P 2p enabled precise calculation of APTCD63 grafting ratio (Supplementary Fig. 6). APTCD63 binding efficiency exhibited progressive reduction with decreasing aptamer grafting ratios, demonstrating pronounced attenuation at 40 and 20% grafting ratios (Fig. 2c). Under fixed 20% APTCD63 grafting ratio, MNPs functionalized with PEG having a molecular weight of 8, 10, and 12 kDa displayed marginal binding efficiency reductions compared to MNP-APT (PEG0K). Given APTCD63’s molecular weight of 8,259.4 g/mol, this observation indicated subcritical steric hindrance when PEG chain length approximated or slightly exceeded the aptamer. A marked efficiency decline occurred in PEG14K, with progressive attenuation continuing until PEG chains molecular weight surpassed 20 kDa, beyond which binding efficiency plateaued at baseline levels (Fig. 2d). These data established that PEG polymers exceeding 14 kDa could generate sufficient steric barrier to effectively block APTCD63-cAPT hybridization.

To establish MNP-APT-PEG systems with defined upper diameter thresholds for MSN-cAPT-HRP capture, systematic binding efficiency analyses were conducted using low APTCD63 grafting ratio (20% and 40%) and long PEG chain (Mw = 14–20 kDa) co-functionalized MNPs. Progressive reductions in binding efficiency were observed against MSN-cAPT-HRP with increasing diameters (Fig. 2e, f), confirming enhanced steric hindrance from cAPT at larger MSN surface. At 40% grafting ratio, APTCD63 retained measurable binding efficiencies (≥ 4%) across all MSN-cAPT-HRP diameters and PEG molecular weights (Fig. 2e). This residual activity stemmed from APTCD63-saturated microdomains on MNP surface that preserved spatially confined hybridization zones. In contrast, MNPs with 20% APTCD63 grafting ratio exhibited <0.5% binding efficiency against 182.9 nm MSN-cAPT-HRP across all PEG variants, while MNP-APT20%-PEG20K showed equivalent suppression against MSNs exceeding 93.1 nm (Fig. 2f). Notably, MNP-APT20%-PEG20K retained strong binding against sub-93.1 nm MSNs, and MNP-APT20%-PEG14K demonstrated comparable performance for sub-182.9 nm MSNs, establishing capturable upper diameter limits of 93.1 nm and 182.9 nm, respectively. Consequently, MNP-APT20%-PEG20K and MNP-APT20%-PEG14K were employed to sequentially isolate MSNs with diameters <93.1 nm and those between 93.1–182.9 nm. To validate this separation capability, differentially sized MSN-cAPT-HRP particles (61.1, 124.1, and 182.9 nm) were fluorescently tagged with 7-Amino-4-methyl-3-coumarinylacetic acid N-succinimidyl ester (AMCA-NHS), fluorescein 5-isothiocyanate (FITC), and rhodamine B isothiocyanate (RITC), respectively. The labeled particles were mixed and subjected to sequential treatment with MNP-APT20%-PEG20K, followed by MNP-APT20%-PEG14K, and finally MNP-APT. This procedure resulted in the progressive disappearance of AMCA, FITC, and RITC fluorescence peaks from the residual mixture, accompanied by the emergence of corresponding fluorescence peaks with matching intensities in the isolated MSN fractions (Fig. 2g). The ordered elimination of AMCA, FITC, and RITC signals conclusively demonstrated the sequential isolation of 61.1 nm, 124.1 nm, and 182.9 nm MSNs by MNP-APT20%-PEG20K, MNP-APT20%-PEG14K, and MNP-APT, respectively.

Steric hindrance-mediated EV size fractionation

For EV size fractionation, MNP-APT20%-PEG20K, MNP-APT20%-PEG14K, and MNP-APT were sequentially applied to process EV containing solution. The concentration and purity of ultracentrifugation-isolated EVs were verified through nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), transmission electron microscope (TEM), and western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 7). Prior to dynamic light scattering (DLS) and TEM characterization, EVs isolated via magnetic separation were chemically detached from MNPs using 10 mM dithiothreitol38. DLS measurements revealed mean hydrodynamic diameters of 54.4 ± 11.9 nm for EV subtype 1 and 127.9 ± 17.1 nm for EV subtype 2 (Fig. 3a), with corresponding size distributions spanning 23.8–86.5 nm (subtype 1) and 87.9–186.4 nm (subtype 2). These results confirmed that MNP-APT20%-PEG20K and MNP-APT20%-PEG14K maintained consistent size-selective performance for both synthetic MSNs and biological EVs. Residual nanostructures in EV subtype 3 (isolated by MNP-APT) exhibited a mean diameter of 220.2 ± 26.3 nm with a size distribution of 180.0–280.5 nm, demonstrating effective removal of sub-182.9 nm nanostructures during preceding fractionation steps. TEM analysis of the original EV solution revealed characteristic nano-vesicular structures exhibiting high-contrast peripheral membranes and electron-lucent compartments, with a mean diameter of 114.8 ± 38.0 nm (Fig. 3b, Input). Analogous vesicular morphologies were preserved across all fractionated EV subtypes: EV subtype 1 (56.8 ± 15.4 nm), EV subtype 2 (112.6 ± 6.4 nm), and EV subtype 3 (200.4 ± 20.2 nm), as investigated through systematic TEM measurements. This size-stratified conservation of structural features conclusively validated successful EV subtypes isolation via the steric hindrance-mediated methodology. Western blotting further confirmed subtype purity, with bands for EV biomarkers: CD63, heat shock proteins 70 (HSP70), ALG-2-interacting protein X (ALIX) detected in all size subtypes, while absent signals for apolipoprotein B (APOB) excluded plasma protein contamination across all subtypes (Fig. 3c). Note that the successful fractionation of the three EV subtypes was also validated by analyzing the TEM images of MNP-EV hybrid obtained through magnetic separation (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Three distinct EV size subtypes with diameters <90 nm, 90–180 nm, and >180 nm were successfully isolated from HUVEC, HMC3, SK-N-BE(2), and THP-1 cell culture mediums using steric hindrance-mediated fractionation (Supplementary Fig. 9), validating the method’s efficacy across diverse cellular origins. A strong linear correlation (R2 = 0.9079) was observed between EV protein concentrations from steric hindrance-isolated subtypes and NTA-quantified particle concentrations (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 10), confirming high size-fractionation accuracy and specificity. Notably, EV subtype 2 (90–180 nm) showed higher abundance compared to the other two subtypes across all four cell lines, consistent with published data and attributable to inherent EV biogenesis characteristics. The 90–180 nm range encompasses fractions of two major EV subpopulations: large EVs (100–1000 nm) generated by plasma membrane budding, and exosomes (<200 nm) derived from the endosomal pathway39,40. Total EV recovery rates exceeded 95% at EV concentrations ≤1.0×1012 particle/mL, with <5% inter-subtype variability across solutions of varying EV concentrations (Fig. 3e). However, at 5 × 1012 particle/mL, a significant recovery rate elevation occurred specifically in subtype 3, revealing incomplete isolation of sub-180 nm EVs by MNP-APT20%-PEG20K and MNP-APT20%-PEG14K. This delineated the method’s operational upper limit at 1 × 1012 particle/mL, consistent with clinically relevant EV concentrations (sub-1×1012 particle/mL) observed in plasma from stroke or cancer patients41,42.

The SHEAF platform offers significant advantages over established EV fractionation methods, such as size exclusion chromatography (SEC), density gradient ultracentrifugation (DGUC) and asymmetric-flow field-flow fractionation (AF4). Using a self-made SEC column, EVs in native biofluids were separated into three size fractions (<50 nm, 110 nm, and 210 nm) within 1 h (Fig. 3f upper)27,43–45. However, the SEC method had limited EV recovery (65.53%) and substantial protein contamination in the <50 nm fraction (Fig. 3g, h). Per established protocols, DGUC requires 12-hour ultracentrifugation to accelerate particle movement through density gradient flotation, plus pre- and post-fractionation ultracentrifugation to remove protein contaminants46–48. Our results confirmed DGUC exhibited poor size resolution due to overlapping EV densities (Fig. 3f lower)46 and APOB contamination issues in F7 (Fig. 3g right). Additionally, DGUC demonstrated a low EV recovery rate of only 71.33% (Fig. 3h right), attributed in part to EV membrane rupture during the high-speed ultracentrifugation48,49. AF4, a recently developed microfluidic EV fractionation method, was reported to provide high size resolution but require pre-processing with ultracentrifugation (2 h) to remove protein aggregations, limiting its clinical utility25,50. Overall, SHEAF can separate EV in complex biofluids into three high-purity EV size subtypes in 1 h without pre-processing, achieving near-complete recovery (>95%) and minimal protein contamination (Supplementary Table 1).

Molecular profiling of EV size subtypes

The SHEAF platform was employed to analyze EV membrane proteins across three EV size subtypes secreted by HUVEC and HMC3. Cells were stimulated with tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) or oxidized low-density lipoprotein (OX-LDL) to establish inflammatory models prior to medium collection (Supplementary Fig. 11). Six inflammation-related proteins, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), C-reactive protein (CRP), TNF-α, and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), were selected for analysis based on their known associations with EV membrane and elevated circulatory levels in stroke patients51–56. EV membrane proteins were first labeled via double-stranded DNA comprising an aptamer sequence against target protein and an HRP-conjugated transducing sequence (APTtarget-TRtarget-HRP). Activating sequence (ACTtarget) subsequently displaced TRtarget-HRP from EV surfaces within 30 min, initiating the CL reaction through interaction with ELISA substrate. This HRP-catalyzed system demonstrated target-specific membrane protein recognition, evidenced by substantially amplified CL signals in EV solutions treated with APTVEGF-TRVEGF-HRP plus ACTVEGF (Fig. 4a). By contrast, negligible signal enhancement occurred when APTVEGF-TRVEGF-HRP was replaced with its scrambled sequence counterpart or when EV solutions were treated solely with APTVEGF-TRVEGF-HPR or ACTVEGF (PBS). The steric hindrance-mediated EV size fractionation technique and membrane protein analysis workflow were subsequently incorporated into a microfluidic platform, which achieved a VEGF detection limit of 0.380 × 107 particle/mL during titration experiments on VEGF associated to HMC3 EV membrane (Fig. 4b).

Expression levels of the six proteins in the whole EV population were quantified using both the SHEAF platform and sandwich ELISA for comparative analysis. SHEAF-derived measurements showed strong linear correlation with ELISA results (R2 = 0.9682, Fig. 4c), confirming method reliability. Subsequent analysis revealed distinct protein expression patterns across all three EV subtypes in various inflammation models. Specifically, elevated signals were observed in EV subtype 1 and EV subtype 2 compared to whole EV population and EV subtype 3 (Fig. 4d), demonstrating inflammation-related protein enrichment in these subtypes. Pairwise comparisons of EV membrane protein compositions were further used to evaluate the correlations between the three EV size subtypes and whole EV population. Positive correlations were detected between nearly all EV size subtypes and the whole EV population, given their shared common cellular origins (Fig. 4e). However, weak correlations or negative correlations (r ≤ 0.1) were identified between EV subtype 1 and EV subtype 2 derived from HMC3 cells, along with EV subtype 2 and EV subtype 3 isolated from TNF-α stimulated HUVEC cultures. Furthermore, moderate correlations (0.1 <r ≤ 0.5) were observed between EV subtype 3 and two other EV subtypes in OX-LDL-stimulated HUVECs, and with EV subtype 2 in TNF-α-stimulated HMC3 cells. These weak inter-subtype correlations revealed substantial biomolecular and biophysical heterogeneity intrinsic to EVs, thereby emphasizing the indispensable requirement for sophisticated EV size subtype methodologies in elucidating EV-associated biological mechanisms. Proteomic analysis further delineated distinct protein expression profiles among the three EV subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Analysis of the proteomic dataset for subcellular localization indicated that the proteins significantly enriched in EV Subtype 2 were derived from diverse intracellular compartments (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d), suggesting this subtype is likely produced mainly through the endosomal pathway. This origin underpinned its enhanced potential as a source of diagnostic biomarkers. Notably, proteins detected by western blotting were also identified within the proteomic dataset.

Clinical sample analysis

To assess the diagnostic efficacy of the SHEAF platform for stroke classification, plasma samples were collected from 25 ICH patients and 25 IS patients. In addition, plasma samples from 20 healthy donors were collected through age- and sex-matched cohort enrollment as control group (Supplementary Table 2). Employing the SHEAF platform, EVs in patient plasma samples were categorized into three size subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 13), followed by membrane protein profiling for both fractionated EVs and whole EV population. Substantial disparities in expression levels were detected among the six target proteins when comparing the three EV size subtypes with whole EV population (Fig. 5a). This finding was consistent with the cellular analysis, thereby reinforcing the functional specialization of EV subtypes in inflammatory pathway regulation.

Two-dimensional projections of EV protein compositions across three size subtypes and the whole EV population were generated through principal component analysis (PCA). Significant spatial overlap was identified between ICH, IS, and healthy cohorts in the protein profiles of EV subtype 1, subtype 3, and the unfractionated EV pool (Fig. 5b). Conversely, EV subtype 2 demonstrated minimal inter-group overlap in protein composition. This differential clustering pattern suggested superior diagnostic capability of EV subtype 2 protein signatures for distinguishing stroke from healthy control and ICH from IS. Consistent with PCA findings, EV subtypes 1, subtype 3, and whole EVs demonstrated limited ability to differentiate between stroke and healthy plasma samples, and between ICH and IS samples (Supplementary Fig. 14). The mixed area under the curve (AUC) scores for these populations were 0.807 (subtype 1), 0.695 (subtype 3), and 0.792 (whole EVs) in distinguishing stroke from healthy controls, with most individual protein AUC values below 0.70. For differentiating ICH from IS, the mixed AUCs were 0.728 (subtype 1), 0.680 (subtype 3), and 0.736 (whole EVs), again with most individual protein AUCs below 0.60. In contrast, proteins from EV subtype 2 showed markedly improved diagnostic performance in both comparisons (Fig. 5c, upper). Specifically, TGF-β and PD-L1 achieved AUC values above 0.80 for distinguishing stroke from healthy controls, while a combined six-protein panel in subtype 2 yielded an exceptional AUC of 0.951 in stroke diagnosis. Similarly, in differentiating ICH from IS, all individual proteins in subtype 2 had AUC values exceeding 0.60, with VEGF, CRP, and ICAM-1 surpassing 0.70 (Fig. 5c, lower). The combined model based on EV subtype 2 reached an AUC of 0.958 for classifying ICH versus IS. The cut-points for EV subtype 2 showed a sensitivity of 0.88 and a specificity of 0.92 in differentiating ICH from IS, as well as a sensitivity of 0.84 and a specificity of 0.95 in distinguishing stroke from healthy controls (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

The functional specialization of EV size subtypes across diverse physiological and pathological pathways has emerged as a critical research frontier in EV biology. However, traditional EV size fractionation tools face inherent methodological limitations. SEC suffers from low EV recovery caused by EV stacking and significant protein contamination (especially in <50 nm fraction) due to overlapping hydrodynamic diameters between small EVs and protein aggregates, alongside practical drawbacks like sample dilution that hinder analysis of low-abundance samples. DGUC requires prolonged processing (>12 h), exhibits poor size resolution from overlapping EV densities, and shows APOB contamination issues due to density similarities between protein aggregates and EVs. The EV membrane rupture during ultracentrifugation further results in low yields of DGUC. AF4 requires preprocessing via ultracentrifugation (>2 h) to remove protein aggregates, introducing risks of membrane rupture and diminishing clinical utility. To address these limitations, we developed the SHEAF platform—an EV size fractionation method based on steric hindrance effects. The key to this approach lies in the steric barrier created by PEG chains modified on the surface of MNPs, which, without affecting the structure or specificity of the conjugated aptamer, controls binding efficiency by spatially shielding the interaction between the aptamer and its target protein CD63, thereby regulating their contact probability. The choice of aptamer as the capture ligand is based on three key advantages: precise control of PEG-mediated steric hindrance, consistent batch-to-batch performance, and potential for point-of-care applications. The SHEAF platform separates unprocessed EVs into three distinct size subtypes within 45 min, achieving >95% recovery and minimal protein contamination with high purity. Crucially, SHEAF demonstrated transferability to other aptamer-protein pairs, showing applicability for selectively isolating EV size subtypes expressing pathology-specific membrane proteins. Moreover, the platform’s size-resolved EV fractionation enable precise quantification of associated biomolecules (such as miRNAs and glycoproteins) through tailored signal conversion procedures. Despite these advantages, potential limitations of the SHEAF platform include the aggregation of EVs during fractionation and the potential loss of biologically relevant EV subpopulations that express negligible CD63.

Timely clinical intervention remains critical for optimizing therapeutic outcomes for ICH patients. Current ICH diagnostic protocols remain deficient in validated circulating biomarkers, being primarily dependent on neuroimaging modalities including CT and MRI. EVs, as blood-brain barrier-permeable nanoscale carriers of pathophysiological biomolecules, have emerged as promising candidates for developing circulating biomarkers enabling early ICH detection. To establish robust EV-based biomarkers and engineer a prehospital diagnostic system, we developed a microfluidic chip combining steric hindrance-mediated EV size fractionation with amplified protein profiling. Following integration of an automated fluidic control system, the resulting SHEAF platform was designed as a complementary tool to the current gold-standard imaging techniques. The key advantages of the SHEAF platform lie in its significantly shorter time-to-result compared to the logistical process required for CT/MRI scanning, as well as its potential for use in ambulances or other resource-limited settings where immediate access to advanced imaging is not available (Supplementary Table 4). In accordance with current stroke management guidelines that stress intervention at the earliest possible time after symptom onset to limit irreversible brain damage, we recommend that blood sampling for the SHEAF platform be performed immediately upon patient presentation.

SHEAF analysis revealed EV subtype 2 (90 nm <diameter <180 nm) exhibit superior diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.958) in discriminating ICH from IS through differential expression of six inflammation-associated proteins. The pronounced biological relevance of the 90–180 nm EV fraction in stroke can be attributed to a distinct set of biophysical characteristics that align closely with pathophysiological mechanisms. Critically, small EVs (<200 nm) possess a well-documented capacity to cross the blood-brain barrier into the systemic circulation21,57,58, which positions the sub-180 nm population as physiologically capable of migrating from the brain to the blood during the early phases following a stroke. Furthermore, while EVs smaller than 90 nm often provide an insufficient membrane surface area for adequately displaying multiple cell-type-specific protein markers22,25, those larger than 200 nm are prone to incorporate heterogeneous apoptotic bodies. Consequently, the 90–180 nm EV subset represents a uniquely optimal compromise, offering substantial membrane surface for carrying disease-specific biomarker panels while maintaining a compact size conducive to efficient blood brain barrier transit and high circulatory abundance. This exceptional ability of EV subtype 2 to encapsulate disease-specific proteins was also supported by its elevated protein counts with diverse subcellular locations. However, these promising initial findings highlight the need for larger, multi-center validation studies in which variables, such as sampling time points and prehospital conditions are systematically controlled.

Methods

Materials

DNA oligos were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. and were stored at −20 oC before use. Aptamer sequences against CD63, VEGF, TGF-β, PD-L1, CRP, TNF-α, and ICAM-1 were established according to previous publications (Supplementary Table 5)59–64. Primary amine capped MNP (MNP-NH2) and OptiPrep™ density gradient medium (60%, w/v, iodixanol) were obtained from Merck Ltd. TNF-α, OX-LDL, SuperSignal™ ELISA Femto substrate, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Invitrogen, catalog no. A16066), and primary antibodies against the following proteins - CD63 (Invitrogen, clone Mo34, catalog no. MA5-47655), HSP70 (Invitrogen, clone 2A4, catalog no. MA3-008), ALIX (Invitrogen, clone 3A9, catalog no. MA1-83977), APOB (NeoBiotechnologies, clone APOB/4335, catalog no. 338-MSM5-P1) - were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Diacrylate PEG (DAPEG, Mw = 1000), methoxyPEG amine (mPEG-NH2, Mw = 8000, 10,000, 20,000 and 30,000), PEG bis(amine) (NH2-PEG-NH2, Mw = 2000, 4000, 6000 and 8000), PEG methyl ether acrylate (PEG-MA, Mw = 10,000 and 20,000), triethylamine, and N,N-methylenediacrylamide were purchased from Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Co., Ltd. AMCA-NHS, FITC, RITC, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES), hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), and polyetherimide (PEI, Mw = 600) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin BioChem Technology Co., Ltd, and were used as received. Sepharose® CL-2B agarose gel, ELISA for IL-1β and MCP-1, bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA), radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) containing protease inhibitors, HRP streptavidin, TMB single-component substrate solution, and ELISA stopping solution were purchased from Shanghai Acmec Biochemical Co., Ltd. EV-depleted fetal bovine serum were prepared according to standard protocol49.

Cell culture and EV collection

HUVEC (catalog no. GPC0114) cell line was purchased from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (Wuhan, China). HMC3 (catalog no. SCSP-540), SK-N-BE(2) (catalog no. TCHu200), and THP-1 (catalog no. SCSP-567) cell lines were purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) containing 10% EV-depleted fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). After being cultured for 48 h, the cell culture mediums were centrifuged at 1000 g for 20 min and treated by a 0.22 μm filter. The obtained supernatant was sequentially centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min and 100,000 g for 90 min to isolate EV. The EV pellet was washed by resuspending in PBS and centrifugation at 100,000 g for 90 min. For analyzing EV secreted by cells under inflammatory states, HUVEC and HMC3 cells were exposed to TNF-α or OX-LDL according to previous publications65–67. In a typical procedure, 1.0 × 106 HMC3 cells were incubated with 100 ng/mL TNF-α or 50 μg/mL OX-LDL for 24 h before collecting cell culture mediums. Cellular inflammatory models were validated by measuring inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and MCP-1) levels in cell culture mediums via ELISA per the manufacturer’s protocol. The harvested EVs were investigated by western blotting, NTA, and TEM to confirm their protein composition, concentration, and structure, respectively. To preserve EV integrity, disaggregation was not performed during the EV isolation step.

Surface modification of MNP-NH2

In a typical procedure, 0.5 mg MNP-NH2 and 10 mg DAPEG were incubated together in 1.0 mL PBS for 24 h at 37 °C. The obtained MNP-DAPEG was further incubated with APTCD63 (2.0 μM, primary amine at 5‘ end) and mPEG-NH2 (8.0 μM, Mw = 20,000) for 24 h to obtain MNP with 20% APTCD63 grafting ratio (MNP-APT20%-PEG20K). The APTCD63 grafting ratio of MNP was adjusted by varying the dosage of APTCD63 and mPEG-NH2. After each step, the MNP was dialyzed (MWCO = 50,000) against PBS for 24 h to remove residuals. The MNP surface modification process was illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2.

mPEG-NH2 with molecular weight of 8000, 10,000, 20,000, and 30,000 were commercially available and directly used in MNP surface modification. mPEG-NH2 with molecular weight of 12,000, 14,000, 16,000, 18,000 and 24,000 were synthesized through aza-Michael reaction. Typically, 1.0 mg PEG-MA (Mw = 10,000) and 10 mg NH2-PEG-NH2 (Mw = 2000) were added into 2.0 mL PBS and reacted at 37 oC for 24 h. Then, the mixture was treated by a dialysis bag (MWCO = 5000) against PBS for 48 h, followed by lyophilization to give mPEG-NH2 with a Mw of 12,000.

Preparation and surface modification of MSN

Primary amine capped MSN (MSN-NH2) were prepared according to previous publications34,35. Briefly, 0.4 g APTES and 1.6 g TEOS were mixed and slowly added into 140 mL CTAB solution (4.0 mM). Then, 0.5 mL NH3•H2O (30%) was added into the mixture under stirring, followed by reacting at 25 °C for 5 h. Subsequently, the obtained solid was collected by centrifugation and washed with ethanol. To remove residual CTAB, the obtained MSN-NH2 was treated by 100 mL HCl solution (3.7%) for 12 h at 60 °C. To control the size of MSN-NH2, the dosage of silane and NH3•H2O were adjusted.

For labeling MSN with HRP and cAPT (primary amine at 5‘ end), 1.0 mg MSN-NH2 and 10.0 mg N,N-methylenediacrylamide were incubated in 1.0 mL PBS buffer at 37 °C for 24 h. After being washed by centrifugation and re-dispersion, the solution was further incubated with 100 μL mixture solution containing HRP (1.0 mg/mL) and cAPT (100 μM, primary amine at 5‘ end) for 24 h at 37 °C. The obtained MSN-cAPT-HRP was purified by centrifugation and re-dispersing in PBS. In a typical procedure for labeling MSN with fluorophores, 1.0 mL MSN-cAPT-HRP (1.0 mg/mL) was reacted with 0.5 mg FITC for 12 h at 25 °C in dark. Then, the FITC labeled MSN was purified by centrifugation and re-dispersion in PBS. The MSN surface modification process was illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Steric hindrance mediated EV size fractionation

In the first round of EV size fractionation process, 10 μL MNP-APT20%-PEG20K (0.5 mg/mL) solution was incubated with 40 μL EV containing medium for 30 min at 25 °C. Then, the mixture was treated by a magnetic bar for 3 min to collect EV subtype 1 (EV diameter <90 nm). The EV containing supernatant was further treated by 10 μL MNP-APT20%-PEG14K (0.5 mg/mL) and a magnetic bar under the same procedure to collect EV subtype 2 (90 nm <EV diameter <180 nm). Finally, the EVs remained in the supernatant were treated by 10 μL MNP-APT (0.5 mg/mL) and a magnetic bar to harvest EV subtype 3 (180 nm <EV diameter). Prior to size fractionating, all EV containing mediums were treated by 0.22 μm filters. For analyzing the morphology and size of each EV subtype, cystamine was conjugated to the 5‘ end of APTCD63 before it was decorated to MNP surface. After fractionating, EV and MNP mixtures were treated by 10 mM dithiothreitol for 4 h at 37 °C to release EV from MNP surface for measuring hydrodynamic size by a DLS instrument38.

The expression levels of EV membrane associated proteins, including VEGF, TGF-β, PD-L1, CRP, TNF-α, and ICAM-1, were analyzed by an HRP catalyzed CL reaction. Subsequently, the released TRVEGF-HRP were transferred and treated by SuperSignal™ ELISA Femto substrate to measure CL signal at 425 nm. Microfluidic chip fabrication

The microfluidic chip comprised three layers. The cover layer was fabricated from a PMMA sheet using a CO2 laser engraver (Atomstack K60). The microchannel layer was produced through SU-8 photolithography. Specifically, SU-8 photoresist was spin-coated onto a silicon wafer and UV-exposed to pattern a casting mold. A PDMS/crosslinker mixture (10:1) was poured onto this mold, cured at 60 °C overnight, and then peeled off. Holes for the inlet and outlet ports were punched into the PDMS layer using a hole puncher. To covalently bond the layers together, the cover and substrate layers were surface-activated with oxygen plasma, followed by immersion in APTES solution (5% v/v) for 20 min. The microchannel layer was then surface-activated with oxygen plasma and attached to the cover and substrate layers overnight at 70 °C. The fabrication processes and operational procedure of microfluidic chip were illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 15, 16.

Device integration and manipulation

The pumping system consisted of four components: a peristaltic pump (Kamoer), a manifold with nine solenoid valves (Dehnker), a control board, and six electromagnets (JX-P10/10). This pumping system, along with three microfluidic chips and an optical detector, was assembled into a 3D-printed enclosure (Bambu Lab X1) to form the SHEAF platform, which was connected to a smartphone interface via Bluetooth.

For the work-flow of the integrated SHEAF device, 10 μL plasma sample and 2.5 μL solution containing MNP-APT20%-PEG20K (0.5 mg/mL) and APTVEGF-TRVEGF-HRP (0.5 μg/mL) were introduced through inlet 1 and inlet 2, respectively. The mixture solution was directed through the serpentine channel into the incubation chamber, followed by incubating for 10 min and electromagnetic separation to isolate EV subtype1. The supernatant was transferred to the second microfluidic chip for isolating EV subtype 2 and the third microfluidic chip for isolating EV subtype 3 under the same protocol in a sequential manner. The obtained MNP-EV hybrid was mixed with 7.5 μL ACTVEGF (0.1 μM) solution introduced via inlet 3 and was incubated together in the next incubation chamber for 10 min to release TRVEGF-HRP. Then, the electromagnet was activated to sediment MNP-EV hybrid, while the TRVEGF-HRP was further mixed with 2.0 μL ELISA substrate introduced via inlet 4. The CL reaction was carried out for 120 s with the CL intensity being recorded by a smartphone. Plasma samples treated by the scrambled structure of APTVEGF-TRVEGF-HRP were used as negative control. The system configuration and operational logic of the SHEAF device were illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 17. The optimization and reproducibility of SHEAF device was illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 18.

Clinical samples

Clinical analyses were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval No. 2024-126). Informed consent was secured from each participant before blood samples collection. Clinical samples of stroke patients were collected at the Department of emergency of The Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University, while clinical samples of healthy controls were collected at the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine. The initial cohort of samples was collected from January to September 2024, while the additional samples incorporated during the revision phase were obtained between August and October 2025. Clinical diagnoses were performed by medical staff from the Department of Emergency Medicine and the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine. Subsequent analyses based on SHEAF platform were conducted by researchers from the School of Pharmacy at Guizhou Medical University and School of Mechanical Engineering at Northwestern Polytechnical University. All sample-related assays were performed in a blinded manner. For stroke patients, blood was collected prior to any treatment intervention. Diagnosis of either ICH or IS was confirmed within one hour after blood sampling. Prior to the study, none of the patients had a history of stroke or myocardial infarction, nor had they received any form of treatment for stroke. The baseline characteristics of patients were summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes, followed by immediate centrifugation at 1,000 g for 10 min to harvest upper plasma layer. The collected plasma was further centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min and was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter to remove large aggregations. Then, the plasma samples were stored at −80 °C before use. A total of 70 samples were collected, including 25 patients with ICH, 25 patients with IS, and 20 healthy donors. The clinical diagnoses were made by neurologists following a series of clinical tests for each patient. These tests included physical and neurological examinations, blood tests, MRI, and computed CT scans. The clinical samples were analyzed by the integrated SHEAF platform according to work-flow described in Section “Device integration and manipulation”.

Western blotting

EVs were lysed by RIPA buffer and were quantified using BCA assay. Subsequently, the EV proteins were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto a poly(vinylidene fluoride) membrane. After being washed by Tris buffered saline with Tween-20 and blocked with 5% skimmed milk, the blots were further incubated with primary antibodies against the following proteins: CD63, ALIX, HSP70, and APOB at 4 °C for 12 h. After incubation, the blots were washed and treated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody for 2 h. Finally, the CL of blot was analyzed using a BIORAD ChemiDOC™ XRS imaging system.

Proteomic analysis of EV size subtypes

EV size subtypes were fractionated using the SHEAF platform from the cell culture mediums of HUVEC and HMC3. Each EV size subtype was re-dispersed in PBS and treated with 1.0% Triton X-100 for 30 min at 37 oC, followed by magnetic separation to remove MNP. The obtained sample solution was further treated with lysis buffer (4% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6) directly. Subsequently, dithiothreitol was added to each sample to reduce disulfide bonds by incubating at 37 °C for 1.5 h, followed by incubation with iodoacetamide for 30 min at 25 °C to block cysteine residues. Trypsin was added into sample solution at a trypsin-to-protein ratio of 1:50 (w/w), and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C overnight. The digested peptides were desalted using an MCX desalting column (Omicsolution, OS-MCX-1ML), concentrated by vacuum centrifugation, and reconstituted in 20 μL formic acid solution (0.1%). The peptide concentration was estimated by measuring the UV absorbance at 280 nm. The obtained sample solution was mixed with iRT peptides (Biognosys) according to manufacturer’s guidance for data-independent acquisition (DIA) experiments.

Peptides from each sample were analyzed using an OrbitrapTM AstralTM mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled with a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific) in DIA mode. MS1 spectra (intact form) were acquired over a mass range of 380–980 m/z with a resolution of 240,000 at 200 m/z, a normalized automatic gain control target of 500%, and a maximum injection time of 5 ms. MS2 (fragmented form) analysis was performed with 299 windows, an isolation window of 2 m/z, and an HCD collision energy of 25 eV, using a normalized automatic gain control target of 500% and a maximum injection time of 3 ms. The acquired data were processed with DIA-NN (version 1.8.1) using the following parameters: enzyme set to trypsin, maximum missed cleavages set to 1, fixed modification set to carbamidomethylation, and dynamic modifications set to oxidation and N-terminal acetylation. Subcellular localization of proteins was predicted using CELLO, a multi-class support vector machine-based classification system. All reported identifications were based on 99% confidence at the protein level, corresponding to a false discovery rate ≤ 1%.

EV size fractionation by SEC and DGUC

The SEC-based EV size fractionation was performed according to previous publicatons27,43. Sepharose® CL-2B agarose gel was mixed 3:1 (v/v) with PBS buffer and stacked into plastic columns (inner diameter 5 mm) to a height of 20 cm. The columns were equilibrated with 10 mL of PBS buffer for 30 min prior to the loading of 500 μL EV containing medium. The eluate collection was initiated immediately using graduated 2.0 mL glass tubes, yielding 300 μL per fraction. The presence and size distribution of EVs in each eluate were assessed by DLS analysis.

For DGUC-based EV size fraction, OptiPrep™ density gradient medium (60%, w/v, iodixanol) were diluted in cold PBS to give solutions with 40, 20, 10, and 5% iodixanol46,47. Then, 2 mL of those iodixanol solutions each was gentle layered in an ultracentrifugation tube in descending order of concentration to generate a discontinuous density gradient. Crude EV pellet isolated by ultracentrifugation was placed at the bottom of the tube. The bottom-loaded 5–40% gradient was centrifuged at 100,000 g for 12 h at 4 °C. After ultracentrifugation, 8 fractions (1 mL each) were collected from the top of the gradient, followed by resuspension in PBS and ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 90 min at 4 °C. The obtained pellet was resuspended in PBS for further analysis.

Instrument

The solution absorbance, fluorescence, and CL measurements were performed on a plate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 25 °C. The hydration diameters (number average) and surface zeta potentials of EVs were analyzed by a DLS instrument (NanoBrook 173Plus, Brookhaven) at 25 °C. The concentration of the isolated EV was determined using an NTA system (ZetaView, Particle Metrix) in PBS buffer at 25 °C. For each NTA analysis, 11 positions were recorded using a maximum area of 1000, a minimum area of 10, and a minimum brightness of 30. TEM images were acquired using a TalosTM L120C G2 (Thermo) instrument operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. EV size subtypes were visualized without staining, while EV-MNP hybrids were visualized after staining with phosphotungstic acid to enhance the contrast of EVs. The morphology and size of MSN-NH2 were analyzed by a scanning electron microscope (CLARA S-3400N, TESCAN). MSN-NH2 was dispersed on a silica wafer and subjected to 60 s of gold sputter-coating prior to SEM analysis. XPS analysis was performed on an ESXCALAB Xi+ instrument (Thermo), and data was analyzed by Thermo Scientific™ Avantage software. The ultracentrifugation was performed by an Optima XPN (Beckman) at 4 °C. A SW 41 Ti rotor (Beckman, k factor = 124) and Thinwall Polyallomer tubes (Beckman) were employed for this procedure.
