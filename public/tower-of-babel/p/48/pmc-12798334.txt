Visualizing physiological sclerotic dentin via micro-computed tomography: A pilot study
Authors: Muhammad Zaid Zainuddin, Noor Shafini Mohamad, Su Keng Tan, Nurul Ain Ramlan, Mohd Yusmiaidil Putera Mohd Yusof
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePhysiological dentin sclerosis is a distinctive phenomenon observed in transverse sections of the tooth root as a tooth ages. Since this effect has only been visualized 2-dimensionally, this pilot proof-of-concept study aimed to propose a novel method to visualize and quantify physiological sclerotic dentin 3-dimensionally using density-based micro-computed tomography (CT)-generated gray values.Materials and MethodsForty single-rooted premolars of known chronological age from the Malay male population were scanned with a micro-CT scanner. The X-ray source was operated at 160 kV, 100 μA, and a voxel size of 48.8 μm. Three-dimensional volume datasets for each tooth were reconstructed, and volumetric analysis was performed on the root dentin region using the open-source software FIJI (ImageJ) (ver. 1.54k, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA).ResultsMicro-CT imaging enabled the selection, isolation, and quantification of high-density areas in the tooth root that correspond to physiological sclerotic dentin. The generated gray value profile also reflected the characteristic butterfly-shaped pattern seen in transverse root sections. A comparison of premolars from different age groups revealed that individuals aged 31–60 years exhibited a larger butterfly-shaped area, reflecting the increase in high-density sclerotic dentin, compared with younger premolars.ConclusionIn this pilot proof-of-concept study, visualization and measurement of physiological sclerotic dentin were shown to be feasible using micro-CT. However, the method is highly dependent on the capability of the micro-CT scanner and the performance of the image-processing software. Further research is needed to improve image quality using advanced micro-CT scanning techniques.Keywords: Aging, Dentin, Tooth Root, X-Ray Microtomography

Introduction

Dentin sclerosis is defined as the accumulation of mineral deposits within the lumen of dentinal tubules located in the crown or root of a tooth.1 Physiological dentin sclerosis refers specifically to the natural occlusion of dentinal tubules that occurs with age, whereas reactive dentin sclerosis occurs in response to carious lesions or irritation of dentinal tissue.2 In physiological dentin sclerosis, mineral deposits accumulate within the dentinal tubules starting from the external end near the cementum and progress toward the pulp chamber, beginning at the apical root and extending coronally. Physiological sclerotic dentin is associated with alterations in dentin physical properties such as fracture toughness, fatigue resistance, hardness, elasticity, permeability, and mineral density, all of which eventually influence clinical judgment and procedures.3,4,5 Clinically, the presence of physiological sclerotic dentin may negatively affect the performance of root canal sealers due to the reduced number of tubules mesiodistally, as well as increase the likelihood of vertical root fractures in the buccolingual direction.6 To date, visualization of physiological sclerotic dentin has been limited to 2-dimensional (2D) methods, including transverse sections of the tooth root and observations of root canal sealer penetration into unsclerosed tubules, as illustrated in Figure 1. Developing a 3-dimensional (3D) approach to visualize physiological sclerotic dentin could be valuable for dental specialties that rely on tracking the progression and accumulation of the sclerotic region, such as endodontics and forensic odontology involving age estimation.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) is a high-resolution imaging technique that enables non-destructive 3D visualization of the internal microstructure of hard tissues, including bone and teeth.7,8 In dental research, micro-CT has increasingly been used to provide non-destructive, precise, and detailed images of tooth morphology, while also capturing subtle age-related changes in dental hard tissue.9,10 Furthermore, micro-CT-generated gray values have demonstrated the ability to detect minute differences in tooth mineral density profiles between areas affected by arrested versus active dentinal lesions.11

By leveraging the increased density of occluded dentinal tubules in physiological sclerotic root dentin, this study aimed to visualize the effect in 3D based on micro-CT-generated gray values. The authors hypothesized that the density differences between normal and sclerosed dentinal root regions could be detected via micro-CT, enabling imaging software to render a 3D representation of the sclerotic root region. Such imaging would permit volumetric analysis of the area of interest and could support future dental research. This pilot proof-of-concept study therefore sought to visualize and quantify the volume of physiological sclerotic root dentin in extracted single-rooted premolars, based on density-based gray values generated by micro-CT. Further research to refine image generation and expand its practical applications in dentistry is strongly warranted.

Materials and Methods

Sample collection

This study was approved by the Faculty of Dentistry, Universiti Teknologi MARA (UiTM) Research Ethics Committee (Ref. Number: REC/04/2022 (ST/MR/50)). As a pilot proof-of-concept investigation, a small sample size of 40 extracted permanent mandibular premolars was collected and grouped into 4 specific age categories: Group 1, 20–30 years (n=10); Group 2, 31–40 years (n=10); Group 3, 41–50 years (n=10); and Group 4, 51–60 years (n=10). All teeth were obtained from patients at the faculty’s student dental clinic, extracted during orthodontic or periodontic treatment. To minimize anatomical or hormonal variability, only teeth from Malay male patients were included. Eligible samples were limited to sound mandibular premolars with intact crowns and roots. Teeth that had undergone endodontic treatment, displayed resorption, or had multiple roots were excluded. Following extraction, the teeth were cleaned and stored in normal saline until scanning.

Micro-CT scanning

Scanning was performed using a micro-CT system (Xsclicer SMX-6010, Shimadzu, Japan) under the following conditions: tube voltage, 160 kV; tube current, 100 μA; field of view (XY), 25.0 mm; CT condition, LowNoise; voxel size, 48.8 μm; and number of views, 600. Each tooth was placed horizontally on a small Styrofoam platform. A toothsized pit was carved into the Styrofoam to secure the tooth and minimize movement during scanning. The scanning procedure required approximately 26 minutes, although the duration varied depending on the resolution settings used.

Image processing

Raw scan images were processed using the open-source software Fiji (ImageJ) (ver. 1.54k, National Institutes of Health, USA), with its built-in image-processing functions and the 3DSuite plugin.12 To start image processing, a background subtraction was conducted on all raw image stacks, as the true background is a technical offset of the detector and would negatively impact the ratios used for comparisons. Image sequence files in .cd format were imported into FIJI (ImageJ) software. Using the “Rectangle” function, images were cropped to only show the scan of the entire tooth (Image → Crop). The image stack was converted to 8-bit format (Image → Type → 8-bit) and smoothen (Process → Smooth) to allow better image modifications. The authors of this study recommend adjusting the image to 8-bit for practicality in selecting the gray value region of interest later in the analysis. Using the “Rectangle” function, a region of the background area of an image in the image stack was selected. Using the “Analyze” function in FIJI (ImageJ), the mean gray value of the image stack was determined (Analyze → Set Measurement → Select Mean gray value → OK → Analyze → Measure). After determining the mean gray value of the image stack, a global background subtraction was conducted (Process → Math → Subtract → Key-in Determined Value). The “Plot Profile” function was used to observe changes in gray value of the dentinal root (Analyze → Plot Profile). To observe the changes in gray value, using the “Straight Line” function, a line was drawn from the apical area of the root dentin towards the coronal region (Fig. 2). The gray value range of interest was identified based on the observed increase in density in the root dentin. As this was a pilot proof-of-concept study, the chosen range was operator-dependent due to the absence of a standardized protocol. In this study, the range of 116–138 was selected, reflecting the increase in gray value toward the apical region (Fig. 2B). Further analysis relating to 3D representation of the physiological sclerotic dentin was conducted using the FIJI (ImageJ) 3D Manager in the 3DSuite plugin (Plugins → 3DSuite → 3DManager). Using the “3D Segmentation” option, the identified gray value range was inserted as the threshold. The program subsequently visualized only areas corresponding to that range. In the new image stack, the Control Panel (Left-Click → Control Panel → Image → Adjust → Brightness/Contrast) was used to optimize visualization, with maximum brightness and contrast selected for this study. Using the “Add Image” option, the area of interest was isolated for quantification and 3D rendering. The “3D Viewer” function was employed to visualize the sclerotic dentin in 3 dimensions. For volumetric analysis, the “Measure 3D” option within the 3D Manager function was used, with volume displayed as “Vol (pix),” indicating volume in pixel units. All imaging steps were conducted and independently verified by 2 authors (MZZ and NSM).

A. A section of a tooth with the straight line tool (in yellow) is dragged from the coronal end of the root to the apical end. B. The corresponding gray value profile is shown from the “Plot Profile” selection in FIJI ImageJ. Note the increase in gray value towards the apical end of the tooth.

Statistical analysis

As the study aimed to visualize and measure the volume of physiological sclerotic dentin in single-rooted premolars across different age groups, statistical analysis was performed to compare the volume of high-density regions between groups. The nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used for overall group comparison. Where significant differences were detected, the Dunn post hoc test was applied for multiple pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Visualization of physiological sclerotic dentin

Micro-CT scanning under the predetermined settings successfully visualized varying density levels within the tooth root. The scans detected changes in gray value within the root dentin, as shown in the gray value plot profile (Fig. 2). Higher gray values corresponded to regions of greater density, whereas lower values indicated less dense areas. To enhance visualization of the root, the crown region was digitally removed by cropping the image stack at the cementoenamel junction (CEJ). When viewed in 3D, with high-density areas highlighted according to the selected gray value range of interest, larger regions of increased density were observed at the mesial and distal ends of the root compared with the buccal and lingual aspects. These high-density regions were concentrated near the external ends of the tubules adjacent to the cementum and extended progressively toward the pulp chamber. This pattern was consistently observed across tooth scans from all age groups (Fig. 3).

Volumetric measurement of physiological sclerotic dentin

The FIJI (ImageJ) 3DSuite plugin calculated the number of voxels corresponding to the operator-defined gray value range. The voxel count represented regions of higher density within the dentinal root, which are more likely to correspond to areas of dentin sclerosis than to normal, unsclerosed dentin. To compare volumes between age groups, a normalized ratio of volume of physiological sclerotic dentin (VPSD) to volume of the whole tooth root was calculated for each tooth. The average normalized ratio for each age group was then used to evaluate differences in VPSD across age categories. As shown in Figure 4, there was a significant difference in the amount of high-density area between the 20–30-year-old group and each of the older age groups. However, no significant differences were detected among the 31–40, 41–50, and 51–60-year-old groups.

Discussion

In an attempt to explore the feasibility of visualizing physiological sclerotic dentin in 3 dimensions using density-based gray values generated by micro-CT, this study highlights several key findings. First, micro-CT successfully differentiated areas of varying density within dental hard tissue and provided specific gray values corresponding to these density differences. Moreover, because micro-CT is a non-destructive imaging technique, it requires no elaborate specimen preparation, thereby avoiding tissue loss or damage. This characteristic is especially advantageous in forensic and bioarchaeological contexts, where sample preservation is of high importance. With respect to visualizing sclerotic dentin, previous studies have shown that occluded dentinal tubules possess greater mineral density than surrounding normal dentin.3,4 The authors hypothesized that these density differences would allow for 3D rendering of sclerotic dentin based on micro-CT-generated gray values. As illustrated in Figure 2, a gray value plot profile of root dentin from the coronal to the apical region revealed a slight increase, consistent with a rise in mineral density toward the apical end. This increase provided a gray value range of interest, which distinguished the denser sclerotic regions from the less dense surrounding dentin. Using this range, image processing with FIJI (ImageJ) successfully highlighted the region of interest, generated a 3D representation, and enabled volumetric assessment of the corresponding area.

Extraction of the gray value range of interest from the root dentin profile consistently yielded 3D visualizations that confirmed the distinctive location and distribution of high- density sclerotic dentin, particularly in the mesial and distal regions of the root. This unique characteristic has been well documented in previous studies. The 3D shape observed in this study corresponds to features described in the literature as the “dentin butterfly effect”,1,13,14 “bucco-lingual barbell shape”,15 “frosted dentine”, and “chalky dentine”16 when observed in 2D sections. The “dentin butterfly effect” was first described by Beust17 in 1931 as an optical phenomenon seen in transverse sections of tooth roots, presenting as a butterfly-shaped pattern resulting from variations in dentin translucency between the mesiodistal and buccolingual directions.13,18 This phenomenon has been attributed to physiological dentin sclerosis, which develops with age as dentinal tubules decrease in number, permitting greater light transmission and creating a translucent appearance. Vasiliadis et al.19 reported that dentin sclerosis differs between the mesiodistal and buccolingual aspects, producing the butterfly-shaped pattern due to the increased translucency of sclerosed dentin compared with normal dentin. Although several studies have confirmed the existence of this pattern, the precise mechanism underlying its prominence at the mesiodistal ends remains unclear.1 For example, in a study comparing root canal sealer penetration in dentinal tubules, Ramlan et al.18 found that sealer penetration was focused in the buccolingual region as opposed to the mesiodistal region (Fig. 1). This finding suggests that mesiodistal dentinal tubules are more heavily sclerosed, thereby limiting sealer penetration, while buccolingual areas remain unsclerosed and more permeable. The progression of sclerotic dentin at the mesiodistal ends alters dentin’s physical properties, including increased mineral concentration, reduced fracture toughness, and diminished fracture resistance.3,20,21,22 Kinney et al.3 documented that mineral concentration in sclerotic dentin is significantly higher than in normal dentin due to tubule lumen occlusion. They also reported an approximately 20% reduction in fracture toughness in sclerotic root dentin. The authors proposed a fracture mechanism in which tubule occlusion reduces stress concentration at peritubular cuffs, decreasing microcrack nucleation. With fewer microcracks forming, uncracked ligaments are less likely to develop, thereby lowering dentin’s overall fracture toughness. Furthermore, analyzing and quantifying the volume of sclerotic root dentin in aged dentition has potential clinical applications, particularly in endodontics. Identifying fracture-prone areas could improve treatment planning and outcomes. Russell et al. suggested that the dentin butterfly effect contributes to vertical root fractures, as significantly greater hardness mesiodistally compared with buccolingually predisposes teeth to fractures in the buccolingual direction—a pattern commonly observed in root canal—treated teeth.14,23,24 This has important implications during apical surgery, where the use of ultrasonic retrotips for root-end preparation can increase dentinal crack formation, potentially leading to microleakage and elevating the risk of vertical root fractures.25 The fact that occlusal forces are typically greater in the buccolingual direction, combined with increased mesiodistal sclerosis, may help explain the higher incidence of fractures in older patients. Nevertheless, the clinical correlation between dentin crack formation and physiological sclerotic dentin manifested by the dentin butterfly effect remains underexplored. Further research is warranted, as the current literature provides only limited evidence on this relationship.

Volumetric analysis of sclerotic regions across different age groups showed that the volume of high-density sclerotic root dentin differed significantly between premolars from 20–30-year-olds and those from older individuals. However, no significant differences were observed among the 31–60-year-old groups (Fig. 4). The authors suspect this finding may be attributable to the small sample size in this pilot proof-of-concept study. A follow-up investigation with a larger sample and an improved methodology is currently in progress. Nevertheless, the observed differences in 3D sclerotic dentin between younger and older groups are consistent with existing literature documenting the progressive increase in root dentin sclerosis with age.1,26 After the second decade of life, normal dentin gradually transforms into physiological sclerotic dentin as dentinal tubules in the apical zone become occluded with hydroxyapatite crystals, accompanied by an age-related reduction in pulpal blood vessels. Over time, dentinal tubules progressively fill with mineral deposits, beginning in the apical root region and often extending coronally.27 To the naked eye, sclerotic dentin appears more translucent than normal dentin, a phenomenon known as root dentin translucency (RDT). RDT has become a focus of interest in dental age estimation, as its progression is considered one of the most reliable biomarkers of dental age.28,29 RDT has been applied as a sole determinant in numerous studies using both direct visualization of transparent zones in sectioned teeth19,30 and indirect visualization in intact teeth.28,31 Quantitation methods have included subjective indices,32 direct measures of length and area;33 length expressed as a proportion of total root length,34 and area expressed as a proportion of the total root area.35 Traditionally, forensic odontologists measure RDT by manually sectioning the tooth of interest and assessing translucency length and area with Vernier calipers.29 While widely used, this approach is time-consuming, prone to human error,36 and unsuitable for cases where sample preservation is critical, such as in forensic odontology and bioanthropology. A non-destructive method of age estimation based on RDT would therefore be of considerable value. Volumetric measurement of RDT using micro-CT may represent an important advance, as it permits holistic visualization of translucent regions without compromising tooth integrity. Ongoing studies by the authors are currently exploring this approach.

Several limitations were encountered in this study. Foremost, the present method could not histologically distinguish between normal dentin and sclerotic dentin. Instead, it relied on density-based gray value profiles to identify higher-density sclerotic regions located in the apical root. However, histological sections, being inherently 2D, would not adequately represent the full extent of sclerosis. Additionally, tooth samples in this study were preserved for future analyses using advanced imaging techniques. The practicality of micro-CT also remains dependent on the technology and resolution of the scanner employed. High-end systems generate superior resolution compared with older-generation models, which directly affects scan quality and image clarity. Similarly, image analysis is highly dependent on the software used. While this study benefited from the freely available open-source software ImageJ, it may be limited compared with more advanced platforms. Moreover, correct visualization of sclerotic dentin requires precise annotation of gray values corresponding exclusively to sclerosis, without interference from other dense dental structures such as cementum or secondary dentin. The current methodology could not fully differentiate among these structures. Achieving this distinction will likely require more advanced micro-CT imaging combined with sophisticated image-processing algorithms. These represent areas of active interest for the authors and will form the focus of future research.
