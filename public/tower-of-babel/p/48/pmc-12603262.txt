sFlt-1/PlGF ratio use does not reduce hospitalisation duration in suspected preeclampsia: the PRECOG study, a multicentre randomised trial
Authors: J. Sibiude, N. Winer, O. Morel, E. Lecarpentier, D. Masson, F. Perrotin, C. Huissoud, G. Kayem, E. Piver, M. Rota, J. P. Bondon, V. Buth, G. Masson, J. Guibourdenche, V. Tsatsaris

ABSTRACT
The aim of the PRECOG study was to evaluate if the use of the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio in patients hospitalized for suspected preeclampsia before 35 weeks could improve patient management and reduce the length of hospitalization. A prospective randomized multicenter interventional open-label study in a hospital population with 2 parallel groups with or without taking into account the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio for the management of patients admitted for suspected pre-eclampsia. 80 patients were included in the study. Characteristics of patients were equally distributed among randomization groups. There was no difference in the primary outcome between the two groups. Hospitalization for more than 24 h was 75% in the group reveal versus 80% in the group conceal (Relative Risk p = 0.59). Groups did not differ in hospitalization duration for different cut-off values such. When stratifying by ratio value, among those with a ratio < 38, 37% were discharged home < 24 h in the conceal group and 47% in the reveal group. There was no difference in the secondary outcomes between the two groups. Introduction

Preeclampsia is a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy associated with placental insufficiency and is a leading causes of extreme prematurity, accounting for approximately 20% of preterm births before 32 weeks of gestation(WG)1. Currently, no curative treatment exists, and delivery of the fetus and the placenta alleviates maternal symptoms. Furthermore, progression from mild to severe preeclampsia is often rapid and difficult to predict. Therefore, it is recommended that patients with preeclampsia be managed in hospital settings, and patients with suspected preeclampsia are often admitted to prenatal units. Each year, many patients are hospitalized for clinical monitoring and blood and urine tests in order to rule out the diagnosis of preeclampsia.

Preeclampsia is now understood to be associated with maternal endothelial dysfunction caused by the release into the maternal circulation of excess sFlt-1 (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase), a soluble receptor that binds angiogenic factors. A concurrent decrease of the placental growth factor (PlGF) levels in the peripheric blood can also be observed in preeclamptic patients and typically precedes the symptoms by 5 weeks2. The sFlt-1/PlGF ratio has demonstrated a strong and robust negative predictive value for ruling out preeclampsia and adverse pregnancy outcomes in large cohorts3–5. Women with a sFlt-1/PlGF ratio < 38 do not have preeclampsia at the time of testing and are unlikely to develop it within the following week. This ratio is therefore highly valuable for reassuring the clinician and the patient. It may also help reduce unnecessary intervention and possibly maternal morbidity. Regarding hospitalization, two clinical trials have evaluated the clinical impact of using the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio, either alone or integrated in an algorithm, in women with suspected preeclampsia. Neither study found a difference in hospitalization rates between groups where the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio was used and those where it was concealed6,7. Concerning maternal outcomes, two large trials assessed whether PlGF guided management could reduce preeclampsia-associated maternal morbidity; however, their results were conflicted8,9. A recent stepped-wedge cluster-randomized trial evaluated whether implementing a risk stratification model using the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio could safely reduce prematurity, but the results suggested that the intervention might be associated with increased risk risk of preterm delivery10.

Since 2016, British guidelines of the National Institute for health and Care Excellence (NICE) have recommended the use of the PlGF alone or the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio to rule out preeclampsia and thereby avoid unnecessary medical interventions11. Such interventions include hospitalization or prolongation of hospitalization, corticosteroids administration, and in utero transfers to referral centers. However, due to limited formal evidence, other national guidelines either do not address or do not recommend the use of this ratio12–14. Additional evidence is therefore needed in order to determine whether this tool should be incorporated into routine clinical practice for suspected preeclampsia. To date, no study has evaluated whether the use of the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio in women hospitalized for suspected preeclampsia can safely guide clinical management and particularly avoid unnecessary prolongation of hospitalization.

The aim of the PRECOG study was to evaluate whether using the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio in patients hospitalized for suspected preeclampsia before 35 WG could safely reduce the duration of hospitalization.

Methods

PRECOG (Preeclampsia Ratio (sFlt-1/PlGF) Evaluation for Clinical and Obstetrical Guidance) was a prospective, randomized, multicenter, interventional open-label study in a hospital population. The trial comprised two parallel groups: one managing patients with knowledge of the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio, and the other without. The study took place in seven French perinatal centers between April 26, 2018 and March 12, 2020. The allocation was determined by the clinical team using 1:1 randomization using a computer-based system (Cleanweb Telemedicine Technologies SAS). Randomization was stratified by center and gestational age (< 30WG and ≥ 30 WG), with block of four.

Study population

Eligible participants were pregnant women aged 18 years or older, hospitalized for suspected preeclampsia between 24 + 0 and 34 + 6 WG. Inclusion criteria were: hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg), proteinuria (> 0.3 g/24 h or 0.3 g/l or urine dipstick ≥ 3 + or proteinuria/creatinin ratio > 30 mg/mmol), epigastric pain, generalized edema, elevated liver enzymes(> 1.5 upper limit of normal (ULN)), or thrombocytopenia (< 150,000/mm3).

Exclusion criteria comprised preexisting diagnosed preeclampsia/eclampsia or HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzyme, low platelets), intra-uterine growth restriction (IUGR) with absent or reversed end-diastolic flow (due to its independent cause for prolonged hospitalization), fetal heart rate abnormalities, multiple pregnancy, inability or unwillingness to provide informed consent, lack of medical insurance. All patients gave written informed consent to participate to the study.

Intervention (Fig. 1)

At the time of the study, the standard management for suspected preeclampsia consisted of inpatient monitoring for at least 48 h, considered necessary to rule out significant proteinuria and/or assess blood pressure control. Hospitalized patients underwent thrice-daily blood pressure monitoring, 24-hour proteinuria collection, baseline blood tests (full blood count, creatinine, liver function, coagulation), physical examination and cardiotocography. Ultrasonographic assessment of the fetus was usually performed. Continuation of hospitalization or discharge home was decided by the attending physician based on this initial evaluation.

Participants enrolled in the study were randomized individually to either standard clinical management as described above without information regarding the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio (group conceal) or management according to sFlt-1/PlGF ratio result (group reveal). Cut-off values used for clinical guidance were based on prior reference studies assessing predictive values3,4. For the reveal group, in the event of a ratio < 38, it was recommended to promote outpatient surveillance with at least weekly home visits and consultations, tailored to the patient’s condition. In case of sFlt-1/PlGF ratio between 38 and 85: initial inpatient surveillance was recommended, with discharge possible based on maternal and fetal parameters at the physician’s discretion. In case of sFlt-1/PlGF ratio > 85: prolonged hospitalization was recommended with enhanced maternal-fetal monitoring, although discharge remained possible per clinical judgement.

For the sFlt-1/PlGF Test, serum samples were sent from the clinical area to the laboratory in emergency packaging and were centrifuged within 1 h of sample collection. Testing was available 24/7. Each center had the possibility to quantify sFlt-1 and PlGF concentrations locally. sFlt-1 and PlGF were measured using fully automated immunoassay on the Roche Cobas e analyzer (Roche Diagnostics Limited, Berlin)3. Method reproducibility, assessed as interassay percent coefficient of variation, for sFlt-1 was 5.1% at 102 pg/mL and 2.8% at 1043 pg/ mL and for PlGF was 3.0% at 106 pg/mL and 2.9% at 1069 pg/mL.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients hospitalized for more than 24 h. Secondary outcomes included a composite of unfavorable outcome (eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, abruptio placentae, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), delivery before 34 weeks, IUGR < 3rd percentile, stillbirth); severe hypertension, confirmed diagnosis of pre-eclampsia, caesarean section rate, postpartum haemorrhage > 500 mL, eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, DIC, abruptio placentae, and time from randomization to delivery. Secondary neonatal outcomes were gestational age at delivery; birth weight; a composite of severe perinatal morbidity (including perinatal mortality, prematurity < 34 WG, birth weight < 3rd percentile). Preeclampsia diagnosis was made according to the following criteria: diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg or systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg combined with proteinuria (≥ 0.3 g/24 hours).

Statistical aspects

Sample size calculations were based on an expected reduction of 80% to 60% in the number of patients requiring hospitalization for more than 24 h. A preliminary study carried out at the Port-Royal maternity ward in 2011 showed 82% of women with suspected preeclampsia before 35 WG were hospitalized > 24 h15. To calculate the required sample size, we used a two-sided test with a power of 80% and a significance level of 0·05. Considering considered an allocation ratio of 1:1, the sample size to achieve this objective was 90 patients in each group. The main analysis of the primary and secondary outcomes was performed in intention-to-treat population and no interim analyses were performed.

The baseline maternal and pregnancy characteristics of the trial participants were described with qualitative variables expressed as proportions and quantitative variables as medians with inter quartile ranges (IQR).

The primary outcome was the proportion of women hospitalized for more than 24 h and we reported the relative risk between the two groups. The secondary outcomes were reported as differences in proportions between groups using the chi2 test or differences in medians using the Wilcoxon test.

Preplanned secondary analyses were performed comparing outcomes according to ratio levels. All tests were bilateral with a significance level of 5%. All analyses were performed using STATA software (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA; version 14.2).

We used a computer-based system to randomize patients with a predetermined ratio of 1:1. The trial was open-label: participants, investigators, and clinical teams were aware of group assignment. In the reveal arm, sFlt-1/PlGF results were disclosed; in the conceal arm, results were withheld until study completion for analysis.

The anticipated prevalence of suspected preeclampsia before 35 WG in the centers participating in the study was 2%, these being tertiary referral centers with expertise in the management of preeclampsia. The total number of patients giving birth was around 24,200 per year. The number of patients with suspected preeclampsia before 35 WG was estimated at 480/year. With an acceptance rate of 50% and a rate of hospitalization of 50%, we evaluated the number of patients agreeing to take part in the study to be 240 over 2 years. The target enrollment was 200 patients over 24 months.

Results

A total of 81 patients were randomized, one was excluded because not meeting inclusion criteria, thus 80 patients were included in the study, there was no loss to follow-up. The study was interrupted at 24 months before reaching the total number of patients because the rate of inclusion was too slow. This was partially due to change in clinical guidelines with the widening use of urinary spot protein/creatinine ratio16.

Baseline characteristics of patients were well balanced between randomization groups (Table 1). The median maternal age was 33 years, and the median pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was 26 kg/m2. Most participants were parous, and 50% had a history of preeclampsia (24/48). At randomization, the median gestational age was 31 WG. The primary reason for enrollment was hypertension, followed by significant proteinuria (Table 1). In the reveal group, 48% of patients had a low-risk sFlt-1/PlGF ratio (< 38) and 33% had a high-risk ratio. The clinical team accessed tests results within 4 h in 38% of cases.

Groups did not differ in hospitalization duration for different cut-off values such as 36 h, 48 h or 2 full days (date to date). When stratifying by ratio value, among those with a ratio < 38 (n = 38), only 37% (7/19) were discharged home < 24 h in the conceal group and 47% (9/19) in the reveal group.

Regarding maternal secondary outcomes, 22 women (27.2%) developed confirmed preeclampsia during hospitalization, with no difference between groups. Other pregnancy outcomes, including gestational age at delivery, labor onset, and mode of delivery were comparable between groups. Severe maternal adverse outcomes were rare and did not differ significantly: one case of HELLP syndrome occurred in each group, with no cases of eclampsia, disseminated intravascular coagulation, or abruptio placentae reported. Neonatal outcomes also showed no significant differences by randomization arm (Table 2). There were no stillbirth or neonatal death, and rates of preterm delivery were similar between groups.

Severe morbidity was reported in three women in the reveal groupe (one ischemic stroke, one placental abruption, and one pulmonary embolism) and in 1 woman in the conceal arm (deep vein thrombosis) with no assessed imputability to randomization.

Stratified analyses by gestational age at inclusion (Supplemental table 2)

Randomization was stratified by gestational age: < 30 WG and 30–34 WG. Stratified analyses of primary outcomes showed no significant differences between groups.

Among women included < 30 WG, hospitalization > 24 h occurred in 86% (12/14) of the conceal group versus 60% (9/15) of the reveal group (p = 0.22). Composite maternal adverse outcome was 57% versus 47% (p = 0.57) and composite neonatal advserse outcome was 29% versus 33% (p = 0.78), respectively.

Among women included between 30 and 34 WG, hospitalization > 24 h occurred in 77% (20/26) of the conceal group and 84% (21/25) of the reveal group (p = 0.73). Composite maternal adverse outcome was 50% versus 40% (p = 0.47) and composite neonatal adverse outcome was 50% versus 36%, respectively (p = 0.31).

Prediction value of the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio

Pooling both randomization arms, the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio was strongly associated with most studied outcomes. In the low-risk group (ratio < 38), preeclampsia incidence rate was 5.1% (2/38) versus 47.5% (19/40) in the at-risk group (ratio ≥ 38), p < 0.001. HELLP syndromes occurred exclusively in the high-risk group. Delivery during the initial hospitalization occurred in 5.1% (2/38) of the low-risk versus 45% (18/40) of at-risk patients (p < 0.001). Conversely, delivery at ≥ 37WG was observed in 74% of low-risk women versus 10% of at-risk women (p < 0.001). Composite maternal adverse outcome occurred in 16% of the low-risk group versus 78% of the high-risk group (p < 0.001) and composite neonatal adverse outcome in 18% vs. 55% (p = 0.001), respectively. Birth weight did not significantly differ between groups.

Discussion

The main finding of the PRECOG study is that implementing a management protocol based on the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio in women hospitalized for suspected preeclampsia before 35 weeks of gestation was not associated with a reduction in the length of hospitalization.

Patients were admitted at a median gestational age of 31 weeks, primarily due to hypertension or proteinuria. This study population was at high risk, with 27.5% developing preeclampsia, 57% delivering preterm, 25% delivering during the initial admission, and 47.5% experiencing adverse outcomes. The objective was to assess whether the use of the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio in patients hospitalized for suspected preeclampsia before 35 weeks could decrease the number of women hospitalized more than 24 h. Given the high negative predictive value of a low sFlt-1/PlGF ratio in suspected preeclampsia, it was hypothesized that this biomarker could help reduce unnecessary interventions and healthcare costs. In the reveal group of the study, 50% of patients had a ratio < 38 at admission, yet only 25% were discharged within 24 h. One possible explanation is the delay between admission and availability of the sFlt-1/PlGF results to the clinical team. Although all participating centers were university hospitals with 24/7 centralized laboratories able to quantify sFlt-1 and PlGF concentrations using fully automated methods on a similar analyzer (Roche Diagnostics), time between inclusion in the trial and publication of the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio by the laboratory was > 12 h in 35% of the cases. This delay was mainly due to pre-analytical factors. However, once results were published, electronic accessibility and phone notifications ensured minimal delay in clinician awareness.

Beyond result timing, other clinical factors likely contributed to prolonged hospital stays despite low ratio values. As expected hypertension was the reason for admission in 75% of patients, necessitating stabilization prior to considering outpatient management. Additionally IUGR was noted in 17% of cases which may require fetal surveillance for more than 24 h. Collectively, these factors limited the potential impact of a low sFlt-1/PlGF ratio on reducing hospitalization duration.

Our study has an important limitation which is the fact that we were unable to include the number of women initially planned, due to the smaller than expected number of women who necessitated hospitalization for suspected preeclampsia. Our study is thus underpowered to show the expected difference. At the time of the study initiation, hospitalization was standard care for suspected preeclampsia, with initial 48-hour admissions to confirm diagnosis and plan outpatient follow-up. However, during the course of the study, two changes in clinical care management occurred in several of the centers participating in the PRECOG study:1 the proteinuria/creatininuria ratio was implemented in order to avoid collecting urine for 24 h in order to confirm the diagnosis of preeclampsia, and led to fewer hospitalizations, and2 some centers developed a very functional outpatient monitoring system that led to avoid some hospitalizations as well. The small number of patients included may have also had an effect on the efficacy of randomization, with unbalanced rates for several variables such as smoking or “high risk for preeclampsia”, although none of the differences were statistically significant. Despite these limitations, we have a certain confidence in the absence of difference between the groups. The number of women who stayed hospitalized > 24 h or delivered during hospitalization was 80% in the conceal group, i.e. identical to our expectation, but it was 75% in the reveal group, far from the 60% expected. The decrease in hospitalization in the reveal group was thus much smaller than expected.

Our study also has several strengths: it is the only randomized trial evaluating sFlt-1/PlGF ratio implementation in hospitalized women with suspected preeclampsia, specifically assessing effects on hospitalization duration and maternal/neonatal outcomes. Its randomized design ensured the comparativeness of both trial arms. The multicenter design, covering diverse regions in France, enhances generalizability.

Indeed, few randomized controlled trials have evaluated the impact of this test in suspected preeclampsia, and all focused on outpatients populations. The INSPIRE study6 included 370 women with suspected preeclampsia and failed to show a reduction in the number of women necessitating hospitalization in the 24 h following the test (32% in the reveal group and 26% of women in the unreveal group). The majority of women were thus not hospitalized in this trial, and the negative predictive value of the ratio was not sufficient to avoid unnecessary hospitalizations in the reveal group. However, the authors reported that the contrast in the severity of the disease among women admitted and not admitted to in-patient care was higher in the reveal group, suggesting that the test helped better discriminate the women who did indeed necessitate hospitalization while not changing the overall number. The recent PRERISK study7 has included over 800 pregnant women with suspected preeclampsia, and did not show a difference in the hospitalization rate, or duration of hospitalization between the group using a sFlt-1/PlGF- based risk algorithm and the group where this risk was concealed to the clinician. In this study, the rate of preeclampsia associated complications was also similar in the two groups. The role of the sFlt-1/PlGF in hospitalization decision was also reported in an observational study17 showing that this decision changed in 17% of the time, following the consultation of sFlt-1/PlGF ratio, as compared to the initial plan. This change was judged “appropriate” in all cases according to the outcome of pregnancy, the authors thus also suggest that the test may help better discriminate women at risk, the limitation of this study being the absence of control group. Finally, the PREPARE trial has included 1250 pregnant women in with suspected or confirmed preeclampsia in 7 centers with a different primary objective which was to assess whether the implementation of a risk stratification model based on the use of the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio combined to Risk stratification using fullPIERS, could safely reduce prematurity. This step-wedged cluster-randomized trial could not demonstrate the hypothesized reduction in preterm birth10. On the contrary, for patients with preterm preeclampsia the use of the ratio combined to fullPIERS risk assessment was associated with a higher risk of preterm birth (aRR 1,45 p = 0.029). No difference was found between the comparison arms concerning maternal morbidity.

Two other large studies have investigated the use of a PlGF-based strategy in case of suspicion of preeclampsia with conflicting results. Both studied women in ambulatory care. The PARROT trial8 reported a significant decrease in maternal morbidity with the use of a bedside PlGF test implemented in the clinical management of pregnant women with suspected preeclampsia. Conversely, the PARROT-Ireland trial9 did not find any benefit in the use of the PlGF test in the same circumstances.

Thus, the role of angiogenic factors in clinical care management of women with suspected preeclampsia remains debated for both inpatient and outpatient care. Despite strong negative predictive value as shown in initial observational studies3, interventional studies have to date not yielded sufficient positive results to support implementation as routine care.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial in women hospitalized for suspected preeclampsia did not demonstrate a benefit of sFlt-1/PlGF ratio use at admission on hospitalization duration or maternal and neonatal outcomes. Interpretation is limited by the smaller-than-planned sample size. Further studies are needed to clarify the clinical utility of angiogenic biomarkers in this setting.
