Robotic‐Assisted Bile Duct Cannulation During Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography: A Randomised Controlled Crossover Animal Study

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionEndoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is technically challenging, with high operator radiation exposure, physical strain, and a steep learning curve, necessitating a robotic solution. Introduction

Since its introduction in 1968 [1], endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) has been regarded as one of the most challenging gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy procedures due to its technical difficulty, inherent risks, and the extensive training required to achieve proficiency. The greatest technical challenge in ERCP is selective bile duct cannulation. Success requires proper sagittal alignment [2] between the sphincterotome (or other inserted catheters) and the duodenal papilla, ampulla, and common bile duct. Attaining this alignment requires coordinated manipulation of the duodenoscope knobs, channel elevator, scope body, curved sphincterotome, and contrast catheter by the endoscopist in close collaboration with an assistant. These complex manoeuvres pose significant challenges. Even minor loss of position forces the endoscopist to re‐establish the view, increasing the procedure time and post‐pancreatitis risk. Current manual technique is limited by scope dislocation and suboptimal papillary positioning. Furthermore, patient movement, GI peristalsis, and unstable endoscope positioning can readily lead to loss of the visual field and scope dislocation, resulting in cannulation failure. Even for experienced endoscopists, the cannulation failure rate remains approximately 5%–15%, and the incidence of post‐ERCP pancreatitis can be as high as 9.7% [3, 4].

ERCP necessitates fluoroscopy for visualisation of the bile and pancreatic ducts. Operators must wear personal protective equipment (PPE) to mitigate radiation exposure, but PPE typically weighs 10–15 kg, demanding considerable physical stamina. Despite its use, radiation exposure remains a concern [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Despite advances in the ERCP technique, several unresolved challenges persist. First, basic prerequisites for adequate ERCP training include at least 300 ERCPs and 12 consecutive months. Second, prolonged wearing of lead aprons and cumulative radiation exposure increase the risk of health issues. Third, cooperation errors can lead to procedural failure and postoperative complications. Fourth, because ERCP is an advanced endoscopic technique, there is an uneven distribution of medical resources among specialists. To address these challenges, our team developed the YunSRobot‐ERCP robotic system, which employs a leader‐follower control architecture and has been successfully applied in animal experiments, human trials, and remote operations for upper GI endoscopy [11, 12, 13].

In this study, we utilised the YunSRobot‐ERCP system, an optimised version of the YunSRobot robotic system, to perform robotic‐assisted ERCP. This system centralises the control of endoscopic and surgical instruments at a master console, allowing the operator to perform the procedure from a shielded area, thereby minimising radiation exposure. A single operator in the protected zone manipulates the robotic system to conduct duodenoscopy and instrument operation, completing the ERCP without the need for multiple assistants. The system offers stable scope holding and instrument manipulation, with the potential to enhance cannulation efficiency and reduce complications. We herein present the safety, feasibility, and efficacy of this robotic system for performing ERCP in an animal model.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Sample Size

This study employed a randomised, controlled, crossover design conducted on beagles. This design was chosen to minimise the number of animals required while allowing for a direct intra‐individual comparison of the two techniques, in accordance with the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement).

A sample size calculation was performed based on a preliminary cannulation success rate of 90% (9/10) in beagles. Assuming a success rate of 90% and a non‐inferiority margin of 25%, with a one‐sided α of 0.05 and a power (1‐β) of 0.8, a minimum of 10 procedures per group (20 total) were required.

Three experienced endoscopists (each with > 500 ERCP cases) participated in the study. Prior to the animal experiments, all endoscopists underwent standardised training on the YunSRobot‐ERCP system using simulated models to mitigate the learning curve effect. The training consisted of 8 h of hands‐on sessions over 2 weeks, during which each endoscopist performed at least 10 simulated cannulation exercises to achieve proficiency in master console manipulation and coordinated accessory control. Four healthy adult beagles were included. A computer‐generated randomisation sequence was prepared by an independent statistician to determine the order of procedures (robotic‐assisted vs. manual) for each endoscopist on each animal. This sequence was concealed in opaque sealed envelopes, which were opened only immediately before each procedure. According to this randomisation, each endoscopist performed one robotic‐assisted and one manual ERCP cannulation on each beagle, resulting in a total of 24 procedures (12 per group; Figure 1). To minimise any potential carry‐over effects from the first procedure to the second, a minimum washout period of 1 week was strictly observed between the two procedures on the same animal. While the performing endoscopists could not be blinded to the intervention, the analysis of cannulation outcomes was based on objective pre‐defined criteria and recorded videos.

Flow chart of animal experiment design.

Notably, the experimental protocol was limited to bile duct cannulation and contrast injection without sphincterotomy. Cannulation is the most critical step in ERCP, and successful cannulation accounts for at least half of the overall procedural success. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the feasibility and stability of robotic‐assisted cannulation; sphincterotomy and other therapeutic manoeuvres are beyond the scope of this initial investigation and will be addressed in future studies.

The YunSRobot‐ERCP Robotic System

The YunSRobot‐ERCP system, co‐developed by the Chinese PLA General Hospital and the Shenyang Institute of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences, is a teleoperated robotic platform based on a leader‐follower architecture (Figure 2).

Schematic of the YunSRobot‐ERCP system: (A) Master control workstation, (B) Control handle, (C) Image‐processing workstation, (D) Robotic arm for operating the duodenoscope, (E) Robotic arm for manipulating the accessories (sphincterotome and guidewire), (F) Console for endoscopy, (G) X‐ray equipment, (H) Experimental subject.

The leader console (Figure 2A–C) is the operator's radiation‐shielded workstation. It comprises the master operating panel of the robot (Figure 2A), three control handles (Figure 2B), and an image‐processing workstation (Figure 2C). Two handles control endoscopic movements (insertion/withdrawal, tip bending, scope rotation, air/water insufflation), while the third handle controls accessory manipulation (advancement/retraction of the sphincterotome or guidewire, sphincterotome bowing). The master computer interprets these commands and transmits them to the follower robot.

The follower robotic platform (Figure 2D–E) is positioned at the patient's bedside and executes the operator's commands. It consists of an endoscopic manipulator and an accessory‐operating robotic arm.

The system is powered by a total of 20 drive units, providing 20 degrees of freedom (DOFs) (Figure 3). These units are functionally allocated to achieve all necessary motions for a complete ERCP procedure:

Endoscopic Systems and Instruments

All procedures were performed using a standard Olympus duodenoscope (JF‐260V; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Sphincterotomes (1.9–2.4 mm × 2000 mm; Anrai Medical, Hangzhou, China) and guidewires (0.64 mm × 4500 mm; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) were used for cannulation.

Animal Preparation and Experimental Procedure

Four healthy adult beagles (weight 13.40 ± 0.80 kg, age 2.75 ± 0.29 years) were included. Animals were fasted for 96 h and water‐restricted for 24 h before the procedure. Pre‐anaesthesia involved intramuscular injections 20 min before surgery, followed by intravenous propofol for induction. After endotracheal intubation, anaesthesia was maintained with butorphanol, and the animal was placed in the supine position. For the robotic‐assisted group, the endoscopist controlled the robot to advance the scope from the oral cavity into the stomach. Because of the anatomical challenges of the beagle's pylorus, trans‐pyloric intubation was challenging. To ensure feasibility and minimise animal trauma, we adopted a strategy of trans‐pyloric intubation with manual assistance when necessary. Once the scope was positioned in the duodenum, control was transferred to the robotic system. All subsequent steps, including final positioning at the major duodenal papilla, bile duct cannulation, and contrast injection, were performed solely by a single operator using the YunSRobot‐ERCP system without further manual assistance (Supplementary Video S1). Manual ERCP procedures followed the standard clinical technique with a physician and an assistant.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical data as number and percentage. Categorical data were analysed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, and continuous data using the Mann–Whitney U test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses used SPSS 21.0 (IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA), and sample size calculation used PASS 15.0 (NCSS LLC, New Canaan, CT, USA).

Results

Endoscopic Stability: Maintaining the Papillary Position

The robotic‐assisted group demonstrated significantly superior endoscopic stability. While the median total cannulation time was comparable between the manual (169.50 s, IQR 106.25–207.25) and robotic‐assisted (163.5 s, IQR 126.50–210.75) groups (p = 0.862), the proportion of time the papilla was maintained in the desired position was markedly higher in the robotic‐assisted group. The maintenance rate was 95.74% (IQR 89.09%–96.94%) for robotic‐assisted ERCP compared to 77.25% (IQR 67.02%–87.57%) for the manual technique (p < 0.01) (Table 1).

Cannulation Success and Efficiency

The successful cannulation rate was identical in both groups at 83.3% (10/12 procedures). The robotic‐assisted group required a median of 2.00 (IQR 1.25–3.00) cannulation attempts, compared to 3.50 (IQR 2.00–4.00) in the manual group. This difference, while favouring the robotic system, did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.154) (Table 2).

Endoscope Dislocation and Adverse Events

Duodenoscope dislocation to the pylorus was observed five times in the manual group but did not occur in any robotic‐assisted procedure (0 vs. 5, p = 0.07). The rates of minor adverse events were similar between the groups. Mucosal abrasion with minor oozing occurred in 4 (33.3%) manual and 5 (41.7%) robotic‐assisted cases (p = 0.673). Mild mucosal oedema was noted in 4 (33.3%) manual and 3 (25.0%) robotic‐assisted cases (p = 0.653). No severe adverse events, such as perforation or mortality, were observed in either group (Table 3).

Discussion

Current international research on robotic systems for digestive endoscopy primarily focuses on endoscopic submucosal dissection, such as the MASTER, STRAS, and EndoMaster systems [14, 15, 16]. These systems provide two‐arm manipulation for tissue traction and dissection but lack the specialised duodenoscope elevator control and coordinated guidewire/sphincterotome management required for ERCP. Existing GI endoscopy robotic systems are currently incapable of performing ERCP adequately. To address this, we developed the YunSRobot robotic‐assisted endoscopy system, which has been successfully employed for remote upper GI endoscopy in humans [17]. The YunSRobot‐ERCP system is unique in dedicating 20 degrees of freedom to duodenoscope handling and accessory manipulation. In this study, utilising the optimised YunSRobot‐ERCP system, we conducted the first animal study demonstrating the feasibility of a single operator performing ERCP, including cannulation and contrast injection, thereby validating the feasibility, efficacy, and safety of robotic‐assisted ERCP.

The system employs a leader–follower control architecture, in which the physician console and the robotic unit exchange real‐time video data and control commands through network‐based communication. Unlike conventional ERCP requiring physician‐assistant coordination, this robotic system enables a single operator to manipulate both the endoscope and instruments simultaneously, potentially reducing coordination errors and improving efficiency. Furthermore, it permits full physical separation between the physician and patient, eliminating operator radiation exposure.

The beagle gastrointestinal tract is anatomically similar to that of humans and is therefore a commonly used animal model in digestive endoscopy research. In healthy beagles, there are two duodenal papillae: a major duodenal papilla and a minor duodenal papilla. The bile duct and the smaller accessory pancreatic duct share a common orifice at the major papilla, whereas the larger main pancreatic duct opens independently at the minor papilla, which lacks a sphincter [18]. This anatomical feature makes the beagle model more anatomically analogous to humans with respect to cannulation. However, the common bile duct diameter in adult beagles is approximately 3.04 ± 1.89 mm. The accessory pancreatic duct branches coalesce in the pancreatic body, with diameters of ∼0.88 ± 0.14 mm (right) and ∼0.93 ± 0.28 mm (left) [19]. The guidewire diameter used was 0.64 mm (0.025 inches). Due to the unique anatomy of beagles, only bile duct cannulation procedures were performed. Cannulating the relatively small pancreatic ducts was challenging; thus, pancreatic duct imaging was not performed.

Compared to manual cannulation, robotic‐assisted ERCP achieved a similar success rate of 83.3%, a marginally shorter median cannulation time, and fewer required attempts, suggesting that its efficacy is at least comparable to the manual technique. Precise papillary alignment and duodenoscope stability are fundamental for successful ERCP and serve as key indicators of technical proficiency [20, 21]. Operators noted exceptional stability with the robotic system, mitigating concerns about papillary displacement or scope dislocation. This was attributed to the system's controlled endoscope movement/rotation and fixed positioning of control knobs and the channel elevator. Manual ERCP does not allow a single physician to perform multiple manoeuvres simultaneously. As seen in previous studies [22, 23], surgical robotic systems can offer advantages such as an improved field of view and enhanced operational precision.

Routine manual ERCP requires collaboration between one endoscopist and one assistant. The endoscopist controls the scope while the assistant operates instruments, necessitating skilled coordination. Inexperience or poor cooperation can lead to high failure rates. While experienced endoscopists often operate the guidewire themselves, managing multiple tasks increases the risk of scope dislocation and loss of view. The YunSRobot‐ERCP system integrates all accessory controls at the master console, enabling a single operator to manage the duodenoscope and instruments simultaneously, thereby addressing the challenge of inadequate coordination.

Even with PPE, manually performing ERCP exposes operators to radiation. Guidelines suggest that the average entrance skin dose for a single ERCP procedure ranges from 55 to 347 mGy, with therapeutic ERCP doses approximately three times higher than diagnostic ones [24]. ERCP specialists facing long‐term exposure have an increased risk of conditions such as cataracts and malignancies [25, 26]. Robotic‐assisted ERCP can effectively protect operators by enabling them to work completely or partially outside the radiation field. The YunSRobot‐ERCP's leader‐follower mode allows operation from a radiation‐free area or remotely, solving the radiation exposure issue. Additionally, similar to clinical studies on surgery robots (Naidu et al. and Klein et al. [27, 28]; van der Schatte Olivier et al. [29]), leader–follower systems may reduce operator muscle fatigue and mental stress during lengthy procedures. The YunSRobot‐ERCP system also protects operators from physical damage to the spine, joints, and muscles caused by bearing the weight of PPE, including the physical strain associated with wearing heavy lead vests and prolonged standing.

As exploratory research, this study has several limitations. The small sample size and use of only four beagles, despite the randomised crossover design, may have influenced outcome estimates. Another limitation is the need for partial manual assistance to traverse the pylorus in robotic procedures due to the beagle's specific pyloric anatomy. While this underscores the need to improve robotic navigation through angulated regions, it does not diminish the system's excellent stability and cannulation performance once the papilla is accessed. Finally, the absence of haptic feedback remains a challenge shared with most flexible robotic endoscopes. Despite these limitations, this study clearly demonstrates that robotic‐assisted ERCP is feasible, safe, and offers superior endoscopic stability with comparable cannulation success to manual techniques.

Conclusion

This randomised controlled animal study demonstrates that robotic‐assisted ERCP using the YunSRobot‐ERCP system is a safe and feasible technique. It achieves a cannulation success rate comparable to the standard manual approach while providing significantly superior endoscopic stability. By enabling a single operator to perform the procedure from a shielded environment, this system has the potential to reduce procedural complexity, enhance patient safety, and eliminate occupational radiation exposure. These results provide a strong foundation for future research, paving the way for clinical application and the development of telerobotic ERCP platforms capable of delivering specialised care across distances.
