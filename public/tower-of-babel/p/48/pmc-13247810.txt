Rethinking nutritional risk in cancer: muscle quality, myosteatosis, and prognosis
Authors: Shangze Jiang, Haifeng Xu
Published: 2026

Nutritional risk in cancer has traditionally been assessed using body weight, body mass index (BMI), serum biomarkers, and clinical nutritional scores; however, these conventional parameters may not fully capture the host-derived metabolic vulnerability that shapes treatment tolerance and prognosis (1). Simple anthropometric metrics like weight loss and BMI are increasingly recognized as inadequate, primarily because they fail to distinguish between skeletal muscle and adipose tissue depletion, often masking severe underlying catabolic derangements (2,3). In contrast to these traditional surrogates, body composition better reflects a patient’s true metabolic profile and physiologic reserve, which are the fundamental determinants of oncologic outcomes. The complex host-tumor interaction drives systemic metabolic dysfunctions that deplete these critical reserves, thereby directly compromising patient survival and quality of life (4). Consequently, routine oncologic computed tomography (CT) imaging offers an opportunity to move beyond simple anthropometric assessment and evaluate body composition in a clinically accessible manner, allowing clinicians to accurately capture this hidden metabolic vulnerability.

While sarcopenia has gained increasing attention in oncology, muscle quantity alone may not adequately reflect the functional and metabolic integrity of skeletal muscle (5). Indeed, cancer patients presenting with similar muscle mass can exhibit profoundly different clinical trajectories, chemotherapy toxicities, and overall survival outcomes depending on the extent of intramuscular adipose infiltration (6). This discrepancy highlights that muscle quality represents a critical, yet historically underestimated, dimension of a patient’s true nutritional and metabolic risk. The deterioration of muscle quality is primarily characterized by the pathological accumulation of ectopic fat within the muscle compartment, a clinical condition widely recognized as myosteatosis (7). On routine cross-sectional imaging, this lipid derangement translates to a quantifiable reduction in skeletal muscle radiodensity and decreased muscle attenuation (2). Consequently, recognizing the burden of intramuscular fat infiltration allows clinicians to move beyond simple muscle depletion, capturing a distinct phenotype of metabolic dysfunction that heavily dictates oncologic prognosis.

Myosteatosis, commonly reflected by reduced skeletal muscle attenuation on CT, should be interpreted not merely as a radiological descriptor but as a nutritional-metabolic phenotype. This phenotype may integrate malnutrition, systemic inflammation, lipid dysregulation, and impaired metabolic reserve, thereby providing prognostic information beyond muscle mass alone. Unlike sarcopenia, which primarily reflects a physical loss of muscle quantity, myosteatosis serves as an early and distinct biological biomarker that bridges structural muscle decline with systemic metabolic risk (8). The pathological accumulation of ectopic fat within the skeletal muscle compartment actively mirrors profound host-tumor interactions, characterized by insulin resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction, lipotoxicity, and altered muscle-adipose crosstalk (9). Furthermore, compelling clinical evidence demonstrates that this intramyocellular lipid accumulation is intricately driven by, and exacerbates, cancer-induced cachexia and chronic systemic inflammation (7,10). Ultimately, myosteatosis captures a highly vulnerable metabolic state where malnutrition and inflammatory catabolism converge, rendering it a uniquely powerful determinant of a patient’s oncologic fitness and survival trajectory.

In hepatopancreatobiliary oncology, the clinical relevance of myosteatosis may be particularly pronounced because malnutrition, cachexia, digestive dysfunction, impaired hepatic reserve, and systemic inflammation often coexist. Unlike many other malignancies, tumors of the pancreas, liver, and biliary tract directly compromise vital metabolic and digestive organs. For instance, in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma and biliary tract cancers, the intersection of tumor burden and underlying chronic liver disease severely impairs hepatic functional reserve and disrupts bile acid metabolism, which further exacerbates systemic inflammation and catabolic pathways (11-13). The convergence of these site-specific physiological disruptions strongly accelerates the pathological infiltration of intramuscular fat. In the demanding perioperative setting of major hepatopancreatobiliary resections, this deterioration in muscle quality acts as a critical determinant of oncologic fitness, significantly increasing the risk of severe surgical complications and diminishing long-term survival (14). Therefore, CT-derived muscle quality may serve as a bridge between nutritional assessment, metabolic vulnerability, and oncologic prognosis in this patient population.

A key unresolved question is whether myosteatosis carries the same prognostic and biological meaning in men and women. Men and women inherently exhibit profound baseline disparities in skeletal muscle mass and regional adipose tissue distribution. Furthermore, their divergent hormonal backgrounds—specifically the distinct protective and anabolic roles of estrogens and testosterone in maintaining muscle homeostasis—drive sexually dimorphic responses to catabolic stress (15). Recent transcriptomic profiling provides compelling evidence for this biological dimorphism, revealing that up to 70% of gene expression alterations during cancer-induced muscle wasting are entirely sex-specific (16). Because of these fundamental physiological differences, the systemic inflammatory responses, lipid metabolic dysregulation, and mitochondrial remodeling triggered by tumors often manifest differently between the sexes (17). Despite this striking biological dimorphism, the majority of current oncologic studies limit their methodology by merely adjusting for sex as a confounding covariate, rather than performing comprehensive sex-stratified analyses to uncover true prognostic variations. Recognizing that the pathophysiological consequences of intramuscular fat infiltration may fundamentally differ by sex, future studies should move beyond simple statistical adjustments to actively explore the underlying biological mechanisms driving why myosteatosis develops differently between male and female patients.

Another emerging question is whether CT-defined myosteatosis can be linked to molecular alterations in skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, blood, or tumor tissue. Emerging multi-omics and transcriptomic studies reveal that the loss of muscle radiodensity is not merely a structural artifact, but rather the macroscopic footprint of profound metabolic reprogramming (18). At the cellular level, the deterioration of muscle quality is orchestrated by a complex interplay of systemic inflammation and severe lipid metabolism dysregulation, which is heavily amplified by pathological adipose-muscle crosstalk (8,19). These systemic intercellular signals trigger widespread intracellular derangements within myocytes, characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, dysregulated autophagy, and the hyperactivation of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (20-22). Indeed, recent cancer cachexia transcriptomic signatures indicate that intramyocellular lipid accumulation actively exacerbates catabolic signaling and energetic failure, driving a vicious cycle of wasting (9,23). Although direct evidence linking CT-defined myosteatosis to tumor sequencing remains limited, transcriptomic studies of cancer cachexia provide important clues that impaired muscle quality may reflect molecularly active processes rather than passive tissue degeneration.

These considerations point toward a future framework of sex-aware and molecularly informed nutritional phenotyping in cancer. As the field of precision oncology advances, it is increasingly evident that relying solely on conventional BMI, absolute muscle mass, or universal imaging cutoffs is fundamentally inadequate to capture the true biological complexity of muscle wasting. Instead, future clinical algorithms must embrace a multidimensional stratification model. This comprehensive framework should seamlessly integrate CT-derived myosteatosis, sex-specific interpretations, cancer type, cachexia status, and the underlying burden of systemic inflammation (24). Furthermore, incorporating tumor and skeletal muscle transcriptomics into this clinical model will be pivotal to bridging the gap between macroscopic radiological phenotypes and active molecular pathophysiology. By synergizing these diverse layers of information, clinicians can move beyond mere prognostic associations to accurately predict individualized treatment tolerance and survival outcomes.

Ultimately, rethinking nutritional risk in cancer requires moving beyond weight, BMI, and even muscle mass. CT-derived myosteatosis offers a practical window into muscle quality, but its full clinical value will depend on whether future studies can define sex-specific relevance, uncover molecular mechanisms, and translate imaging phenotypes into actionable nutritional and therapeutic strategies.
