Fibrosarcomatous Transformation of Recurrent Thigh Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans Presenting With Spontaneous Hemorrhage
Authors: Simeon Mulugeta Mengistu, Getaw Alamne, Dagmawi Geremew, Daniel Hailemichael, Habtamu Tadele, Harom Gari, Abinet Admas, Mekonnen Eshete

ABSTRACT
Summary:Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare cutaneous sarcoma characterized by locally aggressive growth and a high rate of local recurrence. Fibrosarcomatous DFSP is an uncommon variant associated with increased risks of recurrence, metastasis, and disease-related mortality. A 32-year-old man with a history of DFSP of the left thigh presented with spontaneous life-threatening hemorrhage from a recurrent ulcerated mass. After hemodynamic stabilization, wide local excision with a 3-cm margin was performed, followed by split-thickness skin graft reconstruction. Histopathologic examination confirmed fibrosarcomatous transformation of recurrent DFSP. All surgical margins were free of tumor. The postoperative course was uneventful with satisfactory graft take and no recurrence at 5 months of follow-up. Spontaneous hemorrhage is a rare presentation of fibrosarcomatous DFSP. Prompt stabilization, oncologically sound resection, and appropriate reconstruction are essential for optimal outcomes. This case highlights the importance of recognizing aggressive clinical behavior in recurrent DFSP and emphasizes the role of reconstructive surgeons in multidisciplinary management.

Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare cutaneous sarcoma of fibroblastic origin characterized by locally aggressive growth and a high propensity for local recurrence.1 Although metastasis is uncommon, the tumor frequently infiltrates surrounding tissues with ill-defined margins, making complete surgical excision essential for disease control.

Fibrosarcomatous transformation occurs in approximately 5%–15% of DFSP cases and represents a higher grade variant associated with increased cellularity, mitotic activity, and more aggressive biological behavior.2 Compared with classic DFSP, fibrosarcomatous DFSP (FS-DFSP) carries higher risks of recurrence, metastasis, and disease-related mortality.

Hemorrhagic presentation of DFSP is extremely uncommon. Most tumors demonstrate slow, indolent growth and remain asymptomatic for prolonged periods. However, recurrent or aggressive lesions may ulcerate and bleed due to rapid tumor expansion or vascular compromise.3,4

We report a rare case of recurrent thigh DFSP with fibrosarcomatous transformation presenting with spontaneous life-threatening hemorrhage. To our knowledge, hemorrhagic presentation of FS-DFSP has been rarely reported, particularly in the setting of tumor recurrence.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 32-year-old man presented to the emergency department with spontaneous bleeding from a mass on the left upper thigh that had been enlarging for 3 months. The lesion had progressively ulcerated in the weeks preceding the bleeding episode.

The patient had previously undergone wide local excision and split-thickness skin grafting for DFSP at the same site 3 years earlier. The new mass developed near the posterosuperior margin of the previous graft and gradually increased in size.

On examination, an 8 × 6 cm ulcerated mass was present on the posterolateral aspect of the upper left thigh, surrounded by previously grafted skin (Fig. 1). Continuous oozing from the ulcerated surface was noted. Initial bleeding control was achieved using adrenaline-soaked compressive dressings.

Laboratory findings were the following: hemoglobin, 13.8 g/dL; platelet count, 130,000/µL. Coagulation profile and liver and renal function tests were within normal limits.

Imaging, including abdominopelvic computed tomography and chest radiography, showed no evidence of metastasis. The patient was admitted for observation and surgical planning.

On the second day of admission, the lesion ruptured spontaneously, resulting in massive bleeding unresponsive to compression. The patient developed hypotension and required fluid resuscitation and blood transfusion before emergency surgery.

Under general anesthesia, an ulcerated tumor measuring approximately 8 × 6 cm was identified, infiltrating the underlying fascia and gluteus maximus muscle. Wide local excision with a 3-cm radial margin was performed, including the involved fascia and a portion of the underlying muscle (Fig. 2).

Wide local excision of thigh FS-DFSP. A, Intraoperative image after wide local excision with a 3-cm margin showing the underlying involved deep fascia and part of the gluteus maximus muscle. B, Gross image of the excised mass.

The wound was temporarily dressed while awaiting histopathologic confirmation of tumor-free margins. Reconstructive options, including local flap coverage and skin grafting, were considered. Because the defect did not expose bone, major vessels, or tendons, reconstruction was performed using a split-thickness skin graft harvested from the contralateral thigh after confirmation of clear margins.

Histopathologic examination demonstrated fibrosarcomatous transformation within recurrent DFSP, characterized by spindle cells arranged in fascicular and herringbone patterns with moderate atypia. The postoperative course was uneventful. The skin graft healed well with complete take. At 3 weeks, the patient had satisfactory wound healing, and at 5 months of follow-up, there was no clinical evidence of recurrence. Adjuvant radiotherapy was recommended but had not yet commenced due to limited access.

DISCUSSION

Fibrosarcomatous transformation significantly alters the biological behavior of DFSP. Compared with classic DFSP, FS-DFSP demonstrates higher rates of recurrence, metastasis, and disease-specific mortality. A systematic review reported recurrence rates of approximately 29.8% in FS-DFSP compared with 13.7% in classic DFSP, with substantially increased risks of metastasis and mortality.5

Spontaneous hemorrhage in DFSP is rare. Most tumors present as slowly enlarging cutaneous masses and remain asymptomatic for extended periods. Only a limited number of cases describing hemorrhagic presentation have been reported in the literature3,4 (Table 1). Bleeding may occur in recurrent or aggressive tumors due to ulceration, rapid growth, or increased vascularity within fibrosarcomatous components.

Our case is notable for the coexistence of 3 high-risk features: tumor recurrence, fibrosarcomatous transformation, and spontaneous life-threatening hemorrhage requiring urgent surgical intervention. These factors highlight the potential for aggressive clinical behavior in recurrent DFSP.

Complete surgical excision with negative margins remains the cornerstone of treatment. Margin status is one of the most important modifiable prognostic factors. In the present case, a 3-cm radial margin was achieved, consistent with recommendations suggesting margins of at least 2 cm to reduce the risk of recurrence.7

Reconstructive planning must balance oncological safety with functional outcomes and postoperative surveillance. In this case, split-thickness skin grafting was selected because the defect lacked exposed critical structures and because graft coverage facilitates early detection of recurrence compared with flap reconstruction.

Adjuvant radiotherapy may reduce the risk of recurrence in DFSP, particularly in cases with aggressive histology or recurrent disease.8 However, access to radiotherapy may be limited in some healthcare settings. In such circumstances, achieving adequate surgical margins and ensuring close follow-up are especially important. Surgeons managing recurrent DFSP should maintain a high index of suspicion for aggressive transformation when tumors demonstrate rapid growth, ulceration, or bleeding.6

CONCLUSIONS

Fibrosarcomatous transformation represents an aggressive variant of DFSP that may rarely present with spontaneous life-threatening hemorrhage. Prompt stabilization, wide oncological excision with negative margins, and appropriate reconstruction are critical for optimal outcomes. This case emphasizes the importance of recognizing aggressive clinical presentations of recurrent DFSP and highlights the role of reconstructive surgeons in multidisciplinary management.
