Development and validation of an algorithm to identify severe sepsis onset from electronic medical records
Authors: Ramin Homayouni, Shane Morrell, Joel David Karsten, Riya Chhabra, Paul D Bozyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and evaluate an automated algorithm to identify sepsis onset, referred to as time-zero (t0), from the electronic medical records (EMR).Materials and MethodsWe developed an algorithm to construct a comprehensive timeline of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) criteria and organ dysfunction (OD) using structured data, and documentation of infection (DOI) using both structured data and unstructured clinical notes. Algorithm performance was assessed using 2030 manually abstracted adult sepsis cases from a multicenter health system in southeast Michigan.ResultsOn average, the algorithm DOI time was significantly earlier than abstractors (mean: −0.33 hour, 95% Cl, −0.55 to −0.11), resulting in a significantly earlier t0 (mean: −0.60 hour, 95% CI, −0.74 to −0.45).DiscussionAutomated approaches to analyzing EMR data offer a scalable framework for SEP-1 monitoring, research, and quality improvement.ConclusionIncorporating unstructured clinical notes improves DOI detection.

Introduction

Sepsis is characterized as a life-threatening organ dysfunction (OD) caused by an unregulated host response to infection1. In the United States, approximately 1.7 million adult hospitalizations and at least 350 000 deaths annually are related to sepsis2. In the hospital setting, more than one-third of the deaths involve sepsis.2,3 Therefore, ongoing surveillance and adherence to treatment guidelines are central to quality improvement efforts.4

In 2015, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) introduced the Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock Early Management Bundle (SEP-1) as a publicly reported measure within the Hospital Inpatient Quality Reporting (IQR) Program.5,6 SEP-1 operationalizes time-bound elements such as initial lactate measurement and re-measurement, blood cultures before antibiotics, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and fluid resuscitation into composite 3- and 6-hour bundles. While SEP-1 has focused hospital attention on sepsis care, evidence regarding its association with improved outcomes is varied. Large multihospital evaluations of SEP-1 implementation found increases in lactate testing and some changes in treatment patterns, but no associated improvement in short-term mortality.7,8 These findings, together with concerns about measure complexity and potential unintended consequences, have fueled ongoing debate about how best to drive better sepsis outcomes.

National guidance emphasizes the need for robust hospital sepsis programs capable of surveillance, feedback, and targeted training across care settings. Prior work has leveraged automation to define sepsis t0 for SEP-1 evaluation using structured data, such as physician orders for blood culture or antibiotics. Order-based proxies underlie widely used electronic surveillance definitions (eg, CDC Adult Sepsis Event), which define “presumed serious infection” by culture orders plus antibiotic administration near the time of OD.9,10 For example, Baghdadi et al11 used an automated algorithm to mark t0 when Sepsis-3 physiologic criteria were first met, including clinician suspicion of infection based on orders for antibiotics, antifungals, or clinical cultures within a 48-hour window. However, prior studies have shown substantial variability in determining SEP-1 t0 because it relies on specific note phrases to document suspicion of infection by a physician.10 This emphasizes the need for including clinical notes that may better document the onset of sepsis.

The goal of this project was to develop and evaluate an automated algorithm to identify sepsis t0 retrospectively from electronic medical records (EMRs) using both structured data and information extracted from clinical notes.

Methods

Study population

A total of 2214 cases across 8 geographically and demographically distinct hospitals in southeast Michigan were abstracted by Quality & Safety personnel between April 27, 2021 and July 1, 2024 (Figure 1). These cases were randomly selected from a pool of cases identified by Vizient Inc. for the hospital Quality & Safety team. Of these, 41 (1.9%) cases were excluded because sepsis onset (time-zero, t0) was not be determined by abstractors, and an additional 58 (2.6%) cases were excluded because the algorithm did not determine t0. Finally, 85 (2.6%) cases were removed as outliers using the IQR × 3 rule, where any data point below Q1 − (3.0 × IQR) value or above Q3 + (3.0 × IQR) value is considered an outlier. Here, Q1 is the first quartile, Q3 is the third quartile, and IQR (Interquartile Range) is Q3 − Q1. After removal of outliers, differences between algorithm-derived and abstractor-derived t0 ranged from −22 to +20 hours.

Study overview. Inclusion and exclusion criteria used to refine the study cohort and subset analysis to compare the algorithm’s performance and abstractor-determined t0, SIRS, organ dysfunction (OD), or documentation of infection (DOI).

For SEP-1 component subset analyses, differences in timing between the algorithm and abstractors for systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), OD, and documentation of infection (DOI) were processed by excluding missing values for each component and removing outliers using the IQR × 3 rule described above. This resulted in the exclusion of 1456 missing and 34 outlier observations for Δ-SIRS, 1456 missing and 39 outliers for Δ-OD, and 1164 missing and 20 outliers for Δ-DOI, yielding final analytical samples of 540, 546, and 839 cases, respectively.

Definition of severe sepsis and septic shock

Sepsis t0 was defined according to the CMS criteria for severe sepsis or septic shock.5,6 The criteria for severe sepsis were met when the following 3 conditions were met within a 6-hour window: (1) evidence of infection or suspected infection, (2) fulfillment of at least 2 SIRS criteria [temperature >38.3 °C or <36 °C, heart rate >90 beats/min, respiratory rate >20 breaths/min, or white blood cell count >12 000/µL, <4000/µL, or >10% bands], and (3) evidence of acute OD. Organ dysfunction was defined by at least one of the following: lactate >2 mmol/L, hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg, mean arterial pressure <65 mmHg, or systolic blood pressure decrease >40 mmHg from baseline), acute respiratory failure requiring invasive or noninvasive mechanical ventilation, creatinine >2 mg/dL, urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for ≥2 hours, bilirubin >2 mg/dL, platelet count <100 000/µL, or coagulation abnormalities (INR >1.5 or aPTT >60 s). Chronic conditions or medication-related abnormalities were not considered as qualifying evidence of OD. The criterion for septic shock is met if it is explicitly stated in the encounter note or when severe sepsis is accompanied by either (1) serum lactate ≥4 mmol/L or (2) persistent hypotension within 1 hour following a 30 mL/kg crystalloid fluid bolus.

Sepsis t0 algorithm

The workflow for the sepsis t0 algorithm is shown in Figure 2. Sepsis cases were identified using the following inclusion criteria: (1) Inpatient or ED encounters, (2) Age ≥18, and (3) Primary or secondary billing ICD-10 sepsis codes as defined by CMS specifications Appendix A: A02.1, A22.7, A24.1, A26.7, A31.2, A32.7, A40.0, A40.1, A40.3, A40.8, A40.9, A41.01, A41.02, A41.1, A41.2, A41.3, A41.4, A41.50, A41.51, A41.52, A41.53, A41.54, A41.59, A41.81, A41.89, A41.9, A42.7, A54.86, R65.20, and R65.21.

Workflow diagram of the sepsis algorithm.

As indicated in Figure 2, the data extraction was decoupled from the algorithm, enabling the algorithm to process data from any source. In this implementation, the structured data and unstructured notes were extracted from the Epic Clarity database across the entire duration of the inclusion encounters. The output of the extraction process was a combination of CSV and plain text files, which served as the input to the algorithm.

The algorithm consists of 3 main components. First, all clinical notes for all encounters were processed using a set of manually refined regular expressions (RegEx) (available upon request) to identify septic shock, severe sepsis, or physician documentation of suspected infection. The timestamp for DOI was based on the time when the note was created rather than the time that may have been documented by the physician within the note. Second, outputs from the structured data fields and regular expressions were integrated to generate an encounter-specific event timeline. The algorithm pseudocode and the generated timeline produced by the algorithm are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Figures S1 and S2).

Results

Proxies for documentation of infection

Detection of infection documentation in EMR is challenging because it requires analysis of unstructured clinical notes. Prior studies have relied on proxy markers of infection, such as antibiotic or culture orders.9 During the development of the DOI component of the algorithm, we focused on a subset of 516 abstracted cases (Figure 1) to develop and refine the RegEx patterns from the notes and test the impact of using proxies (antibiotic or culture orders) on the accuracy of t0 detection (Figure 3). The algorithm t0 exactly matched the abstractor-recorded t0 in 41.9% of cases when DOI was extracted solely from the clinical notes. When culture or antibiotic orders were used separately as proxies for suspected infection, concordance with abstractor t0 was 34.7% and 27.9%, respectively. Combining all 3 sources achieved a concordance of 44.4%, which was marginally better than using the clinical notes alone. Notably, all 3 sources, separately or combined identified t0 earlier than abstractors, ranging from 26.9% to 38.4% of cases.

Evaluation of algorithm performance

First, we examined the performance of the algorithm for each component of the SEP-1 t0 criteria (Table 1). On average, there was no significant difference in the time of OD identified by the algorithm and by human abstractors. In contrast, SIRS time was detected significantly later by the algorithm compared to abstractors. Notably, the algorithm identified DOI significantly earlier than the abstractors, resulting in a significantly earlier t0.

Using the final analytical sample of the 2030 cases, the algorithm t0 exactly matched the abstractor t0 in 1372 (67.6%) cases, but was earlier in 506 (24.9%) cases and later in 152 (7.5%) cases (Figure 4A). To determine the potential causes for the discrepancies in t0, we randomly selected 36 earlier cases and 11 later cases for manual evaluation (Figure 4B). Overall, the false positive rate of the algorithm was 4.2% (2/47) and the false negative rate was 23.4% (11/47). The false negatives corresponding to the later t0 were due to missing septic shock in the notes (5), evidence of infection in imaging data (3), missing infection documentation in the notes (2), or an unclear abstraction source (1). In the cases where the algorithm detected an earlier t0, 15 (31.9%) of the cases were because suspicion of infection was missed by the abstractors, 13 (27.6%) were because of earlier culture orders, and 6 (12.8%) were due to earlier antibiotic orders.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that defining sepsis t0 with an automated, EMR-based algorithm that incorporates both structured data and clinical notes can identify sepsis more accurately than structured data alone. In this study, the algorithm identified t0 earlier or at the same time as abstractors when DOI was based on clinical notes compared with culture or antibiotic orders (72.3% vs 61.6% and 50.0%, respectively). While proxies such as culture or antibiotic orders capture most cases, augmenting them with information from clinical notes produces more realistic t0 determinations at scale.

Large multihospital evaluations have reported increased lactate testing and changes in treatment patterns after SEP-1 implementation but no improvement in short-term mortality.7,8 However, these studies operationalized t0 and suspicion of infection primarily from structured EMR data (eg, culture orders). Repeating such analyses with t0 definitions that incorporate free-text documentation could clarify whether measure timing, rather than the bundle itself, contributes to the null findings.11 If earlier, documentation-informed t0 demonstrates stronger associations between timely care and outcomes, this would support refining both surveillance and quality measures toward more clinically grounded anchors. Earlier t0 is clinically meaningful, as delays in antibiotic therapy and completion of early bundle elements are associated with higher mortality.12,13

In our study, using clinical notes led to significantly earlier identification of sepsis t0 compared with abstractors. This finding reflects that suspicion of infection is often documented before orders for cultures or antibiotics are placed. Earlier detection also partly reflects the algorithm’s ability to overcome human error. For example, Figure S1 illustrates a 4.5-hour discrepancy between algorithm- and abstractor-determined t0, which resulted from missed documentation in a note. Such errors are expected when manual abstraction requires review of large volumes of clinical text. From an operational standpoint, our work demonstrates a path toward more efficient core measure reporting. SEP-1 abstraction is labor-intensive and vulnerable to inter-abstractor variability, particularly around time-zero determination.10 Having an algorithm-derived timeline for each sepsis case could improve both the efficiency and accuracy of human abstraction.

Automated, note-aware identification of t0 also enables larger-scale, more granular studies of sepsis epidemiology and outcomes. By anchoring encounters at a clinically relevant t0, researchers can more reliably examine how patient factors, care processes, and system features influence trajectories and outcomes across diverse populations and settings. Automated extraction of t0 and related bundle elements can further support surveillance, feedback, and targeted training by hospital Quality & Safety teams as emphasized in national guidance for hospital sepsis programs.3

The algorithm did not significantly differ from abstractors in detecting OD, but it did identify SIRS criteria later. This delay is largely attributable to emergency medical services (EMS) documentation of prehospital vitals into scanned documents, which are not accessible to the algorithm in its current form. Performance could be improved by incorporating this information; however, reliable extraction from scanned documents remains a technical challenge despite advances in optical character recognition (OCR) and natural language processing (NLP).14 Moreover, EMS data integration into hospital EMRs remains inconsistent and unreliable.15

Other limitations of our study include the reliance on rule-based regular expressions to identify suspicion of infection within clinical notes. Such approaches are inherently brittle and may not generalize well across different note types or documentation styles. Future research should evaluate the transferability of the NLP algorithm across other health systems and directly compare its performance with that of large language models, which are expected to offer greater flexibility in detecting suspicion of infection from physician notes. However, LLM processing time, consistency, and reliability are still a major concern for large-scale clinical applications. In addition, the current algorithm uses the timestamp for when the note was created, rather than extracting times documented within the note. This may result in some variability between when the physician suspected infection and when the note was created in the EMR. Lastly, our work was conducted within a single health system. However, the dataset encompassed 8 hospitals in distinct geographic locations and diverse patient populations, partially mitigating this concern.

Conclusion

Our algorithm integrates structured data and clinical notes to identify sepsis t0 more precisely than proxies alone. Overall, it detected DOI significantly earlier than human abstractors. Scalable automation can reduce abstractor burden and human error, strengthen sepsis surveillance, and enable robust research to improve patient care.
