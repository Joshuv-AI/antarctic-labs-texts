Noncoding RNA family classification based on multifeature fusion and convolutional block attention residual network
Authors: Qian Xu, Feifei Li, Guosheng Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the development of sequencing technology, it has been confirmed that many noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) can participate in various biological processes. Although significant progress has been made in the classification of ncRNA families, numerous challenges remain. On the one hand, accurately characterizing the biological properties of ncRNAs presents a technical challenge, which includes not only information about the sequence itself but also structural information of ncRNAs. On the other hand, different types of ncRNAs exhibit significant differences in sequence length, structure, and function. Accurate classification by extracting valid information from sequences remains a challenging problem. To address these issues, we propose a novel 3D graphical representation method based on the Z-curve and chaos game representation of RNA secondary structure to mine the underlying information of bases. This 3D graphical representation method converts RNA secondary structures into a set of 3D graphical representation of sequences under different base classifications. Furthermore, we have verified the effectiveness of this 3D graphical representation method on viral sequence datasets. Subsequently, we propose an ncRNA family classification model called nRMFCA, based on multifeature fusion and convolutional block attention residual networks. In this study, nRMFCA was compared with previously proposed ncRNA family prediction methods using two commonly used public ncRNA datasets, NCY and nRC. The results demonstrate that nRMFCA outperforms other prediction methods on both datasets. Overall, by integrating a novel 3D graphical representation method with a multifeature fusion-based convolutional block attention residual network, the nRMFCA model achieves better classification of ncRNA families, providing a powerful tool for in-depth research on ncRNAs.

Introduction

Noncoding RNAs (ncRNA) typically refer to RNA molecules that do not encode proteins [1]. It has been confirmed that many ncRNAs can participate in various biological processes, including regulating gene expression, RNA splicing, and maintaining chromosome structure [2]. Furthermore, ncRNAs play crucial roles in many disease processes. For example, long ncRNAs can influence the expression of tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes, and are closely associated with the occurrence, development, and metastasis of cancers [3].

In the study of ncRNA classification, existing identification methods are mainly divided into two categories: biological experiment-based methods and computation-based methods [4]. RNA-CODE [5] represents a typical biological experiment-based approach for ncRNA identification. Another model, RNAC [6], extracts statistical features, local binary patterns, and histogram features based on alignment-type genomic descriptors, then constructs a classification model using gradient boosting algorithms. RNA sequencing methods can be widely used for classification, quantification, and functional studies of ncRNAs. However, the requirement for expensive equipment and substantial computational resources may lead to high costs. To better adapt to high-throughput technology development and reduce research costs, efficient computational methods have emerged as an essential need of our time.

The primary objective of mining ncRNA sequence information is to fully utilize the original base information of sequences and perform classification within a deep learning framework. The RPC-snRC model proposed by Asim et al. [7] is a deep learning classification tool based on the Residual Network (ResNet) architecture [8]. This model directly extracts nucleotide positional information from sequences and learns hierarchical representations of features. The ncRDeep model proposed by Chantsalnyam et al. [9] is a simple yet efficient tool that encodes sequences through one-hot encoding, followed by feature extraction and classification using convolutional neural networks (CNNs). Wang et al. [10] proposed the NcRFP model, which predicts ncRNA families by automatically extracting features from sequences. The ncDENSE model proposed by Chen et al. [11] is a deep learning network based on one-hot encoding. Subsequently, Chen et al. [12] also proposed MFPred, a multifeature fusion method for ncRNA family classification.

With the rapid development of graph neural networks, graph features have also become an effective method for extracting structural information, as graph can better capture the complex interactions between bases of RNA molecules [13]. Rossi et al. [14] proposed an end-to-end convolutional network that first implemented graph convolution in RNA folding data. The Spike2CGR model [15] converts synaptic sequences into images as input for deep learning and demonstrates the effectiveness of this approach. The RNAGCN model, proposed by Deng et al. [16], is an end-to-end graph convolutional network (GCN) model that takes folded ncRNA sequence graphs as input and performs classification by extracting graph features.

In this study, we first propose a novel 3D graphical representation method. This approach can convert RNA sequences into a set of 3D graphs, visually displaying sequence morphologies under different base classification methods to facilitate better feature extraction. Secondly, we propose a new model called nRMFCA to address the classification problem of ncRNA families by multifeature fusion and convolutional block attention residual nework. Convolutional block attention residual network CBAM-ResNet is constructed by introducing a convolutional block attention module (CBAM) [17] into the residual network, and CBAM-ResNet can focus more effectively on important feature channels, thereby identifying key discriminative information in ncRNA sequences. Finally, we conducted comprehensive experimental evaluations of the nRMFCA model. The results across various metrics demonstrate that our model exhibits superior classification performance compared with previous approaches.

Materials and methods

Data preparation

We primarily used the nRC and NCY datasets. The nRC dataset, proposed by Fiannaca et al. [18], contains 13 classes of ncRNAs: 5S_rRNA, 5.8S_rRNA, tRNA, Ribozyme, CD-box, miRNA, Intron_gpI, Intron_gpII, HACA-box, Riboswitch, Leader, IRES, and scaRNA, totaling 8920 ncRNA sequences. The training set consists of 6320 ncRNA sequences, with 320 data samples from the IRES family and 500 data samples each from the remaining 12 families. Correspondingly, the test set contains 2600 ncRNA sequences, with 200 data samples from each family. Some sequences in the dataset contain the base letter N, which we replaced randomly with one of the four standard bases.

The NCY dataset was proposed by Noviello et al. [19], who further removed data samples exceeding 150 bases in length and excluded categories with fewer than 400 samples. The final dataset contains 162 608 sequences divided into 29 categories. Based on Noviello’s dataset, Chen et al. selected 45 447 sequences from 13 categories to form the NCY dataset, which is an imbalanced dataset.

The nine viral datasets analyzed in this study were obtained from the research work of Feng et al. [20], including the following viruses: Alfalfa mosaic virus (AIMV-3), Citrus leaf rugose virus (CiLRV-3), Tobacco streak virus (TSV-3), Citrus variegation virus (CVV-3), Apple mosaic virus (APMV-3), Prunus necrotic ringspot virus (PDV-3), Lilac ring mottle virus (LRMV-3), Elm mottle virus (EMV-3), and Asparagus virus II (AVII).

Evaluation criteria and experimental setup

To comprehensively evaluate the prediction accuracy of the nRMFCA model for ncRNA categories, we adopted specificity, precision, recall, F1-score, and MCC as evaluation metrics, which are specifically defined as follows:

TP (True Positive) refers to samples correctly predicted as positive by the model, FP (False Positive) refers to negative samples incorrectly predicted as positive by the model. TN (True Negative) refers to samples correctly predicted as negative by the model, FN (False Negative) refers to positive samples incorrectly predicted as negative by the model.

Three-dimensional graphical representation of RNA secondary structure based on chaos game representation and Z-curve

Chaos game representation

The chaos game representation (CGR) is a method for representing sequence data using iterated function systems [21]. The core principle of CGR employs the attractor concept from chaos theory to map biological sequences onto a 2D plane [22]. In this method, each symbol in a biological sequence corresponds to a specific point on the plane. Through iterative generation of new points from sequence symbols, these points collectively form patterns that reveal the sequence’s intrinsic structural regularities. This representation approach was first introduced by Jeffrey in 1990 [21] for DNA sequence analysis and visualization. The method assigns the four nucleotides to the vertices of a square: adenine (A) to point (0,0), cytosine (C) to (0,1), guanine (G) to (1,1), and thymine (T) to (1,0). For a DNA sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $S$\end{document} of length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n$\end{document}, the process initiates from a starting point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P_{0} = (x_{0}, y_{0})$\end{document}, which may be any planar point but is conventionally set at the square’s center (0.5, 0.5). For each nucleotide in the sequence, its corresponding vertex \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $V$\end{document} (A, C, G, or T) is identified. Given the current point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P_{n} = (x_{n}, y_{n})$\end{document}, the subsequent point is computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P_{n + 1} = (P_{n} + V)/2$\end{document}.

This equation generates the midpoint between the current point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P_{n}$\end{document} and the selected vertex \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $V$\end{document}. Consequently, each nucleotide is mapped to a uniquely corresponding point on the plane, and the complete sequence is represented by this set of points. Subsequently, Feng et al. [20] proposed an innovative 3D graphical representation of RNA secondary structures, which simultaneously integrates sequence information with the chemical properties of RNA secondary structures. Based on hydrogen bond strength, the four nucleotides can be categorized as weak-bonding bases W = {A, U} and strong-bonding bases S = {G, C}; by functional group classification, they are divided into amino bases M = {A, C} and ketone bases K = {G, U}. Structurally, they may be categorized as purine bases R = {A, G} and pyrimidine bases Y = {C, U}. Based on these three classification methods, the bases in a sequence are iteratively processed through coordinate calculation formulas to generate coordinate matrices, ultimately forming 3D representations of the sequences: the W-S 3D graph, the M-K 3D graph, and the R-Y 3D graph.

The W–S classification maps bases to tetrahedral vertices via vertex assignment: unpaired strong-bonding bases are assigned to the proximal vertex (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $z = 1$\end{document}), while paired weak-bonding bases are mapped to the distal vertex (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $z = -1$\end{document}), thereby directly encoding the stability basis of RNA secondary structures. The M–K classification likewise uses the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $z$\end{document}-axis to distinguish paired and unpaired states. The R–Y classification divides bases into purines and pyrimidines, with variations in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $x$\end{document}/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $y$\end{document} coordinates reflecting differences in molecular size—smooth curves represent stable R–Y alternating stacks, while jagged curves correspond to conformationally distorted loop regions, collectively encoding spatial structural features. By adopting a regular tetrahedral vertex layout and iterative coordinate calculation, and using uppercase letters (A, G, C, U) for unpaired bases and lowercase letters (a, g, c, u) for paired bases, these methods provide integrated “sequence-structure-function” feature support for the nRMFCA model, enabling accurate classification. The Z-curve Formulas

The Z-curve [23] theory is a mathematical approach for analyzing DNA, RNA, and protein sequences. It represents nucleic acid and protein sequences as points in geometric space, enabling the analysis of their characteristics and patterns through geometric and mathematical means. The Z-curve is a 3D spatial curve that encodes biological sequences into curves, thereby revealing specific sequence attributes such as nucleotide composition, distribution, and potential biological functions [24]. Certain geometric representations of RNA symmetry can be identified through their division into triplets [25].

Three-dimensional graphical representation

CGR generates fractal structures through iterative function systems, preserving both local and global features of the sequence, while the Z-curve retains sequence information by accumulating 3D spatial coordinates. This study proposes an innovative 3D graphical representation method that transforms iterative coordinate formulas into the form of a Z-curve while also applying the concept of attractors from chaos theory to map RNA sequences onto a 2D plane. Based on the principle of the Z-curve, sequences are mapped onto three regular tetrahedrons, and spatial positional relationships are visualized through the distance between the curve and the vertices of the tetrahedrons.

In RNA secondary structure analysis, distinguishing between paired and unpaired bases is crucial. Therefore, the original four-base system {A, C, G, U} is extended to an eight-base system {A, C, G, U, a, c, g, u}, where uppercase letters denote unpaired bases and lowercase letters represent paired bases. Sequence representations

To further extract features from sequences, we employed additional encoding methods: k-mer, GCN, word2vec, and 3D graphical representation.

k-mer encoding: k-mer is one of the commonly used encoding methods that extracts local sequence information through sliding windows with width k. The empirically validated effective is 3 [4], Moreover, the choice of k=3 aligns well with our multifeature fusion architecture, ensuring consistency across different encoding methods at the sequence level while providing appropriate feature complexity for the CBAM attention mechanism. Consequently, we adopted the trinucleotide encoding for sequence feature representation. For RNA sequences composed of four nucleotides (A, G, C, U), each base is converted into a vector via one-hot encoding. Therefore, when using k-mer encoding, a ncRNA sequence of length n can be encoded as a 2D array with the dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $(n-2, 12)$\end{document}.

GCN encoding: GCN [27] encoding is a method that transforms input sequences into graph representations by constructing node and edge features, thereby converting sequences into graphs [28]. We set the output channels of GCN to 16, so each node and edge can output a vector of length 16. For each ncRNA sequence of length n, after GCN processing, we ultimately obtain a 2D array of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $(n-2, 32)$\end{document}, reflecting the global features of the ncRNA sequence.

Word2vec encoding: Word2vec [29] is a technique for generating word vectors that learns vector representations of words by predicting their contexts. Word2vec has two main models: CBOW and Skip-gram. The CBOW model learns word vector representations by predicting the current word from its context, while the Skip-gram model learns by predicting the context words given a target word. We employ the CBOW model to encode subsequences, where a sequence of length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n$\end{document} can be divided into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n-2$\end{document} triplet nucleotides. To predict the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $m$\end{document}th subsequence, the model utilizes context vectors. By setting the vector dimension parameter to 100, each sequence of length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n$\end{document} generates a 2D array of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $(n-2, 100)$\end{document} after training.

3D-base encoding: building upon the previously proposed 3D graphical representation learning method, sequences are transformed into 3D graphs where each nucleotide is assigned corresponding coordinates within a tetrahedral framework. Assuming the nucleotide coordinates are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $(X_{1}, Y_{1}, Z_{1})$\end{document} in the W-S 3D graph, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $(X_{2}, Y_{2}, Z_{2})$\end{document} in the M-K 3D graph, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $(X_{3}, Y_{3}, Z_{3})$\end{document} in the R-Y 3D graph, each nucleotide can then be represented as a 9D vector: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $[X_{1}, Y_{1}, Z_{1}, X_{2}, Y_{2}, Z_{2}, X_{3}, Y_{3}, Z_{3}]$\end{document}. Experiments and results

Experimental setup

To evaluate model performance, a combined approach of 10-fold training-validation (10-CV) and independent testing was adopted. The dataset was partitioned in a 9:1 ratio, with 90% allocated to the training-validation set—ensuring coverage of all sample categories—and the remaining 10% reserved as an independent test set. The cross-validation set was used for model training and hyperparameter tuning, while the independent test set was exclusively employed to assess the model’s generalization capability, with a strict separation between training and testing data to prevent overlap.

In terms of model performance comparison, the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) and the area under the precision-recall (PR) curve (AUPR) are comprehensive metrics to evaluate performance in binary classification problems. In addition, we also use other classic metrics, such as precision, specificity, F1-score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC).

Comparison of different models

The overall performance of nRMFCA is assessed by comparing it with six baseline methods in prediction: ncDENSE [11], ncRFP [23], NCYPred [10], ncDLRES [16], Evo [32], and MMnc [33]. The above models are evaluated using 10-CV on the same two benchmark datasets.

As shown in Tables 4 and 5, the nRMFCA model outperforms other RNA family classification models on nearly all evaluation metrics across both benchmark datasets. For instance, on the NCY dataset, the nRMFCA model achieved a top Precision of 0.97, surpassing the ncDENSE [11], ncRFP [23], NCYPred [10], ncDLRES [16], Evo [32], and MMnc [33] models by margins of 1%, 6%, 5%, 3%, 2%, and 1%. On the nRC dataset, it attained a best Precision of 0.94, exceeding the same set of models by 7%, 15%, 6%, 10%, 3%, and 2%. These results underscore the superior performance of the nRMFCA model.

These results collectively demonstrate that nRMFCA achieves a significant breakthrough in ncRNA family classification accuracy by integrating 3D graphical representation into multifeature fusion and convolutional block attention residual network. The model’s excellent performance across different ncRNA families, combined with its ability to capture complex structural features, makes it a powerful tool for computational RNA analysis.

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we propose nRMFCA, a novel classification model for ncRNA families that integrates 3D graphical representations of RNA sequences with a convolutional block attention-enhanced residual network architecture. This approach effectively addresses key challenges in ncRNA family classification, including insufficient feature extraction, limited representation capacity, and the complexity of multifeature fusion. Our method demonstrates robust discriminative capabilities, strong adaptability to diverse datasets such as nRC and NCY, and promising potential for applications in ncRNA functional studies and disease mechanism exploration.

We developed an innovative nucleotide encoding method named 3D-base, which integrates k-mer frequency, GCNs, and word2vec-based embedding strategies. Building upon this foundation, the nRMFCA model enhances its representational capabilities through multifeature fusion and the CBAM. The model first processes sequence features through a BiGRU) and an attention-based residual network, followed by final classification via the CBAM-ResNet module. Experimental results demonstrate outstanding performance on both nRC and NCY datasets, achieving an average prediction accuracy of 94% on nRC. This surpasses all baseline methods, including the multimodal interpretable approach MMnc and the large-scale foundational model Evo. Ablation studies further confirm that the CBAM attention mechanism and 3D foundational encoding strategy significantly contribute to model performance, highlighting their crucial role in feature refinement and fusion.

Despite these promising results, several limitations of the current approach warrant discussion and point toward avenues for future improvement. In terms of sequence encoding, the current word2vec-derived embeddings rely on a predefined vocabulary and may not fully capture nucleotide-level contextual semantics. Subsequent work could incorporate advanced pretrained genomic language models, such as the recently proposed Evo model by Hie et al. [36]—a foundational model specifically designed for large-scale genomic sequence analysis. This innovative framework combines CNNs with Transformer architectures, enabling efficient modeling of megabase-scale sequences while circumventing the computational constraints of standard Transformers. It systematically addresses challenges related to long-range dependency capture, cross-species generalization, and practical deployment, offering considerable potential for enhancing ncRNA representation learning.

Furthermore, the current evaluation is limited to two public datasets. Future studies should validate the model on broader datasets encompassing more ncRNA families to strengthen generalizability. Although the proposed 3D graphical representation shows encouraging results, its ability to capture high-order structural and functional features merits deeper investigation. Additionally, the development of a user-friendly online web server could greatly facilitate widespread adoption by providing researchers with accessible tools for ncRNA family classification and predictive analysis, thereby accelerating discovery in related biological and clinical studies.

In summary, the 3D graphical representation method developed in this study, combined with the nRMFCA model, demonstrates high efficiency in the classification of ncRNA families. Future optimizations aimed at addressing current limitations will further enhance the classification performance and expand its application prospects in ncRNA research.
