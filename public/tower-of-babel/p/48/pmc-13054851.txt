Prevalence of Traditional East Asian Medicine Patterns among Older Adults with Frailty: a systematic review of observational studies
Authors: Chan-Young Kwon, Boram Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrailty is a common geriatric syndrome that increases vulnerability to adverse health outcomes. Traditional East Asian medicine (TEAM) provides a distinct framework for understanding frailty through syndrome differentiation; however, the prevalence and distribution of TEAM patterns in frail populations remain unclear. This review aimed to systematically evaluate and meta-analyze the prevalence of TEAM patterns in frail older adults. A comprehensive search of five electronic databases was conducted through May 2025. Observational studies reporting TEAM patterns in adults aged ≥ 60 years with validated frailty diagnoses were included. Two independent reviewers screened studies, extracted data, and assessed methodological quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Random-effects meta-analysis was used to pool prevalence estimates. Eleven observational studies involving 3,345 participants met the inclusion criteria. All studies were conducted in China; no eligible studies from Korea or Japan were identified. Methodological quality was generally low, and no study was rated as high quality. Deficiency-related TEAM patterns predominated. The most frequently reported patterns were qi-blood deficiency (exploratory pooled prevalence: 24.0%) and kidney essence deficiency (24.0%), followed by spleen deficiency with phlegm-dampness (23.0%). Liver-kidney yin deficiency had a pooled prevalence of 26.0%, based on two studies. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 > 80%), and overall methodological limitations reduce the precision and generalizability of these findings. Syndrome differentiation was conducted by TEAM practitioners using the four diagnostic methods, consistently incorporating tongue and pulse examination; however, diagnostic criteria varied among studies. Age, comorbidities, and nutritional markers were associated with frailty. Exploratory analysis suggests frail older adults predominantly exhibit deficiency-related patterns, particularly qi-blood deficiency, kidney essence deficiency, and spleen deficiency with phlegm-dampness. However, the low methodological quality, substantial heterogeneity, and restriction to Chinese populations indicate that these findings should be considered preliminary and require confirmation through rigorously designed studies.Keywords: frailty, traditional East Asian medicine, pattern identification, older adults, systematic review

INTRODUCTION

Frailty is a complex, multidimensional geriatric syndrome characterized by decreased physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, including disability, hospitalization, and mortality [1]. This condition markedly impacts the quality of life of older adults and places a substantial burden on healthcare systems. Its prevalence increases with age, affecting approximately 15% of community-dwelling older adults and reaching higher rates among populations with chronic diseases or institutionalized settings [2]. As the global population ages rapidly, understanding, preventing, and managing frailty has become a critical public health priority [3]. Addressing this challenge requires comprehensive approaches that extend beyond conventional symptom management to target underlying systemic imbalances [4].

The conventional Western medical approach primarily focuses on specific pathophysiological mechanisms of disease. However, frailty, with its complex and systemic nature, often necessitates a more holistic understanding [4]. Traditional East Asian medicine (TEAM), encompassing Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), Korean Medicine (KM), and traditional Japanese medicine (Kampo), provides a holistic paradigm for understanding health and disease through the concept of syndrome differentiation [5]. TEAM is particularly relevant for managing complex conditions involving multiple system dysfunction. For example, patients with cancer frequently develop treatment-related frailty following chemotherapy due to systemic toxicity and metabolic disruption [6]. Similarly, studies in KM have demonstrated that individual constitutional types may influence disease susceptibility and patterns of frailty development [7]. While Western medicine often focuses on isolated components, TEAM emphasizes the identification of patterns derived from a comprehensive assessment of an individual’s symptoms, signs, and constitutional characteristics [8]. This integrative perspective accounts for the interplay of various physiological systems, which is particularly relevant to the multifaceted presentation of frailty [9].

Within TEAM theory, aging and frailty are fundamentally linked to the decline of kidney essence, which can manifest as various kidney-related syndrome patterns, including kidney yang deficiency, kidney yin deficiency, and kidney qi deficiency [10, 11]. However, frail older adults often present with diverse patterns, such as qi deficiency, blood deficiency, and phlegm-dampness, reflecting the multifaceted clinical presentation of frailty [11]. Accurate differentiation of these patterns is crucial for guiding effective individualized treatment strategies within TEAM [12]. Nevertheless, a standardized syndrome differentiation system specifically addressing frailty is currently lacking. This absence contributes to fragmented and inconsistent empirical evidence regarding the prevalence and distribution of specific patterns. Additionally, regional differences in diagnostic approaches among TCM, KM, and Kampo remain largely unexplored. Given that syndrome differentiation inherently involves clustering of symptoms and signs [12], understanding predominant patterns is essential for elucidating the complex nature of frailty.

A comprehensive understanding of predominant patterns in frail older adults is needed for several reasons. First, it provides evidence-based validation for TEAM’s theoretical foundations, enabling more precise clinical applications. Second, identifying common patterns is critical for developing standardized, geriatric-specific assessment tools that enhance diagnostic consistency across clinical settings. Finally, this foundational knowledge supports the design of truly personalized treatment strategies based on individual syndrome patterns to improve clinical outcomes in frail older adults.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to address these gaps by comprehensively synthesizing existing evidence on the prevalence of TEAM patterns in frail older adults and by exploring potential regional variations across TCM, KM, and Kampo traditions.

METHODS

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement (Supplementary File 1) [13]. The detailed protocol for this review was prospectively registered on Open Science Framework (OSF) Registries (Registration ID: osf.io/z2kpf) before the commencement of the literature search.

1. Search strategy and information sources

A comprehensive literature search was performed across five electronic databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE (via Ovid), Oriental Medicine Advanced Searching Integrated System (OASIS), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Citation Information by NII (CiNii). The search included all publications from database inception through May 2025 (Supplementary File 2). No language restrictions were applied for publications in Chinese, Korean, Japanese, or English. Database-specific search terms and syntaxes were adapted for each platform to optimize retrieval. Additional sources were consulted to minimize the risk of missing relevant studies, including reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews identified during the search.

2. Eligibility criteria

1) Inclusion criteria

(1) Population: Adults aged ≥ 60 years diagnosed with frailty using established and validated criteria (e.g., Fried Frailty Phenotype, FRAIL scale, Clinical Frailty Scale, or other validated instruments); (2) Intervention/Exposure: TEAM syndrome differentiation; (3) Outcomes: Identification of specific syndrome types/patterns and clear reporting of prevalence; (4) Study Design: Observational studies, including cross-sectional, prospective cohort, or retrospective cohort designs; (5) Publication Period: No restrictions; and (6) Language Publications in Chinese, Korean, Japanese, or English were included.

2) Exclusion criteria

(1) Animal or in vitro, review, editorial, conference abstract without full data, or case studies/series; (2) Intervention studies; (3) Studies not focused on frailty, not exclusively targeting older adults, or combining frailty with other conditions without separate data; (4) Studies lacking validated frailty diagnostic criteria, not investigating syndrome patterns, or not reporting prevalence; (5) Studies with unclear age criteria or that did not distinguish frail from pre-frail participants; and (6) Duplicate publications were excluded.

3. Study selection process

Two independent reviewers conducted study selection in a structured, two-stage approach. In the first stage, all retrieved citations were screened by title and abstract against the predefined eligibility criteria. In the second stage, full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and assessed for final inclusion. Any disagreements between the two independent reviewers were resolved through discussion.

4. Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was developed and piloted on five randomly selected included studies to ensure completeness and clarity. Data extraction from all included studies was conducted independently by two reviewers, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion to achieve consensus. The following information was systematically extracted: (1) Study characteristics: Author(s), year of publication, country of origin, study design, sample size, and study period; (2) Participant characteristics: Age, sex distribution, frailty assessment method, reported frailty severity, and common comorbidities; (3) Syndrome differentiation methods: Assessment tools used, practitioners qualifications, diagnostic criteria, and the overall differentiation system applied; (4) Syndrome patterns: Identified patterns, and their reported prevalence (absolute counts and percentages); (5) Statistical analysis details: Use of multivariable analysis and significant associated factors; and (6) Methodological details: Components relevant to quality assessment, declared conflicts of interest, and study limitations.

5. Quality assessment

Methodological quality of each included observational study was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [14], adapted for cross-sectional studies. Each study was evaluated across three domains: Selection (maximum 3 points), Comparability (maximum 2 points), and Outcome (maximum 2 points), for a total of 7 points. Studies scoring 7 points were considered “good quality” based on established criteria [15]. Discrepancies in quality scores were resolved through discussion.

6. Data analysis

Despite anticipated heterogeneity and methodological limitations, meta-analysis was considered appropriate for several reasons. First, quantitative pooling provides exploratory estimates of pattern prevalence, informing hypothesis generation and future research priorities. Second, meta-analysis allows systematic identification of predominant patterns, which is difficult to achieve through narrative synthesis alone. Third, formal quantification of heterogeneity using the I2 statistic provides insights into variability in diagnosis across studies and highlights areas for standardization. Random-effects meta-analysis using inverse-variance weighting was conducted to pool prevalence estimates for each syndrome pattern. The random-effects model was selected a priori to accommodate expected heterogeneity arising from differences in diagnostic approaches, practitioner qualifications, and patient populations. Individual study proportions were transformed using the Freeman-Tukey method to normalize distributions and stabilize variance. Pooled prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated and visually represented in forest plots. Meta-analyses were conducted using Stata statistical software (Version STATA 13, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) with the metaprop command. Studies with incomplete or insufficient prevalence data were excluded from quantitative synthesis but included in the qualitative review.

Between-study heterogeneity was quantitatively assessed using both the I2 statistic and the Cochran’s Q-test. Substantial heterogeneity was defined as an I2 value greater than 50%. In instances of significant heterogeneity, potential sources were explored through comprehensive subgroup analyses and meta-regression, provided a sufficient number of studies were available. Pre-specified subgroup analyses included key characteristics expected to influence the distribution of syndrome pattern: geographic region (China, Korea, Japan) and methodological quality score (high vs. moderate/low).

7. Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots, contingent on the availability of ≥ 10 studies for meta-analysis for each syndrome pattern.

RESULTS

1. Study selection

The systematic search identified 1,539 records across all databases. After removing duplicates, 1,430 unique records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 59 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. A total of 48 studies were excluded for the following reasons: conference abstracts without sufficient details (n = 4), non-observational study designs (n = 10), studies not focused on frailty (n = 22), studies combining frailty with other conditions without separate data (n = 1), studies not exclusively targeting older adults (n = 2), studies lacking validated frailty diagnostic criteria (n = 2), studies not investigating syndrome patterns (n = 1), studies not reporting pattern prevalence (n = 4), studies with unclear age criteria for ≥ 60 years (n = 1), and studies including frail populations without distinguishing pre-frail participants (n = 1) (Supplementary File 3). Ultimately, 11 studies were included in this systematic review [16-26]. The PRISMA flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1.

PRISMA flow diagram.

2. Study characteristics

The 11 included studies were published between 2017 and 2024 and encompassed 3,345 participants with frailty. All studies were conducted in China and exclusively examined TCM syndrome patterns, despite the original intention to include studies from Korea and Japan to represent diverse TEAM practices, including KM and Kampo. Sample sizes ranged from 61 to 666 participants (median: 189). The mean age of participants ranged from 72.0 to 84.4 years across studies that reported this information. Female participants comprised 39.5% to 63.9% of the study populations. Nine studies (81.8%) used the Fried Frailty Phenotype as the primary diagnostic criterion [16-20, 23-26], one study employed the Frailty Index for Integrative Medicine (FI-IM) [21], and another used the Clinical Frailty Scale-09 (CFS-09) [22]. All included studies used cross-sectional designs. Study periods ranged from 2016 to 2023, with most data collection occurring between 2018 and 2021 (Table 1).

3. Prevalence of syndrome patterns

Exploratory prevalence estimates were calculated, and predominant patterns were identified despite methodological heterogeneity. Formal meta-analysis was conducted for TEAM syndrome patterns reported using mutually exclusive diagnostic categories. Tang (2021) [19] was excluded from quantitative analysis because it reported complex or concurrent syndrome patterns (e.g., qi deficiency with blood stasis, qi-yin deficiency), which differed from the single primary syndrome classifications used in other included studies. The meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model with a Freeman-Tukey transformation to pool prevalence for six distinct TEAM syndrome patterns among frail older adults. Results are summarized below and presented visually in Fig. 2.

Meta-analysis results.

1) Qi-blood deficiency

Seven studies reported qi-blood deficiency [16, 18, 20, 23-26]. The exploratory pooled prevalence was 0.24 (24.0%; 95% CI: 0.17-0.32). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (heterogeneity statistic [I2] = 93.30%, p < 0.001). Individual study prevalence ranged from 0.08 [24] to 0.40 [16].

2) Kidney essence deficiency

Five studies reported kidney essence deficiency [16, 20, 23, 24, 26]. The exploratory pooled prevalence was 0.24 (24.0%; 95% CI: 0.11-0.42). Heterogeneity was high (I2 = 96.50%, p < 0.001). Individual study prevalence ranged from 0.08 [23] to 0.52 [24].

3) Spleen-kidney yang deficiency

Seven studies contributed data for spleen-kidney yang deficiency [16, 18, 20, 23-26]. The pooled prevalence was 0.19 (19.0%; 95% CI: 0.15-0.25). Significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 85.59%, p < 0.001). Individual study prevalence ranged from 0.10 [16] to 0.27 [18, 25].

4) Spleen deficiency with phlegm-dampness

Seven studies reported on spleen deficiency with phlegm-dampness [16, 18, 20, 23-26]. The exploratory pooled prevalence was 0.23 (23.0%; 95% CI: 0.18-0.27). Heterogeneity was high (I2 = 80.91%, p < 0.001). Individual study prevalence ranged from 0.15 [20] to 0.35 [16].

5) Five-organ deficiency

Three studies provided data on five-organ deficiency [20, 23, 26]. The pooled prevalence was 0.16 (16.0%; 95% CI: 0.09-0.23). The I2 was not reported because the limited number of studies (k = 3) precluded reliable estimation of between-study variance. Individual study prevalence ranged from 0.11 [20] to 0.22 [26].

6) Liver-kidney yin deficiency

Two studies reported on liver-kidney yin deficiency [18, 25]. The pooled prevalence was 0.26 (26.0%; 95% CI: 0.23-0.29). The I2 statistic was not reported due to the limited number of studies (k = 2). Individual study prevalence was 0.26 in both studies [18, 25].

4. Syndrome differentiation methods

The primary method for syndrome differentiation across most included studies involved TCM practitioners employing the traditional four diagnostic methods: inspection, auscultation and olfaction, inquiry, and palpation. An exception was Li et al. (2018), who used participant self-assessment via questionnaire for pattern identification [17]. A methodological limitation observed across the studies was the limited reporting of practitioner qualifications, the lack of standardization of diagnostic procedures, and the absence of assessment of inter-rater reliability. Examination of diagnostic criteria for commonly identified patterns revealed both consistencies and variations across studies.

1) Qi-blood deficiency

A high degree of consistency was observed for this syndrome, which universally included fatigue, mental exhaustion, palpitations, shortness of breath, pale complexion, and a thin, weak pulse. Common tongue presentations included a pale tongue with a thin coating. Variations primarily involved the inclusion of additional symptoms, such as spontaneous sweating, blurred vision, or specific blood-related manifestations, including scanty menstruation.

2) Kidney essence deficiency

This pattern consistently presented with core manifestations, including forgetfulness, dizziness, tinnitus, soreness, and weakness of the waist and knees, as well as frequent nocturia. Characteristic tongue and pulse findings included a dry, thin tongue with a thin coating and a deep, weak, or thin pulse. Some studies additionally emphasized general constitutional symptoms, such as physical weakness and fatigue, whereas others focused more on specific organ-related manifestations.

3) Spleen-kidney yang deficiency

This pattern demonstrated good consistency in core cold-related symptoms, including cold limbs and aversion to cold, as well as digestive manifestations such as dawn diarrhea and poor appetite. Characteristic tongue and pulse findings included a pale, enlarged tongue with teeth marks and a white, slippery coating, along with a deep, thin, weak pulse or a deep, thin pulse. The emphasis on kidney-related symptoms versus spleen-related manifestations varied between studies.

4) Spleen deficiency with phlegm-dampness

Diagnostic criteria consistently included mental exhaustion, drowsiness, heaviness of the limbs, poor appetite, loose stools, and a slippery pulse. A pale tongue with a greasy coating was also consistently reported. The main variations involved differences in the level of detail used to describe manifestations of phlegm-dampness and associated constitutional symptoms.

5) Five-organ deficiency

This pattern commonly included mental fatigue or exhaustion, cold limbs, forgetfulness, dizziness, insomnia, soreness and weakness of the waist and knees, palpitations, shortness of breath, poor appetite, and weight loss. The tongue was typically described as pale with a thin white coating, and the pulse as deep and thin or thin and weak. While details were provided, the analysis of consistency is limited by the number of contributing studies.

6) Liver-kidney yin deficiency

This pattern consistently included symptoms such as dizziness, tinnitus, dry eyes, insomnia, dream-disturbed sleep, and soreness and weakness of the waist and knees. Common constitutional symptoms included five-center heat or night sweats, as well as dry mouth and throat. The tongue was typically described as red with little or no coating, and the pulse as thin and rapid. However, the limited number of studies restricts a broader assessment of diagnostic consistency.

Tongue and pulse diagnosis, a fundamental component of TEAM assessment, was systematically documented and served as a key diagnostic indicator in eight studies [16, 18-23, 26]. Tongue characteristics showed consistent pattern-specific presentations, including a pale, enlarged tongue with teeth marks in yang deficiency conditions, a red tongue with minimal coating in yin deficiency patterns, and a greasy coating for phlegm-dampness syndromes. Pulse characteristics provided complementary diagnostic information: deficiency patterns were typically associated with thin, weak, or deep pulses, whereas excess patterns were associated with slippery, wiry, or rapid pulse qualities. The consistency of these TEAM indicators across studies supports their continued clinical relevance for syndrome differentiation; however, the lack of standardized assessment methods remains a key limitation (Table 2).

5. Quality assessment

Quality assessment using NOS revealed substantial methodological limitations across the included studies. No studies achieved a good-quality rating (≥ 7 points), and scores ranged from 0 to 4 points. Specifically, four studies (36.4%) scored 4 points and were classified as poor quality, whereas seven studies (63.6%) scored 0-2 points and were classified as very poor quality. The most common methodological weaknesses included a lack of representative sampling (all 11 studies scored 0 for representativeness), inadequate description of the methods used to assess syndrome differentiation (10 studies scored 0 for outcome assessment), and insufficient control for confounding factors (7 studies scored 0 for comparability). Only five studies reported adequate response rate information, and only one study used validated measurement tools for syndrome assessment (Table 3).

6. Factors associated with frailty

Only three studies performed multivariable analyses to identify factors associated with frailty [19, 20, 22]. Tang identified three significant factors associated with frailty, including advanced age (odds ratio [OR] = 4.057, 95% CI: 2.845-5.784, p < 0.001), number of comorbidities (OR = 1.258, 95% CI: 1.157-1.367, p < 0.001), and osteocalcin levels (OR = 1.127, 95% CI: 1.084-1.170, p < 0.001) [19]. He also reported that nutritional markers were significantly associated with frailty, including lower prealbumin (OR = 0.951, 95% CI: 0.938-0.965, p < 0.001), lower total protein (OR = 0.931, 95% CI: 0.880-0.984, p = 0.012), and lower albumin (OR = 0.966, 95% CI: 0.881-1.057, p = 0.048) [20]. Zhang identified multiple factors associated with higher Frailty Index scores, including female sex (B = 0.043, p < 0.001), advanced age (B = 0.054, p < 0.001), chronic diseases such as diabetes (B = 0.047, p < 0.001) and coronary heart disease (B = 0.028, p = 0.005), and functional impairments including chronic pain (B = 0.051, p < 0.001) and urinary incontinence (B = 0.083, p = 0.004) (Table 4) [22].

7. Other analysis

Despite the original objective of examining regional variations across Chinese, Korean, and Japanese studies, all 11 included studies were conducted exclusively in China and represented only TCM syndrome patterns. Despite comprehensive searches of Korean and Japanese databases, no eligible studies examining KM or Kampo approaches to frailty were identified. Owing to the limited number of studies and substantial methodological heterogeneity, sensitivity analyses were not conducted. Similarly, formal assessment of publication bias using funnel plots was not performed because fewer than 10 studies were available for any individual syndrome pattern. Although extensive searches of international databases were conducted, all included studies originated from China. This finding suggests potential publication bias or selective reporting, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to other East Asian countries.

DISCUSSION

1. Summary of main findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide exploratory evidence suggesting that deficiency-related patterns are predominant among older adults with frailty (Fig. 3). Given the substantial heterogeneity and low methodological quality of the included studies, the reported prevalence estimates should be interpreted as preliminary descriptive findings rather than definitive rates. Specifically, the most frequently observed exploratory pooled prevalence rates were for qi-blood deficiency (24.0%), kidney essence deficiency (24.0%), and spleen deficiency with phlegm-dampness (23.0%). Liver-kidney yin deficiency also demonstrated a pooled prevalence of 26.0%; however, this estimate was based on only two studies. These exploratory findings are consistent with core TEAM theory, which associates aging and frailty with progressive decline in vital substances and organ functions, particularly kidney essence [10, 11]. However, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating observations and require confirmation in higher-quality studies with standardized diagnostic criteria.

2. Clinical interpretation

The observed prevalence of specific patterns provides crucial insights into the underlying TEAM pathology of frailty, supporting the interpretation of these patterns as distinct clinical manifestations of frailty within the TEAM framework. This conceptualization may facilitate more individualized and therapeutic approaches. For example, qi-blood deficiency, characterized by fatigue, mental exhaustion, palpitations, shortness of breath, pale complexion, and a thin, weak pulse, closely corresponds to the exhaustion, weakness, and reduced physical activity central to the frailty phenotype [27]. This pattern reflects a depletion of vital energy and nourishing substances, which may correspond to reduced physiological reserves and functional capacity in frailty. From a biomedical perspective, this state may be associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP production, which have been proposed as key mechanisms underlying fatigue and muscle weakness in frailty [28]. This energetic perspective could inform targeted interventions to restore systemic metabolic vitality.

Kidney essence deficiency, manifesting as forgetfulness, dizziness, tinnitus, soreness and weakness of the waist and knees, and frequent nocturia, reflects the cognitive impairment, musculoskeletal decline, and organ dysfunction commonly observed in frail older adults. In TEAM theory, kidney essence serves as the fundamental basis for growth, aging, and physiological function [10]. Its depletion may therefore correspond to age- physiological decline and multisystem vulnerability [10]. This interpretation is supported by the identification of osteocalcin as a significant risk factor associated with frailty in the included studies [19], consistent with the TEAM kidney-bone axis concept, in which kidney essence decline may be associated with impaired bone metabolism and increased vulnerability to physical decline [29].

Spleen deficiency with phlegm-dampness, characterized by mental exhaustion, heaviness of the limbs, poor appetite, loose stools, and a slippery pulse, reflects impaired digestive function, nutritional deficiency, and metabolic dysregulation. In TEAM theory, the spleen governs nutrient transformation and transport; dysfunction in this system may result in the accumulation of dampness and phlegm, contributing to reduced vitality and metabolic efficiency [30]. This pattern is particularly relevant to sarcopenia and weight loss, key components of frailty. It highlights the ‘digestive-metabolic’ aspect of frailty, emphasizing how compromised nutrient assimilation contributes to the frailty cascade. This clinical conceptualization aligns with our findings, which showed that lower levels of prealbumin, total protein, and albumin were significantly associated with higher frailty risk [20]. These biomarkers support the TEAM spleen-stomach theory, suggesting that impaired nutrient transformation may contribute to the systemic debilitating state observed in frailty [31].

Spleen-kidney yang deficiency, characterized by cold-related symptoms such as cold limbs and aversion to cold, and digestive manifestations such as dawn diarrhea, reflects impaired metabolic function, reduced warmth, and decreased functional activity, all common in advanced frailty. The yang qi of the spleen and kidney provides warmth and drives physiological processes, supporting overall vitality and organ function [32]. Its decline may lead to systemic hypofunction, potentially associated with lower basal metabolic rate and reduced physical performance. These manifestations represent a ‘hypometabolic’ or ‘cold-type’ clinical profile, addressing energy dysregulation and thermoregulatory deficits. This metabolic decline has been hypothetically linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by impaired oxidative phosphorylation and reduced ATP production, providing a potential cellular mechanism for the decreased thermogenesis and physical performance observed in yang-deficiency states of frailty [28, 33].

Liver-kidney yin deficiency, including symptoms such as dizziness, tinnitus, dry eyes, insomnia, and soreness and weakness of the waist and knees, may be associated with chronic inflammation, internal heat, and autonomic dysregulation, recognized as contributors to frailty [34]. Yin deficiency may represent a depletion of cooling and nourishing substances, potentially exacerbating age-related systemic stress and contributing to musculoskeletal and neurological decline [35]. This pattern reflects the ‘inflammatory’ (inflammaging) dimension of frailty, where depletion of systemic yin may impair the regulation of oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory stimuli [36].

Five-organ deficiency, characterized by a complex array of symptoms including mental fatigue, cold limbs, forgetfulness, insomnia, palpitations, shortness of breath, and weight loss, underscores systemic and multi-organ involvement inherent in advanced frailty. This pattern reflects global physiological decline and interconnected functional impairments, suggesting profound depletion of vital resources and systemic failure. Biologically, this state may correspond to multisystem dysregulation, in which cumulative decline across multiple physiological axes, rather than a single organ system, leads to a critical loss of homeostatic resilience, a hallmark of advanced frailty [37]. Collectively, these patterns demonstrate that TEAM syndrome differentiation offers a valuable framework to understand frailty not as isolated symptoms but as manifestations of interconnected systemic imbalances.

A notable strength of this review is the consistent reliance on experienced TCM practitioners employing the traditional four diagnostic methods—inspection, auscultation-olfaction, inquiry, and palpation—for syndrome differentiation across most included studies. Tongue and pulse diagnosis, fundamental components of TEAM assessment, were systematically documented in eight studies [16, 18-23, 26]. Consistent tongue characteristics (e.g., pale, enlarged tongue with teeth marks for yang deficiency, red tongue with little coating for yin deficiency patterns, greasy coating for phlegm-dampness syndromes) and pulse qualities (e.g., thin, weak, or deep pulses for deficiency patterns) suggest shared clinical understanding regarding the manifestation of these patterns in frail individuals. This consistency, despite variations in detailed descriptions, provides preliminary validation for the clinical relevance of these TEAM diagnostic approaches in geriatric populations. However, a critical methodological limitation was the limited detail on practitioner qualifications, the standardization of diagnostic procedures, and the assessment of inter-rater reliability. Furthermore, despite some observed consistencies, the diagnostic criteria for commonly identified patterns varied significantly across studies that provided such details. This lack of rigorous standardization in the syndrome differentiation process poses a challenge for research reproducibility and clinical consistency, highlighting the urgent need for a standardized syndrome differentiation system specifically for frailty within TEAM.

Our analysis of factors associated with frailty, identified through multivariable analyses in a subset of the included studies, provides a bridge between Western medical understanding of frailty and the differentiation of TEAM syndrome. These findings collectively support the potential explanatory framework of TEAM in the context of geriatric frailty, suggesting that specific underlying physiological changes recognized by conventional medicine may correspond to distinct TEAM patterns. Consistently identified risk factors, such as advanced age, align with TEAM’s conceptualization of systemic decline. In TEAM theory, advanced age is associated with a decline in kidney essence [10], a foundational contributor to various deficiency patterns. Similarly, associations between poor nutritional markers, such as lower prealbumin, total protein, and albumin levels, and frailty are consistent with the TEAM concept of spleen deficiency, particularly when accompanied by phlegm-dampness. The spleen’s role in digestion and nutrient transformation suggests its dysfunction may underlie the observed nutritional deficits [30], potentially initiating or exacerbating the frailty cascade. Additionally, functional impairments, including chronic pain and urinary incontinence, may predispose individuals to specific underlying deficiencies or pathological accumulations, contributing to the nuanced presentation of frailty from a TEAM perspective. Collectively, these observations illustrate that TEAM provides a holistic framework capable of interpreting the complex interplay of factors contributing to frailty, extending beyond a purely symptomatic approach. By linking established risk factors to specific TEAM patterns, this framework provides a more comprehensive understanding of individual frailty presentation, thereby informing the development of targeted, personalized interventions.

3. Limitations

This review has some limitations that affect the interpretation of our findings. First, the methodological quality of the included studies was uniformly low (NOS scores 0-4 points; no study achieved “good quality” ≥ 7 points), with weaknesses in representative sampling, documentation of syndrome differentiation, and control for confounding factors. These limitations reduce the robustness and generalizability of pooled prevalence estimates, although our systematic assessment provides benchmarks for future research. Second, the evidence base was exclusively derived from TCM studies. Despite comprehensive database searches, no studies from KM or Kampo studies met the inclusion criteria. Therefore, the findings represent TCM-specific evidence that cannot be generalized to other East Asian medicine traditions, which may conceptualize frailty patterns differently, using distinct diagnostic terminology and theoretical frameworks. This finding underscores the need for targeted research in underrepresented traditions. Third, substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 80% for most patterns) reflects variations in diagnostic criteria, practitioner qualifications, patient populations, and the absence of standardized protocols. Meta-analysis was conducted despite these limitations to provide exploratory prevalence estimates for hypothesis generation, enable systematic comparisons, and quantify heterogeneity to highlight areas requiring standardization. This approach balanced quantitative synthesis with extensive narrative synthesis while acknowledging the exploratory nature of the findings. Fourth, the limited number of studies (fewer than 10 per syndrome pattern) precluded formal sensitivity analyses and the assessment of publication bias, thereby limiting the evaluation of result robustness.

4. Implications for future research

To advance the understanding of TEAM syndrome patterns in frailty, future research should consider the following recommendations. First, studies should adopt rigorous designs, including representative sampling methods, detailed and standardized documentation of syndrome differentiation procedures, clear qualification criteria for practitioners, and robust control for confounding factors. Second, research is urgently needed from other East Asian countries, particularly Korea and Japan, to determine whether KM and Kampo identify similar or distinct syndrome patterns in frail older adults. These traditions may employ different theoretical frameworks, diagnostic approaches, and pattern conceptualizations compared with TCM. Such research is essential not only to validate TCM-derived findings but also to elucidate tradition-specific perspectives on frailty that cannot be inferred solely from Chinese studies, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive and globally applicable understanding of TEAM in geriatric frailty. Third, developing standardized and validated diagnostic criteria for frailty-related TEAM syndrome patterns is essential. Standardization will improve diagnostic consistency across clinical settings, enhance comparability of research findings, and may incorporate objective measures to complement traditional diagnostic methods. Finally, future studies should move beyond descriptive prevalence to investigate associations between specific TEAM syndrome patterns and objective clinical outcomes in frail older adults, such as hospitalization rates, mortality, and functional decline. Furthermore, intervention studies are needed to evaluate the efficacy of TEAM treatments specifically targeting these prevalent syndrome patterns in improving frailty outcomes.

CONCLUSION

This exploratory analysis suggests that, within TCM practice, frail older adults predominantly exhibit deficiency-related patterns, including qi-blood, kidney essence, and spleen deficiency with phlegm-dampness, potentially reflecting frailty as a systemic imbalance. However, the low methodological quality of existing studies and their exclusive focus on Chinese TCM populations preclude generalization to KM or Kampo traditions. Future research should prioritize rigorous, regionally diverse studies using standardized diagnostic criteria to validate these patterns. Such efforts will enhance understanding of frailty in older adults and may support the development of individualized, precise treatment strategies within TEAM, contingent upon further validation.
