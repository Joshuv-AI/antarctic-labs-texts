DeepSeek-R1 vs. OpenAI o1 in colorectal cancer screening: a binational evaluation
Authors: Yongpeng Chen, Sen Deng, Priska Hakenberg, Chengpeng Li, Tianzuo Zhan, Dejun Fan, XuTao Lin, Xianzhe Li, Cui Yang, Jiancong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge language models (LLMs), such as OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1, demonstrate promising applications in healthcare through structured reasoning and decision support. This study evaluates the responses and chain-of-thought (CoT) outputs of OpenAI o1and DeepSeek-R1 in answering questions about colorectal cancer (CRC) screening.MethodsFifteen questions about CRC screening were posed to OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1. Four experts rated the responses for accuracy and comprehensiveness and three further experts evaluated the CoT reasoning output for logical-coherence and error-types and handling, using the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines as the primary reference standard.ResultsBoth LLMs demonstrated high accuracy without significant differences (median accuracy scores: OpenAI o1 = 4.5, DeepSeek-R1 = 5; p = 0.5243). However, DeepSeek-R1 significantly outperformed OpenAI o1 in comprehensiveness (p < 0.0001), logical coherence (p = 0.0001), and error types and handling (p = 0.0149). DeepSeek-R1 generated more detailed responses (word count: 110 ± 40 vs. 57 ± 24, p = 0.0001), with longer response times (25 ± 10s vs. 7 ± 4s, p < 0.0001).ConclusionDeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1 both offer high accuracy for CRC screening guidance, with DeepSeek-R1 providing more comprehensive responses with logically more coherent, and robust error-handling reasoning process, compared with OpenAI o1. Context-specific evaluation is critical for practical clinical integration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04810-9.

Introduction

Early detection and management of precancerous and cancerous lesions can be achieved by colorectal cancer (CRC) screening, which can significantly reduce the incidence of CRC and the mortality rate [1, 2]. Substantial efforts have been undertaken to enhance adherence to CRC screening, encompassing the implementation of comprehensive public health campaigns and the development of simplified, patient-friendly screening modalities [3]. However, CRC screening rates remain poor, ranging from 30% to 80% [4]. The persistently suboptimal CRC screening participation rates may be attributable to multifactorial determinants, including inadequate public knowledge regarding screening protocols, difficulties in selecting appropriate screening modalities, procedural anxiety associated with colonoscopy, insufficient medical resources, medical insurance does not cover screening colonoscopy and insufficient understanding of post-diagnostic management pathways.

Advanced Artificial Intelligence (AI) models are rapidly transforming various sectors, including healthcare, by enabling the efficient management and analysis of massive amounts of data. Prior work has demonstrated that GPT-4 could answer questions related to colon cancer screening with high accuracy, comprehensiveness and patient comprehensibility [4]. ChatGPT also achieved high accuracy in the study of acute pancreatitis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and Helicobacter pylori infection [5–7]. Currently, reasoning models, such as OpenAI o1(September 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025), stand out for their abilities to deal with complex problems through structured and sequential thought processes, marking a significant evolution from traditional large language models (LLM) [8]. OpenAI o1 is built on the GPT family of LLMs and stands out for its implementation of the chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning method [9], which enables the model to break down complex questions into a series of successive logical steps, improving consistency and reducing the risk of incorrect or incomplete answers [10, 11]. Comparative studies have demonstrated that o1 outperforms previous versions (e.g., GPT-4) and other models in clinical decision-making tasks [12]. With CoT reasoning, o1 is also likely to excel in answering CRC queries. By breaking down inquiries into smaller logical steps, it could integrate factors such as patient age, family history and comorbidities into its recommendations and influence patient adherence and long-term outcomes greatly.

DeepSeek-R1 is another emerging LLM introduced in January 2025 that exhibits excellent accessibility and adaptability due to its open-source nature and impressive reasoning skills [4]. DeepSeek-R1 employs a reinforcement learning (RL)-based approach during pre-training, enabling the model to develop advanced reasoning capabilities without relying on traditional supervised pre-training [9]. DeepSeek-R1 showed superior performance in mathematical examinations compared to GPT-4o and OpenAI o1 [13]. While OpenAI has decided not to show the raw CoT to users [14], DeepSeek’s fully transparent CoT reasoning architecture provides unprecedented visibility into its thinking process, offering users a unique opportunity to audit the model’s clinical logic and self-refinement capabilities during error correction processes. While the transparency in its decision-making process is valuable for medical application, further investigation is required to validate the model’s performance in clinical settings, considering various aspects beyond accuracy.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically compare the accuracy, comprehensiveness, the logical coherence and error types & handling of CoT of OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1 in answering CRC screening questions. The evaluation is conducted by expert assessment in China and Germany, using the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines [15] as the primary reference standard.

Methods

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted from February to March 2025 in Germany and China. Institutional review board approval was not required because neither patient-identifiable data nor intervention methods were used.

CRC screening related questions

A list of 15 questions about CRC screening and its diagnostic and therapeutic implications was created with reference to previous literature [4], reprinted with permission through Rights Link® license number 5,983,430,821,980. Questions were focused on general screening (Q1-Q6 and Q11), and endoscopy (Q7-Q10) and therapeutic measures (Q12-Q15). All the above questions are attached in the supplementary document.

Generation of responses

The compiled list of questions was then submitted to OpenAI o1 (https://chatgpt.com) and Deepseek-R1 (https://chat.deepseek.com) on February 10, 2025. We set the temperature parameter to zero during all interactions with the AI model, which effectively minimizes the randomness of the model’s outputs and thus produces more consistent responses. To minimize base bias, we structured each interaction as a separate query by starting a new chat session with the same prompt. The same prompt was introduced before each question was asked: “You are an experienced gastrointestinal specialist. You will be asked a question about colonoscopy and colorectal cancer, and it is your job to answer it as accurately, briefly, and precisely as possible. Your answer should be aligned with the up-to-date NCCN guidelines. The answer should be understandable to patients. If you don’t know the answer, just say ’I don’t know’, and don’t try to make up an answer”. This approach ensures that each response is processed independently, allowing fewer independent responses to be evaluated. The generated answers, the corresponding answer thought chains and the time of thoughts were recorded for subsequent analysis.

Evaluation of responses and CoT

For the evaluation, we conducted two separate online surveys using Google Forms (https://docs.google.com/forms) for LLM-generated responses and the CoT outputs. Regarding the final responses generated by the LLMs, our evaluation prioritizes accuracy and completeness to ensure, from an outcome-oriented perspective, that the models provide correct clinical guidance. In contrast, for the models’ CoT, the evaluation focuses on logical coherence and error types and handling. This approach is designed to verify that the models possess rigorous, evidence-based medical reasoning capabilities that align with real-world clinical practice [11].To minimize bias, the reviewers for responses were blinded to the source of the responses and the order in which the responses were presented was randomized. The LLM-generated responses were evaluated for accuracy and comprehensiveness by four physician authors DJF, XTL, TZ and CY, three gastrointestinal surgeons and one gastroenterologist. A separate group of three reviewers (CPL, PH and XZL), who included two surgical attendings and one researcher (former gastrointestinal surgeon), assessed the CoT for logical coherence and error types and handling. They were blinded to the index question, source of the responses, and scoring of the response.

Five-point Likert scales were used to evaluate the responses and CoT. The scoring criteria are shown in Tables 1 and 2. In instances where a reviewer’s score differed by a minimum of two points from that of another reviewer, a discussion was initiated to address the discrepancy. This was followed by a re-scoring process to ensure the consistency and integrity of the evaluation process. If there was still a discrepancy of ≥ 2 points after re-grading, a third independent reviewer (JCH) was consulted and provided a final rating based on the discussion.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12.6 (Python Software Foundation, 2025). The results were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables, frequency with percentages for categorical variables, and medians for ordinal categorical variables. Scores were compared using the t-test, with a p-value of < 0.05 being considered statistically significant.

Results

Accuracy and comprehensiveness

Among the 15 questions evaluated, the median accuracy scores for OpenAI o1and DeepSeek-R1 were 4.5 and 5, respectively, while the median comprehensiveness ratings were 3.5 and 5, respectively. Although there was no statistical difference between OpenAI o1and DeepSeek-R1 in terms of overall accuracy, DeepSeek-R1 achieved significantly higher scores for comprehensiveness (p < 0.0001, as shown in Table 3; Fig. 1).

In general screening (Q1-Q6 and Q11), endoscopy (Q7-Q10) and therapeutic measures (Q12- Q15), DeepSeek-R1 provided more complete and detailed responses (p = 0.0028, p = 0.0082 and p = 0.0069, as shown in Table 3) with no significant difference in accuracy (P = 0.7882, P = 0.2283 and P = 0.1135, as shown in Table 3).

Logical coherence and error types and handling of CoT

In the reasoning processes demonstrated by OpenAI o1and DeepSeek-R1, the experts’ median ratings for both logical coherence ratings and error types and handling were all 4 and 5, respectively. DeepSeek-R1’s CoT was significantly superior to that of OpenAI o1 in both logical coherence (P = 0.0001) and error types and handling (P = 0.0149, as shown in Table 3; Fig. 1).

In our study, DeepSeek-R1 outperformed OpenAI o1 in logical coherence for CoT on general screening, endoscopy, and treatment measure related questions. In error types and handling for endoscopy-related questions was superior to OpenAI o1, and was comparable for general screening and therapeutic measures questions.

Time of response and word count

The mean of word count of DeepSeek-R1’s responses is 110 ± 40, which is significantly more than OpenAI o1’s 57 ± 24 words (P = 0.0001). The mean thinking time of DeepSeek-R1’s was 25 ± 10 s, which is significantly longer than OpenAI o1’s 7 ± 4 s (P < 0.0001).

Discussion

This study shows that both DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1 achieve high diagnostic accuracy in answering questions regarding CRC screening. However, DeepSeek-R1 outperformed significantly in comprehensiveness. The higher word count and more detailed explanations of DeepSeek-R1 likely contributed to this observation. In addition, DeepSeek-R1 earned better ratings for logical coherence and demonstrated more robust error detection and handling strategies, suggesting that its transparent CoT architecture may enhance the model’s utility in clinical decision support. However, further experiments are needed to demonstrate its role in clinical practice.

There are only a few studies comparing these two reasoning models, demonstrating varying results. In a recent study, OpenAI o1 showed significantly higher accuracy than DeepSeek- R1 in answering 500 pediatric multiple-choice questions (92.8% vs. 87.0%) [16]. Several factors could explain why our findings are different. Firstly, multiple-choice questions formats may allow models to potentially infer correct answers through pattern recognition rather than clinical reasoning. Secondly, presenting questions in blocks of 25 could induce unintended learning effects, especially in models employing advanced reasoning. Thirdly, the absence of prompt engineering might limit the true evaluation of the models’ full reasoning capacity. In a psychiatric report from Taiwan, OpenAI o1 successfully identified the risk of charcoal burning suicide, while DeepSeek-R1 missed the suicide risk and interpreting the mother’s behavior as “psychological coping with family conflicts”. Both models did not understand the cultural customs in a particular cultural context, which human experts would immediately recognize [17]. This report provided initial insight into model performance across diverse socio-cultural contexts. However, it was based on a single case and was not a comprehensive study. In contrast, the questions in our study are open questions. They focused on standardized medical knowledge (CRC screening guidelines), and the answers usually have clear consensus (such as age recommendations, examination procedures), thereby providing a more direct and reliable comparison of accuracy.

LLMs often face criticism for their “black box” nature, which restricts interpretability and makes it difficult to understand the logic behind their responses. DeepSeek shows its problem-solving capabilities through CoT reasoning, an approach that breaks down complex tasks into more manageable steps [9]. While CoT can be applied to other LLMs, they often require users to carefully construct CoT prompts (e.g., explain the reasoning) or implement agentic system designs (e.g., a fact checker agent) [9]. Our research indicated that DeepSeek-R1 produces CoTs that are significantly better than OpenAI o1 in both logical coherence and error types and handling. This may be because OpenAI o1 did not fully demonstrate the CoT process to users. Moreover, DeepSeek-R1’s “Critical Thinking” or “Thinking Out Loud” improves the transparency of the decision-making process and its performance. Its self-reflection capability, through which the model autonomously verifies and optimizes its logical steps, improves its performance on complex tasks. Such robust CoT reasoning and self-reflection mechanisms make DeepSeek-R1 especially attractive for healthcare applications because it can manage complex reasoning tasks that require stepwise logic and multiple potential options to resolve uncertainty in order to narrow down possible diagnoses and improve decision making [9]. Therefore, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated advantages in structured reasoning within guideline-based CRC screening scenarios.

Our findings revealed that DeepSeek-R1 delivered significantly more comprehensive answers for CRC screening questions, along with longer responses exhibiting greater clinical detail. On the other hand, lengthy answers due to more detailed reasoning may pose challenges to healthcare. Overly detailed outputs may burden clinicians who are responsible for reviewing and approving these outputs and lead to important details being overlooked [9]. The accuracy of OpenAI o1 is comparable, with fewer words in the answer and less thinking time, indicating that OpenAI o1 is more efficient and may be more suitable for simple clinical scenarios. In addition, lengthy responses may discourage patients from reading, while OpenAI o1provides comparable accuracy with fewer words. This verbosity raises important questions regarding the readability-completeness tradeoff - a critical consideration for implementing AI decision aids in time-constrained clinical environments that warrants systematic evaluation.

Another frequently discussed aspect of LLM is its ethicality and privacy protection. Unlike OpenAI o1, DeepSeek-R1 is known for being low-cost, flexible and open source, allowing the model to be downloaded and deployed on private servers for offline use and enhanced data security [18]. Clinically, a local deployment can be run for better information security [19]. As an open-source model with free licensing, DeepSeek-R1 enables hospitals to implement localized AI deployments that strictly comply with medical data governance frameworks, whereas proprietary systems like o1 necessitate third-party cloud dependencies that may compromise patient privacy and incur substantial operational costs.

Our study has different medical experts in China and Germany assessing LLM for clinical application, thereby reducing bias and increasing the objectivity and reliability. However, the small number of experts is a limitation of this study. Furthermore, several limitations warrant consideration in interpreting these findings as well. Firstly, the evaluation was confined to CRC screening scenarios, which may not fully represent the model’s performance in other clinical domains requiring specialized knowledge, such as therapeutic decision-making for complex reasoning medical scenarios. Thereby, further experiments are needed to demonstrate LLM’s role in complex clinical practice. Secondly, the assessment relied on standardized clinical questions rather than real-time clinician-AI interactions, potentially overestimating practical utility in dynamic care environments. Thirdly, our research only discussed the roles of DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1 in CCR screening, but they are not artificial intelligence models specifically designed for clinical practice. Our next step is to further investigate the role of specialized medical artificial intelligence in clinical practice. Finally, while gastroenterologists provided rigorous specialty-specific evaluations, patient perspectives remain unassessed. These constraints highlight the need for prospective trials embedding AI tools in authentic clinical pathways with diverse end-user validation to establish real-world effectiveness.

Conclusion

This multicenter evaluation shows that both DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1exhibit clinically acceptable accuracy in guiding colorectal cancer screening decisions. DeepSeek-R1 achieves higher comprehensiveness scores, enhanced logical and less erroneous CoT. However, these findings are context-specific to screening-related queries evaluated through simulated clinical scenarios. The application of LLM in complex reasoning medical scenarios requires further research.
