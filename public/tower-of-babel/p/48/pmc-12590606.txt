Combination of QTL mapping and GWAS for fine mapping and gene mining of drought tolerance and seed yield components in faba bean (Vicia faba L.)
Authors: Natalia Gutierrez, Ana M. Torres
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncreasing crop yields in the face of climate change is a key goal in faba bean breeding. By integrating multiple data sets, we combine recent QTL and GWAS analyses for yield and drought resistance and uncover the most consistent genomic regions related with these traits. We determine the physical position of the significant markers in the reference faba bean genome and assess the co-localization of significant QTNs with stable QTLs, thus pinpointing the most likely candidate genes.ResultsOne hundred fifty-two annotated genes, found in 10 overlapping genomic regions, were predicted as faba bean candidate genes for drought and yield related traits and many of them were closely related to genes previously identified and validated in other crops. Several significant markers appear to influence multiple traits, sometimes even seemingly unrelated ones suggesting potential pleiotropy or close physical linkage, although further validation is required.ConclusionIntegrating previously published QTLs and GWAS results for yield and drought related traits and projecting the significant markers onto the physical reference genome we identified overlapping regions and mine candidate genes within those intervals. The results of this study significantly advance our understanding of the genetic architecture of various traits and provide useful information of candidate genes that might have potential for selection in future faba bean breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07630-3.

Background

Climate change poses a major threat to global agricultural production, and it is expected to worsen in the upcoming years. To overcome this challenge, it is required to optimize climate change adaptation, agricultural productivity, food security and environmental protection [1]. Legumes are one of the most suitable groups of crops to meet these requirements by diversifying the currently simplified rotations and increasing the productivity and sustainability of current farming systems.

Faba bean (Vicia faba L.), one of the oldests cool season grain legumes, is a high protein food and feed crop used worldwide as a dry pulse, green grain/pod and a green manure. In addition, faba bean exhibits broad environmental adaptability and high biological nitrogen fixation rates, which improves soil fertility and contributes to reducing the use of synthetic fertilizers, thus promoting circularity and mitigating the environmental impact. Moreover, faba bean crop rotations promote diversification, improves the yield of the subsequent crop and breaks the cycles of pests and diseases enhancing the soil health and structure [2, 3]. Despite these nutritional and environmental benefits, the worldwide faba bean cultivation has decreased by 52% over the past 60 years [4]. This decline is inducing, in turn, a production deficit of plant proteins and a loss of the ecosystem services effects provided by the crop.

To promote the higher cultivation and production of this environmentally beneficial protein rich crop, the major priorities in current breeding programs are focused on the resistance/tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses together with the improvement of yield and quality traits. Valuable progress has been made through conventional breeding in the last century but faba bean genomic studies have lagged far behind other grain legume crops. One limitation for such development in faba bean has been its huge and complex genome (13 Gbp), the largest among the cool season legumes, containing more than 85% repetitive DNA [5], which has hampered the development of saturated genetic linkage maps and the identification of determinant genes underpinning key agricultural traits.

There are two major approaches to dissect the genomic regions controlling quantitative trait loci (QTL) that are responsible for phenotypic variation: QTL mapping and genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Traditionally, QTL mapping has been the most common method used though it has limitations such as low marker resolution, requiring large populations for fine mapping and sometimes yielding only broad chromosomal regions instead of precise gene locations. If recombination has not had enough time to break down and shuffle the genome into small fragments, the linked markers will remain within large segments of the chromosome. Moreover, rare alleles or QTLs with minor effects are difficult to be identified due to the relatively low resolution and the lack of genetic diversity existing in a single segregating biparental population. Thus, the QTL segregating in a cross, represents only a small fraction of the phenotypical variation existing in a species and may often not be consistent across mapping populations [6, 7].

More recently, GWAS (also known as linkage disequilibrium mapping), is increasingly being adopted as the method of choice for dissecting the genetic basis of complex traits. Using wide germplasm collections that mimic historical recombinations, GWAS provide higher resolution than QTL mapping, for the identification of both major and minor QTLs [8, 9]. GWAS allow identifying marker–trait associations based on the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between a marker and the causal polymorphism. The decay of the LD determines the resolution with which a QTL can be mapped and the marker density required. If LD decays too fast within a region, mapping resolution is expected to be high, but a larger number of markers are required [10]. This approach is particularly useful in plant breeding where alleles associated with desirable phenotypes can be introduced efficiently into selected lines. It should be stressed, however, that both interval mapping and association studies are complementary approaches that can be exploited nowadays to increase statistical power and mapping resolution. The rising availability of low-cost DNA sequencing and genotyping methods should stimulate plant researchers to consider both methods in breeding programs.

The aim of most mapping studies is to identify the functional genetic variants or the quantitative trait nucleotides (QTNs), that are responsible for phenotypic variation. Current data sets are commonly obtained using SNP microarrays that can genotype hundreds of thousands of markers over a large number of samples. Nevertheless, even with such large numbers of markers, it is unlikely that searched after functional genetic variants will be among the markers genotyped. The genetic markers that are in strong LD with the functional genetic variants become, however, proxies or sentinels for the functional variant because their genotypes are highly correlated with the genotypes of the functional variant (reviewed in [7]).

A low linkage disequilibrium (high number of recombinations) in faba bean has been previously reported [11–13]. This is an expected outcome for an outbreeding species with a high genetic diversity [11, 12, 14, 15]. The rapid LD decay reported in this crop reveals the variation present in a highly allogamous panmictic population and suggest that the marker trait associations (MTAs) or the closely linked genes on either flanking side of the significant SNP (single nucleotide polymorphism), are nearly or completely independent from each other, and hence are sufficient for association mapping in this crop [13].

In the last 20 years, QTLs and more recently GWAS have been applied in faba bean to uncover the genetic architecture associated with disease resistances such as ascochyta and broomrape resistance [16–30]; abiotic stresses [19, 31–34]; tolerance to herbicides [35]; phenological and morphological traits [12, 36]; architectural, flowering, seed quality and yield related traits [12, 13, 21, 25, 26, 37, 38], in different genetic backgrounds and populations, identifying hundreds of QTLs and QTNs associated with causative loci. Despite the substantial number of GWAS studies reported, some of them are still underpowered due to the small size of the panels used and the lack of studies focusing on multiple traits.

A major milestone has been the development of the first faba bean reference genome [36], which provides a critical resource to overcome molecular breeding challenges and increase the opportunities to identify the specific genes and alleles that underpin valuable traits. This new genomic tool represents a step change in the process, allowing the integration of significant markers for major genes or QTLs with QTNs identified by GWAS. The approach can overcome some of the limitations mentioned above by refining the position of the most consistent QTLs, revealing the co-localization with QTNs and thus, leading to the identification of candidate loci with potential use in plant breeding.

Recently our group generated a high dense genetic map in the Recombinant Inbred Line (RIL) population Vf6 x Vf27 with 124 F8:9 individuals, using the Affymetrix GeneChip 'Vfaba_v2′ 60 k SNP array, with phenotypic data accumulated along 13 years [38]. Phenotypic evaluations for autofertility [39], flowering time [21, 40], plant architecture [25, 39], yield related traits [21, 25, 39] were used for QTL mapping [38]. QTL detection was conducted using the maximum likelihood (ML) algorithm in the R package R/qtl [41], performing a single-QTL genome scan with a normal model. The threshold for the detection of a QTL was determined using 1,000 permutations and a significance (p-value) level of 0.05. The confidence interval of each QTL position was determined by a drop of 1-LOD or 2-LOD from the maximum LOD score [42, 43]. The fine QTL mapping led to the identification of 99 significant QTLs corresponding to 35 of the traits with diverse contributions to the overall phenotypic variation explained [38]. A great percentage of QTLs were stable across years and environments and represent important targets for breeding, as they corresponded to genomic regions highly heritable and less affected by environmental factors [38]. All years and QTLs detected in these studies were used equally in the present study.

On the other hand, in a GWAS study we used the same Affymetrix GeneChip to genotype a worldwide collection of 100 faba bean accessions, including genetic stocks, landraces and breeding lines from diverse geographical origins. The phenotyping assays were performed in 2019 and 2020 at the Julius Kühn-Institut (JKI) in Groß Lüsewitz, Germany. The experiment employed a randomized block design to compare irrigated (control) and drought-stressed plants (14 plants per plot) using rain-out shelters, with four replications, two blocks per shelter and two shelters in total. Drought stress was induced when approximately 30% of the plots began to flower and during rainfall, protective shelters were moved over these specific plots to prevent irrigation. Six morphological and phenological traits were recorded: maturity date (MAT), plant height (PH); number of pods per plant (PP); number of seeds per plant (SP); hundred seed weight (HSW) and plot yield (PY) in kg. Moreover, four physiological traits were assessed in leaves: free proline content (PRO); total content of soluble sugars (TSS) and chlorophyll content (SPAD1 and SPAD2). Twenty-nine QTNs were found to be significantly correlated with these traits under stress [33].

Using the same high-density SNP genotyping array, a GWAS analysis for yield-related traits [13] was performed in a worldwide panel of 352 accessions that were phenotyped in three geographic locations: 1) Huelva, Spain (2019 and 2020); 2) Aberystwyth, United Kingdom (2019) and 3) Krusevac, Serbia (2020), following an augmented design MAD type 2. The experimental trials included 440 plots (80 seeds/plot). Twenty accessions were chosen as checks and distributed in four incomplete blocks and the remaining 380 accessions were unreplicated and randomly distributed within and between blocks. The plants were measured and scored for six agronomic traits affecting the final yield response: pods per plant (PP), seeds per pod (SP), seeds per plant (SPL), plot yield (PY), 100 seed weight (HSW) and shattering (SH). The study detected 112 MTAs in 97 candidate genes, eight of the MTAs were detected in at least two environments and five were associated with multiple traits in pleiotropic QTLs regions (MLMM) method [44], implemented in the R package ‘mlmm.gwas’ [45], was used to analyze SNP-trait associations, improving detection power and minimizing false positives. The Bonferroni threshold was then applied to identify statistically significant associations.

Building on the work described above, the aim of this study was to apply a holistic approach to: (i) combine the multi-traits QTL mapping and GWA analyses for yield and drought resistance, recently reported by our group, (ii) determine the physical position for all the significant markers in the reference faba bean genome (iii) to assess the co-localization of significant QTNs with stable QTLs and thus uncover and refine the most consistent genomic regions related with these traits. This combined approach aims to enhance understanding of the genetic architecture underlying yield and drought related traits by validating earlier candidate genes and uncovering novel ones with potential for selection in future faba bean breeding programs.

Materials and methods

Alignment of the high-density SNP array with the Vicia faba reference genome

Transcriptome datasets from different faba bean plant tissues/varieties [46–49] were used to produce the "Affymetrix GeneChip Vfaba_v2" [50–52]. The 59,871 SNP markers included, were physically positioned on the first faba bean reference genome sequenced with PacBio HiFi, assembling 11.9 Gb (96.3%) from the 13 Gb reported in this species and represented by 34,221 protein-coding genes [36]. In the three datasets used in this study the SNPs detected were previously filtered to remove those with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05 and > 15% of heterozygotes. The missing values were imputed with the minor allele frequency [53]. To determine the physical chromosomal position the SNP flanking sequences were aligned against the reference genome by performing the “map to reference” procedure, using the medium sensitivity with fine tuning by iterating up to 5 times as an option of the method implemented in Geneious v.7.1.9 (Supplementary File 1). The extremely large chromosome (chr.) Vf1 (> 3 Gbp) was divided at the centromere by the faba bean genome consortium to form chr. 1S and chr1L. Genome assemblies and annotations of the reference genome are available for download at www.fabagenome.dk and can be accessed via an interactive genome browser (http://w3lamc.umbr.cas.cz/lamc/resources.html).

Development of a physical map in Vicia faba

To develop the high density map we used 19,907 SNPs markers whose physical position (Mbp) had been estimated by [12]. The statistical analyses for the map was conducted in R 3.6.1 [54] using the LinkageMapView package [55]. Following, the QTLs and QTNs were projected into the physical reference map to allow the comparison of the QTLs and GWAS data.

QTLs and QTNs for yield and drought related components

The alignment of the high-density SNP array with the Vicia faba reference genome allows us to determine the physical position for all the QTNs found to be associated with drought stress, architecture, phenology and yield related traits [13, 33, 38]. QTLs and QTNs were integrated in the reference genome and projected into a high-density physical map to detect overlapping regions. Comprehensive details on the QTLs considered, including descriptions, evaluation methods and acronyms used are given in the Additional File 1. The file also presents the physical map with the overlapping QTNs and QTLs. The QTL intervals (1-LOD) are represented with the color of their corresponding trait.

Functional annotation and candidate genes identification

Functional annotation of the 34,221 sequences of reference genome was done using eggNOG-mapper v.2 with the eukaryotic database [56] based on fast orthology assignments using precomputed eggNOG v5.0 clusters and phylogenies [57]. The eggNOG-mapper was also used to classify the candidate genes predicted proteins into clusters of orthologous groups (COGs) and plotted using the ‘lessR’ R-package [58, 59]. Only QTLs and QTNs overlapping in physical genomic regions were retained for candidate gene analysis. Target intervals were defined as follows: (i) For QTLs, the 1-LOD support interval was used to delineate the candidate region, (ii) For QTNs, annotated genes located around ± 150 Kb of the significant SNPs were selected, based on the local LD decay previously reported [13]. Briefly, this LD was calculated by filtering SNP markers (MAF of 5%), imputing missing genotype data using the LD-kNNi method [60], and then computing the squared allele frequency correlation (r2) for all pairwise marker combinations within 1 Mbp [61]. These r2 values were plotted against the marker distance in kilobases (kbp), and a LOESS regression curve was fit to show LD decay against genomic distance. Due to incomplete annotation and positional data in the current Vicia faba reference genome, we expanded this window where necessary. In such cases, up to 10 of the closest annotated genes within the QTL support interval were considered putative candidates for yield- and drought related traits.

Results and discussion

Chromosome assignment of the SNP markers

After aligning the 59,871 SNPs markers against the faba bean genomic sequence, 45,195 of the SNPs flanking sequences (75%) matched the reference [36]. These SNPs were associated with 21,317 of the 34,221 annotated protein-coding sequences, with multiple SNPs often linking to the same protein-coding sequence. While most SNPs (42,758) could be assigned to one of the six chromosomes, a small portion (2,437) remained unassigned (Additional File 2). The presence of repetitive regions, assembly gaps, or insufficient flanking sequence specificity might be the main reasons for failed alignment. Chromosomes Vf1L (7,529 SNPs markers) and Vf2 (7,352) had the highest number of SNPs markers, while chromosomes Vf5 (5,098) and Vf6 (5,385) showed the lowest number (Additional File 2). To obtain a comprehensive physical map, the dataset of SNP markers aligned with the reference genome was expanded by incorporating the remaining available coding sequences (12,904) and ordered by the name of the protein-coding sequence (Additional File 1).

Overlapping of QTLs and QTNs in the physical genomic regions

The distribution along the six faba bean chromosomes of the significant QTLs and QTNs reported by the group [13, 33, 38] is shown in the Additional File 3. Their projection in the physical genomic map resulted in 10 overlapping genomic: two regions in chr. Vf1; three in chr. Vf2; two in chr. Vf3 and one in chr. Vf4, Vf5 and Vf6. Candidate genes identification

While QTL mapping studies in faba bean have identified regions for agronomic traits, the genes within those regions remain unisolated partly due to limited recombination and parental diversity. GWAS offer a complementary approach, using large diverse populations and historical recombination to find minor effect variants across the genome. Combining both methods leverages their respective strengths, improving accuracy and accelerating gene discovery. Combined analysis methods have successfully linked loci to traits in different crops such as maize [62, 63], wheat [64] or soybean [65] and this has been the method of choice in this study.

Depending on the number of genes annotated or physically located in each of the overlapping regions the genomic distances covered vary from 0.08 to 3.16 Mbp. A total of 152 annotated genes were predicted as candidates in the established range up- and downstream of the stable QTLs. Chromosome Vf1S (overlapping region 1). Two QTNs (1g078200 and 1g079040) environmentally stable for the yield related traits pods per plant (PP_Serbia.2020) and plot yield (PY_UK.2019), co-localized with three QTLs for pod setting (PSF_2009/10; PSF_2012/13 and PSC_2014/15), under different conditions (Fig. 1; Additional File 1). From the five candidates found in the 1g078200 interval, the only annotated gene encodes a E3 SUMO-protein ligase (Additional File 4). The second interval (526,358—527,666 Mbp) included six annotated genes (Additional File 4) among which the two serine/threonine protein kinases are the most promising candidates for yield related traits (PP, PY, PS). Although protein kinases are well-known regulators of plant processes, including developmental pathways, responses to stress, and symbiotic interactions like root nodule symbiosis [66], in major cereal crops like maize [67], rice [68, 69] and wheat [70] orthologous serine/threonine protein kinases have also been been identified and shown to influence grain yield. In soybean, a serine/threonine kinase family protein (Glyma.20g105300), also showed differential expressions during late reproductive stages, consistent with a role in controlling protein content in the seeds [65].

Chromosome Vf1L (overlapping region 2). Two QTNs (1g340840 and 1g346160) associated with seeds (SPL_Spain 2020) and pods per plant (PP_Spain 2020), overlapped in the intervals of five QTLs for flowers per node detected in four years (FNC_2008/09; FNG_2014/15; FNC_2014/15; FNF_2011/12; FNF_2007/08) under different conditions and one QTL for height to the first pod (H1P_2007/08) (Fig. 1). Twenty-one candidate genes were identified in this overlapping region 2 (Table 1; Additional File 4). Among the possible candidates, we found again a protein kinase together with an ABC transporter G, an ATPase and a Golgi complex subunit. ABC transporters also play a crucial role in plant seed development (seed size and seed number) which directly impacts seed yield. Recent findings in chickpea and rice [71, 72] have highlighted their value in quality and crop yield improvement. Similarly, ATPases also affect seed yield by influencing different aspects of plant growth and development, including nutrient uptake, photosynthesis, etc. [73]. Significant in drought conditions for plant height (PH) and SPAD2 were identified 11 candidate genes among which two pentatricopeptide repeat-containing proteins (PRP), a serine/threonine protein kinase, a repressing transcription factor binding, a glutamate-gated receptor, a phosphatase 2 C (PP2C), the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme, the TRAM domain and the cytochrome P450 are the genes presumed to be more reliable candidates mediating drought response. A recent study in peanut to identify the hub genes in the key modules related to drought stress response, pointed out to genes encoding a PRP together with several serine/threonine-protein kinases [74]. Both genes have been found to be linked in chickpea playing a role mainly in pollen maturation, fertilization, flowering time and final pod and seed yield [75]. A glutamate receptor promotes internodes elongation and plant growth in the brassinosteroid signaling pathway in rice [76]. Finally, PP2Cs are known to play a crucial role in plant responses to drought stress [77]. The Arabidopsis ABI1 and ABI2 genes encoding two PP2Cs are involved in abscisic acid (ABA) signal transduction, which affects various plant responses, including growth inhibition in response to stress. Therefore, although not explicitly mentioned, it is possible that PP2Cs may have an indirect effect on plant height by regulating growth under stress conditions [78]. Finally, the ubiquitination pathway and the cytochrome P450 monooxygenases have been reported to regulate growth, development, and drought stress responses in M. truncatula [79, 80], mung bean [81] or rice [82]. Similarly, overexpression of TRAM proteins significantly improved the tolerance of rice to drought stress [83].

Chromosome Vf2 (overlapping regions 3, 4 and 5). In the overlapping region 3, the QTN 2g002120 linked to seeds per plant (SPL_Spain.2019) colocalized with the correlated QTL for pods per node (PNF_2012/13). Seven candidates were found in this QTN interval. In the overlapping region 4, QTN 2g076560 for hundred seed weight in drought conditions (HWS_Drought) overlapped with a QTL for plant height (PH_2006/07) and included six candidates. Finally, in the overlapping region 5, QTN 2g164440 associated with plant height in drought conditions (PH_Drought) overlapped with QTLs for days to maturity (DM_2007/08) and with number of branches (NBF_2006/07) and containing ten candidates, two of them unannotated (Table 1; Additional File 4). Among the 23 candidate genes found in these overlapping clusters we highlight a serine/threonine-protein kinase, a GPI-anchored protein, a cytochrome P450, an ABC1 protein transporter, a carboxylesterase (CXEs), a B3 domain-containing protein, an E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase and again a phosphatase 2 C as the most promising for improving seed production, seed size and plant height in drought conditions. As mentioned above, several serine/threonine protein kinases have been reported to act as key regulators of grain yield in different crops [67, 84, 85]. GPI-anchored proteins are critical for flowering reproduction because of their essential roles in the fertility of the male and female gametophytes [86]. Moreover, both the LHC family members and the P450s play important roles in regulating growth, development and plant responses to abiotic stresses [80, 82, 87]. Similarly, in Arabidopsis, the expression of the ABC genes controlling floral organ identity increased during early drought treatment and decreased with further drought, suggesting complex drought effects on floral regulatory genes [88]. CXEs and E3 ubiquitin ligases are known to play a vital role in plant growth, development, flowering time control and stress response [89]. Thus, in tobacco a CXE gene regulates axillary bud growth [90] and in Arabidopsis, increases branches and tillers through strigolactone metabolism [91]. Two adjacent B3 domain-containing proteins were also found in the interval. This transcription factor family has varied functions, especially related with vegetative and reproductive growth (eg. seed development, auxin signalling pathway, flowering time, and maturation) [92]. In Arabidopsis, a B3 domain transcription factor (REM16) promotes flowering time via directly binding to the promoters of SOC1 and FT [93] and the B3 domain-containing transcription factor ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE3 (ABI3) has been reported to control several downstream genes to resist dehydration stress [94]. Interestingly, as observed in chr. Vf1L, a phosphatase 2 C, was again associated in chr. Vf2 with plant height under stress conditions.

Chromosome Vf3 (overlapping regions 6 and 7). In the overlapping region 6, QTNs 3g038240 and 3g038960, associated with plot yield (PY_Spain.2019) and seeds per pods in different environments (SP_Spain.2019, SP_Spain.Global and SP_Global), respectively, overlapped in the intervals of four stable QTLs for height to first flower (H1F_2011/12; H1F_2012/13) or height to first pod (H1P_2007/08; H1P_2011/12) (Table 1; Fig. 1). The overlapping cluster 6, included 13 candidates, three of them unannotated or uncharacterized (Additional file 4). Among them we highlight a YLS9-like protein, a ribosomal protein and sodium-dependent phosphate transporter. The YLS9-like protein whose sequence contains a LEA domain (Late Embryogenesis Abundant protein) that besides regulating plant growth and responses to various abiotic stresses in Glycine max [95], are known to play an important role in seed and pod development in other legumes such as Cajanus cajan [96]. Ribosomal proteins have also been reported to accumulate in Medicago preceding the intense protein synthesis activity, occurring during seed filling as a preparation for storage and maturation [97]. On the other hand, some phosphorous transporters and regulatory factors have been confirmed as useful candidates for breeding crops with improved yields [98]. In the overlapping region 7, three QTNs (3g197400, 3g200600 and 3g205240) environmentally stable for the yield related traits, seed and pod set (SP_Spain.Global, PP_Spain.2019 and SP_UK.2019) together with QTN 3g200080 for plant height in drought conditions (PH_Drought), co-localized with QTLs for number of ovules per ovary (OVC_2008/09; OVF_2012/13) and pod setting (PSF_2012/13) (Additional File 1; Fig. 1). This overlapping region included 26 candidates (Table 1; Additional file 4) such as a cysteine-rich receptor-like protein kinase that play key roles in immunity, abiotic stress response, and growth and development [99], a Ndr kinase and a rhomboid protease both indispensable for pollen fertility in Arabidopsis [100, 101]. Following, associated with PH_drought (QTN 3g200080), the most promising candidates seem to be the exocyst complex component, the RNA binding and the pentatricopeptide repeat-containing proteins. Exocyst subunits are implicated in plant cell and organ morphogenesis. Mutants exo70A1 lead to reduced organ size and overall smaller plants in Arabidopsis because of organ-specific reductions in cell division [102]. Both the RNA binding and the pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins have been reported to regulate plant responses to abiotic stresses in different plant species [103–105] and the Arabidopsis glycine-rich RNA-binding protein in rice can improve its grain yield under drought stress conditions [106, 107]. Following, in the QTN 3g200600 interval, seven candidates were found among which we highlight the zinc finger protein CONSTANS-like (ZFP) and the flowering-promoting factor 1-like (FPF1). ZFPs are involved in a wide range of plant functions, such as growth and development. A ZFP has been related with seed size in the model legume M. truncatula and lines with knockdown expression showed a direct relation with reduced seed size and pod number accompanied with short stems and internodes [108]. Similarly, [109] characterized the first gene encoding for a ZFP in soybean functioning in petals, stamens and late developing seeds and playing a role as a transcriptional factor enhancing reproductive organs development. On the other hand, FPF1 is one of the most important families involved in the genetic control of flowering time in plants, a key trait for crop yield [110]. Constitutive expression of this gene in Arabidopsis and other species resulted in a dominant heritable trait of early flowering under both short- and long-day conditions [111] and in Cicer, CaFPF1 plays an important role in regulating the flowering time, pollen germination, and growth [112]. The last interval (QTN 3g205240), included six candidates from which we point out the UDP-sugar transporter, the ATP synthesis coupled proton transporter, the FCP1 homology domain-containing protein and the E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase (Table 1; Additional file 4). In Fabaceae, sugar transporters play a crucial role for plant productivity, as they coordinate sugar fluxes from the leaf towards sink organs (seed, fruit, root) and the supply of carbon towards the microorganisms of the rhizosphere [113]. The unique transporter AtSWEET15 gene in Arabidopsis is mostly expressed during seed development being involved in nutrient provision towards the embryo and for seed filling in soybean and rice [114, 115]. Noteworthy, it has been shown that these transporters were subjected to selection during crop domestication. The ATP synthesis coupled proton transporter, plays a key role in photosynthesis where a proton gradient drives ATP production to fuel the energy-intensive processes of seed development and storage, thus linking improved photosynthetic efficiency to increased crop yield [116]. Next, the FCP1 gene is linked to a stem cell pathway that may lead to increased crop yields by regulating stem cell activity [117]. Finally, the ubiquitin system is known to influence plant reproduction, growth, development, responses to the environment and final crop yield. E3 ubiquitin ligases are the major players in this pathway particularly affecting agronomic traits influenced by ubiquitination: seed size and weight, seed germination and accessory plant agronomic traits [118].

Chromosome Vf4 (overlapping region 8). Included two QTNs (4g114560 and 4g124760) environmentally stable for the yield related traits, hundred seeds weight (HSW_Spain.2020), seeds per pod (SP_Spain.2019) and pod per plant (PP_Spain.2019, PP_Spain.Global and PP_Global) in different environments co-localizing with QTLs for leaf size (LS_2006/07) and pod per node in field conditions (PNF_2010/11) (Fig. 1). The region contains 14 candidates, four of them unannotated (Table 1, Additional file 4). Prominent among the candidates associated with HSW are the NAC domain-containing protein and specially the serine protease. In rice, several NAC genes are highly expressed during seed development and are strongly associated with grain size and weight [119]. Remarkably, a serine protease specifically expressed in the endosperm of M. truncatula and Pisum sativum seeds during development was reported to be located at a chromosomal position coinciding with a seed weight QTL [120]. Similarly, a QTL for faba bean seed weight on chr. Vf4 has been reported [12] and both facts support the probable role of serine proteases in the control of seed size in legumes. Out of the remaining annotated candidates we highlight the heat shock factor protein and the adenylate isopentenyltransferase 5 (IPT5). In Medicago, the acquisition of dormancy and longevity occurs during late seed maturation concomitantly with the up-regulation of heat shock proteins [121] that also regulates seed and pod development in Brassica rapa [122]. On the other hand, the involvement of IPT5 in the biosynthesis of cytokinin during flowering has been widely confirmed [123]. From flowering to the early stages of ovule formation, exogenous cytokinin treatment has been shown to significantly prevent abortion and promote the number of pods per node pointing to a critical role for cytokinin in legume seed and pod development [124–126].

Chromosome Vf5 (overlapping region 9). Four QTNs for the yield related traits, pods per plant (PP_Spain.Global and PP_Global, QTN 5g030840), hundred seed weight in drought conditions (HSW_Drought, QTN 5g036440) and hundred seed weight global (HSW_Global, QTN 5g039080) and plot yield (PY_UK.2019, QTN 5g039640) co-localized with QTLs for the height of the first flower (H1F_2007/08), days to flowering (DF1_2006/07) and ovules per ovary (OVC_2008/09) in this region (Fig. 1). The overlapping cluster revealed 34 candidates, six of them unannotated (Additional file 4). The most promising, associated with PP, include a transcription factor HY5-like, the E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase and the transcriptional co-repressor. The bZIP-type transcription factor HY5 is a master regulator of physiological and biological processes and nutrient signalling which have a significant impact on plant development and crop yield [127]. As mentioned above, the ubiquitin system is known to affect seed size determination and weight, seed germination, and accessory plant agronomic traits affecting crop yield in different crops [118]. In soybean the expression pattern analyses of a ubiquitin conjugating enzyme in wide-pod resources suggested a positive regulatory role in pod width as well [128]. Interestingly, the next candidate is a transcriptional co-repressor that has been reported to play important roles in establishing the appropriate levels of gene expression during growth and development [129]. Both candidates may be part of a regulatory module for the control of grain width and weight in faba bean as the one described in rice and mediated by the GW2-WG1-OsbZIP47 module [130]. Thus, in rice, an E3 ubiquitin ligase (GW2) ubiquitinates WG1, encoding a glutaredoxin protein that promotes grain growth by increasing cell proliferation and targets it for degradation. WG1 associates with OsbZIP47 and represses its transcriptional activity by associating with the transcriptional co-repressor ASP1. Thus, GW2, WG1, and OsbZIP47 function in a common pathway to control grain growth in this crop. In line with these outcomes, our findings suggest that this regulatory module may have conserved functions in other crops providing new targets for improving seed size and weight in faba bean. The next QTN interval associated to HSW in drought conditions revealed eight candidates among which four, a vacuolar pyrophosphatase (VP), a lipid transfer proteins (LTP), a poly (A) binding protein (RBP protein) and a lactoylglutathione lyase/glyoxalase are underlined for their possible involvement in drought resistance. The overexpression of a VP in Arabidopsis, enhances plant resistance to salt and drought stresses [131] and VPs have been proposed as a possible strategy to engineer drought-resistant crop plants [132]. LTPs have also been reported to be involved in plant defence responses induced by biotic and abiotic stresses in different crops [133–136] and might be involved in the control of seed size as well. In wheat specific LTPs have been identified that are activated by drought stress, making them candidates for use in genetic engineering to improve drought tolerance [137]. Additionally, an LTP was found to confer resistance against rust infections, though overexpressing the relevant gene (TaLTP3) resulted in smaller seeds and lower thousand-grain weight in wheat [135]. RBPs are likely to modulate more than 60% of the plant transcriptome including stress responses. In Arabidopsis, an RNA-binding protein was induced while three were repressed in response to drought, cold and high-salinity [138]. Concurrently, several poly (A) binding protein transcripts exhibited differential expression in chickpea under the same stress conditions [139], which indicates that both plants employ changes in RNA-binding proteins to regulate their responses to environmental stress. The last candidate, a lactoylglutathione lyase/glyoxalase, helps plants tolerate drought by reducing the accumulation of reactive carbonyl species and reactive oxygen species which accumulate under stress. Thus, for example, a maize glyoxalase confers salt and drought tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis plants [140] and the overexpression of glyoxalase genes in species such as rice, tobacco or tomato resulted in plants with improved tolerance to abiotic stresses (reviewed by [141]). The QTN interval for HSW_Global included ten candidate genes among which an ABC transporter, a GPI transamidase component and a cytochrome P450 pointed out as the most promising candidates. ABC transporters are essential for plant development, playing roles in gametogenesis, seed development and germination, organ formation, and secondary growth [142]. The GPI transamidase complex is involved in the transition to flowering in plants because of their essential roles in the fertility of the male and female gametophytes [86]. Finally, the cytochrome P450 appears to function as a key regulator of flower maturation, synchronizing petal expansion and volatile emission [143]. In controlled pollination experiments with Arabidopsis a cytochrome P450 responds to fertilization and mutants with loss-of-function have reduced seed set [144]. A gene encoding cytochrome P450 in wheat affects seed size [145] and the over-expression of a cytochrome P450 gene from Jatropha curcas L., in transgenic tobacco seeds resulted in increased seed size and weight, as well as improved seed protein and fatty acid content [146]. The last QTN interval associated with PY_UK.2019 included six candidates among which we highlight two pentatricopeptide repeat-containing proteins (PRP) and the E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase BRE1-like. As reported by [13], genes encoding PPR proteins have been shown to play prominent roles in seed development in different crops. Thus, a PRP protein was partly responsible for the increased seed size and weight during domestication in peanut [147], influencing traits like photosynthesis and grain filling in maize [148, 149] and pod number in chickpea [150]. A mitochondrial pentatricopeptide repeat protein also affects flowering time in Arabidopsis thaliana [151]. On the other hand, extensive studies have demonstrated that protein ubiquitination plays key roles in multiple plant developmental stages and several abiotic stress responses [89]. A E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase BRE1-like has shown to regulate flowering time and yield potential in different crops [152]. The ubiquitin system is also involved in determining seed size. It was first noted when a QTL for grain size and weight in rice was found to encode the RING-type E3 ligase GW2 (GRAIN WIDTH 2) that negatively regulates seed size and final yield [153].

Chromosome Vf6 (overlapping region 10). The last overlapped region with QTN 6g117120 for seeds per plant (SPL_Spain.2019) co-localized with QTLs for HSW (HSW_2010/11) and seed per pod in field conditions (SPF_2010/11) (Fig. 1). Ten candidates were found in the interval (Additional File 4) with the phospholipase gene standing out as the most likely candidate. The overexpression or suppression of phospholipase genes was shown to be beneficial for crop yield due to an increase in resistance of the plants to a variety of pathogen infections and abiotic stresses [154]. A phospholipase D (PLD) enhanced Brassica napus growth and seed production in response to nitrogen availability [155]. Moreover, overexpression of an Arabidopsis PLD in soybean plants also increased seed production under nitrogen deprivation with and without nodulation [156]. These findings further suggest that different PLDs perform unique and important functions in specific plant growth, development or stress response processes [157] and for these reasons are expected to be utilized for crop improvement by genetic manipulations in the future [154].

COG analysis of candidate genes

Of the 152 candidate genes, 67 (44%) could not be assigned to a functional COG category (Additional file 5). These included genes with poorly characterized functions, those lacking orthologous group matches in the database, and others with no homology sequence found in the eukaryote database (Fig. 2). This may reflect assembly gaps or incomplete genome annotation. Alternatively, it could suggest that a substantial portion of these candidate genes are species-specific or poorly conserved across related taxa. Of the remaining 85, 35 (23%) of the candidate genes were associated to “cellular processes and signaling” (involved in cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis; post-translational modification, protein turnover, chaperones; signal transduction mechanisms; intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport; defense mechanisms; and cytoskeleton). Twenty-six (17%) were related with “metabolism” (energy production and conversion; amino acid, nucleotide, carbohydrate, lipid and inorganic ion transport and metabolism and secondary metabolites biosynthesis, transport and catabolism). Finally, the other 24 candidate genes (16%) were involved in “information storage and processing” (RNA processing and modification; translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis; transcription; and replication, recombination and repair) (Fig. 2; Additional file 5). No clear differences on the predicted biological function can be distinguished among the candidates for yield and drought response. Pleiotropy among candidate genes

Pleiotropism is a common event reported in QTL mapping and GWAS studies across different crop species [158–161] and is particularly valuable for breeding strategies such as pyramiding and marker-assisted selection (MAS) [162]. Pleiotropic effects may arise when a gene product performs multiple functions or influences different developmental pathways. Previous QTL studies performed in the RIL population Vf6 x Vf27 [38] identified 99 significant QTLs related to plant architecture, phenology and yield related traits. Colocalization of QTLs occurred in 13 major regions, joining three or more overlapping QTLs pleiotropic for architecture, autofertility and yield-related traits. In the present study, several putative candidate genes (QTNs) for yield and drought related traits, identified by GWAS [13, 33] in a worldwide panel of faba bean. Several significant markers appear to influence multiple traits, sometimes seemingly unrelated traits. In most cases, these repeated candidates were closely related to genes previously identified and validated in other crops. Notably, the 40S and 60S ribosomal subunits, found in chrs. Vf3, Vf5 and Vf6, exhibited clear pleiotropy among QTNs for SP, SPL, PP and PH under drought conditions. The ribosomal subunits are known to be crucial for protein synthesis and their proper function is essential for plant development and yield (COG classification, within the "Information Storage and Processing").

Two carboxylesterases (CXEs) located on chr. Vf2 were associated with HSW and PH under drought conditions, while significant SNPs for four E3 ubiquitin-protein ligases (on chrs. Vf2, Vf3 and Vf5) appear to be pleiotropic for SP, PP, PY and PH under drought conditions as well. CXEs and E3 ubiquitin ligases are both involved in “cellular processes” related to defense mechanisms, posttranslational modifications and signal transduction. They play crucial roles in plant growth, development, flowering time regulation and stress response [89] by regulating axillary bud growth in tobacco [90] or increasing branches and tillers in Arabidopsis through strigolactone metabolism [91].

Two ABC transporter family members were associated with HSW on chr. Vf5 and PP on chr. Vf1L. ABC transporters are involved in “metabolic processes” and play a crucial role in seed development and flowering. In rice, for instance, two ABC family transporters have been shown to participate in grain-filling highlighting their value in crop yield improvement [72]. Similarly, in chickpea an ABC transporter not only enhances seed weight and yield but also improves multiple desirable components, such as flowering/maturity time, seed protein, etc. [71].

Six Ser-Thr protein kinases related with “cellular processes and signalling”, showed pleiotropism with SPL, PP, PY and PH_under drought conditions and were located on chr. Vf1L and Vf1S. As mentioned above, protein kinases in chickpea play key roles in pollen maturation, fertilization, flowering time and final pod and seed yield [75].

Four pentatricopeptide repeats (PRP) were significant in the study, two of them, associated with PH in drought conditions in chr. Vf1L and Vf3 and with PY in chr. Vf5. Although their role is complex and not fully understood, PRPs are known to participate in various aspects of plant growth and development, including the regulation of plant height [163]. PRPs also regulate mitochondrial RNA editing which is essential for maintaining mitochondrial function, energy production, and stress tolerance [104]. For instance, [164] showed that specific rice PPR proteins, like OsPPR674, can regulate growth and drought sensitivity. Moreover, in chickpea PRPs have been linked to pollen maturation, fertilization, flowering time and final pod and seed yield [75].

Finally, three transcription factors and six proteins of unknown function (DUF), were also associated with yield related traits (PP and PY) in different chromosomes. Transcription factors appear to be crucial regulators of plant yield by influencing various plant growth and developmental processes such as photosynthesis, flowering, cell wall formation and responses to stress [165, 166], while the specific functions of proteins DUF remain to be determined. Therefore, further research is needed to fully understand their specific roles in faba bean.

The integration of QTLs and GWAS revealed overlapping regions where yield-related QTNs tended to colocalize more with QTLs than stress-related ones. This colocalization suggests that these overlapping regions harbor genes with a direct and significant influence on yield determination, rather than genes primarily involved in stress response. Such a pattern reflects a concentrated genetic architecture for yield, where evidence from both linkage-based and genome-wide analyses converge on the same genomic locations, highlighting key regulatory or structural genes contributing to agricultural performance. Nevertheless, extending QTNs intervals with 1-LOD support intervals from co-localized QTLs may obscure specific signals and increase the number of candidate genes, particularly in species like faba bean, with rapid LD decay. This approach, while strengthening evidence across methods, may oversimplify complex genetic regions and hinder the identification of precise causal genes.

While the single faba bean reference genome available is a crucial tool for comparative studies, gene identification, and basic research, it represents only a fraction of the total genetic variation within a species, potentially leading to variations in their actual gene landscapes, impacting how genes are located and what function they serve. Despite these limitations, the outcomes described here have facilitated both the refinement and the validation of candidate genes linked to yield-related traits in different genetic backgrounds. Thus, earlier studies related to PH and HSW [12, 167] identified QTLs on chr. Vf2 and Vf4. Interestingly, the position of the respective significant markers (AX-181187562 and AX-181462618) reported in chr. Vf2, falls within the confidence interval defined by HSW and PH in this study (Table 1, overlapping region 4). Similarly, a significant marker (D01869, 1g080440) for yield encoding a phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) reported by [37] in chr. Vf1S, colocalized in the overlapping interval determined by a QTN for PY and a QTL for PS reported here (Table 1, overlapping region 1). Different studies have shown that altering PEPC levels or activity can impact seed yield and quality [168, 169], thus confirming the accuracy and contribution of these candidates in yield.

Conclusion

The present study identifies candidate genes in faba bean with promising predicted functions related to yield improvement and drought resistance. Among the 152 significant QTNs detected, 19 (12.5%) lacked annotations in the current reference genome, underscoring the need to enhance genome accuracy and fill the gaps and missing regions, particularly in repetitive and complex genomes such as that of faba bean. For drought related traits the most frequent candidate genes included pentatricopeptide repeat-containing proteins, phosphatase 2 C, carboxylesterase, B3 domain-containing protein and cytochrome P450 while for yield components, members of the serine/threonine protein kinases family were the most abundant candidates. Although further experimental validation through fine-mapping, expression analyses, or reverse genetics approaches (e.g. CRISPR-based gene editing) is required, these genes, especially those exhibiting pleiotropy, represent valuable targets for marker-assisted breeding in the crop. Overall our findings provide new insights into the genetic mechanisms underlying drought tolerance and yield and pave the way for the identification of reliable candidate genes for improving these traits in faba bean.
