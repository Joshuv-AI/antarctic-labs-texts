Embedding Music in Care at Mayo Clinic
Authors: Kathleen J Van Buren, Christina Wood, Sarah A Mensink, Brandon N Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs interest in arts in health care contexts grows, arts practitioners and scholars are pointing to the need for more strategic and sustainable approaches that will enable the continued growth of programming in the future. It is critical not only to identify factors necessary for developing new programming but also to consider how to fully embed services within an institution or system. This case study examines the provision of music services for patients by the Dolores Jean Lavins Center for Humanities in Medicine at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota, as one example of how arts programming for patients can be embedded into an institution. Although this case offers a useful example of how programming can be enhanced by aligning with existing structures and refining processes, it also draws attention to the tension between an ideal framework for embedding programming and the challenges that can come from limited resources.

In a 2023 survey of over 3000 American adults, 75% of participants noted that they would likely follow the recommendation of a doctor to “participate in the arts as a means of improving your physical or mental health,” and 60% agreed with the statement, “The arts have helped me cope during times of mental or emotional distress.”1 A 2007 United States national survey by The Joint Commission confirmed that nearly half of the nation’s hospitals and health care institutions had active arts programs, and indications are that this number has continued to increase since then.2 Such expansion has been accompanied by a robust body of scholarly evidence of the major roles arts can play in supporting health and in preventing and treating illness.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 In some cases, arts interventions also offer equivalent or greater cost-effectiveness in comparison with other interventions.4,5

As interest in arts in health care contexts grows, arts practitioners and scholars working across fields—including but not exclusive to arts in health,9 creative arts therapies,10,11 and medical and health humanities12—are pointing to the need for more strategic and sustainable approaches that will enable the continued growth of programming in the future.8,13,14 It is critical not only to identify factors necessary for developing new programming but also to consider how to fully embed services within an institution or system.15 To be most broadly effective, innovations must be carefully integrated into existing care pathways, technical infrastructures, and ways of working.16 Although multiple frameworks and toolkits are available for various types of programming,17, 18, 19, 20 Bos et al15 identify the following 5 helpful guiding principles for successfully embedding arts services in health care contexts: create awareness for the need and value of services; build trust with stakeholders to create a cyclical referral process; invest in skill-building for those offering services; ensure clients receive support to engage in services; and invest in aligning relevant structures, processes, and resources.

Case Report

Building on a history of innovation and a team-based approach, Mayo Clinic is a place where “experts work together to solve the most challenging unmet needs of patients.”21 Part of the historical and ongoing fabric of Mayo Clinic is “a philosophy of ‘an environment of healing’”22 in which arts and humanities play a prominent role. Examining the development of music therapy and music at the bedside services for patients by the Dolores Jean Lavins Center for Humanities in Medicine (henceforth Lavins Center) at Mayo Clinic offers an example of how the guiding principles for embedding arts services by Bos et al15 apply to a real context.23

Music therapy involves board-certified music therapists working with patients as part of their clinical care plans to meet clinical goals. Bedside arts programming, including music at the bedside, enables professional artists to visit patients in their rooms to provide enjoyment and distraction during hospital stays. Both services are available at no cost to Mayo Clinic patients through benefactor funding. Studies at Mayo Clinic confirm the diverse benefits patients experience by engaging in these services, including for stress relief, relaxation, pain relief, spiritual support, and emotional support.24, 25, 26, 27

Music therapy services have been provided through a contracted agency, Healing Rhythms Music Therapy, since 2007. The Lavins Center began contracting with Healing Rhythms in 2009, focusing on filling requests outside departments with direct contracts. Primary areas of focus are the long stay unit, internal medicine, cardiology, family medicine, neurology, intensive care, and trauma. Top referral reasons are to support coping, cognitive stimulation, and pain/symptom management and for psychosocial support. Since 2025, the Lavins Center team has taken a leadership role in managing a joint contract with Healing Rhythms for multiple departments. This change has created a more efficient system for renewing contracts and has led to greater consistency in management of services for patients.

Music at the bedside was established in 2008 and initially conceived as a miniconcert series featuring contracted artists in patient/visitor lounges and nursing stations. In 2024, the Lavins Center created a supplementary Artist Humanities in Medicine position with a focus on bedside arts in order to further integrate providers into Mayo Clinic as an organization and to facilitate standardized training of staff and implementation of programming. This supplemental position enables the Lavins Center to increase numbers of artists, variety of artistic genres, and staff hours without the administrative logistics and time required to create new contracts or to request approval from the institution for additional full-time staff positions.

In 2024, music therapists provided 4 hours per week, or 226 hours per year, of services through the Lavins Center. This included 257 patient visits, with 117 family/caregivers present, in addition to 84 check-ins (face-to-face visits but where additional support is declined, for example, because patients are engaged with another provider). Departments served include Hospice, Palliative, Charter House, and the Intensive Care Unit. Between June and December 2024, 147 patients received music at the bedside services. In addition, weekly visual arts and music group sessions were held for patients on the long stay unit.

Music therapy and bedside arts services are integrated into the electronic health record (EHR) and available through consult orders placed by care teams. Integration of these services into the EHR has multiple benefits: easing the burden on care providers submitting requests, improving turnaround time between service requests and the services, eliminating paper documentation of services, providing a mechanism for tracking patient contact and services provided, and enhancing data reporting options. It is also within the American Music Therapy Association Code of Ethics and Certification Board for Music Therapists Code of Professional Practice to document services.28,29 EHR processes for music therapy and bedside arts services continue to be optimized in response to health care best practices, leadership guidelines, and clinical needs, with attention to achieving outcomes in a timely and succinct way. Recent changes have included adding options for triaging to allow artists to review requests and ensure that the appropriate services are provided to patients based on needs.

Lavins Center staff take an approach that the closer the person is to the bedside, and the higher the acuity, the more training that should be in place to protect patients, caregivers, and the professionals delivering services. Music therapists must complete robust training with a minimum of a bachelor’s degree in music therapy, practicum, internship, and board-certification requirements and must maintain their certification.28 Bedside artists are highly trained artists with performance or other arts-specific expertise, but they are not licensed creative arts therapists. Recognizing that practitioners are working in a hospital environment with systems in place to support safety and privacy and that they are working with patients with unique needs, the Lavins Center has developed a training process in collaboration with Mayo Clinic’s Simulation Center. Job shadowing and preceptorship also are important components of onboarding and training.

Lavins Center staff are continually exploring how to pull data from the medical record. Historically, this process has been cumbersome, inefficient, and incomplete, resulting in inefficient use of staffing with efforts spent on reporting that could otherwise be redirected to patient care. In addition, several departments currently are duplicating efforts to generate reports. To address these issues, Lavins Center staff in consultation with Healing Rhythms staff are updating the consult order to include additional fields for data mining. Lavins Center staff facilitate regular meetings for bedside artists and music therapists to work together to consider further enhancements to patient programming including EHR processes. Lavins Center staff also are working to increase visibility for programming to ensure that patients and care teams know that services are available to them.

Discussion

The infrastructure for patient music programming at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota, has trended toward providing more efficient, impactful, and sustainable services, as well as enabling better documentation and data mining. The guiding principles identified by Bos et al15—in particular, focus on aligning with relevant structures, processes, and resources as well as creating awareness of services and training staff—have been features of our work to embed and enhance programming. Lavins Center staff build on Mayo Clinic’s long history of dedication to patient needs, team-based approaches, and a focus on a holistic healing environment and are grateful for support from benefactors for programming.

Yet, the process toward embedding and enhancing music services for patients at Mayo Clinic has not always been linear or according to a predetermined plan; changes to services and consult orders, for example, have often been based on institutional changes and opportunities and limited by resource availability. Currently, demand for services outweighs supply. We do not have sufficient staff to be able to serve the number of patients who might benefit. Furthermore, with staff focused on day to day running of programming, little time is available for analyzing and publishing the data that we are collecting. To help address these issues, we are seeking to partner with Mayo Clinic colleagues and learners on research focused on arts services for patients. We hope that such partnerships will result in more evidence-based reports that can support quality improvement and aid us in advocating for additional resources.

Conclusion

The attention by arts practitioners and scholars to identifying frameworks for developing and embedding arts into health care contexts is critical for the expansion of programming. This case suggests that it will also be important to discuss challenges and limitations that might impact on the adoption or full implementation of proposed frameworks.
