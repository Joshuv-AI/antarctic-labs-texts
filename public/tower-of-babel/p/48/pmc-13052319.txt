Exploring small‐world brain connectivity in aging and its relationship with cognitive reserve
Authors: Chiara Pappalettera, Selene Rossi, Anna Fabbrocino, Alessia Cacciotti, Lorenzo Nucci, Francesca Miraglia, Federico Frasca, Lavinia Valeriani, Petra Vukoja, Daniela Iacoviello, Paolo Maria Rossini, Guido Maria Giuffrè, Xu‐Yun Hua, Fabrizio Vecchio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe aging brain's vulnerability to decline emphasizes the importance of strategies preserving cognitive function and independence. As age‐related brain changes do not always match clinical symptoms, reserve, encompassing brain reserve (BR) and cognitive reserve (CR), has emerged as a key mechanism supporting resilience and mitigating cognitive decline during aging. This study adopts an exploratory approach to examine whether age‐ and CR‐related differences are reflected in large‐scale brain network organization, as assessed through electroencephalographic (EEG) connectivity metrics.METHODSResilience to age‐related cognitive changes was investigated by relating CR, measured using the Cognitive Reserve Index (CRI), to brain network topology quantified by the Small‐World (SW) index derived from resting‐state EEG recordings. SW differences were first examined between age groups and then within each age group according to CRI levels, across EEG frequency bands (Delta, Theta, Alpha 1, Alpha 2, Beta 1, Beta 2, Gamma).RESULTSThe analysis of SW values showed distinct patterns between groups. Adults exhibited significant higher SW values in Delta and Theta bands, but lower values in Alpha 2 compared to Elderly. Within Elderly, higher CRI correlated with greater SW values in Delta, Beta 2, and Gamma bands, and marginally significant lower Alpha 2 values.DISCUSSIONSimilar Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores across groups suggest these differences may reflect compensatory mechanisms, highlighting CR's potential role in preserving cognitive efficiency and resilience during aging. In line with the cognitive reserve framework, these findings provide supportive—though indirect—evidence that individual differences in CR are associated with distinct patterns of brain network organization during healthy aging. Overall, this study highlights the potential relevance of large‐scale neural network dynamics as markers of adaptive brain functioning in later life, while underscoring the noncausal and exploratory nature of these associations.Keywords: brain aging, cognitive reserve, cognitive reserve index, electroencephalography, Small World index

BACKGROUND

Aging is a natural process characterized by a gradual decline of physiological systems’ efficiency, 1 impacting various functions, increasing frailty and heightening susceptibility to diseases. 2 Aging strongly affects the nervous system, making brain and cognitive health vital for independence. 3

A central concept related to maintaining brain health during aging is that of “reserve”. 4 Repeated observations showing that clinical manifestation often does not align with the extent of brain degeneration or pathology led to the hypothesis of a reserve acting as a protective buffer for the brain and its cognitive functions. 5 Individuals with greater reserve could be better equipped to withstand aging and related diseases. 6

The concept of “reserve” is typically divided into brain reserve (BR) and cognitive reserve (CR). BR refers to interindividual differences in brain structure and biological resources—such as brain volume, neuronal count, or synaptic density—that may influence the brain's tolerance to age‐related or pathological changes. 7 Within this framework, greater BR is considered protective while lower BR signifies greater vulnerability. 8 The onset of symptoms, as observed in neurodegenerative diseases, indicates that BR declined significantly. However, thanks to CR, individuals can maintain cognitive function. 9

CR captures the brain's ability to adapt and compensate for damage by using existing or supplementary resources. This suggests that while BR may be insufficient, a robust CR can delay or reduce symptoms of underlying brain pathology. 10 Rather than positing a fixed threshold at which impairment emerges, the CR framework proposes that individual variability in life experiences—such as education, occupational complexity, and cognitively stimulating activities—shapes the efficiency, flexibility, and adaptability of large‐scale neural systems. 11 From this perspective, CR can be thought of as an “active”, experience‐dependent process that influences how distributed neural networks are recruited and coordinated during cognitive processing. 12

CR is typically assessed through proxies such as years of education, occupational complexity, and intellectual activities. While offering valuable insight, these proxies may not capture the complexity of CR. 13 To better reflect this complexity, tools like the Cognitive Reserve Index questionnaire (CRIq) have been developed. 14 The CRIq evaluates an individual's engagement in three domains—education, occupational complexity, and leisure activities—providing a comprehensive score reflecting the accumulation of CR over their life course. While useful, these parameters are indirect; linking them to neurophysiological mechanisms, especially electroencephalographic (EEG) data, is essential. 15 The electroencephalogram (EEG) represents an available, cost‐effective and easy to use resource, reflecting characteristics of the brain itself but also of underlying neural dynamics. 16 EEG enables the noninvasive measurement of brain activity with excellent temporal resolution, making it especially suitable to capture rapid neural processes underlying cognitive functions. EEG provides an effective method to investigate the dynamic neural mechanisms that support CR. Some studies have analyzed the relationships between CR and EEG‐derived indices. 17 Higher CR individuals showed smaller changes in event‐related potentials as task difficulty increased, suggesting more efficient neural processing. 18 Individuals with high CR also exhibited higher long‐range connectivity between the occipital lobes and cortical regions, associated with better spatial working memory and sustained attention performances. 19 High‐CR individuals also exhibited better performance on working memory tasks and distinct brain activity patterns. 20

A valuable tool to uncover neural mechanisms underlying CR is represented by the implementation of graph theory to EEG data, 21 characterizing functional segregation and integration across different regions of the brain. 21 A promising metric is the Small World (SW) index, which synthetizes both specialized and integrated information processing in the brain. 22 Alterations in SW organization have been associated with aging‐related changes in brain network efficiency, often reflecting a reorganization of neural resources across frequency bands.

For the present study, 157 subjects were enrolled and divided into Adults and Elderly based on age, with the initial aim of exploratorily examining differences in SW network organization between these two groups. More importantly, the study aimed to examine how brain network organization, assessed via the SW index, is influenced by different levels of CRI in Adults and Elderly healthy individuals, seeking to uncover potential age‐related variations in brain network organization and assessing whether CR contributes to network modulation across the adult‐to‐elderly lifespan. In particular, it was hypothesized that the impact of CR on network organization might differ across age groups, becoming more evident when age‐related neural changes increase the demand for compensatory mechanisms.

A central question understands whether the impact of CR on brain network metrics differs between adults and older individuals. To verify participants’ cognitive function, their Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) 23 scores were examined, providing a general screening measure to examine whether differences in cognitive performance are mirrored by corresponding patterns in EEG network organization. In this context, the findings are interpreted within a CR framework, in which network‐level alterations are considered potential markers of resilience or compensatory adaptation in healthy aging, rather than direct indicators of neuroprotection or cognitive decline.

METHODS

Participants

A total of 157 neurologically and psychiatrically healthy individuals were enrolled for this study. Participants were recruited through a dedicated recruitment campaign conducted within our research center, as well as via external channels, including social media. Written informed consent was obtained in accordance with the World Medical Association Code of Ethics (1997). All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, complied with national regulations, and were approved by the IRCCS San Raffaele Roma Institutional Review Board. Cognitive status was assessed via the MMSE as a global measure of cognitive function. Participants were stratified into two age groups: Adults (< 60 years; age range: [35–59]) and Elderly (≥ 60 years, age range: [60–91]). EEG recording and preprocessing

Resting‐state, eyes‐closed EEG was recorded for ≥5 min while participants sat comfortably in an electrically shielded, acoustically isolated, dimly lit room. Brain activity was acquired using a 64‐channel EEG system (Brain Products Brain Amp DC) arranged to the augmented International 10–20 system, with electrodes mounted on the cap, grounded at Az and referenced to FCz. Additional vertical/horizontal EOG channels for ocular monitoring and an ECG channel on the left clavicle. Electrode—skin impedances were kept < 5 kΩ, and signals were sampled at 5000 Hz. Data were processed in MATLAB (MathWorks) using custom scripts based on EEGLAB (Swartz Centre for Computational Neuroscience, La Jolla, CA). EEG was downsampled to 512 Hz and band‐pass filtered (0.2–47 Hz) using a band‐pass finite impulse filter (FIR). Artifact removal followed a multistage pipeline: EEG was segmented into 2 s epochs, visually inspected, and artifact‐contaminated epochs discarded, ensuring ≥100 clean epochs per subject. Independent component analysis (ICA) was then applied for artifact detection via EEGLAB. Components identified as artifactual were manually removed, with < 20% of components rejected to preserve neural signal integrity.

Functional connectivity

Functional connectivity was assessed using exact low‐resolution electromagnetic tomography (eLORETA), a linear inverse solution estimating the 3D cortical distribution of current density. Connectivity was computed across 84 regions of interest (ROIs), corresponding to the 42 bilateral Brodmann areas (1–47, excluding 12, 14–16, 26), by averaging voxel‐wise neuronal activity within each ROI. For each subject and hemisphere, intracortical Lagged Linear Coherence—calculated using the “all nearest voxels” method—was computed for all ROI pairs within each frequency band: Delta (2–4 Hz), Theta (4–8 Hz), Alpha1 (8–10.5 Hz), Alpha2 (10.5–13 Hz), Beta1 (13–20 Hz), Beta2 (20–30 Hz), and Gamma (30–45 Hz). SW

A weighted network is a framework used to represent relationships between a set of elements (vertices) that might be connected by links (edges) of varying weight. This concept can be applied to the context of brain networks, where vertices represent specific brain regions and edges represent anatomical or functional connections. In this study, weighted, undirected networks were built where the vertices correspond to the estimated cortical sources in the Brodmann areas and edges were weighted by the Lagged Linear value calculated within each pair of vertices.

To characterize the network's organizational properties, segregation and integration measures were evaluated using clustering (C) and path length (L) coefficients. The clustering coefficient represents the degree to which network elements form separate clusters. Specifically, for a given vertex i, C is quantified by the number of triangles in which vertex i participates, normalized by the maximum possible number of triangles. To obtain the weighted clustering coefficient CW , the number of triangles can be replaced with the sum of triangle intensities. For all vertices of the graph, the mean clustering coefficient is firstly computed and then averaged, representing the tendency of a network's elements to form local clusters. On the other hand, the path length coefficient represents the capacity of a network to become interconnected and exchange information. Given two vertices i and j, the weighted characteristic path length LW represents the shortest weighted path length between i and j.

To account for individual variability and facilitate comparisons across subjects, the characteristic path length and clustering coefficient values were divided by their mean values, computed by averaging in the frequency bands, in order to obtain individual normalized measures.

The SW coefficient (SW) was then calculated to assess the balance between local segregation and global integration within the brain network. To evaluate the SW coefficient, the ratio between normalized C to the normalized L (CW to the normalized LW) with respect to the frequency bands was computed.

Statistical analysis

To address the study's preliminary aim—examining differences in brain network organization associated with physiological aging across age groups—an unpaired two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to examine SW values across the factors Group (Adults, Elderly) and Band (Delta, Theta, Alpha 1, Alpha2, Beta 1, Beta 2, Gamma). To explore the role of CR, two additional unpaired two‐way ANOVAs were conducted to analyze SW values within specific age groups. In particular, these two analysis ANOVA were conducted, one for each group ‐Adults and Elderly‐ across the factors Level (Low CR, High CR) and Band (Delta, Theta, Alpha 1, Alpha2, Beta 1, Beta 2, Gamma) to evaluate the differences in SW between subjects with Low CR and those with High CR. To account for violations of the sphericity assumption in the repeated‐measures ANOVA, the Greenhouse‐Geisser correction was applied. Duncan's post hoc test was used with a significance level of 0.05. All statistical analyzes were performed using Statistica v.8 (StatSoft Inc.).

Furthermore, a Student's t‐test was conducted to compare raw MMSE scores between the Adult and the Elderly group. Additionally, the test was performed within the same group, between Low CR and High CR subjects, in order to assess potential statistically significant differences in cognitive performance across these subgroups.

RESULTS

Age‐related SW modulation

The unpaired two‐way ANOVA conducted to explore differences in terms of EEG SW characteristics and physiological aging between the groups of Adults and Elderly revealed a statistically significant effect between Group and Band (F (6,936) = 6.8417, p = 0.0001). Duncan's post hoc analysis indicated that Adults exhibited higher values of SW than the Elderly in Delta (p = 0.0242) and Theta (p = 0.0465) bands. Conversely, Adults presented significantly lower SW values in the Alpha 2 band (p = 0.0001). CRI‐related SW modulation in the two age groups

The unpaired two‐way ANOVA conducted on the EEG characteristics of the two age groups revealed different results. Specifically, the ANOVA examining differences between adults with Low and High CRI did not produce a statistically significant effect across levels and bands (F (6,252) = 0.868, p = 0.518). The Student's t‐test assessing differences in MMSE raw scores revealed no significant differences between the Adult and Elderly groups (p = 0.1839). DISCUSSION

Brain changes during aging mirror “natural/physiological” body age‐related deterioration, making brain health crucial for maintaining independence and quality of life during years. In this context, CR plays a fundamental role as a protective factor that may help buffer or modulate the effects of age‐related neural decline. 24 Aging is associated with reduced efficiency in key cognitive domains, interrelated cognitive domains‐processing speed, working memory and long‐term memory—that play a crucial role in supporting complex cognitive tasks, including reasoning, problem‐solving, and decision‐making. Declines in one domain can have cascading effects on others, amplifying overall cognitive difficulties. Several studies have confirmed that these domains tend to decline with age. 3 , 25

By analyzing differences in brain network organization using the SW metric across age groups and CRIq, the results showed some possible evidence of age‐related differences across EEG frequency bands. In particular, Adults exhibited higher SW values than Elderly in Delta and Theta bands, and lower values in Alpha 2. It is worth reminding that lower SW values reflect more ordered networks, while higher SW values indicate more random‐like networks organization. 26 For these reasons, higher SW values in Adults within the Delta and Theta bands, namely a more random‐like network, may reflect less structured activity in these “slow” frequency bands, which can be interpreted as a marker of typical brain dynamics. This aligns with the idea that higher network randomness in the Delta and Theta bands reflects a more flexible and adaptive brain function, in graph theoretical sense. In contrast, Elderly showed reduced randomness in graph network, potentially due to aging‐related reductions in synaptic plasticity and neuronal communication. 27 As these low‐frequency rhythms play a limited role in active cognitive function in eyes‐closed resting states, 28 a less organized network, as seen for Adults, may indicate a healthy and physiologically coherent brain state while a more regular, organized network, as seen for Elderly, may be indicative of functional disconnection. This is supported by numerous studies showing increased Delta and Theta activity in the EEG power spectrum of the healthy aging brain, representing the electrophysiological counterpart of the network organization. 29

Additionally, Adults demonstrated lower SW values in the Alpha 2 band compared to Elderly. The Alpha rhythm is related to cognitive and memory performance and is typically associated with strong phase synchronization and organized activity in eyes‐closed resting states. 30 In particular, the Alpha 1 rhythm is primarily linked to overall attentional readiness, whereas the Alpha 2 rhythm is particularly responsive to semantic memory demands or the processing of sensorimotor information. 31 The higher SW values observed in the Elderly group for the Alpha 2 band suggest a less coherent, more fragmented network organization, indicative of reduced efficiency in cognitive processes reliant on these neural dynamics. This aligns with the established age‐related Alpha rhythm slowing, implying that with aging a less organized network in Alpha 2 is expected due to the peak shifting toward lower frequencies, and is consistent with previous research. 32 These findings are in line with prior research and should be interpreted as age‐related alterations in network dynamics rather than markers of impairment per se.

When examining the role of CR, notable differences emerged between the two age groups. In Adults, CRI level did not significantly affect SW values, while the Elderly group showed a clear class distinction: high‐CRI individuals exhibited significantly greater SW in Delta, Beta 2, and Gamma bands, and marginally significant lower values in the Alpha 2 band compared to their low‐CRI counterparts. Rather than indicating superior or inferior network organization, these findings suggest that CR‐related modulation of network dynamics may become more evident when age‐related neural changes increase the demand for adaptive or compensatory mechanisms.

In adults, the absence of significant effects suggests preserved neural resources, reducing the need for compensatory strategies to support cognitive functioning. 33 In the Elderly group, where age‐related changes lead to an overall brain integrity decline, CR becomes increasingly important; it is in this group that significant differences emerged. High‐CRI participants generally reported a more enriched life history, encompassing their education, work experiences, and lifestyle. 34 A life enriched of stimuli, novel challenges, and continuous learning opportunities enhances and optimizes brain networks by fostering processes such as neuro/synaptic‐genesis, the strengthening of synaptic connections, and increasing neural plasticity. 35 These neurobiological changes contribute to a greater CR, enabling individuals to maintain cognitive function and performance through compensatory mechanisms despite brain decline. 36 Supporting this view, other studies 18 , 20 have proposed that variations in neural efficiency associated with CR may provide protection against age‐related decline and neuropathological damage. Interestingly, in our research, these significant effects in the Elderly group emerged in the Delta frequency band, with individuals with higher CRI showing higher SW values. This mirrors previous findings in which Adults outperformed Elderly, suggesting that higher CR in the Elderly may serve a similar functional role to a younger age in maintaining cognitive efficiency. 37 In other words, while age‐related neural decline may generally place elderlies at a disadvantage, a higher CR appears to mitigate this effect. These findings align with previous studies investigating brain network topology through graph‐theoretical metrics in physiological and pathological aging. Regarding physiological aging Knyazev et al. 38 reported that older participants exhibited higher pathlength values than younger participants. Consistently, the study found that younger participants showed higher clustering coefficient values, further indicating more efficient local connectivity. Similar patterns have been observed in pathological conditions. For instance, Miraglia et al. 39 analyzed differences in SW properties of EEG recorded under eyes‐closed conditions among healthy controls, mild cognitive impairment (MCI) subjects, and Alzheimer's disease (AD) patients. Higher SW values were observed in the Delta band for healthy subjects, while MCI participants showed intermediate values between healthy and AD groups, suggesting that higher Delta band SW values are associated with a more preserved brain network configuration, closer to that of healthy individuals. 40

Notably, in our research these differences in graph measures were also found in the Beta 2 band, with Adults showing higher SW values. This band is associated with age‐vulnerable cognitive processes such as attention, memory, and cognitive control. 41 In individuals with higher CRI, these networks may remain functionally efficient despite age‐related decline, supporting the notion of a compensatory mechanism preserving cognitive performance through more resilient and adaptable connectivity patterns. 42 Similarly, Stam et al., 43 revealed a reduction in phase synchronization of EEG signals among AD in the Beta band, accompanied by significantly longer pathlengths, indicating decreased global efficiency in brain network organization. Notably, a significant negative correlation was observed between MMSE scores and pathlengths, linking better cognitive performance with more efficient large‐scale information transfer across brain regions. 44 These results support the hypothesis that, as brain integrity declines with age or disease, compensatory mechanisms may help maintain cognitive function. 45 Shorter pathlengths in individuals with higher MMSE scores, may reflect the recruitment of more efficient, resilient network configurations that counterbalance neurodegenerative effects. This interpretation aligns with prior evidence suggesting that in Adults preserved neural resources reduce the need for compensation. 46 In contrast, CR becomes crucial in sustaining efficient network dynamics and preserving cognitive abilities in Elderly, especially with AD. Moreover, the finding in the Gamma band aligns with previous research 47 showing positive correlations between Gamma‐band SW and performance on both the Forward and Backward Digit Span tasks. 48 Since these tasks rely on cognitive functions that are particularly vulnerable to aging, such as working memory and executive processes, the increased Gamma‐band SW in high‐CRI Elderly may reflect preserved capacity to supports these functions. This pattern therefore supports the compensatory role of CR in sustaining cognitive performance in older individuals. Lastly, a trend toward significance was found in the Alpha 2 band, revealing higher SW values in low‐CRI Elderly compared to their high‐CRI counterparts. Considering that Alpha activity during eyes‐closed resting state is associated with strong phase synchronization and large‐scale network organization, this result suggests that higher CR in elderly individuals is linked to a more organized brain network. 49 This trend was also found in Adults, suggesting that CR contributes to preserve typical brain dynamics processes and architecture, potentially mitigating age‐related changes.

Cognitive functioning, as assessed by the MMSE, did not differ significantly across age groups or CRI levels. Importantly, all participants scored within the normal range, confirming the absence of overt cognitive impairment. The inclusion of the MMSE was therefore intended as a global cognitive screening tool, rather than as a measure expected to discriminate between groups. The lack of MMSE differences indicates that the observed alterations in EEG‐derived brain network organization may not be driven by measurable cognitive decline, but instead likely reflect underlying compensatory mechanisms associated with CR. In this context, individuals with higher CR may preserve cognitive performance through more efficient or adaptable network configurations, despite age‐related changes in brain organization. This dissociation between preserved global cognitive scores and altered neural network properties supports the hypothesis that CR buffers the functional impact of brain aging before deficits become detectable at the behavioral level.

It is important to acknowledge that this study has some limitations, including the non‐proportionality between low‐ and high‐CRI groups, which may affect generalizability and statistical power, and the fact that the CRI is a subjective, self‐reported measure, which may introduce variability. In addition, EEG‐derived graph‐theoretical metrics provide indirect indices of large‐scale brain network organization and do not allow inferences about underlying causal mechanisms. Moreover, MMSE is a global screening scale not capturing subtle cognitive change. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as supportive rather than confirmatory. Future research should therefore aim to recruit larger and more balanced samples, integrate subjective measures with objective proxies of CR, and employing more sensitive neuropsychological measures to more precisely characterize the relationship between cognitive performance, network organization, and compensatory mechanisms, and adopt longitudinal designs to better characterize how reserve‐related network adaptations contribute to resilience and compensatory processes across the aging trajectory.

Despite these limitations, these findings support the idea that CR is associated with differences in large‐scale brain network organization in healthy aging, particularly in later life. While not establishing causality, the results are consistent with the view that CR may contribute to maintaining functional brain efficiency through compensatory network mechanisms. Overall, this study enhances understanding of how CR may support the maintenance of brain function with age and suggests the potential value of integrating rehabilitation strategies and promoting healthy, active, and stimulating lifestyles to sustain these protective mechanisms.
