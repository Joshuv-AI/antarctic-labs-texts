Research on metabolic characteristics of multiple sclerosis
Authors: Diyuan Wang, Wenguang Feng, Haibin Wang, Xuhua Ying, Wenqiang Cai, Longcheng Guo, Jinteng Hou, Tingjia Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
By comparing the fecal metabolite profiles of multiple sclerosis patients with those of healthy controls, to screen for significantly different metabolites and provide a basis for the discovery of potential biomarkers; further analyze the molecular functions of differential metabolites and their enriched metabolic pathways, aiming to reveal disease-related metabolic abnormalities and offer clues for the study of multiple sclerosis pathogenesis and treatment strategies. Fecal samples were collected from 37 multiple sclerosis patients and 30 age- and gender-matched healthy controls. Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-TOF MS) was used to detect fecal metabolite profiles. Data quality was assessed through total ion chromatograms (TIC) of quality control (QC) samples and principal component analysis (PCA) of all samples. Multivariate statistical analyses, including PCA, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were performed. Differential metabolites were screened based on variable importance in projection (VIP > 1) in the OPLS-DA model and significant differences (P < 0.05) in t-tests. Further analyses, including cluster analysis, correlation analysis, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, were conducted to elucidate the biological functions and metabolic pathway characteristics of the differential metabolites. A total of 552 metabolites were identified in the multiple sclerosis group and the healthy control group, with 56 differential metabolites (P < 0.05, VIP > 1) screened. Among them, 8 metabolites, including L-pyroglutamic acid, 4-pyridoxic acid, 2-oxoadipic acid, L-phenylalanine, and 3-methylphenylacetic acid, were upregulated; 48 metabolites, including propionic acid, isobutyric acid, oleic acid, α-linolenic acid, adenosine, 9,10-DiHOME, palmitic acid, succinate, lithocholic acid, pentadecanoic acid, L-isoleucine, androsterone sulfate, L-leucine, 3-(3-hydroxyphenyl)propionic acid, nicotinic acid, L-arginine, isomaltose, thymidine, 4-aminobutyric acid, uracil, choline, hydroxyarachidonic acid, trehalose, 4-oxoretinol, 3-methylhistidine, DL-norvaline, creatinine, and capsaicin, were downregulated. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the differential metabolites were mainly enriched in pathways such as protein digestion and absorption, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, central carbon metabolism in cancer, ABC transporters, mTOR signaling pathway, amino acid biosynthesis, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis. Significant differences exist in the fecal metabolite profiles between multiple sclerosis patients and healthy controls, with amino acids and fatty acids being the most enriched differential metabolite categories, suggesting potential biomarker value. Metabolic pathways such as protein digestion and absorption, central carbon metabolism in cancer, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, ABC transporters, mTOR signaling pathway, amino acid biosynthesis, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis may be involved in disease development. This study provides metabolomic evidence for uncovering the pathophysiological mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets of multiple sclerosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42501-3.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease characterized by inflammatory demyelination and neurodegeneration in the central nervous system, primarily affecting young adults, with a higher incidence in women than men1. Clinically, the relapsing-remitting type is the most common, often leading to progressive neurological dysfunction and disability after repeated episodes, making it the leading cause of non-traumatic disability in young people1. Current diagnosis relies on the 2017 McDonald criteria2, and treatment mainly involves high-dose steroid therapy during acute phases and immunomodulation during remission. However, challenges such as difficulties in early diagnosis, significant treatment side effects, and high costs remain, necessitating deeper exploration of its pathogenesis and the discovery of reliable biomarkers.

Recent studies have revealed that the gut microbiome plays a critical role in regulating immune responses and inflammatory reactions via the “gut-brain axis“3. Feces, as a direct product of gut microbiota and their metabolic activities, offer the advantages of being non-invasive, easily accessible, and capable of dynamically reflecting host-microbe co-metabolic states4. Gut microbiota-derived metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids have been shown to modulate regulatory T cell (Treg) function, influencing autoimmune processes5,6. Therefore, analyzing fecal metabolites in MS patients not only helps uncover the link between gut metabolic disturbances and disease progression but also provides a valuable window for exploring non-invasive biomarkers.

Metabolomics, a key branch of systems biology, comprehensively reflects the pathophysiological state of an organism at the downstream small-molecule level. Compared to genomics and proteomics, metabolomics is closer to phenotype7, and untargeted techniques can unbiasedly detect thousands of metabolites, with functional pathways further elucidated using databases like KEGG. Among these, ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS) has become a core technology in metabolomics research due to its high sensitivity, resolution, and broad coverage. It has revealed significant disruptions in pathways such as energy metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and lipid metabolism in MS studies involving blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and animal models.

Although blood and cerebrospinal fluid metabolomics have been widely reported in multiple sclerosis (MS)7, these biological matrices are difficult to directly reflect the intestinal microecology and its co-metabolic status with the host. Although previous studies have explored gut metabolic characteristics of MS through fecal metabolomics, such as the reductions in SCFAs reported in MS patients by Nohara S et al.8. most research has focused on Western populations with limited sample sizes and constrained metabolic pathway analysis. This study is the first to conduct a non-targeted fecal metabolomic analysis in a northern Chinese MS cohort, systematically revealing alterations in a variety of metabolites including amino acids, fatty acids, and nucleotides. We also identified dysregulated metabolic pathways that have been less frequently reported in MS, such as the “mTOR signaling pathway” and “central carbon metabolism in cancer.” Furthermore, by employing standardized protocols for sample matching and data analysis, this study enhances the comparability and biological interpretability of the findings, providing new regional evidence and potential biomarkers for the gut metabolic mechanisms underlying MS.

As the end product of intestinal flora metabolic activities, feces have the advantages of non-invasiveness, easy accessibility, and strong dynamics9, and can more sensitively capture early signals of “gut-brain axis” dysfunction. However, current fecal metabolomics studies on MS patients are still very limited, and most focus on microbial composition analysis, lacking systematic and untargeted metabolic profile characterization10. Therefore, based on ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-TOF MS)11, this study conducted the first untargeted fecal metabolomics analysis in the MS population in northern China, aiming to comprehensively characterize their metabolic profiles, identify potential biomarkers, and explore their association with immune-metabolic pathways, providing a new perspective for the mechanism analysis and non-invasive diagnosis of MS.

Research content

A total of 37 patients diagnosed with multiple sclerosis between November 2020 and May 2021 at the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University and Inner Mongolia International Mongolian Medical Hospital were enrolled as the case group (MS group), including 35 relapsing-remitting cases and 2 primary progressive cases, aged 21–62 years (13 males, 24 females). Additionally, 30 healthy individuals matched for age, gender, and dietary habits were included as the control group (CG group)12, aged 26–59 years (7 males, 23 females). Dietary habit matching was roughly stratified based on a simple standardized questionnaire (including 10 indicators such as staple food type, frequency of fruit and vegetable intake, consumption of red meat and dairy products) to ensure no significant differences in main dietary patterns between the two groups; however, 72-hour dietary recall or food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was not used for precise quantification13. This study was approved by the relevant institutional ethics committees( Medical Ethics Committee of Inner Mongolia Medical University ), and all participants provided informed consent and signed written consent forms.

Data analysis

Raw mass spectrometry data (.wiff format) were first converted to .mzXML format using ProteoWizard (v3.0), followed by peak detection, retention time correction, and peak alignment using XCMS software. Metabolite annotation was graded according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI): metabolites with commercial standards were confirmed by retention time and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) matching (Level 1); the remaining metabolites were annotated based on the exact mass (m/z error < 10 ppm) and fragment ion information from HMDB, METLIN, and KEGG databases (Level 2/3). Data preprocessing included removing variables missing in more than 80% of samples, imputing remaining missing values with half the minimum value, log₂ transformation to improve distribution normality, and Pareto scaling to balance the impact of high and low abundance metabolites on the model.

To evaluate data quality, all samples were subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) together with quality control (QC) samples. Tight clustering of QC samples indicated good system stability; total Ion Chromatogram (TIC) further verified the reproducibility of chromatographic-mass spectrometric signals. Univariate analysis used two-tailed Student’s t-test to compare intergroup differences, and Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to correct for multiple testing (false discovery rate FDR < 0.1). Multivariate analysis included unsupervised PCA and supervised PLS-DA/OPLS-DA models. The latter evaluated model robustness through cross-validation parameters (R²Y, Q²) and 200 permutation tests. Finally, differential metabolites were defined as those meeting the criteria of variable importance in projection (VIP > 1), raw P value < 0.05, and adjusted q value < 0.1.

Multidimensional statistical analysis

Metabolomics data differ from other data types due to their high-dimensional characteristics and complex inter-variable relationships. Univariate analysis cannot accurately uncover detailed information within the data. Therefore, our experiment employs multidimensional statistical methods such as PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA.

PCA

PCA can reduce the dimensionality of high-dimensional data, effectively reflecting the information of original variables, and is an unsupervised data analysis method. As shown in Fig. 4: In the results, the separation between the two sample groups under positive ion mode is not distinct, which may be related to the small sample size in our experiment. Under negative ion mode, the data within each group are relatively concentrated, while the data between groups show separation, allowing for clearer differentiation between the case group and the control group.

Correlation analysis

Positively correlated metabolites may originate from the same synthetic pathway, while negative correlations suggest they might be decomposed for the synthesis of other metabolites, indicating a conversion relationship. Correlation analysis of differential metabolites revealed that, in positive ion mode, the first five substances (from L-pyroglutamic acid to histidine-threonine) showed positive correlations among themselves but were largely negatively correlated with the latter 27 substances (from 2-azacarbazole to choline). The latter 27 substances were mostly positively correlated among themselves. In negative ion mode, the last three substances (from norethindrone acetate to 2-oxoadipic acid) exhibited positive correlations among themselves but were largely negatively correlated with the first 21 substances (from shikimate to oleandrin B). The first 21 substances were mostly positively correlated among themselves, as shown in Fig. 10.

Pathway analysis of differential metabolites

This study utilized the KEGG database to identify metabolic pathways associated with the differential metabolites. Among the 56 differential metabolites, 36 were encoded in KEGG. If a metabolic pathway is abnormal and related to the pathophysiological changes of multiple sclerosis, metabolites in this pathway may also exhibit abnormalities, and the differential metabolites we identified may be enriched in this pathway. Pathway enrichment results showed 20 significantly altered pathways, with seven being particularly notable: protein digestion and absorption, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, central carbon metabolism in cancer, ABC transporters, mTOR signaling pathway, amino acid biosynthesis, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis. The results are presented as a bubble plot, as shown in Fig. 11.

Discussion

Through untargeted fecal metabolomics analysis, this study systematically compared the metabolic profile differences between 37 MS patients and 30 healthy controls, and identified a total of 56 significantly differential metabolites, which were mainly enriched in amino acid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and related signaling pathways. These findings not only verify the existence of intestinal metabolic disorders in MS but also expand the understanding of the metabolic basis of “gut-brain axis” dysfunction.

Recent studies have suggested the key role of intestinal microbiota and their metabolites in MS immune regulation. Jangi et al.16 and Miyake et al.17 both reported that the levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as propionic acid and butyric acid in the feces of MS patients are reduced, and they believe that this may promote neuroinflammation by impairing the function of regulatory T cells (Tregs). The results of this study are highly consistent with this: we observed that SCFAs such as propionic acid and isobutyric acid were significantly downregulated in the MS group, further supporting that SCFA deficiency may be an important link in MS immune imbalance. However, this study has three important advancements on this basis: first, we systematically depicted the fecal metabolic panorama in the MS population in northern China for the first time, and found that in addition to SCFAs, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs: L-leucine, L-isoleucine), unsaturated fatty acids (oleic acid, α-linolenic acid), niacin, bile acid derivatives (lithocholic acid), and various nucleotides (adenosine, thymidine) were also significantly reduced; second, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that differential metabolites were significantly enriched in pathways such as “protein digestion and absorption”, “amino acid biosynthesis”, “aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis”, “niacin and nicotinamide metabolism”, “ABC transporters”, “mTOR signaling pathway”, and “central carbon metabolism in cancer”—the latter two pathways have rarely been reported in previous MS metabolomics studies, suggesting that MS may be accompanied by extensive reprogramming of host cell energy metabolism and translation regulation; third, this cohort was mainly composed of relapsing-remitting MS (35/37) with highly homogeneous clinical phenotypes, which reduced the dilution of metabolic signals by disease subtype confounding and improved the reliability of potential biomarkers18.

Consistent with the findings of Nohara S et al.8 the present study also observed a significant downregulation of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as propionic acid and isobutyric acid. However, our research further extends the coverage of metabolites, revealing significant alterations in a variety of other metabolites, including branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), unsaturated fatty acids, niacin, and bile acid derivatives. Moreover, KEGG pathway analysis identified dysregulation in the “mTOR signaling pathway” and “central carbon metabolism in cancer,” which have been less frequently reported in previous fecal metabolomic studies of multiple sclerosis (MS). This suggests that MS may be associated with a reprogramming of host cellular energy metabolism and translational regulation. Compared to the study by Chen et al.19 in a European cohort, the downregulation of α-linolenic acid was more pronounced in our cohort, which may reflect the influence of regional dietary differences on metabolic profiles.

From a biological mechanism perspective, these metabolic changes may be involved in MS pathogenesis through multiple pathways. The reduction of SCFAs and adenosine may impair Treg induction and anti-inflammatory functions; downregulation of BCAAs and arginine may affect energy supply and nitric oxide balance in neurons and immune cells20; as a nutrient sensing hub, the activity of the mTOR pathway is regulated by amino acid levels21, and its dysregulation may lead to Th17/Treg differentiation imbalance22. In addition, abnormal function of ABC transporters may impair blood-brain barrier integrity or intestinal toxin clearance23, while aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis disorders may interfere with protein translation efficiency and affect myelin repair24. Notably, the upregulation of oxidative stress-related metabolites such as L-pyroglutamic acid also suggests systemic oxidative damage in MS patients.

Although this study provides valuable metabolomics evidence, it still has several limitations. First, fecal metabolites are easily affected by environmental factors such as diet and drugs25; although we roughly matched 10 indicators such as staple food type, fruit and vegetable intake, and dairy product consumption through a simple questionnaire, we did not use a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) or 24-hour dietary recall for precise quantification. Second, the sample size is relatively small (n = 37 vs. 30). Although the detection power for medium effect size (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.8) is about 80%, weak effects may still be missed. Third, covariates such as body mass index (BMI)26, EDSS score, disease duration, and disease-modifying therapy (DMT) medication history were not collected, so multivariate correction could not be performed. More importantly, this study is a cross-sectional design, which cannot determine whether metabolic changes are the pathogenic driving factors of MS or the secondary results of disease progression27—for example, the reduction of SCFAs may be a consequence of gut microbiota dysbiosis or an initiating factor triggering autoimmunity. In the future, longitudinal cohorts, multi-omics integration (such as metagenomics + metabolomics + immune phenotype)28, and animal intervention experiments are needed to further verify the causal relationship.

Conclusion

Significant differences exist in the fecal metabolite profiles between multiple sclerosis patients and healthy controls, with amino acids and fatty acids being the most enriched differential metabolite categories, suggesting potential biomarker value. Metabolic pathways such as protein digestion and absorption, central carbon metabolism in cancer, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, ABC transporters, mTOR signaling pathway, amino acid biosynthesis, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis may be involved in disease development. This study provides metabolomic evidence for uncovering the pathophysiological mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets of multiple sclerosis.
