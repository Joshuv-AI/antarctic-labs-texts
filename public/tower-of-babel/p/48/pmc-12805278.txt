Introduction

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) are an effective therapy for primary and secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death (SCD) owing to ventricular arrhythmias.1,2 In athletes aged <35 years, inherited cardiomyopathies are the most common cause of SCD, whereas coronary artery disease is far more prevalent in older athletes.3 Accordingly, in active and younger patients, lifelong exposure to the risk of lead-related complications is undesirable. In addition, the sport-specific risk of collision and damage to a cardiac implantable electronic device component must be considered. Thus, subcutaneous ICDs (SICDs) are frequently considered for athletes who meet an ICD indication and do not have coincident bradyarrhythmia pacing needs. SICDs have been shown to have far fewer lead-related complications and noninferior inappropriate shock rates4,5 than transvenous ICDs while preserving vascular access. Recent nonrandomized studies suggest that modern noise filters for noncardiac signals and cardiac oversensing (eg, SMART Pass) have further reduced inappropriate shock rates.6

Exercise stress testing after SICD implantation to reduce inappropriate shocks can be useful but is not universally adopted in the prescreening process or during clinical follow-up. Here, we report a case of an athlete with an SICD who received maximum-effort stress testing after several inappropriate shocks for upper rate bundle behavior. Recognition of features of his exercise-induced bundle block allowed reprogramming of his SICD and eliminated his repeated inappropriate shocks, emphasizing the importance of stress testing in postimplant SICD programming optimization.

Case report

A 32-year-old male basketball player with chronic nonischemic and dilated cardiomyopathy experienced progressive left ventricular ejection fraction decline (nadir <20%) and severe mitral regurgitation despite guideline-directed medical therapy and mitral valve repair. He ultimately received a primary prevention SICD. At the time of implant, a posteroanterior and lateral chest radiograph showed the ICD lead overlying the midline of the chest wall and appropriate posterior positioning of the generator along the left midaxillary line (Figure 1). Postimplant impedance was 45 Ω, and his device was programmed to a conditional shock zone of 220 beats/min (bpm) and a ventricular fibrillation zone of 240 bpm; a secondary sensing vector was used. SMART Pass was turned on. He did not receive an exercise stress test after his SICD implant. He did well after his implant and continued to exercise regularly, including weightlifting, without significant pain or limitation from his device.Figure 1Post–subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implant posteroanterior and lateral chest radiograph.

Approximately 5 months later, while running, he experienced 3 consecutive shocks from his ICD and presented to the emergency department. He was evaluated by electrophysiology, and his device was interrogated (Figure 2).Figure 2Subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator interrogation after inappropriate shock.

No other episodes of oversensing or noise were recorded on his device before this episode. He was otherwise asymptomatic, denied any prodrome while running, and was discharged. He subsequently underwent a synchronous maximum-effort stress test and device interrogation (Figure 3). Electrocardiogram, heart rate (HR), oxygen saturation, and blood pressure were obtained at baseline and during exercise testing and recovery. Given his primary sport, treadmill testing was performed using a tailored graded ramp protocol consisting of a 5-minute warm-up stage at 1% incline and set speed, followed by an incline increase of 0.5% every 15 seconds until subjective peak effort and a 2-minute recovery stage at walking pace on 0% incline.Figure 3Stress testing and device interrogation at baseline and peak effort.

During sinus rhythm at rest, sensing was 1:1 and appropriate in the secondary vector. At an HR of approximately 180 bpm, a change in the QRS morphology consistent with a rate-dependent right bundle branch block was observed. The patient remained asymptomatic throughout the test. Alternative sensing vectors were tested and passed using the automatic setup optimization feature, but were not found to be superior to the previously programmed secondary vector. Oversensing seen in the inappropriate therapy episode owing to the bundle block morphology at the upper rate behavior was reproduced. A reference standard electrocardiogram (S-ECG) was captured at this rate with the reproducible bundle block morphology and stored as a sinus template, which the device would then use as part of its conditional zone discriminators. His device was reprogrammed to increase his detection zones from 220–240 bpm to 230–250 bpm, and his SmartCharge was increased from 0 to 2.5 seconds. He returned to running and follow-up device interrogations over the next 2 years recorded no further episodes.

Discussion

Specific cohorts have been shown to benefit from the practice of exercise testing post-ICD implant, which is not routine.7,8 In data predating the use of modern filters, inappropriate shocks owing to T-wave oversensing occurred more often in younger patients and patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or congenital heart disease.9 Here, this case suggests that athletes may also represent such a cohort. A stress test at 6 weeks for postimplant healing and specifically to maximum effort (ideally replicating the athlete’s sport at a similar intensity) to allow observation of device behavior at peak HR and generate a storable reference S-ECG may reduce the risk of inappropriate shocks and T-wave oversensing, especially if rate-related bundle behavior is unveiled during that process. In addition, maximum-effort exercise testing allows individualized zone programming based on an athlete’s HR kinetics. This approach aligns with recommendations from the 2024 Heart Rhythm Society Expert Consensus Statement on Arrhythmias in the Athlete.10

Existing evidence from transvenous devices supports the use of ICDs in athletes; however, data from athletes with SICDs are limited. The Multinational ICD Sports Safety Registry showed no events of death or shock-related physical injury among 440 athletes with a transvenous ICD over a follow-up of 31 months.11 Transvenous lead fracture has not been linked with sports activity (except for intense weight lifting).12 Inappropriate sensing remains a significant concern given the many potential confounders in athletes. Especially during prolonged endurance activity, electrolyte shifts, dehydration (affecting thoracic conductivity), hyperventilation- or posture-related electrode movement, catecholaminergic surges, and core temperature changes (eg, hyperthermia) may alter R/T ratios, signal amplitude, and sensing vectors with unpredictable responses from the SICD and filtering algorithms. Higher rate cutoff and long detection duration have been shown to reduce inappropriate shocks in athletes with dual- and single-chamber transvenous devices.13 An analysis of a subset of 152 athletes with an SICD extracted from registry data showed no difference in device-related complications or inappropriate shocks compared with nonathletes, although myopotential oversensing and lead infections were more common, perhaps partly owing to differences in body composition.14

In our case, the storage of an S-ECG template close to peak HR highlights a useful and unique feature of SICDs. SICD sensing algorithms are designed to be a rhythm discriminator, which analyzes a 4-beat average while computing 41 data points per QRS complex, compared with the beat-to-beat detection of transvenous intracardiac electrograms. Thus, to maximize accurate recognition of true ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation while avoiding inappropriate therapy, a dynamic sensing threshold is used. As the detected rate increases but importantly remains below the conditional zone (at nominal settings, this occurs at a rate of 168 bpm), a “mid-rate 1” detection profile is used and the sensing threshold is set by the similarity of the QRS morphology to the stored S-ECG template. With high similarity, the threshold is set low, whereas, with low similarity, it is set high before decay. If the HR continues to rise into the conditional zone, a “mid-rate 2” detection profile is used, the dynamic floor is no longer used, and the sensing threshold decays rapidly to nominal settings. Thus, a stored S-ECG template and changes to the conditional and shock zones alter the threshold at which mid-rate 1 and mid-rate 2 are applied and correspondingly alter the sensitivity of the SICD for detecting small, fast signals. This means that the faster the detected HR, the more aggressive ventricular tachycardia detection becomes. In an athlete routinely operating at or near peak HR, a stored S-ECG obtained during a maximum-effort stress test has the potential to optimize sensing threshold even before an inappropriate shock. Individualized zone programming, stored S-ECG templates, and sensing vector optimization, guided by a maximum-effort exercise stress test, can be powerful tools in troubleshooting SICD inappropriate sensing, especially for reproducible upper rate morphology changes.

Conclusion

For clinicians caring for an athlete, SICD programming can benefit from direct observation of device detection behavior at upper HRs during a maximum-effort stress test, specifically given that athletes can be anticipated to achieve HR rate zones frequently.
