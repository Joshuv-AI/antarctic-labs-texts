A global analysis of quarantine and isolation policies governing outbreak responses
Authors: Amanda M Rosner, Ciara M Weets, Rory Wilson, Rebecca Katz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCountries across the world implemented diverse quarantine and isolation policies throughout the COVID-19 pandemic with varying levels of efficacy. Their widespread use invites new considerations regarding the effectiveness of domestic quarantine and isolation policies, the ways they are enforced, and the jurisdictions responsible for ordering these measures.MethodsWe systematically analysed legally enforceable policies in current standing in each United Nations (UN) Member State, assessing the authorities to quarantine and isolate individuals within national borders. We captured the text of each policy and categorised the responsible jurisdictional authority and enforcement mechanisms.ResultsOf UN Member States, 91.67% (176/192) had legally enforceable policies that addressed both quarantine and isolation. Two countries only had quarantine policies, seven only had isolation policies and seven countries had neither. Jurisdictional quarantine authority was primarily vested in the national level (74.16%; 132/178), with the remainder mixed (22.47%; 40/178) and subnational only (3.37%; 6/178). Isolation authority was also primarily at the national level (69.40%; 127/183) but with a greater proportion mixed (27.87%; 51/183) and subnational only (2.73%; 5/183).Quarantine enforcement mechanisms were codified in a majority of countries (80.91%; 144/178) with nearly all (94.44%; 136/144) enforcing quarantine through penalties for non-compliant individuals. Isolation enforcement mechanisms were codified in an even greater number of countries (86.89%; 159/183), with 95.51% (149/156) having penalties for non-compliant individuals.ConclusionsWe created a novel repository for quarantine and isolation policies to assist in future outbreak responses. We identify specific country-level policy gaps, which can be addressed through epidemic and pandemic preparedness efforts. Finally, the repository provides the necessary evidence base for future research analysing the impact of quarantine and isolation policies on disease outbreak response outcomes.

Introduction

Quarantine and isolation policies are critically important public health measures to control infectious disease outbreaks. Quarantine–the separation of persons exposed to an infectious disease–and isolation–the separation of individuals who are symptomatic or are confirmed to have an infectious disease–have been used throughout history to manage outbreaks, with passages from the Old Testament suggesting that isolation of ‘unclean’ individuals was commonplace.1 The use of both quarantine and isolation and the codification of associated policies have been well documented over the millennia, from the Plague of Justinian to the Venetian practice of holding ships at anchor in the fourteenth century.2 3

In the modern context, policies typically make national governments responsible for quarantine and isolation at their own international borders.4 Countries typically have additional wording or separate policies granting quarantine and isolation authority within their national borders to applicable levels of government. Quarantine and isolation policies were widely invoked around the world during the COVID-19 pandemic.5 However, the implementation of these policies by different countries during the pandemic was diverse and resulted in varying levels of effectiveness.6 This widespread use of quarantine and isolation invites new considerations regarding the effectiveness of policies, the ways they are enforced, and the jurisdictions responsible for ordering these measures.

In domestic outbreak responses, having a clear understanding of who has the authority to quarantine or isolate in different situations and how they can enforce these measures is critical for timely action. On an international level, understanding which policies provide quarantine and isolation authority can help coordinate global responses to outbreaks and help identify country-level policy gaps to assist epidemic and pandemic preparedness efforts.

We aimed to identify the domestic quarantine and isolation policies in each United Nations (UN) Member State, the level of government authorised to carry out these measures, and what enforcement mechanisms are available. These efforts build the evidence base necessary to enable research into how quarantine and isolation policies impact outbreak response outcomes. Additionally, this research will provide a repository for quarantine and isolation policies which can be used by national or international actors to assist future outbreak responses.

Methods

This research was conducted as part of the Analysis and Mapping of Policies for Emerging Infectious Diseases project, for which subject matter experts systematically surface, collect and assess legally enforceable health-related policies from each UN Member State.7 We included all UN Member States in this study, with the exception of the Democratic People’s Republic of North Korea; this country was omitted due to the research team’s inability to access and verify policy documents.8

We followed a standardised operating procedure for data collection, beginning with a literature review, where the research team identified applicable search terms to use when surfacing policies for each country. Findings from a landscape review informed the development of research questions and the data taxonomy (online supplemental materials, figure 1). The research team then conducted a sample study on 10 countries, selected to represent diverse governmental systems, economic statuses and geographical locations. After the completion of this sample study, the team resolved any gaps in the data collection protocol, data taxonomy and coding methodology.

Definitions

Throughout our research, we only looked at the authority to quarantine and isolate humans within national borders. We excluded quarantine and isolation provisions about animals, cargo, international borders, international travellers and ships. For our research, patient refers to the sick person in instances of isolation, and contact refers to the person exposed to a disease for instances of quarantine. Our standardised definitions are available in online supplemental table 1.

Policy identification

We identified policies through a protocol that encompassed reviewing existing document repositories, standardised searches of peer-reviewed publication platforms, search engines and digitised national legal databases.

We first inspected known repositories for health policies, including FAOLEX and geographic-specific policy repositories such as Droit Afrique and PACLII.9 10 A standardised search using Google Scholar and the broader Google search engine was then run using the series of queries developed during the literature review and tested by the sample study in countries where the government operates and publishes laws in a language other than English; machine translation was used to translate the search terms into the official language(s) of the country. For non-English-speaking nations, all searches were first run in English, and then all translated search terms were run, altering the word order within each term or substituting a synonym as needed, to make the search structure appropriate for the language into which it was translated. When search engines failed to directly surface a policy, yet peer-reviewed sources yielded evidence of an applicable document, the identified document of interest was individually searched for either by title or through other search terms relevant only to that policy (figure 1).

Policy identification protocol and screening procedure. GAI, generative artificial intelligence; PI, Principal Investigator.

After reviewing available peer-reviewed and grey literature, in addition to documents included in previously compiled policy databases, the research team searched national governmental websites where policies might be located. The standardised search terms were employed using search functions on the websites (Supplementary Materials, Search Terms). In the case that there was no available search capacity on the website, the research team manually reviewed the repository for keywords related to the topic. If no potentially relevant policies were discovered through these processes, the researchers consulted constitutional case law to see if authority was vested through vague language in the constitution but had been used to allow quarantine and/or isolation in practice. On several occasions, governments were contacted directly to inquire about the presence or the content of a policy.

Screening and creation of the quarantine and isolation database

All policies were reviewed against inclusion criteria to ensure they had current standing and were legally enforceable (online supplemental figures 2-4 and table 1). Plans, strategies or guidance documents were excluded. Likewise, laws and other policies created and implemented only for the COVID-19 pandemic, but that were no longer enforceable, were excluded from this dataset when there were other standing, legally enforceable policies in place. In the cases where there were no identifiable standing policies for quarantine and/or isolation in a country, we included laws developed and enforced during the COVID-19 pandemic, as a demonstration of the legal course of action a country has previously taken during an infectious disease outbreak to implement either or both of these interventions.

Included policies were downloaded as PDFs and captured in Airtable, a cloud-based relational database platform.11 For each country, policies were qualitatively coded for each subtopic. All policies were collected between November 2023 and September 2024. Documents written in languages other than those spoken by the research team were translated using machine translation from Google Translate. When possible, fluent speakers and subject matter experts were contacted to verify machine translations and interpretations of policies written in languages not spoken by the research team.

Data validation

For quality assurance and control, the research team had a secondary reviewer use Perplexity AI, an artificial intelligence search engine, to assess the concordance of the results. Use of this technology increased the efficiency of the secondary review process and allowed a member of the research team with less subject-matter expertise to independently complete the review. The specific generative artificial intelligence (GAI) tool was selected as, at the time of the research project, it was the only GAI platform to our knowledge that included direct citations for each generated response without prompting. This allowed the research team to immediately assess the source of the information.12 Any results that were non-concordant between the GAI tool and primary researcher were reviewed by the research team and reconciled based on the available information and the inclusion criteria.

Results

Across 192 UN member states, we found that over 90% (91.67%; 176/192) had policies that addressed both quarantine and isolation. Two countries had policies for quarantine but not for isolation. Seven countries had policies for isolation but not quarantine. We found no legally enforceable policies for either quarantine or isolation in seven countries, namely: Central African Republic, Eritrea, Mauritania, Republic of Congo, Rwanda, Somalia and South Sudan. The seven countries which lacked both quarantine and isolation policies were all in Africa. Three of these countries shared borders: the Republic of Congo, Central African Republic and South Sudan. For 13 of the 192 countries (6.77%), we captured policies developed in response to the COVID-19 pandemic as a proxy for standing legislation or if they replaced antiquated laws and/or supplemented existing health policies.

Jurisdictional authority for quarantine and isolation

Since different countries assign the authority to quarantine or isolate to different levels of government, we created three categories of governments that could have quarantine or isolation authority: national, subnational and mixed (online supplemental table 1). Authority to quarantine was identified in 92.71% countries (178/192). The vast majority of countries with quarantine authority (74.16%; 132/178) vested quarantine authority at the national level. A further 22.47% (40/178) of countries had mixed authority, maintaining some aspect of national quarantine and devolving some quarantine authority to subnational levels, like in the USA, where Article X of the Constitution grants quarantine and isolation authority to the states and Title 42 of the US Code bestows authority to the federal government for specific diseases in scenarios that fall under federal jurisdiction.13 14 The complete delegation of domestic quarantine authority to subnational jurisdictions was uncommon, occurring in only 3.37% (6/178) of countries. Within these six countries, there was notable variation in where the subnational power to quarantine rests: exclusively intermediate-level governments for four countries; solely local governments for only one country; and a combination of intermediate and local governments for only one country.

While the majority of countries had policies allowing for both government-mandated quarantine and isolation, there was variation in the target of these interventions. Quarantine can be imposed on individuals or it can take place at a population level through cordon sanitaire, which is a series of measures that can be implemented to prevent groups of people from exiting a geographic area. We found that 21 of the countries with quarantine authority (11.8%; 21/178) allowed for both individual and population-level quarantine, allowing the government to enforce quarantine orders on certain persons and/or geographically distributed sections of the population. In three countries, the only codified method of quarantine was at the population level.

Isolation authority was found in 183 countries (95.31%; 183/192). In the majority of countries, isolation authority was found to be vested in only the national level government. There were 127 countries (69.40%; 127/183) with solely national-level authority for isolation; 51 countries (27.87%; 51/183) had isolation authority shared between national and subnational governments, depending on specific scenarios that were articulated in the respective policies. The remaining five countries, all of which also vested quarantine powers within subnational governing bodies, vested the power to isolate in subnational jurisdictions. Only 13 countries had different levels of government in charge of quarantine than they did for isolation (online supplemental table 2) (figure 2).

Enforcement mechanisms for quarantine and isolation

Various mechanisms were found to enforce quarantine and isolation around the world, including penalties for the violation of quarantine or isolation orders, the use of force, arrest and forced confinement. Enforcement mechanisms were codified in 80.91% (144/178) of countries with quarantine authority. Nearly all of these countries (94.44%; 136/144) enforced quarantine orders by imposing penalties for non-compliance on the quarantined individuals. A further 2.78% (4/144) of countries devolved the authority for enforcing quarantine to subnational governments, creating heterogeneity in enforcement mechanisms across subnational jurisdictions. The remaining countries included provisions for penalising overseeing physicians when they failed to quarantine contacts of infectious diseases. In three countries, penalties for non-compliance were imposed on both the contact and the physician. In one country, Czechia, the law penalised physicians who failed to order quarantine but did not penalise contacts who violated quarantine orders. Notably, 34 countries with quarantine authority did not have quarantine enforcement mechanisms (online supplemental table 3).

The majority of countries with quarantine authorities (70%; 124/178) used a monetary fine and/or imprisonment to enforce quarantine. 17 of these countries state that one of these penalties would be enforced for non-compliance, but they did not define the value of the penalty in existing policy. A further six countries used monetary penalties specific to an individual’s daily wage or base salary. Such is the case in Costa Rica, which penalises individuals for disregarding quarantine orders through a fine of three base salaries.15 Thus, only 118 countries have a universally applied monetary penalty that can be directly compared across contexts. Similarly, six countries dictate a number of penalty units that may be assigned to individuals who break quarantine orders. Ghanaian policy, for instance, orders that individuals who engage in such indiscretions be assigned a fine of up to 50 penalty units.16 A total of six countries listed their monetary penalties in a currency that is no longer used in the country. For example, the financial penalty for breaking quarantine or isolation orders in Italy is listed in Lira, a unit of currency that has not exclusively been used in the country since 1999.17

For countries that specify a monetary fine or incarceration period for violating quarantine orders, we found substantial variation in the penalties across different nations. South Africa and Namibia allow individuals to be sentenced to up to 10 years in jail for breaking quarantine, while Australia and the Netherlands can impose fines greater than US$90 000.00. These penalties are outliers, as the median value for a fine was US$200.14. In 25 countries, the maximum penalty was less than US$100.00, while 11 of these nations imposed a maximum fine of less than US$5.00. The maximum fine values ranged from less than US$0.01 to nearly US$100 000.00. Likewise, the median jail sentence was less than 1 month. Maximum values for length of imprisonment ranged from 15 days to 10 years (figure 3). Additionally, some countries have enforcement mechanisms besides monetary fines and/or imprisonment, either in addition to fine/imprisonment or in place thereof. Examples of these other enforcement mechanisms included police involvement, arrest and forcibly quarantining the contact.

National-level quarantine enforcement mechanisms: fines and incarceration.

Enforcement mechanisms for isolation were present in a higher proportion of countries than quarantine enforcement mechanisms. Of the 183 countries with legally enforceable isolation policies, 159 (86.89%) had a codified enforcement mechanism, while the remaining 24 did not include such mechanisms in policy. Three countries devolved isolation enforcement powers to subnational jurisdictions, for which each jurisdiction’s enforcement mechanisms can differ. Within the remaining 156 nations that enforced isolation in national level policy, the vast majority (95.51%; 149/156) imposed penalties solely on non-compliant individuals placed under isolation. Broader isolation enforcement mechanisms for both patients and physicians were in place in six countries, all of which are in Europe and half of which also have quarantine enforcement mechanisms for both patients and physicians.

We found that 131 countries penalise violations of isolation orders through monetary fine and/or incarceration (online supplemental table 4). There were 20 countries with a different penalty for breaking isolation than for breaking quarantine requirements. The median value of the fine for breaking isolation was US$124.10, with penalties ranging from less than one tenth of a US cent to US$99 000.00. 35 countries had a fine less than US$100.00, with 20 of these countries including a fine in current policy that was valued at less than US$12.00. There was an average of 9 months of incarceration permitted in legislation, with maximum sentences ranging from 15 days to 10 years (figure 4).

Discussion

In this policy mapping and analysis work, we collated a dataset of quarantine and isolation policies from all UN Member States, identified notable gaps in codified authorities and analysed mechanisms used to enforce these non-pharmaceutical interventions. Notably, we found that 16 countries did not have quarantine and/or isolation authorities. We note that many of these countries without legally enforceable authorities for these interventions have had notable outbreaks of infectious disease and significant periods of political instability in the recent past.1820 The spread of these outbreaks may have been exacerbated by the lack of universally recognised political authority to implement and enforce quarantine and isolation policy, and highlight the domestic and international health implications of such policy gaps.21 The connection between political instability, gaps in quarantine and isolation policy, and disease outbreaks remains an understudied but critical area of global health governance; this novel dataset enables new opportunities to systematically explore these relationships.22

Among countries that do have codified quarantine and isolation policies, we observed substantial heterogeneity in the allocation of legal authority and the enforcement mechanisms used. Many empower actors across multiple strata of government to implement and enforce these measures, while others centralise authority at the national level. Enforcement strategies also vary, ranging from voluntary compliance to physical enforcement, with differing implications for community trust and public health outcomes.

Jurisdictional authority

Against a backdrop of growing calls for localisation of public health emergency response, the COVID-19 pandemic provided an opportunity to examine the efficacy of quarantine and isolation policies invoked and enforced by local and national authorities.2325 Our findings suggest that these calls for localisation have not been broadly realised in domestic quarantine and isolation policies. At the time of this study, only 26% of countries with quarantine authorities devolved this power to subnational governments, while just 31% of nations with isolation policies empowered subnational authorities to isolate patients. Disease surveillance programmes typically operate at the local level, while the authority to invoke non-pharmaceutical interventions often resides at higher levels of government, creating a disconnect that may delay response efforts.26 Additionally, localisation advocates often suggest that empowering local leaders to make health decisions about their communities improves outcomes across socioeconomic levels.24 Our novel policy repository will be foundational to retrospective analyses that will assess the impact of jurisdictional authority in quarantine and isolation, particularly as it impacted outcomes in COVID. It will also facilitate exploration of the relationship between the outcomes of vulnerable populations and the level of government responsible for invoking quarantine and isolation policy. Such findings could provide critical insights to inform improved policymaking for future public health emergencies.

Enforcement mechanisms

Policy enforcement mechanisms, particularly for public health interventions during an emergency, must strike a balance between disincentivising non-compliance and upholding public trust.27 28 While the goal of public health risk communications and community engagement efforts is to drive voluntary compliance with health interventions, enforcement mechanisms remain an important safeguard and policy lever for protecting population health.29 However, as demonstrated during the outbreak of Ebola virus disease in West Africa30 and the COVID-19 pandemic,31 32 enforcement mechanisms for quarantine and isolation both reflect and drive political and cultural interpretations of disease and government power, and may be implemented to varying degrees of success.33 Thus, the heterogeneity across countries of enforcement measures used was unsurprising, yet variation in the target and scope of these enforcement measures was notable.

Criminalisation, including financial penalties and/or incarceration of non-compliant individuals, was the most commonly used enforcement mechanisms in identified policies. The majority of countries that had a financial penalty for non-compliance used a static monetary value for breaches of quarantine or isolation policy. Doing so may reinforce socioeconomic inequalities that exist in a country, as well as diminish in effectiveness over time due to currency inflation.34 To promote equitable penalisation across socioeconomic levels and account for inflation, some countries tie penalties to a portion of an individual’s daily wage over a set number of days or use penalty units whose value adjusts over time. These approaches aim to ensure that penalties remain both fair and effective, regardless of individuals’ income levels or broader economic fluctuations.

Select countries have employed physical enforcement mechanisms in conjunction with other penalties, or as standalone measures. These include forcible confinement and the use of force against individuals attempting to leave quarantine or isolation. We suspect that such aggressive measures, even in extreme situations, may erode community trust. They risk framing quarantined or isolated individuals not as contributors to community health, but as criminals. When enforcement includes physically restricting individuals, even without noncompliance, it transforms the narrative from public good to criminalisation.35

Layered enforcement mechanisms provide greater opportunities for quarantine or isolation policies to be effectively enforced. For example, some countries impose monetary penalties on individuals who violate quarantine or isolation orders, as well as on their physicians. This approach creates both more opportunities for enforcement to be carried out and more significant social pressure to comply with orders. However, effective implementation requires adequate governmental resources to be devoted to enforcing the interventions.

Findings from this work that some countries either lack clear enforcement mechanisms in current policy, or have listed monetary penalties in antiquated currencies suggest that some countries have not updated their quarantine and isolation policies, and are thus less likely to be devoting significant resources to preparedness efforts for their invocation. In both cases, the absence of clear, current enforcement tools likely delays implementation and weakens the overall impact of quarantine and isolation policies. Together, these findings highlight the importance of not only designing robust enforcement strategies, but also maintaining them through regular policy updates and sustained investment in public health infrastructure and community involvement.

Limitations

The methodology employed in this research has several limitations. We relied solely on online repositories of policy, maintained either by national governments or by third-party organisations. Thus, this biases our work towards countries with robust online footprints, and it is possible that we were unable to surface important quarantine and isolation policies that have not been digitised.

Given the nature of the global policy mapping exercise, we sometimes relied on translation software, which may be inaccurate. Furthermore, while we verified translations with subject matter experts of the language when translations were ambiguous, cultural interpretations of some policies may differ from literal readings. This is best illustrated by the frequent conflation of the terms quarantine and isolation. A critical aspect of our work was the definition of both of these terms, yet we found that, particularly in the policies enacted during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, these terms were used interchangeably in some settings. As such, we were required to make assumptions as to the meaning of these terms based on the context of their use within the policy.3638

In the case of 13 countries, we relied on temporary policies invoked during the COVID-19 public health emergency as a proxy for standing quarantine and isolation policy. While it could be expected that similar policies would be invoked for future large-scale infectious disease outbreaks, the threshold for the invocation of such policies was ambiguous. As such, their use may not fully reflect a country’s baseline legal preparedness or its likely response under non-pandemic conditions. This reliance on temporary measures may limit the generalisability of our findings, particularly when comparing countries with longstanding legal frameworks to those where reactive policies were adopted during a crisis.

While we found that the vast majority of countries codify quarantine and isolation authority in legally enforceable policy, we were unable to surface an applicable policy in Rwanda. However, during this work, an outbreak of Marburg virus disease was reported in Rwanda. This outbreak was swiftly controlled through robust testing and a host of non-pharmaceutical interventions, including isolation and quarantine.39 This example suggests that authorities for isolation and quarantine may not be codified in policy, or may not be available in an online repository, and therefore, would not be captured by our methodology, yet may still facilitate an effective outbreak response.

We note that this work captures just one point in time, and that the policy environment around the world is dynamic. As such, future monitoring of this space is warranted.

Conclusions

Quarantine and isolation are foundational public health tools. When paired with robust risk communication and community engagement to promote voluntary compliance with these non-pharmaceutical interventions, enforceable quarantine and isolation policy is critical for limiting the transmission of infectious diseases. However, uncertainty surrounding the level of government that can most effectively implement this measure during a public health emergency, as well as which enforcement measures best balance community trust and compliance, persists. This work creates a novel policy dataset for quarantine and isolation, which will facilitate critical analyses on the relationship between jurisdictional authority, enforcement mechanisms and health outcomes during infectious disease outbreaks. By clarifying how legal authority and enforcement strategies vary across contexts, this dataset equips decision-makers with the evidence needed to design more effective and context-appropriate quarantine and isolation policies in future public health emergencies.
