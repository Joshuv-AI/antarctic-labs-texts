The role of natural and engineered exosomes in repairing damage to the nervous system
Authors: Tiangui Liu, Yang Fan, Qiong Zhang, Li Yang, Jing Jiang, Yuqing Zhang, Tao Xin, Lizeng Gao, Min Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing neurological injury, neuronal cell death and neuroinflammation mutually reinforce each other, further exacerbating neurological damage. Concurrently, the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB) impede drug penetration into the injured region, rendering neurological injury challenging to treat. Natural exosomes can penetrate biological barriers and deliver bioactive molecules to target sites, offering new possibilities for treatment. Engineered modifications further enhance the bioactivity and targeting capabilities of exosomes. Both natural and engineered exosomes, as a cell-free therapy, hold great promise for applications in the repair of neurological injuries. This review summarizes exosomes from different cellular sources and their engineering strategies, discusses the neuroinflammatory regulation and neuroprotective effects of natural and engineered exosomes, reviews the current status of exosome clinical research and key areas for clinical translation, with the aim of providing new insights for the clinical treatment and research of neural injury.Graphical abstract Keywords: Exosomes, Engineered exosomes, Nervous system, Damage repair, Clinical trial

Introduction

Neurological injuries are categorized into central nervous system (CNS) injuries -such as ischemic stroke (IS), traumatic brain injury (TBI), and spinal cord injury (SCI)-and peripheral nervous system (PNS) injuries. These conditions frequently result in severe functional impairment, significantly diminish quality of life, and shorten life expectancy, collectively constituting a major global disease burden [1–3]. However, due to the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB) intercepting the vast majority of drugs, it is difficult for medications to achieve effective therapeutic concentrations in the injured area. Additionally, cell therapies may pose safety concerns and face issues with low survival rates of transplanted cells, resulting in suboptimal outcomes for most treatment regimens [4, 5].

As a form of “cell-free therapy”, exosomes retain the core functions of cell therapy while circumventing the risks associated with stem cell transplantation [6]. Unlike ectosomes (50–1000 nm), exosomes are extracellular vesicles originating from the endosomal system, with diameters ranging from 30 to 150 nm (Fig. 1). First, the plasma membrane invaginates to form early-sorting endosomes (ESEs). Subsequently, ESEs undergo endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent or -independent pathways to form multivesicular bodies (MVBs) containing intraluminal vesicles (ILVs, future exosomes). Finally, MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane to release exosomes [7]. Characterized by tetraspanins (CD63/CD81/CD9), TSG101, and Alix, they carry nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, serving as intercellular communication mediators that play critical roles in both normal and pathological states. Under physiological conditions, exosomes primarily mediate intercellular communication. Research also suggests they may maintain cellular homeostasis by clearing excess or unnecessary intracellular components. In pathological states, early and accurate diagnosis is crucial for clinical treatment [8]. Therefore, monitoring cellular or tissue states via exosomes can be applied to disease diagnosis and prognosis assessment. Additionally, exosomes can deliver nucleic acids, proteins, and other molecules to recipient cells, thereby regulating recipient cell fate and disease progression [9, 10]. Further enhancement of exosome bioactivity can be achieved through engineered strategies that modulate specific biomolecular content, load exogenous molecules, or perform targeted modifications. Compared to traditional drug therapies, exosome-based treatments offer distinct advantages: (i) inherent ability to cross biological barriers and target specific sites; (ii) low immunogenicity, enabling allogeneic use; (iii) protection of native cargo from clearance; (iv) dual functionality as therapeutic agents and delivery vehicles. These characteristics address core limitations of conventional neurotherapies. The innate ability of exosomes to cross the BBB/BSCB, their low immunogenicity, circulatory stability, and cargo versatility establish a foundation for their therapeutic potential in nervous system repair [11].

Despite demonstrating significant potential in the field of nerve injury repair, the clinical application of exosomes still faces several critical scientific challenges: First, the molecular mechanisms underlying exosome-mediated nerve injury repair remain incompletely elucidated, and their role in synergistically regulating nerve regeneration and the inflammatory microenvironment requires further investigation. Second, the low efficiency of in vivo targeted delivery of exosomes limits therapeutic efficacy. Third, engineering modification techniques remain immature, with loading efficiency, stability, and metabolic pathways of exogenous molecules requiring optimization. Fourth, the absence of dynamic, precise delivery control systems hinders meeting complex neural repair demands. This review systematically summarizes research progress on natural and engineered exosomes in various types of neurological injuries, focusing on their mechanisms of action, delivery strategies, clinical studies, and optimization directions. It aims to provide guidance for the clinical translation of exosomes in the field of nerve injury repair.

Common cellular sources of exosomes and engineering strategies

Natural exosomes sources used for treatment are widely available, with common cells including mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), Neural stem cells (NSCs), macrophages, dendritic cells, etc [12]. Exosomes derived from different cell sources inherit certain innate properties of their parent cells, such as natural targeting capabilities, immune regulation, and neuroprotective effects (Fig. 2).

The microstructure of exosomes and common cellular sources. Exosomes can originate from various cell types, such as mesenchymal stem cells, macrophages, and neural stem cells. Different types of exosomes possess unique functional characteristics, enabling them to transport diverse substances including nucleic acids, proteins, drugs, and nanomaterials, and allowing for targeted modifications

MSCs are a type of pluripotent stem cell and an ideal source of exosomes. The exosomes secreted by MSCs possess the inherent ability to cross biological barriers while transferring some of their own immunomodulatory properties to the secreted exosomes (Fig. 3). They have found extensive application in the field of nerve injury repair. Research has shown that MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-Exo) can reprogram pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages into anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages through the miR-204 and miR-182 they contain, thereby inhibiting inflammation and reducing damage [13, 14]. Another study demonstrated that MSC-Exo can reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) production while enhancing cellular antioxidant capacity, utilizing the NRF2 defense system to treat oxidative stress-induced skin damage [15]. MSC-Exo exerts comprehensive therapeutic effects through multi-pathway synergistic regulation: On one hand, it activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, effectively inhibiting apoptosis while promoting angiogenesis, cell migration, and proliferation. On the other hand, it activates AMPK-the “metabolic master switch”-to maintain metabolic balance by enhancing cellular energy utilization, suppressing neuroinflammatory responses, and mediating neuroprotection. Furthermore, MSC-Exo suppresses pathological overactivation of the JAK2/STAT3/NF-κB signaling pathway, synergistically alleviating inflammatory responses and apoptosis, ultimately promoting tissue repair. These mechanisms collectively form the molecular basis for its anti-injury and pro-repair functions in regenerative medicine [16, 17].

NSCs possess the ability to differentiate into functional neurons and hold great potential for repairing neural damage. However, their slow proliferation and differentiation rates, coupled with low efficiency, limit their therapeutic efficacy [19]. On the other hand, due to the influence of the damaged microenvironment, the survival rate of transplanted NSCs is less than 10%. Currently, the reasons why NSCs can still promote neural injury repair under low survival rates have been revealed. Research has found that NSCs-derived exosomes (NSC-Exo) can independently promote axon regeneration and inhibit inflammation, significantly promoting injury repair [20]. After NSC-Exo is internalized by microglia, it effectively inhibits the activation of microglia, thereby reducing inflammatory responses, while also blocking the infiltration of peripheral immune cells. Therefore, it plays a crucial role in regulating inflammation in the nervous system. Additionally, an increasing number of studies have found that NSC-Exo also possesses neuroprotective functions, inhibiting oxidative stress by activating the ERK pathway, thereby suppressing cell apoptosis [21].

Macrophages, as immune cells, play a crucial role in tissue repair and can communicate with other cells via exosomes. In multiple sclerosis treatment research, macrophage-derived exosomes loaded with resveratrol can alleviate multiple sclerosis by targeting microglia [22]. Macrophage-derived exosomes also demonstrate excellent potential in treating bone degeneration. By constructing exosomes from bone marrow macrophages overexpressing miR-378a, the Pparα-Abca1 axis can be targeted to regulate the fate of bone stem/stem cell lineage, effectively reducing bone loss in obese mice, and may offer a new therapeutic strategy for bone degeneration in clinical obese and overweight patients [23]. Macrophage-secreted exosomes also holds great potential in neural injury repair. For example, CD206+ macrophage-derived exosomes can deliver DNase 1 to the injury site to treat IS [24]; CD163+ macrophage exosomes can promote vascular regeneration and stabilize the BSCB after SCI by delivering TGF-β, thereby facilitating neural injury repair and functional recovery, and may offer a potential therapeutic approach for improving SCI outcomes [25].

Dendritic cells are a special type of immune cell with strong antigen-presenting capabilities, capable of activating T cell immune responses [26]. Additionally, research has shown that dendritic cell exosomes possess excellent tissue repair capabilities. Dendritic cells can secrete exosomes containing miR-3064-5p, which mitigate BBB damage following subarachnoid hemorrhage by inhibiting the SIRT6/PCSK9 signaling pathway, offering a promising therapeutic approach for SAH patients [27]. Further studies have shown that dendritic cells can be divided into mature and immature dendritic cells. Immature dendritic cells can exert immune-suppressive and immune tolerance-maintaining effects, and the exosomes they secrete also inherits these immune-suppressive properties. They can significantly improve the inflammatory microenvironment and regulate the polarization of macrophages toward M2 macrophages, demonstrating great potential in improving tendon injury and post-myocardial infarction cardiac function [28, 29].

Natural exosomes have demonstrated significant potential in disease treatment (Table 1), and related research has to some extent elucidated their specific molecular mechanisms of action. In recent years, engineering modifications to exosomes have also garnered increasing attention. In terms of targeting, research on engineered exosomes has made significant progress. By modifying specific targeting ligands on the surface of exosomes, researchers can achieve precise localization to specific cells or tissues. For brain diseases, by using somatostatin as a ligand, engineered exosomes can be targeted to the brain, thereby achieving better therapeutic effects [30]. In the future, with further research into the targeted delivery mechanisms of exosomes, engineered exosomes are expected to play a greater role in personalized medicine and targeted therapy. Exosomes possess excellent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity. Its natural structure enables it to effectively encapsulate various bioactive molecules, including small-molecule drugs, proteins, and nucleic acids, thereby enhancing drug bioavailability and targeting. As an ideal delivery vehicle, engineering exosomes to construct a series of novel drug delivery systems could provide promising treatment options for various diseases [31]. Studies have shown that engineered exosomes exhibit good therapeutic effects in fields such as nerve damage repair and wound healing. For example, exosomes have garnered significant attention in the field of regenerative medicine. By constructing engineered exosomes loaded with Sephin1, cellular repair and regeneration can be promoted, demonstrating positive effects in wound healing and bone regeneration [32]. In summary, research on engineered exosomes as drug carriers not only expands the pathways for drug delivery but also provides new insights for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Exosomes in central nervous system injury and repair

In neurological injuries, immune-related cells such as microglia become activated and migrate to the injury site, constituting neuroinflammation. The body attempts to mitigate damage and promote repair through this inflammatory response. However, dysregulation of neuroimmunity and neuroinflammation can conversely exacerbate neural damage. Following CNS injury, damaged brain tissue can activate the aforementioned immune cells by producing a series of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). As a key carrier of DAMPs, exosomes play a crucial role in regulating inflammatory responses [33]. Therefore, inhibiting DAMPs-containing exosomes may alleviate inflammatory progression. Studies have also revealed that this pro-inflammatory signaling from exosomes may mask their “anti-inflammatory” effects. For instance, lncRNA 4933431K23Rik carried by exosomes released from astrocytes after TBI can upregulate SMAD7 expression in microglia, thereby suppressing their inflammatory phenotype and alleviating neuroinflammation [34]. Although this protective effect is often masked, it provides a therapeutic target for mitigating post-CNS injury neuroinflammation and promoting neural repair. Conversely, peripheral inflammation can also influence the CNS via exosomes. Inflammatory signals may indirectly alter secreted exosomes by affecting cellular gene expression and other changes, subsequently interacting with neurons to induce functional disorders in the brain [35]. Given the pivotal role of exosomes in brain communication and CNS injury mechanisms (Fig. 4), utilizing natural or engineered exosomes to modulate relevant molecular pathways or deliver bioactive substances like anti-inflammatory molecules may offer novel clinical therapeutic strategies for CNS injury (Table 2).

Application of exosomes in the treatment of ischemic stroke

The core pathological mechanisms of neurological damage caused by IS include excessive neuroinflammation and neuronal death. As key immune regulatory cells in the CNS, the activation status of microglia directly influences the progression of injury. Exosomes, leveraging their innate intercellular communication capabilities, offer a dual intervention strategy for regulating microglial fate and protecting neurons. Existing research indicates that naturally derived exosomes exert therapeutic effects through multiple pathways, while engineered modifications further enhance their targeting and functional specificity.

In the application of natural exosomes, their mechanisms of action can be summarized across two dimensions: immunomodulation and neuroprotection. At the immunoregulatory level, MSC-Exo can reprogram microglia through the HMGB1-TREM1-p38 MAPK signaling axis, promoting their transition to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. This effectively alleviates post-ischemic neuroinflammation and facilitates functional recovery [36]. A meta-analysis demonstrated that glial cell-derived exosomes significantly reduce levels of proinflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-6, thereby suppressing local neuroinflammation [37]. Regarding neuronal protection, adipose-derived MSC-Exo deliver miR-760-3p to target the neuronal CHAC1 gene, inhibiting the ferroptosis pathway and mitigating cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [38]. NSC-derived exosomes, meanwhile, activate PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitochondrial autophagy to improve mitochondrial dysfunction and alleviate oxidative stress-induced neuronal death [39].

To overcome the limitations of natural exosome targeting efficiency and drug-loading capacity, various engineering strategies have been extensively explored. Regarding surface modification, anchoring the recombinant fusion protein RGD-C1C2 to the exosome membrane surface enhances its homing ability to ischemic brain regions and leverages endogenous miRNAs to exert anti-inflammatory effects [40]. Plasmid-engineered M2pep-modified exosomes specifically target M2-type microglia, suppressing ferroptosis to regulate the inflammatory microenvironment and improve neurological function [41]. Regarding cargo engineering, loading exosomes with baicalin significantly enhances the compound’s solubility and bioavailability, mitigating brain injury by scavenging ROS [42]. A more promising engineered strategy involves synergistically integrating targeted modification with drug delivery. c(RGDyK)-modified exosomes precisely accumulate in ischemic brain tissue, delivering either pro-angiogenic miR-210 or anti-inflammatory curcumin to exert therapeutic effects through angiogenesis and inflammation suppression, respectively [43, 44]. While monoclonal antibody against GAP43 (mAb GAP43)-modified exosomes can specifically recognize damaged neurons, and the quercetin they carry can efficiently scavenge ROS, significantly alleviating reperfusion injury [45]. The above strategies reflect the evolution from “natural carriers” to “smart delivery systems,” providing important technical pathways for the precise treatment of IS.

Application of exosomes in traumatic brain injury treatment

The pathological process of TBI involves multiple injury pathways, including neuroinflammatory cascade activation, neuronal structural disruption, synaptic dysfunction, and oxidative stress. Exosomes, leveraging their inherent intercellular communication properties and low immunogenicity, can participate in TBI repair through multidimensional mechanisms such as regulating the immune microenvironment, promoting neural repair, and scavenging ROS. Existing research demonstrates significant therapeutic potential through both the functional exploration of natural exosomes and their engineered precision design.

In the application of natural exosomes, those derived from different cell sources exert specific effects on key pathological processes in TBI. MSC-Exo, rich in regulatory miRNAs, effectively suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation in microglia, reducing the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-18. This alleviates neuroinflammation and improves neurological dysfunction following TBI [46]. Exosomes secreted by microglia themselves activate the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway by downregulating PTEN expression. This dual mechanism promotes axonal regeneration and synaptic remodeling in damaged neurons while inhibiting endoplasmic reticulum stress-mediated apoptosis, synergistically facilitating neural functional recovery [47, 48]. Furthermore, glial cell line-derived precursor cell-derived exosomes accelerate post-TBI neural tissue repair by reducing the number of astrocytes that inhibit neural recovery through miRNA-mediated regulation [49].

To overcome the limitations of natural exosomes in targeting efficiency and functional expansion, a series of engineered strategies were developed. For targeted delivery, the neuron-specific targeting peptide RVG29 was conjugated to the exosome surface and loaded with the neuroprotective peptide NR2B9c. The resulting RVG-ExoNR2B9c system efficiently enriched in injured brain regions, significantly enhancing neuronal targeting and exerting antioxidant effects, thereby providing a novel precision intervention strategy for TBI [50]. For functional enhancement, fine-tuning the structure and function of nanomaterials enables ROS scavenging and other effects [51, 52]. For instance, integrating ultrafine selenium nanoparticles into NSC-Exo created an engineered carrier that not only effectively suppressed neuronal apoptosis but also synergistically modulated glia-neuron network interactions, demonstrating significant neuroprotection and functional recovery in TBI animal models [53]. These advances demonstrate that integrating the intrinsic biological properties of exosomes with engineered precision design offers an innovative technical pathway for multi-target, highly effective TBI treatment.

Application of exosomes in spinal cord injury treatment

The secondary pathological cascade in SCI involves key processes such as glial cell-mediated neuroinflammation, neuronal death, glial scar formation, and axonal regeneration barriers. Exosomes, with their cross-cell communication capabilities and excellent biocompatibility, demonstrate multi-level intervention potential in regulating the immune microenvironment, protecting neurons, and promoting structural repair. Existing research has established systematic therapeutic strategies ranging from endogenous regulation of natural exosomes to targeted delivery of engineered exosomes.

In the application of natural exosomes, exosomes derived from different cell sources exert synergistic repair effects through specific signaling pathways. MSC-Exo can regulate microglial polarization by inhibiting the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway, effectively curbing SCI inflammatory progression [54]. Furthermore, miR-216a-5p carried by exosomes from the same source synergistically suppresses neuronal damage and microglial inflammatory responses, enhancing neuroprotective effects [55]. Additionally, peripheral macrophage-derived exosomes enhance microglial autophagy activity and promote their transition to the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby supporting microenvironmental remodeling at the injury site [56].

Regarding engineering strategies, research focuses on enhancing targeting efficiency and functional specificity. Engineering MSC-Exo to carry PTEN siRNA and delivering them intranasally significantly promotes angiogenesis and axonal regeneration in injured areas while reducing the number of activated microglia and astrocytes, thereby improving neurological function [57]. Another strategy involved constructing MSC exosomes loaded with CTGF siRNA, which synergistically suppressed inflammatory responses, neuronal apoptosis, and glial scar formation, effectively promoting motor function recovery [58]. Regarding targeted delivery optimization, autologous plasma-derived exosomes modified with the neuron-targeting peptide RVG achieved precise recognition and delivery to neurons, significantly promoting axonal regeneration and neural circuit reconstruction [59]. These advances demonstrate that integrating the biological functions of natural exosomes with the precise regulatory capabilities of engineered design offers a promising innovative pathway for multi-target, highly efficient repair of SCI with translational potential.

Exosomes in peripheral nerve injury and repair

Following PNS injury, Schwann cells reprogram to a reparative phenotype to promote nerve repair. However, the regenerative process is extremely slow, and nerve repair capacity progressively diminishes [60]. As shown in Fig. 5, Schwann cells recruit macrophages by secreting key modulators such as IL-17B and sFRP1 [61]. Schwann cell-derived exosomes play a crucial role in both peripheral nerve injury and repair by regulating macrophage phenotypes. Exosomes derived from normal Schwann cells promote M2 polarization of macrophages, enhancing anti-inflammatory factor secretion and axonal outgrowth. Conversely, hypoxia-damaged Schwann cell-derived exosomes drive M1 polarization, increasing pro-inflammatory factor secretion and inhibiting axonal outgrowth, thereby limiting peripheral nerve axon regeneration and functional recovery [62]. CX3CR1-dependent inflammatory responses prompt recruited macrophages to release exosomes containing NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) complexes. These NOX2-containing exosomes are subsequently internalized by damaged axons and transported retrogradely to the cell body. There, NOX oxidizes PTEN to inhibit its activity, thereby activating the PI3K-p-Akt pathway to promote axonal regeneration [63].

Exosomes derived from multiple cell sources demonstrate neuroprotective effects

Schwann cells themselves have become a therapeutic target due to their crucial role in peripheral nerve regeneration. Exosomes derived from MSC-differentiated Schwann cells significantly promote axonal regeneration and angiogenesis while upregulating neurotrophic factors, thereby achieving the goal of treating peripheral nerve injury [64]. Research reveals Exo-mediated cell-cell interactions between Schwann cells and adipose-derived stem cells. Adipose-derived stem cell exosomes can inhibit Schwann cell apoptosis and promote proliferation through this interaction mechanism, effectively improving peripheral nerve injury [65]. Additionally, adipose-derived MSC-derived exosomes promote peripheral nerve regeneration by inhibiting the CSF-1/CSF-1R axis [66]. Exosomes derived from endothelial cells with high Netrin-1 expression can activate multiple signaling pathways, including axon guidance, to effectively promote the construction of a vascular niche, providing a microenvironment conducive to injury repair for peripheral nerves [67]. For exosomes derived from red blood cells, a study modified the exosomes surface with tetanus toxin-C fragments, enhancing its ability to target peripheral nerves, thereby providing an effective delivery vehicle for peripheral nerve disorders [68].

Exosomes delivery strategy

Natural and engineered exosomes have demonstrated significant potential in the treatment of neurological injuries. However, maintaining their effective concentration at the local injury site remains a critical factor limiting the therapeutic efficacy of exosome-based therapies. Traditional methods such as intravenous injection and repeated local injections at the lesion site suffer from drawbacks such as poor bioavailability and low retention rates [69]. To address these issues, researchers have begun exploring strategies involving the loading of exosomes onto biomaterials to achieve sustained-release effects. Related studies have found that hydrogels possess excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, making them ideal drug delivery carriers [70]. To date, various types of hydrogels have been developed, such as hyaluronan-based hydrogels [71], gelatin methacrylate [72], triglycerol monostearate [73], agarose-based hydrogels [74], and so on. An injectable thermosensitive hydrogel composed primarily of hydroxyethyl chitosan (HECTS) and β-glycerophosphate sodium (β-GP) can maintain an effective therapeutic concentration by sustained-release of adipose-derived MSCs exosomes (ADSC-Exo), significantly improving cavernous nerve injury [75]. Further exploration revealed that decellularized extracellular matrix hydrogels possess excellent tissue bridging and bioactive substance delivery properties, making them highly promising for application in neural tissue engineering. By loading them with ADSC-Exo, the release time of exosomes can be extended to over 21 days, effectively promoting functional recovery in sciatic nerve injury models and providing a theoretical basis for the repair of other peripheral nerve injuries [66]. Based on the design principle of slow-release exosomes using biomaterials, researchers have developed a hydrogel microneedle array patch loaded with exosomes (Fig. 6). This patch enables in situ sustained release of exosomes and has demonstrated promising efficacy in the treatment of SCI and IS [69, 76, 77].

Biological scaffolds represent another excellent exosome delivery vehicle. Personalized bionic scaffolds constructed using multimodal imaging and 3D printing technology can sustainably release engineered exosomes (Fig. 7). By delivering siRNA targeting PTEN to suppress its expression, these scaffolds activate the downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby promoting axonal growth. This approach holds significant promise for clinical translation in SCI treatment [78]. In TBI, utilizing 3D printing technology, umbilical cord-derived MSC-Exo stimulated with brain-derived neurotrophic factor were combined with collagen/chitosan composites to construct a bio-scaffold with excellent mechanical properties and biocompatibility. This scaffold effectively promotes neural network remodeling and significantly improves functional recovery following TBI [79]. Additionally, bioengineered scaffolds constructed through tissue engineering mimic biophysical and biochemical cues. The incorporation of graphene oxide enhances the conductivity of sericin hydrogels, while the addition of fibroblast exosomes endows them with cell-modulating capabilities. These synergistic effects collectively promote functional recovery following peripheral nerve injury [80].

Clinical application of exosomes

The use of exosomes themselves or as therapeutic carriers to deliver functional cargo to target cells has garnered extensive attention at both fundamental and applied levels. Exosome therapy circumvents the low survival rate issue associated with stem cell transplantation, leading to rapid advancement in clinical research. Based on preclinical findings, most clinical trials select MSCs as the exosome source. Given the low immunogenicity of MSC-Exo, most studies employ allogeneic exosomes for treating neurological disorders. A Phase 1 clinical trial (IRCT20200502047277N1) evaluating the safety and efficacy of intrathecal administration of allogeneic human umbilical cord MSC-Exo for SCI No early or late adverse events attributable to exosome therapy were observed during the 12-month follow-up, indicating this exosome therapy is safe and well-tolerated in patients with SCI. Compared to pre-treatment, significant improvements were demonstrated in ASIA Pin Prick and Light Touch scores, NBD scores, and SCIM III total scores, suggesting exosome therapy may be associated with functional improvement in patients [81]. Additionally, in the treatment of peripheral nerve injury using exosomes, a preliminary clinical study demonstrated that MSC-Exo facilitate rapid recovery in patients with radial nerve injury. Following autologous sural nerve transplantation, 1 mL of injection solution containing 5 billion exosomes was administered via subepineural injection to both the proximal and distal nerve stumps. Subsequent 180-day follow-up revealed significant improvements in motor function, sensory function, and electrophysiological parameters (Fig. 8). These findings hold significant implications for the clinical translation of exosome-based therapeutic strategies [82]. By September 2025, a number of registered clinicaltrials of exosomes in the treatment of nervous system injury have been retrieved at www.clinicaltrials.gov (Table 3). One of these clinical trials employs miR-124-engineered exosomes for the treatment of acute IS, utilizing a randomized, single-blind, placebo-controlled design. Although currently in Phase I and II clinical trials, it demonstrates the immense clinical translation potential of exosomes.

Limitations and future directions

Despite demonstrating transformative potential in repairing nerve damage, the clinical translation of both natural and engineered exosomes faces multiple challenges. On the technical front, existing isolation methods still fall short of clinical requirements. Ultracentrifugation is the gold standard for exosome isolation [83]. This well-established method is suitable for large-volume samples, but high centrifugal forces may cause structural damage to exosomes, compromising their integrity. Ultrafiltration methods suffer from membrane clogging and shear-induced exosome damage; precipitation methods yield exosome preparations with low purity; immunoaffinity separation is characterized by high costs and low yields [84]; microfluidic technologies lack large-scale, standardized clinical sample testing and are constrained by low sample throughput [85]. These techniques suffer from exosome damage, low yield, insufficient purity, and batch heterogeneity, significantly impairing functional reproducibility and standardization. At the biological level, natural exosomes exhibit limited BBB penetration efficiency. Moreover, key effector molecules within their complex contents (nucleic acids, proteins, lipids) remain unidentified, and mechanism elucidation still relies on simplified in vitro models that fail to reflect dynamic interactions within the injured microenvironment. Regarding clinical translation, key barriers to clinical application include: large-scale GMP production challenges, long-term safety concerns (e.g., immunogenicity, potential tumor-promoting risks), lack of unified quality control standards (morphology, particle size, biomarkers, functional activity), and insufficient high-quality randomized controlled clinical trial evidence.

To address these challenges, future research should focus on the following directions: (1) Develop high-throughput purification techniques and establish international quality control standards by strictly adhering to MISEV guidelines; (2) Advance engineering strategies to enhance BBB penetration, lesion targeting, and therapeutic efficacy through surface-modified targeting peptides or novel bio-nanomaterial-loaded formulations; (3) Integrating single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and AI-driven multi-omics analysis to precisely identify core effector molecules and signaling networks in neural repair; (4) Advancing the development of “exosome-biomaterials” smart delivery systems to prolong half-life and achieve spatiotemporal controlled release; (5) Optimize GMP-grade exosome production processes, establish rigorous quality control systems, design stringent clinical trials to clarify dose-response relationships, biodistribution, and long-term safety, and explore synergistic strategies with rehabilitation training and neuromodulation. Additionally, AI-assisted exosome design and optimization hold promise as an emerging research direction. In summary, through deep collaboration among materials science, neurobiology, clinical medicine, and regulatory science, exosomes can be transformed from a laboratory concept into safe and effective tools for neural repair, ultimately benefiting patients with neurological injuries worldwide.
