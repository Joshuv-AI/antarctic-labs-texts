High-temperature shear-type vibration sensor based on langasite piezoelectric crystal
Authors: Xiaorui Liang, Wenhua Cheng, Shuang Li, Dan Hu, Qiulin Tan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As aero-engines operate under extreme conditions such as high temperatures and speeds, the effectiveness of the engine tends to decrease. Therefore, real-time vibration monitoring of such structures is essential to ensure flight safety. However, the sensors used in the control system are prone to interference, thus leading to ineffective monitoring and eventually reducing the service life of the equipment. Therefore, in this study, a shear vibration sensor for real-time structural monitoring at 750 °C is designed and produced using a Langasite (LGS) crystal. Further, the high-temperature performance of the sensor is examined in the frequency, acceleration, and temperature ranges of 80–1000 Hz, 0.5–5g, and 30–750 °C, respectively. Its linearity is less than 1 %, which meets the application requirements of high-temperature piezoelectric vibration sensors. The average sensitivity of the sensor over the entire temperature range is 0.074 V/g. The sensor has good viability and can be used for testing in high-temperature environment applications as the sensitivity fluctuates by less than 2 % at different temperature points when applied to the same frequency. Furthermore, a temperature–vibration cross-decoupling approach is proposed to determine the acceleration value following temperature demodulation. The highest inaccuracy with respect to the reference acceleration is 4.54 %. Sensors also have a small size, are easy to install, and can maintain high reliability in harsh environments, playing an essential role in health assessment, fault prediction, and diagnosis of engines and other components. Therefore, the developed LGS acceleration sensor offers a promising alternative for real-time monitoring of structures in high-temperature environments, including aero-engines.

Introduction

Aero-engines frequently operate under challenging conditions, such as high temperatures, high speeds, and rapid rotations. The turbine blades are damaged or even broken because of the high-speed frictional aerodynamic thermal effect, which increases the engine's surface vibration and hinders the general operation of the equipment. Extreme increase in temperature decreases the working effectiveness and measurement accuracy of the engine, and the sensors of the control system are prone to interference, eventually reducing the service life of the equipment [[1], [2], [3]]. Providing timely feedback on the operational status of the engine is essential to ensure flight safety. Therefore, real-time vibration monitoring is extremely important in environments with high temperatures, rapid speeds, and other demanding conditions [4]. High-temperature piezoelectric vibration sensors have received considerable attention owing to their comparatively straightforward structure, quick reaction time, and extended service life. High-temperature piezoelectric vibration sensors are made of various high-temperature piezoelectric materials, such as quartz [5], LiNbO3 [6,7], lead zirconium titanate (PZT) [8], YCa4O(BO3)3 (YCOB) [9], barium strontium titanate (BST) [10], and langasite (LGS) [11]. Quartz's application at high temperatures is restricted owing to its dual characteristics and phase transition at 573 °C. The resistivity of LiNbO3 decreases with temperature owing to oxygen loss, and its sensing range is restricted to temperatures below 600 °C [12]. Although PZT has a high piezoelectric coefficient, its operating temperature (300 °C) is constrained by its Curie temperature [10]. YCOB is a promising high-temperature piezoelectric material that does not undergo phase transition until its melting point at 1500 °C. The high-temperature piezoelectric vibration sensor based on YCOB crystal can operate steadily under a high-temperature environment of 1000 °C, according to the analysis reported by Xiaoning Jiang et al. Although the YCOB crystal is a monocline crystal structure with strong temperature stability and piezoelectric capabilities [13], the crosstalk between various vibration modes may impair the effectiveness of the sensor. At 160 °C, BST undergoes a phase change. By swapping out the Ba site atoms for Sr, Jiang Chao et al. boosted its piezoelectric activity and increased the phase transition temperature, but the temperature was still constrained at 600 °C [14]. LGS reaches its melting point of 1470 °C before it transitions into another phase. Ferroelectric and Curie-free LGS crystals display little temperature dependency [15] and no pyroelectric characteristics [16]. Therefore, LGS is an attractive piezoelectric material for creating vibration sensors [17]. Due to the excellent performance of LGS, LGS piezoelectric materials can be applied to manufacture various types of sensors, such as temperature sensors, pressure sensors, strain sensors, etc. Pressure sensors based on piezoelectric materials can achieve high-temperature pressure testing at 1000 °C [18], while strain sensors can achieve high-temperature strain measurement at 800 °C and 1800 με [19]. In addition, piezoelectric materials can also be applied to vibration sensors. The research on piezoelectric pressure sensors and piezoelectric strain sensors in the early stage laid the research foundation for the study of vibration sensors. Currently, cantilever beam structures produced by micro-structure machining technology comprise the majority of vibration sensors based on LGS [20]. A strong anti-bias load and high accuracy are the advantages of cantilever beam vibration sensors; however, their low response frequency restricts their widespread use in high-response situations. Owing to its straightforward structure, quick reaction, simple integration, and efficient isolation of the lateral motion of sensors, the shear-type sensor has received considerable attention. In this study, a shear vibration sensor based on an LGS crystal is designed and fabricated, and its high-temperature properties are examined. The vibration sensor can operate stably in the frequency range of 80-1000Hz at 750 °C, with an average sensitivity of 0.208V/g from room temperature to 750 °C. At the same frequency, the sensitivity change at different temperature points is less than 2 %. To prevent the temperature from having a cross effect on the sensor, the temperature and vibration are decoupled. The piezoelectric vibration sensor based on LGS has a higher operating temperature and better stability than other reported vibration sensors, making it ideal for monitoring structural health in high-temperature environments.

Sensor design

The D15 mode of langasite is used in the design and production of the shear vibration sensor. Shear vibration sensors have several advantages over compression vibration sensors, including low interference. A three-dimensional representation of the shear vibration sensor is presented in Fig. 1(a–c). The tangent of the LGS crystal used in this study is (0, 138.5, 26.7). The overall size of the sensor package is 28 mm × 18 mm × 14.5 mm. The sensor package shell and base, mass block, and bolt are made of 304 stainless steel. The internal structure of the sensor is composed of LGS chips combined in series and parallel. The wafer dimensions are 7 mm × 5 mm × 0.5 mm. The wafer is bolted between the base and mass block. As the sputtering metal film electrode exhibits agglomeration, fall-off, and other phenomena in a high-temperature environment limited by high temperature, the sensor fabricated in this study does not use a metal film electrode, thus improving the production structure and enabling the steady functioning of the sensor in a high-temperature environment. When the sensor receives vibration acceleration, the mass acts on the chip to produce a shear force in the shear direction and an output signal with a proportional voltage. A schematic of the test device used to analyze the performance of the sensor is shown in Fig. 1(d). The link between the sensor sensitivity and temperature at various frequencies was examined in the frequency range of 80–1000 Hz to further investigate the viability of high-temperature vibration sensors. The fluctuation in the sensor sensitivity with the temperature at various frequencies is shown in Fig. 4(b). This figure illustrates the changes in sensitivity with varying frequencies. At each temperature, the sensitivity varies by less than 2 %. The results demonstrate that the sensor is highly feasible and suitable for high-temperature environments.

To analyze the stability of the shear vibration sensor in a high-temperature environment, the sensitivity of the sensor was tested in the frequency and temperature ranges of 80–1000 Hz and 30–750 °C, respectively. Fig. 4(c) shows the relationship between the temperature and sensitivity at different frequencies. As shown in the figure, the relationship between temperature and sensitivity increases monotonically, and the sensitivity of the sensor steadily increases with increasing temperature. The average sensitivity of the sensor is 0.074 V/g in the range of test frequency and temperature. The average sensitivity is 0.208 V/g at 750 °C. Thus, the sensor is confirmed to have good temperature stability in a high-temperature environment.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed and fabricated a shear vibration sensor using an LGS piezoelectric crystal. The sensing capabilities and temperature variation of the developed sensor in the high-temperature range of 750 °C were evaluated in the frequency range of 80–1000 Hz. The sensitivity of the sensor is determined as 0.053 V/g at the room temperature of 30 °C and 0.208 V/g on average between 30 and 750 °C. At various temperatures, the sensitivity varies by less than 2 % at the same frequency. The results demonstrate that the sensor is highly feasible and suitable for high-temperature environments. Additionally, this study proposes a temperature decoupling technique to avoid the problem arising because of the temperature changing the dielectric constant of the LGS material and interfering with acceleration loading. The highest inaccuracy after demodulation is 4.54 %, and the acceleration value is essentially consistent with the reference acceleration value. Vibration sensors based on LGS piezoelectric crystals are promising new alternatives in the area of high-temperature sensing.

Materials and methods

A stainless steel rod connects the sensor to the shaker surface. The sensor end is positioned in a vertical tubular furnace. The vibrator is composed of a digital electric vibration test system R0500SL, digital vibration controller (Elite8/8), and power amplifier (SA-50). A sinusoidal signal is generated via a digital vibration controller (Elite8/8), and the output sinusoidal signal is amplified by the power amplifier after the input shaker, thus generating the vibration signal. The charge amplifier circuit, using a self-designed converter, amplifies the sensor's output charge signal and converts it to a voltage signal. The oscilloscope records the voltage signals for transient signal analysis. The acceleration was calibrated at room temperature(25 °C) using a commercial acceleration sensor as the reference. The commercial acceleration and vibration sensors created for this experiment were mounted side by side, and both received the same sinusoidal stimulation amplitude.
