Feasibility and safety of ipsilateral antegrade 4-French access in the endovascular treatment of peripheral artery disease
Authors: Arkadiusz Kacała, Jędrzej Fischer, Mateusz Dorochowicz, Julia Chernyk, Joanna Szydziak, Aleksandra Hrapkowicz, Martyna Wrześniewska, Julia Wołoszczak, Kinga Janowska, Kamil Litwinowicz, Andrzej Szuba, Maciej Guziński
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTraditionally, contralateral retrograde or ipsilateral antegrade 6 F access has been the predominant approach for endovascular interventions in peripheral artery disease (PAD). This study explores the feasibility and safety of using ipsilateral antegrade 4 F access as an alternative in the endovascular treatment of PAD.ObjectivesThe objective of this study is to evaluate the technical success and complication rates associated with ipsilateral antegrade 4 F access in PAD patients undergoing endovascular procedures.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective study included 113 PAD patients (77 males and 36 females) who underwent endovascular treatment using the ipsilateral antegrade 4 F technique between February 2020 and July 2023. Data on patient demographics, procedural characteristics, technical success, and periprocedural complications were collected and analyzed.ResultsComplete technical success was achieved in 106 cases (93.8%), partial technical success in 6 cases (5.3%), and technical failure in 1 case (0.9%). Complications occurred in 4 patients (3.5%), while 109 patients (96.5%) had no complications.ConclusionsIpsilateral antegrade 4 F access demonstrates high technical success and low complication rates, suggesting it is a viable and safe alternative for endovascular treatment of PAD. However, further prospective studies are recommended to validate these findings and to assess the long-term safety and effectiveness of this technique.Keywords: Endovascular treatment, ipsilateral antegrade access, peripheral artery disease, 4 French access

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) stems from various causes, but it most commonly emerges as a manifestation of atherosclerosis, causing compromised blood flow to the lower limbs. The global impact of PAD exceeds 230 million individuals, with its occurrence steadily increasing alongside population aging and the prevalence of chronic diseases. In Poland, it is estimated that PAD affects approximately 7% of the adult population, particularly among the elderly and those with cardiovascular risk factors, underscoring the local significance of the disease [1,2]. PAD covers a spectrum of conditions, ranging from asymptomatic stenosis or intermittent claudication (IC) to chronic limb‐threatening ischemia (CLTI). Approximately 10% of PAD patients experience CLTI, and within 5 years, 5% to 10% of individuals with asymptomatic PAD or IC may progress to CLTI [3]. Patients suffering from diabetes and individuals with calcified arteries are especially prone to CLTI [4,5].

Management of PAD begins with lifestyle modifications, including smoking cessation, exercise, and dietary modifications, aimed at reducing the risk of cardiovascular complications. Patients experiencing intermittent claudication can often be treated with a combination of exercise and medical therapy. Should the patient’s quality of life and functioning fail to improve, vascular procedures are considered. Urgent revascularization is imperative in instances of critical and acute limb ischemia due to the imminent threat to the limb [6,7].

Historically, PAD was treated primarily through open surgical methods, however, with technological advancements there has been a growing trend toward prioritizing endovascular interventions for an increasing number of peripheral cases [8–10] and several recent reviews have documented these advances [11].

In the past ten years, the use of paclitaxel‐coated and more recently dual‐drug-coated devices has led to a major advance in endovascular device therapy by lowering restenosis rates [12]. Although drug‐coated devices may reduce restenosis rates [13], it remains unclear how these surrogate improvements translate into direct patient benefits and there is uncertainty regarding a possible increased risk of long‐term mortality [14]. This uncertainty highlights the need to develop alternative endovascular approaches [15].

In this vein, this study seeks to evaluate the feasibility and safety of ipsilateral antegrade 4‐French access in the endovascular treatment of PAD.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study evaluated consecutive patients with PAD who underwent endovascular treatment using ipsilateral antegrade 4‐French access between January 2022 and December 2023. (Patient enrollment details, including the total number of patients, are provided in the Results section.) Written informed consent for the endovascular procedure was obtained from all patients at the time of treatment. For this retrospective study, informed consent for data analysis was waived in accordance with local IRB guidelines. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 version) and was approved by Bioethics Committee at the Medical University of Wrocław.

Inclusion criteria included patients with a confirmed diagnosis of PAD who presented with symptoms classified as Fontaine class 2a or higher and/or Rutherford class 1 or higher. Exclusion criteria comprised patients undergoing diagnostic arteriography without percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA), those treated using an alternative to the ipsilateral antegrade approach, and patients treated with non‐compatible 4‐French devices.

Under ultrasound guidance and local anaesthesia, the preferred technique was to puncture the most proximal segment of the superficial femoral artery; however, in cases where the SFA was stenosed/obstructed at its origin, an alternative puncture site such as the common femoral artery was utilized. Such alternative access strategies have been well documented in recent studies [16]. A short 4‐French sheath was then inserted over the guidewire. Diagnostic arteriography of the peripheral arteries was performed; if the lesion extended to the distal arteries, the short sheath was exchanged for a longer (45 or 65 cm) sheath. Heparin was administered only after sheath exchange. Both stents (up to 7 mm in diameter) and balloons compatible with 4‐French sheaths were available. Stent implantation was performed when balloon angioplasty resulted in residual stenosis >30% or when flow‐limiting dissection was observed. (Figure 1).

The sheath was left in place for removal within the angiology department approximately 2–3 h post‐procedure to allow for controlled haemostasis, although in selected cases it was removed immediately if deemed safe by the operator. Haemostasis was achieved through controlled ultrasound‐guided compression followed by a pressure dressing. Patients were instructed to remain in a supine position with the accessed limb extended for about 12 h post‐procedure. After removal of the pressure dressing, a follow‐up ultrasound was performed to assess the patency of the treated vessels and to evaluate the access site for complications. Additionally, every patient was scheduled for a follow‐up visit at 3 months in the angiological outpatient clinic or on the ward, during which a repeat ultrasound examination was performed. This 3‐month follow‐up period is considered sufficient for assessing the short‐term safety and feasibility of the procedure.

Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.4.1. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The relationship between clinical success and continuous variables was determined using the Student’s t-test. For categorical variables, Fisher’s exact test was employed. The relationship between concomitant diseases and clinical success was evaluated using logistic regression with R’s glm function. The impact of the intervention on categorical variables was determined using the Stuart-Maxwell test. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 113 patients with PAD who underwent endovascular treatment using ipsilateral antegrade 4‐French access were included. The study group comprised 77 males and 36 females, aged 35 to 94 years, with a mean age of 70.1 years. All patients presented with symptoms corresponding to Fontaine class 2a or higher and/or Rutherford class 1 or higher prior to the procedure. All procedures were performed at a single center, ensuring a consistent approach to patient care.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that ipsilateral antegrade 4‐French access for the endovascular treatment of PAD is both feasible and safe, with high technical success and a low rate of complications. Accurately determining the true prevalence of PAD is challenging due to its often asymptomatic presentation. However, the consensus is that the occurrence of PAD is steadily increasing due to the aging population and the prevalence of chronic diseases [1–2]. Although lifestyle modifications are the first-line treatment, endovascular revascularization is crucial when patient quality of life and limb viability are threatened.

Endovascular treatment depends on establishing successful vascular access to reach the targeted lesion. In our experience, the proximal part of the superficial femoral artery was usually selected as the access site due to its consistent anatomy, superficial location, and large caliber. This approach minimizes complications that may arise when accessing the common femoral artery higher up, where guidewires may inadvertently enter the deep femoral artery, thus prolonging the procedure [17,18]. The contralateral approach is common worldwide but requires crossing the aortic bifurcation, and the use of longer devices can be problematic during deployment and navigation [19,20].

While the widely researched standard employs 6‐French devices [21–23], the use of 4‐French devices offers significant advantages. The 4‐French approach has been associated with the elimination of vascular closure devices (VCDs) and a significant reduction in access‐site complications [24–26]. Ambulatory endovascular treatment protocols—including same-day discharge—have been successfully implemented in patients with intermittent claudication and further supported by multicentre trials comparing 4-French and 6-French access strategies [21,22].

The overall complete technical success rate of 93.8% supports both the feasibility of the antegrade approach and the effectiveness of 4‐French devices. Nearly half of the cases (43.9%) involved interventions on multiple vessels, with partial technical success observed in only 6 instances. Out of 182 vessels treated, successful recanalization was achieved in 173 (95.1%). Importantly, in procedures involving multiple vessels, any failure was confined to a single vessel.

The 3‐month follow‐up period, during which patients underwent ultrasound evaluation in the angiological outpatient clinic or on the ward, is generally regarded as sufficient for assessing short‐term safety and feasibility.

This study’s retrospective design and single-center nature limit the ability to establish causality and generalize findings to other settings. Additionally, the relatively short follow-up period restricts assessment of long-term outcomes, and the lack of data on certain procedural details, such as patient adherence, may have influenced the results. Further multicenter, prospective studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

In patients with PAD, the use of ipsilateral antegrade 4‐French access for endovascular revascularization offers several advantages, including a high success rate, minimal complication risk, and the ability to access multiple distal vessels simultaneously, thus broadening the scope of treatable lesions. This approach facilitates shorter hospital stays and quicker patient recovery. The 3‐month follow‐up period in our study was sufficient to assess the safety and feasibility of the procedure, supporting its viability for endovascular treatment of PAD. Future studies may further evaluate long-term durability and clinical outcomes.
