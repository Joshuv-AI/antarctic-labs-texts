Navigating crisis: teleconsultation use and its determinants among internally displaced persons in wartime Sudan
Authors: Esra Abdallah Abdalwahed Mahgoub, Manahil Nouri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmidst the ongoing conflict in Sudan, several voluntary initiatives have emerged to address the collapse of the healthcare system. This study aimed to investigate the utilization and determinants of teleconsultation services among internally displaced persons (IDPs) in Sudan.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional community-based study was conducted among IDPs in Shendi city, Sudan. A multistage sampling technique was employed to select 600 adult IDPs from 10 clusters within the city. Data were collected between the 1st and the 27th of December 2024, through face‒to-face interviews. Data were collected using a structured, closed-ended questionnaire, specifically developed for the study, and administered by ten trained data collectors via the Kobo Collect platform. The data were analyzed via the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Version 26.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 36.9 years (SD = 15), with 58.2% female. Over half (51.7%) had been displaced for more than a year. Smartphone ownership (77.5%) and internet access (73.0%) were high. Teleconsultation utilization was low (19.2%). Nearly half of the respondents (48.5%) felt uneasy discussing sensitive health issues over teleconsultation, and 52.8% lacked trust in the diagnoses provided through the service. Factors associated with increased utilization included female gender, employment, chronic illness, social media use, and prior teleconsultation experience. Over 80% of the users expressed satisfaction with the service. No association was found between the acceptability of teleconsultation and its actual utilization.ConclusionTeleconsultation offers a valuable healthcare delivery tool for IDPs in conflict settings. However, its low utilization highlights the need for strategic planning and targeted interventions to enhance uptake. Ongoing monitoring and adaptation are essential to ensure the service remains responsive to the evolving challenges of conflict-affected contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13430-0.Keywords: Sudan, War, Armed conflict, Telemedicine, MHealth, Remote consultation, Internally displaced persons

Introduction

As of 2023, conflicts and violence have resulted in the internal displacement of 68.3 million people around the world [1]. Half of these internally displaced persons (IDPs) are concentrated in five countries: Sudan, Syria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Colombia, and Yemen [1]. Unlike refugees, IDPs continue to suffer from the direct and indirect effects of the conflict that initially forced them to flee their homes [2]. They face immense hardships, including poverty, stigmatization, and ongoing mobility or repeated displacement, all of which can hinder their access to health services [2]resulting in higher mortality and morbidity rates than those of the general population [3].

Successful telemedicine innovations have been implemented globally within humanitarian contexts. Teleconsultation, which provides remote clinical care to patients and connects healthcare providers with individuals in different locations [4]helps to overcome financial constraints and address the challenges of insecurity, violence, and restricted movement in difficult-to-reach and remote areas [5–10]. Bowsher et al. reviewed 27 eHealth interventions for clinical management operating in conflict zones, including Syria, Afghanistan, Lebanon, South Sudan, the DRC, the Central African Republic, and Chechnya [9]. Furthermore, Parkes et al. reported the use of telemedicine interventions in conflict-affected countries in the Eastern Mediterranean Region [11]. While numerous studies have investigated public acceptance and attitudes toward telemedicine in non-disaster settings [12–28]Parkes et al. highlighted the scarcity of studies on patient perspectives toward the adoption of telemedicine in conflict settings compared with studies on healthcare worker perspectives [11].

The war that commenced in Sudan on 15 April 2023 between the Sudanese armed forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces Militia (RSF), had displaced 10,890,722 people internally by September 2024 [29]. The massive decimation of the country’s healthcare system, severe shortages of medical supplies, and the continued displacement of healthcare workers have resulted in a significant reduction in accessibility to health services [30, 31]. To mitigate this, several measures have been implemented to ensure healthcare access for IDPs and those living in conflict-affected areas. Among them are teleconsultation programs initiated by the Sudan Federal Ministry of Health [32]the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB) [33]various national associations (such as the Sudanese Association of Physicians, family doctors, obstetricians, gynecologists, and pediatricians) and diaspora groups (such as the Alliance for Sudanese Doctors in Qatar [34]). However, despite the numerous teleconsultation programs, two published studies have examined this area, focusing primarily on healthcare workers’ experiences and perceptions [34, 35].

The effectiveness of teleconsultation interventions cannot be evaluated without a comprehensive understanding of the experience and perspectives of the end users. To that end, our study seeks to investigate the utilization, acceptability, and determinants of the use of teleconsultation among IDPs in Sudan. Because most studies on the health of IDPs focus on those living in IDPs camps [3] (as they are more identifiable and accessible for research) this study focuses instead on community-based IDPs to capture their unique experiences. Ultimately, we aim to empower humanitarian relief actors to make informed decisions regarding the integration of telemedicine into healthcare interventions in conflict-affected settings, both during and after crises.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and population

We conducted a community-based, descriptive cross-sectional study in Shendi City, situated on the southeastern bank of the Nile River in River Nile State, northern Sudan, approximately 150 km northeast of Khartoum (the capital). Shendi City is part of Shendi Locality, which comprises six administrative units, with only Shendi city classified as an urban area. Due to the ongoing conflict in Sudan, Shendi City has served as a refuge for IDPs fleeing violence in Khartoum and other affected regions [36].

We included IDPs as defined by the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) [37]. The inclusion criteria were; being over 18 years of age, self-identifying as an IDP, having experienced displacement since 15 April 2023, residing in Shendi City for at least one week at the time of data collection; and being hosted by the community in private homes or renting a house. We excluded IDPs residing in accommodation centers (because their access to healthcare access from humanitarian actors, could influence their teleconsultation needs), non-Arabic speakers, and non-Sudanese individuals (to account for potential differences in displacement experiences resulting from varying social factors).

Sampling

To determine the appropriate sample size, the total number of Sudanese IDPs in Shendi Locality was acquired from the International Organization for Migration website. These data revealed a population of 259,510 individuals [38]. Due to the lack of information at the locality level, the rural-to-urban distribution of IDPs in River Nile State was applied to the Shendi Locality data. This distribution indicates that 60% of IDPs reside in urban areas, whereas 40% are located in rural areas [39]. After excluding 5% of the IDPs who live in schools or public buildings [39]the estimated number of Sudanese IDPs in Shendi city was 147,920. Using Epi Info, the required sample size was calculated on the basis of a 95% confidence level, a design effect of 1.5, clusters of 10, and an expected frequency of 50%. The resulting target sample size was 580. By dividing the total number of IDPs in the locality by the total number of households, we estimated an average household size of five individuals [39].

Multistage sampling was used to select the participants. Cluster sampling was used in the first stage, and the city was divided into 23 clusters, with the main roads serving as natural boundaries; for simplicity and practicality, then 10 clusters were selected randomly. In the second stage, each data collector was accompanied by a key informant who had knowledge of the area and the households hosting IDPs. These informants were either members of the neighborhood committee or long-standing residents who had lived in the area prior to the conflict; their role was to guide the data collectors about the household locations and facilitate introductions. Data collection began at a point deemed accessible and practical for each data collector, and 20 households were identified based on proximity and informant guidance.

In the third stage, the key informants facilitated introductions, after which data collectors conveniently selected three adults from each household. Each interviewer alternated between interviewing a man and a woman in each household (when possible) to ensure gender balance in the sample.

Relying on key informants for household selection may have introduced selection bias, as informants’ personal knowledge and preferences, rather than a systematic method, determined household selection, potentially affecting representativeness of the sample.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured, closed-ended questionnaire designed specifically for this study in Arabic, and subsequently translated into English (the English version of the questionnaire is provided in Additional file 1). The questionnaire comprises three sections: demographics and medical history, the use of teleconsultation, and the acceptability of teleconsultation. The questionnaire was reviewed by three researchers and piloted with 10 IDPs from the Almatama locality, River Nile State, to test comprehension. Pilot feedback led to wording improvements. The Cronbach’s alpha for the acceptability scale was 0.703, indicating acceptable reliability.

Data were collected through face‒to-face interviews. Ten medical students from Shendi City were selected as data collectors, and trained on KoboCollect, interview techniques, and the questionnaire. KoboCollect is an Android application that allows users to gather data in the field, even without an internet connection. It is part of KoBoToolbox, a free, open-source platform for data collection, management, and visualization used globally for humanitarian aid, development, and research [40]. The data were collected in between the 1 st and the 27th of December 2024.

Data analysis

To facilitate data analysis, the KoboCollect form included each question in English, followed by its Arabic translation. The data were extracted from KoboCollect in Excel, cleaned (including the removal of the Arabic text) and analyzed the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) program version 26. Univariate analysis was presented using frequency tables, graphs, and narratives. Chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and the Kruskal‒Wallis test were used for bivariate analysis. Multivariate analysis was conducted using binary logistic regression to identify predictors of teleconsultation use. The independent variables included gender, age, employment status, history of chronic illness, social media use, prior teleconsultation use (during the COVID-19 pandemic), number of displacements, health insurance coverage, and long-term medication use.

To evaluate the acceptability of teleconsultation, responses to nine questions were measured using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Scores for Question 5 “ I would feel uneasy discussing personal or sensitive health issues over teleconsultation” and Question 6 “I am concerned that I wouldn’t be able to trust a diagnosis made via teleconsultation” were reverse-scored, with 5 representing “Strongly Disagree” and 1 representing “Strongly Agree. A total score ranging from 9 to 45 was calculated for each respondent based on their responses. K-means cluster analysis was performed to group respondents based on the similarity of their acceptability scores. This analysis produced three distinct clusters, and the thresholds for these groups were determined by the cluster boundaries. Accordingly, participants scoring less than 27 were classified as having low acceptability, those scoring between 27 and 34 as having moderate acceptability, and those scoring 35 or above as having high acceptability.

Operational definitions

Teleconsultation programs refer to remote, online medical consultations provided to IDPs by healthcare professionals who are licensed either in Sudan or in the country in which they currently practice. These services are part of initiatives led by medical associations, diaspora groups, the Ministry of Health, medical institutions, or NGOs since the onset of the war. These services are offered at no cost to the IDPs and utilize digital platforms to facilitate real-time or asynchronous communication between the healthcare provider and the patient.

Results

Demographic and Technology Access

A total of 600 IDPs participated in the study. The mean age of the participants was 36.9 years (SD = 15). Among them, 349 (58.2%) were female, 243 (40.5%) had a paid job, and 244 (40.7%) held a university degree. Approximately half (310, 51.7%) had been displaced for more than one year, and 368 (61.3%) had experienced displacement once. Furthermore, 465 (77.5%) owned a smartphone, 438 (73.0%) had internet access on their mobile devices, and 301 (50.2%) were regular social media users. On average, there were approximately five adult IDPs per household, and about three adult IDPs with smartphones per household. Teleconsultation usage

The rate of teleconsultation use among the IDPs in this study was 19.2% (115). Among those who utilized teleconsultation services, 72 (62.6%) used it for themselves, 57 (49.6%) for a family member, 68 (59.1%) used it once or twice, and 46 (40%) used it for emergency conditions.

The most commonly consulted specialty was general medicine (61, 53%), followed by obstetrics and gynecology (25, 21.7%), and 77 (67%) participants were already acquainted with the doctor they consulted. The majority (100, 87%) shared their medical history with the consulted doctor, and 96 (83.5%) reported being satisfied or very satisfied with their experience. The most commonly used platform was WhatsApp (81, 70.4%), followed by phone calls (47, 40.8%). No significant associations were found between teleconsultation use and age, gender, marital status, employment status, displacement frequency, household size, chronic illness, medication use, health insurance, or acceptability level (p >.05), (Additional file 2). Significantly greater levels of teleconsultation use were observed among participants with postgraduate/university degrees (p <.001), those displaced for more than one year (p =.002), those with prior teleconsultation experience (during the COVID-19 pandemic) (p <.001), smartphone owners (p =.009), those with internet access (p =.001), and regular social media users (p <.001) (Additional file 2).

Binary logistic regression revealed a statistically significant model (χ²(13) = 104.563, p <.001), explaining 25.6% of the variation in teleconsultation use (Nagelkerke R²) and accurately predicting 83.8% of cases. Positive predictors included employment status, history of chronic illness, social media use, and previous use of teleconsultation (during the COVID-19 pandemic). IDPs with paid jobs were 1.83 times more likely to use teleconsultation services than unemployed individuals were, whereas those with chronic illnesses had 2.90 times higher odds of utilization. Regular social media users were 2.13 times more likely to use teleconsultations, and prior teleconsultation users demonstrated the strongest association, being 8.93 times more likely to continue services. In contrast, male IDPs had 45.1% lower odds of using teleconsultation services than females did (Additional file 2). The non-significant variables were age, number of displacements, health insurance, and long-term medication use.

Discussion

This descriptive, cross-sectional study investigated teleconsultation utilization and acceptance among IDPs in Sudan amidst the ongoing conflict. Our findings revealed low utilization rate, more than one-third of the participants reported high acceptability of teleconsultation, and no association was found between the usage and the acceptability. Factors such as gender, employment status, history of chronic illness, social media use, and prior use emerged as significant predictors of teleconsultation utilization.

Teleconsultation utilization rate among IDPs in our study was low (19.2%). This might be partially explained by the heavy focus on social media to promote these services, which may have excluded people who are not active online. Our findings support this assumption, showing that social media users had a higher rate and odds of using teleconsultation. To enhance utilization, future efforts should use more inclusive and varied promotion methods that go beyond digital platforms and ensure broader community reach. Leveraging alternative channels such as community outreach programs, traditional media outlets (radio, television), and healthcare provider networks can effectively reach populations that are less active on social media, ensuring equitable access to these valuable healthcare services.

The rates of smartphone ownership (77.5%) and internet access (73%) were high among the IDPs in our study. This may be attributed to the focus of the study’s focus on an urban population, with potentially higher socioeconomic status than IDPs in rural areas. As of 2022, internet penetration in Sudan was only 29% [41]; therefore, to ensure health equity, the integration of teleconsultation services within the local health system could help bridge the digital divide and ensure access to healthcare for underserved communities [42]. This requires collaboration among key stakeholders, including the Ministry of Health, the Telecommunications and Post Regulatory Authority, and active NGOs, to provide the necessary technical infrastructure and coordinate service delivery to this vulnerable group.

Among our participants, WhatsApp was the platform most commonly used for teleconsultation (70.4%). This application has been widely used for patient‒doctor communication in Sudan even before the conflict [43, 44]. United Nations organizations also used WhatsApp to spread information in Sudan during the COVID-19 pandemic [45]. With over 9 million users in Sudan [46]WhatsApp’s user-friendly interface, low cost, and proven ability to enhance communication in healthcare [47] make it a particularly strong candidate to serve as the primary platform for teleconsultation in the country.

IDPs with higher educational levels had significantly higher rates of teleconsultation utilization. Similarly, Kontos et al. reported education as a key determinant of eHealth use [48]. This may stem from the expected higher digital health literacy among individuals with higher education levels [49]. Therefore, targeted interventions, such as digital health literacy programs, are essential to improve the uptake among IDPs with lower educational levels. Additionally, the design of the platforms should be user-friendly and consider reducing technical barriers for individuals with limited education.

In our study, being male was a negative predictor of teleconsultation use, a finding that is consistent with previous studies that linked female gender with greater telehealth adoption [48, 50, 51]. In contrast, Pessoa et al. (2016) [52] and Vera Cruz & Dlamini (2021) [53] reported greater male utilization and willingness to use teleconsultation. These discrepancies may reflect cultural or social norms shaping how gender affects care-seeking behavior. To ensure equitable access, further research is needed to understand the barriers that hinder male engagement with teleconsultation and to develop tailored interventions accordingly.

Individuals with chronic illnesses were three times more likely to use teleconsultation services, which aligns with findings from Al-Shroby et al. [54]. This is particularly promising, as telehealth has been recommended for managing chronic conditions and facilitating follow-ups for patients with regular outpatient appointments [55, 56]. The higher adoption among this group underscores the potential of teleconsultation to improve access, continuity of care, and outcomes for patients with chronic health needs. Teleconsultation should be prioritized in interventions targeting patients with chronic diseases in humanitarian settings by integrating it into routine follow-up, medication management, and remote monitoring.

Compared with those displaced for shorter durations, those displaced for more than a year presented significantly higher rates of teleconsultation use. This may be attributed to several factors. Prolonged displacement can lead to an accumulation of health issues, both physical and mental, increasing the need for healthcare services. Moreover, prolonged displacement can significantly impact the financial stability of IDPs, making teleconsultation a more accessible and convenient healthcare option for those who cannot afford in-person visits and those with limited access to transportation or healthcare facilities.

Most of the participants who used teleconsultation had prior familiarity with the doctor they consulted. This finding indicates that personal trust outweighs trust in the teleconsultation platform or the system itself and could reflect broader hesitancy toward unfamiliar practitioners, potentially due to concerns about qualifications, accountability, or perceived competence. Patient trust is recognized as a significant factor influencing the utilization of digital health service platforms [57]. To build trust, several strategies can be employed. The Ministry of Health can adopt an accreditation system for teleconsultation services to ensure that they meet acceptable quality standards set by the ministry and to safeguard patient data. In addition, partnering with local healthcare or academic institutions can enhance the credibility and authority of teleconsultation programs. Providers should ensure transparency about the practitioner by showcasing the credentials, licenses, and affiliations of teleconsultation practitioners. Displaying patient reviews or ratings may help build trust in unfamiliar doctors, and providing fast and responsive communication services to users might further enhance patient trust.

Approximately half of the participants in our study expressed discomfort in discussing personal or sensitive health issues via teleconsultation. This reluctance is likely due to fears around data protection, confidentiality, and security, which are concerns that are well documented in the telemedicine literature [58–61]with the WHO emphasizing the importance of appropriate legislation and data protection policies [61]. During conflict, heightened caution is necessary to prevent information misuse by warring parties [60]. Another factor that might discourage IDPs from discussing sensitive issues with healthcare providers through teleconsultation is overcrowding in host households, which may inhibit private discussions. This raises questions about the suitability of teleconsultation for sensitive issues such as mental health or stigmatized conditions such as HIV.

Over half of the participants in our study expressed a lack of trust in diagnoses made via teleconsultation. This mirrors concerns voiced by Sudanese doctors, as Eljack et al. reported that 76.8% of doctors were concerned about the potential for misdiagnosis during teleconsultations [34]. Diagnostic errors are a well-documented concern in telemedicine [50, 62]with the absence of physical examination being a key limitation. This shared apprehension between patients and providers highlights the need for education on the capabilities and limitations of teleconsultation to address misconceptions. Furthermore, service providers should offer clear guidelines for doctors in situations where virtual consultations are not appropriate due to an elevated risk of diagnostic errors [62].

Our findings reveal a nuanced picture of teleconsultation acceptability. While one-third of the participants exhibited high acceptability, a substantial portion demonstrated moderate acceptability, suggesting a general openness to the concept, albeit with some reservations. Previous research has demonstrated generally positive attitudes among Sudanese people toward teleconsultation before the war [44]. However, the lack of a significant association between the level of acceptance and the rate of utilization underscores the complexity of factors influencing healthcare behavior. It may be partly due to unmeasured confounding factors such as digital literacy and the psychological impact of displacement, which may have affected both a participant’s ability to use the service and their overall attitude toward it. While fostering positive attitudes is crucial, efforts must also address practical barriers and concerns that may prevent individuals from utilizing these services.

The finding that more than 80% of participants in this urban setting were satisfied with teleconsultation services, even amidst an ongoing conflict, suggests that these services have the potential to address the healthcare needs of populations in crisis effectively. This positive feedback, while observed in an urban context, provides encouraging evidence for the viability and importance of teleconsultation as a crucial component of healthcare delivery, particularly in emergency situations. Future research and implementation efforts should focus on strategies to increase the accessibility and effectiveness of teleconsultation services for populations in more remote and marginalized areas.

Limitations

While this study offers valuable insights into teleconsultation use among IDPs in an urban setting, several limitations should be considered. Being conducted in Shendi city, the findings of this study are most applicable to similar urban contexts in Sudan; therefore, further research is needed in rural areas, where different challenges may affect teleconsultation use. Owing to the reluctance of neighborhood committees to share the IDPs’ hosting household lists, the sampling of the households relied on key informants and nonrandom selection. This approach may have introduced selection bias and excluded less accessible households. The choice of households was based on the informants’ knowledge, which may have been incomplete or influenced by personal preferences. Even when aware of eligible households, informants might have directed data collectors toward certain households for subjective reasons. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as the sampling approach may have affected the representativeness and generalizability of the results. Additionally, the highly dynamic nature of the ongoing conflict in Sudan, particularly its impact on electricity and internet access, may influence the use of teleconsultation services. Despite these limitations, as the first study to explore teleconsultation use among internally displaced persons in Sudan, our research addresses a critical and timely knowledge gap.

Conclusion

This study highlights a critical yet underutilized opportunity to leverage teleconsultation for IDPs in Sudan amidst conflict, particularly in areas with reliable internet access and widespread smartphone use. Overall usage was low, despite variable but promising acceptability levels. The key positive predictors for utilization included higher education, chronic illness, and prior experience, whereas male gender was negatively associated with teleconsultation use.

The dynamic nature of the conflict necessitates continuous monitoring and adaptable strategies to ensure relevant and effective telemedicine interventions. Additionally, future interventions must adopt inclusive outreach strategies, integrate services within the existing health system, address trust and privacy concerns, and enhance digital health literacy. Ultimately, tailored and collaborative efforts are essential to ensure equitable access to this vital healthcare modality for IDPs in this evolving humanitarian crisis.
