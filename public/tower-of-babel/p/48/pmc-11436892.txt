A systematic review on the techniques, long-term outcomes, and complications of partial breast irradiation after breast-conserving surgery for early-stage breast cancer
Authors: Jing Ning, Guanghui Cheng, Ning Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of four techniques of partial breast irradiation (PBI) including interstitial brachytherapy (ISBT), balloon-based brachytherapy (BBT), Intraoperative radiotherapy (IORT) and three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3DCRT) in the treatment for early-stage breast cancer patients after breast-conserving surgery. A systematic search was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines using the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library and Web of Science databases. The inclusion criteria were clinical trials and observational studies that reported on outcome measures of principal PBI techniques. The methodological quality of the included research data was assessed using bias risk assessment tool with the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS), and the research information were analyzed using data analysis software. Clinical studies were collected from the earliest available date until September 2023. Fifty-one studies were included, with a total sample size of 7708. The results of network meta-analysis (NMA) showed that ISBT can lower the local recurrence (SUCRA: 73.8%). In terms of reducing distant metastasis, 3DCRT may be the best choice (SUCRA: 52.5%). And IORT has the highest 5-year overall survival (SUCRA: 90%). Furthermore, ISBT also has the advantages of lowest risk with fat necrosis (SUCRA: 72.5%), infection (SUCRA: 78.3%) and breast pain (SUCRA: 86.2%). BBT may be the optimal solution for fibrosis (SUCRA: 76.9%) and hyperpigmentation (SUCRA: 66.7%). 3DCRT has lower incidence of telangiectasia (SUCRA: 56.7%) and better cosmetic result (SUCRA: 85%). Postoperative PBI treatment using ISBT after breast-conserving surgery in patients with early-stage breast cancer may be a more valuable choice based on the treatment efficacy and is associated with fewer late side-effects. Large-scale, prospective, long-term studies are warranted to clarify the role of different PBI techniques in selected patients.Keywords: Breast cancer, Partial breast irradiation, Interstitial brachytherapy, Balloon-based brachytherapy, Intraoperative radiotherapy, Three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy, Network meta-analysisSubject terms: Breast cancer, Radiotherapy

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a worldwide public health dilemma, and is currently the most common malignant tumor and the leading cause of cancer-related death among women1. Early detection and diagnosis of breast cancer2, as well as women’s requirements for beautiful and functional breasts have gradually improved, breast-conserving surgery has been increasingly accepted as the standard surgical option for suitable breast cancer patients3. Radiotherapy is required after breast-conserving surgery for early-stage breast cancer. Adjuvant radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery not only halves the risk of local recurrence, but also prolong the disease-free survival, and it is considered the standard of care for the vast majority of patients with early invasive breast cancer4.

Whole breast irradiation (WBI) represents an integral part of combined breast-conserving treatment of early-stage breast cancer.A number of studies5–7 have shown that local recurrence after breast-conserving surgery mostly occurs around the tumor bed. Therefore, partial breast irradiation (PBI) uses large, high-dose radiation to illuminate the breast tissue around the tumor bed, rather than the entire breast, which can not only reduce the area of irradiation, but also shorten the duration of treatment. Especially for the appropriate population classified by the consensus panel of the American Society of Radiological Oncology (ASTRO). Low-risk individuals who meet the ASTRO8 consensus may consider PBI based on the following criteria: age ≥ 50 years, no BRCA1/2 gene mutation, confirmed T1N0M0 by pathology, single center, single focus, no neoadjuvant therapy, at least 2 mm negative incisal margin, no extensive DCIS component, hormone receptor-positive invasive ductal carcinoma or other breast cancer with good prognosis. A meta-analysis of 9 randomized controlled trials, including 15,187 women with early-stage breast cancer after breast-conserving surgery, showed that there was little difference in local recurrence-free survival and similar overall survival between the PBI and WBI groups9. The OCOG-RAPID and NSABP B-39/RTOG 0413 trials10 also demonstrated that PBI had no significant difference in ipsilateral breast tumor recurrence rates compared with WBI. In the largest randomized controlled trial of PBI conducted by the Groupe Europeen de Curietherapie-European Society for Therapeutic Radiology and Oncology (GEC-ESTRO)11, there was no difference between the PBI and WBI groups at 5 years in regional recurrence, distant metastasis, breast cancer mortality, and overall survival. In summary12,13, compared with WBI, PBI techniques provide an alternative method of postoperative radiotherapy for selected patients with early-stage breast cancer after breast-conserving surgery with acceptable toxicities14.

PBI involves a variety of techniques and may be applied intraoperatively or shortly after the surgery. Interstitial brachytherapy (ISBT)15 is a multi-tube brachytherapy in which several catheters are inserted into the tumor bed and its surrounding area of 1 to 2 cm that resembles the method routinely used to boost a dose for the tumour bed after WBI. The most commonly used fractionation schedule in PBI employing the pulse or low dose rate (PDR/LDR) of 45–50 Gy, and high dose rate (HDR) unit involves the administration of 34 Gy in 10 fractions over 5 days. Balloon-based brachytherapy (BBT) with the MammoSite brachytherapy applicator, a balloon with a centrally placed catheter holding a source of HDR radiation, was designed to simplify the intracavitary brachytherapy procedure for PBI16. The whole tumor cavity was filled up with a saline solution and contrast agent inflated balloon and irradiated covers the tumour bed with a margin of 1–1.5 cm with the fractionated dosage as ISBT. Intraoperative radiotherapy (IORT)17 involves a delivery of a single high dose of 20 Gy with 50 kV photon radiation achieved at 2 mm from the surface of the spherical applicator, or electron beam with energy of 3–12 MeV to deliver a dose of 21 Gy prescribed to the 90% isodose at the depth of 1.5–3 cm from lumpectomy bed identified during a surgical procedure. As compared to the other PBI techniques, postoperative irradiation of the tumor bed with external beam based on three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3DCRT) offers a very homogeneous dose distribution and low invasiveness with widely available therapy planning and delivery equipment18. However, less research has focused on comprehensively reviewing the efficacy and safety of several principal PBI techniques at present. Furthermore, there is also a lack of evidence-based recommendations on which technique may be possibly recommended as a routine alter native for WBI in carefully selected patient groups with early-stage breast cancer.

Network meta-analysis (NMA) is a relatively recent evidence-based method which expands the scope of a conventional pairwise analysis by analysing simultaneously both the direct and the indirect evidence from different studies, in order to compare the impact of multiple interventions on disease and estimate the relative effectiveness and rank ordering of each treatment19. Therefore, this study aimed to conduct a NMA to comprehensively assess the comparative efficacy of different PBI techniques (ISBT, BBT, IORT and 3DCRT) and provide evidence-based recommendations for patients and clinicians by systematically summarizing the results obtained.

Materials and methods

The Cochrane Handbook guidelines were applied and this article reported on according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) checklist (see Supplementary Table 1). The study has been registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) website (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/) with CRD42023495337.

Search strategy

The researchers in this paper searched four electronic databases (PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and Web of Science) from their creation to September 2023. References of eligible manuscripts were collected and checked for other relevant manuscripts on PBI after breast-conserving surgery for early-stage breast cancer. English-language articles examining management and factors affecting PBI were considered for eligibility, and a comprehensive search strategy was developed using applicable Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords. The search took the form of combining subject and free words, and the search terms included: breast neoplasms, mastectomy, segmental, interstitial implantation radiotherapy, balloon brachytherapy, brachytherapy, radiotherapy, conformal, intraoperative radiotherapy, intraoperative period and radiotherapy dosage. The detailed search strategy is shown in Supplementary Tables 2–5.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Experimental group with different PBI techniques as a treatment for early-stage breast cancer after breast-conserving surgery. (2) Clinical trial. (3) Outcome indicators including at least one of the following: survival, local recurrence, distant metastasis, late adverse effects [including fibrosis, telangiectasis, cosmetic result, fat necrosis, infection, breast pain and hyperpigmentation].

Exclusion criteria

(1) Studies with incomplete or unreported data. (2) Studies from animal studies, protocols, conference abstracts, case reports or correspondence. (3) Suffering with advanced breast cancer (4) Treatment without PBI or patients receiving the combination radiotherapy of WBI.

Study selection

The literature management software Endnote was utilized for the screening and exclusion of literature. Initially, two researchers conducted a preliminary screening to identify duplicate titles, review papers, conference papers, protocols and correspondence. Subsequently, the abstracts of the literature were thoroughly examined by two researchers in order to determine their inclusion or exclusion. Finally, the remaining literature underwent a comprehensive evaluation by both researchers to ascertain its compliance with the inclusion criteria. Throughout this process, independent screenings were performed by both researchers who subsequently compared their findings on the remaining literature. If there was agreement on its content, it was ultimately included. However, in cases where discrepancies arose between them, these differences were discussed and resolved with input from a third researcher.

Data extraction

A standardized and preselected data extraction form consisting of seven sections was utilized in this study to record data under the following headings: (1) author, (2) publication year, (3) country, (4) period of study, (5) sample size, (6) mean age, and (7) details of PBI intervention. The extraction of PBI related data included the type of intervention techniques (ISBT, BBT, IORT and 3DCRT), detailed treatment evaluation indicators (therapeutic outcomes and late adverse effects), and the number of people who achieved these indicators.

Methodological index for non-randomized studies

Both researchers independently assessed using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) tool which is a valid instrument designed to assess the methodological quality of non-randomized studies, whether comparative or non-comparative. The evaluation encompassed eight aspects: (1) the purpose of the study was clearly stated, (2) continuity of the included patients, (3) anticipated data collection, (4) the end points appropriately reflected the objectives of the study, (5) objectivity of endpoint evaluation, (6) whether the follow-up time was adequate, (7) the rate of loss to follow-up was less than 5%, and (8) whether the sample size was estimated. Each criterion was scored as 0 (unreported), 1 (reported but inadequate) or 2 (reported and adequate), totaling 16 points with higher overall scores indicating higher quality of trials. The quality of evidence was graded into three levels: low quality (10–11 points), moderate quality (12–14 points) and high quality (15–16 points). Specific details will be presented in Supplementary Table 6.

Data analysis

In studies that utilized PBI as an intervention, all variables were continuous and presented as mean plus standard deviation (SD)20. Continuous variables in the study will be reported and analyzed as mean difference (MD), which is defined as the absolute difference of means between treatment and control groups calculated using the same scale, or standardized mean difference (SMD), which is calculated by dividing the difference of means between groups by the standard deviation of subject outcomes. Both MD and SMD will be accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CI). Considering potential differences between studies, a random-effects model was chosen for our analysis instead of a fixed-effects model21.

We conducted NMA aggregation and analysis using Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation chains within a Bayesian framework with R language (version 4.3.3) following the PRISMA NMA instruction manual22. The nodal method was employed to quantify and assess agreement between indirect and direct comparisons based on instructions provided in R language if the I2 values<50%. The consistency test passed successfully23.

Network diagrams depicting different exercise interventions were generated using R language (version 4.3.3) to present and describe intervention levels. Each node represents a distinct exercise intervention along with its corresponding control condition, while lines connecting nodes represent direct comparisons between interventions. The size of each node and width of connecting lines are proportional to the number of studies included24.

The intervention levels were summarized and reported as the P score, which were treated as a frequentist analogue to surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) value. We calculated the average degree of certainty to determine if one treatment was superior to others across all competing treatments. The P score ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating the best treatment without uncertainty and 0 indicating the worst treatment without uncertainty. However, caution should be exercised when interpreting these scores unless there are clinically meaningful differences between interventions25. Network funnel plots and visual tests were conducted using the symmetry criterion to identify potential publication bias resulting from small sample studies in NMA.

Results

Study and identification and selection

A total of 7661 documents were retrieved from the electronic database, and an additional 19 documents were manually searched. After eliminating 2103 duplicates, the remaining 5577 documents were read for titles and abstracts, and 5292 documents were again excluded. The remaining 285 documents were read in full and 234 documents were again excluded (for primary reasons including: incomplete data, conference papers and failure to meet the interventions included in this review), leaving a final remaining 51 documents to be included in this study. Flow diagram of literature selection.

Quality assessment of the included studies

The MINORS tool was well calibrated and employed to assess a quality level of eligible studies (low, moderate, or high quality, n = 51), revealing that 8 studies were classified as low quality, 34 studies as medium quality, and 9 studies as high quality. Specifically, there were 2 studies rated at a score of 10 and 6 studies at a score of 11. Our results also indicated that there were 13 studies at a score of 12, 16 studies at a score of 13, followed by 5 studies with a rating of 14 points. Additionally, it presented a data set consisting of 6 studies scoring at the level of 15, and 3 more studies achieved an overall rating of 16 points.

Characteristics of the included studies

In total, we included studies from 51 clinical trials, which included 7708 patients diagnosed with early-stage breast cancer after breast-conserving surgery. Interventions included ISBT (23studies)26–48, BBT (9 studies)49–57, IORT (12 studies)58–69 and 3DCRT (7 studies)70–76. Twenty-seven studies reported local recurrence as an outcome indicator, 11 studies reported distant metastasis as an outcome indicator and 13 studies reported 5-year overall survival as an outcome indicator. The fibrosis, telangiectasis, cosmetic results, fat necrosis, infection, breast pain, hyperpigmentation and skin reaction were reported as types of radiation-induced adverse reactions in research literatures, respectively. There were twenty-three studies from America, eight studies from the Italy, three studies from France, three studies from Spain, three studies from Europe, two studies from Canada, two studies from Brazil, one study from Japan, one study from Hungery, one study from Korea, one study from Germany, one study from South Africa, one study from China and one study from Poland. Network meta-analysis

Local recurrence

A consistency test for local recurrence yielded an I² of 20%, an outcome measure that was considered to be in good agreement.

The results of NMA showed that, (1) relative to 3DCRT group, BBT group [HR = 0.994, 95% CI=(0.969, 1.026)], ISBT group [HR = 1.005, 95% CI=(0.981,1.033)], IORT group (HR = 0.998, 95% CI=(0.973, 1.026)]; (2) relative to BBT group, ISBT group [HR = 1.012, 95% CI=(0.991, 1.027)] and IORT group [HR = 1.004, 95% CI=(0.984, 1.022)]; (3) relative to ISBT group, IORT group [HR = 0.993, 95% CI=(0.98, 1.007)] found no significant differences. (see in Supplementary Table 7) It is noteworthy that ISBT ranked the first in reducing local recurrence rate among different PBI techniques for early-stage breast cancer after breast-conserving surgery (SUCRA: 73.8%). The second was 3DCRT (SUCRA: 50.8%). IORT (SUCRA: 45.3%) ranked the third, and the fourth was BBT (SUCRA:20.8%). Local recurrence rates were lowest from ISBT, and this intervention had the highest probability of being optimal. (as shown in Fig. 2A and B)

Distant metastasis

The consistency test for distant metastasis yielded an I² of 28%, which was deemed consistent with this outcome measure.

Compared with 3DCRT group, both ISBT group [HR = 0.97, 95% CI=(0.97, 1.02)] and IORT group [HR = 0.97, 95% CI=(0.963, 1.029)] showed no significant difference in reducing distant metastasis, and there was also no significant difference between ISBT and IORT groups [HR = 1, 95% CI=(0.971, 1.03)]. (see in Supplementary Table 8) The results of NMA indicated that among different PBI techniques, 3DCRT ranked the first in reducing distant metastasis (SUCRA:52.5%). The second-ranked method was ISBT (SUCRA:50%), followed by IORT (SUCRA:48.3%). Notably, 3DCRT had the lowest occurrence of distant metastases and thus had the highest probability of being considered optimal. (as shown in Fig. 3A and B).

5-year Overall survival

A test for consistency of 5-year overall survival yielded an I² of 18%, which was considered to be consistent with the outcome measure.

While 5-year overall survival differed among the intervention measures, this difference was not statistically significant: (1) compared with 3DCRT group, BBT group [HR = 1.044, 95% CI=(0.938, 1.163)], ISBT group [HR = 0.977, 95% CI=(0.910, 1.060)], IORT group [HR = 0.968, 95% CI=(0.897, 1.049)]; (2) compared with BBT group, ISBT group [HR = 0.937, 95% CI=(0.863, 1.023)] and IORT group [HR = 0.927, 95% CI=(0.850, 1.014)]; (3) compared with ISBT group, IORT group [HR = 1.011, 95% CI=(0.946, 1.031)]. (see in Supplementary Table 9) IORT has the highest 5-year survival rate (SUCRA: 90%) among different PBI techniques. ISBT (SUCRA: 78.3%) ranked second. The third was 3DCRT (SUCRA: 56.5%). BA (SUCRA:46.7%) ranked the fourth. The expected higher 5-year overall survival rate in IORT was observed, and this intervention had the highest probability of being optimal. (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A,B)

Fibrosis

A test for consistency of the fibrosis measure yielded an I² of 5%, which was considered to be consistent with the outcome measure.

The results of NMA showed that, (1) compared with 3DCRT group, BBT group [HR = 1.2, 95% CI=(0.850, 1.699)], ISBT group [HR = 1.076, 95% CI=(0.867, 1.334)], IORT group [HR = 0.955, 95% CI=(0.725, 1.334)]; (2) compared with BBT group, ISBT group [HR = 0.898, 95% CI=(0.641, 1.251)], IORT group [HR = 0.796, 95% CI=(0.547, 1.156)]; (3) compared with ISBT group, IORT group [HR = 0.888, 95% CI=(0.685, 1.15)] presented no significant differences. Discussion

This study aims to systemically investigate and compare the efficacy and safety of different PBI techniques in the treatment of patients with early-stage breast cancer after breast-conserving surgery. A total of 51 studies consist of 4 different PBI techniques were subsumed, including 7708 patients diagnosed with early-stage breast cancer after breast-conserving surgery, which is a sizable sample size. The summative content analysis results of these data supported the view that ISBT might be the optimal choice for reducing the local recurrence rate, 3DCRT was considered to be the most suitable method to reduce distant metastasis and the 5-year overall survival rate of IORT seemed to be the highest. In terms of safety, ISBT might have the lowest risk of fat necrosis, infection and breast pain. The possibility of fibrosis and hyperpigmentation caused by BBT was minimal. And 3DCRT had the best cosmetic outcome after treatment, moreover, it might also be the optimal choice to reduce telangiectasia.

ISBT

Considering the effectiveness and safety of treatment comprehensively in current practices, ISBT might be the most potential method in PBI techniques after breast-conserving surgery for patients with early-stage breast cancer. In 2018, the American Brachytherapy Association stated that it strongly recommended multi-catheter ISBT as the preferred technique for PBI77,78. The ESTRO-Advisory Committee for Radiation Oncology Practice (ACROP) presented guidelines for multi-catheter ISBT treatment after breast-conserving surgery in breast cancer patients as not inferior and equally effective as WBI. Its first-line recommendation strongly suggested that ISBT should be applied to carefully selected patients with low risk of postoperative recurrence of ipsilateral breast cancer12,79. At present, many literature data support ISBT as the only method with the highest scientific evidence to perform PBI after surgery with longer follow-up than new, emerging radiation techniques, whose effectiveness is proven by over 20 years of experience.

Many pathological studies and clinical data described that 80–90% of ipsilateral breast cancer recurrences could be found in the vicinity of the lumpectomy. However, the absolute number of ipsilateral breast recurrences far from the cancer bed is very low, 3–5%, and not influenced by radiotherapy at all. ISBT has the advantage of delivering high dose levels in the close proximity of the target volume and enables the shape of a reference isodose to be adjusted to the shape of the tumour bed, thus covering the entire tumor bed and greatly reducing the local recurrence with low toxicity rates80. The implantation of the multi-catheter could be performed by guidance with CT or ultrasound during the second surgery under tumor cavity, or implemented intraoperatively during the same operation after the breast cancer has been removed81. Therefore, concentrating the required total dose to the target area of highest risk of recurrence, a lot of normal breast tissue at greater distance from the primary tumor could be spared from side effects, which also could eventually favorably influence long-term physical changes include fat necrosis, chronic breast pain, and infection12,34,82. This modality, however, requires high skills and experience from the radiation oncologist. Wherein, the prediction and monitoring of the risk of surgical site infections has always been a concern in the course of postoperative radiotherapy for breast cancer. Recent studies have found that butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), as a molecular marker abnormally expressed in various cancers, might not only participate in cell proliferation regulation and differentiation during the process of oncogenesis and progression, but was also closely related to the infections83–85. Substantial evidence from the prospective observational research of Verras et al.86 revealed the potential of BChE levels for providing evidence of the increased risk for developing surgical site infections, and thus being interesting targets for future safety studies in the PBI research.

3DCRT

The results of this NMA showed that external beam PBI with 3DCRT technique for early-stage breast cancer patients after breast-conserving surgery had the advantages of reducing the rate of distant metastasis, lower incidence of telangiectasia and good cosmetic result. Nowadays, a large number of studies have shown that 3DCRT, as one of the treatment methods for PBI, has no difference in local recurrence, overall survival, or cancer-free survival compared to WBI at 5 to 10 years87. In 202010, a 7-year phase II experiment was conducted on T1-2N0M0 patients who underwent breast-conserving surgery for PBI based on 3DCRT. The total dose was 36 Gy/9f, with good tolerability and a local recurrence rate of 2.0% within 7 years. Two cases (1.9%) of distant metastases and seven cases (6.7%) of secondary tumors were observed, indicating that 3DCRT is a safe adjuvant treatment method. 3DCRT technique has several potential advantages, such as not requiring invasive surgical procedures and being significantly different in operation and execution from other PBI techniques. It can provide treatment after obtaining complete pathological information, while having lower medical costs. It is evident that 3DCRT has potential for PBI of a selected group of early-stage breast cancer patient. However, issues with setup errors and breathing motions need to be adequately addressed.

BBT

BBT was developed as an alternative to ISBT. The implantation with a balloon may be inserted in the surgical cavity during or after the breast-conserving procedure. The balloon should be carefully adapted to the surgical cavity, considering the distance to the skin surface (at least 7 mm) to ensure adequate coverage and dose homogeneity, as well as to lower the risk of complications. The superficial skin results after BBT seem to be more satisfactory than other PBI techniques because of the relatively strict skin spacing. To achieve such results the proper selection of patients (large breasts, the central location of the primary tumor) is important3,88. According to the results of this NMA, BBT became the option with the lowest observed risk of occurrence among the two outcomes of fibrosis and hyperpigmentation, which may be due to the requirements of balloon implantation procedures. Firstly, the balloon can adapt to the postoperative residual cavity size as much as possible by selecting its optimal size and controlling the amount of fluid injected into the balloon. So the maximum dose exposed to the breast can be concentrated in the surgical cavity to reduce adverse late stage side effects such as tissue fibrosis. Secondly, it is required that the balloon should be kept at a certain distance from the skin and at least larger than the breast size with B-cup, which may be the reason for BBT leading to less skin pigmentation.

However, we do not rule out the possibility that the small sample size included in BBT experiment and the strict criteria for the inclusion of breast cancer patients may have biased the conclusions89. The limitations of the use of BBT, including poor consistency and insufficient skin distance, are major factors that require more research to improve this technique or more clinical trials to determine the long-term efficacy of this treatment.

IORT

IORT offers the benefit of delivering irradiation exactly to the treatment area with little risk of off-target, convenience of implement and shortening of total treatment time by virtue of the application of just one radiotherapy fraction. Some randomized experimental studies have shown that IORT has a probably higher local recurrence rate than WBI90, and the results of two randomized trials showed that although the local recurrence rate was increased, the overall survival rate of the two techniques was similar91. Vaidya et al.92 believe that IORT can be selected as an effective alternative to external irradiation for patients who meet the criteria and have appropriate risks, and IORT has considerable long-term efficacy in terms of non-breast cancer mortality. The results of this NMA analysis have also confirmed the above research conclusions.

Indications for the use of IORT, the current study supports local de-escalation of a single high-dose IORT instead of postoperative radiotherapy in patients over 70 years of age with early-stage tumors of pT1N0 (clinically axillary node-negative) M0. Although this makes IORT occupy a place in the treatment of low-risk patients, it also requires IORT to accurately select patients93–95 ASTRO/ESTRO recommends11 that a full dose of IORT is as effective as standard WBI for patients at low risk of recurrence within the breast. So, some national guidelines make IORT one of the standard techniques for PBI in carefully selected patients. However, the study of Brown et al.96 indicated the limitations of ASTRO guidelines in selecting patients with IORT. Among the 191 patients initially classified as suitable in the experiment, 31% of the patients were classified as high-risk group due to the noticeable characteristics such as lesion size (2.1–3.0 cm), surgical margin stenosis (less than 2 mm), invasive lobular tissue, lymphatic vessel infiltration or extensive intraductal composition. About 15% of patients who were initially suitable for IORT were changed after surgery as unsuitable for IORT alone due to lesions larger than 3.0 cm, positive surgical margins, and positive occult lymph nodes. Although tumor size, grade, various receptor status, and lymph node status are considered in the selection of patients, other factors may also make patients change the suitability of this treatment after surgery, such as the patient’s breast density or body mass index. These influencing factors may ultimately lead to additional whole breast treatment97. It’s worth noting that other limitations of this technique also include the absence of final accurate pathologic information at the time of radiation and the potential risk of late complications related to the administration of single high-dose radiotherapy. Given current data and guidelines, PBI treatment with IORT technique should not be utilized outside of prospective clinical trials at this time.

However, for patients with early-stage breast cancer, the magnitude of technical differences and related risks should be considered when selecting the PBI individualization method. For example, patients with small breast gland volume, insufficient depth of tumor bed, or large target area have relatively high difficulty in receiving brachytherapy; a potential risk of surgical site infection, prolonged surgery time, or other comorbidities may increase the incidence of adverse reactions; differences in patient constitution and compliance could also affect treatment efficacy. Suitable patient selection, elaborately designed plan, and general consensus among radiotherapy treatment teams might be crux to achieving successful outcomes during PBI. What’s even more exciting is that increasing research literatures describing PBI provided considerable positive results in patient satisfaction and other important results. Although it mainly existed in small-scale research or institutional experience, any of the PBI technologies has not been fully studied or proved to bring significant all-round benefits for local control rate, long-term survival and sufficient security after breast-conserving surgery for early-stage breast cancer.

The studies involved treatment outcomes of individual PBI modalities need be sufficiently reported in future research, in order to lifting restrictions for evaluation of subgroup results from currently insufficient data. Because randomized assignment to which PBI technique is not rigorously blinded in previous most clinical practices, treatment assignment might have influenced patient or clinician perceptions of subjective measures such as cosmesis and adverse events. Although the points raised in the above studies require consideration, the main questions about safety and efficacy of PBI can only be answered in the randomized clinical trials.

Strengths and limitations

In this NMA, we compared the outcomes of different PBI techniques in the treatment of early-stage breast cancer, including 51 studies and 7708 patients, which is a adequately large and valid sample size, and this systematic review is based on published research results using various PBI techniques over several decades. Updated and more comprehensive evidence-based recommendations are provided.

Because there were no statistically significant differences in the full assessment of outcome among these PBI techniques, we did not have enough data to draw sufficiently reasonable conclusions. But even with some uncertainties and limitations, our study represents the best evidence on this issue to date. Yet it would be premature to allow the identification of an optimal PBI technique based on the current knowledge. We are unable to provide any answers with absolutely tendency, other than to provide as much reference as possible to the clinical selection of PBI techniques and the possibility of feasible strategies of treatment. Due to the constraint of inclusion and exclusion criteria in this NMA, the diversity of research population was to some extent restricted. Therefore, the results above may not fully report the situation of specific patient groups, especially those with particular age range, other comorbidities, or different ethnicities. Furthermore, probable differences in treatment regimens and outcome evaluation criteria among various studies may also affect the generalizability of these research results from clinical patients with wider range. In addition, as a result of the varying follow-up duration in these studies, continuous long-term efficacy and potential late side effects may not have been fully evaluated.

This review is critical to highlight the need for more extensive and in-depth relevant researches in this area. Undoubtedly, further clinical studies are required to compare different PBI techniques to define if there are any other differences in effectiveness, or more persuasive randomized controlled trials and larger sample analysis to determine the reasonable application of PBI techniques in clinical practice in carefully selected patient groups.

Conclusions

With regard to specific patients with low-risk breast cancer after breast-conserving surgery, PBI is a safe and effective treatment. For individual patients, according to the diversity of tumor-related characteristics, the tradeoff between therapeutic efficacy and the risk of late radiation toxicity is made to select the optimal PBI techniques. Different PBI techniques have their own unique therapeutic advantages under specific and personalized application conditions. It is undeniable that, based on current research results, the performance of ISBT is more prominent. In terms of clinical therapeutic effect, the local recurrent control situation and adverse reactions (at least including fat necrosis, infection and breast pain) of ISBT are no less than or even better than other PBI techniques. In light of this, it is expected to become the main PBI treatment plan of postoperative radiotherapy for strictly screened breast cancer patients. Other PBI techniques consist of BBT, IORT and 3DCRT are also candidate treatment options with certain application value. Above all, more clinical studies of PBI are needed to address the existing problems.
