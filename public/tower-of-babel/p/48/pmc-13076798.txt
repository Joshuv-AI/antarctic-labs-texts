Single-cell chromatin accessibility landscape of cardiac non-myocytes identifies tissue repair program during heart regeneration
Authors: Zihao Chen, Yage Nie, Liying Huang, Ni Zeng, Jixing Gong, Yichen Gao, Qiye Wen, Xiaoyong Chen, Xiaoqian Ji, Yun Li, Tian Lan, Lan Jiang, Jia Wang, Jin Xu, Nan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe restricted regenerative potential of adult hearts poses a significant barrier to effective repair following injury. In contrast to numerous vertebrates, mammalian hearts exhibit only transient neonatal renewal capacity during the initial days of life. Beyond cardiomyocytes, understanding the diverse compositions of non-cardiomyocytes (non-CMs) is imperative for maintaining heart microenvironment homeostasis during neonatal heart regeneration. Here, we conduct single-cell ATAC sequencing on neonatal hearts at varying time points post-apical resection to profile the epigenetic landscape. Intriguingly, fibroblasts and endothelial cells, as the most abundant populations in the heart, exhibit the most dynamic chromatin remodeling upon injury. Furthermore, we reveal CEBPD and AP-1 family transcriptional factors as pivotal trans-regulators orchestrating these alterations, governing beneficial fibroblast activation and endothelial cell angiogenesis crucial for cardiac regeneration, respectively. Collectively, our study delineates the cellular identity of non-CMs at the epigenome level using single-cell approaches, offering insights into cell type-targeted interventions for heart regeneration.Subject terms: Cardiology, Cell biology, Developmental biology, Stem cells

Introduction

The limited regenerative potential of adult hearts presents a significant challenge in the effective repair of heart injuries, such as myocardial infarction (MI), which remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide. In contrast to many vertebrates, the transient renewal capacity observed in newborn mice, which rapidly diminishes within a short timeframe1, underscores the intricate mechanisms underlying cardiac regeneration. Understanding the intrinsic factors that drive the transient regenerative capacity during the neonatal period and the obstacles that impede regeneration in adulthood is of paramount importance.

Upon injury, intricate cellular signaling networks are activated within the heart, involving various resident cell types beyond cardiomyocytes (CMs). These non-cardiomyocyte (non-CM) populations, such as endothelial cells (ECs), cardiac fibroblasts (FBs), epicardial cells, smooth muscle cells (SMCs), pericytes, and immune cells, play essential roles in heart regeneration and reconstitution of the damaged myocardium2. They not only provide a supportive niche for CMs but also actively modulate CM behaviors during heart development and injury3–5. Although the importance of the cellular microenvironment in regulating heart regeneration is well recognized, the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain poorly understood, particularly at the epigenomic level. Although previous studies have primarily explored the transcriptomic heterogeneity and open chromatin landscapes during neonatal mouse heart regeneration, key cell types within the microenvironment that exhibit the most dynamic injury responses remain unclear. Furthermore, these studies used mice at a later developmental stage as a non-regenerative control, thereby not able to make direct timepoint-matched comparisons and distinguish the non-regenerative changes from developmental differences.

Recently, we have reported that sphingosine kinase 2 (SphK2)-mediated sphingolipid metabolism plays an essential role in mammalian cardiac regeneration6. Specifically, the absence of SphK2 entirely abrogates injury-induced regenerative responses in neonatal mouse hearts, resulting in fibrotic healing rather than the scarless regeneration normally occurs. Therefore, we utilized SphK2 knockout (SphK2-/-) mice as a non-renewable model to exclude developmental heterogeneity and employed single-cell Assay for Transposase Accessible Chromatin sequencing (scATAC-seq) to investigate the epigenetic remodeling of major non-CM cell types in neonatal hearts following injury.

Results

Dynamic cellular transformations in non-CMs during heart regeneration

To evaluate the regulatory epigenomic landscapes of individual cells in neonatal hearts undergoing regenerative and non-regenerative processes, we obtained ventricles from wild-type (WT) or SphK2-/- mouse hearts at 3, 7, and 14 days post-apical resection (AR3, AR7, and AR14) surgery performed after birth. These samples were categorized into the regeneration group (ARWT) and non-regeneration group (ARSphK2–/–), respectively (Fig. 1a). Additionally, uninjured WT controls at corresponding timepoints were included as PWT. Single cells were isolated and subjected to analysis using the 10x Genomics Chromium scATAC-seq platform. After rigorous quality control measures, a final set of 57,899 cells was retained for subsequent analyses (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1a–c).

To obtain a comprehensive understanding of the cellular composition of the regenerating mouse heart, we employed unsupervised clustering based on shared-nearest-neighbor analysis and utilized uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) technique. This approach allowed us to identify a total of 21 distinct clusters. By leveraging the chromatin accessibility profiles of known marker genes in individual cells, we successfully annotated seven non-CM cell types, including ECs, FBs, macrophages, lymphocytes (including granulocytes, T cells and B cells), pericytes, epicardial cells, and SMCs (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1d). Quantitative analysis revealed that ECs and FBs were the most abundant non-CM populations (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1e) observed in line with previous research findings7,8.

To investigate the heterogeneity within cell type groups and study the dynamic chromatin remodeling during regeneration in response to injury, we employed UMAP to visualize all cells in each sample. Interestingly, ECs and FBs exhibited the most pronounced chromatin remodeling dynamics and comprised enriched populations of injury-responsive cells that were predominant in AR3WT (Fig. 1c) but not AR7WT (Fig. 1d) or AR14WT (Fig. 1e). In contrast, other cell types displayed a more uniform distribution across all samples. Strikingly, the absence of SphK2 almost completely hindered the appearance of these injury-triggered cell populations in ECs and FBs at AR3 (Fig. 1c), indicating that they may represent cells undergoing dynamic chromatin remodeling and potentially contributing to regeneration. Moreover, clustering analysis confirmed these results by demonstrating that these sub-clusters in ECs and FBs exhibited the highest enrichment scores in AR3WT (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Fig. 1e), thereby reinforcing our findings that ECs and FBs are activated upon injury during the early stages of heart regeneration and likely play crucial roles in coordinating regenerative responses. Thereby, the subsequent analysis of ECs and FBs became the primary focus of this study.

Dynamic open chromatin changes of the FB subpopulations during heart regeneration

FBs have been identified as a key constituent of the cardiac microenvironment, not only regulating postnatal CM development9 but also providing essential paracrine signal molecules and extracellular matrix necessary for neonatal cardiac regeneration10. To explore the underlying dynamics of open chromatin landscapes and regenerative gene regulatory networks of FBs during regeneration, we conducted further analysis on FBs and identified four major subgroups (designated as FB1-FB4) (Fig. 2, and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Notably, FB1 emerged as the predominant population, constituting approximately 85% of all FBs in uninjured hearts at early stages (day 3 and day 7) (Fig. 2b). Differential chromatin accessibility analysis revealed that FB1 exhibited highly accessible chromatin states in signature genes of neonatal FBs (e.g., Cd36, Cav1, Ptn, Dcn and Egfl7) (Supplementary Fig. 2b), representing immature FBs at the early developmental stage11. In contrast, FB2, constituting approximately 97% of the FB population on day 14 but are almost absent at days 3 and 7 (Fig. 2b), displayed increased chromatin accessibility in genes of relatively developed FBs (e.g., Pdgfra, Ddr2, Gfpt2, Mfap5, Tcf21 and Sema3c) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Moreover, FB2 exhibited open chromatin signatures on functional genes of FBs, such as lipase activity and aminoglycan biosynthesis (Fig. 2c), underscoring its mature features. Interestingly, we have identified a specific population of FB3 cells that exhibit an enrichment of numerous chaperone genes, which are typically crucial for the expression and proper folding of secreted proteins (Fig. 2c). Moreover, our findings indicate that FB3 cells display higher expression levels of proline-rich proteins compared to other populations of FB cells (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Given that many profibrotic proteins contain proline-rich regions, such as collagens12, it is likely that FB3 cells play a pivotal role in the synthesis of profibrotic proteins.

Notably, following injury, the proportion of FB1 in WT samples sharply decreased to less than 10% at day 3 post-AR, while FB4 became the predominant FB subtype comprising approximately 80% of AR3 FBs (Fig. 2b). Crucially, this population was essentially non-existent in the non-regenerative SphK2–/– control on AR3. This enabled us to define FB4 as a distinct “regenerative FB” population specific to the successful repair program. To precisely define this subtype, we identified a unique signature of highly accessible genes in FB4, including Thbs1 and Malat1, which are well-established regulators of injury response and tissue remodeling13–16 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Furthermore, FB4 exhibited more open chromatin structure on genes associated with cell-cell adhesion and cell proliferation (e.g., Brinp1, Pim1, Bric8, Il1rapl1, Cdh18, and Grid2) and completely disappeared in the non-regenerative ARSphK2–/– control at day 3 (Fig. 2c, and Supplementary Fig. 2d). It has been shown that FB activation during the acute phase of injury is essential for cardiac repair since the activated FBs ignite a proliferative and pro-angiogenesis program17. Therefore, it is plausible that FB4 cells represent an enriched population of activated FBs involved in dynamic chromatin remodeling during early heart regeneration and may contribute to myocardial repair.

CEBPD governs neonatal FB activation and promotes heart regeneration after injury

To elucidate the key determinants governing FB4 formation, we conducted an analysis of overrepresented transcription factor (TF) binding motifs within the subtype-enriched peaks. Our findings revealed that the CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP) family exhibited a marked preference for enrichment in FB4-specific open chromatin regions (Fig. 2d). Notably, downstream binding sites of the C/EBP family displayed the highest level of chromatin accessibility in the FB4 population (Fig. 2e). Amongst other members of the C/EBP family, Cebpd motif is significantly enriched in FB4-specific open chromatin regions, particularly evident in WT AR3 compared to other conditions (Fig. 2f, g). These observations suggest that CEBPD may regulate the emergence of FB4 during early stages of heart regeneration post-injury.

To further investigate the role of CEBPD in FBs during regeneration, we analyzed its expression using publicly available RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data. Our results indicated that Cebpd was transiently expressed in neonatal hearts 1.5 days post-injury (Fig. 3a). Further examination of CEBPD -binding sites revealed their predominant localization near genes involved in cardiac cell growth and wound healing (Fig. 3b), suggesting a potential role of CEBPD in early FB activation following injury. To validate this hypothesis, we isolated FBs from neonatal mouse hearts, treated them with the profibrotic molecule transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1), and assessed the phenotype of FB activation by measuring the expression of key marker genes with and without short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-mediated knockdown of Cebpd. Our findings demonstrated that TGF-β1 treatment significantly increased the transcription of Cebpd and FB activation-related transcripts, such as Col1a1, Col3a1, and Acta2, in postnatal day 1 (P1) neonatal FBs (Fig. 3c, d). Notably, the upregulation of these genes and their encoded proteins was completely inhibited by Cebpd knockdown (Fig. 3d, e). Consistent with this, the inhibition of TGF-β1-induced FB activation and proliferation by Cebpd knockdown was further confirmed through immunofluorescence staining analysis using alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), a well-characterized marker of activated FBs, and 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation, which indicates cell proliferation (Fig. 3f). In summary, these findings highlight CEBPD as a central driver of neonatal FB activation following injury.

To explore whether CEBPD-mediated FB activation is essential for neonatal heart regeneration, lentiviruses carrying shRNAs targeting Cebpd (shCebpd) or scrambled (shScr) controls were administered to P1 mouse hearts, followed by AR surgery one day later (Fig. 3g). The knockdown efficiency and FB activation were assessed on AR3, while tissue reconstruction and cardiac function were evaluated on AR21 (Fig. 3g). We verified that the lentivirus does not effectively infect CMs, and there was no discernible decrease in Cebpd expression within the CMs themselves after knockdown. This finding rules out the direct effect of the shRNA on the CM lineage (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Moreover, the percentages of proliferative CMs that were EdU+/PCM1+ (Supplementary Fig. 3b), or pH3+/PCM1+ (Supplementary Fig. 3c), were comparable between the shCebpd and shScr control groups. To further eliminate the involvement of epicardial lineages and ensure that the observed phenotypes were driven by FBs, we evaluated Cebpd expression in epicardium-derived cells (EPDCs). Similar to CMs, EPDCs exhibited no significant reduction in Cebpd levels following knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 3a), confirming that our knockdown approach most selectively targets the FB population.

We discovered that the deficiency of Cebpd significantly reduced the overall activation of FBs during the acute stage of cardiac injury, as demonstrated by the decreased staining of α-SMA. In contrast, the expression levels were similar to those of the corresponding control in the uninjured groups (Fig. 3h, i). This reduction in FB activation led to impaired recovery of cardiac systolic function during the subsequent regenerative period, whereas the baseline cardiac function remained unaltered in the absence of injury (Fig. 3j–l). Moreover, an increased scar area indicated impaired myocardial tissue regeneration in Cebpd-depleted hearts (Fig. 3m).

Next, we further characterized the role of CEBPD in the FB4 population. Subsequently, we conducted co-immunofluorescence staining for Thrombospondin-1 (TSP1, encoded by Thbs1, a specific FB4-enriched gene) and α-SMA. To assess the relevance of this population to regeneration, we compared P2 (regenerative) and P8 (non - regenerative) hearts at 3 days post-AR. The abundance of TSP1+/α-SMA+ activated FBs was significantly induced only in P2-AR3 hearts, rather than in P8-AR3 hearts and uninjured controls (Supplementary Fig. 3d), which suggests that TSP1+ FBs represent a regeneration-specific cell state. Crucially, knockdown of Cebpd significantly reduced the abundance of these TSP1+/α-SMA+ FBs after injury (Supplementary Fig. 3e), further validating that CEBPD is essential for maintaining this activated pro-regenerative subpopulation.

Collectively, these findings indicate that CEBPD plays a vital role in the early post-injury activation of FBs, which is indispensable for successful heart regeneration. Moreover, the functional impairment observed in our regeneration models is a specific outcome of the failed injury response rather than a baseline developmental defect.

Regulatory landscapes of ECs at the single cell resolution during heart regeneration

As the predominant non-CM population within the myocardium, the orchestration of angiogenesis and EC functions in capillary growth, migration, and vessel lumen organization holds a central role in cardiovascular dynamics18. ECs contribute to coronary artery formation that is essential for cardiac regeneration19. However, divergence between vascular and microvascular EC signatures, alongside the imperative role of newly synthesized vessels across a spectrum of sizes, is underscored in the context of complete regeneration post-infarction19. Therefore, we employed an UMAP-based clustering approach to categorize five distinct subgroups of ECs denoted as EC1-EC5 (Fig. 4a). Each clusters exhibited elevated accessibility of subtype-specific marker genes, validating their heterogeneity identity (Supplementary Fig. 3a).

To delineate the endothelial subtypes, capillary-specific EC markers such as Car4 and Fabp4, which are enriched in EC1-EC4, were compared with non-capillary EC5. EC5 is characterized by increased chromatin accessibility of venous and arterial genes, notably Car4 and Mgp20 (Fig. 4b). Consequently, our subsequent investigation will focus on capillary ECs (EC1-EC4) and their potential contributions to heart regeneration. Among these subtypes, EC1 is the predominant population at day 14 (Fig. 4c), characterized by a high open chromatin structure in TGF-β pathway genes, such as Tgfb1 (Fig. 4d, e), indicative of a relatively mature EC phenotype. In contrast, EC2 is the most prevalent subtype at both day 3 and day 7 (Fig. 4c). Notably, EC2 experiences a significant decline to less than 10% in AR3WT samples post-injury, subsequently being replaced by EC3, an injury-induced population that constitutes approximately 75% of the AR3 ECs (Fig. 4c). EC3 exhibits enriched regulatory elements associated with genes involved in cell-cell adhesion and cell junction assembly (Fig. 4d, e), processes critical for vascular development and vessel formation following injury21. This indicates a potential regenerative function of EC3, characterized by dynamic chromatin remodeling in the reconstitution of the vascular network within the injured heart. Additionally, the EC4 population is distinguished by increased chromatin accessibility of genes associated with de novo protein folding, such as Hspa8 and Hspa1a (Fig. 4d, e), highlighting their role in modulating the extracellular environment of the heart.

To identify potential upstream regulators of the injury response in the EC3 population during regeneration, we conducted a comprehensive profiling of TF binding motifs within the open chromatin regions associated with EC3-specific peaks. Although motif-enrichment analyses across all EC subtypes were constrained by the transient nature of EC3, a comparative analysis between EC1 and EC3 populations revealed a shared set of TFs with distinct binding preferences (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Consequently, a direct comparison between EC3 and non-EC3 populations (EC1, EC2 and EC4) was performed, uncovering differential chromatin accessibility patterns. The nearest genes to these cis-regulatory elements are likely to play crucial roles in regulating vasculature development and angiogenesis, which are essential processes for heart regeneration (Fig. 4f). Furthermore, motif analysis of EC3-specific peaks reveals a significant enrichment in cis-regulatory elements associated with EC3. Notably, motifs for AP-1 family TFs, such as Fra2 and Jun, are prominently represented (Fig. 4g). These findings suggest that AP-1 signaling undergoes transient activation in response to cardiac injury, playing a crucial role in the repair process among ECs.

AP-1 inactivation impaired post-injury angiogenesis and led to regenerative failure in neonatal mouse hearts

Previous studies have demonstrated that the activation of AP-1 family TFs in response to injury initiates downstream regulatory networks that promote CM dedifferentiation and proliferation in the zebrafish heart22. Conversely, recent studies have demonstrated that AP-1 can facilitate maturation in neonatal rat CMs, indicating context-dependent and species-specific roles for AP-1 in cardiac development23. The dynamic role of AP-1 in various pathways across different conditions and species prompted us to investigate its potential in re-establishing EC homeostasis during heart regeneration.

To this end, we initially treated human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) with T-5224, a specific inhibitor of the DNA-binding activity of AP-1 family TFs24. Recognizing revascularization as a hallmark of complete regeneration post-infarction19 and considering ECs as the primary source for vessel formation, our initial evaluation examined whether AP-1 inhibition influences EC proliferation. The results demonstrated that T-5224 treatment significantly impairs HUVEC proliferation, highlighting its critical role in the early stages of vascular reconstruction in the heart (Fig. 5a). Consistent with this finding, a reduced percentage of mitosis marker phosphorylated histone 3 (pH3)-positive HUVECs, along with a significant decrease in EdU incorporation and Ki67-positive cells in the T-5224-treated group compared to the control group, suggests that AP-1 TFs regulate EC proliferation in vitro (Fig. 5b). Additionally, we found that AP-1 inhibition markedly impaired key aspects of normal EC functions25, including reduced migration (Fig. 5c) and diminished tube formation (Fig. 5d). Collectively, these findings highlight the critical roles of AP-1 in maintaining EC growth and activation in vitro.

To elucidate the specific role of AP-1 in ECs during in vivo heart regeneration, we conducted sham or AR on neonatal hearts and administered T-5224 via intraperitoneal injection for seven consecutive days to inhibit AP-1 activity (Fig. 5e). Validation of this pharmacological approach in ECs sorted via Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) confirmed the substantial downregulation of crucial AP-1 target genes, including Icam1, Stim1, and Traf226–28, verifying effective inhibition in the endothelial compartment (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Notably, at three weeks post-injury, AP-1 inhibition significantly compromised cardiac regeneration. Echocardiographic analysis revealed that T-5224 treatment led to diminished cardiac systolic function, characterized by reduced ejection fraction and fractional shortening compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 5f–h). Histological assessment via Masson’s trichrome staining revealed that AP-1 inhibition exacerbated fibrotic scarring (Fig. 5i). Simultaneously, angiogenesis was significantly impaired. Both Microfil vascular casting and CD31 immunostaining indicated a notable reduction in vascular density within the regenerative area of T-5224-treated hearts (Fig. 5j, k). Significantly, we excluded developmental defects as the primary causes of this phenotype, as T-5224 treatment did not alter cardiac function or vascular structure in uninjured sham controls (Fig. 5f–h, k). Furthermore, the inhibition of AP-1 did not exert an impact on the basal proliferation of CMs (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c), indicating that the observed regenerative failure is predominantly associated with impaired angiogenesis. Collectively, these findings indicate that AP-1 activity is essential for maintaining EC homeostasis and facilitating vessel reconstruction following injury, which are critical processes for complete heart regeneration.

Discussion

Our understanding of heart regeneration within the microenvironment remains limited, hindered by challenges such as the complexity of cellular types, inter-cellular and intra-subtype differences, and cell type- and subtype-specific functions. To address these limitations, this study utilized scATAC-seq to investigate the dynamics of chromatin accessibility in major non-CM populations. By integrating data from multiple time points (P3, P7, and P14) in both WT and SphK2-/- mice, which serve as a non-regenerative control, we explored previously uncharted territories of chromatin accessibility and potential regulatory elements governing cardiac regeneration. This comprehensive analysis enabled us to elucidate the relationship between cis- and trans-regulatory elements and gene expression, offering valuable insights into the shaping of cell identity during post-injury heart repair. Our analysis of cellular heterogeneity has unveiled previously unrecognized contributions of specific non-CM subtypes to heart regeneration, with particular emphasis on FBs and ECs, which exhibit the most dynamic changes in chromatin accessibility following cardiac injury. Within these cell types, regeneration-specific enriched subtypes (FB4 and EC3) emerged, notably prevalent in the cell population derived from AR3WT. By deciphering the open chromatin landscape of these cells, we have identified enriched TFs and elucidated their critical roles in promoting heart regeneration. Importantly, our strategy meticulously accounted for potential developmental influences, ensuring robust comparisons with other datasets and validating our findings.

Cardiac fibrosis resulting from FB activation is widely recognized as a significant barrier to heart regeneration. However, during the acute phase following injury, precise modulation of FB activation plays a crucial role in tissue repair29. Notably, despite being exposed to the same paracrine environment, neonatal and adult FBs exhibit distinct responses to injury-induced signals. In neonates, myocardial injury triggers a transient and moderate FB activation characterized by limited proliferation and collagen synthesis that contributes to scar formation. Conversely, this response diminishes in juvenile and adult stages, where FBs exhibit robust proliferation leading to the formation of fibrotic scars. The underlying mechanisms that enable neonatal and elder FBs to translate identical signaling inputs into distinct responses, thereby facilitating coordinated scarless regeneration in the neonates, remain an unresolved mystery in the field. By comparing chromatin remodeling during FB activation in both regenerative and non-regenerative hearts, we identified the C/EBP family of TFs, particularly enriched in FB4 (the regeneration-associated subgroup of FBs), as potential regulators linked to the regenerative FB response. The transient activation of CEBPD in neonatal FBs is critical for promoting regenerative healing without inducing pathological fibrosis. This finding corroborates previous studies in the epicardium, which have established a functional link between C/EBP family activation and neutrophil infiltration, leading to improved cardiac function30.

Complete heart regeneration necessitates the effective activation of the angiogenesis program in endogenous ECs to restore the vascular network following injury31. However, the molecular characteristics of regenerating ECs and the key regulators governing their formation during neonatal cardiac regeneration remain largely unexplored. This study identifies an injury-induced EC3 subpopulation uniquely present in regenerating hearts and elucidates the regulatory element landscape of these cells. Our findings reveal a significant enrichment of AP-1 family TFs in EC3-active open chromatin regions compared to other EC subsets. The AP-1 family exhibits a wide range of functions, from facilitating CM maturation in neonatal rat23 to inducing CM dedifferentiation, proliferation, and protrusion in zebrafish22. However, the role of AP-1 in ECs during heart regeneration remains underexplored. Our study builds on these findings, elucidating critical roles of AP-1 in promoting angiogenesis and restoring EC homeostasis in vivo that is indispensable for successful heart regeneration. Further research is warranted to identify the specific AP-1 members that contribute to regenerative EC remodeling, dissect the underlying regulatory mechanisms and target genes, and determine whether ectopic activation of the essential AP-1 member in adult ECs can promote cardiac regeneration following myocardial injury.

In summary, our study constitutes a comprehensive and in-depth investigation into cellular identity within cardiac non-CM populations at the single-cell level, mitigating potential biases arising from developmental heterogeneity. By characterizing the open chromatin landscapes across various post-injury stages in both regenerative and non-regenerative hearts, our findings not only elucidate the cellular composition and cis-regulatory mechanisms that underpin neonatal heart regeneration but also underscore the pivotal roles of CEBPD and AP-1 family trans-regulators in modulating FBs and ECs, respectively, in response to myocardial injury. Manipulation of these key regulators create a conducive regenerative microenvironment to enhance heart regeneration outcomes and may offer promising strategies for improving cardiac repair following MI.

Experimental animals

All husbandry and experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Research Committee of Sun Yat-Sen University and performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines on the Care and Use of Animals. The Sphingosine kinase 2 knockout (SphK2–/–) mice (JAX 019140) mice were acquired from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Sun Yat-Sen University. Mice were housed two to five animals per cage with a 12-h light/dark cycle at constant temperature (23 °C) and humidity (~50%) with ad-libitum access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Chinese Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals and the international ARRIVE guidelines, and were approved by the Sun Yat-sen University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (SYSU-IACUC) (Approval No. SYSU-IACUC-20223001342). For euthanasia, adult mice were subjected to cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia, while neonatal mice were euthanized by decapitation. These methods were chosen to minimize suffering and were conducted in strict adherence to the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines for reporting animal research.

Cell culture

All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 20% O2 and 5% CO2. Cell culture reagents were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific unless otherwise specified. Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) (cc-3162) were purchased from Lonza and cultured in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (EGMTM-2, cc-3162, Lonza) and passaged to new dishes using Accutase upon reaching 80%-90% confluence. T-5224 (530141-72-1, TargetMol) was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 5 mM stock solution. Subsequently, this stock solution was diluted in EGMTM-2 to achieve a final concentration of 15 µM. In functional assays, HUVECs were treated with 15 µM T-5224. The treatment duration was 9 h for the migration assay, 8 h for the tube formation assay, and 14 days for the clonal expansion assay.

T-5224 intraperitoneal injection in neonatal mice

For in vivo delivery, T-5224 was formulated in a 15% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) (9003-39-8, TargetMol) vehicle at a concentration of 2 mg mL⁻¹ and subsequently diluted to half of its original concentration with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Neonatal C57BL/6 J wild-type mice were randomly assigned to either a vehicle-control group or a T-5224 treatment group. Beginning on postnatal day 0, mice in the treatment group were administered T-5224 via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 20 mg per kg body weight per day. Control mice received an equivalent volume of the 15% PVP vehicle solution. This dosing regimen was continued once daily for seven consecutive days.

Lentivirus production and delivery

Complementary shRNA oligonucleotides targeting the Cebpd transcript were annealed and directionally cloned into the pLKO.1-blast (26655, Addgene) vector. For lentivirus production, HEK293T cells at 80% confluence were cultured in 10 cm dishes and transfected at approximately 90% confluency using LipofectamineTM 2000 (11668019, Thermo Fisher) with a plasmid mixture containing 16 μg lentiviral vector, 4 μg envelope plasmid (pMD2.G, 12259, Addgene), and 12.5 μg packaging plasmid (psPAX2, 12260, Addgene). Viral supernatants were collected twice at 48 h and 72 h post-transfection, pooled, and concentrated through PEG-8000 (25322-68-3, Solarbio) precipitation. Virus titer was determined by a Lentivirus Titration Kit (BF06202-10, Biodragon). Viruses were directly injected into the cardiac ventricles using an insulin syringe, the infectious unit (IFU) was 1 × 108 mL–1. Scramble control shRNA: CAACAAGATGAAGAGCACCAA

Neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes (NMCMs) and cardiac fibroblasts (NMCFs) isolation

NMCMs and NMCFs were isolated from the hearts of 1–3 days-old C57BL/6 J mice using an established protocol modified from a previous study32. NMCFs were passaged at a ratio of 1:2 until they reached approximately 90% confluence. Culture media were refreshed every 48 h, with first- and second-passage cells utilized for experiments.

Isolation of murine epicardial cells

Epicardial cells were isolated from hearts harvested three days post apical resection as described previously33. Under stereomicroscopic guidance, the epicardial layer was mechanically dissected from the ventricular surface. The collected tissue was minced and subjected to sequential enzymatic digestion with trypsin-EDTA in a 37 °C water bath with gentle agitation. Dissociated cells from multiple digestion rounds were pooled, neutralized with serum-containing medium, and passed through a 100-µm cell strainer. The resulting single-cell suspension was centrifuged to obtain a cell pellet, which was processed for total RNA isolation.

Cardiac endothelial cells isolation

Mouse hearts were excised, perfused with PBS to remove residual blood, and the pericardium was carefully removed. The cardiac tissue was then minced into fragments of less than 1 mm³ using fine scissors. Tissue dissociation was performed by incubation in a solution of collagenase IV (400 U mL⁻¹) (40510ES76, Yeasen), dispase (1.2 U mL⁻¹) (HY-131577, MCE), and DNase I (32 U mL⁻¹) (LS002138, Worthington) in PBS supplemented with Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺. The digestion was carried out at 37 °C for 60 min, with the suspension being triturated every 10–15 min. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by adding 1 mL of Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 20 mL of ice-cold PBS. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 40-μm cell strainer (BS-40-CS, Biosharp) and washed with 20 mL of cold PBS. The cells were then pelleted by centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 5 min, resuspended in 10 mL of 3% FBS, and centrifuged again under the same conditions. Subsequently, the cell pellet was resuspended and blocked with 3% BSA on ice for 10 min. Cells were incubated with APC-conjugated anti-CD31 (1:100, 551262, BD Pharmingen) and PE-conjugated anti-CD45 (1:100, 567111, BD Pharmingen) antibodies for 45 min at 4 °C, with gentle mixing every 10 min. Following incubation, the cells were washed with 1 mL of 3% FBS and centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in 3% FBS for subsequent fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS).

Single cell preparation and nuclei isolation

Isolated hearts were washed with PBS twice, and then collected in 1.5 mL EP tubes. Tissues were minced into pieces (~1 mm) on ice using scissors and digested into single-cell suspensions with 1 mg mL–1 type II collagenase (17101015, Gibco) and 1 mg mL-1 type IV collagenase (17104019, Gibco) at 37 °C for 30 min with intermittent agitation. Dissociated cells were filtered through a 70 μm strainer (15-1070, Biologix), centrifuged at 300 rcf for 5 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in 1 mL ice-cold PBS containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). After filtration through a 40 μm strainer (BS-40-CS, Biosharp), cells were washed twice under identical centrifugation conditions.

For nuclei isolation, 500,000 cells were resuspended in 100 μL chilled lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% NP40, 0.1% Tween-20, 0.01% digitonin and 1% BSA) and pipetted gently 10 times. Following 5 min of incubation on ice, 1 mL chilled Wash Buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Tween-20 and 1% BSA) was added to the lysed cells. The mixture was pipette-mixed five times and then centrifuged at 300 rcf for 5 min at 4 °C. Based on number of cells used for isolation and assuming ~50% nuclei loss during cell lysis, isolated nuclei were resuspended in chilled Diluted Nuclei Buffer (PN-2000153, 10x Genomics). If cell debris and large clumps were observed, pass through a cell strainer. For low volumes, a 40 µm Flowmi Cell Strainer (H13680-0040, Bel-Art) was used to minimize volume loss.

scATAC-seq libraries preparation and sequencing

Single-cell Assay for Transposase Accessible Chromatin (scATAC) library was prepared using the 10x Genomics platform with the Chromium Single Cell ATAC Library & Gel Bead Kit (PN-1000110, 10x Genomics) as instructed by the manufacturer. A total of 15,000 nuclei per sample were used as input for single-cell ATAC-seq following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after tagmentation, the cells were loaded on a Chromium Controller Single-Cell instrument to generate single-cell Gel Bead-In-Emulsions (GEMs) followed by linear polymerase chain reaction (PCR) as described in the 10x Genomics scATAC-seq protocol using a Veriti 96-well thermal cycler (1851197, Bio-Rad). After breaking the GEMs, the barcoded tagmented DNA was purified with SPRIselect Reagent Kit (B23318, Beckman Coulter) and further amplified to enable sample indexing and enrichment of scATAC-seq libraries. Final libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500 sequencer.

Preprocessing of scATAC-seq data

The Cell Ranger ATAC Software34 (version 1.2.0) was used for initial processing of scATAC-seq data. The ‘cellranger-atac count’ pipeline was employed to perform read filtering, alignment, peak calling, and count matrix generation for each sample from fastq files. Reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome mm10 (refdata-cellranger-atac-mm10-1.1.0) provided by 10x Genomics. The gene-barcode matrix was generated using only confidently mapped reads with valid barcodes and unique molecular identifiers.

Cell quality control was performed using the ArchR35 R package (version 1.0.1). This workflow was adapted from the ArchR manuscript (https://www.archrproject.com/index.html). Doublets were identified using ‘addDoubletScores (ArrowFiles, k = 10, knnMethod = “UMAP”, LSIMethod=1, dimsToUse=1:8)’ and removed using ‘filterDoublets(filterRatio = 1)’. For dimensionality reduction and correction of batch effect, the ‘addIterativeLSI(iterations = 2, varFeatures = 25,000, dimsToUse = 1:8)’ was applied.

Cell clustering and annotation for scATAC-seq data

Cell clusters were identified using the addClusters() function in ArchR. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was performed using the addUMAP() function to visualize the chromatin profiles in a two-dimensional space. Cell type identification was annotated based on prior knowledge of cell type-specific marker genes (described in Supplementary Fig. 1b), including fibroblasts (FBs), endothelial cells (ECs), macrophages, smooth muscle cells, lymphocyte, epicardial cells, pericytes and glial cells. A gene score matrix was generated to predict gene expression levels based on the accessibility of regulatory elements near each gene. Cell type-specific marker genes were identified and visualized using the getMarkerFeatures() and markerHeatmap() functions in ArchR. Clusters composed of a small number of cells lacking enrichment for any positive marker genes were removed, as they likely represented cell-cell multiplets.

Open chromatin peaks specific to each cell cluster were identified using the addReproduciblePeakSet() function with a cut-off value of 0.01, followed by the getMarkerFeatures() function. Transcription factor (TF) motif enrichment analysis was conducted using the addMotifAnnotations() function.

Subpopulation analysis

For subpopulation analysis, fibroblast cells were extracted based on their primary annotation, and subclusters were identified using the addClusters() function in ArchR. This analysis resulted in the identification of four fibroblast sub-populations designated as FB1, FB2, FB3 and FB4. Subsequently, differential peak analysis and motif enrichment analysis were performed using the same approach as in the cell-type specific analysis. Additionally, TF deviation scores were calculated using the addDeviationsMatrix() function. The same analytical approach was applied to endothelial cells, leading to the identification of five distinct EC subpopulations.

Functional and regulatory annotation of scATAC-seq data

Differential peaks were identified using the ArchR pipeline, with a focus on the comparison between EC3 and other EC subpopulations. These differential peaks were extracted for downstream functional and regulatory annotation. MA plots were generated to visualize the distribution of differential peaks using the ggplot236 R package (version 3.4.2). Additionally, motif enrichment results generated by ArchR were visualized using ggplot2 to highlight transcription factor binding motifs that were significantly enriched in the differential peaks. For functional annotation, clusterProfiler (version 4.2.2) and ChIPseeker (version 1.38.0)37,38 R packages were used. Differential peaks were mapped to their nearest genes, and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed, to ensure statistical significance, a q-value cut-off of ≤ 0.05 was applied.

Immunofluorescence staining

Heart tissues were harvested from the anesthetized mice, rinsed with cold PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight. The tissues were then dehydrated using 30% sucrose, embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (4583, Sakura), and stored at –20 °C. The hearts were sectioned vertically into 7 μm slices and further processed for immunofluorescence staining. Tissue sections and cultured cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (MA0192-1, Meilunbio) for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (9036-19-5, Sigma) in PBS for 15 min and blocked in 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies and secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies used in this study include Phospho-Histone H3 (Ser10) (pH3) (1:200, 9706S, Cell Signaling Technology), Ki67 (1:200, ab16667, Abcam), Vimentin (1:200, 10366-1-AP, Proteintech), alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (1:200, BM0002, Boster Bio), CD31 (1:200, ab28364, Abcam), Thrombospondin 1 (TSP1) (1:200, F1912, Selleck), PCM1 (1:200, HPA023374, Atlas antibodies). After washing with PBS, the samples were incubated with species-matched isotype secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, protected from light. Secondary antibodies included Alexa555-conjugated goat antirabbit (1:500, Invitrogen, A21428), Alexa488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG2a (1:500, Invitrogen, A21131), Alexa488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG1 (1:500, Invitrogen, A21121) and Alexa647-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG1 (1:500, Invitrogen, A21240). Nuclei were counterstained with 1 mg mL⁻¹ DAPI (C1002, Beyotime) for 10 min at room temperature. Samples were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent (P36930, Thermo Fisher). Images were capture by Operetta CLS high-content imaging system (PerkinElmer Life).

EdU (5-ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine) Incorporation assay

Proliferating cells were labeled with EdU under distinct conditions in vitro and in vivo. In cell culture, a 3-h pulse with 20 µM EdU was applied immediately preceding fixation. In neonatal mice, a single dose of EdU (20 mg per kg body weight) was delivered via intraperitoneal injection 24 h prior to sampling. Detection of the incorporated EdU was carried out on fixed cells and heart cryosections using the commercial Click-iT EdU Imaging Kit (C0081, Beyotime), in strict adherence to the provided protocol. A final counterstaining was performed with 1 mg mL⁻¹ DAPI (C1002, Beyotime) for 10 min at room temperature. Image acquisition was conducted on confocal laser scanning microscopes, with cultured cells documented on a Zeiss LSM 780 platform and tissue sections visualized using a Nikon C2 system.

Public RNA-seq data analysis

Public data (GSE123868) were downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), and Counts Per Million (CPM) values were used for this study. The visualization of bar plots was performed by GraphPad Prism 8.0 (https://www.graphpad.com/).

Western blot analysis

Cultured cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (9806S, Cell Signaling Technology) containing proteinase inhibitors (C0001, TargetMol) on ice. Proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred to a PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight with gentle shaking. Primary antibodies included COL1A1 (1:1000, PA5-29569, Thermo Fisher), COL3A1 (1:1000, 22734-1-AP, Proteintech) and α-SMA (1:1000, BM0002, BosterBio) and GAPDH (1:5000, AF0006, Beyotime). The membrane was then incubated with isotype-matched secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Bio-Rad) and imaged using a ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad). Protein abundance was quantified using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/).

Clonal expansion assay

HUVECs (3000 cells per well) were seeded and maintained in EGMTM-2 medium for approximately 14 days. Subsequently, cells were fixed and stained with 0.2% crystal violet. Relative cell numbers were quantified using ImageJ software and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Cell migration assay

HUVEC migration was assessed using a wound healing assay. HUVECs were plated in 96-well plates and cultured to 100% confluence. A linear scratch was made, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 9 h. Time-lapse images were captured at 0, 3, 6, and 9 h using a Leica DMi8 microscope. The migration area was quantified using ImageJ software.

Tube formation analysis

HUVECs were resuspended in EGMTM-2 and seeded onto 24-well plates precoated with Matrigel at a density of 6 × 104 cells per well, with triplicate repeats. After 8 h of incubation, images were captured using a Leica DMi8 microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Neonatal mouse heart apex resection (AR) and tissue sampling

Apical resection in neonatal mice was performed as previously described39. Neonatal mice were anesthetized by ice exposure for 3–5 min. A precise incision was then created between the third and fourth intercostal spaces, facilitating the exteriorization of the heart through gentle pressure application. The apex of the left ventricle was precisely excised using iridectomy scissors. The subsequent closure of the thoracic and dermal incisions was meticulously performed with 7–0 silk suture. Postoperatively, the mice were placed on a 37 °C warming pad to ensure thermoregulation and promote recovery. Sham controls underwent the same procedure without apex resection.

To ensure consistent capture of the regenerative border zone for scATAC-seq, tissue sampling was standardized using the resection plane as a reference landmark. A cylindrical tissue block, measuring approximately 1.0 mm in diameter and extending 0.8 mm proximally from the incision line towards the heart base, was dissected. This sampling strategy was designed to encompass both the injury site and the immediate border zone. For Sham controls, a spatially matched volume of apical tissue was harvested.

Cardiac function assessment by echocardiography

Mice were anesthetized with 1–1.5% isoflurane. Cardiac function and morphology were assessed using a Vevo 3100 high-resolution imaging system (FUJIFILM Visual Sonics) with a high-frequency transducer (MS550). M-mode imaging was used to measure left ventricular end-systolic anterior wall thickness, ejection fraction, and fractional shortening using VevoLAB software (version 3.0, FUJIFILM Visual Sonics).

Histological examination

Myocardial fibrosis was assessed histologically. Briefly, excised hearts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, processed, and embedded in paraffin. Consecutive 5 μm-thick sections were prepared, deparaffinized through a graded alcohol series, and stained with Masson’s trichrome (G1006, Servicebio) according to a previously published method6, which differentially colors collagen fibers blue and muscle fibers red. Digital images of the left ventricle were analyzed in a double-blinded fashion using ImageJ software. The fibrotic area was quantified and expressed as a percentage of the total cross-sectional area of the left ventricle.

Microfil injection

A heparin/PBS solution (diluted 1:10) was administered via intraperitoneal injection. After a 5-min interval, the mice were euthanized. Hearts were rapidly excised and retrogradely perfused via the aorta with a non-recirculating Tyrode’s buffer (145 mM NaCl, 5.9 mM KCl, 1.1 mM CaCl₂, 1.2 mM MgCl₂, 11 mM glucose, 5 mM HEPES; pH 7.4) at a constant flow rate of 2.5 mL min⁻¹ and maintained at 37 °C. After a 5-min stabilization period, the hearts were perfused for 5 min with Tyrode’s buffer containing 5 mM adenosine (HY-B0228, MCE) to induce maximal vasodilation, followed by a 2-min perfusion with vessel-stasis solution (110.0 mM NaCl, 10.0 mM NaHCO₃, 16.0 mM KCl, 16.0 mM MgCl₂, 1.2 mM CaCl₂; pH 7.8). Coronary vasculature was then infused with 1 mL of Microfil compound (MV-122, Flow Tech) using a slow retrograde injection technique, performed according to manufacturer specifications and established methodology40. Specimens were cryopreserved at –20 °C for 2 h to facilitate polymer cross-linking, then fixed in 4% formaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h. Extensive PBS washing was performed post-fixation before further processing.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t test, while one-way ANOVA with post hoc analysis or two-way ANOVA was used for comparisons involving three or more groups. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with significance levels indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. All representative images or results were obtained from at least three independent experiments.
