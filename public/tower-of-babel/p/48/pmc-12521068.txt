A Review on the Double Burden of Diabetes and Myocardial Infarction: Healthcare Challenges and Future Prospects in Low and Middle‐Income Countries
Authors: Md. Shajid Hossain Rafi, Sanjida Chowdhury Ivy, Syed Masudur Rahman Dewan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimThe dual burden of diabetes and myocardial infarction (MI) significantly increases morbidity and mortality, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). This study evaluates current treatment strategies, emphasizing the impact of limited access to medications, diagnostics, and specialized care. It proposes adaptable solutions tailored to resource‐constrained settings.MethodFor this review study, we investigated many databases—including PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library—using different relevant terms.DiscussionLow public awareness and healthcare limitations often delay diagnosis and treatment, leading to poor outcomes. While comprehensive care, including medication, lifestyle modification, and education, is ideal, it remains out of reach for many LMICs due to systemic barriers.ConclusionImproving chronic disease outcomes in LMICs requires targeted investment in healthcare infrastructure, patient education, and scalable interventions. Community‐based care, digital tools, and task‐shifting models show strong potential for enhancing disease management.

Background

Diabetes is a metabolic disorder that involves chronic high blood sugar levels and varying degrees of impairment in the metabolism of carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins [1, 2]. In 2021, the global population of individuals with diabetes was 529 million, and the standardized diabetes prevalence worldwide was 6.1% (range 5.8–6.5). It is projected that by 2050, there will be over 1.31 billion (range 1.22–1.39) people with diabetes [3]. Furthermore, diabetes presents a significant risk for the onset of cardiovascular disease, which ultimately becomes the leading cause of death among individuals with diabetes [4]. As diabetes progresses, the risk of developing macrovascular complications, such as coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease, and carotid artery disease, increases in combination with the microvascular complications associated with the condition [5]. “Heart attack,” commonly known as myocardial infarction, occurs when blood flow to part of the myocardium is reduced or stopped. Myocardial infarction can occur without symptoms and remain unnoticed, or it can be a severe event causing sudden deterioration in the body's blood circulation and potential death [6].

Low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) are defined according to the World Bank classifications. The World Bank classifies countries into four income groups based on their Gross National Income (GNI) per capita: 1.Low‐income countries (LICs): Countries with a GNI per capita of $1045 or less.2.LMCs: Countries with a GNI per capita between $1046 and $4095.3.Upper‐middle‐income countries (UMCs): Countries with a GNI per capita between $4096 and $12,695.4.High‐income countries (HICs): Countries with a GNI per capita of $12,696 or more.

This classification is based on their economic development and provides a framework to understand the unique challenges faced by LMICs. Specifically, low‐income and lower‐middle‐income countries are often characterized by limited healthcare infrastructure, lower financial resources, and a higher burden of infectious diseases and non‐communicable diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular conditions [7].

Currently, LMICs are facing a dual burden of infectious (communicable) and non‐communicable diseases due to an ongoing epidemiological transition [8]. Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) account for 41 million deaths worldwide each year. LMICs bear 77% of these NCD‐related deaths [9]. The prevalence of diabetes is rising worldwide, with 80% of individuals with diabetes residing in LMICs, and approximately half of them being undiagnosed [10]. Cardiovascular disease is significantly more common in individuals with diabetes, with a 2–4‐fold increase in prevalence. The presence of diabetes raises the likelihood of myocardial infarction, and when these two conditions occur together, it significantly deteriorates patient results [11]. The mortality rates of diabetic individuals who have experienced acute myocardial infarction (AMI) are still 1.5–2 times greater than those of nondiabetic individuals [12]. A recent study indicates that individuals with diabetes in LMICs have significantly higher rates of cardiovascular disease, all‐cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality compared to individuals in high‐income countries [13].

In LMICs, healthcare systems often have trouble because they lack enough resources, their infrastructure is not stable, and there is a lack of skilled healthcare workers. The objective of our study is to figure out how these resource‐poor systems are connected to chronic diseases like diabetes and MI. We carry out the above by looking at the current rates of occurrence, figuring out the most important problems, and thinking about possible, situation‐specific answers.

Methods

This review paper utilized a range of relevant keywords to conduct searches across multiple databases, including Scopus, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library. The terms looked into included “diabetes,” “hyperglycemia,” “myocardial infarction,” “MI,” “management,” “diabetic patients,” “diabetes and MI co‐morbidity,” “MI in LMIC,” “diabetes in LMIC,” “hyperglycemia and MI,” “healthcare strategy,” and “MI in diabetic patients.” This effort did not involve data collection or analysis; instead, it examined and compared findings from various relevant scientific studies concerning the specified illness comorbidities.

Low‐income countries like Sudan and the Syrian Arab Republic have high rates of diabetes, while Burkina Faso has the lowest prevalence. Lower middle‐income countries such as Pakistan and Egypt also show higher rates of diabetes (Table 1). Middle‐income countries with aging populations are expected to see a significant increase (Figure 1) in diabetes prevalence from 2021 to 2045. Overall, there is a wide variation in diabetes prevalence among different income groups and countries, with some nations facing more significant challenges in managing this chronic disease [15, 16].

Prevalence of Diabetes and MI Comorbidities

Diabetes poses a significant healthcare challenge worldwide, with cardiovascular disease being the leading cause of mortality and illness in individuals with diabetes. There is a rising number of people diagnosed with diabetes, and the most substantial growth will occur in low and middle‐income areas [18]. A critical and under‐discussed factor in the interplay between diabetes and cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the role of systemic inflammation. Over‐inflammation plays a pivotal role in both the pathogenesis and progression of CVDs, especially in diabetic patients. Evidence shows that inflammatory pathways not only drive the development of atherosclerosis but also exacerbate acute myocardial injury and post‐infarction complications. In this context, inflammatory markers could serve as valuable tools for diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapy monitoring in both diabetes and CVD management. Evaluating these markers may inform early interventions, guide the use of anti‐inflammatory therapies, and optimize cardiovascular risk reduction strategies in high‐risk populations, especially in resource‐constrained LMICs [19]. The relationship between CVD and sex‐specific factors is becoming more widely acknowledged. Notably, even during the premenopausal stage, which is typically regarded as cardioprotective due to the vascular benefits of estrogen, women may display coronary dysfunction and adverse cardiovascular events [20]. Research indicates that endothelial dysfunction has a role in cardiovascular disorders, such as coronary artery disease, atherosclerosis, and hypertension, which are also marked by insulin resistance [21]. Moreover, diabetes reduces the usual sex‐related advantage in vascular function, causing premenopausal women to lose their relative endothelium protection and display dysfunction comparable to or worse than that of males [22, 23].

Diabetes is frequently seen in patients admitted for an acute coronary syndrome event, and its prevalence is on the rise. Patients hospitalized with acute myocardial infarction may experience higher mortality rates and increased risk of new cardiovascular events if they have diabetes, whether diagnosed or undiagnosed [24, 25]. Data on the combined prevalence of diabetes and MI is not well‐documented. However, studies suggest that around 25%–30% of ACS patients have diabetes, and their prognosis is worse compared to those without diabetes. One study found that 28% of acute MI patients had either known or newly diagnosed diabetes. Pre‐existing diabetes in ACS patients ranges from 19% to 23% in different studies [26]. Between 2025 and 2050, there is an anticipated 194.4% increase in the occurrence of AMI, expected to rise from 482 to 1418 per 100,000 populations. It is projected that there will be a 215.7% increase in the prevalence of type‐2 diabetes mellitus among the population with AMI [27].

Current Treatment Strategies

Treatment Strategies Used in Diabetes

Diabetes treatment and management primarily focus on regulating blood sugar levels, preventing complications, and enhancing overall patient health. There are currently three distinct approaches to monitoring glucose: blood glucose or HbA1c laboratory testing, self‐monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG), and CGM [28]. Common medications for treating type 2 diabetes include metformin, sulfonylureas, SGLT2 inhibitors, and DPP‐4 inhibitors. SGLT2i use was associated with improved outcomes in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), notably by reducing stent restenosis rates post‐AMI, likely due to improved endothelial function and attenuated vascular remodeling [29]. Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion and basal insulin are the primary treatments for most adults with Type 1 diabetes. Advanced cases of Type 2 diabetes also require insulin [30]. Significant lifestyle changes for patients, as well as medication therapies, are critical to disease management effectiveness. These involve changes in physical activity, nutritional adjustments, and enhanced sleep habits [31].

Treatment Strategies Used in MI

Patients with chest pain in the community are a varied group who frequently request assistance from pre‐hospital responders. According to local EMS guidelines, rapid risk evaluation and triage are crucial. If the initial medical provider suspects ACS, they should promptly obtain and analyze a 12‐lead ECG. It is advised that all medical and paramedical staff tending to ACS patients in the EMS environment have access to defibrillation equipment and receive basic cardiac life support training [32]. Upon identification, viable patients with spontaneous circulation should be promptly taken to the cardiac catheterization laboratory of a hospital capable of performing PCI. For patients with AMICS, PCI of the infarct‐related artery is the preferred reperfusion method, regardless of the delay in time [33].

Inflammation is a cause and aggravating element of CVD, as well as a mediator of its most alarming prognostic impact. Increased inflammatory markers including CRP, IL‐1β, IL‐12, IL‐6, and TNF‐α are linked to increased cardiovascular events, plaque instability, and endothelial dysfunction. These markers may assist in early diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic monitoring, thereby supporting their potential use in the clinical management of CVD [20, 34]. The cardioprotective properties of SGLT2 may stem in part from their anti‐inflammatory effects. These agents reduce the infiltration of inflammatory cells into atherosclerotic plaques and limit the oxidized‐LDL accumulation and apoptosis within the arterial wall. This points to their potential role not only in preventing MI but also in reducing recurrence through plaque stabilization (Jia et al., 2020). Post‐acute coronary syndrome, most patients are advised to get dual antiplatelet medication, utilizing ticagrelor or prasugrel in conjunction with low‐dose aspirin, for 12 months. Engagement in secondary prevention is essential following acute coronary syndrome. This includes intensive lipid‐lowering therapy, antithrombotic therapy, neurohormonal agents, and lifestyle modifications, such as participation in cardiac rehabilitation [35].

Treatment Strategies Used in Diabetes and MI Comorbidities

Patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) face a significantly higher risk of mortality during and after acute myocardial infarction (AMI), highlighting the urgent need for improved treatment options for this patient population. It is crucial to pursue and maintain intensive medical treatment, prolonged surveillance, and strict control of other risk factors for as long as possible in these patients [36]. In the critical care setting, intensive glucose management, including during AMI, has been shown to enhance outcomes. The preferred approach in this context involves intravenous insulin infusion, using a validated protocol to minimize the risk of hypoglycemia [37, 38]. It is essential to carefully consider the existing pharmacological treatments for patients with DM after experiencing an AMI to decrease their susceptibility to ischemia and enhance their overall long‐term outlook. Patients with both DM and coronary artery disease (CAD) should receive intensive secondary prevention. The mainstay of secondary cardiovascular prevention involves using antiplatelet medications. Antithrombotic therapy for revascularization does not vary based on DM [39]. Evidence indicates that novel antidiabetic medications, such as SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and GLP‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1‐RA), have cardioprotective benefits in both diabetic and nondiabetic patients. Post‐AMI SGLT2i therapy has been associated with decreased stent restenosis and enhanced vascular remodeling, leading to improved clinical outcomes [40]. These medications exhibit anti‐inflammatory characteristics, reducing oxidized LDL accumulation, apoptosis, and inflammation of coronary plaques [41]. GLP1‐RAs have demonstrated the ability to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and directly influence atherosclerotic plaques by downregulating the expression of inflammatory and oxidative stress‐related molecules, along with apoptotic indicators [42, 43].

The combination of GLP‐1 receptor agonists with SGLT‐2 inhibitors results in an additional decrease in HbA1c levels between 0.74% and 0.91% when compared to SGLT‐2 inhibitor monotherapy. Combination therapy yields greater weight loss outcomes relative to monotherapy. Research indicates weight reductions ranging from roughly 1.46 kg to 1.95 kg surpassing the effects of SGLT‐2 inhibitors alone [44]. A recent study indicates that the combination therapy of SGLT‐2 inhibitors and GLP‐1 receptor agonists is linked to a reduced incidence of a composite outcome comprising acute coronary syndrome, hospitalization for heart failure, and all‐cause mortality, in comparison to the use of either SGLT‐2 inhibitors or GLP‐1 receptor agonists alone in patients with type 2 diabetes and a recent acute myocardial infarction [45].

Managing cardiovascular risk factors in people with diabetes relies heavily on lifestyle interventions such as making changes to diet and engaging in regular physical activity. These interventions are essential for achieving optimal blood sugar control, effectively controlling blood pressure, and promoting weight loss [46].

Challenges Faced in LMICs

Most LMICs do not have the essential resources, tools, and technology to detect and manage diabetes and myocardial infarction accurately. Diabetes patients in LMICs face a higher likelihood of not only being diagnosed late but also not receiving the best possible treatment, which can result in poor diabetes management and the onset of diabetes‐related complications [47]. In LMICs, the lack of a centralized ambulance system, insufficient equipment for prehospital ECG, ECG telemetry system, and inadequate paramedic training, have hindered advancements in MI care [48, 49].

Healthcare systems in LMICs frequently lack adequate funding, leading to a lack of resources to treat chronic illnesses such as diabetes and cardiovascular conditions. Individuals with diabetes need consistent self‐care and medical supervision to avoid complications and promote good health results, leading to increased financial costs. Identifying the barrier is the challenge of affording the financial cost of certain diabetes medical tests and treatment needs that are not included in the health insurance coverage, including the HbA1c test and continuous glucose monitor [50]. Even in high‐income countries, funding remains a significant obstacle to MI care due to its high level of complexity. Dealing with the high expenses, lack of knowledge regarding the increasing impact of IHD, and competing demands in LMICs are obstacles that need to be addressed [51, 52].

Patients may struggle to engage with health care services and stick to treatment plans due to the lack of knowledge about diabetes and its related health issues [53]. Poor adherence to treatment regimens, such as missing appointments or not following medication instructions, can impact glycemic control [54]. Low levels of education and health literacy in LMICs lead to a lack of awareness of health conditions, difficulty recognizing symptoms, and limited use of medical resources, ultimately resulting in poorer outcomes in MI care [55, 56].

Risk factors are unevenly distributed in LMIC, with urban areas experiencing the most severe impact. Furthermore, numerous developing nations face a lack of personnel, which adds extra strain to the healthcare system [57]. Several countries experience a notable brain drain in these regions as skilled healthcare workers move to wealthier countries for better prospects. This leads to a severe lack of qualified staff, directly affecting the level of care provided. Physician migration from LMICs to HICs results in a yearly loss of approximately US$16 billion (95% CI $3.4–$38.2) due to the cost of increased mortality [58].

Essential diabetes medications are lacking in LMICs due to limited availability and high costs. A study found that metformin was unaffordable for 26·9% of households in low‐income countries, while insulin was unaffordable for 63·0% [59]. A major concern in LMICs regarding pharmacological treatment for MI is the absence of thrombolytics, particularly bolus medications for prehospital use, in regions lacking PCI‐capable facilities. Additionally, delaying the initiation of medications such as antiplatelets and anticoagulants can have a detrimental effect on results [49].

A study indicates that the significance of obstacles is influenced by the socioeconomic and cultural environments in which individuals reside [60]. Cultural aspects to consider when managing chronic conditions include preferences for food and diet, lifestyle, traditional and religious views, and beliefs on overall health [61]. Tobacco usage, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, and harmful alcohol consumption are the key risk factors in these cases [62].

Healthcare services face significant obstacles due to natural disasters, particularly in low‐income nations. Natural disasters such as floods, earthquakes, and hurricanes have the potential to cause harm to essential infrastructure, such as healthcare facilities, leading to challenges in delivering care to vulnerable populations [63]. For people with chronic illnesses such as diabetes and MI, these interruptions could create major obstacles to their continued treatment and control.

The latest data from the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) suggests that there are expected to be 35.3 million refugees globally [64]. These individuals frequently encounter insufficient healthcare availability, poor living conditions, and restricted resources [65]. Refugees in LMICs may create notable difficulties for healthcare systems, especially when dealing with chronic illnesses such as diabetes and MI comorbidities. The unexpected increase in refugees can strain healthcare facilities that are already overburdened and lack resources.

Recent advances in noninvasive cardiovascular imaging (like coronary computed tomography angiography, CCTA) and biomarkers of insulin resistance offer new opportunities for early risk stratification in diabetic patients. Studies have shown that CCTA can help identify high‐risk individuals by assessing coronary plaque burden, while insulin resistance markers can enhance cardiovascular risk prediction in patients with chronic coronary syndrome [66, 67]. Although these technologies are not widely accessible in LMICs, integrating biomarker screening and centralized imaging hubs into care pathways could improve early detection and management of diabetes and cardiovascular comorbidities, complementing strategies like task shifting and digital health [68].

Current Prospects in LMICs

Global health initiatives and international organizations are essential in tackling the impact of NCDs in LMICs. Initiatives like the WHO's Global Action Plan for NCD Prevention and Control prioritize decreasing the rates of NCDs, including diabetes and cardiovascular diseases. These efforts have enhanced primary healthcare and advanced universal health coverage to address the diagnosis, management, and support of individuals with NCDs and those at risk, while also boosting financial resources, technical assistance, and capacity enhancement in LMICs. This is crucial for advancing healthcare infrastructure, educating healthcare workers, and expanding the availability of necessary medications [69].

A new study shows that, despite substantial government initiatives, health insurance programs in LMICs frequently do not reach the most vulnerable populations and mainly favor more affluent groups. To accomplish Universal Health Coverage (UHC), health insurance plans must be reassessed to better assist vulnerable communities [70]. Progressive tax‐based health insurance systems, exemplified by Ghana and Thailand, provide valuable examples for LMICs while pointing out potential pitfalls to avoid in achieving universal coverage. LMICs must acknowledge that there is no universal solution; achievements are determined by each nation's particular political and economic circumstances [71].

Swift developments in digital health technologies like telemedicine, mobile health apps, and electronic health records show potential in enhancing diabetes and MI care in LMICs by improving healthcare access in remote and underserved regions. Connecting patients with regional experts for treatment guidance improves STEMI care through telemedicine. In LMICs, telemedicine MI systems like the Latin America Telemedicine Infarct Network and Rajasthan Heart Attack Treatment have enhanced triage efficiency, decreased reperfusion times and emergency department delays, and in certain instances, led to a 50% decrease in mortality [48]. The Indian Diabetes Prevention Program‐3 showed that personalized lifestyle changes through mobile phones lowered the risk of diabetes compared to standard advice, indicating the efficiency of mobile technology in overcoming barriers to health education like cost and access [72].

Due to the growing prevalence of NCDs in LMICs, policymakers, and healthcare providers are looking for ways to offer holistic care for multiple health conditions. Integrated care models are being introduced through primary healthcare systems in LMICs. In fully integrated care, all services are provided in a single location. In a partially integrated model of care, patients with diabetes can also receive care for other diseases but not the full range of services [73]. This method boosts the quality of care and the efficiency of healthcare delivery in settings with limited resources.

Task‐shifting, reallocating prevention tasks to non‐nurse and nonphysician workers, is a promising solution for chronic conditions like HIV, easing the human resource crisis [57]. Shifting tasks can potentially improve the care of diabetes and MI in LMICs. To ensure effectiveness, issues like defined roles, evaluation, training, and supervision should be addressed. A hybrid approach, where task shifting is integrated with digital health technologies, could significantly enhance the effectiveness, cost‐efficiency, and scalability of healthcare interventions in LMICs. For instance, telemedicine could support community health workers (CHWs) in providing remote consultations, overcoming the challenge of limited access to healthcare specialists in rural areas [48]. Simultaneously, mobile health applications could empower patients to self‐monitor vital metrics such as blood glucose or cardiac symptoms, sending real‐time data to healthcare providers [72]. This integration would not only optimize chronic disease management (such as diabetes and heart disease) but also enable early intervention and continuous care, which is critical in LMICs where healthcare systems often struggle with providing consistent, long‐term care.

By combining task shifting with digital health technologies, healthcare systems can overcome both the shortage of healthcare workers and limited access to technology. This synergy ensures that care is not only more accessible and efficient, but also more personalized, adapting to the individual needs of patients in resource‐constrained settings. For example, CHWs can use telemedicine to connect with specialized care when needed, while mobile applications provide patients with ongoing education and tools to take ownership of their health [48, 74].

In LMICs, community‐based interventions involve local communities in promoting health through activities such as awareness campaigns, screenings, and lifestyle changes to enhance the management of diabetes and MI. Community health workers, volunteers, and other professionals have successfully decreased diabetes rates by 54.0% through targeted interventions focusing on crucial health indicators [75, 76]. Community‐focused initiatives effectively increased understanding of CVD and risk factors and impacted physical activity and eating habits. These actions also led to improved levels of blood pressure and blood sugar. Strategies involved mobilizing the community, providing health education, counseling, and training healthcare providers [62, 77].

Discussion

The coexistence of diabetes and MI in LMICs presents a significant public health issue (Figure 3). As both conditions become more prevalent, healthcare systems face difficulties handling the double burden in these areas. This not only adds complexity to patient care but also puts significant pressure on already limited healthcare resources.

LMICs face a significant challenge due to their insufficient healthcare infrastructure. Many regions lack advanced diagnostic equipment, healthcare facilities, and access to essential medications, which are crucial for managing chronic conditions such as diabetes and MI [48, 72]. Rural and underserved areas, in particular, struggle with a shortage of trained healthcare professionals, making it even more challenging to provide adequate care where it is most needed [57]. It has been found that brain drain in LMICs can also contribute to a shortage of healthcare providers, which may exacerbate health outcomes and, therefore, increase rates of mortality [58]. Promising advancements in the treatment of diabetes and MI comorbidities. Organizations like the WHO have played a significant role in global health initiatives, making valuable contributions such as enhancing healthcare capacity, increasing medication availability, and implementing new care models [69]. Task‐shifting can be an effective solution to address the lack of personnel in managing these chronic conditions.

Unlike high‐income nations with superior infrastructure and universal health coverage, LMICs struggle to manage chronic diseases because of their underfunded healthcare systems and the absence of universal health insurance. Managing diabetes and MI in LMICs can pose challenges after diagnosis, including issues with medication adherence, lifestyle adjustments, lack of awareness, and follow‐up care [53, 54]. A lot of people do not realize the connection between diabetes and the risks associated with cardiovascular health, which can lead to delays in pursuing preventive care or making necessary lifestyle changes. This lack of awareness results in poor disease management, as individuals might not focus on vital lifestyle adjustments like changes in their diet, engaging in regular physical activity, and consistently monitoring their blood sugar levels. Socioeconomic factors, cultural beliefs, and phenomena like natural disasters contribute significantly to managing chronic conditions like diabetes and MI. This paper also finds that the increasing number of refugees in these regions may also pose a threat to managing the comorbidity of diabetes and MI. The progress in digital health technologies offers fresh possibilities for tackling healthcare challenges in LMICs. Tools such as telemedicine, mobile health (mHealth) apps, and electronic health records can play a role in closing the healthcare access disparities, especially in remote areas [48]. In addition, holistic care models and community‐centered strategies tackle both the medical and social influences on diabetes and MI management, resulting in a more comprehensive healthcare approach [73]. In these settings, improving preventive care and early detection is crucial to reduce complications.

One important element is the vulnerability of either sex to coronary dysfunction. According to research, women may experience adverse cardiovascular events before menopause. The inherent vascular protective properties of estrogen are largely offset by insulin resistance and endothelial dysfunction, which contribute to this contradiction [20, 21, 22]. This highlights how the dual burden of diabetes and myocardial infarction in LMICs must be evaluated with gender disparities in mind. The growing burden of comorbid diabetes and myocardial infarction (MI) in LMICs reflects systemic healthcare limitations and the often‐overlooked role of chronic inflammation in disease progression. Addressing this dual burden requires a multifaceted strategy: integrating inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP and IL‐6 into diagnostic and prognostic workflows [19]; expanding access to cardioprotective therapies such as sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RA) [29, 78, 79, 80]; and implementing scalable, community‐centered and digital health solutions to bridge gaps in care delivery [48]. Ensuring equitable access to combination cardiometabolic therapies [81] and strengthening preventive care infrastructure will be critical in mitigating long‐term complications and reducing mortality in these vulnerable populations [46, 53].

Limitations

While our review does well of putting together a lot of secondary data to show how diabetes and heart disease affect people around the world, it has some limitations, too. If we only use studies that have already been done, we will not be able to get first‐hand, patient‐level information or the specifics of how healthcare works in different areas. Without first‐hand information or new empirical results, our conclusions might not fully show how things vary in different areas or how well focused interventions work. In the future, using meta‐analytic methods or modeling studies could help close the gap between global and local health problems by giving us a better picture of how they change over time in different areas.

The absence of detailed data on the prevalence of chronic diseases in rural areas of LMICs may lead to an underestimation of the true impact of diabetes and MI. Due to the disparities in healthcare access and infrastructure across LMICs, creating universally applicable solutions for managing diabetes and MI comorbidities remains a complex task. While digital health solutions have potential, their scalability in LMICs with limited internet access and technological infrastructure could present a significant hurdle.

Conclusion

The dual burden of diabetes and MI poses a significant challenge to health professionals in LMICs owing to, poor healthcare infrastructure, low public awareness, socio‐cultural factors, limited access to medicines, and lack of financial resources. These challenges are compounded by the displacement of populations like refugees or asylum seekers as well as the drain on already stretched health‐care systems. Yet, it is other ideas with great potential such as digital health technologies, community‐based interventions and task shifting are hailed as credible solutions for bettering care delivery. Tackling these challenges requires a coordinated, layered approach while keeping in mind the need for sustainable healthcare management, public and social awareness, increased access to affordable treatments, and sustainable capacity building among healthcare systems in LMICs.
