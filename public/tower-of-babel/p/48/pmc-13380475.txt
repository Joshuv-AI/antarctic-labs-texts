Neonatal stress hyperglycemia mimicking diabetes mellitus secondary to massive intraparenchymal brain abscess due to escherichia coli sepsis: A case report
Authors: Bana Sunil Reddy, Likhitha B
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stress hyperglycemia is a common metabolic response in critically ill neonates but may rarely present with extreme hyperglycemia mimicking neonatal diabetes mellitus. We report a term female neonate presenting on day 18 of life with lethargy, poor feeding, dehydration, and severe hyperglycemia (>1000 mg/dL), hypernatremia, and acute kidney injury. Insulin therapy was initiated due to suspicion of neonatal diabetes mellitus. Blood cultures grew Escherichia coli, confirming neonatal sepsis. Neuroimaging revealed a massive right fronto-parieto-temporal intraparenchymal abscess with significant mass effect and midline shift. The patient underwent staged neurosurgical intervention including anterior fontanelle aspiration followed by right frontal craniotomy and abscess evacuation. Pus culture confirmed multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli, and targeted antimicrobial therapy was administered. Follow-up imaging demonstrated reduction in abscess size with residual ventriculitis and gliotic changes. Genetic testing for neonatal diabetes mellitus was negative. Hyperglycemia resolved completely following infection control and discontinuation of insulin therapy, confirming stress hyperglycemia. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge in differentiating stress hyperglycemia from neonatal diabetes mellitus and emphasizes the importance of early neuroimaging and prompt neurosurgical intervention in neonates with sepsis and neurological manifestations. Early recognition and multidisciplinary management are critical to improving survival and neurological outcomes.

Introduction

Stress hyperglycemia is a common metabolic response in critically ill neonates and is characterized by transient elevation of blood glucose levels in the absence of underlying diabetes mellitus. It occurs due to activation of neuroendocrine stress pathways during severe illness, resulting in increased secretion of counter-regulatory hormones such as cortisol, catecholamines, glucagon, and growth hormone. These hormonal changes stimulate hepatic gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis while impairing peripheral glucose utilization, leading to hyperglycemia [1], [2]. In addition, inflammatory cytokines released during systemic infections contribute to insulin resistance and further exacerbate hyperglycemia [2].

Although mild to moderate hyperglycemia is frequently observed in neonatal intensive care settings, extreme hyperglycemia may mimic neonatal diabetes mellitus, a rare genetic disorder characterized by impaired insulin secretion due to mutations affecting pancreatic beta-cell function [3], [4]. Neonatal diabetes mellitus requires long-term insulin therapy and genetic evaluation, whereas stress hyperglycemia is typically transient and resolves with treatment of the underlying illness. Differentiating between these two conditions is critical, as misdiagnosis may lead to unnecessary prolonged insulin therapy and inappropriate clinical management.

Neonatal sepsis is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and is frequently associated with severe metabolic and neurological complications. Escherichia coli is one of the most common pathogens responsible for late-onset neonatal sepsis and is associated with increased risk of central nervous system infections due to hematogenous dissemination and the immature neonatal blood-brain barrier [5]. Neonatal brain abscess is a rare but devastating complication of neonatal sepsis and results from bacterial invasion of brain parenchyma, leading to localized inflammation, tissue necrosis, and abscess formation [6].

The clinical presentation of neonatal brain abscess may be nonspecific and includes lethargy, irritability, seizures, poor feeding, and bulging fontanelle. Early diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion and prompt neuroimaging. Magnetic resonance imaging is the gold standard diagnostic modality and allows precise assessment of abscess size, location, and associated complications [7]. Management involves prompt neurosurgical drainage combined with prolonged targeted antimicrobial therapy to prevent further neurological injury and improve clinical outcomes [8].

We report a rare and clinically significant case of severe neonatal stress hyperglycemia mimicking neonatal diabetes mellitus associated with massive intraparenchymal brain abscess secondary to multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli sepsis, highlighting the diagnostic challenges, management considerations, and clinical implications of this condition.

Case presentation

A term female neonate was born at 38 weeks and 5 days of gestation via caesarean section due to prolonged rupture of membranes. The birth weight was 2600 g, and Apgar scores were 7, 9, and 10 at one, five, and ten minutes, respectively. The infant remained clinically stable during the immediate neonatal period and was discharged in good condition.

At 18 days of life, the infant presented with lethargy, poor feeding, irritability, and decreased spontaneous activity. There was no history of fever, trauma, or prior seizures. Given the infant’s lethargy, poor feeding, dehydration, and bulging anterior fontanelle, neonatal sepsis with central nervous system involvement was suspected at admission. Blood cultures were obtained promptly, and empiric intravenous antibiotic therapy was initiated along with fluid resuscitation and metabolic stabilization. Neuroimaging was subsequently performed because of persistent neurological abnormalities and bulging fontanelle. On examination, the infant was lethargic with signs of severe dehydration, including sunken eyes, dry mucous membranes, prolonged capillary refill time, and poor peripheral perfusion. Neurological examination revealed depressed sensorium and a tense, bulging anterior fontanelle, suggestive of raised intracranial pressure.

Laboratory evaluation revealed severe metabolic derangements. Initial blood glucose was 555 mg/dL and rapidly increased to a peak value of 1017 mg/dL. Serum sodium was markedly elevated at 180 mmol/L, consistent with severe hypernatremic dehydration. Severe metabolic acidosis was present, with serum bicarbonate of 11.3 mmol/L. Renal function was significantly impaired, with blood urea of 307 mg/dL and serum creatinine of 7.16 mg/dL, consistent with KDIGO stage 3 acute kidney injury. These findings indicated severe systemic metabolic stress and multiorgan dysfunction.

Given the extreme hyperglycemia, neonatal diabetes mellitus was initially suspected, and the infant was started on intravenous insulin infusion along with careful fluid resuscitation and gradual correction of electrolyte abnormalities. Blood cultures subsequently grew extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Escherichia coli, confirming late-onset neonatal sepsis.

Neuroimaging was performed due to neurological deterioration and bulging fontanelle. Cranial ultrasonography revealed a large cystic lesion measuring 6.6 × 5 cm in the right periventricular region, causing significant mass effect and midline shift. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain demonstrated a large right fronto-parieto-temporal ring-enhancing intra-axial lesion measuring 9.1 × 5.9 × 6.2 cm, with surrounding cerebral edema, sulcal effacement, ventricular compression, and an 11-mm midline shift, consistent with a massive intraparenchymal brain abscess (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging demonstrating a large ring-enhancing intraparenchymal abscess involving the right fronto-parieto-temporal region with surrounding edema, ventricular compression, and midline shift.

The infant subsequently developed generalized seizures and worsening neurological status, necessitating urgent neurosurgical intervention. Initial anterior fontanelle aspiration and drainage were performed to relieve intracranial pressure. This was followed by definitive right frontal craniotomy, evacuation of the abscess, and partial excision of the abscess capsule. Microbiological analysis of the drained pus confirmed multidrug-resistant ESBL-producing Escherichia coli sensitive to carbapenems and colistin. Intravenous antibiotic therapy was escalated accordingly with meropenem and colistin, and prolonged antimicrobial therapy was administered. The infant received intravenous meropenem and colistin for a total duration of 8 weeks.

Follow-up neuroimaging demonstrated reduction in abscess size but revealed several complications, including ventriculitis, subdural hygroma, porencephalic cyst formation, thinning of the corpus callosum, and areas of gliosis, consistent with post-infective structural brain injury (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging showing reduction in abscess size with residual ventriculitis, subdural hygroma, porencephalic cyst formation, and gliotic changes.

An NGS-based neonatal diabetes gene panel including KCNJ11, ABCC8, INS, EIF2AK3, GCK, PDX1, FOXP3, and NEUROD1 did not reveal any pathogenic variants. Whole exome sequencing was not performed. With appropriate treatment of sepsis, surgical drainage, and metabolic stabilization, blood glucose levels gradually normalized. Insulin infusion was tapered and permanently discontinued. The infant remained euglycemic thereafter without further requirement for insulin therapy, confirming the diagnosis of stress hyperglycemia rather than neonatal diabetes mellitus.

At three-month follow-up, the infant demonstrated partial neurological recovery. However, persistent left-sided motor weakness and developmental delay were noted, consistent with sequelae of extensive cerebral injury.

Discussion

Stress hyperglycemia is a well-recognized metabolic response in critically ill neonates and occurs due to activation of complex neuroendocrine and inflammatory pathways. During severe systemic illness such as sepsis, increased secretion of counter-regulatory hormones including cortisol, catecholamines, glucagon, and growth hormone leads to increased hepatic glucose production through gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis while simultaneously impairing peripheral glucose uptake due to insulin resistance [1], [2]. Inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin−6 further exacerbate insulin resistance by interfering with insulin receptor signaling pathways, resulting in marked hyperglycemia [2], [9].

Although stress hyperglycemia is commonly observed in critically ill neonates, extreme hyperglycemia exceeding 1000 mg/dL is rare and may closely mimic neonatal diabetes mellitus. Neonatal diabetes mellitus is a monogenic disorder characterized by impaired insulin secretion caused by mutations affecting pancreatic beta-cell function and requires long-term insulin therapy [3], [4]. Differentiating stress hyperglycemia from neonatal diabetes mellitus is essential, as stress hyperglycemia resolves following treatment of the underlying illness, whereas neonatal diabetes mellitus persists and requires prolonged management. In the present case, severe hyperglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and dehydration initially raised suspicion for neonatal diabetes mellitus. However, normalization of blood glucose following treatment of sepsis and negative genetic testing confirmed the diagnosis of stress hyperglycemia.

Neonatal sepsis is a major cause of morbidity and mortality and is frequently associated with severe metabolic disturbances. Escherichia coli remains one of the most common pathogens responsible for neonatal sepsis and central nervous system infections. The immature neonatal immune system and increased permeability of the blood-brain barrier facilitate hematogenous spread of infection into brain parenchyma, resulting in cerebritis and eventual abscess formation [5], [6]. Brain abscess formation involves localized tissue necrosis, inflammatory response, and encapsulation of purulent material, leading to progressive enlargement and mass effect.

Neonatal brain abscess is a rare but life-threatening condition associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and include lethargy, poor feeding, irritability, seizures, and bulging fontanelle, which may delay diagnosis [6], [7]. In this case, early neuroimaging played a crucial role in identifying the massive intraparenchymal abscess and guiding management.

Magnetic resonance imaging is the preferred diagnostic modality due to its superior sensitivity and ability to delineate abscess characteristics, including size, location, and associated complications such as ventriculitis and midline shift [7]. The large abscess size and significant mass effect observed in this patient indicated severe intracranial involvement and risk of neurological deterioration.

Management of neonatal brain abscess requires prompt neurosurgical drainage combined with prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy. Surgical drainage is essential to reduce intracranial pressure, remove infectious material, and prevent further neurological damage [8]. In this case, staged neurosurgical intervention involving anterior fontanelle aspiration followed by definitive craniotomy and abscess evacuation was performed successfully.

Multidrug-resistant ESBL-producing Escherichia coli poses additional therapeutic challenges due to limited antibiotic options. Carbapenems and colistin are effective agents for treating multidrug-resistant gram-negative infections and were used successfully in this patient [6]. Appropriate culture-guided antimicrobial therapy is critical for infection control and clinical recovery.

Despite successful surgical and medical management, neonatal brain abscess is associated with significant long-term neurological sequelae due to extensive neuronal injury. Complications such as ventriculitis, porencephalic cyst formation, gliosis, and corpus callosum thinning reflect permanent structural brain damage. These findings correlate with persistent neurological deficits, including motor weakness and developmental delay observed during follow-up in this patient.

This case highlights the importance of recognizing stress hyperglycemia as a transient metabolic response to severe neonatal illness and differentiating it from neonatal diabetes mellitus. It also emphasizes the need for early neuroimaging in septic neonates presenting with neurological symptoms and the critical role of prompt neurosurgical intervention and targeted antimicrobial therapy in improving survival and neurological outcomes.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare and severe presentation of neonatal stress hyperglycemia mimicking neonatal diabetes mellitus in the setting of multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli sepsis complicated by massive intraparenchymal brain abscess. Extreme hyperglycemia in critically ill neonates may lead to diagnostic uncertainty and inappropriate long-term insulin therapy if stress hyperglycemia is not recognized. Careful clinical evaluation, close metabolic monitoring, and appropriate genetic testing are essential to differentiate transient stress hyperglycemia from true neonatal diabetes mellitus.

Additionally, this case underscores the devastating neurological complications that may arise from neonatal sepsis, particularly intracranial abscess formation due to hematogenous dissemination of infection. Early recognition of neurological deterioration, prompt neuroimaging, timely neurosurgical drainage, and targeted antimicrobial therapy are critical for infection control and improving survival. Despite appropriate intervention, significant neurological sequelae may occur due to extensive cerebral injury. This case emphasizes the importance of early diagnosis, multidisciplinary management, and close long-term neurological follow-up in neonates with severe sepsis and intracranial complications.
