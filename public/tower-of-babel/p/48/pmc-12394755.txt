Optimizing Vancomycin Area under the Concentration-Time Curve Targets in Enterococcal Bacteremia: Balancing Efficacy and Nephrotoxicity
Authors: Young Rong Kim, Ha-Jin Chun, Jung Yeon Heo, Hakjun Hyun, Young Hwa Choi, Eun Jin Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeVancomycin is critical in treating enterococcal bacteremia; however, its optimal pharmacokinetic (PK)/pharmacodynamics (PD) targets remain unclear. This study evaluates the association between vancomycin PK/PD parameters and clinical outcomes in patients with enterococcal bacteremia.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 70 patients with enterococcal bacteremia treated with vancomycin at a university-affiliated teaching hospital. The primary and secondary outcomes were unfavorable clinical outcome (30-day mortality or persistent bacteremia) and nephrotoxicity, respectively. Vancomycin area under the concentration-time curve (AUC)/minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) was calculated using Bayesian methods. Receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis determined AUC/MIC thresholds for predicting unfavorable clinical outcomes and nephrotoxicity. Logistic regression analysis identified risk factors for clinical outcomes.ResultsUnfavorable outcome occurred in 21 patients (30.0%), and 10 (14.3%) experienced nephrotoxicity. The ROC-derived AUC24/MIC cutoff for unfavorable outcome and nephrotoxicity were AUC24/MIC ≥466.0 [AUC=0.740; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.618–0.862] and ≥643.2 (AUC=0.963; 95% CI, 0.922–1.000), respectively. Clinical success was achieved in 44.9% (22/49) of patients with an AUC24/MIC <400, whereas 47.6% (10/21) experienced unfavorable outcome despite having an AUC24/MIC of 400–600. Nephrotoxicity [adjusted odds ratio (aOR)=15.05; 95% CI, 2.15–105.14; p=0.006] and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) (aOR=1.57; 95% CI, 1.18–2.08; p=0.002) were independent risk factors for unfavorable outcome. High AUC24/MIC and CCI were associated with nephrotoxicity (aOR=1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.04; p=0.005, and aOR=1.83; 95% CI, 1.01–3.35; p=0.045).ConclusionNephrotoxicity and multiple comorbidities were stronger risk factors for unfavorable outcomes than vancomycin AUC/MIC. These findings highlight the need for individualized strategies to optimize efficacy while minimizing toxicity. Further large-scale studies are warranted to refine the optimal AUC/MIC threshold.

INTRODUCTION

Enterococcus species are widely distributed in the environment and are a normal part of the flora in the gastrointestinal tract of humans and animals. Their resilience in environmental conditions and capacity to colonize nearby medical devices make enterococci a leading cause of nosocomial infections, particularly in immunocompromised patients.1 Enterococcal bacteremia is associated with high mortality rates, ranging from 20% to 35%, with outcomes strongly influenced by key risk factors, such as multiple comorbidities, immunocompromised status, advanced age, and the presence of complications like infective endocarditis.234

Among more than 30 known Enterococcus species, E. faecalis and E. faecium are the leading causes of human infections, including endocarditis, urinary tract infections, wound infections, and bloodstream infections.5 These species exhibit significant genetic differences, often requiring distinct therapeutic approaches. Ampicillin is a cornerstone in treating enterococcal infection; however, E. faecium frequently exhibits high-level resistance to penicillin due to the low-affinity penicillin-binding protein 5.6 In South Korea, approximately 7.4% of E. faecalis isolates were resistant to ampicillin in 2022, compared with 89.2% of E. faecium, as suggested by the National Institute of Health (https://nih.go.kr/nohas). Consequently, there has been a rise in vancomycin usage for enterococcal infections.

Although pharmacokinetic (PK)-pharmacodynamic (PD) targets for vancomycin therapy are well-established for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infections, data for enterococcal infections are limited. Three retrospective studies have suggested that the area under the concentration-time curve (AUC)/minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) ratio is a reliable indicator of therapeutic efficacy for enterococcal infections.789 Jumah, et al.7 found that an AUC24/MIC ≥389 was associated with reduced mortality, whereas Komatsu, et al.9 identified an AUC24/MIC ≥414.3 as a significant predictor for treatment success. These findings align with clinical guidelines for vancomycin dosing in MRSA infections, which recommend an AUC/MIC >400. However, three other studies proposed that AUC/MIC may not reflect therapeutic efficacy, suggesting that disease severity or trough level could be more suitable indicators of treatment effectiveness.101112

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the association between PK/PD parameters and treatment outcomes in enterococcal bacteremia. Additionally, we sought to estimate AUC24/MIC thresholds for unfavorable clinical outcomes and nephrotoxicity to provide guidance for optimizing vancomycin therapy and improving clinical outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Ajou University Hospital, a 1187-bed tertiary hospital in Gyeonggi Province. Patients with their first episode of enterococcal bacteremia were screened to determine whether they had been treated with vancomycin between January 2013, and January 2023. Inclusion criteria were an age over 18 years, presence of enterococcal bacteremia, and receipt of intravenous vancomycin for at least 3 days. Exclusion criteria included presence of vancomycin-resistant isolates, lack of vancomycin level monitoring, presence of polymicrobial bloodstream infections, receipt of renal replacement therapy, and receipt of antimicrobials effective against isolated enterococci prior to vancomycin initiation.

Data collection

Data collected from patients’ electronic medical records included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, date and duration of antibiotics treatment, source of bacteremia, concurrent use of immunosuppressants, and response to vancomycin, as indicated by mortality, duration of bacteremia, and persistence of fever. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated by reviewing the patient’s medical records.13 Disease severity was assessed using the Pitt bacteremia score, which was calculated based on the worst parameters within 48 hours before or on the day of the first positive blood culture.14 The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Ajou University School of Medicine, which waived the requirement for informed consent as the data of the participants were fully anonymized (IRB approval number: AJOUIRB-DB-2025-061).

Definitions and outcome measurements

The primary outcome was an unfavorable clinical outcome. Unfavorable outcome at the end of the treatment was defined as death within 30 days of the date of the first positive culture or persistent bacteremia, defined as a positive culture after 7 days of vancomycin treatment.15 Nephrotoxicity was defined as an increase of more than 0.5 mg/dL in serum creatinine or a 50% rise from baseline levels on 2 consecutive days, occurring at any time from the initiation of vancomycin therapy up to 7 days after its cessation.16 Immunosuppressive therapy was defined as the use of chemotherapy, moderate-dose steroids (equivalent to 0.3–0.5 mg/kg/day of prednisolone for over 3 weeks), or immunosuppressants following solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

Vancomycin dosing and PD data

At our institution, the vancomycin dosing guideline recommends administering 15–20 mg/kg per dose. Dosages were adjusted based on creatinine clearance (CCr), calculated using the Cockcroft–Gault equation: for patients with CCr ≥60 mL/min, CCr 30–60 mL/min, CCr 20–30 mL/min, and CCr <20 mL/min, doses were administered at intervals of 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours, respectively. The guideline also recommends monitoring vancomycin trough concentrations before administration within 72 hours of vancomycin initiation. The dates and times of vancomycin administration, along with vancomycin concentrations, were recorded and analyzed using Capcil® software (Simkin Inc., Gainesville, FL, USA). The vancomycin AUC24/MIC was calculated using a Bayesian approach, where prior population PK data for vancomycin in adults were combined with patient-specific data to update Bayesian posterior parameter values. The AUC72/MIC for the first 72 hours was calculated using the trapezoidal approximation method, incorporating the updated Bayesian posterior parameter values, dosing data, and individual patient details. The AUC24/MIC for 24 hours was then derived by dividing the AUC for 72 hours by 3. The initial 72-hour AUC72/MIC was assessed because achieving an early target AUC/MIC is crucial for treating bloodstream infections.16 Species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed using the automated broth microdilution method with the VITEK 2 system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France).

Statistical analysis

This study investigated the correlations between vancomycin AUC24/MIC and clinical outcomes, as well as the association between specific patient characteristics and outcomes. Associations were explored using both categorical and continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to the continuous variables as appropriate. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify the risk factors for clinical outcomes. Potentially significant variables (p≤0.2) from the univariable analysis were incorporated into a multivariable model to identify risk factors for unfavorable outcomes. A receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis was conducted to determine the AUC24/MIC cutoff values for predicting unfavorable outcomes and nephrotoxicity. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 537 cases of enterococcal bacteremia were identified over a 10-year period from January 2013 to January 2023. Of these, 280 were cases of polymicrobial bacteremia, and 95 patients received other antibiotics. Forty-nine patients received vancomycin for less than 72 hours. Among the 113 patients who received vancomycin for more than 72 hours, 28 patients underwent dialysis, 11 patients did not undergo therapeutic dose monitoring, and 5 patients had incomplete data (Supplementary Fig. 1, only online). Consequently, 70 patients were ultimately included in this study.

The mean age of patients was 61.81±13.79 years, and 45 patients (64.3%) were male. The rate of unfavorable outcome was 30.0% (21/70), and nephrotoxicity occurred in 10 patients (14.3%). Among the cases of bacteremia, 54 (77.1%) were caused by E. faecium and 16 (22.9%) by E. faecalis. The three most common comorbidities were solid organ tumors (37.1%), hematologic malignancies (30.0%), and cerebrovascular or neurologic diseases (27.1%). The most common sources of infection were intra-abdominal infections (40.0%) and primary bacteremia (27.1%). Baseline characteristics were similar between the favorable and unfavorable clinical outcome groups, except for age (59.47±13.56 vs. 67.29±13.03; p=0.029). Disease severity and nephrotoxicity were significantly higher in the unfavorable outcome group compared to the favorable outcome group (Table 1). Most vancomycin MIC values were ≤1 µg/mL (97.1%). The median AUC24/MIC level [interquartile range (IQR)] was significantly higher in the unfavorable outcome group than in the favorable outcome group [405.82 (318.52–550.05) vs. 558.09 (444.34–750.49); p=0.002] (Table 2).

PK/PD determinants of vancomycin for treatment outcome

Nephrotoxicity was significantly higher in the AUC24/MIC ≥466.0 mg·hr/L group (0.0% vs. 31.3%; p<0.001), indicating a greater burden of nephrotoxicity with increasing AUC24/MIC in the unfavorable outcome group. Although vancomycin dosing between the favorable and unfavorable outcome groups was not significantly different, clearance was significantly lower in the unfavorable outcome group [59.16 (IQR, 43.10–77.02) vs. 44.95 (IQR, 25.81–71.37); p=0.019] (Table 2). The distribution of AUC24/MIC according to clinical outcome showed that 32 out of 49 patients (65.3%) with an AUC24/MIC <466.0 mg·hr/L experienced favorable outcomes, whereas 10 out of 21 patients (47.6%) with an AUC24/MIC of 400–600 mg·hr/L experienced unfavorable outcomes. Among the 9 out of 21 patients (42.9%) with unfavorable outcomes and an AUC24/MIC >600 mg·hr/L, 8 developed an acute kidney injury (Fig. 1). The ROC-derived cutoff value for predicting unfavorable outcomes was an AUC24/MIC ≥466.0 mg·hr/L [AUC=0.740; 95% confidece interval (CI), 0.618–0.862], while the cutoff for predicting nephrotoxicity was AUC24/MIC ≥643.2 mg·hr/L (AUC=0.963; 95% CI, 0.922–1.000) (Fig. 2).

Risk factors associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes and nephrotoxicity

In univariable analysis, an AUC24/MIC ≥466.0 mg·hr/L was associated with unfavorable outcomes [odds ratio (OR)=4.71; 95% CI, 1.54–14.35; p=0.006]; however, this association was not significant in the final multivariable model. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, nephrotoxicity and the CCI were identified as risk factors for unfavorable outcomes [adjusted OR (aOR)=15.05; 95% CI, 2.15–105.14; p=0.006, and aOR=1.57; 95% CI, 1.18–2.08; p=0.002, respectively] (Table 3). Regarding nephrotoxicity, both a higher CCI and higher AUC24/MIC were associated with nephrotoxicity (aOR=1.83; 95% CI, 1.01–3.35; p=0.045, and aOR=1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.04; p=0.005, respectively) (Table 4). Furthermore, when logistic regression analysis was performed using AUC24/MIC as a categorical variable, AUC24/MIC ≥643.2 mg·hr/L was strongly associated with nephrotoxicity in the multivariable analysis (aOR=99.0; 95% CI, 10.33–948.48; p<0.001).

Stratified analysis of the association between vancomycin parameters with clinical outcomes based on the presence of nephrotoxicity and CCI

Due to the higher burden of nephrotoxicity in the unfavorable outcome group, we performed stratified analysis based on the presence of nephrotoxicity. In patients without nephrotoxicity, only CCI was associated with unfavorable outcomes, with an aOR of 1.47 (95% CI, 1.11–1.96; p=0.008). A subgroup analysis of patients with a CCI ≤6 (AUC=0.795; 95% CI, 0.674–0.906; p<0.001) demonstrated that nephrotoxicity remained a strong risk factor for unfavorable outcomes (aOR=14.63; 95% CI, 1.86–115.21; p=0.011). Vancomycin AUC24/MIC and trough levels were not associated with clinical outcomes (Supplementary Table 1, only online). Among patients without nephrotoxicity, vancomycin parameters showed no significant differences between those in the favorable and unfavorable outcome groups. However, among patients with a CCI ≤6, those in the unfavorable outcome group exhibited a significantly higher AUC24/MIC, indicating a higher incidence of nephrotoxicity, compared with those in the favorable outcome group (Supplementary Table 2, only online).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we identified underlying comorbidities and nephrotoxicity as stronger risk factors for unfavorable outcomes than vancomycin AUC24/MIC. Additionally, vancomycin AUC24/MIC ≥643.2 mg·hr/L was an important predictor of nephrotoxicity, with a 99-fold increased risk. These findings align with the 2020 guideline advocating for AUC/MIC-based monitoring to prevent nephrotoxicity.17 A recent meta-analysis further supports this approach, showing a higher rate of nephrotoxicity in patients with elevated vancomycin AUC24/MIC (OR=3.11; 95% CI, 1.65–5.89).18 These findings suggest that monitoring AUC/MIC and maintaining it below 643.2 mg·hr/L may help reduce nephrotoxicity in enterococcal bacteremia, while highlighting the need for an individualized therapeutic approach.

In enterococcal bacteremia, underlying comorbidities are well-established risk factors for mortality, and our study confirmed their significance as predictors of unfavorable outcomes (aOR=1.57; 95% CI, 1.18–2.08).15 Nephrotoxicity was more frequent in patients with a high CCI (aOR=1.83; 95% CI, 1.01–3.35) and was also identified as a risk factor in those with higher vancomycin AUC24/MIC (aOR=1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.04). Among patients with an AUC24/MIC >600 mg·hr/L, unfavorable outcomes occurred in 50% (9/18), and nephrotoxicity developed in 90% (8/9) of these cases. While higher vancomycin exposure was associated with nephrotoxicity and subsequent unfavorable outcomes, this does not imply a causation, as nephrotoxicity may reflect a higher disease burden and greater PK variability. These findings underscore the importance of therapeutic drug monitoring, especially in frail and immunocompromised patients.

Interestingly, 44.9% (22/49) of patients achieved favorable outcomes with an AUC24/MIC <400 mg·hr/L, whereas 47.6% (10/21) experienced unfavorable outcomes despite having an AUC24/MIC of 400–600. Unfavorable outcomes in the AUC24/MIC 400–600 group were attributed to higher CCI scores [6 (4–8) vs 4.5 (2.3–5.8)], higher Pitt scores [3 (2–4) vs. 2 (1–3)] and older age [63.5 (50.8–71.8) vs. 59.5 (50.5–66.8)] compared to patients who achieved favorable outcomes with an AUC24/MIC <400 mg·hr/L. This suggests that favorable outcome at AUC24/MIC <400 mg·hr/L may have been influenced more by disease severity and patient-related factors rather than vancomycin exposure alone. Therefore, tailoring AUC24/MIC targets based on patient-specific risk factors, rather than applying a fixed threshold, could further improve clinical outcomes. Additionally, this observation challenges the conventional AUC24/MIC 400–600 mg·hr/L target originally established for MRSA infections. Due to the differences in pathogenicity and vancomycin activity, the applicability of vancomycin to enterococcal bacteremia remains uncertain.19 This also raises the question of whether the same MRSA-based vancomycin dosing strategy should be universally applied to enterococcal infections, highlighting the need for large-scale studies.

Even in the subgroup analysis of patients with a CCI ≤6, nephrotoxicity remained a significant risk factor for unfavorable outcomes (aOR=14.63; 95% CI, 1.86–115.21; p=0.011). Notably, in patients without nephrotoxicity, 84.6% (11/13) experienced unfavorable outcomes when the AUC24/MIC was ≥400. However, multivariable analysis in this subgroup revealed that neither vancomycin AUC24/MIC nor trough level were associated with unfavorable outcomes. Instead, comorbidities remained a significant risk factor influencing clinical outcomes (Supplementary Table 2, only online). These findings suggest that the fixed AUC24/MIC target may not be appropriate for all patients with enterococcal bacteremia. Individualized treatment strategies based on disease severity, comorbidities, and nephrotoxicity risk could help optimize therapeutic outcomes. Further prospective or randomized controlled studies are needed to determine the optimal AUC24/MIC targets for enterococcal bacteremia.

Several retrospective studies have demonstrated a correlation between reduced mortality rates in enterococcal bacteremia and vancomycin AUC24/MIC values within the range of 389–504.7912 Conversely, other studies have reported that vancomycin AUC24/MIC does not directly affect clinical outcomes.101112 Nakakura, et al.’s10 study notably reported that disease severity and multiple comorbidities were associated with mortality, aligning with our findings. These conflicting results might be attributed to variations in study populations and methodologies. Similar to our study, patients in Nakakura, et al.’s 10 and Sohn, et al.’s11 studies were severely ill, with high rates of intensive care unit admissions (26.7% and 37.8%, respectively) and significant comorbidities [CCI=4 (2–6) in Nakakura, et al.’s10 study]. Furthermore, these studies, like ours, reported a high prevalence of E. faecium bacteremia, which is typically associated with increased mortality due to higher CCI, greater resistance profile, and predominant nosocomial origin.2021 In our study, patients with E. faecium bacteremia exhibited a higher rate of unfavorable outcomes (76.2%), although this trend was not statistically significant. Additionally, no differences in underlying comorbidities or vancomycin AUC24/MIC were observed between enterococcal species. However, persistent bacteremia was more frequent in E. faecalis (37.5% vs. 7.4%, p=0.007), and all patients with persistent bacteremia of unknown origin underwent echocardiography to assess for endocarditis.

As most studies on enterococcal infections have been small-scale and retrospective, differences in underlying comorbidities and bacterial strains between patient groups may have introduced statistical bias. Additionally, the significance of the AUC24/MIC and its optimal range may have been distorted. Notably, although an optimal vancomycin AUC24/MIC range of 400–600 is recommended for MRSA bacteremia, there remains a lack of randomized controlled trial data to provide robust evidence. Moreover, previously reported prospective studies have failed to demonstrate that achieving a prespecified AUC24/MIC target improves clinical and bacteriological outcomes.1722 Similarly, our previous study on MRSA bacteremia found no significant association between the vancomycin AUC24/MIC and clinical outcomes.23 Consequently, vancomycin AUC24/MIC targets recommended for MRSA may not be directly applicable to enterococcal infections. Specifically, in cases of ampicillin-susceptible enterococcal bacteremia, using vancomycin may lead to poorer outcomes, and including such cases may confound the results.24 Therefore, a more nuanced approach that considers underlying comorbidities and bacterial susceptibility is essential, as supported by the findings of this study. Further research is needed to clarify the optimal PK/PD target.

This study has several strengths. First, we used a Bayesian method for AUC calculation, which combines Bayesian prior information with individual PKs, enabling AUC estimation even in the absence of steady-state trough levels. This approach is particularly beneficial for our study population, which predominantly consists of patients who are critically ill with multiple comorbidities and unpredictable PK fluctuations. Second, we included patients with both E. faecium and E. faecalis bacteremia, which allowed us to investigate differences in their clinical outcomes. Lastly, we performed stratified analyses classifying patients into various groups to identify the conditions under which higher vancomycin exposure could affect clinical outcomes.

Nonetheless, our study has some limitations. First, it was a single-center observational study with a small sample size, which could limit its predictive power for unfavorable outcomes and reduce its generalizability. Second, we used automated microdilution methods for MIC measurement, which could have resulted in less reliable AUC24/MIC values, particularly when MIC was ≤0.5. Furthermore, in some cases of intra-abdominal infection, surgical intervention for source control was not feasible due to the patients’ severely poor conditions, potentially influencing clinical outcomes. Lastly, we relied on trough-only data for AUC24/MIC estimation, which might be less precise compared to two-point sampling. However, this limitation was inherent to the retrospective study design. Nonetheless, Neely, et al. recently demonstrated that trough-only data, when analyzed using Bayesian software, can achieve 97% accuracy in AUC estimation.2526

In conclusion, our study identified that patient comorbidities and nephrotoxicity are stronger risk factors for clinical outcomes in enterococcal bacteremia than vancomycin AUC24/MIC or trough levels. Importantly, AUC24/MIC monitoring was superior to trough level monitoring in predicting nephrotoxicity, with a threshold of AUC24/MIC ≥643.2 associated with an increased risk of nephrotoxicity. Although an AUC24/MIC ≥466.0 mg·hr/L was associated with unfavorable outcomes, comorbidities played a more significant role, and patients with multiple comorbidities were at greater risk for nephrotoxicity. While nearly half of the patients achieved clinical success at AUC/MIC <400, unfavorable outcomes were frequent within the 400–600 range. These findings challenge the direct applicability of MRSA-based vancomycin targets to enterococcal infections, highlighting the greater influence of patient-specific factors over vancomycin exposure alone. These results suggest that maintaining AUC24/MIC below 643.2 could effectively reduce nephrotoxicity risk, but individualized strategies that consider patient specific factors are needed to balance efficacy and toxicity. Further large-scale prospective studies are warranted to refine these thresholds and establish optimal AUC24/MIC thresholds for enterococcal bacteremia.
