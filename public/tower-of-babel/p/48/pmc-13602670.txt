Longitudinal evaluation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte scoring using automated region of interest registration in breast cancer
Authors: Alessio Fiorin, Laia Adalid-Llansa, Laia Reverté, Esther Sauras-Colón, Noèlia Gallardo-Borràs, Hatem A Rashwan, Ramon Bosch-Príncep, Alba Fischer-Carles, Elena Goyda, Marylène Lejeune, Daniel Mata-Cano, Domènec Puig, Tábata Sánchez-Alcántara, Mikel Relloso Ortiz de Uriarte, Montserrat Llobera-Serentill, José Antonio Izuel-Navarro, Carlos López-Pablo

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are prognostic biomarkers in breast cancer (BC), particularly in HER2-positive and triple-negative subtypes. Assessment follows the international guidelines, in which pathologists evaluate whole hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides while integrating representative regions of the invasive tumor. However, manual region selection can be labor-intensive, subjective, and may introduce variability, particularly across consecutive tissue sections. Automated region of interest (ROI) registration may mitigate this limitation, yet its impact on longitudinal TIL scoring has not been systematically evaluated. Here, we introduce three ROI registration strategies (direct, intermediate, and serial with/without quality control) and present an automated framework validated for consistent TIL scoring and clinical relevance in predicting relapse.MethodsWe analyzed 104 invasive BC cases, each with 12 consecutive H&E slides. A pathologist annotated ROIs on both the first and twelfth slides. We registered these ROIs using the proposed strategies. We then evaluated them with performance metrics, including Intersection over Union (IoU), Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), failure rate, and execution time. Two pathologists scored TILs on manual and automated ROIs. We assessed longitudinal consistency between the first manual ROI and the twelfth slide’s corresponding ROIs. We also tested whether the association between TIL score and patient relapse outcomes was preserved.ResultsThe direct registration strategy achieved the highest geometric accuracy (mean IoU = 0.650, DSC = 0.769), with < 1% failure rate and ∼ 5.8 s execution time. TIL scores for both manual and automated ROIs closely matched (P = 0.84), showing strong agreement (Spearman’s ρ = 0.923, ICC = 0.936, CCC = 0.915, Cohen’s κ = 0.786). Longitudinal analyses showed no significant variability across distant sections (P = 0.79 and P = 0.62). TIL concentrations differed significantly between patients with and without relapse, and this association was preserved on both the first and twelfth slides (P < 0.05).ConclusionsWe present the first automated framework for longitudinal ROI registration to support TIL scoring in BC. By reducing manual effort and variability, the framework supports scalable evaluation of immune biomarkers across tissue depth while preserving clinically relevant signals, supporting precision immuno-oncology applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13058-026-02333-5.Keywords: Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, Breast cancer, Image registration, Histopathology, Whole slide images

Background

Breast cancer (BC) is the most frequent tumor in women worldwide [1]. Beyond tumor-intrinsic genetics, the immune response within the tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a crucial role in disease progression and patient outcomes [2]. Among immune populations in the TME, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) have shown prognostic and predictive value in BC, with higher TIL levels associated with improved outcomes in human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive and triple-negative BC (TNBC) subtypes [3, 4].

Pathologists score TILs following the International Immuno-Oncology Biomarker Working Group guidelines and Salgado’s criteria [5–7], by performing a full assessment of average TILs within the tumor area on the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slide. In particular, stromal TILs should be assessed as the percentage of intratumoral stromal area occupied by mononuclear inflammatory cells, evaluated only within the borders of the invasive tumor. TILs outside the invasive tumor border, around ductal carcinoma in situ or normal lobules, and in areas with artifacts, necrosis, regressive hyalinization, or previous core biopsy changes should be excluded; only mononuclear cells are considered, whereas polymorphonuclear leukocytes are not included. Despite this detailed criteria TILs assessment remains inherently subjective and discrepancies between pathologists produce inter-observer variability mainly due to elements including heterogeneity in the distribution of lymphocytes within the tumor, technical issues related to slide preparation and staining, and inconsistencies in defining the TILs scoring area or accurately determining lymphocyte populations [8].

To reduce some of these sources of variability, representative regions of interest (ROIs) may be used to approximate the overall stromal TIL level of a case rather than focusing on hotspots or areas with the highest lymphocyte density. Such representative ROIs may be viewed as a virtual tissue microarray (TMA)-like strategy, enabling standardized evaluation of comparable tissue regions in a research setting. By minimizing variability related to lymphocyte distribution heterogeneity and differences in scoring area definition, this approach may facilitate the investigation of whether changes in TIL concentration through tissue depth reflect intrinsic biological variation rather than other sources of variability.

The scope of this paper is not to replace Salgado’s evaluation criteria for evaluating TILs inside ROIs; the main objective is to determine if TIL concentration varies in depth under controlled conditions while minimizing variability. Our scope is to demonstrate that TILs concentration remains stable in depth, so then consecutive slides can be used to evaluate subpopulations of TILs or other immune cells (e.g., via immunohistochemistry (IHC), fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH), or multiplex staining) in the same standardized region. According to Salgado’s guidelines, it is explained that the huge complexity of lymphocytes, both from the standpoint of cell type and activation suggests that molecular characterization of this infiltrate, may add both sensitivity and specificity to the predictive value of TILs [7]. Demonstrating the stability of total TIL concentration across tissue depths is fundamental, as this homogeneity would validate the reliable use of serial sections for characterizing specific immune subpopulations via IHC or multiplexed staining. Such findings would support a more granular and clinically relevant immune profiling of the tumor microenvironment, as proposed by the International Immuno-Oncology Biomarker Working Group and other researchers [6, 9].

However, the manual identification and tracking of such representative ROI across a series of slides is a significant practical challenge that limits the scalability of longitudinal tissue analysis and can introduce additional variability stemming from manual ROI selection. Transitioning this manual process into digital workflows is essential to ensure that the ROI remains consistent across tissue depth.

In order to address this challenge to automatize the same ROI selection in consecutive slides, advances in DP have enabled the analysis of whole slide images (WSIs), facilitating teleconsultation, secure archiving, and integration of computer-assisted diagnostics [10, 11]. In BC, DP has been applied to aid diagnosis, classification, and prognosis prediction [12–14]. Nevertheless, automated ROI selection methods, including Vision Transformers [15], U-Net with ResNet-18 encoders [16], attention-based multiple instance learning [17], and superpixel-based approaches [18], improve accuracy, reduce analysis time, and minimize expert dependency. Despite advances in ROI selection and registration, few studies have examined the registration of ROIs across consecutive sections, a labor-intensive process when done manually [19, 20]. Manual identification of corresponding ROIs across consecutive sections can be time consuming, and is subject to intra- and inter-observer variability.

WSI registration techniques offer a promising solution [21]; however, optimal strategies for consecutive slides with known ordering remain largely unexplored, particularly for TIL scoring. While Mani et al. reported that depth-related variation accounts for 2% or less of total variance, their findings relied on automated intensity-based measurements of protein levels using multiplexed immunofluorescence [22]. In contrast, our approach utilizes standardized morphological criteria on H&E-stained sections within defined ROIs, specifically to assess whether depth-related variability may influence TIL scoring.

To the best of our knowledge there are no works that have evaluated TILs variability on depth tissue in regions that accomplish with Salgado’s criteria. To address the existing gaps, this study systematically evaluates three strategies for registering ROIs in serial BC sections. These strategies differ in their use of intermediate slides and aim to determine their impact on longitudinal TIL scoring. Two pathologists conducted TIL scoring on manually annotated ROIs as well as on ROIs generated by the best-performing automated registration strategy, which is selected based on geometric similarity and failure rates relative to the ground truth.

Methods

We present a framework for ROI registration across consecutive BC tissue sections, focusing on registering a pathologist-defined reference ROI from the first to the twelfth (farthest) slide. Three registration strategies, each employing distinct methods for utilizing intermediate slides, are proposed and evaluated.

In this study, pathologists first performed a whole-slide assessment of stromal TILs and then selected a representative ROI whose stromal TIL percentage approximated the overall slide-level score. The ROI was not chosen as a hotspot or region of maximal immune infiltration, but as a standardized sampling area intended to reflect global stromal TIL levels within the tumor.

The proposed method addresses the challenge of consistent ROI registration across multiple tissue sections. The quality control (QC) mechanism in the serial strategy includes adaptive steps to handle tissue preparation artifacts and digitization inconsistencies.

The optimal approach is selected by evaluating geometric similarity metrics alongside the pathologist-defined ground truth, with emphasis on minimizing registration failures. Once the best-performing strategy is identified, two pathologists score TILs on the automatically generated and the manually defined ROIs on the twelfth slide. A statistical analysis evaluates variability, correlation, and concordance between TIL scores derived from these two ROI selection methods.

Additionally, a longitudinal comparative analysis is performed to evaluate TIL scores across consecutive tissue sections by comparing the manual ROI on the first slide with both the manually defined and automatically generated ROIs on the twelfth slide. This analysis assesses the reliability of automated ROI registration and the consistency of TIL assessment across serial sections.

Finally, we evaluate the clinical relevance of automated ROI registration by comparing TIL-based stratification between relapsed and non-relapsed patients using both the first-slide manual ROI and the farthest-slide automated ROI. This analysis is exploratory and intended to assess whether the registration framework preserves previously observed biological associations rather than to establish new clinical endpoints. Consistency is defined as preservation of the same directionality or statistical pattern across both settings, suggesting that the automated workflow maintains clinical signals derived from manual ROI assessment.

For each case, twelve consecutive 2-µm H&E-stained sections were digitized using a PANNORAMIC 250 Flash II scanner (3DHistech, 20× , 0.2433 µm/pixel), resulting in a total of 1248 WSIs. A pathologist selected a relevant ROI for TIL scoring based on established criteria [7]. A fixed ROI was used as a standardized sampling within the same tumor region across non-consecutive serial sections for image registration and TIL quantification. This approach was adopted solely for methodological standardization and was not intended to replace guideline-based whole-slide stromal TIL assessment. The ROI size was defined by the expert consensus of the HUTVC pathologist committee, with a median width of 1.91 mm (interquartile range (IQR): 0.01 mm) and a median height of 1.00 mm (IQR: 0.02 mm). The ROI approximates the linear dimension of a 1 mm TMA core but represents a larger sampled area (), providing a standardized rectangular sampling unit rather than a circular core.

The ROI was not intended to emulate routine whole-slide TIL assessment, but to provide a standardized and repeatable sampling unit for paired comparison across non-consecutive sections.

Once the optimal area was identified, the same ROI was annotated with a rectangular shape on the first and twelfth sections of each specimen using proprietary SlideViewer software developed by 3DHistech. This process resulted in 312 ROI annotations.

The ROIs were then converted to GeoJSON format for analysis in QuPath [23]. Finally, TILs were scored by two pathologists on the manually selected ROIs from the first and twelfth slides, as well as on the automatically generated ROI from the twelfth slide, which was produced by the best-performing registration strategy based on geometric performance metrics and failure rates.

ROI registration strategies and metrics evaluation

We developed and evaluated three distinct strategies for registering ROIs between tissue sections that are farther apart: direct, intermediate, and serial (Fig. 1). Direct approach: single-step registration

The first strategy, the direct approach, creates a direct registration between the first and twelfth slides without using any intermediate slides (Figs. 1a and 2a). The direct approach uses the Virtual Alignment of Pathology Image Series (VALIS) method [20], a state-of-the-art WSI registration algorithm that ranked among the best-performing methods in the AutomatiC Registration Of Breast cAncer Tissue (ACROBAT) 2022 challenge [24, 25]. We specifically adapted VALIS for this application by implementing rigid registration to preserve the rectangular shape of the ROI, in line with the manual annotations provided by the pathologist. Since the tissues analyzed did not exhibit major artifacts and their overall morphology was largely preserved between consecutive sections, rigid registration was sufficient to accurately reposition the ROIs while maintaining their geometric properties. This modification ensures that the pathologist-defined scoring area retains its rectangular shape while being properly aligned on the target slide.

Scaling transformations were deliberately excluded because all WSIs in our dataset have identical resolution and magnification, which eliminates the need for scale adjustments and reduces potential transformation errors. In our implementation, after applying VALIS to register the reference (first) and moving (twelfth) slides, the ROI is established by warping the four vertices of the rectangular ROI contour from the reference slide to the moving slide.

Intermediate approach: two-step registration via midpoint slide

The second strategy, known as the intermediate approach, addresses potential limitations of direct registration by dividing the process into two sequential steps. It uses the automatically generated ROI on the sixth slide as a bridging reference (Figs. 1b and 2b). This approach is designed to mitigate registration errors that can occur due to substantial tissue deformation between distant slides.

By breaking the registration process into smaller, more manageable steps, this method balances computational efficiency with accuracy. Using the sixth slide as an intermediate reference helps reduce the impact of cumulative tissue deformation throughout the sequence of twelve slides.

The procedure is based on the same technical foundation as the direct approach but is carried out in two phases: first, registering slide one (reference) to slide six (moving), and then registering slide six (now serving as reference) to slide twelve (moving). This stepwise approach is particularly valuable in cases where direct registration may fail due to substantial tissue differences between the first and twelfth slides.

Serial approach: progressive registration with quality control

The third strategy, known as the serial approach, is the most comprehensive registration method. In this approach, pairwise registration is conducted across all consecutive slides in the sequence (Figs. 1c and 2c). This approach maximizes registration accuracy through incremental transformations and addresses potential quality issues using an innovative QC mechanism.

The serial approach implements a progressive registration strategy that registers the farthest slide through eleven sequential stages. Each stage uses the output of the previous registration as the reference for the next step: the first phase uses slide one as the reference and slide two as the moving image, the second phase uses slide two as the reference and slide three as the moving image, and so on through the entire sequence. This incremental approach minimizes registration errors by focusing on minor tissue changes between adjacent slides.

Quality Control Innovation: A major advancement in our method is the introduction of a QC mechanism for the serial approach (Fig. 2c). This novel component addresses a critical limitation in existing registration methods by dynamically adapting to potential quality issues in specific slides. The QC mechanism evaluates registration quality at each step and determines whether to use the newly registered slide or to retain the previous reference when quality issues are detected.

To implement this QC phase, we first calculate the median relative Target Registration Error (mrTRE) [20] of the features from the moving slide matched to the features from the reference slide, transformed using the least-squares estimation [26]. In accordance with the VALIS pipeline, we employ the Binary Robust Invariant Scalable Keypoints (BRISK) [27] as the feature detector and the Oxford Visual Geometry Group (VGG) descriptor [28] as the feature descriptor. Features are matched by brute force, with outliers removed using the Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) algorithm [29] and Tukey’s box plot.

If the mrTRE falls below a predefined threshold (t), the QC is passed, and the ROI is computed on the moving WSI, which serves as the reference for the subsequent registration step. Conversely, if the mrTRE exceeds the threshold, indicating poor registration quality, the previous reference WSI is retained, and the next slide becomes the new moving WSI. This adaptive approach ensures that suboptimal slides, potentially affected by tissue preparation artifacts or digitization issues, do not propagate errors through the registration sequence.

We comprehensively evaluated threshold values from 1 to 9 (inclusive) with a step size of 1 to determine the optimal threshold for QC implementation. This thorough parameter optimization provides methodological insights for WSI registration.

Comparative analysis framework

To facilitate a comprehensive evaluation, we integrated an existing neighbor-filtering approach (referred to as NF_naive). This method registers the ROI on the twelfth image based on the first one, using all available serial cuts [20]. Since the order of serial cuts is known in our dataset, this procedure does not rely on ordering based on feature similarity [20].

To optimize computational efficiency while maintaining registration accuracy, we developed a modified approach (NF_filtered). This method applies the neighbor-filtering technique only to the subset of slides in the series of cuts that are not discarded by the optimal serial approach with QC.

Evaluation metrics and clinical relevance

To evaluate the performance of each registration approach, we employed a comprehensive set of metrics that quantify spatial overlap and positional accuracy. Evaluation of TIL scoring variability between automatically generated and manual ROIs

To assess the reliability of automated ROI registration, we evaluated variability in TIL scoring between manually defined and automatically generated ROIs. TIL scores were independently obtained by two pathologists from both types of ROIs. Comparisons were performed at three levels: (1) first pathologist’s scores on manual ROIs vs. scores on automatically generated ROIs, (2) second pathologist’s scores on manual ROIs vs. scores on automatically generated ROIs, and (3) the mean score of both pathologists on manual ROIs vs. the mean score on automatically generated ROIs. This approach allowed us to assess the consistency of automated ROI registration relative to the assessments made by individual and combined pathologists.

Validating the usability of automatically registered ROIs for TIL scoring

After identifying the registration approach with the best geometric metric performance and the lowest failure rate compared to pathologist-defined ROIs, we conducted a comprehensive validation of the usability of automatically generated ROIs for TIL scoring. Two pathologists independently scored TILs on both manually generated and automatically defined ROIs on the twelfth slide. Each ROI was assessed individually and presented in random order to minimize bias, ensuring that pathologists were not influenced by previous scoring decisions.

This validation phase directly addresses the practical utility of automated ROI registration for TIL scoring. By comparing TIL scores between manually defined and automatically generated ROIs, we evaluate whether the proposed registration approach can reliably reproduce the TIL assessments obtained from manual ROIs.

Longitudinal comparative analysis

To assess the impact of ROI registration on TIL scoring across consecutive tissue sections, we performed a longitudinal comparative analysis. TIL scores, computed as the mean of assessments from two pathologists, were compared between the manual ROI on the first slide and both the manually defined and automatically generated ROIs on the twelfth slide. This sequential comparison allowed us to determine whether substantial changes existed in TIL distributions across slides and to evaluate the reliability of automated ROI registration over multiple tissue sections. Comparisons were performed using an independent samples t-test for normally distributed data or Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. The results provide insight into the consistency of TIL scoring and the potential clinical applicability of automated ROI registration for longitudinal studies in serial sections.

Clinical differences of the longitudinal analysis

After assessing whether substantial changes occurred in TIL scores between the manual ROI on the first slide and both the manual and automatically generated ROIs on the twelfth slide, we evaluated whether the automated registration approach preserved the outcome-related differences in TIL concentration. Specifically, we examined whether the differences in TIL concentration between relapsed and non-relapsed patients observed in the ROI of the first slide were also present in the corresponding ROI automatically identified on the twelfth slide.

Through this analysis, we aimed to determine not only whether TIL measurements remain stable across slides from the same sample but also whether the clinical signal associated with TIL levels is preserved when using automatically transferred ROIs. Demonstrating such consistency would support the use of this registration framework in future studies that integrate consecutive sections stained with IHC, enabling automated localization of corresponding regions across slides. This capability could facilitate the characterization of immune cell populations associated with TILs and potentially support more personalized immunotherapy strategies.

To compare the distributions of TILs between patients with and without relapse, we applied an independent samples t-test for normally distributed data or a Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. These tests were performed on the mean TIL scores from the two pathologists, comparing the manual ROI on the first slide with the automatically generated ROI on the twelfth slide.

Results

We evaluated the performance of different strategies for registering ROIs and their impact on the reliability of TIL scoring. Since the selection and registration of ROIs can affect TIL assessment, we systematically examined the following: (i) the performance of ROI registration, (ii) the agreement between manually and automatically generated ROIs, (iii) threshold optimization through disagreement analysis, (iv) longitudinal comparison of TIL scores across serial slides, and (v) the clinical relevance of automated ROI-based TIL scoring in relation to relapse outcomes. Detailed information about the experimental setup and reproducibility is provided in the Supplementary material.

Using the entire series of cuts to find the ROI in the twelfth slide from the ROI established in the first slide (NF_naive) resulted in worse performance (see Supplementary material Table 2) and longer execution time (see Supplementary material Table 3) compared to the direct approach. Similarly, applying the Gatenbee et al. method to the tissues that passed the QC phase (t = 2) of the serial approach (NF_filtered) did not yield any improvement in either performance or execution time compared with the direct strategy.

TIL scoring agreement between manual and automated ROI

After determining that the direct method was the optimal approach, the pathologists noted that the automatically generated ROI where the failure occurred did not adhere to established criteria and could not be utilized for TIL scoring. As a result, the statistical analysis was conducted on 103 out of the 104 cases, with the excluded case corresponding to a patient who experienced a relapse.

The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that TIL scores derived from both manually defined and automatically generated ROIs did not follow a normal distribution for pathologist 1, pathologist 2, or the mean score of both observers (all P < 0.001). Thus, comparisons between ROI selection methods were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. No statistically significant differences in TIL concentrations were observed between manual and automatic ROIs for pathologist 1 (P = 0.68), pathologist 2 (P = 0.35), or for the mean score of both pathologists (P = 0.84).

For pathologist 1, both manual and automatic ROIs resulted a median TIL score of 2 (IQR = 9). For pathologist 2, the manual ROI displayed a median of 3 (IQR = 8), while the automatic ROI showed a median of 5 (IQR = 14). When considering the mean score of both pathologists, the manual ROI had a median of 3.5 (IQR = 8.75), and the automatic ROI had a median of 3.5 (IQR = 11.5).

Spearman correlation analysis revealed a strong monotonic correlation between TIL scores derived from manually defined and automatically generated ROIs. The ρ was 0.853 for pathologist 1, 0.896 for pathologist 2, and 0.923 for the mean score of both pathologists (all P < 0.001). These high correlation values suggest a strong relationship between the two ROI selection methods, although minor differences in individual scores remain. The violin plots in Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrate the TIL density distributions for pathologist 1, pathologist 2, and the mean of the two, demonstrating similar curves for both manual and automatically generated ROIs. This finding reinforces that automated ROI registration yields comparable scoring results across individual and aggregated assessments.

ICC and CCC were calculated to evaluate the reliability and agreement of TIL scores between manual and automatic ROI selection. For pathologist 1, the ICC was 0.936 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.907–0.956) and the CCC was 0.888 (95% CI 0.829–0.926), indicating excellent reliability and high agreement. For pathologist 2, the ICC was 0.810 (95% CI 0.731–0.867) and the CCC was 0.855 (95% CI 0.793–0.899), reflecting good reliability and agreement. The mean score calculated from both pathologists resulted in an ICC of 0.936 (95% CI 0.907–0.956) and a CCC of 0.915 (95% CI 0.877–0.942). This indicates excellent reliability and very high agreement between manual and automated ROI-based TIL scoring.

Longitudinal comparison of TIL scores between first and twelfth slide ROIs

TIL concentrations were compared between the manually define ROI on the first slide and both the manually defined and automatically generated ROIs on the twelfth slide using the Mann–Whitney U test. For each case, the TIL score was calculated as the mean of the two pathologists’ assessments. No significant differences were observed across slides, with P values of 0.79 for the manual vs. automatic comparison, 0.62 for the manual vs. manual comparison, and 0.84 for the automatic vs. manual comparison. The first slide exhibited a mean TIL score of 4 with an IQR of 14, whereas the automatically generated ROI had a mean of 3.5 with an IQR of 11.5, and the manually defined ROI on the twelfth slide had a mean of 3.5 with an IQR of 8.75. These results indicate that ROI registration preserves TIL concentration and yields measurements consistent with manual ROI selection.

Assessment of the clinical relevance of automated ROI-based TIL scoring

The automatically generated ROIs did not result in a significant change in TIL concentration compared to the reference measurements. Therefore, we assessed the clinical relevance of this approach. The Mann–Whitney U test revealed that TIL concentrations significantly differed between patients with relapse and those without relapse on the first slide (P < 0.05). Notably, the same significant difference was also observed when using the automatically transferred ROIs on the twelfth slide (P < 0.05). This consistency indicates that the automated ROI registration preserves the clinically relevant signal associated with relapse status, supporting its potential clinical application in clinical settings.

Discussion

Standard clinical assessment of stromal TILs follows established international guidelines, as detailed in the Background section. Here, representative ROIs were used only as a standardized framework, with findings supporting consistent TIL measurements across serial sections and enabling reproducible downstream analyses; relapse data served as a biological anchor to confirm preservation of outcome-related signals within the automated workflow.

The results of this work shows that the direct method proves optimal for ROI registration, achieving lower failure rates, better geometric accuracy, and faster processing than the alternatives. This method directly transfers the reference ROI from the first to the twelfth slide without processing intermediate sections. This contrasts with conventional 3D reconstruction pipelines [35–37], which require complete serial sections to maintain anatomical consistency and avoid cumulative errors [38, 39]. While sequential methods are essential for precise 3D reconstruction, our direct strategy offers an efficient alternative when dealing with distant slides or incomplete series, thereby reducing execution times and minimizing the risk of error propagation, all while maintaining registration accuracy. This method is particularly beneficial when full 3D reconstruction is not required or feasible.

Although Sect. 3.2 describes the serial QC strategy as a means to maximize accuracy and manage quality issues between adjacent slides, our study found that the direct method outperformed it. This is likely due to the relatively small distance (∼ 24 μ m) between the analyzed slides, which preserves tissue appearance and morphology, making direct alignment straightforward. In scenarios with more widely spaced sections, incremental registration may offer an advantage by gradually accommodating larger morphological differences. However, incremental approaches are prone to error propagation across intermediate slides; although our QC lessens this issue, small accumulated misalignments can still slightly compromise accuracy compared to a single direct registration when sections are closely spaced.

It is noteworthy that failures do not necessarily stem from poor selection of the ROI; rather, they can occur when tissue-level alignment becomes misaligned. This misalignment can happen, for example, if the background estimation of the tissue is inaccurate. In such cases, dark tissue may be confused with border artifacts, while contrast normalization or equalization can over-amplify noise, and subtraction operations on single-channel images may introduce additional artifacts. An example of this issue is depicted in Fig. 3, which shows instances of both failure and success. As illustrated in the preprocessing phase, the reference WSI of the failed case incorrectly detects the foreground (highlighted in white).

The top row illustrates a failed example, which includes the preprocessing stage and the output of the registration module prior to the warping step. In this case, incorrect foreground detection in the reference WSI leads to misalignment. Conversely, the bottom row presents a successful case, where the preprocessing and registration steps effectively identify the tissue regions, resulting in accurate registration. The technical advantages of our ROI registration framework address a key challenge in pathology: variability in TIL scoring [40, 41]. Inter-observer variability has been extensively documented in the literature, with moderate to substantial disagreement often occurring even under standardized conditions [8, 33, 42, 43]. In contrast, the focus of the current work is on intra-observer agreement. Unlike conventional intra-observer studies that examine temporal consistency, our approach assesses consistency across different ROI proposals under the same evaluation conditions. This provides valuable insights into algorithmic performance from a clinical pathology perspective. The most comparable reference is the study by Buisseret et al., in which pathologists assessed TILs on the same tissues on two occasions under unchanged conditions [44].

Beyond intra-observer variability, another significant source of inconsistency arises when evaluating consecutive tissue sections. Although these studies are not specific to TILs, several investigations have documented heterogeneity in histological markers across consecutive slides, indicating that tissue depth can introduce variability in quantitative assessments. Early research by Layfield et al. [45] and Charpin et al. [46] demonstrated that estrogen receptor expression can vary across adjacent BC sections, impacting image analysis quantification. More recently, Rehman et al. [47] reported similar heterogeneity in PD-L1 expression in non-small cell lung cancer, comparing quantitative methods with pathologist scoring. These findings highlight the importance of considering tissue depth and slide-to-slide variability when performing computational assessments. To streamline evaluations like those presented in this scenario, the proposed direct approach can make the process more efficient and can be readily adopted by other researchers.

The results confirm strong intra-observer agreement across both individual pathologists and the mean score. For pathologist 1, the CCC for TIL scoring was 0.888, closely matching the value reported by Buisseret et al. (CCC = 0.85, 95% CI 0.77–0.91) in a repeat assessment scenario after a 3-month interval [44]. For pathologist 2, the CCC was 0.855, and for the mean score of both pathologists, it was 0.915, reflecting excellent reliability. Similarly, the ρ was 0.853 for pathologist 1, 0.896 for pathologist 2, and 0.923 for the mean score, all exceeding the value reported by O’Loughlin et al. (ρ = 0.727), who used a 4-month interval between scoring rounds [33]. Using a clinically relevant threshold of 25% to categorize high TIL levels [33], the was 0.886 for pathologist 1, 0.650 for pathologist 2, and 0.719 for the mean score, indicating near-perfect agreement for pathologist 1, substantial agreement for the mean score, and moderate agreement for pathologist 2, surpassing the previously reported value of 0.53 (range 0.158–0.947) in their study.

Our findings suggest that TIL concentration shows minimal variability across twelve serial sections, supporting the use of consecutive slides for the evaluation of immune subpopulations or other biomarkers in spatially matched regions. In addition, the difference in TIL concentration between relapsed and non-relapsed patients observed on the first slide was also detected in the corresponding ROI automatically identified on the twelfth slide, suggesting preservation of the relevant outcome-related signal across tissue depth. By demonstrating that TIL concentration is stable across tissue levels, we show that consecutive slides can be reliably used to evaluate subpopulations of TILs or other immune cells (e.g., via IHC, FISH, or multiplex staining) in the same standardized region. As explained in the International Immuno-Oncology Biomarker Working Group guidelines, the huge complexity of lymphocytes, both from the standpoint of cell type and activation suggests that molecular characterization of this infiltrate may add both sensitivity and specificity to the predictive value of morphologically defined TILs [7]. Several studies have since reinforced this perspective, noting that the characterization of specific TIL populations, such as the balance between immune effector and pro-tumorigenic cells, is capable of discriminating patients at different risks of relapse and refining the prognostic utility of the global TIL count [6, 9, 48].

It is worth noting that a limitation of this study is the absence of publicly available paired H&E–H&E datasets specifically designed for TIL scoring on consecutive sections. While there are publicly available datasets addressing TIL scoring or related tasks, they are not based on consecutive tissue sections, which limits direct validation of our approach against comparable external resources [41]. This lack of suitable benchmark datasets may be seen as a constraint, but it simultaneously highlights the novelty of our work.

Additionally, HER2-positive and TNBC cases represent only 15% of our cohort, which may limit the strength of conclusions regarding the applicability of our method to these clinically established TIL-relevant subgroups. However, the majority of the cohort includes Luminal A (40.4%) and Luminal B (35.6%) tumors, reflecting the real-world distribution of molecular subtypes in BC, where luminal tumors are the most prevalent [49]. It is well recognized that luminal tumors tend to be relatively immune-cold, exhibiting lower TIL densities and reduced biological heterogeneity compared to HER2-positive and TNBC subtypes [50]. TILs in luminal subtypes remains under active investigation and is not yet fully established [7].

Nevertheless, demonstrating the stability of global TIL concentration across tissue depths is fundamental, as this homogeneity validates the reliable use of serial sections for characterizing specific immune subpopulations via IHC or multiplexed staining. López et al. [51] has demonstrated that even within ’immune-cold’ environments, specific immune markers, such as CD83 or CD21 dendritic cells, show distinct expression patterns between Luminal A and TNBC profiles and are significantly associated with patient outcomes. Therefore, luminal tumors should not be overlooked in immune assessment, as a more granular subclassification in spatially matched serial sections may reveal prognostic signals that the global TIL count alone might miss [51].

The inclusion of luminal tumors thus represents both a limitation and a strength. Although the lower representation of immune-hot subtypes may restrict direct generalization to high-TIL tumors, evaluating methodological reproducibility in predominantly immune-cold tumors allows for assessment under conditions that closely mimic everyday clinical practice. This broader spectrum of immune contexts enhances the external relevance of our findings, ensuring that the proposed methodology is applicable not only to high-TIL tumors but also to the more common luminal BCs.

Overall, the combination of strong performance metrics, short execution time, minimal failure rate, and close agreement between manual and automatically generated ROIs demonstrates the potential for implementing the direct approach in clinical settings. This method may also provide practical advantages in cases where the original slide contains digitization artifacts, such as blurred regions [52], by allowing pathologists to select the ROI on a neighboring section without the needing to re-examine the tissue or manually redefine the ROI.

Pathology variability extends beyond TIL assessment. Lejeune et al. [53] showed that intra-observer variability in immunostained cell quantification could exceed inter-observer differences, particularly for membrane-staining patterns in tissue microarrays. López et al. [54] further demonstrated that manual nuclear marker quantification suffers from variations in staining intensity, cell morphology, and tissue complexity. Although their focus is on nuclear markers, these studies underscore the importance of reproducibility, which depends critically on consistent ROI selection across diverse tissue characteristics.

Our findings highlight the feasibility and reliability of direct ROI registration for TIL assessment in clinical-grade WSIs. Using this method could streamline TIL quantification workflows, support reproducible biomarker evaluation, and enable future integration with multiplex IHC and spatial immune profiling [55, 56]. Importantly, we observed no significant variability in TIL scores across consecutive slides, and the association between TIL score and patient relapse outcome was preserved, showing that the automated approach maintains clinically relevant signals for longitudinal analyses.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the feasibility of using the direct approach for ROI registration, enabling consistent TIL scoring across multiple slides of the same specimen without the need to manually reselect the ROI. For the first time, we investigated longitudinal differences in TIL concentration between the manual ROI on the first slide and both manually defined and automatically generated ROIs on a distant slide. We observed minimal variability in TIL scores, indicating that ROI registration preserves the quantitative characteristics of TIL infiltration across consecutive sections. This approach enhances the robustness and reproducibility of TIL assessment, providing a more efficient workflow for pathologists, and it can also be extended to other histological biomarkers.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate ROI registration specifically for TIL assessment, addressing an important gap in DP. When combined with machine learning or deep learning algorithms, this framework has the potential to further reduce inter- and intra-observer variability, particularly in studies investigating whether tissue depth introduces variability in histological biomarker evaluation.

Future work should extend ROI registration to additional histological techniques, particularly IHC, to enable assessment of TIL subpopulations using specific biomarkers such as CD4, CD8, CD20, and FOXP3 [57–59] . This would broaden the applicability of the direct approach and support a more detailed characterization of the tumor immune microenvironment.
