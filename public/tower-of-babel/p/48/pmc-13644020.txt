Surgical versus medical management of PFAPA (Periodic Fever, Aphthous Stomatitis, Pharyngitis, Adenitis): a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammed Alsabri, Shree Rath, Mohamed Amr Elkarargy, Omar Ahmed Abdelwahab, Aisha Habib Ahmed, Asmaa G. Alwarraqi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodic Fever, Aphthous Stomatitis, Pharyngitis, and Adenitis (PFAPA) syndrome is the most common autoinflammatory periodic fever disorder of childhood. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of surgical and medical treatments for PFAPA using a systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA).MethodsMedline, Embase, and CENTRAL were searched through November 2025. Randomized and non-randomized comparative studies evaluating surgical interventions (tonsillectomy, adenotonsillectomy, tonsillotomy) and medical therapies (corticosteroids, cimetidine, colchicine, and others) in PFAPA were included. The primary outcome was clinical remission or symptom resolution; secondary outcomes included variation with age and duration of disease. Pairwise and network meta-analyses were performed using random-effects models. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2 and ROBINS-I, and certainty of evidence was evaluated using GRADE.ResultsSixteen studies involving 2,048 patients were included. In the primary NMA, no statistically significant difference was observed between pooled surgical and medical management (OR 1.10, 95% CI 0.21–5.69), with very high heterogeneity (I² 97.4%). Exploratory comparisons suggested trends favoring some medical therapies over surgery; however, confidence intervals were wide and the certainty of evidence was very low. Bayesian sensitivity analyses and meta-regressions did not identify age or episode duration as meaningful effect modifiers.ConclusionsCurrent evidence is insufficient to draw reliable comparative conclusions regarding the relative effectiveness of surgical and medical management for PFAPA. Substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, sparse comparative data, and very low-certainty evidence limit interpretation of pooled estimates. Well-designed prospective comparative studies with standardized outcomes are needed to inform treatment decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12969-026-01242-3.

Introduction

PFAPA (Periodic Fever, Aphthous Stomatitis, Pharyngitis, Adenitis) syndrome is the most common autoinflammatory periodic fever syndrome in childhood and remains a frequent therapeutic dilemma in pediatric practice. Its etiology remains unclear, its clinical expression is heterogeneous, and optimal management strategy remains debated [1]. Since its initial description in 1987 [2], PFAPA has been increasingly recognized across diverse populations [3]. Although the condition is often self-limited, its recurrent and predictable inflammatory flares impose a substantial burden on affected children and their families, resulting in impaired quality of life, repeated healthcare encounters, frequent laboratory investigations, and considerable parental anxiety [4, 5].

Epidemiological data indicate that PFAPA is predominantly a disorder of early childhood. In a large population-based cohort of 336 children, nearly 90% developed symptoms before the age of five years, with fewer than 3% presenting after ten years of age [6]. The estimated mean annual incidence is approximately 0.86 per 10,000 children under 18 years, rising to about 2.6 per 10,000 among children younger than five years [6]. Clinically, PFAPA is characterized by recurrent febrile episodes lasting approximately four days and recurring at regular intervals of roughly one month, accompanied by aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, and cervical adenitis [7]. Additional symptoms such as headache, abdominal pain or vomiting, and a fatigue prodrome are frequently reported [3, 7]. For the treating physician, however, the central challenge is usually not diagnostic recognition alone, but choosing among several management strategies that target different clinical goals.

Current treatment options for PFAPA can be broadly divided into three clinical approaches: abortive therapy for individual flares, preventive/interval therapy aimed at reducing attack burden, and surgery intended to induce sustained remission. Acute episodes often respond promptly to a single dose of corticosteroids, typically prednisolone at 1 mg/kg [7–9]. However, while this approach is effective for aborting individual flares, it primarily shortens flare duration and does not consistently reduce long-term attack frequency or overall disease duration. For children with frequent attacks, substantial family burden, or repeated reliance on corticosteroids, clinicians often consider interval medical therapy. Colchicine is commonly used in this setting, particularly when attacks are frequent or when a steroid-sparing strategy is desired, but the supporting evidence is derived largely from retrospective cohorts and other non-randomized studies [8, 9].

Cimetidine has also been used because of its favorable safety profile and ease of administration, yet published results have been inconsistent and the quality of evidence remains limited [7]. Other medical approaches, including montelukast, vitamin D supplementation, probiotics, and biologic agents in selected refractory cases, have been reported, [9], but their role in routine practice remains uncertain because data are sparse, heterogeneous, and often uncontrolled.

A major difficulty in evaluating all PFAPA treatments is the syndrome’s natural history. In the cohort reported by Salazar et al., spontaneous resolution occurred after a mean disease duration of 33.2 months in only approximately 20% of children, whereas 63% continued to experience recurrent episodes at the end of follow-up [7]. Thus, spontaneous remission is clinically important but temporally unpredictable as some children improve without escalation, whereas others remain symptomatic for years. This uncertainty directly influences management decisions and complicates interpretation of observational studies, because apparent improvement after medical therapy or surgery may partly reflect the underlying tendency of PFAPA to remit over time.

These limitations of “on-demand” medical therapy have prompted increasing consideration of surgical interventions as potentially definitive treatment options.

Tonsillectomy, with or without adenoidectomy, has emerged as the most frequently evaluated surgical approach [3, 6, 9, 10]. Observational studies have reported high rates of symptom resolution following surgery. For example, in a cohort of 281 children undergoing tonsillectomy or adenotonsillectomy, 83.3% achieved complete recovery [11]. Similarly, a long-term observational study of 275 patients reported complete remission in 59.6% over a mean follow-up of 54.5 months [12]. Across the literature, reported remission rates following surgical intervention range widely from 64% to 100% [13].

Despite these encouraging findings, the evidence base remains heterogeneous. Variability in diagnostic criteria, follow-up duration, outcome definitions, and study design limits the strength of inference. A prior pooled analysis comparing medical therapy (n = 466) with surgical management (n = 201) demonstrated a substantially higher likelihood of symptom resolution with surgery; however, heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 80.8%, p < 0.01), and postoperative complications, although infrequent, were not negligible (6.6%) [14]. Moreover, important recent evidence was either unavailable or incompletely represented in that synthesis.

Notably, a randomized controlled trial evaluating tonsillotomy versus observation demonstrated short-term efficacy at three months, providing the first randomized evidence supporting partial tonsil surgery as a therapeutic option for PFAPA [15]. In addition, a recent observational comparative study published in 2025 directly examined partial versus total tonsil surgery and reported substantial symptom improvement following partial procedures, further expanding the range of surgical strategies requiring comparative evaluation [16].

From a physician’s perspective, the key clinical question is therefore not simply whether a treatment “works,” but which intervention is most appropriate for the intended therapeutic goal: rapid abortion of a flare, reduction in future flare frequency and severity, or induction of sustained remission. An equally important question is whether tonsil surgery meaningfully shortens overall disease duration or increases the likelihood of durable remission beyond what would be expected with observation, spontaneous remission, or medical therapy. In the absence of universally accepted management guidelines for PFAPA, treatment decisions remain highly variable and are often guided by incomplete or conflicting evidence. Consequently, an updated systematic review incorporating a network meta-analysis is warranted to address this clinician-facing question by (1) integrate recently published randomized and observational studies, including those evaluating tonsillotomy and partial tonsillectomy; (2) compare, both directly and indirectly, the full spectrum of medical and surgical interventions currently reported; and (3) re-estimate relative effects on symptom resolution, recurrence, adverse events, and health-related quality of life. By enabling treatment ranking and exploration of heterogeneity across interventions, an updated network meta-analysis may better inform clinicians, guideline developers, and families regarding the comparative benefits and risks of available management strategies for children with PFAPA.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a systematic review and NMA comparing the effectiveness and safety of surgical versus medical management of PFAPA. The review was specifically structured to address a clinician-facing management question: in patients with PFAPA, when should management remain episodic and supportive, when should interval medical therapy be considered, and when might surgery offer the greatest likelihood of sustained remission? The review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) and PRISMA extension for network meta-analysis guidelines [17] and Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses of Interventions [18]. A protocol was developed a priori and registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (ID: CRD420251182375).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they satisfied the following criteria: the population comprised children or adults diagnosed with PFAPA based on clinical or consensus criteria; interventions included surgical procedures such as tonsillectomy, adenoidectomy, adenotonsillectomy, or tonsillotomy (partial tonsil removal), as well as medical therapies used either as abortive treatment for acute flares (e.g., corticosteroids) or as interval/prophylactic treatment intended to reduce attack frequency, severity, or steroid exposure (e.g., colchicine, cimetidine, montelukast, vitamin D, biologics, probiotics, or other preventive regimens). Comparators could be any eligible intervention, a placebo, or observation/no treatment. Because the included interventions were intended to achieve different clinical objectives, outcomes were grouped a priori into clinically meaningful domains: (1) disease-modifying outcomes, including complete or partial remission and time to remission; (2) preventive outcomes, including changes in attack frequency, attack interval, and episode severity; and (3) flare-abortive outcomes, including duration of attacks and need for rescue treatment. Secondary outcomes included adverse events, quality of life, steroid dependence, the need for additional rescue interventions, and health service–related outcomes such as caregiver burden. Eligible study designs encompassed randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, cohort studies, case-control studies, provided that they included a comparator group permitting estimation of relative treatment effects.

We excluded PFAPA-like syndromes not fulfilling PFAPA diagnostic criteria, non-original research (e.g., reviews, editorials), case reports, single-arm case series, animal studies, and studies lacking extractable quantitative data relevant to our outcomes. Non-comparative studies were excluded from the comparative synthesis because they do not allow valid estimation of relative effects in pairwise or network meta-analysis and are particularly vulnerable to confounding by the spontaneous remission characteristic of PFAPA. This criterion may reduce representation of some commonly used medical therapies, especially colchicine and other preventive strategies in the quantitative network, but was necessary to preserve interpretability of comparative estimates.

Information sources and searching

A comprehensive search strategy was developed and implemented to identify both published and unpublished studies. Three major electronic databases were searched: Medline, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Embase, without language or date restrictions. The final search was completed on 4 November, 2025.

Additionally, we conducted citation tracking (forward and backward snowballing) of all included studies, reviewed reference lists, clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP), and manually searched Google Scholar and ResearchGate to identify any grey literature or relevant studies not indexed in the main databases. No restrictions were applied regarding geographical location or publication status.

The full electronic search strategy is available in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection

All retrieved records were screened independently by two reviewers (M.A.E.and S.R.) in a two-step process. First, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Then, full-texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed against the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus; unresolved conflicts were arbitrated by a third reviewer (M.A.). The study selection process was conducted using Rayyan website [19].

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted variables included:

Study characteristics (first author, year of publication, country, study design, sample size), population characteristics (age, sex, diagnostic criteria, and comorbidities), intervention details (type of surgery, medical therapy type and dose, follow-up duration) and outcomes of interest (resolution rate, frequency of attacks, episode duration, recurrence, and adverse events)).

Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or referral to a third reviewer. In cases where relevant data were missing or unclear, study authors were contacted for clarification. If no response was received, the study was excluded from quantitative synthesis of the relevant outcome but retained for qualitative assessment when appropriate.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was independently assessed by two reviewers using validated tools appropriate for each study design to ensure rigorous evaluation of internal validity. Randomized controlled trials were appraised using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) [20] tool across its five domains: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk,” and an overall judgment was derived following Cochrane guidance. Non-randomized comparative studies were evaluated using ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions) [21], which examines seven domains: confounding, participant selection, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Ratings were categorized as “low,” “moderate,” “serious,” or “critical” risk of bias. For both tools, disagreements between reviewers were resolved by consensus or consultation with a senior reviewer. These tools were selected because they are recommended for comparative intervention studies and have been used in prior published evidence syntheses in this field [22, 23].

Data synthesis

All quantitative analyses were performed using both pairwise and network meta-analytic methods within a frequentist framework to integrate direct and indirect evidence across all eligible surgical and medical interventions. Because the included treatments were intended to achieve different clinical goals, we synthesized outcomes by domain and did not assume that abortive, preventive, and remission-inducing effects were interchangeable. Accordingly, only clinically comparable endpoints were pooled within the same analysis. For the network meta-analysis of the primary outcome, remission and symptom resolution were treated as equivalent disease-modifying endpoints. For each comparison with ≥ 2 studies, conventional pairwise meta-analyses were first conducted using random-effects models (DerSimonian–Laird estimator) to obtain pooled effect estimates and assess between-study heterogeneity using the I² statistic, τ², and Cochran’s Q test. A multivariate random-effects network meta-analysis was then constructed to simultaneously compare all treatment modalities, assuming a common heterogeneity parameter across the network. Effect measures were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals for dichotomous outcomes and mean differences (MD) or standardized mean differences (SMD) for continuous outcomes. Network geometry plots were used to visualize available treatment nodes and comparison loops. Due to sparse data for individual medical therapies, certain interventions with limited direct comparisons (specifically glucocorticoids and cimetidine) were grouped into a composite “medical therapy” node to maintain network connectivity; this approach was considered exploratory given differences in mechanism and clinical intent. Global inconsistency was assessed using the design-by-treatment interaction model, while local inconsistency was evaluated through node-splitting, comparing direct and indirect evidence for each contrast. Treatment hierarchy was derived using P-scores, the frequentist analogue of SUCRA, to estimate the probability that each intervention was the most effective or safest option. Observation/no treatment was considered a clinically important comparator whenever available, particularly for remission outcomes, because spontaneous remission is a key source of bias in non-randomized PFAPA studies. Pre-specified subgroup analyses evaluated the influence of age, type of surgery (total vs. partial tonsillectomy), type of medical therapy, study design, and follow-up duration. Sensitivity analyses included the exclusion of high-risk-of-bias studies, restriction to randomized trials, removal of influential outliers, and comparison of fixed- versus random-effects models to test robustness.

Publication bias and small-study effects were examined using funnel plots, Egger’s regression, and comparison-adjusted funnel plots for the network [24, 25]. All analyses were conducted using R (netmeta, metafor) and Stata (network suite), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Certainty of evidence

We assessed the certainty of evidence for all outcomes using the GRADE approach [26], considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. For each outcome, we also conducted subgroup analyses based on study design (randomized controlled trials [RCTs] vs. non-randomized studies), and applied GRADE separately for each subgroup. In accordance with Cochrane guidelines, the main Summary of Findings table presented in the main manuscript was limited to the seven most critical outcomes. For each of these, we reported the highest certainty of evidence either from a subgroup or from the overall analysis that provided for each of those outcomes. All GRADE ratings, along with effect estimates for both the overall analyses and subgroups, were compiled and presented in a comprehensive Summary of Findings table available in the supplementary material.

Results

Screening and study selection

A total of 1,216 articles were obtained on an initial search, of which 943 were screened following removal of duplicates. Twenty-three articles were evaluated via a full-text screening. Finally, 16 studies were included for qualitative and quantitative synthesis [27–42] (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The meta-analysis included 16 studies comprising 2,048 pediatric patients with PFAPA syndrome, conducted across Europe, North America, the Middle East, and Asia between 1988 and 2025. PFAPA diagnosis was generally based on Marshall or modified Marshall criteria or the EUROFEVER/PRINTO classification, with systematic exclusion of alternative periodic fever syndromes such as familial Mediterranean fever, hyper-IgD syndrome, and cyclic neutropenia. Across studies, inclusion criteria consistently required recurrent febrile episodes with symptom-free intervals and normal growth and development, reflecting a largely homogeneous clinical phenotype.

Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable across studies. The mean age at enrollment ranged from approximately 1.4 to 7.8 years, with most cohorts centered between 4 and 6 years, consistent with early childhood onset. A male predominance was observed in most studies, with males accounting for roughly 50–65% of participants. Where reported, a positive family history of PFAPA or recurrent fever syndromes ranged from 7% to 63%. Baseline disease burden was substantial, with annual fever episode frequencies commonly between 8 and 13 episodes, and mean episode durations of about 3–5 days (Supplementary Tables 2, 3).

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was assessed according to study design. Observational and non-randomized studies were evaluated using the ROBINS-I framework. Overall, most observational studies were judged to have low to moderate risk of bias, with the most frequent concerns arising from residual confounding (D1) and incomplete or inconsistently reported outcome data (D5). Two studies (Chong et al. 2025 and Feder et al. 2009) were rated as having serious overall risk of bias, primarily due to issues related to participant selection, confounding, and selective reporting. In contrast, several large contemporary cohorts demonstrated robust methodology with low risk across most domains, supporting the internal validity of the pooled estimates.

Randomized controlled trials were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, covering bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Two trials (Renko et al. 2007 and Lantto et al. 2024) were judged to be at low risk of bias across all domains, reflecting appropriate randomization, minimal missing data, and objective outcome assessment. One trial (Garavello et al. 2009) was rated as having some concerns, mainly related to deviations from intended interventions and limited reporting of allocation concealment. No randomized study was judged to be at high risk of bias overall (Supplementary Figs. 1–4).

Primary outcome

Across the included studies, a total of 2,048 patients contributed data comparing surgical intervention with medical therapy for PFAPA. The network meta-analysis framework is used as it integrates direct and indirect evidence across multiple treatments to provide a comprehensive estimate of relative effectiveness, allowing clinicians to compare options even when head-to-head trials are limited. This method allows standardization of effect modifiers such as age, severity, and diagnostic criteria. Given the limited number of studies and sparse data for individual medical therapies, glucocorticoids and cimetidine were combined into a single “medical therapy” node to preserve network connectivity and improve statistical stability. We acknowledge that these agents have distinct therapeutic roles (acute flare abortion vs. prophylaxis/remission) and differing levels of evidence, particularly as cimetidine has variable and uncertain efficacy and is not considered a standard contemporary therapy. Therefore, this grouping introduces clinical heterogeneity and indirectness, and results from this node should be interpreted as exploratory.

The primary network meta-analysis comparing pooled medical therapy with surgery did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in treatment effect (OR 1.10, 95% CI 0.21–5.69, P = 0.91), indicating that, on average, neither approach conferred a clear advantage. However, the wide confidence intervals and very high between-study heterogeneity (I² = 97.4%, τ² = 6.32) highlight substantial variability across trials, underscoring uncertainty in the pooled estimate and the need for cautious interpretation.

Exploratory analyses examining specific medical therapies revealed potential differences in efficacy. Comparisons of surgery with glucocorticoid therapy suggested a trend towards benefit for glucocorticoids (OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.09–1.49, P = 0.16), whereas comparisons with cimetidine indicated a similar trend favoring cimetidine (OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.03–1.57, P = 0.13). Both analyses were characterized by high heterogeneity (I² 95.7% for glucocorticoids; I² 91.1% for cimetidine) and wide confidence intervals, reflecting substantial variation between studies. This wide variation likely reflects multiple sources of clinical and methodological heterogeneity, including differences in intervention strategies, patient characteristics, and particularly follow-up duration. Follow-up periods varied substantially across studies, ranging from only a few months to more than a decade. Given the self-limiting nature of PFAPA and the possibility of spontaneous remission over time, studies with longer follow-up are more likely to capture remission events regardless of intervention, whereas shorter studies may primarily reflect immediate treatment effects. Consequently, variability in follow-up duration likely represents a major contributor to the extreme heterogeneity observed in the pooled analyses.

While the P-scores suggested a higher probability of benefit for the medical therapies in these exploratory comparisons (Gluco P = 0.92; Cimetidine P = 0.93), these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence as results cannot be ranked so cautious interpretation (Table 1).

Network meta-analysis of surgery vs. medical therapies

The P-scores provide a probabilistic ranking of treatments, with higher values indicating a greater likelihood of benefit. In the primary analysis, pooled medical therapy had similar P-scores (0.45) in comparison to the gold standard approach (surgery p = 0.55), reflecting the absence of a clear advantage. In the exploratory analyses, glucocorticoids and cimetidine had higher P-scores (0.92 and 0.93, respectively), suggesting potential superiority over surgery, although these estimates are hypothesis-generating due to small sample sizes and high heterogeneity. Study-level contributions (weights) indicate how much each study influenced the pooled estimates; in the primary network, the two studies with the highest impact were Erdogan et al. 2016 and Kalayci et al. 2023 (weights ~ 0.11 each), highlighting their relatively larger sample sizes and lower variance. Clinically, the odds ratios from the primary analysis were near unity (OR 1.10), indicating comparable outcomes between surgery and medical therapy, whereas the exploratory analyses suggested that glucocorticoids (OR 0.36) and cimetidine (OR 0.23) may reduce the likelihood of adverse outcomes relative to surgery, though uncertainty remains very high shown by wide confidence intervals (Fig. 2A-C).

Secondary outcomes

We explored the potential influence of study-level mean age on the comparative effectiveness of Medical therapy versus Surgery using a Bayesian random-effects meta-regression. In this approach, the observed effect size (log odds ratio for each study) is modeled as arising from an underlying true effect that varies between studies, allowing us to account for between-study heterogeneity. Mean study age was included as a covariate to examine whether differences in patient age across studies could explain variability in outcomes. Bayesian estimation provides a full posterior distribution for each parameter, yielding credible intervals that reflect the uncertainty in the estimates, especially important given the small number of studies. This approach allows us to explore potential effect modifiers while preserving the clinical interpretability of the odds ratios. The posterior estimate for age was − 0.33 on the log OR scale, corresponding to a back-transformed OR of 0.72 (95% credible interval 0.17–3.20), suggesting that increasing age had no meaningful effect on the odds of a favorable outcome with Medical treatment relative to Surgery. Between-study heterogeneity remained high (sd = 3.52, 95% CrI 2.20–5.44), reflecting substantial variation in effect sizes across trials (Table 2).

We fitted another model exploring the influence of the episode duration by the comparative effectiveness of Medical therapy versus Surgery. The Bayesian meta-regression estimated a coefficient of 0.12 on the log odds ratio scale, corresponding to a back-transformed OR of 1.13 (95% credible interval 0.19–6.21) (Table 3). Clinically, this suggests that longer or shorter episode durations do not meaningfully change the odds of successful outcomes with Medical therapy compared to Surgery (Fig. 3A, B). These results are exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously, given the small number of studies [9] and residual heterogeneity, but they indicate that episode duration is unlikely to be a major modifier of treatment effect.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis as a sensitivity check, using the same dataset but without covariates. While the primary analysis employed a frequentist network meta-analysis to compare treatments, the Bayesian approach allowed exploration of the effect estimates under different hierarchical assumptions and priors. Across five Bayesian models with varying prior specifications, the ORs for the effect of medication relative to surgery ranged from 1.04 to 1.09 (95% credible intervals overlapping 1), consistent with the primary network meta-analysis, indicating no clinically meaningful difference in outcomes between interventions, and the 95% credible intervals consistently included the null effect. Estimates of between-study heterogeneity were stable, suggesting that our findings are robust to reasonable variations in prior assumptions. This confirms that the meta-analytic conclusions regarding the relative efficacy of medical versus surgical treatment are not sensitive to prior selection, supporting the credibility of our exploratory Bayesian framework (Supplementary Table 4).

All 95% credible intervals back-transformed similarly; they all include 1, confirming no clinically meaningful difference between medical treatment and surgery.

Publication bias / small-study effects

We assessed potential publication bias using both visual inspection of the funnel plot and a formal statistical test. The funnel plot for the network meta-analysis comparing medication versus surgery appeared somewhat asymmetric, suggesting the possibility of small-study effects (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, the Begg’s test for funnel plot asymmetry did not detect statistically significant bias (t = 0.60, df = 8, p = 0.568), and the bias estimate was 2.30 (SE 3.85). Residual heterogeneity was high (τ² = 41.41), reflecting variability across studies. Taken together, with the test results and the funnel plot asymmetry it is not possible to conclude publication bias.

Certainty of evidence (GRADE)

We assessed the certainty of evidence using the GRADE framework, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias for the primary and exploratory comparisons.

For the primary comparison between surgery and pooled medical therapy, the evidence was rated ●○○○ (very low certainty) due to very high heterogeneity (I² = 97.4%) and wide confidence intervals (OR 1.10, 95% CI 0.21–5.69), indicating comparable outcomes on average. The exploratory comparisons of surgery with glucocorticoids and cimetidine were rated ●○○○ (very low certainty) owing to small sample sizes, high heterogeneity (I² > 90%), and wide confidence intervals, with P-scores suggesting potential benefit for glucocorticoids (0.92) and cimetidine (0.93) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this systematic review and network meta-analysis, including 16 studies and 2,048 PFAPA patients, no statistically significant superiority was observed for surgical versus pooled medical management of PFAPA (OR 1.10, 95% CI 0.21–5.69, I² = 97.4%, very low certainty). Exploratory analyses suggested a non-significant trend favoring medical management overall compared with surgery (glucocorticoids: OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.09–1.49, I² = 95.7%; cimetidine: OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.03–1.57, I² = 91.1%); however, these findings reflect heterogeneous interventions with differing therapeutic targets. Glucocorticoids are primarily used for acute flare control rather than remission induction, and cimetidine is supported by limited and heterogeneous evidence and is not commonly used in contemporary practice. Estimates were imprecise and of very low certainty. Bayesian meta-regressions indicated that neither age nor episode duration meaningfully modified treatment effects.

In the primary network comparison, surgical management (tonsillectomy with or without adenoidectomy or partial tonsillectomy) did not show a statistically significant difference in odds of complete symptom resolution compared with pooled medical therapy (OR 1.10, 95% CI 0.21–5.69). There was very high between-study heterogeneity (I² = 97.4%) and the certainty of evidence was very low. Traditional meta-analyses have often concluded that tonsillectomy may be more effective than medical management in achieving remission. An earlier pooled analysis reported apparent superiority of surgery over cimetidine and antibiotics in reducing persistent symptoms [43]. However, antibiotics are not considered a standard or evidence-based treatment for PFAPA, and their inclusion likely reflects historical or non-specific management approaches. In addition, the observed superiority over cimetidine should be interpreted cautiously, given the limited and heterogeneous evidence supporting cimetidine efficacy and its declining role in contemporary practice. PFAPA is an autoinflammatory syndrome with dysregulated innate immune activation centered on oropharyngeal lymphoid tissue [44]. Surgical removal of tonsillar and adenoidal tissue may interrupt local immune signaling, thereby reducing episodic inflammatory triggers [45]. Medical therapies target systemic inflammatory pathways: glucocorticoids suppress acute cytokine release; H2 antagonists like cimetidine may modulate T-cell responses; and colchicine disrupts microtubule-mediated neutrophil chemotaxis [46]. The lack of clear superiority in our pooled estimate likely reflects the heterogeneity in immune responses among patients and the natural history of PFAPA, which often resolves spontaneously with age. Furthermore, selection bias is common in surgical cohorts: children selected for tonsillectomy often have more frequent, severe, or medically refractory episodes, which may inflate apparent success rates without proper randomization [47]. The natural history of PFAPA also affects comparative effectiveness results. Spontaneous remission is well documented in pediatric cohorts and was observed in roughly half of control patients in the Renko trial within six months, indicating that a significant proportion of children will experience disease resolution without intervention [40]. This inherent tendency towards self-resolution attenuates between-group contrasts over time, particularly in studies with shorter follow-up or those that do not account for remission rates in medically managed populations. The absence of clear superiority suggests that choice between surgery and medical management should be individualized. For children with high episode burden or poor steroid response, surgical intervention may remain reasonable given supportive evidence from some comparative analyses, but parents must be counseled regarding variable effectiveness and surgical risks. For others, medical management may be preferable, particularly when symptoms are less frequent or quality-of-life impact is limited [48]. An important contributor to heterogeneity is the variability in diagnostic criteria used across included studies. Although all studies evaluated patients diagnosed with PFAPA, at least four different diagnostic frameworks were applied, including the original Marshall criteria, modified Marshall criteria, and more contemporary classification systems. Differences in diagnostic requirements and exclusion criteria may result in the inclusion of patients with varying disease phenotypes, severity profiles, and risks of spontaneous remission. Consequently, some of the observed variability in treatment effects may reflect differences in the underlying study populations rather than true differences in intervention efficacy.

Exploratory network comparisons suggested glucocorticoid therapy may have more favorable odds than surgery (OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.09–1.49), but the effect estimate was imprecise and associated with high heterogeneity (I² =95.7%), yielding very low certainty.

Routine clinical practice data indicate that single-dose corticosteroids abort PFAPA flares in 85–95% of patients, with rapid symptom relief [49]. However, glucocorticoids do not consistently prevent recurrence and may in some cases shorten the asymptomatic interval [50]. These findings align with clinical observations, demonstrating robust acute flare control but limited long-term remission [51]. Glucocorticoids potently suppress innate and adaptive inflammatory cascades (IL-1, TNF-α, IL-6), rapidly terminating fever and mucosal inflammation. However, they do not address underlying dysregulated inflammasome activity and may downregulate counter-regulatory immune checkpoints, possibly shortening inter-episode intervals [52, 53]. While not curative, glucocorticoids remain effective abortive treatments with rapid relief for acute flares. They may be preferred as first-line therapy when quick symptom termination is desired, particularly for patients whose quality of life is significantly impacted during episodes. Decision-making should balance symptomatic benefit against the potential for increased recurrence frequency [54].

Comparisons indicated a trend favoring cimetidine over surgery (OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.03–1.57), but confidence intervals were wide and certainty was very low. Cimetidine is an immunomodulatory H2 receptor antagonist historically used for PFAPA prophylaxis. Early studies reported variable efficacy (approximately 25–43%), but its prophylactic effect has been inconsistent across cohorts, with some registry populations showing minimal usage and limited benefit [55]. A previous RCT comparing cimetidine to colchicine suggested both drugs are effective in reducing febrile attack frequency, though with no superiority [56]. Case reports also demonstrate reduced febrile attacks following cimetidine therapy [57]. Cimetidine may suppress T-cell activation and chemotaxis, modulating immune responses during the inter-episode period, though its immunologic effects are modest compared with targeted anti-cytokine strategies. Variability in response across studies suggests heterogeneity in individual immune profiles and genetic predispositions (such as inflammasome gene variants) [58].

Importantly, colchicine could not be incorporated into the network despite its widespread use as a preventive treatment strategy. Therefore, the present analysis should not be interpreted as a comparison of surgery against all major medical treatment options currently used in practice. Given the increasing adoption of colchicine as a steroid-sparing and attack-reducing therapy, direct comparative studies evaluating colchicine against surgical approaches may have greater clinical relevance than comparisons involving less commonly used therapies such as cimetidine.

Bayesian meta-regression did not identify age (OR 0.72, 95% CrI 0.17–3.20) or episode duration (OR 1.13, 95% CrI 0.19–6.21) as significant effect modifiers for medical versus surgical treatment effectiveness. PFAPA typically presents before age 5 and often resolves spontaneously in adolescence [59]. Prior studies indicate that older age at intervention may correlate with longer disease duration and reduced spontaneous remission, but robust effect modifications by age or episode characteristics are not consistently reported in studies [60]. The lack of modification suggests that immunologic and inflammatory drivers in PFAPA are relatively stable across early childhood, with neither age nor episode length dramatically altering responsiveness to medical versus surgical approaches. Genetic and immunophenotypic variations may be stronger determinants of treatment response [61].

This study has several important strengths. It represents one of the most comprehensive and up-to-date syntheses comparing surgical and medical management of PFAPA, incorporating both randomized controlled trials and contemporary observational studies published up to November 2025. By applying a network meta-analysis framework, we were able to integrate direct and indirect evidence across multiple treatment modalities, including newer surgical approaches such as partial tonsillectomy and tonsillotomy, which have not been systematically evaluated in earlier reviews. Several limitations must also be acknowledged. Most notably, the certainty of evidence for the primary outcome was very low, driven by extreme between-study heterogeneity, wide confidence intervals, and reliance on non-randomized studies. Surgical and medical cohorts differed substantially in baseline disease severity, prior treatment exposure, and follow-up duration, introducing confounding and selection bias that could not be fully addressed despite advanced analytic techniques. Outcome definitions were inconsistent across studies, ranging from short-term absence of febrile episodes to long-term complete remission, and follow-up periods varied widely, limiting comparability and inflating heterogeneity. In addition, surgical interventions were not uniform, with studies including total tonsillectomy, adenotonsillectomy, and partial tonsil procedures, which may have distinct biological effects but were necessarily pooled to preserve statistical power. The small number of randomized trials, many with limited sample sizes and short follow-up, further constrained precision and precluded definitive conclusions. Incomplete reporting of patient-centered outcomes such as quality of life, caregiver burden, and long-term adverse effects limited our ability to fully assess the broader clinical impact of each management strategy. Additionally, emerging medical therapies such as ketotifen and probiotics could not be evaluated due to lack of comparative data, representing an important gap for future research. An important methodological consideration is the grouping of glucocorticoids and cimetidine into a single node in the network analysis. These therapies differ substantially in mechanism and clinical use, with glucocorticoids primarily used for acute flare resolution and cimetidine historically used as a prophylactic agent with inconsistent efficacy. This grouping was necessary due to sparse comparative data but introduces clinical heterogeneity and indirectness. In particular, the limited and uncertain effectiveness of cimetidine, along with its declining role in contemporary practice, may influence pooled estimates. Therefore, results involving the combined medical therapy node should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating. Additionally, we were unable to evaluate the effect of disease duration at treatment initiation on remission outcomes, as this variable was inconsistently reported across included studies. This represents an important limitation, given that PFAPA may undergo spontaneous remission over time, and timing of intervention could influence observed treatment effects. We were also unable to evaluate the influence of geographic background on clinical phenotype or treatment response, as regional data were inconsistently reported and insufficient for meaningful subgroup analysis. This is an important consideration, as PFAPA presentation and management practices may vary across populations. An additional limitation is the absence of colchicine from the quantitative network analysis. Although colchicine is among the most commonly used preventive therapies for PFAPA in contemporary clinical practice, most available studies were non-comparative and therefore did not meet eligibility criteria for comparative meta-analysis. Consequently, our findings do not inform the relative effectiveness of surgery versus colchicine, which is arguably one of the most clinically relevant treatment decisions faced by physicians managing patients with recurrent or burdensome disease. This limitation reduces the direct applicability of our results to routine clinical practice and highlights an important evidence gap requiring adequately designed comparative studies. A particularly important source of heterogeneity was the marked variation in follow-up duration across studies. Follow-up periods ranged from approximately 2–3 months to more than 12 years, resulting in substantial differences in the opportunity to observe remission, recurrence, and long-term disease evolution. Because PFAPA frequently undergoes spontaneous resolution over time, studies with longer follow-up may capture remission unrelated to the intervention itself, whereas shorter studies primarily assess short-term treatment response. This variability complicates direct comparisons between studies and may have been a major contributor to the extreme heterogeneity observed in the primary analyses. Variability in PFAPA diagnostic criteria across studies represents an additional limitation. Included studies used multiple diagnostic frameworks, each with differing levels of sensitivity and specificity. As PFAPA remains a clinical diagnosis without a definitive biomarker, differences in case definitions may have resulted in the inclusion of clinically heterogeneous populations. This diagnostic variability introduces indirectness and may have contributed to the substantial heterogeneity observed across analyses, thereby limiting the comparability of pooled estimates.

This study highlights several critical gaps in the PFAPA evidence base that should guide future research. There is a clear need for large, multicenter randomized controlled trials directly comparing standardized surgical approaches (such as total tonsillectomy versus tonsillotomy) with clearly defined medical regimens, using consistent diagnostic criteria and long-term follow-up. Future trials should incorporate uniform outcome definitions, including complete remission, annual attack frequency, steroid exposure, and validated quality-of-life measures, to improve comparability across studies.

In parallel, mechanistic and translational studies are needed to better define PFAPA endotypes and identify predictors of treatment response, such as immunologic, genetic, or biomarker profiles that distinguish patients more likely to benefit from surgical versus medical intervention. Health-economic evaluations and patient-reported outcome studies are also warranted to assess the broader impact of treatment strategies on caregiver burden, healthcare utilization, and cost-effectiveness. Addressing these gaps will be essential to move beyond empiric management and toward precision-based, evidence-driven care for children with PFAPA [62].

Conclusion

In this systematic review and network meta-analysis of 16 studies involving 2,048 patients with PFAPA syndrome, no statistically significant difference was observed between surgical and pooled medical management for the primary outcome of clinical remission or overall symptom resolution, representing a disease-modifying endpoint (OR 1.10, 95% CI 0.21–5.69, I² = 97.4%, very low certainty). This outcome reflects sustained resolution of PFAPA episodes rather than short-term flare control or reduction in attack frequency, and therefore may not fully capture the therapeutic effects of interventions primarily targeting acute episodes, such as glucocorticoids. Exploratory comparisons suggested trends favoring glucocorticoids and cimetidine over surgery, but these findings were imprecise and hypothesis-generating. Overall, current evidence is insufficient to determine the relative effectiveness of surgical versus medical management for PFAPA. The available comparative data are limited by substantial clinical heterogeneity, methodological variability, and very low certainty of evidence, precluding reliable treatment ranking or inference regarding comparative efficacy. Furthermore, the absence of colchicine from the comparative network represents a major evidence gap, limiting the applicability of the present findings to one of the most common treatment decisions encountered in clinical practice. Consequently, treatment decisions should remain individualized until adequately powered comparative studies with standardized outcome definitions become available.
