Latinos’ beliefs regarding the role played by nonmedical factors in the quality of Alzheimer’s disease care: Findings from a NYC community-based sample
Authors: María Cabán, Courtney Brown-Bradley, John B Wetmore, Ruth Ottman, Karolynn Siegel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLatinos represent the fastest-growing subpopulation in the United States and are expected to experience the steepest increase in the coming decades in adults 65 and older living with Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, they also have a higher likelihood of delayed diagnosis, greater difficulty in accessing specialist referrals, treatments and support services and have fewer long-term and nursing care options than non-Latino Whites.A New York City community-based sample of Latinos completed qualitative interviews in English (63%) or Spanish (37%). We investigated participants’ beliefs regarding Latinos’ access to quality AD-related care. Data were coded by three team members using ATLAS.ti and thematic analysis was conducted by the senior qualitative team members.The results are organized along the care continuum from diagnosis through medical, supportive, and long-term care. The data revealed that participants (n = 155) believed a combination of nonmedical factors contributed to Latinos being diagnosed at a more advanced stage of AD and receiving poorer quality of care once diagnosed than non-Latino Whites. These included: limited financial assets, restricted health insurance coverage, cultural values and tendencies, limited availability of providers who understood their background and experiences or spoke Spanish, and to a lesser extent prejudice or discrimination.These findings are important because expectations of poor care may deter care seeking or once diagnosed may influence patients’ level of engagement in care and treatment adherence. They have implications for enhancing patient-centered care for Latinos with AD as it emphasizes the incorporation of their perspectives when assessing the quality of care being delivered.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Social determinants of health, Quality of care, Latinos

Introduction

The number of adults 65 years of age and older living with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) in the United States (US) is projected to reach 13.8 million in 2060 (Alzheimer’s Association, 2024). Of these, 3.2 million will be Latinos1 (Matthews et al., 2019). Among adults 65 and older, Latinos are about one and a half times as likely to have AD or other dementias as Whites (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025a). Numerous minority-White disparities have been documented with regard to AD diagnosis and care. These neurodisparities are a major challenge to achieving equity in brain health (American Brain Foundation, 2024).

With regard to Latinos specifically, they are more likely than Whites to have a delayed diagnosis with one study finding an average delay of 43.8 months compared to 31.2 months for Whites (Lin et al., 2021). A delayed diagnosis often means entering care with more significant cognitive impairment which can limit treatment opportunities to slow disease progression (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025b; Rasmussen & Langerman, 2019). For example, diagnosis at the stage of mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to AD is necessary to be eligible for the new anti-amyloid medications donanemab (Kisunla™) and lecanemab (Leqembi®) (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025b). Latinos are also less likely than Whites to have access to dementia specialist care during the initial assessment and diagnostic process (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025a; Aranda et al., 2021). In a scoping review of the quality of care for AD and related dementias (ADRD) literature from 2000 to 2022 (Hinton et al., 2024), the included studies that reported findings separately by race/ethnicity found Latinos were generally less likely to be prescribed anti-dementia medications, more likely to discontinue those medications, more likely to receive antipsychotics, have higher hospitalization rates (particularly at the end of life), less likely to have an advanced care plan, and less likely to receive hospice care. Other research has found that Latinos are more likely to have difficulties accessing treatments and support services (Aranda et al., 2021; de Marco, 2024; Quiroz et al., 2022) and receive poorer quality home health care (HHC) (Ma et al., 2025).

Using 2016 data from several national sources, Ma et al. (2025) examined how for those who received care from an HHC with a quality rating, the ratings varied across racial and ethnic groups among adults age 65 and older with and without dementia. They reported that among those with dementia, Latinos were more likely than Whites to receive care from low-quality agencies (20.95% vs. 12.61% respectively). As AD progresses, most patients will need residential care or intensive nursing care at home. Nursing home facilities where Latinos with dementia reside have tended to provide lower quality of care than those serving primarily Whites (Aranda et al., 2021; Rivera-Hernandez et al., 2019, 2020, 2022). For example, using multiple sources of data from 2014, an assessment of the quality of care among 268,181 Medicare beneficiaries with ADRD at 13,532 nursing homes found that Latinos were more likely than Whites to receive care in nursing homes that were under-resourced and provided a lower quality of care (Rivera-Hernandez et al., 2019). Further, they were significantly less likely than Whites to be in facilities with Alzheimer’s special care units and had significantly higher 30-day rehospitalization rates. Using 2016 data from more than a million long-term residents from more than 15,000 nursing homes, the quality of care received by Medicare beneficiaries with ADRD with stays in facilities of more than 100 days was assessed (Rivera-Hernandez et al., 2022). Compared with Whites, Latinos were significantly less likely to be in a facility with an Alzheimer’s unit or a 4- or 5-star quality rating. While the studies discussed above have documented White-Latino disparities in AD care, they have generally done so at a single point in the care continuum.

Social determinants of health (SDOH), “… the nonmedical factors that influence health outcomes” (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024), have been implicated in Latino-White care disparities across the continuum of AD care (Adkins-Jackson et al., 2023; Alzheimer’s Association, 2021; Cha et al., 2021). SDOH include cultural (e.g., values, beliefs about illnesses, health practices), social (e.g., race, income, education, employment, health insurance, neighborhood resources), and structural factors (e.g., policies, regulations and systems). A variety of SDOH have been implicated in Latinos lower likelihood of obtaining a timely AD diagnosis. Included among these are knowledge, beliefs and perceptions about AD (Ambriz et al., 2024; Light et al., 2024). In a review of studies that investigated the perceptions, beliefs, attitudes, and knowledge about dementia and brain health among Latinos in the US, Light et al. (2024) found that the preponderance of evidence showed that dementia knowledge was lower among Latinos than Whites, although there were knowledge difference among Latino subpopulations. A common belief among Latinos is that memory loss is an expectable consequence of aging (Cabrera et al., 2021; Connell et al., 2007; Gray et al., 2009), a misconception that may delay seeking help for this problem which could be an early sign of AD (Lines et al., 2014). For example, in a telephone survey of 1,176 English speaking adults aged 35 and older, 50% of Latino respondents versus 67% of White respondents correctly answered that AD is not “normal memory loss as we get older” (Connell et al., 2007, p. 235). Limited access to Spanish speaking providers has also been found to contribute to delayed diagnosis. In a retrospective cross-sectional study of 12,080 English- or Spanish-speaking patients who received an initial diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment (“timely” diagnosis) or dementia (“delayed” diagnosis), Spanish-speaking Latinos were less likely to receive a timely diagnosis than English-speaking Latinos (Silva-Rudberg et al., 2024).

While research exists documenting disparities in AD-related care between Latinos and Whites, to date there has been little investigation of Latinos’ beliefs about the factors that underly disparities in the quality of care. One notable exception is an Alzheimer’s Association survey of 2,491 US adults age 18 and older which found that Latinos perceived numerous barriers to receiving “excellent” healthcare and support for AD including: affordability of care (55%), lack of good healthcare insurance coverage (41%), lack of good health care services in their community (24%), lack of family and social support to help them (21%), being treated differently because of their race, color or ethnicity (18%), and not being able to communicate easily in English (9%) (Alzheimer’s Association, 2021). These perceived barriers appeared to influence their expectations about the quality of AD care available to Latinos. Compared to other racial/ethnic groups Latinos were the least likely to report feeling confident that they would be able to get “excellent” healthcare for specialist assessment and diagnosis (69% of Latinos vs. 79% of Whites), or to get “excellent” healthcare and support to manage the disease (67% of Latinos vs. 74% of Whites). In addition, 85% of Latino respondents reported that they thought it was important for AD and dementia care providers to “understand their ethnic or racial background and experiences”; yet only 59% reported feeling confident that Latinos have access to such providers (Alzheimer’s Association, 2021, p. 73). In addition to the Alzheimer’s Association’s findings, a small qualitative study of 20 Latinos 40–60 years old recruited from the community reported that participants mentioned costs of care and insurance coverage as significant barriers to obtaining an AD diagnosis and treatment (Cabrera et al., 2021).

The Alzheimer’s Association report also included findings from a survey of 1,392 current or recent unpaid caregivers of adults “experiencing problems with thinking, understanding or remembering things” (Alzheimer’s Association, 2021). It found that 56% of Latino caregivers reported having “experienced racial discrimination when navigating health care settings for their care recipient”, and 32% reported “race makes it harder for them [Latinos with AD] to get excellent health care”. Additionally, 28% of Latinos compared to 17% of Whites endorsed “providers or staff do not listen to them because of their race, color or ethnicity”. Latino caregivers were also more likely than White ones to report providers/staff “acted as if they were not smart” (26% vs. 11%, respectively), providers/staff “treated them with less courtesy” (26% vs. 11%, respectively), they were “treated with less respect than others” (24% vs. 8%, respectively), or they “received poorer service than others” (21% vs. 7%, respectively). Furthermore, 17% of Latino caregivers said that language was a barrier to obtaining “excellent” dementia care. While the questions asked in the Alzheimer’s Association’s surveys of adults and of caregivers referred to above did not include the same items, they covered some of the same topics. Both Latino adults and caregivers reported discrimination and language as barriers to receiving excellent healthcare for AD, although caregivers who presumably had some involvement with the AD care system, held more negative views.

Many Latinos living in the US have witnessed or experienced first-hand as relatives or caregivers the challenges faced in obtaining a timely AD diagnosis and subsequently obtaining good-quality medical care and services for the disease. Even those with little knowledge about or no involvement in AD care may hold beliefs about the quality of care available to Latinos and the barriers to obtaining good-quality care based on their experiences with other diseases (e.g., cancer, diabetes). To date, very little qualitative research has focused on Latinos’ beliefs about the quality of AD care available to them and how it compares to that available to non-Latinos. Existing quantitative research, except for the Alzheimer’s Association surveys cited above, has focused largely on knowledge and misconceptions about the disease and comes primarily from large surveys which do not allow participants to share in their own words their beliefs and what underlies them. In this paper, using data from in-depth interviews with a community-based sample of Latinos, we attempt to address this research gap. Our main goal was to identify the nonmedical factors that our participants believed play a role in the quality of AD medical, supportive, and long-term care available to Latinos. This is important to understand because these beliefs may influence patient care seeking and subsequent engagement across the care continuum from diagnosis through late-stage care. Our findings contribute to neurodisparities research and have important implications for enhancing patient-centered care for Latinos with AD and their satisfaction with that care.

Methods

Design

The data for this paper come from the Información de la Enfermedad de Alzheimer para Latinos (IDEAL) Study, a community-based, mixed-methods longitudinal clinical trial designed to assess the psychosocial, behavioral, and cognitive impacts of receiving an estimate of one’s future risk of late-onset AD. APOE-ε4 is a variant (allele) of the APOE gene. It is currently the strongest identified genetic risk factor for late-onset AD; but while it increases disease susceptibility, it is not deterministic as many factors influence risk.

To be eligible to participate in the study, individuals had to: (1) self-identify as Latino/Hispanic; (2) be 40–64 years old; (3) speak English or Spanish; (4) reside in the NYC northern Manhattan neighborhoods of Washington Heights, Inwood, Hamilton Heights, Central Harlem, East Harlem, Morningside Heights, Manhattanville, or North Harlem; (5) have not previously been diagnosed with AD; (6) have not previously had genetic testing for increased susceptibility to AD; (7) not have a family history of AD that appeared consistent with early-onset, autosomal dominant AD, and (8) not have endorsed the suicidality item on the PHQ-9 at initial screening (Kroenke et al., 2001). The rationale for the study age eligibility criterion is, we anticipated that by age 40, participants might start to have concerns about developing AD in the future, and at 64 would still be too young to have late-onset AD symptoms.

The IDEAL Study has both a quantitative (surveys) and a qualitative component (interviews). Participants in the qualitative component were purposively selected from the larger survey sample to maximize heterogeneity with respect to family history of AD and APOE genotype. All participants completed a baseline survey; participants selected for the qualitative component also completed a baseline interview. Subsequently they met with a bilingual board-certified genetic counselor to receive an estimate of their future risk for developing AD that ranged from 21% to 55%. Participants were also told whether their risk estimates were higher, the same or lower than the average risk for Latinos residing in northern Manhattan which was 25%. Some also received the results of their APOE genetic test. Follow-up assessments, by survey and interview, were conducted at approximately 6 weeks, 9 and 15 months later to assess the impact of receiving this information. A full description of the study design and methods has been previously published (Wetmore et al., 2024).

Data collection

Participants in the qualitative component of the study completed in-depth semi-structured interviews. The interview guides for each assessment point were initially drafted by two senior investigators (KS and MC) based on the relevant existing literature. The guides were then reviewed by other study team members experienced with return of genetic results research who suggested modifications. Once finalized, they were translated to Spanish by a senior team member (MC). The interviews primarily explored how participants appraised the personal significance of the genetic risk information they received, and how they attempted to cope with its implications and the emotions the information evoked. However, they also included questions that elicited information on other topics, including some used in this report, that would help us contextualize and better understand their response to the information.

Data for this paper came from the baseline, 6-week and 9-month follow-up interviews all completed between 02 June 2021 and 03 February 2025. Interviews were conducted over Zoom in English or Spanish based on participants’ preference (mean duration = 89 min). Participants received a $100 gift card for each completed interview. The training of the interviewers, carried out by senior research team members (KS, MC), focused on proper administration of the interview guide, neutrality in probing and asking follow-up questions, and imparting skills to build trust and rapport with participants. The transcription and translation to English of Spanish transcripts, were done by two commercial firms with extensive experience in social and health-related research. Both firms had HIPAA compliant programs to safeguard protected health information and custom-built HIPAA compliant SFTP portals for secure and encrypted file transfer. Quality checks of transcriptions and translations were performed by a bilingual study team member (MC). The study protocol was approved by the Columbia University Irving Medical Center Institutional Review Board. All participants in the study provided either written or electronic informed consent.

Data analysis

Of the 216 people in the qualitative sample, 155 completed the baseline, 6-week and/or 9-month follow-up interviews that were the source of the data for this paper. In total, data from 213 interviews were analyzed for this paper. All 216 participants were not asked all the questions as some were added after data collection began. These questions elicited participants’ beliefs regarding Latinos’ access to good-quality AD related care including access to providers who spoke Spanish and to ones who understood their background and experiences as Latinos. They also yielded information on the quality of AD care participants believed was available to Latinos compared to that available to Whites and Blacks and their beliefs regarding racial/ethnic discrimination in the healthcare system. In addition, responses to other interview questions that directly mentioned or implicated nonmedical factors as related to the quality of AD care available to Latinos with the disease were also included in this report.

Using ATLAS.ti (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, 2020), three study team members trained by two senior team members (KS, MC) independently coded the responses to these questions to identify the nonmedical factors that participants saw as associated with the quality of care Latinos with AD receive. The data were analyzed using thematic analysis, guided by the methodological framework developed by Braun and Clarke (2006). All relevant material in transcripts was initially assigned a primary code of “Ethnic/Cultural, Social and Structural Factors”. This material was then extracted and subjected to a thematic analysis. As a first step, it was read multiple times to assess the breadth and quality of the data. After content familiarization, subcodes were created by the qualitative researchers (MC, CBB, KS) that related to specific factors discussed by participants in relation to any aspect of AD care. This list of subcodes were then reviewed and refined through an iterative process that included ensuring they were well substantiated by the data. Finally, the subcodes were built up into themes that captured participants’ beliefs about how nonmedical factors influenced Latinos’ experiences with AD diagnosis, treatment, support services, and long-term care.

Reflexivity (Day, 2012; May & Perry, 2014) was encouraged throughout the study from interview guides development, through coding of the data, data analysis and reporting. All qualitative study team members were women who held a doctorate or master’s degree in sociology, public health, or psychology. Team members all had different ethnic and cultural backgrounds. This diversity in training and backgrounds allowed for a wide range of different perspectives at each stage of the research process. During group discussions, team members challenged each other’s ideas or preconceptions and pointed out potential biases.

Results

Data from the 155 participants were analyzed for this paper. They ranged in age from 40 to 64 (mean = 53) and were primarily Dominican (46%), Puerto Rican (20%) and South American (14%). Overall, they were well educated with 80% having more than a high school education. Almost all (95%) reported having health insurance (56% public plan, 39% private plan). The majority had some family history of AD (63%). Participants’ characteristics are summarized in Table 2.

We identified five principal factors that participants implicated in the lower quality of AD care they believed Latinos received. These included the social factors of limited financial assets and restricted health insurance coverage; the cultural factors of cultural values and tendencies; and the structural factors of limited availability of providers who understood their background and experiences or spoke Spanish, and to a lesser extent prejudice or discrimination. Diagnosis

This stage includes both the period leading up to an AD diagnosis and the event of diagnosis itself. Some participants reported that Latinos were more likely to experience delayed AD diagnosis due to cultural values and tendencies, such as Latinos’ tendency not to discuss health issues with family or medical practitioners or to do so only after the problem has worsened. They pointed out, this sometimes resulted in Latinos learning of their disease at a more advanced and symptomatic stage.

I don’t know how other cultures work, but generally speaking at least my, my family’s, not very open to discussing these types of things [AD]. You know, especially if someone’s not feeling well. Either it’s a sense of pride or is a sense of keeping things private, personal. Uh, and if things aren’t discussed, things aren’t taken care of and usually when it’s time to do something about it, it’s already too late. So, maybe if it’s not the fact that a population gets it [AD], but when they do get it [a diagnosis], uh, it’s already too late. The repercussions already so strong that it may seem to leave a deeper footprint than maybe others that because they talked about it, because they addressed it earlier, um, they were more eased into it. Maybe not as traumatic air quotes of an experience as, these populations that put up with it and then when it comes, when they do have to face it, it’s very, it’s very traumatic.

(M, 40, SCTT, public, $50–74.9K)

One reason participants attributed Latinos reticence to talk about severe diseases like AD was their belief that “putting it out in the universe” (talking about it), “attracted” the disease (made one more vulnerable to it) and “gave it energy” (made it more likely to manifest).

Some emphasized that Latinos, who had bad or unsatisfying experiences with physicians and hospitals, tended not to seek medical care for their symptoms and consequently often did not receive a diagnosis until the disease had already become severe.

I don’t think people who are in the Hispanic community get the immediate treatment or get the immediate help that they need. So, someone who is more aware would immediately go, okay, see certain signs, something is wrong. Maybe I need to go and see someone at a facility, you know, go, go to my PCP [Primary Care Physician]. You know, people are more willing to go to the pharmacy to ask for help than they are to go to a doctor’s office … I think it’s just because people just feel that they’re not going to be paid attention to when they go to a hospital. Um, they’re not going to be listened to. Um, maybe some people, it’s a language barrier and they feel that, oh I don’t have someone to go with me to translate. So, I would just rather deal with my pharmacist in my neighborhood who understands me, who probably comes from the same community that I do and they’re going to help me. So, they’re gonna ignore all the signs. … I just think that the reason it becomes really bad is because we wait until it’s too late. And so then it’s, it’s outrageous by the time you go, someone else in a different community probably already went to a doctor, talked to someone and then started getting, you know, taking some pills, taking the patch that some people have used and gone ahead and try to do something. But if you don’t go and you wait, so I think that’s why it, it looks worse in our neck, you know, in our neck of the woods because people are not going. And I know it’s not only the community, but it’s also the facilities that we go, go to. If a doctor doesn’t pay attention, who, you know, who’s going to want to keep going to that doctor.

(W, 51, GW, public, $50–74.9K)

Taking a broader view, the following participant emphasized how Latinos’ social characteristics posed challenges to accessing healthcare, which could delay or affect the accuracy of an AD diagnosis.

I think that Latinos are less likely to be properly diagnosed, um, compared to Whites, um, because of many social res– you know, many social considerations, including, you know, a, you know, access to medical, um, you know, medical care.

(W, 56, CG, private, income not provided)

When asked about the importance of Latinos with AD having access to healthcare providers who understand their background and experiences, nearly all said it was very important, including for obtaining an accurate diagnosis.

Super important. Super important to avoid the wrong diagnoses, conflicting diagnoses, conflicting because culture is so important with this disease. That it must be kept in mind. … the doctor must understand the person’s culture in addition to the information they must have about the disease itself in order to make a more intelligent and informed diagnosis.

(W, 56, CG, private, >$100K)

Many participants also commented that language barriers made it difficult for non-English speaking Latinos to communicate effectively with doctors, which could impede obtaining a timely and accurate diagnosis. (Language barriers were mentioned by 32.0% of those who completed the interview in English and 32.8% of those who completed the interview in Spanish). They emphasized the need to increase availability of Spanish speaking providers, not simply translators, to avoid misunderstandings and attain an accurate diagnosis of AD.

That’s very important on a general level for Latinos. Being able—even when I go to the doctor, I prefer a doctor—doesn’t have to be Hispanic, but has to speak Spanish because I don’t [speak much] English. I feel more comfortable because I can explain myself better. I know the person will understand me better. When they are using an interpreter, the quality is not always the best in the interpretation. They can make mistakes. For example, my doctor does not speak Spanish and there have been cases that I look for an interpreter, because I understand English much better than I can speak it. Sometimes they’re interpreting and I know what they’re saying. Then I say, “This is not what I’m telling you.” So, you know, with a misinterpretation can come a misdiagnosis because either the doctor didn’t understand what they said, or I didn’t understand it. So yes, it is important [for Latinos with AD] more than one who is Hispanic, one who understands my language.

(W, 56, CG, public, <$25K, Spanish)

Medical care

Medical care involves receiving treatments, interventions and guidance from medical providers in managing AD. Participants believed that social factors, such as no health insurance or insurance that offered very limited kinds of coverage or few financial assets, prevented Latinos from receiving good-quality medical care following an AD diagnosis.

A lot of Latinos are not insured. … We have a program in place at our clinic which we’re giving them [asylum seekers] uh medical attention but for how long? And, you know, it’s free but when they – when they get other illnesses or all the sicknesses, no. Not for Alzheimer’s. A lot of people don’t have the coverage. They don’t have the means; they don’t have the money; and they don’t have the medical insurance.

(W, 63, HS, private, $25–49.9K)

Another noted that finances often restricted Latinos’ access to certain kinds of medical care and treatments, resulting in the development of a more severe form of the disease.

I think that if we [Latinos] had access to all the treatments and all the healthcare the wealthy people can afford, then the development of this disease would be minimal, so to speak. And if it is going to develop, it would be in a more …. How shall I say it? Milder, prettier, so to speak. … Unfortunately, we as Hispanics or Latinos do not have access to these beneficial factors to treat the disease.

(M, 50, CG, private, $25–49.9K, Spanish)

When asked if researchers were to find an effective treatment or cure for AD whether they thought it would be equally accessible to everyone regardless of class, race or ethnicity, most believed that wealthier individuals with more financial resources and social capital or status would be more likely to receive them or at least receive them earlier than most Latinos.

You know that first, it [treatment or a cure for AD] goes to where there is money. There they have discrimination. Why? You know that it always goes to the wealthy people, it is not the same. For example, I who live here, I am an average person, but when we are talking about, for example, a president or a person with a lot of money, with a lot of influence, they will make it available to that person before me.

(W, 46, CG, private, $25–49.9K, Spanish)

Well, COVID taught us that answer, didn’t it? No, it will not [be equally distributed]. The COVID response and how, um – I mean, everything from, uh, vaccine parties for those that had the financial resources and the access. Right? So even if you had the financial resources, it’s hard to have access. So, the people that were in – in positions of power, um, and then money were the ones that had access to it. Um, even – even months later or way after, you had to – you know, you had, uh – there was a – a two-month waiting list at my local pharmacy for a vaccine shot. So yeah, I think we’ve - we’ve already experienced what that would look like.

(W, 50, SCTT, private, $50–74.9K)

Several participants reported that race/ethnicity often determined who had access to treatments or cures and thus Latinos and other minorities would likely not have early access to future therapeutic options.

I don’t think we’re the first group that they give the medicine to. And I’ll give you a perfect example. When they–when they started finding medications for HIV, Latinos and African Americans were not the first ones that they were giving it to, so that’s a perfect example. No, we’re never the first group that they give it to.

(W, 46, CG, private, $25–49.9K)

Some participants (regardless of the language of the interview) mentioned that language barriers further contributed to poor medical care for Latinos. They noted even translators sometimes fail to accurately reflect what the patient is feeling or trying to say. As with diagnosis, many doubted that Latinos with AD typically had access to Latino, or even just non-Latino Spanish fluent providers, which they believed could adversely affect patient-provider communication and comfort, and the quality of care they received.

[It is very important for Latinos with AD to have providers that understand their background, culture and experiences.] For how I see it with my friend [with a cognitive impairment] when he goes to the doctor. Sometimes, or a few times when I accompanied him and he wanted to express his feelings they had to bring him a translator because he doesn’t have all of the words that he would like in order to express his frustration or what he thinks is happening to him, and so he had to ask for a translator. And several times I realized that even then a translator wasn’t translating what he was feeling, what he was saying.

(W, 55, GW, private, $75–99.9K, Spanish)

The following participant discussed how many older Latinos’ strong desire to have a doctor who spoke Spanish and understood them often led them to providers who gave them very little time, attention or respect:

There is, um, lack of access. Um, because in the lower income communities, there is a lot of doctors that all they care about is making money. And when you go to a provider, and I’ve seen this with a lot of my family members because I’ve been to so many different doctors. When you go to a provider who schedules 100 patients a day, they’re not really gonna have time for you. And there’s a lot of people in this community, a lot of elderly people that because they, you know they need somebody, if they don’t speak the language, they want to go to a doctor who just, you know understand and I get that. But, you know, they go to these doctors that they have to wait hours [to see], hours because they schedule so many people and it’s degrading, you know. They are wasting your time waiting, they don’t treat you right, then you go in and they see you for 10 minutes after you spend like - I have family members wait five hours, to see a doctor and these, some of these, um, so-called private offices up here.

(W, 50, CG, public, $75–99.9K)

Supportive care

Supportive care involves home-based services, including nonmedical assistance and supervision. Many participants described how financial assets influenced the types of supportive home healthcare Latinos could obtain. They stated that as AD progressed, the critical 24-hour care coverage patients needed was only available to those who could afford to pay for it and not to those with few financial resources or who were on government insurance programs. Some contended that this could result in gaps in care that left patients vulnerable and also caused the caregiving duties for Latinos with AD to fall more heavily on family and friends.

I mean, as the illness progresses, they definitely need 24-hour care because you can’t leave a person with Alzheimer’s alone at any time, you know, that’s the main thing. And a lot of people who have Alzheimer’s disease don’t get that care, you know … So, you know, they need someone that can take care of them 24 hours a day. … Medicare, Medicaid is not gonna flip the bill for that. And a lot of these old people can’t afford to pay for it out of their own pocket, especially the minorities, they don’t have that big mega bucks. Their kids aren’t doctors. Now, if you have a son who is a doctor, he’ll pay for someone to stay with you 24 hours a day, or a lawyer, they’ll pay here, you know, I’m gonna get your home care person, take care of the house, clean the house and they’re gonna stay with you, you know? But when you’re low income, you know, they give you four hours and that the doctor has to approve it to make sure that you don’t have no functionality. That you need real help, you see? But to get someone there eight hours is almost impossible nowadays. Whoever is with you, they have an eight-hour shift and it’s hard if they only get four hours because now you have your parents [with AD] by themselves for those four hours not knowing if he’s gonna wander off.

(M, 54, SCTT, private, $100K+)

Additionally, several participants commented that Latinos often found it difficult to find Spanish speaking home healthcare providers for a family member with AD.

They don’t have [access to providers who understand their background]. … My cousin, it is so difficult to get somebody to come and take care of her that speaks Spanish or that – You know, they will send anybody that they have, and she doesn’t – she speaks English, but she doesn’t remember. And it’s hard to get someone that understands her and that speaks Spanish.

(F, 61, HS, public, <$25K)

Long-term care (nursing home and residential care facilities)

Long-term care involves medical oversight, continuous nursing supervision, and assistance with daily personal care. Participants frequently reported that a combination of nonmedical factors, including financial assets, insurance coverage, neighborhood disadvantages, language barriers, and discrimination contributed to the types of facilities Latinos with AD could go to and the quality of nursing care they would receive there.

[In a nursing home], language might become a problem like, you know … some, a lot of people don’t actually speak English. But even if you do speak English, when you’re sick, when you’re, you know, um, and, and if you have Alzheimer’s, like some of that might go away. So, there’s that, um, there’s regular prejudice that, you know, even people in healthcare have. Um, you know, so it’s probably it’s worse out by, let’s say geography, in general we have less income, which means less affluent neighborhoods, which means worse facilities, you know, in general, in general.

(W, 52, GW, private, $100K+)

Some participants specified that their lower socioeconomic status often necessitated that Latinos with AD rely upon public facilities where they would likely receive poorer quality care than in privately run ones.

Because unless you have money, the homes that are sometimes, not always, but sometimes run by the government or non-profits don’t have the best quality of care. And those are the ones where Latinos and minorities end up in.

(F, 44, CG, public, $25–49.9K)

When asked to compare the quality of care in nursing homes or residential care facilities available to Latinos, Whites and Blacks with AD, many participants commented that Blacks and Latinos were essentially “in the same boat” with respect to AD care. That is, they believed the care options for both groups were worse and more restricted than they were for Whites. They attributed this primarily to more limited financial assets and restricted or poorer insurance coverage.

I would have to say no [care for Latinos is not the same as Whites], you know. Um, I think it has a lot to do with, you know, equitable care and you know, of how they’re paying, if they’re paying with– because they have Medicaid or Medicare, versus maybe private insurance. I mean, or because you have, you know, a family that’s well off that they can afford to put you in better places, you know, um, where a good majority of the Latinos, I’m not saying all, right? Um, but a good majority can’t. So, you know, I think– yeah, I think it, it– it’s less adequate, the care will be less adequate for them. … I put them [Blacks] in our category, right? So I don’t– I don’t differentiate. I think it’s the same thing, but you know, they are– they’re in line with us, Black and Brown.

(W, 56, SCTT, private, $75–99.9K)

Although many participants shared their belief that the quality of nursing home care available to Latinos and Blacks was similar, several indicated that for Latinos, this might depend on the race/ethnicity and language of the healthcare worker.

Um, [Blacks] they may be treated slightly better [than Latinos] only because people also discriminate. Uh, they use language as a marker to discriminate. So, if you’re an African American person who speaks English versus an Afro Latino who doesn’t speak English, I think the Afro Latino …. is more likely to be discriminated against if the workers don’t speak Spanish. And now if the workers speak Spanish and are Latinos, they might be more, uh, caring towards the Latino versus the African American.

(W, 56, GW, private, $100K+)

Participants suggested that having enough money to pay for care out of pocket also affected whether elderly Latino family members with AD could be cared for at home, as many Latinos preferred to do for as long as possible, or instead had to be placed in a long-term care facility.

[Latinos receive worse AD care than Whites.] Because they [Whites] have more resources of– for example, uh, a White person who has money can even pay for a private nurse to take care of them in their own home. Whereas we would depend on a nursing home, sometimes with a lot of limitations, because they don’t even take you to one—where the staff has to speak completely in Spanish to you. It’s the preparation. That is, money as– money for care has a lot to do with it because they can– they don’t necessarily have to be taken to a facility. They can have everything in their own home. … I believe that they [Latinos and Blacks] receive the same [quality of medical care] because it is a social, cultural matter.

(W, 51, CG, private, $25–49.9K, Spanish)

The following participant emphasized the importance of Latinos having an AD provider who understood their background, culture and experiences because when the patient eventually needed long-term care, such providers would better understand Latinos’ preference to care for older family members at home as long as possible, despite frequent financial barriers to doing so.

Oh, that’s very important. While like, like I was telling you the thing about we would wait to–we would go to the end before putting our family member like in a nursing home, if it’s at all possible. You know, stuff like that. So, they would understand that, you know, that concept.

(M, 62, SCTT, private, $50–74.9K)

Cultural factors were also mentioned by several participants as contributing to poor quality long-term care for Latinos with AD. These included Latinos’ tendencies to not mention problems they might experience at a facility to anyone outside of the family, to not advocate for themselves with staff, and to not question doctors’ directives. They recognized that multiple factors complicated these issues. The following participant explained why she believed that Latinos receive poorer quality of care in nursing homes than other racial/ethnic groups.

They can’t – they don’t advocate, they don’t have the same level of advocating for themselves or knowledge about what the systems are like, you have to be able to navigate a lot of systems and, it’s hard for them to. For many reasons. One is because – one is cultural, we – we see – we tend to see medical providers as gods almost. So, we don’t question them, whatever they say, we just accept and move on. Um, It’s – it’s much harder and especially if you don’t speak a language, they have all these other barriers and you don’t have the insurance or – or their economic need, uh, means.

(W, 56, CG, private, $100K+).

Discussion

While a growing body of primarily quantitative research documents Latino-White disparities in AD care and the factors statistically associated with them, there has been very little investigation of Latinos’ beliefs about the factors that influence the quality of AD care available to them. Further, to our knowledge, there are no published reports that describe these beliefs across the entire care continuum. This paper represents an important step in filling these research gaps. The in-depth interviews used to gather the data for this report allowed participants to express their beliefs in their own terms and from their own vantage points. In doing so they drew primarily upon their lived experiences as a relative or caregiver to someone with AD, or what they had heard or observed regarding the experiences of other Latinos with AD or their caregivers. Less frequently, they extrapolated from their experiences receiving care for other illnesses to arrive at their beliefs about what AD care is probably like for Latinos.

Most participants believed there were notable deficiencies in the quality of AD care available to Latinos across the care continuum which they attributed in large part to a variety of nonmedical factors. The most frequently mentioned ones included the social factors of limited financial assets and restricted health insurance coverage; the cultural factors of cultural values and tendencies; and the structural factors of limited availability of providers who understood their background and experiences or spoke Spanish, and to a lesser extent prejudice or discrimination. It is likely a less educated or more vulnerable sample of Latinos than we obtained would believe that these factors had an even greater negative impact on the quality of AD care available to Latinos. Providing greater understanding about Latinos’ beliefs regarding how these nonmedical factors affect care quality can inform community outreach, education and engagement initiatives along with efforts to adopt patient-centered care and improve patient satisfaction.

Participants identified limited financial assets and restricted health insurance coverage as frequent barriers to good-quality care across the AD care continuum. They also believed that because Latinos were generally of lower socioeconomic status, this would prevent or at least delay their access to new treatments or an eventual cure for AD. Participants also reported that restricted financial resources and insurance coverage constrained supportive services and long-term care options for Latinos with AD. They pointed out that when individuals with AD do not yet need full time nursing care, but require around-the-clock supervision, Latino families typically lacked the resources to pay for hours not covered by insurance. This, they noted, placed greater caregiving responsibilities on family members, or when they could not be available left gaps in supervision that could be unsafe for the patient. They also believed that Latinos generally ended up in public facilities that provided low-quality service, while Whites more often had the money or insurance coverage to obtain better quality long-term care. This finding is consistent with literature documenting racial disparities in the quality of nursing home facilities (Aranda et al., 2021; Rivera-Hernandez et al., 2019, 2020, 2022).

Reforms, including ones that would extend various kinds of health services coverage and reduce healthcare costs, have been difficult to achieve in the US. Until such changes come about, social service agencies can assist Latino families to learn what resources are available to them through Medicaid or other state or community assistance programs that may improve the situation by reducing out of pocket costs for home-health or respite care and potentially reduce caregiver burden. There are federal programs, that although not Latino-focused, provide much needed financial and social support to people with dementia and their caregivers. For example, every state has at least one Medicaid program that pays family members for caregiving (American Council on Aging, 2026) offsetting at least some of the financial burdens. Additionally, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) launched the Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE) Model, a payment model that provides financial support to participating organizations and assesses the impact of providing comprehensive services and support to people with dementia and their caregivers (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025c). As availability of programs of course does not guarantee utilization, efforts must be made to ensure Latinos are both aware of them and able to access them. Martinez et al. (2022), found that both home and community-based providers and Latino AD caregivers they interviewed noted “an absence of a clear path to formal support, available services, and understanding the means of obtaining them” which underscores the need for assistance in accessing available resources.

Our participants also recognized that Latino cultural factors contributed to some of the difficulties they experienced receiving quality AD care. Specifically, they mentioned Latinos’ tendency to refrain from discussing diseases like AD with anyone, including family (out of pride, a desire for privacy, or fear of attracting the disease), as well as delaying seeking care from providers, which often meant that they obtained an AD diagnosis at a more advanced and symptomatic stage of the disease. Family members are often the ones who persuade patients to seek care; however, if problems are not shared with them, they cannot play this important role. They also noted Latinos’ reluctance to advocate for themselves once in care or to be assertive when talking with a physician, a finding also reported in other research (Ch’en et al., 2024). This could cause their providers to mistakenly assume they are satisfied with the care they are receiving. Therefore, Latinos might benefit from education about the value of early diagnosis and how to advocate for the patient to ensure receipt of timely and good-quality care.

Participants frequently noted limited availability of AD providers who understood their background and experiences, which they believed compromised their quality of AD care. They also reported that often they did not even have access to Spanish speaking providers and that available translators would sometimes misunderstand or make mistakes when translating what was said. Health navigators could help Latinos find suitable providers, who are at least Spanish fluent, and encourage them to advocate for themselves (Ch’en et al., 2024). Several participants commented that older Latinos in their community preferred going to local Latino providers who not only spoke Spanish, but they felt also understood their culture better, despite the fact that these providers often showed disrespect by spending little time with them even after they endured long wait times. Although these patient experiences run contrary to the Latino cultural value of personalismo, where a positive patient-provider interaction is established based on a doctor’s understanding the patient’s culture, treating them as a unique individual and establishing trust (confianza), participants felt that patients were willing to tolerate it in order to see a Latino doctor.

Bringing more Latino physicians into the AD workforce will be essential in the coming two to three decades as the steepest increase in the number of cases of AD among older adults during this period will be among Latinos. Otherwise even when Latinos recognize a cognitive problem should be brought to a doctor’s attention, they may not do so if they don’t believe they will be seen by a Latino provider or at least a Spanish speaking one. Among providers who are not Latino, it is important to emphasize the need to develop “cultural humility” (Lekas et al., 2020). Doing so involves an ongoing process in which providers “reflect on their own beliefs, values and biases” and are open to learning and valuing “patients’ expertise on the social and cultural context of their lives”, rather than simply learning discrete pieces of information about a particular minority group (Lekas et al., 2020, p. 2). Providers could benefit from training in understanding patient expectations regarding AD care and patient-provider interactions to better meet those expectations when possible or explain why they are unable to do so (e.g., limits on the amount of time they can spend with each patient or on what the patient’s insurance will cover). This would foster more realistic expectations and in doing so reduce patient dissatisfaction.

To a lesser extent, participants believed prejudice or discrimination affected the quality of AD care available to Latinos. This was mentioned, along with their lower socio-economic status, as a factor that would likely delay Latinos’ access to future new AD treatments or an eventual cure. Additionally, some felt that discrimination (based on their ethnicity, language, and cultural differences) by non-Latino workers occurred in long-term care facilities where Latinos were treated as “second class citizens”. Participants’ beliefs are not surprising given that both Blacks and Latinos report experiencing prejudice and discrimination in healthcare at much higher rates than Whites (Feldman et al., 2022), and perceive it to be a barrier to receiving high-quality AD care (Alzheimer’s Association, 2021).

It is generally recognized that bias among providers is often implicit and not in their awareness. A systematic review of studies investigating racial/ethnic implicit bias (Hall et al., 2015) concluded that most health care providers appear to have implicit bias in terms of positive attitudes toward Whites and negative attitudes toward people of color. Based on a narrative review examining current evidence about how provider implicit bias contributes to health disparities and whether training can effectively reduce such biases, Zestcott et al. (2016) suggested developing training models that not only raise awareness of implicit biases but also teach control strategies and include active practice. Providers play a central role in patients’ perception of the quality of care they are receiving. Modifying their behavior may therefore represent the best opportunity for increasing patient satisfaction and sustaining patient engagement even while dissatisfaction with the larger healthcare system might persist.

Participants often described challenges involving multiple concurrent nonmedical factors Latinos confronted trying to get quality AD care for family members. They were particularly skeptical or pessimistic about Latinos’ prospects for quality care when it came to long-term care, believing Latinos’ limited financial assets and insurance coverage typically would not allow for private nursing care at home, even when families preferred caring for elders at home. Similarly, they believed high-quality private nursing facilities were out of their reach, necessitating sending family members with AD to low-quality public facilities in the lower socioeconomic neighborhoods where they resided. Compounding the problem was the belief that language barriers rendered Latinos vulnerable to their needs going unmet in these facilities. This can be particularly problematic for people with advanced AD who may be unable to communicate their needs or concerns to staff, and family or friends are not present to advocate for them. In addition to these issues were participants’ concerns over possible discrimination from non-Latino nursing home workers. Some of the initiatives described above, such as provider and staff training, increasing the number of Spanish speaking long-term care workers or interventions to help family caregivers develop advocacy skills may be useful in improving Latinos’ long-term care experiences.

This paper provides new information about Latinos’ beliefs about the quality of care available to Latinos with AD and the role played by nonmedical factors. Our findings have particular utility for enhancing patient-centered care for Latinos with AD as this approach emphasizes the consideration of patients’ needs, values and preferences, and most relevant to the current paper, the incorporation of their perspectives when assessing the quality of care being delivered (Jayadevappa, 2011).

The findings reported may have important implications for Latinos’ illness behavior. The expectation of receiving poor-quality care may deter individuals with cognitive symptoms from seeking medical care (Jurgens et al., 2012) and thereby prevent a timely AD diagnosis. While once diagnosed it can delay entry into medical care and adversely impact patients’ level of engagement in care (Jurgens et al., 2012) and treatment adherence (Xiong et al., 2021). For example, in a study of elderly patients with ADRD on anti-dementia medications, investigators found those who were ‘dissatisfied’ or ‘very dissatisfied’ with their care were less likely to take their medications as prescribed (Xiong et al., 2021).

Another contribution of this paper is it provides information about Latinos’ beliefs about the quality of AD care available to Latinos across the care continuum. We believe this is the first published report to do so. While much of the available research on Latinos has focused on the causes of delayed diagnosis given the growing emphasis on early intervention, our contribution to understanding Latinos’ beliefs about the quality of long-term care available to them in nursing homes or residential facilities is particularly new and valuable. While the surveys reported by the Alzheimer’s Association provided data about US adults and AD caregivers’ perspectives about barriers to AD medical care and support (Alzheimer’s Association, 2021), they did not specifically ask about AD care in nursing homes or residential facilities. Furthermore, the studies we found documenting racial/ethnic differences in the quality of ADRD care in long-term care facilities used Medicare beneficiary records or aggregated facility level data (Rivera-Hernandez et al., 2019, 2020, 2022). They did not incorporate perspectives from family members of AD patients.

The nonmedical factors our participants identified are important to consider not only throughout the continuum of care but also in community outreach, education and engagement efforts, and interventions and strategies that promote brain health. In addition to offering education about AD signs, symptoms, and risk factors, community health education and programs can also inform participants about health disparities, inequities and SDOH. Doing so acknowledges and validates their beliefs and experiences which can motivate Latinos to advocate for good quality care at all stages of the disease and seek and use cultural, financial, care coordination and health navigation resources.

Recommendations for future research

Future research should explore Latinos’ experiences and beliefs about AD care in different areas of the country where other kinds of Latino ethnic groups predominate (e.g., Cuban, Mexican). Other locations may also have more limited options for AD specialty care than NYC which has a wide array of healthcare facilities, and many services have language translation accommodations. Another recommendation for future research would be to follow newly diagnosed patients and their caregivers over time to investigate if their beliefs about the quality of care they are receiving change as the disease progresses and if so, what nonmedical factors account for those changes. Longitudinal research will provide a more dynamic and comprehensive picture of Latinos’ assessment of the actual quality of AD care they receive and what they are responding to at different points in the care continuum. When possible, these studies should also include go-along interviews (Carpiano, 2009) in which a researcher accompanies to a medical visit each participant in AD care. This would allow the researcher to observe the lived experience of participants as patients, but also enable the gathering in real time (immediately before and after the visit) data about participants’ thoughts and feelings about the visit (e.g., what they hoped to get out of the visit, whether their expectations were met, how they felt they were treated by the provider or other staff, their satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the care they received). Finally, investigators could explore what experiences or factors contribute to high satisfaction with the quality of AD care among Latinos with the disease or their caregivers. The findings could be used to establish a framework for high-quality AD care in Latino communities.

Limitations

The study has limitations that should be noted. The sample is composed of Latinos who were generally well-educated and primarily Dominican, Puerto Rican and South American. As noted above, they lived in a city with many healthcare resources and a very large Latino population. Therefore, our findings may not be transferable to other Latino subgroups (e.g., Mexicans, Cubans), or to Latinos living in other parts of the country, especially less urban locales. In addition, data collection for this study began in August 2021 and much of it occurred during the first two years of the COVID pandemic, when racial and ethnic inequities in access to care, treatment, and later to vaccines were widely noted (Cervantes et al., 2021; Revens et al., 2023). This historical context may have influenced their beliefs about the quality of AD care that is available to Latinos and that they would not have access to new or best treatments. Finally, it should also be noted that because the study was done under the auspices of a major medical research center where many of the participants received care, they may have been reluctant to be critical of providers or medical care more generally despite assurances of confidentiality and that study participation (or declining participation) would have no effect on their future medical care at the institution.

Conclusion

Although quantitative research highlights disparities in AD care between Latinos and Whites, few studies capture Latinos’ perspectives on these issues. Based upon in-depth interviews with 155 Latinos in northern Manhattan, this paper makes an important contribution to filling that gap. Our data revealed that our Latino participants believed there were significant nonmedical factors that compromise the availability of high-quality AD care to Latinos. Their beliefs were primarily based upon their experiences as a relative or caregiver to someone with AD or what they had seen or heard about the experiences of other Latinos with AD. In a smaller number of cases, they extrapolated from their personal experiences as a Latino being treated for other diseases to form beliefs about what AD care must be like. They attributed the problems Latinos experienced obtaining good-quality AD care to five principal nonmedical factors: limited financial assets, restricted health insurance coverage, cultural values and tendencies, limited availability of providers who understood their background and experiences or spoke Spanish, and to a lesser extent prejudice or discrimination. Understanding these beliefs is essential for improving patient-centered care that is responsive to Latino patients’ needs.
