Bidirectional effect modification of greenness exposure and air pollution on ischemic stroke mortality across different temporal scales
Authors: Bingyin Zhang, Qiyong Cao, Qiongqi Zhang, Haohao Shen, Yang Zhang, Zilong Lu, Xiaolei Guo, Ying Yu, Xianjie Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIschemic stroke (IS) is a leading cause of death and disability. While greenness exposure has health benefits in the general population, its role in reducing mortality in IS patients and its interaction with air pollution are not well understood.MethodsBased on a cohort of 249,537 IS patients from Shandong Province, China (2013–2019), multilevel Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess associations between cumulative and contemporaneous NDVI/EVI exposure and mortality outcomes. Air pollutant data were derived from a combination of satellite remote sensing and ground-level monitoring at high spatial resolution. Restricted cubic spline models were employed to characterize exposure-response relationships. Subgroup analyses identified vulnerable populations, and stratified analyses evaluated bidirectional effect modifications between air pollutants and greenness exposure.ResultsEach interquartile range (IQR) increase in contemporaneous NDVI was associated with decreased mortality risk by 16.9% for all-cause, 17.3% for cardiovascular, and 17.6% for stroke mortality. Cumulative NDVI exhibited stronger protective associations, corresponding to risk reductions of 17.1%, 18.3%, and 14.9%, respectively. Exposure-response curves indicated linear inverse associations between cumulative NDVI and mortality, whereas contemporaneous NDVI exhibited U-shaped associations with all-cause mortality (P-nonlinearity < 0.05). Subgroup analyses revealed amplified protective effects among individuals aged ≥ 60 years, urban residents, and males, particularly pronounced in high air pollution contexts. Notably, air pollution-related mortality risks were significantly mitigated by higher levels of greenness exposure.ConclusionGreenness exposure across different temporal scales significantly reduced mortality risk among IS patients, demonstrating notably enhanced protective effects in regions with elevated air pollution. Among air pollutants, NO₂ significantly modified this relationship. Despite limitations such as spatial resolution variability in exposure assessments, these findings offer compelling evidence for greening interventions in regions experiencing high air pollution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25066-0.

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third major cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) worldwide. The burden is not evenly distributed. More than 80% of stroke-related DALYs occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1–3]. In the Western Pacific region, age-standardized incidence rates continue to rise, which shows the urgent need for better prevention strategies. Risk factors also vary by region. Environmental exposures, especially air pollution, play a larger role in LMICs than in high-income countries [4, 5]. China carries one of the heaviest stroke burdens in the world. Within China, ischemic stroke (IS) mortality shows strong geographic links with air pollution levels and declining greenness coverage. In Shandong Province, a rapidly industrializing region, IS mortality is 17.6% higher than the national average [6]. This pattern highlights the need for targeted environmental actions. However, most existing studies use single-exposure models. They often overlook the combined and interacting effects of greenness and air pollution during urbanization. Filling this gap is important for shaping evidence-based environmental health policies in LMICs.

Recent studies in environmental epidemiology show that the health effects of greenness depend on time scale, and these effects are often shaped by air pollution [7–9]. Short-term greenness exposure, such as changes in vegetation within three months before death, mainly acts through the autonomic nervous system [10]. Research shows that greenness can reduce negative emotions, improve mood, and lower stress compared with areas without greenness [11]. This short-term protection is important in the acute stage of stroke because it helps ease both mental distress and physical burden in patients. In contrast, long-term greenness exposure, lasting three years or more, provides broader benefits through biological pathways. Long-term air pollution is linked to higher inflammatory markers, such as IL-6, and impaired endothelial function. Long-term greenness may counteract these harms by reducing chronic inflammation and improving vascular function, which lowers the risk of stroke recurrence. Together, these processes build a lasting physiological defense and underline the role of greenness in managing chronic disease [12, 13].

More importantly, greenness and air pollution may interact in both directions. Greenness can reduce the harmful effects of pollution, and this protection is often stronger when pollution levels are high. For example, each 0.1-unit drop in contemporaneous NDVI is linked to an 8% increase in stroke mortality, while every 10 µg/m³ rise in PM2.5 raises stroke mortality risk by 13% [14]. A study in Spain found that long-term residence in polluted areas increased ischemic stroke risk, while people living in greener areas had much lower risk [15]. One reason is that vegetation can physically trap pollutants and alter how air pollution spreads across space. Wind tunnel studies show that dense vegetation captures PM2.5 more effectively, especially in areas with high pollution [16]. Vegetation also cools local temperatures through transpiration and influences the formation of secondary aerosols by releasing biogenic volatile organic compounds (BVOCs), which helps regulate both air quality and microclimate [17]. Even in heavy traffic zones with NO₂ above 40 µg/m³, greenness alone provides a psychological buffer that cuts stroke mortality risk by about 10% [18]. Clarifying these relationships is crucial because greenness and pollution are common environmental exposures that often coexist, particularly in rapidly urbanizing regions. Understanding their bidirectional effect modification will provide important insight into the joint impacts of environmental factors on stroke outcomes and will help guide more targeted public health policies [19–21].

Although many studies show that greenness is linked to lower risk of chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, few studies have focused on ischemic stroke (IS) patients. In particular, research that looks at multiple outcomes, including all-cause, cardiovascular, and stroke mortality, is still limited. Most existing studies use cross-sectional designs or narrow exposure windows. As a result, they may miss the dynamic and cumulative effects of long-term greenness exposure. Stroke incidence and air pollution both change over time, but systematic evidence on how air pollution alters the link between greenness and stroke outcomes is still lacking. The role of greenness as an effect modifier in stroke–environment studies has not been fully examined. This gap is especially important in LMICs such as China, where stroke burden is high and urbanization is rapid, but research is still insufficient.

To fill these gaps, we carried out a large, population-based cohort study in Shandong Province, China. Our goals were threefold: (1) to examine how greenness exposure at different time scales relates to multiple mortality outcomes in ischemic stroke (IS) patients; (2) to identify subgroups that are more sensitive to greenness exposure; and (3) to explain the bidirectional effect modification between greenness and air pollutants.

Methods

Study design and participants

Shandong Province (34°22′–38°23′N, 114°47′–122°43′E) is the second most populous province in China, with 101 million residents in 2020. It has gone through rapid industrialization and has a warm-temperate monsoon climate [22]. These features make it a natural setting for environmental health research. Shandong was also a priority region for air quality control. From 2013 to 2019, annual PM2.5 levels fell from 96 µg/m³ to 63 µg/m³, but they still stayed far above the WHO guideline of 10 µg/m³. During the same period, urban greenness coverage rose from 38.2% to 42.1%. However, clear differences between urban and rural areas remained, with mean NDVI at 0.52 in urban cores and 0.34 in rural areas [23]. The province also has one of the highest stroke mortality rates in China (age-standardized rate: 165.3 per 100,000), which overlaps with areas of heavy industrial pollution [24], These conditions make it a strong setting to study how greenness and pollution interact.

We built a longitudinal cohort using data from the Shandong Stroke Incidence and Mortality Surveillance Systems. We used geographically stratified sampling to select residents from 20 counties to ensure population coverage (Fig. S2). The study period was from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2019. Details of cohort construction are available in earlier work and in the supplementary material [25](Fig. S1, Table S1).

In total, the cohort included 249,537 people with first-ever ischemic stroke (IS) (ICD-10: I63), confirmed through medical records between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2019. Eligible participants were 40–99 years old. We excluded those with missing demographic or socioeconomic data, missing follow-up, or logical errors. At baseline, we collected demographic, socioeconomic, residential greenness, and clinical information. Mortality outcomes were classified as all-cause (ICD-10: A00–Z99), cardiovascular (ICD-10: I00–I99), and stroke mortality (ICD-10: I60–I63), based on provincial death records [26]. In total, the cohort contributed 587,708 person-years of follow-up.

Greenness exposure assessment

We measured greenness using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and the Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI). Both were obtained from NASA’s MODIS MOD13A3 product, which has a 1 km spatial resolution and a 16-day temporal resolution [27]. NDVI measures vegetation cover by comparing red and near-infrared reflectance. However, it can saturate in areas with dense vegetation and can be affected by atmospheric conditions. EVI addresses these issues by adding a blue band, which reduces atmospheric noise and improves sensitivity in dense vegetation [28]. During preprocessing, we applied MODIS quality control (QC) flags from the Quality Assurance (QA) layer to exclude data affected by clouds or abnormal viewing angles. This step ensured more reliable exposure measures.

To effectively capture temporal dynamics of greenness exposure, NDVI and EVI were quantified in two distinct temporal dimensions. Contemporaneous exposure was the mean NDVI or EVI during the season of death, used to study short-term effects. Cumulative exposure was the annual mean NDVI or EVI averaged over each participant’s survival period, from stroke diagnosis until death or the study endpoint. Residential addresses were geocoded at the village or community level. We then assigned average NDVI/EVI values within the corresponding administrative boundary at 1-km resolution. When village or community addresses were incomplete, we used township or sub-district addresses instead to ensure complete coverage [29].

Covariates

To reduce confounding and improve robustness, we included both individual- and neighborhood-level covariates based on prior evidence linking greenness to ischemic stroke (IS) outcomes (Fig. S3). Individual-level covariates were baseline age, sex, and residential setting (urban or rural), obtained directly from the IS Surveillance System [30]. Neighborhood-level variables captured socioeconomic, built environment, and climate conditions. These included population density, road density, walkability index, per capita GDP, and other related factors. Data came from local statistical yearbooks, government databases, and published studies [24].

Climate data were collected from the China Meteorological Data Sharing Service System. Air pollutants (PM2.5, NO₂, and SO₂) were treated as effect modifiers. Exposure data were sourced from the High-Resolution Air Pollutant Dataset for China (http://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html). PM2.5 concentrations were estimated using satellite-based models at 1 km² resolution. NO₂ and SO₂ were available at ~ 10 km (0.1°) resolution [31–33]. Annual pollutant exposure levels were assigned to each participant using their geocoded residence and calendar year, in line with NDVI/EVI assignments. The choice of pollutants was based on epidemiological evidence. PM2.5 is linked to stroke through inflammation and vascular dysfunction. NO₂ affects vascular health mainly through respiratory pathways and traffic emissions. SO₂ contributes to stroke risk via oxidative stress and acute inflammatory responses, often from industrial sources [34–36]. Full definitions and calculation methods for neighborhood-level covariates are shown in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

The study population was stratified into tertiles based on residential greenness exposure Baseline characteristics across exposure groups were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables and either one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, contingent upon normality assessment via Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05) [37].

Basic model

To account for clustering within communities, we used a multilevel Cox proportional hazards model with a random intercept at the community level [38]. We reported hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) per interquartile range (IQR) increase in NDVI. We built three models step by step. Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and behavioral risks such as smoking and alcohol use. Model 2 added built environment factors, including population density, road density, per capita GDP, walkability index, and nighttime light. Model 3, our primary model, further included meteorological variables such as relative humidity and temperature. We tested the proportional hazards assumption using generalized linear regression of Schoenfeld residuals. A non-zero slope (P < 0.05) indicated violation [39]. To explore non-linear associations, we fitted restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles. We compared linear and non-linear fits using likelihood ratio tests (LRT) [40]. Missing data were handled by Multiple Imputation using Chained Equations (MICE) to improve robustness [41].

Subgroup analysis

We carried out stratified analyses by sex (male and female), age (40–60 years and 60–99 years), and residential setting (urban and rural). For each subgroup, we calculated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to identify which groups were more vulnerable.

Assessment of bidirectional modification effects

Based on earlier studies, bidirectional effect modification means two related processes: (1) air pollution changes the effect of greenness on stroke mortality, and (2) greenness changes the effect of air pollution. We assessed this relationship through stratified analyses (Fig. 1). First, we divided greenness exposure into three groups: low, medium, and high, using the 33rd and 66th percentiles as cutoffs. In each group, we estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for a one interquartile range (IQR) increase in PM2.5, NO₂, or SO₂. This allowed us to see how pollution effects vary with different greenness levels. Second, we divided each pollutant into the same three groups. In each pollution group, we then estimated HRs per IQR increase in greenness. This allowed us to see how greenness effects vary under different pollution levels. To test for effect modification, we treated the tertiles as ordered variables and applied a trend test. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1), employing the survival package for Cox models, coxme for random effects, and splines for restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis. Statistical tests were two-tailed with a significance threshold of α = 0.05.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of participants grouped by cumulative NDVI during the survival period, which reflects different levels of greenness exposure. We found clear differences across groups. The lowest greenness group had the highest number of deaths, while the highest greenness group had fewer deaths. Rural residents had higher greenness exposure than urban residents (P < 0.001). For environmental exposures, participants in the low greenness group were exposed to higher levels of PM2.5, NO₂, SO₂, and nighttime light compared with those in the medium and high greenness groups. In contrast, temperature and humidity were similar across groups. When we stratified by other time scales of greenness exposure, such as contemporaneous NDVI, the overall distribution patterns were consistent with those seen in the cumulative NDVI groups (Table S3).

Subgroup analyses

Fig. 4 shows subgroup results. We found clear heterogeneity in the associations between greenness exposure and mortality across age, residence, and sex groups (49–51). For all-cause mortality, cumulative NDVI showed stronger protective effects in people aged ≥ 60 years (HR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.785–0.837), urban residents (HR = 0.745, 95% CI: 0.693–0.802), and males (HR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.828–0.894). For stroke mortality, females showed greater protection than males (HR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.74–0.82 vs. HR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.81–0.89). Air pollutants effects modified by greenness

Table 2 shows that air pollutants (NO₂, PM2.5, SO₂) significantly modified the link between greenness exposure and all-cause mortality in IS patients. The protective effects of greenness were strongest under high pollution (Q3). For contemporaneous NDVI, HRs for all-cause mortality were 0.79 (95% CI: 0.758–0.824) under NO₂-Q3, 0.776 (95% CI: 0.753–0.800) under PM2.5-Q3, and 0.818 (95% CI: 0.797–0.840) under SO₂-Q3. The modification was even stronger with cumulative NDVI. Under NO₂-Q3, the HR was 0.389 (95% CI: 0.344–0.440); under PM2.5-Q3, 0.589 (95% CI: 0.552–0.628); and under SO₂-Q3, 0.744 (95% CI: 0.707–0.784). In contrast, at low pollution levels (Q1/Q2), HRs for greenness were close to 1, showing little or no protective effect (for example, cumulative NDVI HR = 1.022, 95% CI: 0.987–1.058 under NO₂-Q1).EVI analyses showed similar trends, with consistent pollution-modified greenness effects on CVD and stroke mortality (Tables S4–S5).

However, in the analysis evaluating the modifying effect of greenness exposure on air pollution-related mortality risks, significant differences in associations between air pollutants and mortality among ischemic stroke (IS) patients across varying levels of greenness exposure were observed only for NO₂. Discussion

In this large cohort of IS patients from Shandong Province, we found that greenness exposure was strongly associated with reduced risks of all-cause, cardiovascular, and stroke mortality. The protective effects were more evident for long-term greenness exposure than for short-term exposure. Short-term exposure showed a U-shaped pattern, while long-term exposure had mostly linear associations. We also observed clear subgroup differences, with stronger protective effects among older adults, men, and urban residents. Importantly, the protective role of greenness was enhanced in areas with high air pollution, especially for NO₂. These findings highlight the need to consider both time scale and environmental context when evaluating the health benefits of greenness.

Short exposure to greenness can lower stress and restore attention. Classic experimental and clinical evidence shows that viewing or accessing nature aids psychophysiological recovery [45–48]. These pathways can ease acute mental distress after stroke, which may help short-term survival. At very high greenness, local aero-allergen load or altered street-canyon airflow could offset benefits in some settings; evidence from wind-tunnel and modeling studies shows that dense tree canopies may trap traffic pollutants in narrow canyons, while low hedges can improve near-road air by interception [49–51]. The net short-term effect therefore depends on site design (vegetation height, porosity, street geometry) and context (traffic, wind). Long exposure to greenness is associated with lower mortality and better cardiovascular profiles in population cohorts []. Proposed biological pathways include lower chronic stress and improved vascular function. Observational studies report links between residential greenness and favorable cardiovascular biomarkers and reduced physiological dysregulation [52–54]. In parallel, long-term PM2.5 exposure elevates cerebrovascular mortality and stroke risk [55–57], which reinforces the plausibility that sustained greenness—via stress reduction, activity support, cooler microclimates, and partial pollutant mitigation—accumulates into a roughly linear health gain. Direct causal evidence for IL-6/CRP reduction specifically due to greenness remains limited; current biomarker findings are suggestive but not definitive [58].

Greenness can buffer pollution harms by (i) intercepting particles (leaf deposition), (ii) increasing source–receptor separation (barrier effects), and (iii) cooling air through shade and evapotranspiration, which can change chemistry and exposure [59–62]. At the same time, vegetation can emit BVOCs that contribute to ozone and secondary organic aerosol under some conditions, so net effects vary by species, climate, and urban form [63, 64]. Large cohorts show that higher greenness attenuates pollution-related mortality, supporting the pollution to health pathway being modified by greenness [14, 65]. Our data also indicate that the greenness to health relationship strengthens under high pollution, especially where traffic emissions are intense—consistent with near-road contexts where vegetation barriers matter most. NO₂ is a strong marker of traffic emissions and near-road exposure. Street-scale studies suggest vegetation’s interception and barrier effects are most influential very near sources, which often means road traffic [66, 67]. Thus, effect modification is often clearest for NO₂. By contrast, regionally homogeneous pollutants (e.g., some components of PM2.5) may be less sensitive to small-area greening unless design and placement are optimized (hedges close to sources, permeable canopies). Evidence at this micro-scale is mixed across cities; species choice and canyon geometry remain key uncertainties.

Older adults are more vulnerable to heat and pollution; they plausibly gain more from stress-relief, cooling, and small reductions in exposure afforded by greenness. Urban residents may benefit more because baseline greenness is lower and traffic exposure is higher, so marginal gains are larger [65]. Sex differences are less clear; they may reflect different time–activity patterns and near-road exposure in daily life (unknown mechanism; evidence is limited). We therefore interpret sex contrasts cautiously and flag them as a target for future work.

Our findings are generally consistent with earlier studies that reported protective effects of greenness on cardiovascular and stroke outcomes [65, 68]. Previous research from regions like Europe and North America has also shown that greenness can reduce stroke mortality, though the magnitude of the effect varies across contexts. For instance, studies in urban Canada have highlighted the protective role of green space in mitigating cardiovascular and stroke mortality [69], while studies in high-pollution urban areas in Asia emphasize the interaction between greenness and air pollution [70, 71]. Similar to these studies, we observed that higher greenness exposure was linked to lower mortality risks. What sets our study apart is the focus on both short-term and long-term greenness exposure, which allowed us to capture different temporal patterns of protection. In addition, we applied a bidirectional effect modification framework to examine how greenness and air pollution interact. This approach goes beyond traditional single-exposure models and highlights how protective effects of greenness are amplified under high pollution, especially for NO₂. Finally, our large cohort from Shandong Province, a region with high pollution and marked urban–rural contrasts, provides new evidence from a LMIC setting, where data remain scarce.

This study has several limits. First, it is a single-province cohort from Shandong. The findings may not generalize to regions with different pollution mixes, greenness patterns, or health systems. Second, the design is observational. We adjusted for many covariates, but residual confounding may remain. We did not measure time–activity patterns, mobility, occupational exposures, green-space quality, or access, which could affect estimates. Third, exposure assignment may have error. NDVI/EVI were at 1-km resolution, while NO₂ and SO₂ were at ~ 10-km resolution, and we used area-level addresses (village/community or higher when needed). These choices can cause exposure misclassification. Fourth, our greenness metrics capture cover, not type or use. They do not reflect maintenance, biodiversity, or actual visitation. Fifth, our time windows (season of death for contemporaneous; survival period for cumulative) may miss other critical windows. Finally, effect-modification estimates for NO₂ at the highest exposure stratum were large. These findings warrant further investigation using continuous modeling of NO₂, including linear and nonlinear specifications, to evaluate the stability of the observed effects.

Conclusions

This study emphasizes the critical role of greenness exposure in lowering mortality risk among IS patients, particularly in older adults, males, and urban residents, where protective effects are most pronounced. Notably, greenness exposure may enhance public health benefits by mitigating the harmful effects of air pollution in high-pollution environments. Therefore, increasing greenness coverage and improving urban green infrastructure planning—especially in highly polluted areas—could have substantial benefits for population health and help reduce mortality rates.
