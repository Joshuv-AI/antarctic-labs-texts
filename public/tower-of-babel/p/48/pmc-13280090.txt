The Gut–Oral–Skin–Heart Microbiota Axis and Oxidative Stress: Where Have We Been and Where Are We Going
Authors: Irena Girna, Alicja Kuban-Jankowska, Anna Jakubczyk-Slabicka, Adam Kosiński, Marcin Gruchała, Magdalena Górska-Ponikowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScientific interest in the relationship between cardiovascular disease and the human microbiome has grown substantially in recent years, with particular emphasis placed on the gut microbiota. Emerging evidence suggests, however, that distinct microbial niches—including the skin, oral cavity, and gut—may interact to influence cardiovascular homeostasis through shared pathogenic pathways, most notably oxidative stress and systemic inflammation. Intestinal dysbiosis has been strongly linked to the increased production of microbial metabolites such as trimethylamine N-oxide, which promotes endothelial dysfunction, oxidative imbalance, and atherogenesis. The oral microbiome has similarly been implicated in cardiovascular pathology through mechanisms encompassing chronic low-grade inflammation, transient bacteremia, and direct vascular colonization by pathogenic species. The contribution of the skin microbiome to cardiovascular risk remains less well defined; nevertheless, chronic inflammatory dermatoses—psoriasis in particular—have been consistently associated with elevated cardiovascular risk, a relationship potentially mediated by systemic immune dysregulation and oxidative stress. This review synthesizes current evidence on the gut–oral–skin–cardiac microbiome axis, underscoring the central role of oxidative stress as a convergent pathogenic mechanism and examining potential therapeutic implications. A more comprehensive understanding of these interconnected systems may inform the development of novel, microbiome-targeted strategies for the prevention and management of cardiovascular disease.Keywords: Gut microbiome, Oral microbiome, Skin microbiome, Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), Cardiovascular diseases, Oxidative stress

Key Summary Points

Alterations in the human microbiome are increasingly recognized as significant contributors to cardiovascular disease, operating through interconnected inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways.This review examines the interdependent roles of the gut, oral, and skin microbiota within a unified microbiome axis, and their collective influence on cardiovascular homeostasis.Key microbial metabolites, including trimethylamine N-oxide, are strongly implicated in endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and the progression of atherosclerotic disease.Chronic inflammatory dermatoses, psoriasis in particular, further substantiate the concept of systemic immune dysregulation as a mediator of elevated cardiovascular risk.A deeper understanding of these microbiome–cardiovascular interactions may inform the development of novel diagnostic biomarkers and microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

The gut–oral–skin–cardiac microbiome axis represents a complex network of interrelations between distinct microbial ecosystems and their collective impact on host physiology, including skin integrity and cardiovascular function. Among these, the gut microbiota has been extensively studied for its beneficial influence on cardiovascular health [1]. Scientific evidence indicates that intestinal dysbiosis—an imbalance in the gut microbial community—is associated with several cardiovascular pathologies, such as hypertension, atherosclerosis, and heart failure (HF) [2]. In the context of HF, reduced perfusion can lead to intestinal ischemia and mucosal edema, compromising the gut barrier and facilitating translocation of bacteria and their metabolites into systemic circulation [2]. This process triggers both local and systemic inflammatory responses, which may subsequently disturb the homeostasis of the skin and oral microbiota [3].

Further supporting this interconnectedness, disruptions in the oral microbiome have also been implicated in cardiovascular dysfunction. Specific oral bacterial species, such as Streptococcus mutans and Porphyromonas gingivalis, have been directly associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease [4].

The cutaneous microbiome plays a fundamental role in immunological defense and pathogen resistance. Dysregulation of the skin microbiota contributes to the etiopathogenesis of inflammatory dermatoses, which may indirectly influence cardiovascular health through immune-mediated mechanisms [5]. The purpose of this review is to present a thorough and integrative summary of existing findings relating the gut, oral, and skin microbiota to cardiovascular disorders, with specific emphasis on oxidative stress as a unifying pathogenic mechanism. By investigating the dynamic interplay between these diverse microbial habitats, this work attempts to outline potential system-level interactions within the gut–oral–skin–cardiac axis and to discover developing translational prospects for risk stratification and targeted therapeutic intervention.

Oxidative Stress in Cardiovascular Diseases

Recent studies have demonstrated a significant association between intestinal dysbiosis and heightened oxidative stress, alongside systemic inflammation, particularly concerning the cardiovascular system [6–8]. The interplay between the human host and the gut microbiota, as well as the contribution of oxidative stress to cardiovascular pathogenesis, is essential for elucidating mechanisms that affect human health [9].

The gut microbiota produces a range of metabolites—such as reactive sulfur species (RSS), short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)—that modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, both of which play pivotal roles in the development of cardiovascular diseases. Notably, TMAO, a key metabolic byproduct of intestinal microbial activity, has been strongly linked to increased oxidative stress in cardiovascular conditions. Evidence suggests that TMAO exacerbates oxidative imbalance by disrupting the equilibrium between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant capacity [10–12].

Moreover, TMAO affects endothelial inflammatory pathways by modulating the availability of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and nitric oxide (NO). It also contributes to platelet hyperreactivity by intensifying oxidative stress within endothelial cells. These pathophysiological mechanisms emphasize TMAO’s critical role in endothelial dysfunction and underscore its potential as a target for further mechanistic and therapeutic investigation in cardiovascular diseases [13, 14].

The diverse oral microbiome similarly exerts significant effects on both local and systemic health. Oral microbial communities produce several bioactive compounds, including SCFAs (e.g., butyrate, acetate, and propionate), lactate, hydrogen sulfide (H2S), NO, and TMAO. SCFAs have demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties that support oral and cardiovascular homeostasis. H2S contributes to vascular tone regulation and inflammatory processes, while NO exerts multifaceted roles in vasodilation and blood pressure regulation. As previously mentioned, TMAO has been implicated in atherosclerosis progression and overall cardiovascular risk [15, 16].

The skin microbiota also produces various metabolites, including SCFAs (e.g., acetate, propionate, and butyrate), hydrogen peroxide, lactate, xenobiotics, and organic acids [17]. However, the specific impact of these skin-derived metabolites on oxidative stress and cardiovascular function remains poorly understood and warrants further exploration (Fig. 1).

The Gut Microbiome in Cardiovascular Disease

Intestinal bacteria are taxonomically divided into several key phyla, including Actinobacteria, Pseudomonadota, Fusobacteria, and Verrucomicrobiota, with Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes being the predominant families. In HF, reduced perfusion to abdominal organs leads to alterations in the composition of the gut microbiota. This includes decreased oxygen supply to intestinal epithelial cells, resulting in hypoxia and a metabolic shift toward anaerobic pathways [2].

Current research has elucidated the influence of microbial secondary metabolites—such as bile acids, SCFAs, and TMAO—on cardiovascular diseases and their underlying pathogenic processes. SCFAs positively affect gut mucosal integrity by reinforcing epithelial barrier function and mitigating apoptosis via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. Conversely, TMAO may exert direct deleterious effects on the heart by promoting cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis, endothelial and arterial inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes, ultimately contributing to the progression of HF [12].

Savi et al. demonstrated that TMAO impairs cardiomyocyte contractility by disrupting intracellular calcium metabolism, leading to diminished calcium clearance and increased glycogen accumulation—findings that provide preliminary evidence for TMAO’s mechanistic involvement in cardiac dysfunction [18].

Pasini et al. were among the first to report increased levels of pathogenic gut flora—such as Campylobacter, Shigella, Salmonella, Yersinia enterocolitica, and various Candida species—in stool samples from patients with chronic HF compared to healthy controls. Venous congestion within the intestinal wall, a hallmark of chronic HF, exacerbates clinical symptoms and enhances systemic absorption of toxic microbial metabolites [19].

Comparative studies of healthy individuals and those with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) showed a higher abundance of pro-inflammatory microorganisms in patients with HFpEF [20, 21]. At the same time, there was a reduced abundance of anti-inflammatory bacteria (e.g., Lachnospiraceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, Ruminococcaceae) capable of inducing regulatory T (Treg) cells, including sCD25-positive populations, and peripheral macrophages through butyrate production [22, 23]. Consequently, microbial diversity was lower in patients with HFpEF.

Eubacterium hallii has been identified as a critical regulator of metabolic balance and intestinal microbial homeostasis through its SCFA production. It can convert glycerol into 3-hydroxypropionic acid (3-HPA), an intermediate in the biosynthesis of cobalamin (vitamin B12) and 1,2-propanediol (1,2-PD), a precursor to propionate [24]. Cobalamin plays a pivotal role in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism, neurological function, erythropoiesis, and DNA/RNA synthesis in erythroblasts. Vitamin B12 deficiency leads to elevated homocysteine levels, a recognized cardiovascular risk factor [24].

Recent evidence has revealed that gut microbiota metabolize phosphatidylcholine into trimethylamine (TMA), which is transported to the liver and subsequently oxidized to TMAO by flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3). TMAO actively participates in modulating the biochemical reactivity of several physiologically critical molecules [25].

Elevated plasma TMAO levels are associated with an increased risk of chronic kidney disease, HF, atherosclerosis, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes—all conditions that significantly heighten the likelihood of major adverse cardiovascular events. Moreover, circulating TMAO levels may serve as a biomarker for HF severity and prognosis [26].

The Skin Microbiome in Cardiovascular Disease

Multiple studies have suggested a potential correlation between the presence of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and atopic dermatitis (AD) [27]. Some research highlights an association between sleep disturbances in individuals with AD and cardiovascular-related insomnia [28]. Additional hypotheses indicate links among AD, obesity, and metabolic syndrome—recognized as major contributors to cardiovascular dysfunction. Furthermore, these comorbidities are often associated with a sedentary lifestyle frequently observed in patients with AD [29].

The inflammatory cascade in AD that contributes to cardiovascular pathology is similar to the one observed in conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis. The role of T helper type 2 (Th2) cytokines has been established in the pathogenesis of AD; however, their involvement in atherosclerosis remains inconclusive. In contrast, T helper type 1 (Th1) cytokines play a critical role in both rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis, as well as in atherogenesis [30].

In this context, psoriasis represents one of the most extensively studied models linking chronic cutaneous inflammation to cardiovascular disease [31]. Psoriasis is a systemic immune-mediated disorder characterized by activation of the Th1 and Th17 pathways, leading to sustained production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), interleukin (IL)-17, and IL-23. These mediators contribute not only to keratinocyte hyperproliferation but also to systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [32].

A growing body of epidemiological evidence indicates that patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis exhibit an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality, often independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors [33–35].

Despite these observations, the extent to which the cutaneous microbiome directly modulates cardiovascular risk remains incompletely understood [36]. Further studies are warranted to elucidate whether skin-derived microbial signals contribute to systemic effects within the gut–oral–skin–cardiac axis.

Atopic dermatitis offers additional insights into barrier breakdown and allergen-driven immune activation, whereas psoriasis offers a model of systemic inflammatory load.

Disruption of epidermal barrier integrity facilitates allergen penetration through the epithelium, triggering a Th2-mediated immune response. This enhanced Th2 activity leads to B cell stimulation and increased IgE production, which in turn activates basophils and mast cells. Allergens also tend to bind to dendritic cells, promoting their activation and the subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines that exacerbate the existing inflammatory state [37, 38].

In a large-scale cohort study conducted in the UK between 1998 and 2015, Silverwood et al. demonstrated an association between AD and various cardiovascular conditions, including myocardial infarction, unstable angina, stroke, HF, atrial fibrillation, and cardiovascular mortality. However, it remains unclear whether this predisposition arises from the underlying disease or from pharmacological interventions used in its management [39].

Data from three major US surveys—NHANES, the National Health Interview Surveys (NHIS) from 2012, and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2005–2006)—confirmed that AD is associated with an increased risk of peripheral arterial disease, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, angina, and congestive HF. These findings emphasize the elevated risk of both cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in individuals with AD [40].

Moreover, a recent 35-year longitudinal study conducted in Denmark reported approximately a 20% increased risk of developing atrial fibrillation among individuals with AD [41]. Numerous other studies have confirmed a higher predisposition to cardiovascular events, including coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, HF, ischemic stroke, and peripheral vascular disease, in patients suffering from AD [41–43].

Conversely, an analysis by Thyssen and Egeberg suggested a low likelihood that AD acts as an independent risk factor for CVD. Given these conflicting findings, further mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the cellular and molecular underpinnings of this association. Furthermore, as previously noted, the functional role of the cutaneous microbiome and its metabolite interactions with the cardiovascular system remain poorly characterized. Further investigations are essential to explore the potential etiopathogenic link between the skin microbiome and cardiovascular diseases [44].

The Oral Microbiome in Cardiovascular Disease

Periodontal diseases have been directly linked to cardiovascular disorders. Numerous studies have confirmed a high prevalence of infections and inflammatory conditions in the periodontal region among individuals with atherosclerosis. Additionally, chronic periodontitis has been associated with increased susceptibility to myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, stroke, and atherosclerosis [45, 46].

Microbial analysis of dental plaque has revealed the involvement of key bacterial species—such as Actinobacillus actinomycetemcomitans, Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola, and P. gingivalis—in the thickening of the intimal and medial layers of the carotid arteries [47]. Furthermore, experimental animal models have demonstrated that inoculation with P. gingivalis promotes advanced atherosclerotic lesions. For instance, ApoE-knockout mice (apoE−/−) developed atherosclerotic plaques following bacterial exposure, in contrast to control animals [48].

Periodontal infections can lead to transient bacteremia, facilitating the invasion of vascular endothelial cells. Oral bacteria employ various mechanisms to evade host immune responses. Notably, oral microbial components have been identified in atherosclerotic plaques, aortic aneurysms, cardiac valves, and within the arterial tree—an area commonly affected by atherosclerotic changes. Specific pathogens, including T. forsythia, A. actinomycetemcomitans, Prevotella intermedia, and P. gingivalis, have been detected in coronary arteries [4, 49, 50].

Substantial evidence supports the notion that systemic inflammation is a major driver of atherogenesis. This has been correlated with elevated circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, TNFα, and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1), as well as acute-phase proteins such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and fibrinogen. Among these, serum CRP is recognized as a highly reliable biomarker for predicting cardiovascular events and underscores the inflammatory component of atherosclerotic progression [51–53].

CRP contributes to foam cell formation by enhancing macrophage uptake of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and promoting monocyte recruitment to the arterial wall. Additionally, CRP upregulates the expression of endothelial cell adhesion molecules, including ICAM-1, sVCAM-1, and selectins, whose elevated levels have been linked to heightened cardiovascular risk and are regarded as indicators of endothelial activation [54–56].

Emerging studies further indicate that oral microbiota may colonize the gastrointestinal tract and contribute to the development of systemic inflammation, which in turn may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of HF [57].

Limitations

Despite the growing body of evidence linking the microbiome to cardiovascular diseases, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, much of the current literature remains observational in nature, limiting the ability to establish causal relationships between microbial alterations and cardiovascular outcomes. Second, significant heterogeneity exists across studies in terms of population characteristics, methodological approaches, and microbiome profiling techniques, which may affect the comparability and generalizability of findings. Third, while the role of the gut and oral microbiota has been relatively well explored, the contribution of the skin microbiome remains insufficiently characterized, with limited mechanistic and longitudinal data available. Furthermore, the complex interplay between microbial metabolites, host immune responses, and oxidative stress pathways is not yet fully elucidated. Finally, potential confounding factors—including diet, lifestyle, pharmacological treatments, and comorbidities—are not consistently controlled across studies, further complicating interpretation. These limitations underscore the need for well-designed, prospective, and mechanistic studies to better define the role of the microbiome within the gut–oral–skin–cardiac axis.

Conclusions

The emerging concept of a gut–oral–skin–cardiac microbiome axis underscores the complexity of host–microbe interactions in cardiovascular disease. While the contribution of the gut and oral microbiota is increasingly well characterized, the role of the skin microbiome remains comparatively underexplored, despite compelling evidence linking chronic inflammatory skin disorders—particularly psoriasis—to heightened cardiovascular risk.

A unifying element across these microbial ecosystems appears to be the induction and modulation of systemic oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation, both of which are central to cardiovascular pathophysiology. However, current evidence remains largely associative, and the precise mechanistic pathways linking these microbiome niches to cardiovascular outcomes require further elucidation.

Future research should prioritize integrative, multi-omics approaches and longitudinal studies to clarify causal relationships and identify actionable therapeutic targets. In this context, modulation of the microbiome through dietary, pharmacological, and lifestyle interventions may represent a promising avenue for personalized cardiovascular prevention strategies.

Ultimately, advancing from compartmentalized models toward a systems-level understanding of microbiome–host interactions may redefine current paradigms in cardiovascular medicine.
