Physiological indicators associated with drought tolerance and post-drought recovery in tall fescue
Authors: Mozhgan Abtahi, Mohammad Mahdi Majidi, Hadi Taleb, Ahmad Reza Behrooz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDefining the metabolic strategies employed by tall fescue to withstand and recover from drought events will be crucial for maintaining the performance and stability of turf and forage aspects in the future; however, research on this vital topic is scarce. This study assessed 142 tall fescue genotypes for forage production, physiological, and biochemical responses under two irrigation regimes from 2019 to 2021. In 2022, a two-month drought was imposed to evaluate post-drought recovery. Drought stress decreased photosynthetic pigments (5.26–27.89%) and yield (18.40-34.16%) in tall fescue genotypes. Simultaneously, antioxidant enzyme activities (catalase, ascorbate peroxidase, peroxidase) increased by 40%, 28%, and 31%, respectively, and osmo-protectants increased by 48.88–67.74%. Genetic variation among genotypes indicates potential for drought tolerance selection. The research demonstrates that drought tolerance and recovery in tall fescue are genetically variable and heritable, enabling selection for improved breeding. Under drought, catalase, ascorbate peroxidase, peroxidase, chlorophyll content, and proline can enhance forage yield. However, under control conditions, plant height and crown diameter predict yield in control conditions. Post-drought recovery relates to carbohydrate accumulation and antioxidant enzyme activity; genotypes 6 and 9 showed 20% higher recovery rates. Twenty-one top genotypes (12, 105, 3, 40, 1, 104, 4, 22, 86, 2, 11, 96, 5, 94, 88, 9, 89, 64, 106, 100, and 18) are highlighted for their productivity and drought resilience. Biochemical and physiological markers facilitate early drought tolerance screening, while this study’s novelty lies in its integrated physiological, biochemical, and genetic approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52705-2.Keywords: Recovery, Grass, Antioxidant enzymes, Osmolytes, Drought toleranceSubject terms: Genetics, Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Global climate change models predict that the frequency and severity of droughts will increase, exacerbating condition in drought-prone regions in the coming decades1. Drought reduces productivity by decreasing water potential, stomatal conductance, transpiration, photosynthetic rates, and dry matter accumulation. It also triggers oxidative stress by increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which can harm lipids, proteins, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids2,3. Leaf-based physiological and biochemical traits have the potential to enhance stress adaptation and performance under abiotic stresses4. To reduce and prevent oxidative damage, plants have developed an antioxidant defense system consisting of enzymes like superoxide dismutase, catalase, peroxidases, monodehydroascorbate reductase, glutathione reductase, dehydroascorbate reductase, and other nonenzymatic components such as α-tocopherol, ascorbate, and reduced glutathione; these antioxidants eliminate, neutralize, and scavenge the ROS at various cellular locations5. Considering the challenges posed by climate change, water scarcity, frequent droughts, destructive floods, severe soil erosion, and insufficient fodder, grasses play a crucial role in nature. Apart from their ecological role in maintaining biodiversity, anti-erosion capacity, and essential carbon sequestration, they contribute to forage production, enhance soil structure, reduce runoff, maintain pastures, enhance urban aesthetics, and ensure health and food security by supplying raw materials for dairy and protein production6,7.

The grasses used in dry areas with hot summers must not only be flexible enough to adapt to these conditions but also be able to withstand drought stress, recover after irrigation interruption, and tolerate summer heat and winter cold8. Therefore, improving the persistence and survival of perennial species is crucial in regions with prolonged drought. Additionally, the rapid recovery of damaged plant tissue and the regrowth of new tissue after drought stress, once water is available, are vital for managing perennial grasses to ensure quick stand re-establishment. Post-drought recovery relies heavily on the regeneration of existing leaf tissue and the growth of new tissue from crowns, stolons, and rhizomes of grass plants9,10. Most perennial grass species have specific advantages, such as rapid recovery after repeated summer drought, which can help them remain in pastures and compete better with other species. The ability of plants to accumulate biomass through rapid growth plays a crucial role in the recovery and regeneration of new tissue after drought stress11. Selecting traits associated with high survival and recovery after prolonged drought stress may be more economically important than focusing solely on improved growth during droughts. Traits that enhance post-drought recovery and survival improve competition with less drought-tolerant species and ultimately improve persistence in pastures12.

For the regrowth and rehydration of existing leaves damaged by drought, physiological factors that control cellular hydration and cell integrity may be crucial. Maintaining cellular hydration and membrane integrity in leaves could enhance the recovery ability in cool-season grass species9,13. In forage grasses, carbohydrates play a crucial role in drought recovery by providing energy for regrowth and maintaining cell turgor. These reserves are mobilized to support the production of new organs and the repair of damaged tissues during the recovery phase. Specifically, high levels of nonstructural carbohydrates such as fructan and starch, are linked to drought tolerance and the ability to recover after drought stress14. The results obtained by Perlikowski and Skirycz15 demonstrated that remodeling patterns of the primary metabolome and lipidome in the crown under drought and recovery differed between genotypes of Festuca arundinacea. Highly drought-tolerant plants accumulated higher levels of primary metabolites in the crown tissue during drought, especially carbohydrates, which could function as osmo-protectants and storage materials. In turfgrass, proline accumulation is not only a stress signal but also alleviates plant damage by maintaining photosynthesis, supporting antioxidant enzyme activities, and preserving levels of non-enzymatic antioxidant compounds. This reduces reactive oxygen species content and helps regulate osmosis16. Research on Cynodon dactylon (bermudagrass) reveals that antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, POD) rise sharply during drought and remain elevated during recovery. This sustained antioxidant activity helps mitigate oxidative damage, allowing tissues to repair and resume growth effectively17. Similar findings were observed in tall fescue, where antioxidant enzymes helped clear reactive oxygen species generated upon rehydration, a process that is critical because rewatering often triggers oxidative bursts18. Safari and Majidi19 demonstrated that increased proline content during drought stress correlates with improved drought tolerance in tall fescue cultivars. In perennial ryegrass, proline accumulation during drought stress and its subsequent decrease upon rewatering suggests a dual role: aiding in drought tolerance through osmotic adjustment and supporting recovery by providing nitrogen for new protein synthesis as the stress subsides20. The responses of grass species to drought stress and their ability to produce forage during and after drought vary greatly. Among the grasses, the fescue genus stands out as one of the largest and most diverse genera in the Gramineae family. It is commonly utilized for both forage and turf purposes, encompassing more than 450 species varying from diploid (2n = 2x = 14) to dodecaploid (2n=12x = 84). Among these, tall fescue (Festuca arundinacea Schreb) stands out as a prevalent and significant species known for its drought tolerance and superior fodder yield in arid environments21,22. It comprises numerous attributes typical of the drought-avoidance strategy, including leaf rolling and the development of long roots and thick cuticles. This species also possesses the ability to recover rapidly after stress cessation15.

Understanding different strategies for drought tolerance, such as recovery after drought and the alterations in crucial physiological traits during drought events and the subsequent post-stress recovery, can enhance our understanding of the response mechanisms of different tall fescue genotypes to drought stress. Previous studies shown the grass species exhibit varying morphological and physiological responses to different durations and intensities of drought stress. Mechanisms that facilitate the ability of turfgrass to recover and persist after drought have been studied in diverse perennial forage species, such as orchardgrass23,24, smooth bromegrass24, tall fescue, meadow fescue, sheep fescue, perennial ryegrass, Italian ryegrass, and Lolium × hybridum12. Cernoch and Kopecky25 indicated a wide range of variation in drought tolerance and after-drought recovery within Festuca species. They observed rapid regrowth of tetraploid meadow fescue after irrigation was resumed. Tall fescue genotypes showed little to no decline during the drought period and substantial regrowth after irrigation was restarted. Among tall fescues, the Mediterranean morphotype, characterized by summer dormancy as a drought avoidance mechanism, demonstrated the highest potential as a source of drought tolerance and exhibited greater growth during autumn. However, the genetic variation in physiological characteristics and antioxidant enzymes, and their responses to drought stress, and their relationship to post-drought recovery remain poorly understood, limiting our ability to predict which genotypes will survive or recover after drought and to develop targeted breeding and management strategies that enhance resilience. The present study aimed to evaluate a large and diverse germplasm panel of tall fescue genotypes to identify key agronomic and physiological responses, together with changes in antioxidant enzyme activities, under multi-year drought stress. In addition, this study sought to determine how these traits contribute to the post-drought recovery capacity of genotypes following prolonged water-deficit conditions.

Results

Genetic diversity in productivity in response to a 3-year drought stress

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) indicated significant variability among the tall fescue genotypes for dry forage yield (DFY), plant height (PH), and crown diameter (CD). The effects of irrigation treatments and genotype × irrigation treatment interaction were also significant for these traits (Table 1). Mean comparisons of 3-year forage yield, PH, and CD for two moisture environments for tall fescue genotypes are presented in Table 2. All mean values decreased under drought stress conditions. Drought stress decreased DFY, PH, and CD by 34%, 18%, and 24%, respectively. There was a marked genetic difference in DFY ranging from 23 g/plant to 771 g/plant under control conditions and from 18 g/plant to 470 g/plant under drought stress treatment, indicating broad genetic variability among genotypes. A wide range of variation was observed for DFY with genotypic coefficient of variation of 41% and 48% under control and drought stress conditions, respectively. Moderate estimates of heritability were observed for DFY under control and drought stress treatments (0.48 and 0.40, respectively) (Table 2).

Genetic diversity in physiological and biochemical responses

The ANOVA results revealed highly significant variation in physiological traits among the 142 tall fescue genotypes (Table 1). The impact of irrigation treatments and genotype × irrigation treatment interaction was also substantial for all investigated features (Table 1). Under drought stress conditions, values of chlorophyll a (Chla), chlorophyll b (Chlb), and total chlorophyll (TChl) were decreased. At the same time, ascorbate peroxidase activity (APX), Catalase activity (CAT), peroxidase activity (POX), protein content (PRO), proline content (PRL), carotenoid content (CART), and soluble carbohydrates (CAR) were increased (Table 2). Under control conditions, TChl ranged from 1.48 mg/g leaf to 3.33 mg/g leaf, CART from 0.22 mg/g leaf to 0.65 mg/g leaf, and PRL from 1.63 mg/g leaf to 6.26 mg/g leaf. For CAT, the range was 0.04 µmol min−1mg−1protein to 0.16 µmol min−1mg−1protein; for APX, 0.12 µmol min−1mg−1protein to 0.22 µmol min−1mg−1protein; and for POX, 0.33 µmol min−1mg−1protein to 2.14 µmol min−1mg−1protein. Under drought stress conditions, TChl extended from 1.34 mg/g leaf to 3.63 mg/g leaf, CART from 0.24 mg/g leaf to 0.74 mg/g leaf, and PRL from 2.42 mg/g leaf to 10.74 mg/g leaf. For CAT, the range varied from 0.05 µmol min−1mg−1protein to 0.6 µmol min−1mg−1protein, for APX it ranged from 0.11 µmol min−1mg−1protein to 0.60 µmol min−1mg−1protein, and for POX it ranged from 0.33 µmol min−1mg−1protein to 3.75 µmol min−1mg−1protein (Table S2). Genetic variation and heritability estimates were higher under drought stress than in the control condition for all measured physiological traits except Chla, Chlb, and TChl (Table 2). Heritability estimates were moderate to high (0.63–0.79) for all traits under both moisture treatments (Table 2).

Genetic diversity in recovery following prolonged drought stress

All recovery-related traits showed significant genotype effects (P < 0.01), so that genotype explained a large proportion of the phenotypic variation. The effect of irrigation treatments and genotype × irrigation treatment interaction was also significant (p < 0.01) for all measured traits (Table 1). The results of mean comparisons of irrigation treatments for the recovery rate (RR) in the fourth year (2022), after stopping irrigation for two months and then re-watering, showed that drought stress reduced recovery forage yield (RFY), recovery height (RPH), recovery crown diameter (RCD), and recovery rate (RR) by 24%, 14%, 21%, and 38%, respectively (Table 2). The range of RR in tall fescue genotypes was 4.5–9.4 under control conditions and − 2.50–7.6 under drought stress conditions. The RFY was extended from 18 g/plant to 422 g/plant under control conditions and from 13 g/plant to 251.5 g/plant under drought stress conditions. Based on genotypic coefficients of variation, a higher range of genetic variation was observed in both moisture treatments for RFY. Heritability estimates varied from 0.46 for RFY to 0.71 for RCD under control conditions and from 0.39 for RFY to 0.63 for RCD under drought stress conditions (Table 2).

Response to selection and correlated response

Indirect selection is a valuable method for selecting genotypes for complex inheritance traits such as dry matter yield (DMY). Table 3 displays the outcomes of response to selection, correlated response to selection for enhancing DMY through the selection of other measured traits, and the relative efficiency of indirect selection. In general, all traits showed a greater response to selection than DMY under both irrigation treatments (Table 3). The highest values of correlated response and relative efficiency of indirect selection (greater than 1) for improving DMY were obtained via selection for CAT, APX, POX, PRO, and TChl under drought stress conditions (Table 3). Under control conditions, the relative efficiency of indirect selection was lower than unity for all traits except for PH and CD (Table 3).

Relationships among traits and selection based on multiple traits

In the present study, the relationship between traits and the selection of genotypes based on the multiple traits was done by the principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 1). Results of the PCA based on agro-morphologic, physiologic, STI, and recovery-related traits under two irrigation treatments are given in Fig. 1. The PCA results justified that the two main principal components (PC1 and PC2) described approximately more than 49.34% and 60.29% of the total genetic variation under control and drought stress treatments, respectively. The angle between vectors clearly showed that DFY was highly correlated with PH, CD, RFY, CAT, APX, PRO, and PRL under control irrigation treatment. Under these conditions, RFY was associated with CD, PH, CAT, POX, APX, TChl, PRL, and PRO. Under drought stress conditions, the association of traits was considerably altered. So, a significant association was found between DFY and PH, CD, STI, RR, PRO, and PRL. Under these conditions, RFY and CAT, POX, APX, TChl, Chla, Chlb, PRL, PRO, and CAR were positively correlated. TChl, CAR, PRO, PRL, CAT, POX, and APX were positively correlated with STI. Under control conditions, the PC1 had a higher positive correlation with Chla, TChl, PRL, CAT, POX, and APX, and was named “photosynthetic capacity”. The PC2 had a negative correlation with RFY, RPH, RCD, and RR, and was named “recovery potential’’. If the purpose is the selection of genotypes with high recovery abilities, then genotypes occupied in the negative side of PC2, regions “III” and “IV”, should be selected. However, if the goal is to improve photosynthetic capacity, then genotypes distributed on the positive side of PC1 and grouped in regions I and IV of the biplot (Fig. 1) are suitable.

Discussion

Differences in the responses of tall fescue genotypes to drought stress varied in physiological and morphological characteristics, forage yield, and recovery after re-watering. The significant variability observed for all measured traits may be attributed to the extensive germplasm collection from various geographical locations, which facilitated a thorough analysis of genetic diversity and screening for future programs. Application of drought stress significantly reduced the growth of tall fescue genotypes and induced notable changes in their physiological attributes. However, the magnitude and nature of these responses varied considerably among genotypes, highlighting substantial genetic variation in drought tolerance and adaptive strategies within the germplasm panel. Drought response involves the initial perception of drought stress by the plant, activation of drought-specific signaling pathways (e.g., abscisic acid production), stomatal closure to reduce transpiration and water loss, and a reduction in growth rate and leaf area to limit water demand26. Drought stress led to a notable decrease in forage yield and its components, aligning with prior research findings in various forage species12,27–30. Also, prolonged drought stress over consecutive years may weaken plant vigor, leading to a significant reduction in recovery in the tall fescue genotypes. Biochemical/physiological shifts include a shortened growth period for plants, resulting in less light exposure, reduced photosynthesis from chlorophyll damage, increased antioxidant activities that lead to higher energy consumption by plants to combat drought. Some changes like stomata closure, reverse quickly with re-watering, but others cause permanent damage26. In other words, re-watering leads to stomata reopening for gas exchange, energy conservation via altered photosynthesis and respiration, and proline accumulation to protect cells. Activation of repair enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase activity (CAT), and peroxidases activity (POX) mitigate oxidative damage by fixing damaged membranes and proteins and removing accumulated ROS. Mobilization of stored carbohydrates and nutrients supports recovery growth and re-establishment of normal physiological homeostasis and metabolic functions. Finally, epigenetic modifications and priming may enhance future drought tolerance.

High estimates of genotypic variation for dry forage yield (DFY), recovery forage yield (RFY), protein content (PRO), proline content (PRL), and antioxidant enzymes can help achieve the fescue breeding goal of developing high-yielding cultivars with strong recovery capabilities and physiological advantages. Moderate to high estimates of heritability for forage yield, physiological, and recovery-related traits suggest minimal environmental influence on these traits, potentially hindering genotype selection efficiency. Thus, phenotypic selection may effectively enhance genetic advancement. Overall, by our findings, moderate to high estimates of heritability for these traits have been observed in different grass species12,28,29,31.

Indirect selection could be a valuable breeding method to make quick advancements in yield if the associated response and selection efficiency are high32. Under drought stress treatment, the correlated response of DFY was maximum through selection for CAT, ascorbate peroxidase activity (APX), POX, total chlorophyll content (TChl), and PRL. The relative efficiency of indirect selection for these traits was also high, indicating a physiological basis for the genotypic response to drought stress. Therefore, by selecting genotypes that exhibit elevated antioxidant enzyme levels, breeders can indirectly select for plants with superior stress resilience and growth performance. Under controlled moisture treatment, physiological traits played a minor role in improving DMY through indirect selection, while plant height (PH) and crown diameter (CD) were deemed crucial in this regard. In orchardgrass Abtahi et al.29 suggested that under drought stress conditions, Chla, Chlb, TChl, and CAR were important for enhancing forage yield through indirect selection.

A positive correlation was discovered between dry forage yield and traits associated with recovery, suggesting that genotypes with higher forage yield can sustain their yield after drought damage when re-watered or during rainfall events. Consistent with our findings, Taleb et al12. reported that forage yield was closely related to survival and persistence in seven cool season species. Also, Positive correlations were observed between recovery after prolonged drought and total aerial biomass and persistence in orchardgrass and smooth bromegrass2431,. Physiological dissection uncovers a positive link between antioxidant capacity and drought tolerance in tall fescue. Also, our results suggest that measuring TChl, carotenoid content (CART), PRO, and PRL levels in plants can be valuable for assessing their resilience to drought stress. Therefore, antioxidant enzymes and related metabolites form a defense system that mitigates drought-induced oxidative stress, enabling the plant to sustain better growth and physiological performance under drought conditions27 Nourbakhsh et al.33 also highlighted the role of antioxidant enzymes in enhancing stress tolerance in orchardgrass. Positive correlation between traits related to recovery and drought tolerance index (STI) suggests that stress recovery is a key indicator for assessing drought tolerance. These results suggested that the plant’s ability to maintain functions during drought and to rapidly recover after re-watering are important for determining final productivity in tall fescue. Similar to our findings, positive relationships have been reported between recovery rate and drought tolerance index in tall fescue under drought stress conditions12. The correlation analysis revealed that physiological capacity and drought recovery are strongly related, and potentially share some common mechanisms. A full recovery from drought requires that plants reactivate all physiological and metabolic systems/pathways suppressed by stress and repair damage caused by drought, enabling them to resume growth34. Based on the regression results, a positive relationship was found between DFY, recovery rate (RR), and RFY, which indicates that genotypes with higher forage yield maintain yield following the drought breaks. In this respect, preferred genotypes were identified as superior in developing high-yielding forage varieties that can withstand prolonged drought stress. Developing genotypes with consistent yields under both drought and post-drought conditions can enhance the sustainability of forage crops and ensure livestock feed security in areas prone to drought.

Comparing the means of moisture treatments for traits related to the photometric pigment content revealed that chlorophyll a content (Chla), chlorophyll a content (Chlb), and TChl were higher in the control treatment compared to the drought stress treatment. Conversely, CART showed the opposite trend. Leaf chlorophyll is crucial for photosynthesis, and its reduction is linked to drought-induced leaf senescence. in different plant species35. Higher chlorophyll content effectively improves the absorption and utilization of light energy in leaves, promoting the recovery of photosynthesis. The literature data also confirmed that stress sensitivity can be correlated with the extent of chlorophyll degradation in forage grass species induced by a moisture deficit36. According to Staniak et al.37 the components of the photosynthetic apparatus could be damaged in drought-sensitive genotypes, whereas drought-tolerant genotypes have a relatively good adaptability for reducing or avoiding the impairment caused by drought stress. Therefore, breeding programs should prioritize selecting for genotypes that maintain higher chlorophyll levels under drought conditions to develop varieties with more stable yields in water-limited environments30. Additionally, chlorophyll content recovers after re-watering, enhancing light absorption and photosynthesis recovery. Studies show recovery within days to a week, varying by drought severity and plant species38. Given the role of carotenoids in protecting photosystems, breeders can use carotenoid content as a selection criterion for drought tolerance, particularly in early-stage breeding lines5.

Regarding antioxidant enzymes, the activities of CAT, POX, and APX increased significantly under drought stress, indicating tall fescue’s adaptability to adversity. Many studies show that the activity of antioxidant enzymes affects drought stress tolerance in plants39,40. Drought stress increased the levels of CAT, POX, and APX in Dactylis glomerate. The authors inferred that elevated levels of these enzymes enhance tolerance to drought stress and are crucial in mitigating its negative impacts41. In other words, the greater the number of compounds effective in plants’ antioxidant activities, the greater the plants’ ability to neutralize the harmful effects of free oxygen radicals in peroxidizing lipids, proteins, and even nucleic acids, and ultimately the greater the tolerance against stress42. Therefore, genotypes exhibiting the highest antioxidant enzyme activity under drought stress conditions are recommended.

Drought stress led to a notable rise in PRL content, possibly due to activation of proline synthesis or inhibition of proline oxidation43. Proline accumulation in plants is linked to increased tolerance to various stresses5. Therefore, based on our research, we recommend using genotypes with the highest proline content under drought stress conditions. There is a consensus among plant researchers that the accumulation of proline is advantageous for plants, particularly during their recovery from stress44.

The protein content increased as a result of drought stress treatment. Fariaszewska et al45. and Meisser et al46. found that mild drought stress notably raised the protein content in forage grasses. However, other studies have shown a decrease in protein concentrations under drought stress47. Genotypes with high protein under drought are important because the extra protein helps them save water, maintain cell function, and fix dehydration damage, letting them survive and recover from drought better than wild-type plants48. Our findings suggest drought stress increases leaf carbohydrate accumulation, an indicator of physiological activity. Carbohydrates function as energy reserves, maintain cell turgor, and protect against stress49.. In tall fescue, this accumulation may serve as a reserve or aid osmotic adjustment. Accumulation in crowns/rhizomes is vital for regeneration of new shoots and roots9. The accumulation of soluble sugars and osmolytes during droughts is also reported in Festulolium braunii, Festuca arundinacea, and Lolium perenne cultivars50 Genotypes with elevated STI values are regarded as more tolerant to drought stress, indicating they can endure drought conditions and recover effectively after experiencing drought51.

The similarity of the genotypes assessed through principal component analysis, resulted in the identification of four distinct groups under both moisture treatments. The genotype 41 from region I, which displays the highest photosynthetic capacity, could be combined with traits from other genotypes to improve recovery or stress tolerance. Additionally, genotypes 18 and 9, found in region IV, exhibit both high photosynthetic capacity and strong recovery potentials, making them prime candidates for breeding programs focused on maximizing carbon assimilation and biomass production. Under drought stress treatment, genotypes 36 and 86 showed the highest yield and antioxidant capacity, along with low recovery potential and drought tolerance. Breeders should consider incorporating these genotypes in crosses with genotypes possessing stronger recovery and drought tolerance. The identification of genotypes 49, 93, and 29 with superior antioxidant capacity holds significant promise for breeding programs aiming to improve forage quality and stress resilience Genotypes 6 and 9 displayed the highest potential for recovery and drought tolerance are valuable for breeding cultivars that recover effectively after drought. Additionally, the superior performance of genotypes 1 and 22 makes them valuable candidates for breeding programs aimed at improving crop productivity and antioxidant capacity. Our research highlights several key applications in tall fescue germplasm screening and breeding programs. Physiological and biochemical markers enable early detection of drought tolerance traits, enabling for faster, more efficient screening than traditional field evaluations. By measuring indicators such as relative water content, osmolyte accumulation, antioxidant enzyme activity, and chlorophyll fluorescence, researchers can screen a large panel of tall fescue germplasm to identify genotypes with superior drought tolerance. Screening for traits related to recovery after drought stress can help in developing varieties that not only survive drought but also recover quickly, maintaining productivity. Selecting parent plants with favorable physiological and biochemical traits can improve the chances of producing drought-tolerant offspring. The limitations of this study include the following: Artificially imposed drought rather than natural occurrences might skew genotype responses. Ignoring soil type, nutrient status, and biotic stresses that interact with drought response may oversimplify drought tolerance assessment. Without suitable check cultivars for forage and seed yields, it becomes challenging to establish a baseline for comparison and accurately evaluate the performance of the studied varieties. Integrating multi-location yield trials and accounting for environmental diversity is essential for robust evaluation of drought tolerance in genotypes.

Conclusion

Our research indicated that the productivity and physiological capacity of tall fescue are influenced by genetic factors, followed by meteorological conditions, particularly moisture levels. Drought stress modifies photosynthetic components and the antioxidant scavenging system, impacting plant growth and development negatively. In conclusion, various genotypes exhibited distinct capabilities for forage production, physiological capacity, and recovery rate under both control and drought stress treatments over three years. These abilities demonstrate the degree of genetic variation and the significant potential of the examined germplasm for introducing appropriate genotypes for semi-arid and arid regions, as well as for developing new varieties for specific breeding purposes. Our results endorse physiological characteristics for indirect selection for forage yield in drought-affected tall fescue. Tall fescue genotypes counteract drought stress damage through a comprehensive network that includes enzymatic antioxidants. A positive association between recovery-related traits and STI suggests that stress recovery is a key indicator of drought tolerance. These results suggested that the plant’s ability to maintain functions during drought and to rapidly recover after re-watering are important for determining final productivity in tall fescue. Tall fescue’s post-drought recovery efficiency depends on its antioxidant capacity and increased accumulation of osmolytes. The top yielding genotypes, 12, 105, 3, 40, 1, 104, 4, 22, 86, 2, 11, 96, 5, 94, 88, 9, 89, 64, 106, 100, and 18 which showed adequate drought tolerance and photosynthetic capacity, represent promising endeavors to improve tall fescue genotypes for drought-prone areas. However, additional experiments would be required to evaluate turf traits and their correlation with persistence and recovery after drought in the germplasm under consideration.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The plant materials in this study comprised 142 tall fescue genotypes (Table S1), chosen from a genetically diverse population of 1,200 primary genotypes with varying levels of sensitivity and tolerance to drought, forage production and morphological traits based on previous studies grass breeding team at Isfahan University of Technology. In other words, these 142 genotypes represent promising breeding genotypes selected based on preliminary observations under both control and drought stress conditions, with the goal of selecting future parent genotypes for developing new synthetic varieties for turf and forage in arid regions.

Experimental design and treatments

The experiment took place at the research farm of Isfahan University of Technology in Isfahan, Iran (320 30’N, 510 20’ E), with an average annual temperature of 14.5 °C and precipitation of 140 mm, from 2019 to 2022. In this region there is no rain in summer from early June to mid-October, so crops must be irrigated during those months. The climate is classified as low semi-desertic by Gaussen and subtropical steppe by Köppen, respectively12. The soil at the field test site was a silty clay loam Typic Haplargid (pH 7.5) with an average bulk density of 1.4 g/cm3. The seedlings of each genotype were cultivated in the greenhouse for three months before being moved to the field in early February 2019, following a randomized complete block design with four blocks (two for each irrigation treatment). Each plot (genotype) had 20 individual plants arranged in two rows. The distance between rows was 50 cm, and the plants within a row were also 50 cm apart. During the first three years post-planting (2019–2021), the genotypes were assessed for agro-morphological and physiological performance under both control and intense drought stress irrigation treatments). Climatic conditions in each cultivation year are shown in Table S2. Under the control irrigated level, irrigation was conducted with no limitation and supplied when 50% of the total available water was depleted from the root zone. Under intense drought stress, irrigation was carried out when 85% of the total available water was exhausted from the root zone52. In each year, the irrigation treatments were continuously applied throughout the growing season, from the first of May to the middle of August. Depending on weather conditions, the irrigation intervals varied during the growing season and among the three water environments (in the control environment, 5–8 days and in drought stress, 15–20 days). Soil samples were collected every two days from different sites within each irrigation treatment at three depths (0–20, 20–40, and 40–60 cm) to determine the gravimetric soil–water content and irrigation frequency. For this purpose, an auger was used to create appropriately sized holes in the field for sampling. The soil samples were then dried in an oven at 70 °C for 48 h to determine soil moisture content. The irrigation timing for the control treatment was determined by soil water depletion in the root zone, with a maximum allowable depletion of 0.5.

Measurements

Measurement of productivity and morphological traits

Morphological and forage-related traits were evaluated under two irrigation regimes (control and drought stress) for three years during 2019 and 2021. Plant height (PH, cm) was measured as the distance from the base of the plant to the tops of the three tallest panicles at full anthesis. Crown diameter (CD, cm) was assessed as the width of the plant after each cut. Forage yield was expressed as dry forage yield (DFY, g/plant), the weight of forage harvested per plant after drying at 72 °C for 48 h. In each year, there were three cuts and the mean of cuts used for analysis.

Measurement of physiological traits

Leaf sampling for physiological traits was conducted two weeks after the application of drought stress under two different irrigation regimes, between 2019 and 2021, only in the first cut. To assess enzymatic activity, 0.1 g of leaves was collected and promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, they were ground in a 1 ml solution consisting of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 1% Polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (w/v), two mM Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.2% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris hydrochloride (Tris–HCL). The homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected for enzyme assays. Catalase (CAT) activity was determined by measuring the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm over a 2-minute period after the breakdown of H2O253. Moreover, APX activity was determined by the reduction in absorbance at 290 nm over two minutes54. The peroxidase (POX) activity was measured by the absorbance increase at 470 nm over two minutes55. Control of antioxidant enzyme activity was achived by expressing enzyme activity relative to the amount of protein present in the sample, determined by protein concentration assays like the Bradford assay. This control accounts for variations in sample size or protein content, allowing for comparison across samples. The enzyme activity was then controlled to mg of protein, providing activity units as micromoles of H2O2 decomposed per minute per mg protein (µmol min−1mg−1protein). To assess chlorophyll and carotenoid levels using Acetone, 0.1 g of finely chopped fresh leaves was utilized and mixed with 10 ml of 80% acetone. The mixture was then centrifuged at 5000–10,000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting liquid was transferred, and the process was repeated until the residue lost its color. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 480, 645 nm, and 663 nm against the solvent (acetone) blank. The concentrations of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid were determined using the provided equation based on mg/g leaf56:

Bradford’s (1976) method, which relies on the binding of protein molecules to Coomassie dye under acidic conditions, was utilized to determine the protein concentration of total plant extracts. Initially, the fresh leaves were weighed and homogenized with 2 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer. After homogenization, each sample was transferred to 2 mL vials. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 12 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used to measure the total soluble protein, expressed as mg g− 1 DW.

Proline was extracted from 0.5 g of each frozen leaf sample. The extraction process involved grinding the samples in 10 ml of 3% (v/v) sulphosalicylic acid. The resulting mixtures were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min. After centrifugation, 2 mL of the supernatant was transferred to a test tube, followed by the addition of 2 mL of freshly prepared acid ninhydrin solution. The test tubes were placed in a water bath at 90 °C for 30 min, and the reaction was stopped using an ice bath. Each reaction mixture underwent liquid-liquid extraction with 5 mL of toluene, which was vortex-mixed for 15 s. The test tubes were then left undisturbed for at least 20 min in the dark at room temperature to allow for the separation of the toluene and aqueous phases. Subsequently, the toluene phase from each sample was carefully collected in a clean test tube, and its absorbance was measured at 520 nm. A standard curve using analytical grade proline was prepared to determine the free proline content in each sample, expressed as µmol/g leaf. Total water-soluble carbohydrate determination was conducted using the phenol-sulfuric acid method, expressed as mg/g leaf57. This involved adding 1 mL of 5% phenol solution and 5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid to 200 µL of the sample, then reading the absorbance at 510 nm after 20 min. Sucrose was utilized as the standard.

Recovery after long drought stress

After a three-year evaluation of genotypes (from 2019 to 2022), drought recovery of all genotypes was assessed in 2022. In the middle of October 2020, after the summer harvest, a long acute drought was imposed on all previous treatments (normal and intense drought stress) by stopping irrigation for 60 days (from 15 October to 15 December) until grass foliage was entirely desiccated. Then to allow for prolonged drought stress recovery, all genotypes were subsequently irrigated to the point of field capacity every week. After almost 40 days (near the end of January) of regular re-watering, the recovery rate (RR) was measured. RR was visually scored based on the scale of 0 to 9, where genotypes with green and fully hydrated leaves were rated as 9, and desiccated brown/dead leaves were rated as 0.
