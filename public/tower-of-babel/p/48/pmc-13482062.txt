Determinants of retention into HIV care among adolescents living with HIV in two selected districts in Copperbelt Province of Zambia
Authors: Soko John, Kaonga Patrick, Jacobs Choolwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRetention into Active Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) care is critical indicator of HIV treatment success, enabling long and healthy lives. However, adolescents face unique challenges, including low retention rates, high new infection rates, and higher AIDS-related morbidity and mortality. This study investigated factors affecting retention in HIV care among adolescents in two selected districts in the Copperbelt Province in Zambia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was carried out in two public health facilities, one in Ndola and the other in Masaiti District, in the Copperbelt Province of Zambia between July 2023 and May 2024. Data were collected using a pre-tested, validated questionnaire administered to adolescents living with HIV aged 10 to 19 years, and their responses were linked to their medical records. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with retention in HIV care. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.ResultsOut of 237 participants, majority were females (55.3%). Retention in HIV care was 91.1%, with Ndola district at 94.2% and Masaiti district at 88.0%. Key factors associated with retention included suppressed viral load (AOR 10.41, 95% CI:1.83, 59.01) and having a treatment supporter (AOR 10.91, 95% CI:1.71, 69.55).ConclusionsAdolescent retention on ART in the Copperbelt Province was 91.1%, indicating generally strong program performance, though some gaps remain. Adolescents in rural areas faced greater barriers and showed higher levels of attrition. Retention was higher among those with treatment supporters and among those with sustained viral suppression, underscoring the value of strong adherence support systems. To build on these gains, there is a need to strengthen adolescent-centred, context-specific interventions; particularly in rural settings where health system challenges are more pronounced. Improving support for adherence and engagement in care will be key to sustaining viral suppression and advancing broader HIV control efforts by 2030.Keywords: HIV/AIDS, HIV stigma, retention, HIV care, adolescents, ART

Introduction

Globally, an estimated 5 million young people aged 15–25 years are living with HIV, and adolescents have worse treatment outcomes than both children and adults on antiretroviral therapy (ART).1–4 In sub-Saharan Africa, HIV services are typically organized into paediatric and adult clinics, and adolescents often receive care in systems not tailored to their needs. 5 This contributes to poorer adherence, retention, and viral suppression in this age group.3,4

In Zambia, adolescents, especially females, experience a relatively high rate of new HIV infections.6,7 Among adolescents, HIV incidence is estimated at 0.6% in females and 0.1% in males annually, and only about 71.7% of young people living with HIV are on ART.6,7 Low comprehensive HIV knowledge (38%), early sexual debut (17%), and low condom use (33.7%) further increase risk of acquisition and onward transmission. 8 Available evidence also suggests that adolescents have lower retention in care and poorer adherence than adults, with some data indicating worse outcomes in rural settings, 9 although findings from Uganda show that rural children can have better adherence and clinical outcomes than urban peers. 10

Studies from other settings have identified multiple factors influencing adolescent retention in HIV care, including access to adolescent-friendly services and peer support, family and provider support, ART adherence, stigma and discrimination, mental health and psychosocial support needs, and health system characteristics such as accessibility and quality of care.11–16 These issues are especially important during the transition from paediatric to adult services.5,14,16,17

Despite Zambia’s overall progress toward the UNAIDS 95–95–95 targets, important gaps remain among adolescents. 18 The 2021 Zambia Population-based HIV Impact Assessment (ZAMPHIA) estimated that 73% of adults living with HIV know their HIV status, 87% of those diagnosed are receiving ART, and 90% of those on treatment have achieved viral suppression.17,19 In contrast, adolescent-specific indicators remain limited and inconsistent. Although studies conducted in intervention settings have reported that up to 88.3% of adolescents know their HIV status, these estimates may not reflect the broader adolescent population. Furthermore, only approximately 78.7% of adolescents living with HIV are receiving ART, and although about 89% of those on ART are reportedly retained in care, diagnosis and treatment coverage remain lower than those observed among adults.1,17,19 These disparities indicate that adolescents continue to experience significant gaps across the HIV care continuum. However, few studies in Zambia have specifically examined factors associated with retention in care among adolescents living with HIV, highlighting an important evidence gap that this study seeks to address.6,17,20

This study therefore aimed to assess factors associated with retention in HIV care among adolescents aged 10–19 years attending ART services in Ndola and Masaiti districts, to inform context-specific interventions for Zambia.

Methods

Study Design and Setting: This facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Arthur Davison Children’s Hospital (ADCH) in Ndola and Masaiti District Hospital in Masaiti District. ADCH, located about 320 km from Lusaka, is a tertiary referral hospital in an urban setting, serving over 2,000 adolescents enrolled in HIV care. Masaiti District Hospital, approximately 300 km from Lusaka and 60 km from Ndola, represents a rural context with about 500 adolescents in care. The two sites were chosen for their contrasting urban and rural settings, high attrition rates, and sufficient sample sizes, enabling comparative analysis of retention in care among adolescents living with HIV.

Sample Size and Sampling: The study population consisted of adolescents receiving ART at Arthur Davison Children’s Hospital (ADCH) in Ndola and Masaiti District Hospital in Masaiti District. A stratified random sampling strategy was used, with the two health facilities defined as separate strata. Within each stratum, adolescents were randomly selected from the ART registers to achieve proportional representation from both sites. The sample size was determined using the single-population proportion formula, assuming a 50% prevalence (a conservative estimate selected due to limited local prevalence data). The calculation was based on a 95% confidence level and 80% power, with an additional 10% added to account for potential non-response and missing data. This resulted in a final sample of 246 adolescents on ART, allocated proportionally between the two facilities.

Data Collection: Data on retention in HIV care were obtained through a combination of structured interviews and document reviews conducted during routine clinic visits. A structured questionnaire was used as the primary data collection tool. The questionnaire was adapted from existing HIV program monitoring and evaluation tools used by the Zambian Ministry of Health and the World Health Organization (WHO), which ensured content validity. To further enhance reliability, the tool was pretested among a small sample of adolescents (not included in the final study) at a similar health facility, and necessary adjustments were made for clarity and contextual relevance. The primary outcome of interest was retention in HIV care at 12 months after ART initiation. Retention was defined as a patient being alive and actively engaged in care at the reporting facility 12 months after ART initiation, with no documented interruption in care exceeding 90 days since the last scheduled appointment. Patients who were transferred out were censored at the date of transfer, while those who died or were lost to follow-up (defined as no contact for >90 days) were classified as not retained. This definition is consistent with standardized HIV program monitoring guidelines from the World Health Organization and the PEPFAR.17,19,21–23 Retention was assessed at 6- and 12-months post-ART initiation, with 12-month retention designated as the primary endpoint and 6-month retention treated as a secondary outcome between October 2021 and September 2023. 17 Data collected included demographic and clinical characteristics such as age, sex, duration on ART, HIV disclosure status, and viral load results. Information from interviews was cross-verified with clinical records to strengthen accuracy and reduce bias. Data collection lasted four weeks, with interviews conducted in private settings within the facilities to ensure confidentiality and encourage open responses.

Measurement of Internalized HIV Stigma: To understand internalized HIV stigma, we used a tool called the Internalized AIDS-Related Stigma Scale (IARSS). 23 It’s a simple and widely used questionnaire designed for people living with HIV. The scale has six short statements that reflect how a person might feel about themselves because of their HIV status. For each statement, participants either agreed or disagreed. We scored each response as 1 for “agree” and 0 for “disagree,” giving a total score between 0 and 6. Higher scores mean higher levels of internalized stigma. For analysis, we grouped the scores into two categories: low and high stigma. A score of 3 or more was considered high stigma, based on how the tool has been used in previous studies.

Measurement of ART Adherence: To assess how well adolescents were taking their treatment, we used a combination of methods rather than relying on just one. First, we asked participants to recall how many doses they may have missed over the past 30 days. Based on this, adherence was classified as high (taking at least 95% of doses) or low (less than 95%). We also looked at viral load results as an objective measure. Viral suppression defined as having less than 1000 copies/Ml, was used as an indicator that treatment was being taken consistently, in line with guidance from the World Health Organization and UNAIDS. 23 Where the data were available, we also considered whether adolescents were attending their clinic appointments on time. This helped give a more complete picture of how engaged they were in their care.

Eligibility criteria

The study included adolescents aged 10 -19 years who initiated ART on or before September 2023 and were enrolled in HIV care at selected hospitals in Zambia, with complete clinical records sufficient to assess retention and follow-up time. Eligible participants were residents within the facility catchment area and, where applicable, had provided assent with caregiver consent in line with national ethical guidelines. Exclusions included adolescents with incomplete records, those transferring into facilities after ART initiation, individuals without verifiable follow-up data, and those lacking assent or caregiver consent.

Data Analysis: Data was checked daily for accuracy and analysed using Stata 16.0 (Stata Corp., Texas, USA) Descriptive statistics were computed to characterize the study population in terms of sociodemographic and other pertinent factors. The relationship between each independent variable and outcome variable (retention into HIV care) was initially determined by bivariable logistic regression analysis and variables with a p-value less than 0.2 were subsequently included in the multivariable analysis. Finally, variables with a p-value ≤ 0.05 were considered to be significantly associated factors with retention into HIV care. The direction and strength of the associations were determined by the adjusted odds ratio (AOR). Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test was used to evaluate the fitness of the final model.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Zambia Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (UNZA-BREC, Ref. No. 3982-2023) and the Norwegian Regional Committee for Medical and Healthcare Research Ethics (REK, Ref. No. 550650). In addition, written permissions were secured from the Provincial Health Office, relevant District Health Offices, the Senior/Medical Superintendent, and departmental/clinic in-charges. Written informed consent was obtained from parents/guardians and from adolescents aged 18 years and above, while assent was obtained from those under 18 years. Privacy was protected by conducting interviews in private rooms. Confidentiality was ensured by using unique study codes instead of names, secure storage of all study documents, and restricted access to records. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw or skip any question at any time without any consequences. Participants were not reimbursed for their time, and no financial or material incentives were provided.

Results

Participants characteristics

The calculated sample size for the study was 246 adolescents, of whom 237 were included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 96.3%. Nine participants were excluded due to incomplete or missing clinical records (n = 6) and refusal or inability to provide assent or caregiver consent (n = 3). Comparison of the available demographic characteristics of included and excluded participants showed no meaningful differences, suggesting minimal selection bias. Participants were recruited from Ndola and Masaiti districts, representing two sentinel ART sites in Zambia. Probability proportional to size was applied at the design stage to allocate the sample according to the adolescent ART population at each facility. As a result, Ndola contributed the larger share of participants, accounting for 50.6% (120/237) of the final sample, while the remainder were recruited from Masaiti District Hospital. Among the adolescents included in the analysis, 55.3% (131/237) were female and 62.4% (148/237) were aged 15–19 years. Most participants, 71.3% (169/237), had been on ART for five years or longer. Although the mode of HIV acquisition was not directly assessed, the long duration on ART suggests that a substantial proportion may have acquired HIV perinatally. Most adolescents had a treatment supporter (93.3%; 221/237), while nearly half had never accessed youth-friendly spaces (49.4%; 117/237). The majority demonstrated good knowledge of HIV and ART (90.7%; 215/237). Stigma was the most commonly reported barrier to retention in care (53.6%; 127/237), whereas a strong connection with clinical staff was the leading reported motivation for retention (53.2%; 126/237). Discussion

This study examined the prevalence of retention in HIV care and its associated factors among adolescents aged 10–19 years receiving ART in selected districts of the Copperbelt Province, Zambia. At 12 months, retention in care was 91.1%, indicating generally strong program performance in the study settings. Despite this encouraging finding, some adolescents were not retained in care, highlighting persistent gaps that require attention. Retention and attrition were influenced by several factors, including viral load status, involvement of treatment supporters, perceptions of youth-friendly services, adolescent motivation, barriers to care, internalized stigma, and access to youth-friendly spaces. Global targets set by UNAIDS focus on achieving 95% viral suppression among individuals on ART, rather than specifying a direct retention benchmark. Nevertheless, sustained retention in care is critical for achieving viral suppression and reducing HIV transmission. This finding underscores the need for targeted interventions that address the identified barriers and strengthen adolescent-focused HIV services. Efforts to improve continuous engagement in care through enhanced psychosocial support, stigma reduction, and youth-centred service delivery may further improve retention outcomes and support broader HIV control efforts.

The study found that prior viral load status was significantly associated with retention in care. Adolescents with suppressed viral load were more likely to remain engaged in care than those with unsuppressed viral load, in line with studies done elsewhere. 23 However, this relationship should be interpreted with caution, as the temporal link between viral suppression and retention is not straightforward. Viral suppression is typically achieved through sustained adherence and ongoing engagement in care. At the same time, adolescents who remain in care are more likely to achieve and maintain viral suppression. Therefore, the observed association may reflect a bidirectional relationship or reverse causality rather than a direct causal effect. Similar interpretations have been reported in studies examining retention and treatment outcomes among adolescents living with HIV. 24 Suppressed viral loads and peer support are crucial in retaining adolescents living with HIV (ALHIV) in care. 24 This finding underscores the importance of monitoring viral loads and providing structured peer support to ensure continuous care and better health outcomes. 25 We recommend strengthening viral load monitoring systems to enhance retention rates among ALHIV.

Additionally, those who were part of treatment support groups, such as Community Adolescent Treatment Supporters (CATS), showed higher retention rates, consistent with a study in Zimbabwe, where CATS provided peer-led support that improved linkage to services and retention in care.21–25 Peer support is crucial in retaining ALHIV in care.21,26 This finding underscores the importance of providing structured peer support to ensure continuous care and better health outcomes. 26 These models are particularly beneficial in rural areas where access to services is limited.23,26 We recommend expanding peer support programs like CATS to enhance retention rates among ALHIV. Regular training and support for peer supporters can also improve the effectiveness of these programs in all facilities, including hard-to-reach areas. Expanding such programs can help bridge the gap between urban and rural retention rates. 27

The study also found that adolescents’ perception of youth-friendly services played a crucial role in their retention. Facilities that offered services tailored to adolescents’ needs, such as flexible clinic hours and a welcoming environment, had higher retention rates, similar to findings from other settings.24,27 The study observed potential differences between urban and rural facilities based on descriptive findings. However, these differences were not formally assessed using stratified analysis. Disparities between urban and rural facilities highlight the need for equitable distribution of youth-friendly services to ensure all adolescents receive the care they need.24–27 We recommend that future research explore this issue in more depth using stratified or longitudinal designs. Such approaches would provide a clearer understanding of how facility type influences retention in care among adolescents living with HIV. In parallel, there is a need to strengthen youth-friendly services, particularly in rural and hard-to-reach areas. Practical measures, such as flexible clinic hours, more welcoming environments, and training healthcare providers to better respond to adolescents’ needs, could significantly improve engagement in care.

Several barriers to retention were identified, including social, economic, and systemic factors. Adolescents often face competing priorities such as school and family commitments, limited understanding of the importance of continuous care, lack of support at home, and financial constraints for transport and services. 28 Systemic issues include inconvenient clinic hours and lack of adolescent-responsive service providers.26,28 1Addressing these barriers is critical for improving retention rates among ALHIV. Unresolved barriers can lead to higher attrition rates and poorer health outcomes. We therefore recommend implementing strategies to mitigate barriers to retention, such as providing transportation support, offering flexible clinic hours, and training healthcare providers to be more responsive to adolescent needs. Community education programs can also help increase understanding of the importance of continuous care.

The study found that adolescents from rural facilities appeared to experience greater challenges with retention in care than those from urban facilities. However, this urban–rural difference should be interpreted cautiously because it was based primarily on descriptive patterns rather than a formal stratified or multivariable comparison.29–32 Urban facilities appeared to have better access to adolescent-responsive supports, including youth-friendly clinic days and Community Adolescent Treatment Supporters (CATS), whereas rural facilities had more visible service gaps.30–35 These observations suggest possible contextual inequities, but the present study cannot conclude that residence or facility location independently caused differences in retention.36,37

The findings nevertheless highlight an important programmatic concern. Where youth-friendly services (services designed to be accessible, acceptable, and responsive to adolescents) and peer-support structures are less available, adolescents may face greater difficulty remaining engaged in care.36–39 A more cautious interpretation is therefore that strengthening adolescent-friendly services in rural and hard-to-reach settings could help improve equity in care, although this hypothesis should be confirmed in studies specifically designed to compare urban and rural outcomes.37–40

Significance for public health

Adolescents living with HIV remain a vulnerable population with poorer continuity of care and treatment outcomes than many other age groups. In this study, retention at 12 months remained below the broader UNAIDS 95% treatment target framework, and retention was shaped most clearly by factors with stronger empirical support in the analysis, including viral load status, treatment support, and adolescent-responsive service experiences. From a public health perspective, these findings support targeted interventions that strengthen psychosocial support, peer and family treatment support, and access to youth-friendly HIV services. Particular attention should be given to rural and underserved settings, while recognizing that the observed urban–rural differences in this study were descriptive and require further analytical confirmation.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study population was heterogeneous because information regarding HIV acquisition status was unavailable. The inability to distinguish between adolescents with perinatally acquired HIV and those with behaviourally acquired HIV may have introduced important differences in clinical history, treatment experiences, psychosocial needs, and adherence patterns. These differences may have influenced retention outcomes and limited the interpretation of subgroup-specific factors.

Second, adolescent mobility may have contributed to loss to follow-up. Adolescents frequently relocate because of schooling, employment, family migration, or changes in caregiving arrangements. Consequently, some adolescents classified as lost to follow-up may have transferred to other health facilities without formal documentation. This may have resulted in misclassification of retention status and a possible underestimation of true retention rates.

Third, some participants became unreachable despite exhaustive tracing efforts. This may partly be explained by inaccurate patient locator information and inconsistencies between ART registers, SmartCare records, and appointment registers. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified factors and retention outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the study utilized primary data collected from both rural and urban settings, thereby providing important evidence on adolescent retention in HIV care in Zambia.

Conclusion

Retention in HIV care among adolescents is critical for achieving viral suppression and improving health outcomes. The study found that retention rates among ALHIV in selected districts in Zambia, are influenced by various factors, including previous viral load results, treatment support, perception of youth-friendly services, barriers to retention, internalized HIV stigma, and access to youth-friendly spaces. There is a significant disparity between urban and rural facilities, with rural areas facing numerous unmet needs. To improve retention rates, it is essential to implement differentiated service delivery models, enhance peer support, address social and systemic barriers and combat internalized stigma. Equitable distribution of resources between urban and rural areas is crucial in ensuring that all adolescents have access to the support and services they need. Maintaining high retention rates among ALHIV is key to achieving better treatment outcomes, reaching UNAIDS targets, and contributing to sustainable development goals. Future research should focus on exploring age-specific and health system factors associated with retention and attrition in care among diverse adolescent populations.

Key findings

Retention in HIV care among adolescents aged 10–19 years was 91.1% at 12 months, indicating relatively good retention but remaining below the UNAIDS 95% target. Viral load suppression, treatment supporter involvement and positive perceptions of youth-friendly services, were significantly associated with retention. Adolescents with suppressed viral loads and those receiving peer support were more likely to remain in care. Although urban–rural differences were observed, these findings were descriptive and not statistically assessed. Internalized stigma showed a negative but non-significant association with retention.

Key implications

The findings highlight the need to strengthen viral load monitoring, adherence support, and peer support interventions to improve retention among adolescents. Expanding youth-friendly services and enhancing healthcare provider responsiveness may promote sustained engagement in care. Addressing psychosocial and structural barriers, including stigma, inadequate support systems, and adolescent mobility, is also essential. These findings provide evidence to inform adolescent HIV programs and policies aimed at improving retention, achieving viral suppression, and advancing progress toward the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets and Sustainable Development Goal 3.
