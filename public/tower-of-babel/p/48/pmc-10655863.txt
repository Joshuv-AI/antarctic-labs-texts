Intersectionality in nursing research: A scoping review
Authors: Athena D F Sherman, Sarah Febres-Cordero, Kalisha Bonds Johnson, Kristen D Clark, Meredith Klepper, Brenice Duroseau, Yufen Lin, Wenhui Zhang, Mercy Coleman, Diane Prakash, Gaea A Daniel, Arzina Tabassum Chand, Ugo Kalu, Robin Tarter, Sarah Allgood, Sydney Cohen, Ursula Kelly, Monique Balthazar
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:An intersectional approach to health research provides an analytical foundation to explain the multidimensionality of health status, resource accessibility, privilege, oppression, and current and historical context. The use of intersectionality in health research has known limitations. Its use in health-related fields too often focuses on outcomes, such as health disparities, rather than processes, such as power structures and social determinants.Objective:This scoping review serves to examine how intersectionality has been implemented by nurses in the peer-reviewed literature. We offer insight into how it may be incorporated to inform future nursing research and healthcare provision.Design & Methods:Systematic searches of PubMed (n = 257), SCOPUS (n = 807), EMBASE (n = 396), CINAHL (n = 224), and Health Source: Nursing and Academics (n = 491), published since the seminal publication on intersectionality (1989 - 2023), identified 131 research articles that met inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction and synthesis were used to describe the breadth and depth of the literature specific to the application of intersectionality in nursing research.Results:The included studies used intersectionality to examine the intersections of numerous identities, such as race, gender, and immigration status. However, most studies were descriptive/observational in nature, underreported their methods, and conducted deficit-based research instead of strength-based inquiries. Of note, the vast majority of included articles were published within the last five years.Conclusions:Future researchers using intersectionality as a framework can improve their approach by reporting clear definitions and operationalization of intersectionality. Observational science dominated the included studies; future research should focus on intervention development and evaluation using an intersectional lens. Lastly, caution should be placed on research that focuses solely on deficits among marginalized communities, which places scientists at risk of perpetuating stereotypes or enhancing already-existing stigmas.Keywords: Black feminist theory, Intersectionality, Oppression, Social justice, Health equity

Background

The theory of intersectionality postulates that an individual’s overlapping demographics, social positions, and social identities interact to create positions of privilege or disadvantage. (Crenshaw, 1991) Intersectionality is rooted in Black feminism and was originally used by theorists to describe the combined effects of racism and sexism for Black women but has since been expanded in the literature and applied to other marginalized groups (Crenshaw, 1991; Ruiz et al., 2021; Bowleg, 2008; Bowleg et al., 2003; Crenshaw, 1989). An intersectional approach to healthcare examines how social determinants of health (i.e., interrelated structures that collectively determine health status and potential) influence patient outcomes (Palmer et al., 2019). Furthermore, it provides an analytical foundation to explain the multidimensionality of health status, resource accessibility, privilege, oppression, and current and historical context (Bowleg, 2012). The complex nature of intersectionality makes it a unifying and appropriate paradigm in which to explore predictors of health and health disparities, as it highlights disparate intersecting identities yet allows for targeted interventions and policies (Bowleg, 2012).

The use of intersectionality in health research has known limitations. Its use in health-related fields too often focuses on outcomes, such as health disparities, rather than processes, such as power structures and social determinants (Harris and Patton, 2019). This results in a focus on social identities as static qualities rather than socially- constructed aspects of individuals’ lives that may be developed or transformed (Kelly et al., 2021). Theorists have criticized intersectionality and its applications in research for this reason, as well as evidence that the theory is increasingly divorced from Black feminist scholarship (Rice et al., 2019). Despite this, intersectionality is a widely accepted theory that has useful applications to nursing research.

Intersectionality is being increasingly considered in health-related research. A 2019 scoping review of Intersectionality-based quantitative health research and sex/gender sensitivity noted that the majority of analyses incorporating intersectionality were from 2010 or later (Mena and Bolte, 2019). Public health studies of marginalized groups have highlighted how socio-cultural positions and historical and present day stigma drive elements of disadvantage and can contribute to negative health outcomes (Poteat et al., 2016; DeWilde et al., 2019; Barefoot et al., 2017; Rao et al., 2018; Hatchel and Marx, 2018). Nurses must also consider how patients’ interconnected identities contribute to their health and well-being – but to date, the extent of the use of intersectionality in nursing research is unclear (Ruiz et al., 2021). Thus, this scoping review serves to examine how intersectionality has been implemented by nurses in the peer-reviewed literature since its introduction (1989 – 2023).

We used the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) to guide our inquiry, which is an appropriate method to identify the nature and extent of peer-reviewed evidence with the goal of characterizing the quality and quantity of the known literature in terms of study design, methods, and location, among other key features of interest (Grant and Booth, 2009). Specifically, the purpose of this scoping review was to describe and critique the application of intersectionality in nursing research and provide recommendations for its future application. Herein we address the following guiding questions: What intersecting identities or social positioning have been observed? Were the investigations generally strength-based or deficit-based? Where was the research originating? What methods or study designs were being employed?

Methods

Search strategy and selection of articles

English language publications from PubMed (n = 257), SCOPUS (n = 807), EMBASE (n = 396), CINAHL (n = 224), and Health Source: Nursing and Academics (n = 491), published between January 1, 1989 (the year of the seminal publication on intersectionality) and June 19, 2023, were gathered for review. Variations of the following search criteria were used (dependent on database thesauruses) to identify appropriate articles: Intersectional* AND (framework* OR theor* OR model OR approach OR method) AND nurs*.For example, the complete PubMed search strategy was: (“intersectional*”[All Fields] AND (“framework*”[All Fields] OR “theor*”[All Fields] OR (“model”[All Fields] OR “model s”[All Fields] OR “modeled”[All Fields] OR “modeler”[All Fields] OR “modeler s”[All Fields] OR “modelers”[All Fields] OR “modeling”[All Fields] OR “modelings”[All Fields] OR “modelization”[All Fields] OR “modelizations”[All Fields] OR “modelize” [All Fields] OR “modelized”[All Fields] OR “modelled”[All Fields] OR “modeller”[All Fields] OR “modellers”[All Fields] OR “modeling”[All Fields] OR “modellings”[All Fields] OR “models”[All Fields]) OR (“approach”[All Fields] OR “approach s”[All Fields] OR “approachability”[All Fields] OR “approachable”[All Fields] OR “approache”[All Fields] OR “approached”[All Fields] OR “approaches”[All Fields] OR “approaching”[All Fields] OR “approachs”[All Fields]) OR (“method s”[All Fields] OR “methods”[MeSH Terms] OR “methods”[All Fields] OR “method”[All Fields] OR “methods”[MeSH Subheading])) AND “nurs*”[All Fields]) AND ((humans[Filter]) AND (english[Filter])).

Inclusion criteria: (1) available in English; (2) peer-reviewed original research articles; (3) explicitly reference Intersectionality theory, framework, or approach; (4) published during or after 1989 when Crenshaw coined the term “Intersectionality;” and (5) published in a nursing-focused journal or discussed implications for nursing research, nursing practice, or nursing education OR received funding from a nursing institution OR had a nurse as the first, second, or last author. Exclusion criteria: (1) reviews, methods papers, dissertations, conference abstracts without accompanying manuscripts, or psychometric evaluations. Articles were identified for inclusion based on two independent reviewer evaluations, using the review software Covidence (Veritas Health, 2021). Any conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer. The original version of this review was registered with PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/export_record_pdf.php; CDC42020192507); however, the design of the review has since changed to a scoping review (reported herein) (Sherman et al., 2020).

Data extraction, analysis, and synthesis

No original study authors were contacted in the process of data collection or in the review of the articles for eligibility. Each article was reviewed in its entirety for data points required for extraction and placed into an Excel spreadsheet. This process was completed by the data extraction team, and the extracted data underwent a 20 % audit by an independent reviewer for quality assurance. The audit revealed an overall inter-rater reliability of 0.92, with individual data extractors inter-rater reliability ranging from 0.74 to 1.0. Any data extractor whose inter-rater reliability was under 0.90 was required to complete remediation and re-extraction. Then a final audit of the final data extraction table was completed by a research team member to verify, discuss, and determine the final data to be included in the review. Extracted items included: (1) study background (e.g., year study was conducted, design [qualitative, quantitative, mixed methods], sample size, location, timeframe); (2) intersecting social positions examined; (3) method used to examine intersectionality; and (4) complementary theoretical frameworks used in the analysis. Overall descriptive characteristics of the studies are aggregated and reported in line with standard methods for scoping reviews (Grant and Booth, 2009; Peters et al., 2020; Tricco et al., 2018).

Characteristics of the reviewed studies

Among the 131 studies, 87 used a qualitative design, 35 used a quantitative design, and nine used mixed or multi-methods (Table 1). The included articles were published between 2007 and 2023 – with the greatest concentration of articles occurring in the last 5 years (Fig. 2). The locations where the study took place spanned 18 countries (Fig. 3) and six continents (Fig 4). Overall, 80 studies (61 %) used a deficit-based approach (i.e., focusing on the perceived weaknesses, barriers, or adversities related to people’s intersecting identities or risk factors), 10 studies (13 %) used a strength-based approach (i.e., focusing on the positive attributes of people’s intersecting identities or protective factors), and 39 studies (30 %) used both (Table 1). Generally, the included studies employed an intersectional approach to understand and explore the intersections of social identities (e.g., gender, age, race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic class) and social positions, processes of oppression or privilege, and policies and institutional practices to further understand the complexity of health outcomes (e.g., inequities in access to care).

Nearly 59 % (51 of 87) of the qualitative articles discussed and interpreted results based on their relation to historical oppression or privilege through an intersectional lens. Moreover, only 51 % (44 of 87) of the qualitative studies reported their specific analytical approaches to operationalize or examine intersectionality (e.g., coding for latent influences of generational trauma). Generally, for the quantitative studies, the vast majority used statistical approaches that included one-way ANOVA/chi-square analyses, linear or logistic regression models, generalized linear models, and structural equation modeling to examine intersections of social identities (e.g., age, gender, race, ethnicity) (Arana et al., 2019b; Ghosh et al., 2019; Greene et al., 2019b; Höglund et al., 2016; O’Brien et al., 2019). These common methods allowed for heterogeneity across intersections (e.g., cross-classified independent variables representing intersections, regression with stratification). Novel analytical methods were also identified, such as classification and regression tree, (Greene et al., 2019b) multiplicative model, (Ghosh et al., 2019) and Good life-index, (Höglund et al., 2016) among others. However, Bosse and Chido (Bosse and Chiodo, 2016) noted that using categorical data to identify intersecting attributes is insufficient for identifying the true complexities of intersectionality. Additionally, only 7 of the 35 quantitative studies (20 %) discussed and interpreted results based on their relation to historical oppression or privilege through an intersectional lens.

Lastly, in the nine mixed methods studies, six (DeWilde et al., 2019; Manca et al., 2022; Abubakari et al., 2021; Sherman et al., 2022; Holmström et al., 2017; Van Der Heever And Van Der Merwe, 2019) used analysis of qualitative and quantitative data to analyze or discuss the health experiences related to intersecting social identities or positions. We found the remaining three studies used either the quantitative or the qualitative method to examine intersectionality instead of presenting a true mixing of the findings through an intersectional lens. Additionally, only two of the nine mixed methods studies (22 %) discussed and interpreted results based on their relation to historical oppression or privilege through an intersectional lens.

Discussion

The purpose of this review was to describe the use of the Theory of Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1991; Crenshaw, 1989) by nurse scientists in peer-reviewed research. Although historically grounded in the Black feminist movement in the United States of America (Crenshaw, 1991; Crenshaw, 1989), it is evident that nurses around the globe recognize the importance of using an intersectional approach to research and policy change. In fact, this review identified English language articles in 18 countries across six continents. In acknowledging the intersection of multiple axes of social identity that coexist simultaneously to inform points of privilege and oppression (Crenshaw, 1991; Crenshaw, 1989), nurse scientists consistently investigated systems of and factors associated with oppression and privilege that influenced the health of study participants of diverse races, ethnicities, genders, and sexual orientations, among other social identities and positions. This serves to reinforce prior assertions of the usefulness of, as well as the necessity for, an intersectional framework in biomedical and health research (Kelly, 2009; Hankivsky et al., 2017; Abrams et al., 2020). While specific experiences and needs differ from population to population, the reality of coexisting social identities intersecting to predict experiences of power and oppression effecting health inequity is a universal reality that should always be taken into consideration in health sciences.

Through this review, we found that the majority (61 %) of the included studies focused mainly on deficits, such as perceived weaknesses, barriers, or adversities related to the intersecting identities or social positions of interest. This deficit-based approach to evaluating health is deeply ingrained in health sciences (Stoller, 2018) and has the potential to reinforce stigmatizing stereotypes among vulnerable groups. (Hyett et al., 2019) While deficit-based approaches have the benefit of identifying potential points of deficit-focused intervention, (Stoller, 2018) the studies under evaluation in this review were grossly descriptive in nature. Deficit-based research often leads to risk-based interventions (avoiding risks associated with poor outcomes), which often fail to sustain change (Alliance for Children and Youth of Waterloo Region, n.d.; The University of Memphis Engaged Scholar, 2023). Overall, the deficit-based studies reviewed primarily reiterated previously reported, and, at times, stigmatizing interpretations of data (e.g., human immunodeficiency virus-related stigma differs between racial and ethnic groups because racial minorities accept human immunodeficiency virus as a community issue (Algarin et al., 2019)), while simultaneously failing to report meaningful action taken to remedy or intervene on noted deficits. Overall, only four deficit-based interventions (Höglund et al., 2016a; Bounds et al., 2020; Loncar and Scott, 2022; Abubakari et al., 2021) and one strength-based intervention (O’Brien et al., 2019) were examined in the reviewed studies. These points suggest a failure by some nurse scientists to understand and apply core tenets of Intersectionality throughout each step of the process – from study conceptualization through to the dissemination of research findings.

Strength-based inquires, on the other hand, identify solutions and perspectives that are otherwise overlooked by a deficit-based approach. (Stoller, 2018; Gottlieb and Gottlieb, 2017) A strength-based approach focuses on what is working well to support the growth of individuals and communities. It is a perspective based on the assumption that people have existing competencies and resources for their own empowerment. It assumes that people are capable of solving problems and learning new skills and that they are a part of the process rather than just being guided by a professional (Alliance for Children and Youth of Waterloo Region, n.d.; The University of Memphis Engaged Scholar, 2023). Only 13 % of the studies reviewed employed a strictly strength-based approach to scientific inquiry by focusing on facilitators of health and the perceived positive attributes of the intersecting identities or social positions of interest; however, 30 % of included articles employed both a deficit and strengths-based approach. For example, McConnell et al. (2016) assessed the use of counterspaces (e.g., spaces of shared cultural gathering) in the promotion of well-being among marginalized groups, using an intersectional lens to improve upon an already existing framework. While the strength-based studies were also largely descriptive in nature, they offered an important perspective not always considered in health sciences research, which can help to improve health intervention uptake and health behavioral change among communities (Stoller, 2018; Gottlieb and Gottlieb, 2017).

Lastly, the reviewed articles rarely went beyond identifying “intersectionality” as their guiding framework, and 18 % of articles even failed to define what they meant by intersectionality. Additionally, only 47 % (61 of 131) of the included studies discussed and interpreted results based on their relation to historical oppression or privilege through an intersectional lens. This lack of historical framing and underreporting of methods used to assess intersectionality is not uncommon in research. A recent review of quantitative intersectional research found that 26.9 % of applied articles did not define intersectionality, and many articles lost or misinterpreted core theoretical tenets of Intersectionality – with 17.5 % including analysis components not reflective of social power (Bauer et al., 2021). To help guide researchers, several recent publications have focused on identifying key methodological attributes (i.e., researcher’s recognition of oppression, relationality, complexity, context, comparison, and deconstruction) (Misra et al., 2020) and describe detailed methodological approaches to integrating intersectionality throughout the research process – from conception of research questions to the dissemination of findings (Abrams et al., 2020; Bauer et al., 2021; Bauer, 2014). Experts have even gone as far as to identify potential challenges to true integration and application of Intersectionality across types of research (i.e., quantitative, qualitative, mixed-methods) and fields of research (e.g., public health, gender studies, etc.) (Rice et al., 2019; Abrams et al., 2020; Bauer, 2014). Using these resources, nurses must strive to improve the rigor and transparency of methods used when conducting intersectional research.

The way forward: implications and recommendations

Nurses are integral to addressing and resolving the world’s most urgent health care needs. Accounting for 59 % of the healthcare workforce (World Health Organization 2020), nurses make up the largest segment of the global health sector and have the capacity of influence at every level, from birth to death and from bedside to policy. The Theory of Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1991; Crenshaw, 1989) is a valuable tool for anti-oppressive framing of that influence. While in this review, we demonstrated that nurses around the world see the value in using an intersectional lens in their research, we simultaneously revealed areas for growth.

It is important to use an intersectional lens through every step of the research process, from conceptualization of the research question to reporting the research findings. However, as previously stated, authors often failed to detail how intersectionality was assessed or how the theory guided different aspects of the research (e.g., question conception, finding interpretation, etc.). This makes replication of research difficult and may act to introduce bias. Moreover, nurses run the risk of inadvertently reinforcing stereotypes and perpetuating health disparities if they fail to recognize and account for the potential impact of various points of power and oppression in different stages of research. This was evident by the abundance of deficit-based studies in this sample. While deficit-based approaches have an important place in the research repertoire, the use of an intersectional lens calls for exploration beyond the perceived deficits of a given community to the policies that govern them.

The studies reviewed were largely descriptive in nature (i.e., describe a phenomenon using descriptive, mechanistic, or observational study designs and methods). Descriptive studies are important; however, after having reviewed over 30 years of data, we conclude it is now time for nurses to act on what has been described (e.g., develop and evaluate interventions to improve outcomes of populations in need). Over the past decade, experts have highlighted the need for emphasis on the social justice aspect of Intersectionality and urged researchers to move descriptive work into intervention and knowledge translation (Kelly et al., 2021; Bowleg and Bauer, 2016). Thus, nurses need to begin making use of the data collected and analyzed to push for policy changes and to design interventions through an intersectional lens that will make meaningful changes in the lives of the people and communities we serve.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review provides an important context for the use of intersectionality within nursing research. It is important because addressing the different positions of power held by social positioning necessitates the nuance provided by intersectionality; however, we should be mindful of using the theory appropriately as described in Black feminist theory (Crenshaw, 1991; Crenshaw, 1989). The use of a scoping review approach with multiple reviewers and evaluators reduces the bias reflected in the article selection. Despite the importance of this contribution and the methods applied, limitations remain.

Our inclusion criteria required that articles use the term “intersectionality” explicitly, which could have resulted in the exclusion of literature that used intersectionality in its approach but did not explicitly use the term. Without clearly defined characteristics of what research is and is not considered intersectionality, the identification of all relevant articles will be incomplete. We also required that included articles reflected a nursing perspective by including “nurse” as a search term and several other criteria related to nursing authorship, nursing implications, or a nursing-focused funding source. This could have resulted in over-attributing the role of nurses in the study and in the exclusion of articles that were related to or written by nurses but did not use search terms that would have enabled their identification. The exclusion of non-English literature also limited the global perspective reflected in this review. Further, the broad date range of inclusion in this review brings into question concerns of time and changes in social identity’s positions of power and privilege. Social positions of power and privilege can change over time, where one identity could increase in power and privilege or could experience their loss. By including such a large range of articles, some social identities represented in the included literature may not be as relevant for that particular population (Collins, 1991).

Conclusions

Nursing has applied intersectionality in research globally, pointing to its growing role in the advancement of health equity by incorporating analysis based on the multiple axes of social identity and their role in privilege and oppression. Future researchers utilizing intersectionality as a framework can improve their approach by ensuring clear definitions and operationalization of intersectionality. Additionally, future work should advance toward developing interventions using an intersectional lens. Caution should be placed on research focusing solely on deficits among marginalized communities, which places scientists at risk of perpetuating stereotypes or enhancing already existing stigmas.
