Muricholic acid mediates puberty initiation via the hypothalamic TGR5 signaling pathway
Authors: Shan Wang, Ke Huang, Ana Liu, Senjie Wang, Runqiu Jiang, Wenlian Chen, Huiying Wang, Rahim Ullah, Chuqing Xue, Wei Wu, Guanping Dong, Peifang Jiang, Junfen Fu, Yan Ni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe onset of puberty is increasingly observed at earlier ages in children, especially in girls with obesity, while the molecular basis of this phenomenon remains partially unfolded. Since bile acids (BAs) have emerged as pivotal signaling molecules in both metabolic and reproductive disorders, we applied targeted metabolomics and identified elevated levels of muricholic acid (MCA) species in the serum of girls with central precocious puberty (CPP), which strongly correlated with indices of hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis activation. Mechanistically, MCA enhanced gonadotropin-releasinghormone (GnRH) expression in hypothalamic GnRH neurons through activating the TakedaG-protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5)-PI3K/Akt-mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway. This study significantly broadens the understanding of the biological roles of BAs and further supplement the understanding of the regulation of sexual development initiation.

The global rise in childhood obesity has been accompanied by an increased incidence of early puberty, particularly in girls, which is associated with adverse long-term health outcomes such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), breast cancer, and cardiovascular disease (1). Multiple epidemiological studies have shown a close relationship between obesity and the early onset of puberty (2). Clinical studies suggested that there is a significant positive correlation between obesity and the incidence of central precocious puberty (CPP) in girls (3, 4). Animal studies also suggest that a high-fat diet (HFD) during lactation and early feeding induces PP in female rats and mice (5). Despite the clear epidemiological, clinical, and experimental link between obesity and the advancement of pubertal timing, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) neurons serve as the central output pathway through which the hypothalamus regulates the reproductive axis (6). Although recent advances have highlighted several hypothalamic neurohormonal pathways that interlink energy metabolism with the timing of puberty, the precise mechanisms remain only partially understood. A key regulatory factor, the neuropeptide kisspeptin, has been extensively studied and is known to be closely associated with the onset of puberty (7). Elevated expression of kisspeptin in the hypothalamus has been linked to advanced vaginal opening (VO) in female rodents subjected to early overnutrition, suggesting its role in mediating the effects of metabolic status on pubertal timing (8, 9). Contradictory evidence has also indicated that the onset of puberty may be influenced by factors beyond kisspeptin (10, 11). Leptin secreted by adipocytes is now widely recognized as a permissive factor in HPGA activation (2), serving as a critical bridge between energy metabolism and pubertal onset, and may mediate the precocious sexual development associated with childhood obesity. However, multiple studies have consistently shown no elevation in peripheral leptin levels in mouse models with HFD-induced PP (5, 12). This dichotomy underscores the complexity of the neuroendocrine regulation of puberty, suggesting that alternative targets and pathways that influence pubertal timing remain to be fully elucidated.

Bile acids (BAs) are bioactive substances synthesized in the liver, primarily known for their essential roles in cholesterol metabolism and the facilitation of lipid digestion and absorption in the intestine (13). Beyond these classical functions, BAs have emerged as critical mediators in various metabolic disorders (14, 15). Obesity, for instance, is associated with altered BA metabolism and disrupted BA profiles, which underscores their potential role in metabolic dysregulation (16). Recent research has expanded the understanding of BAs, highlighting their significant roles on reproductive functions and their involvement in reproductive disorders. In patients with PCOS, significant alterations in BAs profiles were observed, and experimental studies further demonstrated that glycodeoxycholic acid injection could markedly improve PCOS symptoms in animal models (17). In addition, animal experiments showed that BAs could affect the spermatogenesis and endocrine function of testis through the activation of Takeda G protein–coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) (18) and Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) (19), thus profoundly affecting male reproductive ability and sexual development. These findings highlight the critical role of BAs in reproductive health. Emerging evidence positions BAs as pivotal modulators of sexual maturation, supported by transient pubertal BA surges in rodent models and distinct BA profile alterations during human adolescence (20, 21). However, comprehensive BA profiling data and mechanistic insights into BA-mediated HPGA activation in CPP remain lacking, making it imperative to explore BA profiles in children with early onset of puberty and investigate their potential involvement in the pathogenesis of this condition.

In this study, we employed a targeted metabolomics approach to measure BA profiles in the serum of girls with CPP using ultraperformance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Both obese and nonobese CPP girls were enrolled and compared for cross-validation of potential biomarkers. Our results revealed that muricholic acid (MCA) species were elevated in children with CPP and also reached their highest concentrations during the puberty period in the healthy population. These elevated MCA species exhibited strong and positive correlations with clinical biomarkers that indicate the activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis. To further investigate the role of MCA species, we confirmed the MCA accumulation in both serum and hypothalamus of mouse models of PP. We demonstrated that reducing MCA levels effectively mitigated the advancement of puberty in these models, while elevated MCA levels induced premature sexual development in female mice. Additionally, using two neuronal cell lines of mouse hypothalamic gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH), we confirmed that MCA enhanced GnRH expression through the activation of the TGR5-PI3K/Akt-mTOR signaling pathway. Finally, we validated that the activation of TGR5 contributed to the advancement of puberty in mice while TGR5 knockout could prevent this condition. Our findings reveal a link between BAs and the early onset of puberty, highlighting MCA-TGR5 signaling as a potential therapeutic target for managing early puberty.

Results

The Increased Serum Levels of MCA Species in Girls with CPP Are Positively Correlated with HPG-Axis Activation.

We conducted four clinical studies to comprehensively evaluate BA profiles and their associations with the initiation of puberty in children. The first study focused on understanding CPP-related BA profiles among children with obesity. Study I included 136 obese participants (82 girls with CPP and 54 without CPP as controls) (Fig. 1A). Based on the GnRH stimulation test, primary diagnostic biomarkers i.e., the luteotropic hormone (LH) peak value, follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) peak value, and their LH/FSH ratio, were significantly higher in CPP girls compared to controls (SI Appendix, Table S1). Additionally, secondary markers i.e., basal LH, FSH, Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG), and Estradiol (E2) levels, were also elevated in CPP girls (SI Appendix, Table S1).

Subsequently, targeted metabolomics profiling was conducted to quantitatively determine the BA compositions and their concentrations in the serum of CPP and controls. Among 38 BAs identified in the serum, there were significant differences in four BAs between two groups (including TαMCA, γMCA, TγMCA, and GγMCA), which were significantly elevated in the CPP group (Fig. 1B). The BAs can be categorized into three major types according to their chemical characteristics and metabolic pathways: cholic acid (CA), chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), and MCA species (Fig. 1C). And all these four BAs belonged to MCA species. However, no significant differences were found in either CA or CDCA species (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). Consistently, only the concentration and relative content of MCA species were significantly elevated in CPP (Fig. 1 D and E), while no significant changes were observed in either CA or CDCA species (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and F). In addition, there was no significant difference in the ratio of secondary BA to primary BA (SBA/PBA), the relative proportion of primary BA/total bile acid (PBA/TBA), SBA/TBA, or the ratio of conjugated BA to unconjugated BA (Con BA/Uncon BA) between two groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 G and J).

Furthermore, all the MCA species (TαMCA, TγMCA, GγMCA, γMCA) showed strong positive correlations with key indicators of HPG axis activation, including LH peak value, LH/FSH ratio, SHBG, and basal LH level (Fig. 1 F and G). However, TBAs, CA, and CDCA species did not exhibit significant correlations with these indexes (Fig. 1F). In addition to the positive correlations between MCA species and clinical biomarkers in the HPG axis activation (Fig. 1 H and J), TαMCA also showed significantly positive correlation with the Tanner scale, which is known as a rating score of sexual maturity in girls (Fig. 1K).

Study II aimed to measure the BA profiles of CPP among children with normal weight and verify whether the changes of MCA species observed in study I were consistent. This study involved 105 participants with normal weight (63 girls with CPP and 42 without CPP as controls) (Fig. 1L and SI Appendix, Table S2). Remarkably, MCA species, TαMCA in particular, were also significantly elevated in CPP girls (Fig. 1 M and N).

Then we applied Study III to explore whether the concentrations of MCA species could be different during puberty initiation among healthy children. This study included 73 healthy girls at different Tanner stages (25 girls at Tanner stage B1, 25 girls at Tanner stages B2 or B3, and 23 girls at Tanner stages B4 or B5) (Fig. 1O and SI Appendix, Table S3). Interestingly, the concentrations of MCA species and four individual MCA were consistently increased during the pubertal stage (Fig. 1P and SI Appendix, Table S3), supporting a potential link between MCA levels and the onset of puberty.

Finally, Study IV examined the presence of MCA species in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of children, which constitutes about 10% of the total BA pool. The detection of MCA in the CSF further supports the hypothesis that these species may play a regulatory role in the hypothalamus (Fig. 1Q and SI Appendix, Table S4).

In summary, MCA species were elevated in girls with CPP and reached their peak concentrations during puberty in healthy girls. The identification of MCA in CSF samples bolsters the hypothesis of its potential involvement in the regulation of the HPG axis.

Increased MCA Species in the Serum and Hypothalamus of Mouse Models of PP.

To validate the changes of BA metabolism in CPP, we employed a mouse model of PP induced by HFD (5). After four weeks of HFD feeding from birth (Fig. 2A), female mice gained weight (Fig. 2B) and exhibited symptoms of PP, denoted by the earlier age of VO (Fig. 2C) and first estrus (Fig. 2D), as well as enlarged ovaries and uterus (Fig. 2 E and F). Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining of ovarian tissue revealed a noteworthy increase in the proportion of mature follicles in the PP group (Fig. 2G). There were no significant differences observed in circulating LH or FSH levels between groups (Fig. 2 H and I). Western blot showed the expression of GnRH was significantly enhanced in the hypothalamus, indicating the activation of HPG-axis in the PP group (Fig. 2 J and K). Moreover, the increased signal of GnRH was further validated using immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 2 L and M).

Subsequent targeted BA analysis revealed that the concentrations and relative contents of MCA species were significantly elevated in the serum of PP mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). Moreover, individual MCA species (αMCA, βMCA, TαMCA, TβMCA, and TωMCA) were markedly higher in the serum of PP models (Fig. 2N). Consistently, the concentrations and relative contents of MCA species were significantly elevated in the hypothalamus of PP mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D), and specific MCA species (αMCA, βMCA, and TαMCA) showed marked elevations in mice exhibiting PP (Fig. 2O).

Previous studies have established the physiological role of BAs in central appetite regulation by demonstrating transient hypothalamic BA elevation postprandially in adult male mice (22). Interestingly, our study revealed that unlike the transient increase observed in control groups (Fig. 2P), the hypothalamic BA levels remained elevated without significant reduction at 60 min postfeeding in female mice with PP (Fig. 2Q). Accordingly, the dynamic changes of their relative abundances were also significant, highlighting the sustained elevation of MCA species within the hypothalamus of female mice with PP (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 E and F).

Reducing MCA Species Mitigates the PP in Mice.

Due to the dominant proportions and high concentrations of MCA species in the BA pool of mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), it is difficult to achieve a significant increase in MCA species concentration by directly administering MCA species. Cholestyramine is known for reducing BAs by impeding enterohepatic circulation and enhancing BA excretion through feces (23). Murine study has demonstrated that short-term cholestyramine administration (1 to 2 wk) can preferentially induce significant reduction of MCA species, with comparatively minimal effects on other BA subtypes (24). Next, we investigated the impact of reducing MCA species on the timing of PP. In this part, two-week-old mice were treated with 2% cholestyramine supplementation for two weeks (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B and D). Consistent with previous report, there was a significant decrease of MCA species in the liver, serum, and hypothalamus of mice after intervention with cholestyramine, while CA and CDCA species did not show significant changes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 E and M and Table S6). The body weight of the cholestyramine group remained comparable to that of the PP group (Fig. 3A). Notably, the time of VO and first estrus in the cholestyramine group was significantly delayed compared to the PP group (Fig. 3 B and C). Importantly, the circulating leptin level remained unchanged (Fig. 3D), suggesting that the effect on puberty timing may be independent of leptin mediated kisspeptin signaling. HE staining of ovarian tissue revealed a significant decrease in the proportion of mature follicles in the cholestyramine-treated group (Fig. 3E). Additionally, the weights of ovaries and uterus were notably lower (Fig. 3 F and G). Serum levels of LH, FSH, and E2 were significantly lower than those in the PP group (Fig. 3 H and J). Western blot showed that the expression of GnRH was significantly decreased in the hypothalamus of the cholestyramine group (Fig. 3 K and L). This reduction in GnRH expression was further validated by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3 M and N).

Increasing MCA Species Induces PP in Mice.

In the murine liver, MCA species are synthesized through the alternative pathway, whereas the classical pathway predominantly generates CA species (Fig. 4A). The classical pathway involves sequential metabolism of cholesterol by hepatic enzyme CYP7A1 to produce 7α-hydroxycholesterol, which is subsequently processed by CYP8B1 to form CA. Notably, the intermediate metabolite 7α-hydroxycholesterol can also enter the alternative pathway. Previous studies demonstrate that Cyp8b1 knockout or knockdown in mice leads to impaired classical pathway flux and enhanced alternative pathway activity, resulting in significant accumulation of MCA species (25, 26). We employed AAV-delivered shRNA under the liver-specific TBG promoter for targeted Cyp8b1 knockdown (Fig. 4B). Western blot analysis of liver tissues revealed substantial protein-level downregulation of CYP8B1 in the knockdown group, confirming successful gene suppression (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). Consistent with previous report, there was a significant increase of MCA species in the bile, liver, feces, and serum in Cyp8b1-knockdown female mice compared to controls, coupled with significant reductions in CA species (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C–F and Table S7). Notably, the BA alterations were manifested in hypothalamic tissue, demonstrating substantially elevated MCA species in Cyp8b1-deficient mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G). The body weight of the Cyp8b1-knockdown group remained comparable to that of the control group (Fig. 4C). Notably, the age of VO and first estrus in the Cyp8b1-knockdown group was significantly advanced compared to the control group (Fig. 4 D and E). Meanwhile, western blot showed that the expression of GnRH was significantly increased in the hypothalamus of the Cyp8b1-knockdown group (Fig. 4 F and G). This elevation in GnRH expression was further validated by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4 H and I).

MCA Species Upregulate the Expression of GnRH by Activating TGR5.

GnRH neurons represent the pivotal neuronal population within the hypothalamus responsible for HPGA activation, thus we further tested the direct effect of MCA species on GnRH expression and secretion using two GnRH neuron cell lines. First, intervention of GT1-7 cells with nine kinds of MCA species showed that both conjugated MCA (TαMCA, TβMCA, TωMCA, TγMCA, and GγMCA) and unconjugated MCA (αMCA, βMCA, ωMCA, and γMCA) significantly upregulated the transcription of GnRH (Fig. 5 A and B). ELISA analysis of the culture supernatant after 24-h MCA exposure revealed that even at a low concentration of 2 μM, MCA significantly upregulated GnRH secretion (Fig. 5 C and D). Then, we further confirmed the activation effects by western blot using TαMCA, TβMCA, and TωMCA. The results demonstrated their ability to enhance GnRH expression in GT1-7 cells cell lysate (Fig. 5 E and F). And we identified that TαMCA, the most significant marker identified in clinical studies, effectively stimulated the synthesis of GnRH in GT1-7 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5 G and H). Next, we selected another GnRH neuronal cell line GN11 for further validation. Consistently, MCA can enhance the expression of GnRH at the transcriptional level (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B), and simultaneously increased the secretion of GnRH in the supernatant of the culture medium (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D).

Previous studies have reported that MCA can exert biological functions by inhibiting bile acid receptor FXR (27) or activating TGR5 (28). The GT1-7 cell line also expresses both BA receptors TGR5 and FXR. First, we identified that the expression of FXR (gene name: Nr1h4) was not affected by MCA intervention, and its downstream transcription factors Shp and Fgf15 were not even detected (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). However, luciferase reporter gene assays revealed that TαMCA markedly activated TGR5 signaling, and comparable to the well-known endogenous agonist LCA and DCA (Fig. 5I), which substantiated the strong TGR5-activating capacity of MCA species. Other MCA species, including all conjugated and unconjugated variants, demonstrated heterogeneous TGR5 agonistic potency (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 F and G). Next, we investigated whether MCA-induced GnRH production and secretion were regulated by TGR5. Notably, supplementing INT-777, a selective agonist of TGR5 resulted in a significant increase in GnRH in both cell lysate (Fig. 5 J and K) and culture medium supernatant (Fig. 5L). On the other hand, SBI-115, a TGR5 selective inhibitor, effectively counteracted the heightened synthesis of GnRH induced by TαMCA (Fig. 5 M and N). We then verified the effect of TαMCA in si-Gpbar1 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 H and I). Remarkably, in TGR5 knockdown cells, TαMCA-induced GnRH secretion exhibited a significant reduction compared to its effects in control cells (Fig. 5 O and P). To further validate the generality of TGR5-mediated regulation, we extended our investigation to other MCA species. The experimental results proved that both inhibition and knockdown of TGR5 effectively counteracted the heightened synthesis of GnRH induced by both conjugated MCA (TβMCA, TωMCA, TγMCA, and GγMCA) and unconjugated MCA (αMCA, βMCA, ωMCA, and γMCA) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 J and Q). Taken together, MCA species induced GnRH production through TGR5 signaling in GnRH neurons.

MCA Species Stimulate GnRH Expression via the TGR5-PI3K/Akt-mTOR Signaling Pathway.

We have demonstrated that reduction of MCA species ameliorated HFD-induced PP phenotypes in female mice. Next, in order to further investigate the effects of MCA reduction on central signaling pathways, we collected hypothalamic tissues from the PP group and the cholestyramine intervention group at PND28 for RNA sequencing analysis. The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway was the most significantly enriched, with the largest number of differentially expressed genes (Fig. 6A). Previous studies also reported that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating the expression of GnRH (29, 30). Coincidentally, TGR5 can also regulate various physiological functions by activating the PI3K/Akt-mTOR signaling pathway (31, 32) (Fig. 6B). Consequently, we postulate that the PI3K/Akt-mTOR signaling pathway may be a key player in the MCA-TGR5-mediated increase in GnRH expression. To validate this hypothesis, a series of experiments were conducted on GT1-7 cells. We demonstrated that TαMCA, TβMCA, and TωMCA could activate the Akt-mTOR signaling pathway, inducing a significant increase in phosphorylation of both Akt and mTOR in GT1-7 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and D). Subsequently, we observed that the activation of TGR5 with INT-777 induced a time-dependent stimulation of Akt, mTOR, S6K, and S6 in GT1-7 cells (Fig. 6 C and G).

To further test our hypothesis, we investigated GnRH expression following treatment with INT-777, coupled with simultaneous inhibition of the PI3K/Akt-mTOR pathway using LY297002 (broad-spectrum inhibitor of PI3K), rapamycin (selective inhibitor of mTORC1), or torin1 (selective inhibitor of mTOR, mTORC1, and mTORC2). Our findings demonstrated that all three inhibitors effectively suppressed the phosphorylation of Akt and mTOR induced by INT-777 in GT1-7 cells (Fig. 6 H and J), leading to a significant inhibition of the GnRH accumulation (Fig. 6 K and L).

TGR5 Mediates the Early Sexual Development in Mice Models.

Dual immunofluorescence staining of GnRH and TGR5 in wild-type female mice revealed colocalization of TGR5 with GnRH neurons in the hypothalamic preoptic area (POA), indicating TGR5 expression within these neurons (Fig. 7A). To validate the in vitro findings, two murine models were employed to investigate the effects of TGR5 activation or inhibition on sexual maturation in female mice. First, newborn female mice were treated with INT-777 (10 mg/kg/48 h) or vehicle control from PND21 for 2 wk (Fig. 7B). The body weight of the intervention group was equivalent to that of the control (Fig. 7C). As anticipated, mice treated with INT-777 exhibited a significantly earlier age of VO and first estrus compared to the control group (Fig. 7 D and E), accompanied by a notable increase in uterine weight and size (Fig. 7 F and G). Levels of serum LH, FSH, and E2 of the INT-777 intervention group were significantly higher than those in the control group (Fig. 7 H and J). The significant increase in GnRH expression in the hypothalamus also confirmed the activation of the HPG-axis by INT-777 in mice (Fig. 7 K and L). The elevation of GnRH was further validated by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7 M and N). These findings confirmed the role of TGR5 activation in advancing puberty development.

Discussion

Among the various complications associated with childhood obesity, PP, particularly in girls, is often overlooked despite its significant long-term health consequences. The prevalence of PP is rising annually, and it can have detrimental effects on both physical and psychological development, potentially leading to various adverse health outcomes and a shortened life expectancy (33). Although numerous epidemiological studies have established a strong correlation between childhood obesity and PP, the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. Recent evidence has emphasized the role of hypothalamic ceramides in obesity-related PP, yet this alone does not fully explain the activation of the HPG-axis observed in patients with CPP (34). Beyond this, a distinct correlation exists between BA and sexual maturation processes. Studies have revealed that total BA levels in rats undergo transient pubertal surge, which suggests a potential regulatory relationship between BA and sexual development initiation (20). Clinical investigations have correspondingly identified marked alterations in serum BA profiles during female adolescence, which normalize to prepubertal patterns in adulthood (21). Untargeted serum metabolomics studies in female CPP patients have consistently identified BA metabolism dysregulation, with aberrant metabolic pathways involving primary BA biosynthesis and biliary excretion mechanisms, which suggests comprehensive alterations throughout BA synthesis-to-metabolism cascades (35, 36). However, clinical BA profiling data remain unavailable for CPP patients, and the precise mechanistic pathways through which BA activate HPGA require further elucidation.

This study demonstrates significantly elevated levels of serum MCA species in girls with obesity-related CPP, which are strongly correlated with clinical indices of HPG-axis activation. Interestingly, the increase in MCA levels was not confined to obese individuals but was also observed in CPP girls with normal weight. This suggests a potential mechanism independent of obesity that links elevated MCA levels with PP. Notably, reducing MCA species in female mice led to a marked delay in the onset of PP, while elevated MCA levels were demonstrated to causally induce premature sexual development in female mice, providing further evidence of MCA’s critical role in this condition.

The precise quantification of trace amounts of MCA species in human biospecimens, made possible by the high sensitivity of UPLC-MS/MS, adds a significant dimension to our understanding of this mechanism. However, the origin of MCA species in humans remains unclear. While MCA species are predominantly found in mice (αMCA, βMCA, and ωMCA), their contents in humans are low due to the absence of the enzyme CYP2C70, which metabolizes CDCA and UDCA into MCA (37). Interestingly, γMCA (also known as hyocholic acid, HCA) can be synthesized by CYP3A4 through CDCA in the human liver, constituting about 3% of the human bile acid pool (38). In girls with CPP, alternative pathways may exist that lead to the production of MCA species, potentially through metabolism from CDCA. Trace amounts of MCA species have been detected in various human samples, including serum, liver, feces, and urine, indicating the possibility of MCA production either in the liver or via gut microbiota modifications (39–41). Higher concentrations of MCA species have been identified in newborns, with levels significantly decreasing in adults, suggesting a possible link between MCA and the growth and development of children (42). Our study provides a compelling explanation for the relationship between MCA and the onset of puberty. We observed that MCA levels increased with the development of secondary sexual characteristics and gradually decreased in the later stages of sexual development, indicating a potential correlation between MCA and puberty onset.

We detected MCA species in the CSF of children, which enhances our understanding of BA distribution and lays the groundwork for exploring the central regulatory role of MCA species. In the central nervous system, the BA receptor TGR5 is predominantly expressed in neurons, with minor expression in astrocytes and microglia (43). Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of BA receptors like TGR5 in maintaining energy homeostasis in various hypothalamic neurons (22). Beyond this, mechanistic study has demonstrated that activation of the hypothalamic TGR5 triggers significant upregulation of GnRH expression (21). Crucially, overexpression of human TGR5 in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (ARC) of female rats induces substantially accelerated sexual maturation (21). These findings collectively indicate that BA can activate HPGA and potentially mediate the pathogenesis of CPP. Our study confirmed the expression of TGR5 in GnRH neurons in the hypothalamic POA, and the activation of TGR5 by MCA species in GnRH neurons. It is noteworthy that our findings revealed distinct TGR5 activation potentials among different MCA species, with TαMCA exhibiting the most potent agonistic effect (comparable to the effect of the potent agonist LCA), while TωMCA and ωMCA demonstrated relatively weaker activities, a discrepancy that may be attributed to structural variations across MCA species, particularly regarding hydroxyl group positioning and side chain conformations. Importantly, the experimental data indicated that even suboptimal TGR5 activation remained sufficient to enhance GnRH expression, further supporting the robust regulatory capacity of TGR5 in modulating GnRH production. These results provide a solid theoretical basis for further studies on the physiological effects of MCA-TGR5 signaling in the hypothalamus. In addition, the findings underscore the necessity for further investigations to elucidate the efficacy differences among various MCA subtypes in activating TGR5, and exploring the potential synergistic regulatory roles of other BAs receptors (e.g., VDR, PXR, CAR, S1PR2) beyond TGR5 and FXR.

This study proposes a role for MCA in the initiation of puberty, marking it as a key factor in reproductive maturation. Previous research has indicated that BA profiles undergo significant changes in both obese individuals and animal models (44, 45). Recent studies have also explored the role of γMCA in obesity-related metabolic diseases, showing that individuals with higher γMCA levels are less likely to suffer from metabolic disorders like diabetes and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (46, 47). Additionally, BAs have emerged as crucial signaling molecules involved in gonadal development and reproduction (18, 19). For example, BAs have been shown to impact sperm maturation, testicular barrier integrity, and the testicular microenvironment through TGR5 activation in male mice (48, 49). Given the complex relationship between energy metabolism and HPG-axis activation, as well as MCA species’ significant impact on metabolic status, we propose that MCA species may play a crucial role in puberty onset.

GnRH neurons serve as the ultimate output pathway in the brain for controlling the reproductive axis, making their enhanced secretory activity a key driver of puberty onset (6). Currently, it is known that various neurons and glial cells can regulate GnRH neuron function through neuropeptides, neurotransmitters, gas-transmitters, and gliotransmitters (50, 51). In mouse models of PP, we observed increased MCA species levels in both serum and the hypothalamus. Given the centrality of GnRH neurons in HPG-axis activation and sexual development initiation, we investigated the direct impact of MCA species on these neurons using hypothalamic GnRH neuron cell lines GT1-7 and GN11. Our findings confirmed that MCA species directly activate TGR5 in GnRH neurons and increase GnRH expression. Conversely, the application of TGR5 inhibitors and TGR5 knockdown counteracted the GnRH stimulation induced by MCA species. These findings suggest that MCA-TGR5 signaling might regulate GnRH neuron function through both direct and indirect pathways, such as modulating other neurons like AgRP/NPY neurons (22). This evidence underscores the critical role of MCA-TGR5 in central regulation, warranting further investigation. This was further substantiated by experiments using TGR5 activation and TGR5 knockout mouse models, which confirmed the mediating effects of TGR5 on PP. These findings highlight the pivotal role of the MCA-TGR5 signaling pathway in puberty development.

Recent evidence has indicated that the mTOR signaling pathway, specifically mTORC1, within hypothalamic neuronal populations, plays a crucial role in controlling puberty onset (52). For instance, the activation of mTOR by central L-leucine injection acutely stimulates LH secretion in rats (53). Conversely, blocking hypothalamic mTORC1 signaling with central rapamycin administration partially inhibited puberty onset, delaying VO, reducing uterine weight, impairing ovarian follicular development, and lowering serum LH and estradiol levels (54). Interestingly, our study suggests that TGR5 can also activate the mTORC1 signaling pathway, which is consistent with previous study (32). We provide compelling evidence that TGR5 activation by MCA species leads to increased GnRH expression through the PI3K/Akt-mTOR pathway. Further experiments confirmed that TGR5 activation by INT-777 induced the PI3K/Akt-mTOR signaling cascade, resulting in enhanced GnRH expression. Importantly, mTOR inhibitors were able to offset the GnRH stimulation caused by TGR5 activation, indicating a potential link between MCA species and GnRH expression via the TGR5-mTOR pathway.

While our study offers significant insights, it is not without limitations. First, the wide distribution of the TGR5 receptor throughout the body, including in the pituitary gland, ovaries, and uterus, challenges the precision of intraperitoneal INT-777 injection. Due to the developmental stage of the mice, alternative administration methods, such as intraventricular injection, were not feasible. Future studies should explore more specific approaches, such as hypothalamic GnRH neuron-specific TGR5 knockout models, to draw more definitive conclusions. Second, while we report the upregulation of MCA species in CPP girls, further clinical studies are necessary to validate their diagnostic and prognostic value. Additionally, the potential pathophysiological role of MCA species in boys remains unexplored and warrants further investigation. Last, recent studies have shown significant alterations in the gut microbiota of CPP girls (55, 56). The intricate relationship between MCA species metabolism and the gut microbiota warrants further exploration to uncover the complex cross-talk between these systems and their role in puberty.

In summary, this study identifies MCA as a key pathogenic factor for PP. MCA activates TGR5 in GnRH neurons, leading to increased GnRH expression and the subsequent activation of the HPG axis. More importantly, either reducing MCA levels through drug therapy or TGR5 gene ablation can downregulate GnRH expression and alleviate the early initiation of puberty. These findings highlight the important roles of MCA and TGR5 in the pathogenesis of early-onset puberty and provide insights for diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Materials and Methods

Patients were recruited from the Children’s hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. All samples designated for bile acid quantification were cryopreserved at −80 °C until analysis. The human study was approved by the ethics committee of the Children’s hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (2021-IRB-038) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100045616).

TGR5-KO mice (GemPharmatech, Shanghai, China) and wild-type C57BL/6J mice (Slac Laboratory, Shanghai, China) were maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions. Animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the NIH Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and Animal Advisory Committee at Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (202306-0407).

Immortalized mouse hypothalamic GnRH neurons (GT1-7 and GN11) and HEK-293 T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (11995065, Thermo Fisher) with 10% fetal bovine serum (A5670801, Thermo Fisher) and 1.0% Pen-Strep (15070063, Thermo Fisher).
