MSC-mimicking nanovesicle embedded bio-adhesive hydrogel for dual immunomodulation and osteogenesis to promote maxillofacial bone regeneration
Authors: Tingting Yu, Jiale Yan, Jingwei Wang, Zhiqiang Song, Rushui Bai, Yuntao Lu, Qin Niu, Tianyu Cai, Bing Han, Xing Wang, Yan Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSignificant efforts to utilize artificial soft materials in developing tissue engineering scaffolds show considerable promise for maxillofacial bone regeneration. However, most biological materials cannot simultaneously satisfy the multiple requirements of robust adhesive strength under masticatory environments, pathological immune regulation, and efficient cell-specific targeting therapy, ultimately compromising the repair process. Herein, a tissue-adhesive hydrogel loaded with mesenchymal stem cells-derived nanovesicles (PEG-pp@nMSC@MT) is introduced with inflammation regulation and cell-specific targeting as an all-in-one tool for maxillofacial bone repair. Relying on the quick amidation reaction between active ester groups of PEG-SG and amine groups of PEG-NH2 polymers, the uniform networks are rapidly formed with easy injection, favorable biocompatibility, and robust adhesive strength, which is capable of resisting frequent masticatory force in oral cavity. The incorporation of a matrix metalloproteinase 2 (MMP2)-cleavable peptide allows the hydrogel to respond to elevated protease levels at defect sites, enabling on-demand release of encapsulated nanovesicles while concurrently attenuating excessive MMP2 activity. Of note, the efficient transport of therapeutic melatonin to the intended BMMSCs enhances their osteogenic and immunomodulatory functions. Collectively, the reduction of MMP2, secretion of anti-inflammatory factors by BMMSCs, and immunomodulatory effects of nMSC@MT synergistically promote macrophage polarization toward M2 phenotype and facilitate bone regeneration. The therapeutic effect of PEG-pp@nMSC@MT displays superior biodegradability and osteo-inductive capacity compared with Bio-Oss, a first-line grafting material used in maxillofacial bone defect in current clinical treatment. Thus, this innovative hydrogel platform combines precise immunomodulation with cell-specific targeting, representing a promising therapeutic strategy for the effective repair of maxillofacial bone defects.Keywords: Biomimetic nanovesicles, Mesenchymal stem cell, Inflammation-responsive-hydrogel, Maxillofacial bone regeneration

Introduction

Maxillofacial bone defects caused by trauma, infection, tumors, and other factors significantly impact patients' physiological functions, mental health, and overall quality of life [1]. Current clinical strategies mainly involve autologous or allogenic bone grafts, but their application is restricted by limited sources, donor site morbidity, and immunogenic risks. Xenogenic substitutes such as Bio-Oss are widely used as fillers; however, these materials are nondegradable placeholders that fail to coordinate scaffold resorption with new bone formation or regulate the local microenvironment [2]. As a result, conventional approaches often exacerbate uncontrolled inflammation at defect sites, suppressing osteogenic differentiation of stem cells and ultimately leading to poor regenerative outcomes, particularly in large-scale defects [3,4]. So, the major challenge in reconstructing extensive bone defects is to modulate local inflammation while providing degradable biomaterials capable of synchronizing scaffold degradation with natural bone remodeling, thereby establishing a favorable niche for bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMMSC)-mediated bone regeneration.

Hydrogels, composed of crosslinked polymer networks, have emerged as promising candidates for bone regeneration owing to their biodegradability, extracellular matrix (ECM)-mimicking properties, and capacity for localized therapeutic delivery. Their structural adaptability and high water retention can be tailored to complex defect topographies, while physicochemical modifications enhance their injectability and tissue adhesion [5]. These features are particularly important in maxillofacial bone, which is subjected to constant stresses from facial expression and mastication, necessitating biomaterials with robust adhesion, mechanical resilience, and stable therapeutic release [6,7]. However, existing injectable adhesive hydrogels often serve merely as scaffolds in applications, lacking effective responses to the microenvironment of the defect sites [8,9]. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are highly expressed during early bone remodeling and in defect regions [10]. While essential for tissue repair, excessive MMP activity can intensify local inflammation and impair healing [11,12]. Harnessing MMPs as endogenous triggers allows hydrogels to achieve spatiotemporally controlled release of therapeutic agents while simultaneously mitigating inflammatory cascades, thereby conferring intrinsic immunomodulatory capacity [[13], [14], [15]]. Indeed, our previous work demonstrated that a phosphatidylserine (PS)-loaded MMP-responsive hydrogel could release PS in response to MMPs and promote bone repair [8]. However, great challenges remain in ensuring efficient delivery to target functional cells and preserving drug stability after release.

BMMSCs, as key functional cells in bone regeneration, contribute not only to osteogenesis but also to tissue repair through paracrine signaling [16]. They secrete diverse bioactive molecules that regulate vascularization [17], immunomodulation [18], and cell differentiation [19]. To achieve the BMMSC-targeted delivery, plasma membrane vesicles (PMVs) derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are promising drug carriers. Owing to their lipid and protein composition, PMVs inherit the natural tropism of their parent cells, display strong MSC chemotaxis, and, after nanosizing (named nPMV), achieve enhanced tissue penetration, drug stability, and uptake efficiency [20,21]. Additionally, PMVs exhibit excellent biosafety and drug-loading capacity due to their negatively charged surface. In contrast to exosomes and exosome analogues [22], the preparation process for nPMV is simpler, without highly technical sensitivity or complex procedures such as ultracentrifugation. Furthermore, the yield of nPMV produced is 10-100 times greater than that of natural exosomes [23]. Recently, researchers have obtained organoid extracellular vesicles through three-dimensional cell culture, which have shown significant improvements in yield and biological function compared to natural exosomes. However, it still faces challenges such as complex isolation procedures, and a lack of standardized protocols [24,25]. Melatonin (MT), a pineal hormone with well-documented antioxidant properties [26], has also been shown to augment BMMSC immunomodulation and osteogenesis [27]. Encapsulating MT within nPMV enables targeted delivery to BMMSCs, enhancing therapeutic utilization while minimizing off-target or dose-dependent adverse effects [28]. Moreover, macrophages, major immune cells in bone defect regions, can phagocytose nanovesicles, potentially amplifying MT's anti-inflammatory activity and further supporting tissue repair [29].

Based on the above rationale, here we endeavored to develop an inflammation-responsive hydrogel loaded with functional MSC-targeted nanovesicles (PEG-pp@nMSC@MT), aimed at treating maxillofacial bone defects (Fig. 1). This bioactive hydrogel could undergo rapid self-gelation with robust tissue adhesion even under physiological conditions through the spontaneous covalent crosslinks between active ester groups in 6-arm PEG-SG and amine groups abundant in 6-arm PEG-pp-NH2 and biological tissues. The MMP-cleavable peptide embedded the hydrogel network enabled the MMP-triggered drug release at bone defect sites. Additionally, the nanovesicles were engineered for homologous targeting delivery of MT toward BMMSCs, thereby enhancing their proliferation, osteogenic differentiation, and immunomodulatory functions. In vitro and in vivo results further verified the full set of functionalities of PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel scaffolds in effective bone regeneration. Consequently, this bioactive hydrogel synergized tissue-adhesive engineering, inflammation-triggered drug release, and homologous stem cell-targeted therapy, addressing the key challenges in traditional bone repair and offering a precise and immunomodulatory strategy to accelerate maxillofacial bone regeneration.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of nMSC@MT

PMVs with diameters ranging from 5 to 22 μm can be formed using chemical inducers to detach the cell membrane from the cytoskeleton of cells and subsequently encapsulate the cytoplasm [30]. They have demonstrated excellent performance as drug carriers [20,21], and exhibited considerable therapeutic promise for reconstructing extensive maxillofacial bone defects. To obtain PMVs, we collected the supernatant of periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) after chemical stimulation. To confirm the successful extraction of PMVs, laser confocal microscopy was employed to analyze the isolated vesicles (Fig. 2a). Confocal imaging revealed that PMVs consisted of a cell membrane structure (red) encapsulating cytoplasmic components (green), displaying variable sizes. Next, to obtain nPMV for improved intracellular and targeted delivery of drugs, PMVs were subjected to repeated extrusion using a liposome extruder. Subsequently, MT was successfully loaded into PMVs (named nMSC@MT) by repeated coextrusion with a loading rate of 25.6% ± 3.2%. When nMSC@MT was placed in PBS solution, melatonin was gradually and slowly released, with 60% released within 72 h (Fig. S1). Co-extrusion technology enabled the reorganization of phospholipid bilayers while simultaneously facilitating the loading of MT through intermolecular interactions [31]. Furthermore, due to repeated passage through fixed-pore-size polycarbonate membranes with uniform porosity, the resulting nanovesicles tended to display highly homogeneous dimensional characteristics [32]. After extrusion, the vesicle size was favorably reduced from the micrometer scale (PMVs) to the nanometer scale (nPMV and nMSC@MT, Fig. 2b). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed the spherical vesicles of nMSC@MT with a smooth surface (Fig. 2c). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed that both nPMV and nMSC@MT exhibited relatively uniform diameters (Fig. 2d). The mean diameters and surface potentials of nPMV were 178.8 ± 26.7 nm and −27 ± 0.7 mV, respectively. These values were comparable to those of nMSC@MT, which measured 185.37 ± 1.9 nm (p > 0.05, Fig. S2) and −24.9 ± 1.1 mV (p > 0.05, Fig. 2e). This indicated that after nanosizing, both nPMV and nMSC@MT exhibited similar sizes and surface potentials, suggesting that physical properties were not significantly altered by the MT modifications.

Furthermore, to verify whether the membrane proteins were retained during the blebbing and nanosizing processes, we first evaluated the PDLSC membrane, PMVs, nPMV, and nMSC@MT using SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and Coomassie brilliant blue staining (Fig. 2f). The protein profiles of PMVs, nPMV, and nMSC@MT exhibited similar stained bands to those of the PDLSC membrane, suggesting a partial preservation of PDLSC membrane proteins. Western blot analysis was used to confirm the retention of stem cell markers (CD90, CD146, CD105) and the functional protein, chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4), throughout the engineering process of nMSC@MT (Fig. 2g). Furthermore, to confirm the endocytosis of nMSC@MT by BMMSCs in vitro, nMSC@MT was co-cultured with BMMSCs for 24 h. Confocal images showed that nMSC@MT (red) was scattered around the nuclei (blue) of BMMSCs, which confirmed the potential of nMSC@MT for targeted delivery and efficient cellular internalization (Fig. 2h). To assess the bioactivity and targeted delivery of released nMSC@MT, the PEG hydrogel system containing nMSC@MT was implanted into the mandibular defect area of mice for 1 week. Frozen tissue sections revealed the colocalization of red fluorescent nMSC@MT with CD146+ MSCs (green fluorescence), confirming effective uptake of hydrogel-released nanovesicles by MSCs (Fig. 2i).

To determine whether nMSC@MT can be more readily taken up by MSCs compared to other cell types, we co-cultured fluorescently labeled nMSC@MT with BMMSCs, fibroblasts (L929), and epithelial cells (HOK) for 24 h, followed by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. S3). The results showed that BMMSCs exhibited significantly higher uptake of nMSC@MT compared to L929 and HOK cells, suggesting that nMSC@MT specifically targeted stem cells and were preferentially taken up by them. Next, we aimed to confirm whether this selective uptake is due to the presence of stem cell membrane structures on the surface of nMSC@MT. To this end, we used human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), a somatic cell line, as a non-MSC control. After labeling with fluorescence and induction of PMVs, HUVEC-PMVs underwent the same extrusion process to generate HUVEC-nMSC@MT. Equal amounts of HUVEC-nMSC@MT and PDLSC-nMSC@MT were co-cultured with BMMSCs for 24 h, followed by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. S4). We observed that BMMSCs showed a 70% reduction in the uptake of HUVEC-nMSC@MT compared to PDLSC-nMSC@MT, indicating that the stem cell membrane structure on nMSC@MT played an important role in its selective uptake by BMMSCs (Fig. S4a and b). Furthermore, we performed Western blot analysis to evaluate the expression of the functional protein CXCR4 on the cell membranes of HUVEC and PDLSC. The results revealed that the expression of CXCR4 on the PDLSC membranes was significantly higher than that on HUVEC membranes, suggesting that CXCR4 may influence the differential uptake of the nanomembrane vesicles derived from these two cell types by BMMSCs (Fig. S4c and d). To further confirm the role of CXCR4, we pre-treated nMSC@MT with a CXCR4 blocking antibody. The observed reduction in the percentage of DiI-positive BMMSCs and fluorescence intensity indicated the contribution of CXCR4 to the targeting capability (Fig. 2j–m). Thus, these findings indicated that nMSC@MT retained the homologous targeting capabilities, enabling it to be effectively taken up by BMMSCs.

In vitro biocompatibility and physiochemical properties

Integrating therapeutic agents into sustained-release carriers enables localized delivery, effectively reducing systemic toxicity while providing structural support for cellular growth, which is a dual approach especially beneficial for bone regeneration applications [33]. Among these carriers, hydrogels are distinguished by their biocompatibility, capacity to mimic the extracellular matrix, and tunable physicochemical properties. Specifically, modified functional hydrogels exhibit exceptional potential not only in accelerating tissue repair but also in achieving precisely controlled drug release and engineering a pro-regenerative microenvironment [34]. To effectively utilize and modulate the initial inflammatory response during bone repair, we constructed an inflammation-responsive hydrogel (PEG-pp), and the nanovesicles were encapsulated into the hydrogel (PEG-pp@nMSC@MT). The pp (GPLGIAGQ), as a kind of MMP-cleavable peptides, was integrated into 6-arm PEG-NH2 to construct 6-arm PEG-pp-NH2 (Fig. S5b). Since the amino groups in the 6-armed PEG-NH2 polymer had a higher activity than those in the peptides due to the larger number and less steric hindrance, the by-products of PEG-pp-pp-NH2 or other PEG-(pp)n-NH2 polymers were basically not formed in such conditions. Compared to the typical tetra-PEG hydrogels, this multi-arm structure could not only form a denser cross-linking network to enhance the structural robustness and stability, but also provide more active sites for binding functional peptides to optimize the drug release efficiency. Covalent crosslinking of PEG (Fig. S5a) or peptide-modified PEG hydrogels (Fig. S5c) was achieved kinetically by nucleophilic substitution between the amine groups of PEG-NH2 (or PEG-pp-NH2) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS)-activated esters of PEG-SG [35]. Fig. 3a showed that the PEG, PEG-pp, and PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogels exhibited a quick gelation process and easy injectability. The success of pp grafting into PEG-NH2 and PEG hydrogels was verified using proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) analysis and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The new signals observed at 7.7 ppm and 8.2 ppm in the NMR spectrum of PEG-pp-NH2 compared to PEG-NH2 confirmed the successful grafting of the pp peptide (Fig. S6). Additionally, in the FTIR spectra, new absorption peaks at 1668 cm−1 and 1544 cm−1, which were consistent with the pp peptide, appeared in the PEG-pp-NH2 and PEG-pp-Gel compared to PEG-NH2 and PEG-Gel, respectively. These findings further confirmed the successful grafting of the pp peptide in PEG-pp-NH2 and PEG-pp-Gel (Fig. S7). To confirm the effective entrapment, DiI-labeled nMSC@MT was embedded in PEG or MMP-responsive PEG-pp hydrogels, and fluorescence imaging revealed homogeneous 3D dispersion within the hydrogels (Fig. S8).

Taking into account the appropriate gelation time, high adhesiveness, and injectability for clinical applications, we selected 10 wt% of 6-arm PEG-SG and 6-arm PEG-NH2 for the following experiments. Regardless of whether pp or nMSC@MT was incorporated, the injectable hydrogels gelled rapidly within 20 s after the components were mixed (Fig. S9), facilitating convenient handling and conformal filling of defect sites. Rheological experiments showed that the elastic modulus (G′) was greater than the viscous modulus (G″), indicating that the PEG hydrogels could cover the entire defect area after concurrent application of the two components onto the tissue (Fig. S10). Furthermore, Fig. 3b revealed shape-adaptable adhesion of the hydrogels to porcine dermal substrates under mechanical stress, including stretching and twisting. Fig. 3c indicated that the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel showed an adhesion stress of 26.3 ± 2.9 kPa, which was much higher than that of commercial tissue adhesive of fibrin glue (13.1 ± 1.4 kPa). The hydrogel also showed strong adhesion on moist porcine dermal substrates under water flow scouring (Video S1). Because of the rapid gelation of PEG-SG and PEG-NH2 via an amidation reaction between active ester groups and amino groups, the resultant hydrogel demonstrated ultrafast in situ crosslinking. Furthermore, the strong affinity of active ester moieties toward tissue proteins enabled the hydrogel to function as an effective tissue-adhesive at the bone defect interface, mitigating displacement under dynamic mechanical loads and a moist environment while maintaining localized drug delivery. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images delineated the highly porous structures of the hydrogels (Fig. 3d), which were advantageous for the bidirectional exchange of nutrients and catabolites, as well as for cell growth and proliferation. Furthermore, processing under the same cryogenic conditions resulted in highly similar porosity characteristics across all three hydrogel variants. Quantitative analysis revealed consistent porosity values of 50-60% and pore sizes within the range of 15-20 μm for each variant, as detailed in Fig. S11. Additionally, stress–strain curves of PEG, PEG@nMSC@MT, and PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogels presented similar compressive behaviors in Fig. 3e. These hydrogels exhibited excellent mechanical resilience, withstanding compression to 82.63% ± 0.43%, 82.97% ± 0.84%, and 82.95% ± 0.75% of their original height without cracking. The corresponding compression moduli were 7.46 ± 0.82 MPa, 6.85 ± 1.18 MPa, and 6.30 ± 1.05 MPa, respectively, which could be attributed to their high crosslinking density (Fig. S12). Notably, incorporating inflammation-responsive elements and nanovesicles preserved the high compressive modulus, thereby providing robust three-dimensional structural support during bone regeneration [36]. After 10 loading–unloading cycles, the stress–strain curves of PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel remained stable without significant softening or fracture, indicating excellent fatigue resistance and the ability to withstand cyclic mechanical loading in the oral environment (Fig. S13).

In addition to mechanical robustness, the swelling and degradation behaviors of the hydrogels were critical for sustained drug release in bone tissue engineering. Therefore, the in vitro swelling dynamics and degradation patterns were systematically examined in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 37 °C. All three hydrogels reached swelling equilibrium within 24 h in PBS at 37 °C, displaying similar swelling profiles (Fig. 3f). After 42 days of immersion in PBS, the degradation rates of PEG, PEG@nMSC@MT, and PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogels were 76.91 ± 3.22%, 80.25 ± 2.87%, and 82.19 ± 1.61%, respectively (Fig. 3g). The degradation of the 6-arm PEG hydrogel system was driven by hydrolysis of ester bonds. The observed in vitro degradation timeframe was consistent with established literature on PEG-based hydrogel [37,38]. The degradation of hydrogel scaffolds could provide adequate interspace for osteogenic matrix deposition and angiogenic sprouting. These results indicated that the incorporation of nMSC@MT and the graft of pp did not significantly alter the rheological behaviors, mechanical properties, swelling, or degradation characteristics of the PEG network.

Under pathological MMP levels, enzymatic cleavage of peptide linkages induced hydrogel network disassembly, enabling controlled cargo release (Fig. 3h). As previously described, the scaffolds were incubated with 1 μg mL−1 matrix metalloproteinase 2 (MMP2) in PBS for 96 h to simulate inflammatory fluctuations, followed by maintenance in PBS. Release kinetics revealed distinct patterns: MMP2-activated groups exhibited rapid release phases (20% for PEG-based vs 26% for PEG-pp hydrogels on day 1), while they maintained 2% drug release after the removal of MMP2 in both hydrogels (Fig. 3i). To verify the different release rates of the inflammation-responsive hydrogel in MMP2 solution and PBS solution, we immersed the hydrogel in MMP2 solution for one day, then transferred it to PBS solution for another day, repeating this cycle. The amount of released nanovesicles was measured daily until the pp peptide was completely consumed, and a release curve was plotted (Fig. S14). The results showed that the release curve in MMP2 solution was significantly steeper, while the curve became flatter when transferred to PBS solution, indicating that more nanovesicles were released in MMP2 solution. This change disappeared starting from the seventh cycle. Furthermore, to validate whether the pp peptide segment within the hydrogel scaffold could deplete active MMP2, we quantified residual MMP2 levels in solution after 2-day hydrogel immersion using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). It demonstrated a significantly lower MMP2 content in the PEG-pp hydrogel group compared to the PEG hydrogel control (p < 0.05, Fig. S15). These findings collectively indicated that this responsive hydrogel effectively depleted MMP2 while concomitantly releasing nanovesicles. To evaluate the in vivo biosafety and tissue integration capacity, we implanted the hydrogels into the mandibular bone defect region of the C57/BL6J mice, which were euthanized after 8 weeks. Histopathological examination via hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining of vital organs, including cardiac, hepatic, splenic, pulmonary, and renal tissues, revealed no observable morphological abnormalities or pathological lesions (Fig. S16). Further analysis of the main organ tissue sections stained with Alizarin Red S (ARS) did not reveal the formation of ectopic mineralized nodules (Fig. S17a). Biochemical serum assays showed no significant differences in the functional markers of the heart, liver, and kidney when compared to the control mice (Fig. S17b). This suggested that the hydrogel possessed good biocompatibility without causing ectopic mineralization in distal organs and affecting the functions of the heart, liver, and kidneys. Based on the above experiments, we successfully developed an inflammation-responsive, injectable, and adhesive hydrogel system incorporating nMSC@MT and MMP2-cleavable peptides, demonstrating controlled drug release, mechanical adaptability, and biocompatibility for effective bone regeneration.

PEG-pp@nMSC@MT promoted bone regeneration in the mandibular bone defect model in vivo

To investigate the osteogenic potential of the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel system in vivo, we established a C57/BL6J mouse model of mandibular bone defects (Fig. 4a). Standardized diameter of 2.5 mm full-thickness mandibular bone defects, classified as critical-size defects [39], were created, and the PEG hydrogels were injected in situ simultaneously. After 4 and 8 weeks, the mice were sacrificed. The hydrogel residues suspiciously remained in the defects at 4 weeks, but there were no hydrogel residues at 8 weeks, suggesting the favorable biodegradability of the scaffolds in vivo. The expression of MMP2 in the defect area was evaluated at 1, 2, 4, and 8 weeks using immunofluorescence (IF) staining. As shown in Fig. S18, MMP2 levels remained relatively low in the non-defect area. In contrast, a substantial amount of MMP2 was released during the early stage of bone repair, peaking dramatically at the 1-week mark before decreasing significantly over time. The fluctuating expression of MMP2 in the defect area supports the on-demand release of nMSC@MT from PEG-pp@nMSC@MT. To assess whether the MMP2-responsive hydrogel influences MMP2 gene expression at the defect site, we performed quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis on local tissues harvested one week post-implantation. At the transcriptional level, no significant difference (p > 0.05) in MMP2 expression was observed between the blank control group and the hydrogel-implanted group (Fig. S19). Combined with prior in vitro findings, these results demonstrated that while the hydrogel effectively depleted active MMP2 protein, it did not alter endogenous MMP2 gene expression.

Micro-CT and histological staining were performed to assess the new bone formation in the defect areas of the four groups at postoperative weeks 4 and 8. As shown in Fig. 4b, substantial new bone deposition was observed at defect margins in PEG@nMSC@MT and PEG-pp@nMSC@MT groups by week 4. After 8 weeks post-implantation, the defect area in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group had dramatically reduced, while only minimal changes were noted in the PEG@nMSC@MT group compared to the 4-week time point. Control and PEG groups displayed negligible osteogenesis at defect edges across both time points. Quantitative data revealed significantly elevated bone volume fraction (BV/TV), bone surface area, and trabecular density (Tb.N) in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group compared with the other groups, which suggested that a greater quantity of new bone was formed in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group. Additionally, trabecular separation (Tb.sp) in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group was significantly lower (Fig. 4c and Fig. S20). It has been established that elevated Tb.sp, associated with catabolic dominance in bone remodeling, correlates with osteoporotic pathology [40]. The histological results corroborated the micro-CT findings (Fig. 4d). When bone defects occur, fibroblast activation drives fibrotic scar formation during natural repair processes [41]. H&E and Masson's staining demonstrated fibrotic matrices and granulomas dominating defect zones in control and PEG groups at 8 weeks, with sparse bone tissue. Compared to them, the PEG@nMSC@MT group exhibited enhanced osteoid deposition, while PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogels achieved superior regeneration with substantial mineralized bone formation. In addition, we implanted Bio-Oss, a widely used grafting material in clinical treatment, into the mandibular defect area as a control. Micro-CT analysis at 4 and 8 weeks post-implantation revealed that the Bio-Oss group exhibited minimal new bone formation. The defect region was predominantly occupied by the Bio-Oss granules, which mainly served as a passive filler. In contrast, robust new bone formation was observed in the hydrogel-treated groups, particularly in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group, indicating a more active osteogenic process (Figs. S21 and S22). Consistently, subcutaneous implantation experiments further demonstrated that Bio-Oss remained largely undegraded after 8 weeks, whereas the hydrogel was almost completely degraded (Fig. S23). Collectively, these findings indicated that, compared with Bio-Oss, the hydrogel possessed superior biodegradability and osteo-inductive potential. Rather than serving merely as structural space fillers, the hydrogel actively enhanced host bone regeneration, promoted new bone formation, and degraded in a timely manner to create space for newly formed bone.

The osteogenic capacity of BMMSCs is integral to bone repair. To evaluate hydrogel-mediated effects on BMMSCs proliferation and differentiation toward osteogenesis, targeted histological assessments were performed. IF staining demonstrated sustained expression of CD146 (a surface marker of MSCs) in the defect area, detectable at 4 weeks and persisting through 8 weeks (Fig. S24). Quantitative analysis showed that the expression of CD146 in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group was significantly higher than that in both the control and PEG groups at both timepoints. Notably, this value markedly exceeded that of the PEG@nMSC@MT group at 4 weeks, suggesting the improved MSC proliferation driven by PEG-pp@nMSC@MT and establishing an expanded progenitor pool to support bone repair. Moreover, peak expression of osteogenic markers such as osteocalcin (OCN) and runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) was detected in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT-implanted defect sites across both timepoints (Fig. 4e and f). The synchronized elevation of these markers reflected robust osteoblastic differentiation within the regenerating region. Furthermore, angiogenesis within the bone defect area is critical for bone regeneration [42]. To assess this, we examined the expression of angiogenesis markers across the experimental groups. IF analysis revealed the most pronounced expression of CD31 and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group, indicating enhanced angiogenesis (Fig. S25). Taken together, these results demonstrated that PEG-pp@nMSC@MT could promote angiogenesis, stimulate osteoblastic differentiation of BMMSCs within the defect site, and consequently accelerate bone regeneration.

PEG-pp@nMSC@MT effectively improved BMMSCs' biological functions in vitro

Having demonstrated the above outstanding osteogenic effects in vivo, we further investigated the potential mechanisms by in vitro cellular experiments. Due to the inherited biological properties and stem cell markers of parent cells, the nMSC@MT exhibits natural tropism for MSCs, enabling selective endocytosis by the local stem cells present in bone defect areas. To determine whether the targeted delivery of nMSC@MT to BMMSCs could promote their osteogenic differentiation, we treated BMMSCs with nMSC@MT of different concentrations. The cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assays (Fig. S26) demonstrated that nMSC@MT did not exhibit cytotoxicity to BMMSCs when added to the medium at a concentration of 10% and seemed to be most effective in promoting proliferation at a concentration of 5%. Additionally, qRT-PCR and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining were conducted to assess the osteogenic differentiation of BMMSCs after co-culture for 5 and 7 days, respectively. The expression levels of osteogenic markers, osterix (OSX) and OCN, were significantly higher in the 5% nMSC@MT group (Fig. S27), and ALP staining also revealed a significantly greater area of positive staining in the 5% nMSC@MT group (Fig. S28). As a result, based on the assessment of nMSC@MT concentration effects, 5% was identified as optimal for enhancing osteogenic differentiation in BMMSCs in vitro. This concentration demonstrated the most pronounced effects on BMMSC proliferation, upregulation of osteogenic markers (OSX, OCN), and enhancement of ALP activity compared to other concentrations.

MSCs are known to contribute to the regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment through paracrine effects [43,44]. Given that nMSC@MT could be efficiently internalized by BMMSCs and MT is a well-known anti-inflammatory factor, we hypothesized that nMSC@MT could modulate the cytokine secretion profiles of BMMSCs to influence the immune microenvironment. Therefore, we co-cultured BMMSCs with PBS, MT, nPMV, and nMSC@MT for 24 h before harvesting supernatants and cells for subsequent analysis. qRT-PCR and ELISA (Figs. S29 and S30) analysis revealed that nMSC@MT significantly enhanced the expression and secretion of interleukin (IL)-10 in BMMSCs while suppressing tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels at both transcriptional and protein levels. IL-10 has been demonstrated as a promoter of macrophage M2 polarization [45], whereas TNF-α conversely drives M1 polarization [46]. These findings suggested that BMMSCs, which had taken up nMSC@MT, could facilitate the creation of a favorable immune microenvironment for bone repair by bolstering anti-inflammatory signals and diminishing pro-inflammatory mediators.

Based on the favorable evidence on the ability of nMSC@MT to enhance BMMSCs' proliferation, osteogenesis, and immunomodulation, we co-cultured BMMSCs with different hydrogels in the cell culture medium containing 1 μg mL−1 of MMP2 to further assess whether the scaffolds could enhance osteogenic differentiation of BMMSCs and promote bone formation in vitro (Fig. 5a). Cell proliferation assays were performed at 1, 3, and 5 days using the CCK-8 assay. No significant decrease in BMMSCs viability was observed in PEG, PEG@nMSC@MT, or PEG-pp@nMSC@MT groups compared with the control group, indicating that none of them exhibited cytotoxicity (Fig. S31). Osteogenic progression in treated BMMSCs was analyzed through histochemical evaluation (ALP and ARS staining) combined with molecular profiling (qRT-PCR and Western blot). ALP, an established biochemical indicator for bone-forming processes, serves as a key mediator in mineral deposition processes through accelerating the enzymatic decomposition of inorganic pyrophosphate [47]. As shown in Fig. 5b and c, all the samples after 7-day osteogenic induction showed universal ALP reactivity. Among them, the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group exhibited the highest ALP activity. Subsequent extracellular matrix mineralization analysis was conducted using ARS staining to visualize calcium nodules. Similarly, the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group exhibited the highest density of mineralized nodules and a significantly higher proportion of positively stained areas than the control and PEG groups (Fig. 5d and e). Furthermore, qRT-PCR was utilized to evaluate the relative mRNA expression levels of the osteogenesis-related genes, including RUXN2, OCN, and bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2), at days 5 and 10, respectively (Fig. 5f). Expression levels of BMP2 and OCN were higher in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group than those in the other three groups at day 5. However, no significant differences in RUNX2 expression levels were observed among the four groups during the early stage of osteogenesis. Interestingly, after 10 days of osteogenic induction, the expression levels of BMP2, OCN, and RUNX2 increased significantly in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group, indicating that the release of nMSC@MT from the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT scaffold promoted osteogenic differentiation more effectively under inflammatory conditions. Complementing the results obtained from the qRT-PCR analysis, the expression levels of BMP2 and RUNX2 proteins were also studied at days 7 and 14 using the Western blot method (Fig. 5g). The BMP2 and RUNX2 expressions in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group were significantly promoted compared with those in the other three groups at day 7 and day 14 (Fig. S32). These results demonstrated that PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogels could effectively promote osteogenic differentiation of BMMSCs in situ after releasing the nMSC@MT in response to local inflammation, highlighting their potential for applications in bone regeneration.

PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel promoted bone regeneration by modulating the immune microenvironment in the bone defect area

Immune cells are pivotal in shaping the local immune microenvironment. Macrophages' M2-phenotype polarization and the anti-inflammatory signaling pathways of macrophages can counteract local acute inflammation, thereby creating a favorable immune microenvironment and facilitating a smooth transition from the initial inflammatory phase to the repair phase [4]. Therefore, in addition to modulating local host stem cell functions, we further elucidated the effects of the materials on local immune cell functions through both in vivo and in vitro experiments (Fig. 6a). To assess hydrogel scaffolds' capacity to influence macrophage polarization dynamics, mandibular bone sections at 1 week post-implantation underwent IF staining and qRT-PCR analysis for key markers. Compared with the control and PEG group, the PEG@nMSC@MT and PEG-pp@nMSC@MT groups exhibited markedly reduced F4/80+ inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)+ M1 macrophage populations. Meanwhile, the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group demonstrated a significant increase in F4/80+ CD206+ M2 macrophages, suggesting suppression of M1 polarization during acute inflammation and a shift toward anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes (Fig. 6b and c). For a more comprehensive analysis, we included CD68 as a marker for total macrophages, CD86 as a marker for M1 macrophages, and arginine (Arg)-1 as a marker for M2 macrophages. Tissue section staining was performed, and the results were consistent with previous findings. Compared to other treatment groups, the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel increased the number of CD68+Arg-1+ M2 macrophages and reduced the number of CD68+CD86+ M1 macrophages, effectively promoting M2 polarization of macrophages (Fig. S33a and b). Gene expression analysis further supported this trend (Fig. S34). While control groups (without any implants) exhibited baseline levels, both PEG and PEG@nMSC@MT groups showed marked upregulation of M1 markers (iNOS), indicative of material-triggered immune reactions. In contrast, the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group displayed significantly reduced iNOS expression relative to other implants. Concurrently, the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group uniquely elevated M2-associated markers (Arg-1) at the transcriptional level. One week after the implantation of the hydrogel, the levels of local tissue cytokines were evaluated at both gene and protein expression levels. qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 6d) showed that the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α expression in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel group was significantly lower than in the other three groups, while the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 expression was significantly higher in the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT group compared to the other three groups. At the protein level (Fig. 6e), the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel group also exhibited the lowest levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) and the highest levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10). Collectively, these data demonstrated that PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel attenuated M1 activation while promoting M2 reprogramming, thereby establishing an osteogenic immune microenvironment in vivo.

To further evaluate the effect of nMSC@MT on macrophage behavior, RAW264.7 cells were incubated with PBS, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), or LPS combined with MT, nPMV, or nMSC@MT for 24 h. Pro-inflammatory (iNOS, TNF-α, IL-6) and anti-inflammatory (CD206, IL-10, Arg-1) markers were quantified through qRT-PCR and IF. As shown in Fig. S35, LPS significantly increased the mRNA expression level of IL-6 and TNF-α, and decreased the mRNA expression level of IL-10 and Arg-1. nMSC@MT significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of IL-6 and TNF-α and upregulated the mRNA expression of IL-10 and Arg-1 compared with the other three groups. Similarly, IF staining revealed LPS-induced M1 dominance, evidenced by elevated iNOS and reduced CD206 protein levels. In contrast, all treatment groups reduced M1 macrophage proportions and elevated M2 populations compared with LPS controls. The nMSC@MT group exhibited the most pronounced shift: minimal M1 and maximal M2 ratios (Fig. 6f–g). Consequently, nMSC@MT enhanced the anti-inflammatory potential of MT, which could suppress the M1-polarization and promote the M2-polarization. Taken together, the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT scaffold could not only effectively enhance BMMSCs' proliferation and osteogenesis but also promote M1-to-M2 polarization to provide a favorable and pro-healing microenvironment for BMMSCs.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. The precise molecular mechanisms by which the hydrogel system modulates macrophage polarization and osteogenic differentiation of BMMSCs remain unclear and need further investigation in future studies. The present research primarily focuses on the effects of the hydrogel on the phenotypic changes of M1 and M2 macrophages. Future research should explore the roles of other macrophage subtypes, as well as other immune cell types, including neutrophils and T cells. While the mouse mandibular defect model is widely employed for studying maxillofacial bone defects, notable differences remain when compared to humans. To enhance the translational potential of the findings, future studies should incorporate larger animal models, such as dogs or pigs. Additionally, although promising results were obtained in the mouse model, several practical challenges must be addressed before this therapeutic strategy can be translated into clinical applications, including issues related to sterilization, large-scale production, and storage.

Conclusions

In summary, we engineered an inflammation-responsive hydrogel scaffold that integrated the MSC-targeted nanovesicles to synergistically promote maxillofacial bone regeneration. The hydrogel demonstrated robust tissue adhesion via covalent interactions between active ester groups and endogenous amines, providing stable retention under masticatory forces, while its injectability enhanced clinical applicability. The rationally designed PEG-based network incorporated MMP-cleavable peptides, enabling dynamic response to elevated protease levels through on-demand release of therapeutic nanovesicles (nMSC@MT) while simultaneously attenuating excessive MMP activity. This enzymatic feedback mechanism ensured the sustained therapeutic delivery during the critical inflammatory period. Additionally, the MSC-targeted nanovesicles enabled precise melatonin delivery to BMMSCs, significantly enhancing their proliferation, osteogenic differentiation, and immunomodulatory functions. Concurrently, the system effectively modulated the inflammatory microenvironment by promoting macrophage polarization from M1 to M2 phenotype, creating a pro-regenerative immune landscape. Both in vitro and in vivo results demonstrated promising regenerative outcomes. Notably, compared with the clinically used nondegradable filler Bio-Oss, the degradable hydrogel induced significantly more endogenous bone formation. This biomimetic strategy, integrating microenvironment-responsive drug release with cell-specific targeting, established a new paradigm for addressing inflammation-compromised regenerative microenvironments in critical-size bone defects, offering a clinically translatable solution for complex maxillofacial reconstruction.

Materials and methods

In vitro cell culture and induction. We collected reduced or wisdom teeth from patients aged from 12 to 45 years who required extraction for orthodontic treatment. The periodontal tissues were enzymatically dissociated using Collagenase I (8 mg mL−1, C8140, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and Dispase II (6 mg mL−1, D6430, Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 1 h and centrifuged; the supernatant was discarded. Subsequently, alpha minimum essential medium (α-MEM, Biological Industries, Beit Haemek, Israel) medium containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and penicillin-streptomycin (1%; Gibco, USA) was added and mixed thoroughly, and the mixture was cultured in a CO2 incubator until the primary stem cells reached 80% to 90% confluence. The periodontal stem cells (PDLSCs) were then digested using a trypsin-EDTA (Cat. 25300054; Gibco) solution and passaged at a ratio of 1:2 or 1:3. We used the PDLSCs at passages 4 to 6, which reached 90% or more confluence, for further experiments. Studies were conducted with approval from the Ethical Committee of Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology (no. PKUSSIRB-2025107001). Prior to study participation, all subjects underwent comprehensive informed consent procedures.

The protocol for isolating and expanding BMMSCs adhered to established methodologies outlined in our prior research [48]. Second-passage cells were subsequently subjected to osteo-induction medium, which was formulated by augmenting the basal growth medium with three critical components: 10 nM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 50 mg L−1 L-ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

L929, HOK, and RAW264.7 were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). HUVEC were cultured in ECM (ScienceCell, USA). All the cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The medium was refreshed every three days.

Preparation and characterization of nMSC@MT. As previously described [23,30], PMVs were obtained by adding the chemical buffer containing 10 mM HEPES (Cat. H3375; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 150 mM Sodium chloride (NaCl; Cat. S7653; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 2 mM Calcium chloride (CaCl2; Cat. C1016; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), pH 7.4, 25 mM paraformaldehyde (PFA, Solarbio, China) and 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT; Cat. D8220, Solarbio, China) to PDLSCs at a volume of 18 to 25 μL per 1 cm2 of PDLSCs. After the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, the supernatant was centrifuged at 100 g for 10 min to pellet cell debris. The new supernatant was collected for further experiments. Melatonin (MT, Solaribo, China) stock solution (10 mM in DMSO/PBS) was added to the PMVs supernatant at a volume ratio of 1:10 and then co-extruded the mixture using a Liposome Extruder (NEXSTAR, China) for 10 cycles to obtain nMSC@MT. The protein concentration of PMVs supernatant is approximately 1 mg mL−1, which was verified using a NanoDrop 280 nm absorbance (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Additionally, we extruded the PMVs without MT to produce nPMV. Then, the toxic PFA and DTT were removed by the dialysis (MWCO 3500D) against 1000-fold volume of PBS for 6-8 h with four changes of PBS.

MT encapsulation efficiency in lyophilized nMSC@MT composites was determined by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis. Methanol-solubilized samples underwent 30-min ultrasonication followed by high-speed centrifugation (15,000×g, 15 min), with supernatants subjected to chromatographic quantification.

The morphology of nMSC@MT was assessed with TEM (JEM, Japan). In brief, nMSC@MT samples were deposited onto copper TEM grids (3-5 min), negatively stained with 2% w/v phosphotungstic acid (2-3 min), and imaged via TEM. Dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Ultra, Malvern Panalytical, Switzerland) was employed to evaluate particle size distribution and zeta potential.

Labeling and internalization of nMSC@MT. 1,1-dioctadecyl-3,3,3,3-tetramethylindocarbocyanine iodide (DiI, Cat. C1036, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was used to label nMSCs@MT before experiments. Briefly, PDLSCs were incubated in 10 × 10−6 M DiI for 20 min at 37 °C and were rinsed in PBS before adding the chemical buffer. To further label nMSC@MT with Tht, we add 10 × 10−6 M Thioflavin T (Tht, Cat. 228850, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to PMVs before co-extrusion. Then, photographs were taken using a confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Germany).

In cellular uptake experiments, DiI-labeled nPMV was co-cultured with BMMSCs plated in 12-well chambers. After 24 h incubation, BMMSCs were washed by PBS three times, fixed for 30 min by 4% PFA and permeabilized for 15 min by 0.5% Triton-X (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Then, cytoskeleton was stained with FITC-phalloidin (1:200, Cat. C2201S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and 4, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, China) was used for nuclear counterstaining. Photographs were taken using a confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Germany).

To evaluate the involvement of CXCR4 in the uptake of nPMV, prior to co-culture with BMMSCs, nPMV was pre-treated with 160 nM CXCR4 blocking antibody (Ulocuplumab, Cat. HY-P99272, MedChemExpress, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Then nPMV was introduced into BMMSC cultures and incubated for 24 h. Subsequent analyses included flow cytometric analysis and immunofluorescence staining. nPMV without blocking antibody was served as a control.

Coomassie brilliant blue staining and Western blot. PDLSC membrane was collected as previously described [49]. The proteins of PMVs, nPMV, nMSC@MT, PDLSC membrane, HUVEC membrane, and BMMSCs were extracted, following our previous study [8]. Then, protein-matched aliquots were combined with 5 × SDS loading buffer (P1040, Solarbio, China) at 1:4 (v/v), denatured for 10 min at 99 °C, and electrophoresed on 4-20% gradient SDS-PAGE gel (PG42010-S, Solarbio, China). Then, Coomassie brilliant blue staining was conducted using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue Quick dyeing solution (P0017, Beyotime Biotechnology) under standardized conditions.

For Western blot analysis, the protein on the gel was transferred onto polyvinylidenefuloride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% skimmed milk (diluted with TBST), the membranes were probed with the primary antibodies for CD90 (1:1000, Cat. ab92574, Abcam, UK), CD146 (1:1000, Cat. ab75769, Abcam, London, UK), CD105 (1:500, Cat. sc18838, Santa, USA), CXCR4 (1:250, Cat. 35-8800, Invitrogen, CA, USA), Na+/K+ ATPase (1:1000, Cat. sc28800, Santa, USA), BMP2 (1:1000, Cat. ab284387, Abcam, UK), RUNX2 (1:1000, Cat. 12556, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and β-actin (1:10000, Cat. T0022, Affinity, China) overnight at 4 °C, followed by bathing with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Cat. S0002 or Cat. S0001, Affinity, China). The immunoreactive band analysis was performed using the SuperSignal® West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (ThermoFisher, USA) and BioMax film (Kodak, USA).

Preparation of nMSC@MT encapsulated 6-arm PEG hydrogel (PEG, PEG@nMSC@MT, PEG-pp@nMSC@MT). All formulations were constituted with PBS under ambient temperature. To obtain the PEG hydrogel, 6-arm-PEG-NH2 (10 wt%) was dissolved in a 2 mL of bottle, and 6-arm PEG-SG (10 wt%) was dissolved in another bottle. Equal volumes of the two precursor solutions were thoroughly mixed by vortex and subsequently incubated at room temperature (RT) to initiate gelation. Compared with the PEG hydrogel, we firstly prepared the PEG-pp-NH2 polymer by the reaction of MMP2 cleavable peptide, pp peptide (5 mg) and 6-arm PEG-NH2 (10%) in NHS buffer containing catalytic EDCI. Then, the PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel was obtained by adding the PEG-pp-NH2 polymer (10 wt%), nMSC@MT (1 mg/mL) and 6-arm PEG-SG (10 wt%) together as the above method.

PP conjugation was verified using 1H NMR (Bruker Ascend 400, Switzerland). For Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) analysis, lyophilized samples were blended with KBr, pulverized, and pressed into pellets. Spectra were recorded on a Bruker spectrometer (Germany) from 4000 to 400 cm−1 with 32 scans at 2 cm−1 resolution.

Adhesion. To assess interfacial bonding strength, 100 mg portions of PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel and fibrin sealant were assembled between planar porcine dermal substrates with a defined contact area (2 × 1 cm2). Test assemblies underwent 5 min thermostatic conditioning at 37 °C under constant 9 kPa compression. Interfacial stress (τ) was computed as τ = F/(w·l), where F denotes peak detachment force, with w and l representing contact width and length, respectively.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM). To analyze the scaffold microstructure, hydrogel specimens were immobilized in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution, subjected to ethanol gradient dehydration, and freeze-dried prior to SEM analysis. Morphological characterization using a Hitachi SEM instrument (Tokyo. Japan) was performed for PEG, PEG@nMSC@MT, and PEG-pp@nMSC@MT groups (n = 3).

Swelling and degradation ratios. Lyophilized PEG specimens were subjected to swelling capacity analysis by first documenting baseline mass (W0). Specimens underwent PBS immersion under physiological temperature (37 °C) with daily media renewal. Compression stress. PEG, PEG@nMSC@MT and PEG-pp@nMSC@MT were fabricated within cylindrical molds (10 mm diameter × 7 mm height), with mechanical characterization performed on an Instron universal testing machine (Massachusetts, USA) at 2 mm/min strain rate. For cyclic compression test, the hydrogels were subjected to compression at 50% strain at a constant speed of 2 mm/min followed by the return to the original position at the same speed, with the cycle repeated 10 times.

Rheological tests. Rheological behaviors were characterized using a cone-plate rheometer (Thermo Haake, USA, 35 mm diameter, 2.5 mm truncation gap) under oscillatory frequency sweeps (0.1–100 rad/s) at ambient temperature (25 °C).

In vitro release of nMSC@MT from the hydrogels. DiI labeled nMSC@MT was used to prepare the two kinds of hydrogel samples (PEG@nMSC@MT and PEG-pp@nMSC@MT) and all the hydrogel samples were immersed into the PBS with MMP2 (1 μg mL−1) at the first 4 days and the pure PBS at the following time points. The solutions were collected at the time points of 12 h, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 and 16 days. The nMSC@MT concentration was measured by a multimode plate reader (Revvity VICTOR Nivo, PerkinElmer, USA) according to the fluorescence intensity.

Animal Models. All experimental protocol received approval from the Animal Use and Care Committee of Peking University (LA2022506). Critical-sized defects [39] standardized to 2.5 mm were surgically established in the hemimandibles of 6-8 week-old male C57BL/6J mice (n = 6) to systematically compare the regenerative capacity of three PEG-based hydrogels of PEG, PEG@nMSC@MT and PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogels and Bio-Oss. In detail, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium, followed by hair removal, fixation, and disinfection. A longitudinal incision was made on the cheek of the mouse, the skin and masseter muscle were dissected, and the periosteum was separated. The bone plate was exposed through blunt dissection. A 2.5 mm diameter full-thickness circular bone defect was created on the mandibular ramus using an implant machine (1200 r/min) combined with a circular drill, with sterile saline used for cooling and pressure applied for hemostasis. In the control group, 10 μL of PBS was injected into the defect area. In the hydrogel implantation groups, a mixture of 10 μL PEG, PEG@nMSC@MT, or PEG-pp@nMSC@MT hydrogel components, or 20 μg of Bio-Oss bone powder was injected or put into the bone defect site. After gelation, the hydrogel filled the defect site. The wound was sutured using silk thread. To assess the in vivo degradation of the hydrogels and Bio-Oss, the materials were implanted subcutaneously in mice, and the skin tissues were harvested at 8 weeks post-operation for gross examination and histological analysis. The mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., China. Mice were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility with a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle at 25 °C and 60% humidity. They were allowed a sterile diet and autoclaved water ad libitum for 1 week before experiments. Post-defect creation, test groups received in situ injection of the hydrogels, while sham controls underwent primary closure without biomaterial implantation. The mice were euthanized after 4 or 8 weeks with harvest of mandibular complexes and the main organs (immersion-fixed in neutral buffered formalin 10%). Subgroup termination at 7 days post-implantation for isolation of defect-associated mandibular tissues to characterize early-stage macrophage polarization dynamics and the level of MMP2.

Micro-CT scanning and analysis. Mandibular specimens underwent microtomographic imaging using a Bruker SkyScan 1276 system (Bruker, Belgium) with 8 μm isotropic resolution for bone regeneration assessment. Tomographic datasets were reconstructed volumetrically through CTvox and NRecon modules (Bruker) for three-dimensional structural analysis. Bone volume to tissue volume (BV/TV), bone surface area, trabecular number (Tb.N) and trabecular separation (Tb.sp) were calculated using a CTAn (Burker) software.

Histomorphometry analysis. After micro-CT analysis, specimens underwent sequential demineralization in 10% EDTA (weekly replenishment, 4 weeks), ethanol dehydration series spanning 72 h, and paraffin embedding. Serial 5-μm sagittal sections from defect sites were subjected to H&E (G1120, Solarbio, China) and Masson Staining Kits (G1346, Solarbio, China) staining for new bone assessment. Systemic organs underwent parallel processing through dehydration, embedding, and sectioning (5 μm), followed by H&E staining for biosafety profiling. Brightfield microscopy (Zeiss) facilitated morphological evaluation.

Immunofluorescence staining. Ab75769, Abcam, UK) for stem cell surface marker, overnight at 4 °C. In vitro macrophage preparations underwent methanol fixation, 0.1% Triton-X permeabilization, and equivalent blocking prior to immunostaining.

Nuclear visualization was accomplished through DAPI integration within the mounting matrix (ZLI-9556, Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, China). Triplicate specimens per group underwent confocal imaging (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Germany) with quantitative signal intensity analysis via ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Osteogenic induction of BMMSCs. To evaluate osteogenic differentiation, BMMSCs were cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium. Different hydrogels (PEG, PEG@nMSC@MT, or PEG-pp@nMSC@MT) were added into the wells to be the experimental groups, while controls were supplemented with MMP2 solution (1 mg mL−1). Cellular samples underwent qRT-PCR analysis at days 5 and 10 post-induction. Protein expression profiles were assessed by Western blot at 7- and 14-day intervals following osteogenic stimulation.

Immunomodulation of BMMSCs and RAW264.7 cells. BMMSCs were maintained in serum-free α-MEM, while RAW264.7 cells were cultured in complete medium containing 100 ng mL−1 LPS. Experimental groups received PBS, MT, nPMV, or nMSC@MT supplementation. Following 24-h treatment, culture supernatants were harvested for cryopreservation (−80 °C) prior to cytokine quantification by ELISA. Cellular samples were simultaneously processed for transcriptional analysis by qRT-PCR and protein expression assessment through immunofluorescence.

Quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). Total RNA isolation from tissue lysate, BMMSC, and RAW264.7 cell lines was performed according to established protocols from our previous work [50]. β-actin served as the internal reference for normalization of target gene expression levels. Quantitative analysis was conducted through the comparative threshold cycle (2(−ΔΔCT)) approach. Custom-designed primer sequences employed in this study are documented in Table S1.

At each time point, cells were exposed to CCK-8 reagent (3 h of incubation), after which 100 μL aliquots were transferred to clear-bottom plates using a multichannel pipette. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining. Following 7 days of osteogenic differentiation, BMMSCs were processed using an Alkaline Phosphatase Detection Kit (Beyotime, Cat. P0321S) according to the supplier's guidelines. Staining was conducted at ambient temperature, and images were captured for quantitative assessment of ALP-positive regions by Image J software.

Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining. Mineral deposition in BMMSCs was analyzed following a 21-day osteogenic differentiation protocol. After induction, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (RT, 20 min), rinsed thrice with PBS, and stained for 30 min with 1% Alizarin Red S (Cat. 130-22-3; Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). Residual dye was removed by deionized water washes prior to microscopic documentation. Stained areas were quantified using Image J.

ELSA. Prior to assessment, culture supernatants were centrifuged (3000×g, 10 min) to eliminate cellular debris. The protein of tissue lysate was extracted, followed previous study [8]. Levels of M1/M2 polarization markers TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β and IL-10 were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Invitrogen, USA) following the standardized protocols. And the absorbance values were detected at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, USA).

Statistical analysis. Data processing and analysis were conducted with GraphPad Prism (9.5 version; GraphPad Software, CA, USA). All values calculated are expressed as the mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD). For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired Student's t-tests were applied. Multigroup differences were evaluated via one-way ANOVA with Turkey's post hoc correction. Significance thresholds: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, n.s. not significant.
