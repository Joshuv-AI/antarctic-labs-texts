The impact of agricultural trade on green technological innovation in China’s agricultural sector
Authors: Jing Cheng, JingJing Dai, Yang Liu, WanQin Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryAs environmental awareness grows, the agricultural sector must balance rising product demands with sustainable development. This study investigates the relationship between agricultural trade and green technological innovation, emphasizing how trade-induced efficiency improvements promote input-saving practices. Using panel data from 31 Chinese provinces between 2011 and 2021, our analysis reveals that agricultural trade significantly enhances resource use efficiency, particularly in rural areas where agriculture is concentrated. The findings provide empirical evidence of the positive effects of trade on green innovation and highlight the need for targeted policy interventions to support sustainable agricultural practices. This research underscores agricultural trade’s critical role in fostering green innovation and offers valuable insights for policymakers and agricultural businesses seeking to improve competitiveness and sustainability in an increasingly globalized economy.

Introduction

In the modern era of globalization and increased environmental awareness, the agricultural sector is at the crossroads of trade liberalization and sustainable development. The rising demand for agricultural products, coupled with growing environmental challenges, calls for a shift toward green technological innovation in agriculture. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the value of global agricultural trade has tripled over the past two decades. At the same time, the World Bank states that agriculture is responsible for about 70% of global freshwater withdrawals and contributes to 25% of greenhouse gas emissions. As a result, agricultural green technological innovation (AGTI) has become a fundamental aspect of agricultural green development, given its ability to offset the financial challenges associated with environmental regulation. This leads to a dual benefit of improved environmental stewardship and enhanced economic performance.1,2,3,4

International trade, a crucial part of the global economic structure, has a significant impact on the agricultural sector.5 It poses a dual dynamic for agriculture: it unlocks new markets and growth avenues, yet it also subjects local agricultural producers to heightened competition from overseas counterparts. This dual dynamic necessitates a thorough understanding of international trade’s impact on agricultural sector green technological innovation. The sector’s environmental footprint and resource consumption accentuate the need for green technological innovation.3,6 Within this context, input biased green technological innovation emerges as an indispensable element for the sustainable trajectory of agricultural development. This innovation paradigm is characterized by the adoption of technologies that are both efficacious and ecologically benign, resulting in diminished reliance on natural resources and inputs, and an enhancement in both production efficiency and environmental stewardship.7,8,9

Although the literature has examined various aspects of green technological innovation, the impact of trade on the direction of this innovation is relatively unexplored.10,11,12 Our study seeks to fill this void by empirically examining how trade-induced efficiency gains can foster an orientation of AGTI toward input-saving. Utilizing a rich dataset and employing rigorous econometric techniques, we find that agricultural trade has a positive and significant impact on orienting green technological innovation toward input-saving. This finding aligns with the theoretical postulate of technological progress bias, which suggests that external factors such as trade can influence the direction of technological advancements.13 Furthermore, we draw upon the green technological innovation and trade effect theories to propose an integrative theoretical framework. We posit that agricultural trade can foster the orientation of AGTI toward input-saving by enhancing resource utilization efficiency. This theoretical framework not only aids in understanding how trade influences AGTI but also contributes to the comprehension of the drivers of agricultural development.

However, existing literature has primarily explored various dimensions of green technological innovation with limited focus on how trade influences the direction of such innovation, especially in the agricultural sector. This study aims to bridge this gap by empirically investigating how trade-driven efficiency improvements can steer AGTI toward input-saving. By integrating trade and innovation theories and employing robust econometric methods, we seek to understand the mechanisms through which trade can enhance efficiency and promote input-saving green innovation. Our study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, by integrating the concept of technological progress bias, it offers a theoretical lens through which the relationship between trade and green technological innovation can be understood. Second, it provides empirical evidence on the role of trade in influencing the orientation of green technological innovation in agriculture, an area that has been scantily addressed in the literature. Third, our findings reveal how agricultural trade can foster the orientation of AGTI toward input-saving through efficiency gains. This finding not only enriches the Green Technological Innovation theory but also offers valuable strategic insights for policymakers.

This study addresses the following key questions: How does agricultural trade influence the direction of green technological innovation? Through what mechanisms does this influence occur? Answering these questions will contribute to the broader discourse on sustainable agricultural development and provide actionable recommendations for enhancing resource use efficiency and environmental protection in the agricultural sector.

The remainder of this study is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature. Section 3 constructs the theoretical mechanism of the theoretical model. Section 4 outlines the research design and econometric methodology. Section 5 reports the data sources, the results of the measure of bias, and some descriptive statistics. Section 6 reports and discusses the results, their robustness, and some extensions.

Literature review

Trade and innovation are two critical aspects that drive economic growth and development. The interplay between trade and innovation has intrigued economists for decades. The seminal work of Schumpeter laid the foundation for the study of innovation, where he argued that innovation is a key driver of economic growth.14 This was followed by the work of Grossman et al. (1991),15 who integrated innovation into the trade theory, creating a branch of economics known as the Trade Theory. They contended that trade openness heightens competition, thereby spurring firms to innovate to stay competitive. Building on this, Acemoglu (2002)16 devised a theoretical model elucidating the direction of technological change, demonstrating that trade could sway technological change direction. delved deeper into trade’s skill bias by endogenizing firms’ technological innovation direction of biased technological change. This technological innovation bias issue has garnered considerable scholarly attention, particularly regarding skill bias’s technological innovation impact.17,18,19,20,21 For example, Dechezleprêtre et al. (2012)22 deduced that international trade could fuel green innovation by amplifying cleaner product demand and expediting cleaner technology diffusion.

Amid escalating resource and environmental challenges, the green bias of technological innovation has gained prominence.7,8,11,12 Most research on green technology innovation determinants has centered on policy and environmental impacts.2,8,22,23,24,25 However, since international trade crucially impacts firm profitability and survival, it likely plays a key role in shaping innovation incentives and the creative destruction rate.26 Hence, examining green innovation from a trade perspective holds significant research value.27 pioneered this approach, finding that trade openness could stimulate green innovation via heightened competition and environmentally friendly technology diffusion.28 corroborated this, revealing that firms in open economies were more inclined toward green innovation.29 explored the impact of green product imports on green technology innovation in China, providing empirical evidence that supports the notion of trade fostering green innovation. Similarly,30 investigated whether agricultural trade openness could facilitate agricultural carbon reduction, offering insights particularly relevant to the Chinese provincial context.31 examined how trade policy uncertainty affects green innovation in the USA and China from a nonlinear perspective, further highlighting the complex interplay between trade and innovation policies.32 analyzed the inter-provincial flow and influencing factors of agricultural carbon footprint in China, emphasizing the importance of policy implications in addressing environmental challenges.

In the study of green innovation, scholars have identified the non-neutral characteristic of green technological innovation, meaning that technological innovation exhibits a bias.33 In the research on the measurement methods of directed green technological innovation, non-parametric methods primarily analyze the bias of technological innovation by observing the non-equivalent shifts in the production frontier over different periods,34 which alters the marginal productivity ratios of various factors. The Malmquist index method avoids the subjective bias induced by the specification of the production function in parametric methods, thus providing a more accurate identification of factor bias.35,36,37 However, due to its neglect of undesirable outputs, it cannot reasonably measure the level of green technological innovation. Consequently, scholars have incorporated undesirable outputs into the analytical framework.38,3940 maximized the standard variance between the transformed bad outputs to determine the optimal parameter values for the exponential model. The multidimensional decomposition of the Malmquist-Luenberger index by33 to calculate the bias of green technological innovation is particularly enlightening for this study.

Despite the extensive body of literature delving into the influence of trade on technological innovation and the orientation of such innovation, a conspicuous gap remains in the specific examination of how agricultural trade shapes the trajectory of green innovation within the agricultural sector. Prior studies have coalesced around the notion that trade fosters green innovation, yet these investigations have not sufficiently unpacked the nuanced directionality of green innovation. Conventionally, green innovation is anticipated to skew toward mitigating pollution emissions from production processes.41 However, when this lens is applied to agricultural green innovation, it raises compelling questions: Does the green innovation-stimulating effect of trade lean more toward energy efficiency or toward reducing emissions? What are the underlying mechanisms driving these effects? These intriguing queries call for a more profound exploration. This study is poised to bridge this research chasm, offering fresh insights into this underexplored terrain.

A simple model of input biased green technological innovation

Recognizing the importance people place on health and environmental considerations, this paper categorizes agricultural products into two groups: green input agricultural products (GIA) and green output agricultural products (GOA). GIA involves the use of green technology in the cultivation process, replacing traditional inputs like chemical fertilizers and pesticides to enhance food safety. Conversely, GOA involves using green technologies to minimize pollution emissions during cultivation, such as reducing surface pollution, to comply with environmental regulations. We develop a one-period economy model where consumers derive utility from purchasing goods with green costs, with utility being quasi-linear with respect to the outside good C0. The parameters β is the elasticity of demand for agricultural products, ε is the elasticity of substitution between GIA and GOA, and σ is the elasticity of substitution between varieties within each type of agricultural product.Given that customers purchasing GIA highly value health and consider it a quality-of-life indicator, they do not view GOA as a substitute for GIA. Hence, this paper assumes that 1<ε≤σ, making GIA more substitutable with each other than with GOA, and ε>β, indicating that the elasticity of substitution between GIA and GOA exceeds the price elasticity for green output technology. This assumption aligns with the strict standards for controlling agricultural surface pollution.

Hypothesis 1: Agricultural trade has a positive impact on the direction of green technology innovation, biasing it toward the input side.

Hypothesis 2: Trade-driven efficiency improvements enhance input-saving green innovation in the agricultural sector.

Measuring the bias of green technology innovation in agriculture

We assume there are N decision-making units (DMUs) in the agricultural economy, each utilizing W types of inputs, producing Q types of good outputs, and O types of bad outputs in each period. For the nth(n=1...N) DMU in period t(t=1,2…,T), the set of inputs is denoted as xnt=(x1nt,…,xWnt), the set of good outputs as ynt=(y1nt,…,yQnt), and the set of bad outputs as bnt=(b1nt,…,bOnt). Measuring green technology-factor substitution index

Based on the GII and GIO indices, we further identify specific factor biases, namely the Input-biased Green Technology-factor Substitution Index (IGTS) and Output-biased Green Technology-factor Substitution Index (OGTS). This paper identifies IGTS and OGTS by combining the calculation of GII and GIO indices with the intertemporal change of factor marginal substitution rate.37 This paper first constructs the IGTS index based on GII to realize the substitution identification between input factor I (referring to the input of green technology innovation factors, represented by capital input) and factor J (other input factors, including labor factors or energy factors) in the process of technological innovation transition:(Equation 25)IGTSI,J=(It+1Jt+1/ItJt−1)×(GII−1)

When there is technological innovation from period t to t+1, It+1Jt+1/ItJt is the ratio of the marginal substitution rates of factors I and J from stage t to t+1, reflecting the factor changes in agricultural production. When GII>1, It+1Jt+1/ItJt>1, that is IGTSI,J>0, it indicates that I realizes the bias of green technology innovation by substituting J, which is the energy-saving green technology innovation of factor J. Similarly, the identification of OGTS is similar to Equation 25, identified as follows:(Equation 26)OGTSI,J=(Yt+1Bt+1/YtBt−1)×(GIO−1)where Y is “good” output and B is “bad” output. Based on the research hypothesis of this paper, the green innovation impact of agricultural product trade does not bias toward the output end, therefore, further analysis and explanation of OGTS is not provided.

Agricultural green technology innovation (AGTI)

This study begins by addressing the crucial question of whether agricultural trade fosters the innovation of green agricultural technology. Agricultural Green Technology Innovation (AGTI) is measured using the number of green agricultural patent applications. Patents are a widely accepted indicator of innovation as they represent new knowledge and technological advancements.45 Specifically, green patents in agriculture reflect innovations aimed at improving environmental sustainability, reducing resource consumption, and mitigating adverse environmental impacts.

The data for green agricultural patents is sourced from the patent search and analysis system of the National Intellectual Property Administration. The search was conducted using keywords such as “green agriculture” within the patent product abstracts. This method ensures a comprehensive collection of relevant patents that genuinely represent green agricultural innovations. A few missing values in the dataset were filled by linear interpolation to maintain data integrity and consistency.

To enhance the intuitiveness and appeal of our analysis, we have included visual representations such as maps and charts that illustrate the geographical distribution and trends of green agricultural patents across different provinces in China. The scale of agricultural trade (IT-agri) is measured by the total amount of agricultural imports and exports, denominated in hundreds of billions of US dollars. Control variables

In our study, we incorporate a series of control variables to account for various factors that could influence the relationship between agricultural trade and green technological innovation. The selected control variables include the degree of disaster, the level of foreign direct investment (FDI), electricity consumption per unit area in rural areas, financial support for agriculture, highway mileage, and government research and development expenditure intensity. Below are the explanations for choosing each of these control variables:

The frequency and severity of natural disasters can significantly impact agricultural productivity and innovation. Disasters such as floods, droughts, and storms can disrupt agricultural activities and necessitate the development and adoption of resilient and innovative technologies.

FDI can bring in new technologies, management practices, and capital, which can enhance the innovation capacity of the agricultural sector. Higher levels of FDI are often associated with increased technological transfer and development.

Electricity consumption is a proxy for the level of development and infrastructure in rural areas. Higher electricity consumption can indicate better access to modern agricultural technologies and energy-dependent innovations.

Government financial support for agriculture can provide the necessary resources for innovation and development. Subsidies, grants, and loans can lower the financial barriers to adopting new technologies.

Transportation infrastructure is vital for the efficient movement of goods and access to markets. Better infrastructure can facilitate trade and the dissemination of innovative agricultural practices and technologies.

Government expenditure on R&D reflects the commitment to innovation and development. Higher R&D intensity can lead to more significant advancements in agricultural technologies and practices.

These control variables are essential for providing a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing the direction of green technological innovation in agriculture. By accounting for these variables, we aim to present a more accurate and robust analysis of the relationship between agricultural trade, GI and GII. Results

Benchmark model

To examine the impact of agricultural trade on agricultural green technology innovation and whether agricultural trade leads to a higher inclination toward green innovation in the agricultural sector, this study sets up the following two-way fixed effects econometric model based on the theoretical model:(Equation 27)GIi,t=α0+α1IT–agrii,t+α2xi,t+υi+ωt+εi,t(Equation 28)GIIi,t=α0+α1IT–agrii,t+α2xi,t+υi+ωt+εi,tWhere, i represents the province, t represents the year; the dependent variables GI and GII represent the level of agricultural green technology innovation and the input bias of agricultural green technology innovation, respectively; the explanatory variable IT–agrii,t represents the scale of agricultural trade; the matrix vector Xi,t represents a collection of a series of control variables; υi refers to individual fixed effects, ωt refers to year fixed effects; εi,t is the random disturbance term. Meanwhile, the t-statistics adjusted for heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors are used in the empirical analysis below.

Table 4 delineates the core test results of the relationship between IT-agri and GII. Initially, this study investigates whether agricultural trade stimulates agricultural green technology innovation, and then assesses whether innovation is propelled by the input side. In the baseline regression, a progressive regression strategy is adopted, where models M(1) and M(3) evaluate the impact of agricultural trade on agricultural green technology innovation. M(1) reveals that, without including control variables, the regression coefficient of IT-agri is 127.2355 and passes the 5% statistical significance test. Endogenous processing

Despite this study’s meticulous efforts to control for factors that may skew the investment in green technology innovation due to agricultural trade, there remains a theoretical possibility that other unobserved variables could lead to endogeneity issues. Moreover, the issue of reverse causality is also significant. On one side, the expansion of agricultural trade can lead to improved efficiency, which, in turn, can increase the agricultural sector’s inclination toward investment in green innovation. Conversely, the shift toward input-saving green innovation in agriculture can also promote the expansion of agricultural trade as a means to overcome barriers to green technology. To mitigate the potential endogeneity bias on the research conclusions, this study employs instrumental variable analysis.

In our analysis, we selected the interaction term between the agricultural industry structure and the average annual temperature (IV1), and the lagged core explanatory variable (IV2) as instrumental variables for endogeneity handling. The agricultural industry structure is represented by the proportion of total agricultural output to the total output of agriculture, forestry, animal husbandry, and fishery. The original data for average annual temperature comes from the National Meteorological Science Data Sharing Service Platform. The rationale for choosing IV1 is that both the agricultural proportion and weather factors are core factors influencing the scale of agricultural trade, and they are usually highly correlated when both conditions are met. Furthermore, based on theory and previous research, IV1 should not directly affect GI and GII unless it impacts IT-agri. Further analyses

The comprehensive empirical evidence and tests presented above robustly substantiate the core assertion that "agricultural trade can foster AGTI, and this innovation is propelled by the input-side bias." This necessitates a deeper analysis of the mechanisms driving this bias. This study posits that agricultural trade influences the bias in agricultural green technology innovation via two pathways: labor efficiency and energy efficiency improvement.

Firstly, agricultural trade enhances labor productivity and resource utilization efficiency, thereby directing green technology innovation toward the input side. Traditional smallholder economies struggle to achieve large-scale mechanized production. However, international trade expands the market size, facilitating economies of scale and augmenting labor productivity.46 This, in turn, enables the agricultural sector to allocate more resources to green technology innovation.

Secondly, international trade intensifies market competition and facilitates the flow of capital and technology.47,48 This compels agribusinesses to enhance labor productivity and energy efficiency by minimizing energy inputs and reducing energy consumption, thereby steering green innovations toward input reduction.

Thirdly, in the international agricultural trade landscape, importing countries often impose specific standards and requirements on imported agricultural products, such as pesticide residue limits and non-GMO stipulations. These technical prerequisites may induce a biased demand for green technology innovations among agribusinesses.49 Concurrently, importing countries mandate that exporting countries' agribusinesses obtain certain certifications, such as organic and global GAP certifications. The procurement of these certifications incurs costs,50 incentivizing agribusinesses in exporting countries to curtail energy inputs to adhere to the standards while also reducing costs.51

Discussion

Discussion and implication

In conclusion, this study has embarked on an exploratory journey into the relatively uncharted terrain of the impact of agricultural trade on the orientation of green innovation within the agricultural sector. By integrating the theoretical underpinnings of trade and innovation, and drawing upon a rich empirical dataset, we have shed light on the intricate dynamics at play in this complex relationship. Our findings underscore the pivotal role of trade in fostering a shift toward input-saving green innovation in agriculture, thereby contributing to the broader discourse on sustainable agricultural development in the era of globalization and environmental consciousness.

This study makes three main contributions to the literature on agricultural trade and green innovation:

First, we jointly investigated the impact of agricultural trade on the bias of agricultural green innovation and its underlying mechanisms. This approach allowed us to highlight previously overlooked ways in which agricultural trade can stimulate green innovation, particularly by promoting the substitution of traditional polluting inputs with greener alternatives. In doing so, we draw attention to the role of agricultural trade as a catalyst for green innovation, extending the logic of previous studies that have examined the role of trade in promoting innovation in general.53 Our findings align with and expand upon the work of Dechezleprêtre and Glachant (2012),23 who found that international trade can fuel green innovation by increasing demand for cleaner products and accelerating the diffusion of cleaner technologies. Similarly, Chen et al. (2024)29 provided empirical evidence that the import of green products encourages green technology innovation in China, supporting our assertion that trade promotes green innovation.

Second, our research challenges and refines key assumptions of the resource-based view of the firm.54 While this theory suggests that firms can enhance their competitiveness by efficiently utilizing their resources, our study adds to this argument by showing that agricultural trade can improve the resource substitution efficiency of technology, thereby promoting green innovation. This finding puts key propositions of the resource-based view into perspective and offers a synergistic understanding of the interplay between agricultural trade and green innovation. This extends the findings of Costantini and Mazzanti (2012),27 who demonstrated that trade openness could stimulate green innovation through heightened competition and environmentally friendly technology diffusion.

Third, our study advances the literature on green innovation in agriculture by providing empirical evidence from the context of China. We employed a fixed-effects model for panel data to examine the regression results of the core explanatory variable (scale of agricultural trade) on the dependent variable (input bias of agricultural green technology innovation) across 31 provinces in China. Our findings confirm that agricultural trade promotes the input bias of agricultural green innovation, and this effect is primarily achieved by improving the resource substitution efficiency of technology. This contributes to the literature by providing a more nuanced understanding of the role of agricultural trade in promoting green innovation, corroborating and extending insights from studies such as those by Song et al. (2018)38 and Zhou et al. (2020),39 who incorporated undesirable outputs into the analysis of green technological innovation.

Our empirical results further suggest that agricultural trade can enhance green innovation by improving resource use efficiency. Specifically, the import and export of agricultural products drive green innovation to be input-oriented. This means that trade helps in optimizing the use of inputs such as labor, land, and resources, making agricultural practices more sustainable. This finding is significant as it demonstrates that trade not only influences the scale of innovation but also its direction, promoting a shift toward more efficient and sustainable agricultural practices.

In comparison to other studies, our research provides a comprehensive perspective on how agricultural trade impacts green innovation. For instance, while Dechezleprêtre and Glachant (2012)23 emphasized the role of trade in increasing demand for cleaner products and technologies, our study extends this by focusing specifically on the agricultural sector and highlighting the importance of resource substitution efficiency. Moreover, Chen et al. (2024)29 showed that the import of green products stimulates green technology innovation, a finding that aligns with our results and underscores the broader applicability of trade-induced innovation beyond the manufacturing sector.

Furthermore, our findings also complement the resource-based view by demonstrating that agricultural trade can serve as a mechanism for enhancing the efficiency of resource use, thereby promoting green innovation. This adds a new dimension to the understanding of how external trade factors can influence internal resource utilization strategies within firms, particularly in the context of green technologies.

In sum, this study not only deepens our understanding of the linkages between agricultural trade and green innovation but also offers practical insights for policymakers and agricultural businesses seeking to promote sustainable development. Based on our findings, we offer the following recommendations for policymakers and decision-makers:

To promote agricultural trade and international agricultural cooperation effectively, it is crucial to facilitate international trade agreements that reduce tariffs and non-tariff barriers on green agricultural products and technologies. This will make it easier and more cost-effective for countries to exchange environmentally friendly agricultural goods and technologies, promoting widespread adoption and innovation. Furthermore, fostering partnerships between agricultural research institutions across different countries can lead to the development and sharing of region-specific green technologies, enhancing agricultural sustainability globally.

In addition to facilitating trade agreements and research collaborations, developing an international platform for sharing information, technologies, and best practices related to green agriculture is essential. This platform would facilitate knowledge exchange and help farmers and agricultural businesses access the latest green technologies and practices. Implementing pilot projects in different regions that demonstrate the benefits of green agricultural practices and technologies can serve as models for other regions, encouraging the adoption of similar practices and technologies.

Finally, organizing international training programs and workshops to build the capacity of farmers, researchers, and policymakers in green agricultural practices will enhance skills and knowledge, enabling stakeholders to effectively implement and benefit from green technologies. Investing in infrastructure that supports the efficient transportation and distribution of green agricultural products will reduce post-harvest losses and ensure that green products reach markets efficiently, supporting trade and sustainability. By considering these recommendations, policymakers can better harness the potential of agricultural trade and international cooperation to drive green innovation and contribute to sustainable agricultural development.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations that provide opportunities for future research.

First, our research primarily focuses on the impact of agricultural trade on green innovation in the agricultural sector within China. This geographical focus may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future research could consider conducting similar studies in other regions or countries to verify whether our findings hold universally.

Second, our research relies heavily on publicly available data. This approach may overlook some unpublicized or hard-to-quantify factors such as policy impacts, cultural influences, and individual farmer’s attitudes toward green innovation. Future research could consider incorporating these factors into their studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the drivers of green innovation in agriculture.

Third, our study uses the Malmquist-Luenberger index to measure the bias of green innovation and panel data fixed-effects model to examine the regression results. While these methods are widely accepted, they may not capture all the nuances of the relationship between agricultural trade and green innovation. Future research could consider employing other methodological approaches or models to further validate and extend our findings.

In terms of future research, there are several promising directions. One potential avenue is to examine the impact of specific types of agricultural trade (e.g., organic products and genetically modified crops) on green innovation. Another interesting direction would be to investigate the role of different stakeholders (e.g., government, consumers, and farmers) in promoting green innovation in agriculture. Furthermore, with the increasing importance of climate change, future research could explore how climate-related risks and opportunities are influencing green innovation in the agricultural sector.

Finally, as our study has shown that agricultural trade can enhance the resource substitution efficiency of technology, future research could delve deeper into this mechanism. For instance, it would be interesting to explore how exactly agricultural trade improves the resource substitution efficiency of technology and whether there are ways to further enhance this efficiency.

By addressing these limitations and exploring these future research directions, we can continue to deepen our understanding of the complex relationship between agricultural trade and green innovation in agriculture.

Method details

Data collection

Details of the data collection, including data sources and the variables used, can be found in the 'data and methodology' section. This includes the agricultural input-output data and control variables such as foreign direct investment, disaster extent, and rural electricity consumption.

Index Calculations and Measurements

MAXDea software was used to calculate the Green Innovation Index (GII) and the Input-biased Green Technology Substitution Index (IGTS). The methodology for these calculations is described in detail in the "methods of measurement" section.

Econometric models

The econometric models employed in the study, including the fixed-effects model for panel data, are explained in the “benchmark model” section. This includes a description of the models used to assess the impact of agricultural trade on green technological innovation.

Robustness tests and endogenous processing

Additional details regarding the robustness and endogenous processing performed can be found in the “robustness tests” section. This includes handling of outliers, lagged variables, and instrumental variable approaches.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical methods

We employed fixed-effects panel regression models to assess the relationship between agricultural trade and green technological innovation.

Statistical tests

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 17). Fixed-effects panel regression models were applied to analyze the impact of agricultural trade on green technological innovation. Significance was assessed using p-values, with significance levels indicated by asterisks in tables. ∗p < 0.1, ∗∗p < 0.05, ∗∗∗p < 0.01 (as defined in each table note).

Results are considered statistically significant if p < 0.1.

Definition of n

For our study, "n" represents the number of observations in our panel dataset.

Measures of central tendency and dispersion

Descriptive statistics for key variables, including mean and standard deviation (SD), are provided in the "descriptive statistics" section of the manuscript.

Randomization and stratification

As this study uses observational data from secondary sources, randomization and stratification are not applicable. However, robustness checks were performed to ensure consistency across different model specifications.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was determined by the availability of consistent data across all provinces in China, and robustness was ensured through econometric methods that account for potential biases using fixed-effects models.

Data inclusion/exclusion

All available data from the selected sources were included, and no observations were excluded based on subjective criteria. Missing data were addressed using appropriate imputation methods where necessary, as described in the "data and methodology" section.
