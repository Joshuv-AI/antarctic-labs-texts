Quercetin suppresses endometrial cancer stem cells via ERα-mediated inhibition of STAT3 signaling
Authors: Ou Tang, Ling Yang, Zhenyu Cheng, Anchun Xu, Tao Ma, Mengxi Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQuercetin, a secondary metabolite derived from plants with both medicinal and edible values, has demonstrated potential in cancer treatment, including endometrial carcinoma. However, its anti-tumor effect on cancer stem-like cells (CSCs), a subpopulation considered to be a major driver of tumor recurrence and metastasis—remain largely unclear.AimThis study aimed to evaluate the anti-tumor effects of Quercetin by targeting CSCs and suppressing their stemness properties.MethodsCSCs were enriched using serum-free medium and treated with a range concentration of Quercetin. The STAT3/JAK2 signalling was detected by western blot after treatment with Quercetin. The effects of Quercetin on malignant behaviours in CSCs, including proliferation, cell cycle distribution, spheres formation and invasion, were further assessed.ResultsQuercetin treatment inhibited the formation and maintenance of spheres derived from endometrial carcinoma cell lines EMN8 and EMN21.It also downregulated the expression of stemness markers, including ALDH1A1, c-Myc, Nanog, and Oct4.We further revealed that estrogen receptor α (ERα) is critical for mediating the inhibitory effects of Quercetin on stemness and malignant behavior, suggesting that ERα sensitizes CSCs to Quercetin. Quercetin suppressed STAT3/JAK2 phosphorylation and subsequently inhibited the transcriptional activity of STAT3’s downstream target gene, Oct4.These inhibitory effects were reversed by the STAT3 activator Colivelin.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that Quercetin targets the stemness of CSCs in an ERα-dependent manner, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic agent against CSCs to improve clinical outcomes in endometrial carcinoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03863-8.

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) ranks fifth in cancer-related deaths in males and eighth in females worldwide, making it one of the malignant tumors that poses a significant threat to human life and health [1]. EC occurs in the epithelial tissue of the endometria and is closely related to factors such as chronic irritation, inflammation, trauma, and genetic factors. Most patients with EC are diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in limited treatment efficacy. The five-year survival rate for general EC patients is below 30%, and the ten-year survival rate is even lower, at around 24%. Despite the development of multimodal treatments including surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and chemoradiotherapy, the prognosis and survival status of EC patients remain severe [2–4].

Studies have reported that EC patients who undergo neoadjuvant chemotherapy have a higher proportion of tumor-initiating stem cells [5], suggesting that the poor prognosis of EC treatment may be related to recurrence and metastasis caused by tumor stem cells. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) possess unique properties, such as self-renewal, tumor maintenance (proliferation), invasion and migration, immune evasion, and treatment resistance [6]. Recent research has shown that the CSC subpopulation of cancer cells is associated with clinical features of EC, such as drug resistance, self-renewal, and tumorigenicity [7]. For example, the Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, JAK-STAT3, and Hippo signaling pathways are overactivated in small cell carcinoma of EC CSCs [8]. These pathways drive the proliferation, differentiation, stemness, and resistance to treatment of the activated tumors [9]. In addition, CSCs have been found to be resistant to commonly used chemotherapy drugs in leukemia, malignant melanoma, brain, head and neck, breast, pancreatic, and colorectal tumors [10]. Similarly, brain and breast tumors also display resistance to radiation therapy [11]. Therefore, CSCs are considered the cause of tumor recurrence in many tumor types. Although traditional chemotherapy and radiation therapy can eliminate the majority of tumor cells, CSCs are believed to survive and develop resistance during treatment. Based on the widespread recognition of CSCs, an increasing number of studies are considering targeting the eradication of tumor stem cells as a potential therapeutic strategy for EC.

The Janus kinase (JAK)-mediated signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling pathway is one of the most promising new targets for cancer treatment [12]. Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) is a transcription factor that plays many important roles in both normal and transformed cells [13]. Multiple studies have found that STAT3 activation occurs in various aspects of cancer development, including the regulation of cancer stem cell (CSC) properties. Furthermore, STAT3 is a major downstream regulator of the interleukin-6 (IL-6) signaling pathway [14]. Upon binding of IL-6 to the IL-6 receptor (IL-6R), Janus kinases (JAKs) are activated, leading to the phosphorylation and activation of the transcription factor STAT3, thereby constituting the active IL-6/STAT3/JAK signaling pathway that drives cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and stemness maintenance [15]. Kim et al. demonstrated that activation of the IL-6-JAK1-STAT3 signaling pathway leads to the conversion of non-CSCs to CSCs in breast cancer through the regulation of the Oct4 gene expression [16]. Manore et al. showed that the inhibitory effect of IL-6 depends on the activation of the STAT3/JAK pathway, as the expression of dominant-negative STAT3 significantly reduces IL-6-mediated growth inhibition in breast cancer cells [17]. Therefore, the IL-6/STAT3/JAK pathway serves as a major regulatory factor for tumor CSCs by promoting cancer cell proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), CSC enrichment, and inhibition of cell apoptosis.

Quercetin is a flavonoid compound with anticancer activity. It acts as a mitochondrial ATPase and phosphodiesterase inhibitor. It inhibits the activity of PI3 kinase and slightly inhibits PIP kinase activity. Quercetin exhibits anti-proliferative effects on cancer cells by reducing cancer cell growth through type II estrogen receptor and inhibiting human leukemia T cell growth at late G1 phase of cell cycle. Quercetin may also inhibit fatty acid synthase activity. Additionally, it has been reported that quercetin can inhibit tumor cell migration, invasion, and tumorigenicity by suppressing the STAT3/JAK2 signaling pathway in osteosarcoma cells [18]. These findings suggest that quercetin may be a potential drug that targets the stemness of tumor stem cells.

In this study, we investigated the regulatory and inhibitory effects of quercetin on tumor stem cells, explored the potential molecular mechanisms involved, and aimed to provide clinical recommendations for the treatment of EC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and enrichment of CSCs

EMN8 and EMN21 were purchased from Cellbook Technology (Guangzhou, China) and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in RPMI 1640 (11875085, Life technologies) plus 10% FBS and penicillin (100 U mL−1) and streptomycin (100 µg mL−1).

Cells were planted in either 6- or 96-well ultra-low adhesion plates (Corning Life Sciences, Corning, NY, USA). The cells were then cultured for a week in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco), supplemented with B27 and N2 supplements, alongside 20 ng mL−1 of both epidermal growth factor and basic fibroblast growth factor (Invitrogen Life Technologies). Then, the created spheroids were quantified using a microscope.

All experiments in this study were conducted under hormone-depleted conditions using charcoal-stripped FBS (04-201-1B, Biological Industries, Israel) to eliminate the influence of exogenous steroids.

All cell lines used in this study were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free prior to experimentation. The passage numbers for all cells were strictly controlled, and all experiments were performed within fewer than 5 passages after thawing to ensure phenotypic stability. Experiments were independently replicated at least three times to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results. Randomization was applied during sample processing and data analysis where appropriate.

Morphology assessment

The formation of spheroids was documented with the use of an inverted microscope. Additionally, alterations in cell morphology due to drug sensitivity tests were examined subjectively through microscopic analysis.

Plasmids

pGL3-human Oct4 PE-SV40-luc was purchase from Addgene; the vector pGL3 bought from Pomega was used as a control.

Western blot

The protein concentrations were ascertained through the Bradford protein assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Milano, Italy). Afterward, protein samples (30 µg each) were isolated through SDS-PAGE and blotted onto a PVDF membrane. Antibodies utilized were anti-ALDH1A1 antibody (cat. No.: ab52492), anti-c-Myc antibody (cat. No.: ab32072), anti-Nanog antibody (cat. No.: ab109250), anti-Oct4 antibody (cat. No.: ab181557), anti-GAPDH antibody (cat. No.: ab8245), anti-ERα antibody (cat. No.: ab241557), anti-JAK2 antibody (cat. No.: ab108596), anti-p-JAK2 antibody (cat. No.: ab32101), anti-STAT3 antibody (cat. No.: ab68153) and anti-p-STAT3 antibody (cat. No.: ab267373). To ensure the consistency of protein load, blots were probed with anti-GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) antibody. All the primary antibodies were diluted at a ratio of 1:1000, and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were diluted at a ratio of 1:5000. The chemiluminescent signal was captured automatically using the ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (Bio-Rad) with a final resolution of 62.2 pixel/mm2.

CCK-8 assay

In summary, 1 × 104 endometrial cancer cells were placed in a 96-well plate with 100 µL of culture media. A ferroptosis inhibitor, Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 10 µmol/L, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) was then introduced and the cells were cultured at 32 °C. Following this, the cells were exposed to CCK-8 solution (10 µL/well, Beyotime) for a period of 4 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the optical density in each individual well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Cell cycle analysis

For the purpose of examining cell cycle stages through DNA content quantification, cells were collected, cleaned with ice-cold PBS, and preserved overnight at 4 ℃ using ice-cold 70% ethanol. Subsequently, the preserved cells were rinsed with PBS three times and treated with a final concentration of 100 µg/ml RNase A and 40 µg/ml propidium iodide (PI, Beyotime) in the dark for 15 min. Following this, the cells were subjected to analysis using 3 laser Navios flow cytometers (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

Invasion

For the invasion analysis, an additional layer of Matrigel (Corning Life Sciences) was applied to the inner chamber. After a duration of twenty-four hours, the medium was replaced with a serum-free variant. The outer chamber was then filled with 700mL of culture media, which was enriched with 10% FBS, and then cultivated for an additional 24 h. The cells were thereafter fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained using crystal violet. Cells that did not migrate or invade were removed from the inner plate using a cotton swab. Images of the cells that had migrated or invaded were captured using an Olympus IX71 (Olympus).

Colony formation

Trypsin was used to suspend and count the spheroid cells. From each group, 500 spheroid cells were gathered and cultured in 6-well plates filled with 5 ml RPMI-1640 medium that contains 10% FBS, maintained at 37 °C for a week. A solution of 75% ethanol was utilized to fix the cells at 37 °C for a quarter of an hour, followed by staining them with crystal violet alcohol solution (procured from Shangbao Biological Company) at 37 °C for half an hour. For statistical purposes, colonies consisting of more than 3 cells were manually observed and tallied under a light microscope at 40× magnification. This experiment was conducted thrice.

ERα overexpression and knockdown

The ERα cDNA of the human species was bought from Addgene (Cambridge, MA, USA) and was further cultivated by cloning into the EcoRI restriction site of the pBluescript SK vector, which was also bought from Addgene. The ERα cDNA was inserted between the BamHI and SalI restriction sites, which facilitated the reassembly of the cDNA into the pTRE2hyg plasmid. The pTRE2hyg plasmid, obtained from CLONTECH in Palo Alto, CA, houses the hygromycin-resistance gene, strategically positioned downstream of the tetracycline-response element (TRE) and the cytomegalovirus minimal promoters that trigger the activation of ERα expression in the presence of tetracycline or analogs such as Dox. Validation of the pTRE2hygERα construct was done through restriction digestion and sequencing. The forward and reverse sequencing primers were: 5′-CGCCTGGAGACGCCATC-3′ and 5′-CCATTCTAAACAACACCCTG-3′, in that order.

For the process of persistent transfections, cells with 70% confluence were subjected to transfection utilizing Lipofectamine, adhering to the instructions provided by the manufacturer, Life Technologies, based in Rockville, MD. For the transfection process, 5× 106 cells were suspended in 5 mL of Opti-MEM I Reduced Serum Medium, provided by Life Technologies, which contained 20 µg of plasmid in a tissue culture dish measuring 150 × 25 mm. After the transfection process was completed, the cells were placed in a complete DMEM medium, supplemented with 10% FBS, for a period of 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were chosen in a medium with 1 mg/mL G418. Several individual clones were subsequently isolated.

Statistical analysis

Reported values represent the mean ± standard deviation within 95% confidence intervals from at least three distinct experiments. Independent sample t-test and two-way ANOVA were utilized for data assessment using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. A p-value of < 0.05, <0.01, and < 0.001 was deemed significant.

Results

Quercetin inhibits malignancies of CSCs derived from endometrial carcinoma cells

Previous studies reported that Quercetin exhibits potential as a therapeutic agent for endometrial carcinoma through various mechanisms [19–21]. To investigate whether Quercetin influences malignant behaviors of cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) derived from endometrial carcinoma, we enriched CSCs from two endometrial carcinoma cell lines, EMN8 and EMN21. As shown in Fig. 1A, sphere formation was evident from day 3 to day 12 when these cells were cultured under serum-free conditions. We then assessed the expression of stemness markers—ALDH1A1, c-Myc, Nanog and Oct4, in CSCs compared to relative Parental Cells (PCs) via western blot.As expected, all markers were significantly upregulated in CSCs (Fig. 1B, C), confirming successful enrichment of the CSC subpopulation.

Quercetin inhibits stemness hallmarkers and sphere formation in endometrial cancer cells

To assess the impact of Quercetin on stemness and sphere formation, EMN8 and EMN21 cells were cultured in serum-free medium for 12 days and treated with 25 or 50 µmol/L Quercetin. As shown in Fig. 4A, treatment with 50 µmol/L Quercetin markedly inhibited sphere formation. Furthermore, when pre-formed spheres were exposed to 25 or 50 µmol/L Quercetin for 3 days under serum-free conditions, the spheres dissociated into smaller structures (Fig. 4B). In addition, the expression of stemness markers—ALDH1A1, c-Myc, Nanog and Oct4 was examined, and as expected, all were downregulated following Quercetin treatment (Fig. 4C). Together, these results demonstrate that Quercetin effectively suppresses the stemness of endometrial carcinoma cells.

Quercetin exerts inhibitory roles via the presence of estrogen receptor (ER) in CSCs derived from endometrial cancer cells

Quercetin has been reported to interact with ER and thereby exerts anti-tumor effects [22]. Therefore, we examined the expression of ER in CSCs derived from endometrial carcinoma cells. As presented in Fig. 5A, in CSCs, ERα expression was significantly downregulated compared to PCs. Moreover, in EMN8-CSCs, which exhibit relatively high ERα levels, quercetin treatment significantly reduced ERα expression. In contrast, EMN21-CSCs showed extremely low baseline ERα expression, where the change following quercetin treatment was not statistically significant (p >0.05). This cell line-dependent variation strongly supports our central hypothesis that ERα is a key mediator of quercetin’s activity. Specifically, in EMN8-CSCs with high ERα expression, quercetin effectively downregulates ERα protein, leading to suppression of stemness properties. Conversely, in EMN21-CSCs with low basal ERα levels, the regulatory effect of quercetin was less evident, likely due to the limited availability of its primary target; the compound’s ability to inhibit stemness was correspondingly weaker and may involve alternative mechanisms. This differential sensitivity underscores the importance of ERα status in determining cellular response to quercetin. To further validate this mechanism, we expanded our investigation to a panel of endometrial cancer cell lines with well-defined ERα status (including Ishikawa, ECC-1, KLE, and HEC-1-A). These additional data (provided in Supplementary Figure. S2) confirm that quercetin’s modulation of ERα is indeed cell line-specific, reinforcing our proposed mechanistic model.

To investigate whether ER expression influences stemness, we efficiently overexpressed ERα in CSCs using a lentivirus carrying the ERα coding sequence (Supplementary Fig. 1A). It is noteworthy that in ERα-overexpressing CSCs, quercetin at a concentration of 10 µM already exerted a significant inhibitory effect on sphere formation (Fig. 5B)—a concentration substantially lower than the 50 µM used in our initial experiments. To exclude potential confounding effects from ERα overexpression itself, we conducted cell viability assays and cell cycle analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1 C, E). The results indicated that ERα overexpression exerted only minimal influence on CSC proliferation and cell cycle distribution. Thus, these findings clearly demonstrate that ERα expression markedly enhances the sensitivity of CSCs to quercetin treatment.

Quercetin inhibited STAT3/JAK2 pathway depending on the presence of ERα

Quercetin has been reported to inhibit the STAT3/JAK2 pathway [23], which prompted us to investigate whether it mediates its effects through this mechanism in our model. To this end, we first treated CSCs with 50 µmol/L Quercetin for 60 min to evaluate its effect on STAT3/JAK2 signaling. As shown in Fig. 6A, the STAT3/JAK2 pathway was more activated in CSCs than in PCs. Quercetin treatment markedly reduced the phosphorylation levels of both JAK2 and STAT3 in CSCs(All the “p-STAT3” in the text refer to “p-STAT3 (Y705)”).In order to verify the inhibitory dependence of quercetin on CSCs and its relationship with ERα,we established a CSC subclone stably expressing shRNA targeting ERα mRNA, which resulted in efficient ERα knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 1B).Notably, this inhibitory effect was attenuated in ERα-knockdown CSCs, indicating that quercetin’s suppression of the STAT3/JAK2 pathway depends on the presence of ERα (Fig. 6B).

To ascertain whether STAT3 activation is necessary for quercetin’s anti-tumor effects, we used Colivelin, a synthetic neuroprotective peptide and established activator of STAT3 phosphorylation. Similar rescue effects were observed in cell proliferation activity (Fig. 8C), confirming that quercetin exerts its anti-cancer effects primarily through suppression of STAT3 activity.

Discussion

In this study, the research team investigated the effects of quercetin on tumor stem cells derived from ECs, including spheroid formation, stemness maintenance, and malignant behavior. We also explored the potential mechanisms underlying the regulatory effects of quercetin, which may be achieved through the inhibition of the STAT3/JAK2 signaling pathway, possibly dependent on the presence of estrogen receptors.

To bolster the generalizability of our conclusions, we expanded our experimental model to include a panel of endometrial cancer cell lines with defined ERα status beyond the original EMN8 and EMN21 systems. This panel comprised ERα-positive lines (Ishikawa and ECC-1) and ERα-negative lines (HEC-1-A and KLE). Cancer stem cells (CSCs) were enriched from all lines and subsequently treated with quercetin (Supplementary Figure S2A). Analysis revealed that the ERα levels in CSCs derived from ERα-positive Ishikawa and ECC-1 cells were markedly lower than those in their respective parental cells (PCs) (Supplementary Figure S2B). Following quercetin treatment, these CSCs exhibited a significant reduction in the expression ratios of key stemness markers (Oct4 and Nanog) and in the phosphorylation levels of STAT3 and JAK2 (p-STAT3/STAT3 and p-JAK2/JAK2). In contrast, both the parental and CSC populations of the ERα-negative lines HEC-1-A and KLE consistently exhibited low basal ERα expression. Accordingly, quercetin treatment in these models did not elicit a significant inhibitory effect on the ratios of stemness markers or on the activation status of STAT3 and JAK2 (Supplementary Figure S2C, D). Building on these findings, a key priority for our future work will be to employ patient-derived organoids (PDOs) to enhance the clinical relevance of our research.

Moreover, to rigorously verify that the inhibitory effect of quercetin on endometrial cancer stem cells (CSCs) is dependent on ERα, we performed additional experiments using models with well-defined ERα status. Specifically, ERα was knocked down in ERα-positive Ishikawa-CSCs and ECC-1-CSCs (knockdown efficiency data are provided in Supplementary Figure S1-F). Results demonstrated that ERα knockdown significantly attenuated the suppressive effects of quercetin on CSC stemness markers (OCT4 and NANOG) as well as on the phosphorylation levels of STAT3 and JAK2 (p-STAT3/STAT3 and p-JAK2/JAK2). This effect was consistent with that observed following treatment with the ERα-specific inhibitor Fulvestrant (Supplementary Figure S3-A-C).

Conversely, we overexpressed ERα in ERα-negative HEC-1-A-CSCs and KLE-CSCs (overexpression efficiency data are shown in Supplementary Figure S1-G). Following ERα overexpression, quercetin treatment led to a marked enhancement in the inhibition of stemness markers (OCT4 and NANOG) and signaling activity (p-STAT3/STAT3 and p-JAK2/JAK2) in these CSCs (Supplementary Figure S3-D-F).

Furthermore, to better elucidate the kinetic relationship between ERα modulation and downstream signaling, we performed a time-course experiment in which EMN8/EMN21-derived CSCs were treated with quercetin and harvested at 0 h, 2 h, 6 h, and 12 h. Western blot analysis was used to monitor dynamic changes in ERα and key components of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway. The results demonstrate that the inhibitory effect of quercetin on the expression of stemness markers (OCT4 and NANOG) and the phosphorylation of STAT3 and JAK2 (p-STAT3/STAT3 and p-JAK2/JAK2) in CSCs strengthened progressively over time (Supplementary Figure S4).

It is worth noting that quercetin exhibits limited bioavailability. Previous pharmacokinetic studies reported that after oral administration in an aqueous suspension in rats, quercetin had an absolute bioavailability of 16% and a maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of 2.01 µM. When formulated with ethanol and polyethylene glycol 200, its bioavailability increased to 27.5%, with a Cmax of 3.44 µM [24]. Although the concentrations used in our in vitroexperiments are higher than these reported plasma levels, such discrepancies are common in in vitrostudies due to factors such as protein binding, cellular uptake efficiency, and the absence of metabolic clearance. Moreover, we demonstrated that ERα-positive CSCs exhibit heightened sensitivity to quercetin. As shown in Fig. 5, ERα-overexpressing CSCs were effectively inhibited at lower concentrations, suggesting that ERα status could serve as a predictive biomarker for quercetin response. This supports the biological relevance of our chosen doses and underscores the potential for efficacy in sensitive cellular contexts.

To directly address the concern about toxicity to normal endometrial tissue, we conducted additional experiments using the immortalized human endometrial epithelial cell line (hEEC). Treatment with quercetin at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µM for up to 72 h did not significantly reduce cell viability, as assessed by CCK-8 assay (Supplementary Figure S1D). These results confirm that the concentrations effective against CSCs are not cytotoxic to normal endometrial cells, indicating a favorable therapeutic window.

In summary, the combination of pharmacokinetic data from the literature, our findings on ERα-dependent sensitivity, and new toxicity assessments in normal cells provides a strong rationale for the concentrations used in this study. We are optimistic that formulation strategies or targeted delivery approaches may further enhance quercetin’s bioavailability and efficacy in future translational applications.

Many studies and clinical trials have shown the existence of cancer stem cells (CSCs) in certain types of tumors, including acute myeloid leukemia [25], head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [26], breast cancer [27], and others. Based on these studies, it is likely that the unlimited proliferation and metastasis of malignant cells are regulated and driven by these CSCs. Some researchers have suggested that the presence of CSCs may contribute to treatment failure in ECs [28]. For example, dysregulation of CSC signaling pathways, such as Hippo/YAP-1, Wnt/β-catenin, and Hedgehog (Hh), has been associated with tumor maintenance and resistance to therapy in esophageal cancer [29, 30]. In this study, we found that the addition of quercetin not only inhibited the formation of stem cell spheroids in a serum-free culture environment but also caused the disintegration of pre-formed stem cell spheroids. During this process, the expression levels of stemness-related factors also decreased, indicating that the inhibitory effect of quercetin on stem cell spheroids is achieved by inhibiting stemness rather than exerting cytotoxicity. There are various types of stem cell markers for tumor stem cells, such as cell surface markers differentiation cluster (CD) 271 and CD90, which have been routinely used to isolate cell subpopulations with stem-like properties from cancer cell banks, enhancing tumor-initiating ability, increasing recurrence rates, and increasing resistance to treatment [31, 32]. Another study suggested that transforming acidic coiled-coil protein 3 (TACC3) could be used to determine whether TACC3 can serve as a biomarker for the diagnosis and prognosis of ECs, as it can form spheres and display resistance to various drugs in vitro [33]. In this study, ALDH1A1, c-Myc, Nanog, and Oct4 were used as stemness markers, and their expression levels were positively correlated with the spheroid-forming ability of stem cells, demonstrating the feasibility of using them as stemness markers. However, in future research, more reported stemness markers still need to be introduced.

Quercetin is widely found in vegetables and fruits and belongs to the class of flavonoids. Studies have found that it has inhibitory effects on various tumor cells, including breast cancer, prostate cancer, liver cancer, esophageal cancer, ovarian cancer, and others [19]. Currently, both in vivo and in vitro studies have shown that quercetin has inhibitory effects on colon cancer. It not only inhibits the proliferation of colon cancer cells and induces apoptosis but also reduces the number of aberrant crypt foci in the colon [34]. The specific mechanisms underlying the anticancer effects of quercetin are not yet fully understood but may be achieved through the regulation of multiple molecules. In this study, we found that quercetin has an inhibitory effect on the stemness of tumor stem cells, and the expression of estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) is crucial for the sensitivity to quercetin. Overexpression of ERα allows relatively low concentrations of quercetin to inhibit the stemness of tumor stem cells. It is worth noting that overexpression of ERα does not affect stemness and cell behavior, indicating that the action of quercetin primarily relies on the expression of ERα. Estrogen can activate estrogen receptors and downstream signaling pathways, but we did not explore whether quercetin still exerts its tumor-suppressive effects in the presence of estrogen, which needs further investigation in future studies.

Additionally, while this study has not explored whether quercetin influences other signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT, MAPK, or NF-κB, this limitation reflects our focused experimental design. From the outset, our primary objective was to rigorously test a specific hypothesis: that the ERα–JAK2–STAT3 axis serves as the central pathway through which quercetin suppresses cancer stem-cell-like properties. We believe that the collective evidence provided by genetic knockdown, overexpression, and pharmacological inhibition experiments strongly and sufficiently supports this conclusion.

Although a comprehensive investigation into additional pathways would be valuable, it lies beyond the current scope of our work. Our results consistently and specifically implicate the ERα–JAK2–STAT3 pathway in mediating quercetin’s effects. That said, we acknowledge that contributions from other mechanisms—such as PI3K/AKT or NF-κB—cannot be formally excluded and would represent a meaningful direction for future research.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates the anti-tumor effects of quercetin against cancer stem cells derived from ECs. Although quercetin is not a first-line anti-cancer agent, it effectively target the CSCs subpopulation and may suppress chemotherapy resistance, tumor recurrence, and metastasis mediated by this cell group. These findings highlight the potential of quercetin as an adjuvant therapy in the clinical management of ECs.
