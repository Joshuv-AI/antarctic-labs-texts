Transthoracic echocardiographic evaluation of maternal cardiac output: colloid preload versus crystalloid coload during cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia: randomized clinical trial
Authors: Mohamed Ahmed Tolba, Mohammed Abaalkhayl, Rahaf Abdurhman Alkhuraif, Afnan Fahad Alobaid, Rawan Suliman Aldubayan, Narjes S Alotaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal anesthesia-induced hypotension during cesarean delivery primarily results from vasodilation. While phenylephrine restores blood pressure, it may reduce maternal cardiac output (CO) by up to 20%. Limited data exists on the cardiac effects of different fluid strategies as measured by transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), particularly when using albumin as a colloid component. This study compared the CO effects of albumin preload combined with crystalloid coload versus crystalloid coload alone.MethodsIn this randomized, double-blind study, 162 ASA II parturients scheduled for elective cesarean delivery were assigned to crystalloid coload (1000 mL Ringer’s lactate; n = 81) or combination therapy (250 mL albumin 5% preload plus 750 mL Ringer’s lactate coload; n = 81). Both groups received prophylactic phenylephrine infusion. The primary outcome was the group-by-time interaction in CO measured by TTE at baseline, 1 and 5 min post-spinal anesthesia, post-delivery, and 1-hour post-study.ResultsThe CO group-by-time interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.067). In an exploratory post-hoc analysis, the overall group effect across all time points favored the combination group by 0.33 L/min (95% CI: 0.02–0.64; p = 0.038). The combination group demonstrated significantly higher velocity time integral (VTI; mean difference 1.32 cm, 95% CI: 0.87–1.77; p < 0.0001). Urine output was higher in the combination group (p = 0.038).ConclusionsNo significant difference in temporal CO patterns was found between fluid strategies (group-by-time interaction p = 0.067). Introduction

Spinal anesthesia is still the recommended method of anesthesia for elective cesarean delivery [1]. The resulting sympathetic block precipitates high incidence of maternal hypotension with unfavorable maternal and fetal outcomes. These include maternal nausea and vomiting, hemodynamic instability, and disturbed conscious level [2]. The decreased uteroplacental perfusion have potential deleterious consequences for the fetus [2].

The pathophysiology of spinal anesthesia-induced hypotension in obstetric patients primarily involves sympathetic blockade leading to vasodilation, with decreased systemic vascular resistance being the predominant mechanism rather than reduced venous return [2]. While vasopressors effectively normalize blood pressure, maintaining adequate maternal cardiac output (CO) represents a distinct and crucial goal during cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia. Phenylephrine, despite its efficacy in restoring blood pressure, can significantly reduce maternal CO by up to 20% through alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction and reflex bradycardia [3, 4], highlighting the limitation of using blood pressure as the sole indicator of maternal hemodynamic adequacy.

Various protocols for intravascular volume management during neuraxial anesthesia for cesarean delivery exhibit considerable heterogeneity regarding fluid composition (crystalloid versus colloid), administration timing (prophylactic preload versus synchronous coload), and volumetric dosing parameters [5]. Evidence consistently demonstrates that crystalloid preload exhibits negligible prophylactic efficacy against spinal-induced hypotension, whereas alternative regimens, including colloid preload and either crystalloid or colloid coload protocols, demonstrate comparable hemodynamic stabilization properties based on current literature [5].

Efforts to optimize intravascular volume through colloid administration have produced inconsistent effects on maternal CO, with some studies demonstrating significant improvements [6] while others report minimal impact [7, 8]. However, colloids offer theoretical advantages through sustained intravascular volume expansion due to higher oncotic pressure and prolonged plasma retention [9, 10]. Human albumin, in particular, provides physiologically compatible oncotic support with superior safety compared to synthetic colloids, which face increasing regulatory restrictions [11]. Despite these theoretical benefits, the specific cardiac effects of combining albumin preload with crystalloid coload, as measured by modern hemodynamic monitoring, remain unexplored.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) has emerged as a valuable tool for real-time hemodynamic assessment in anesthesia and critical care, offering direct visualization of cardiac function and volume status [12, 13]. Unlike indirect measures such as blood pressure, TTE provides direct assessment of stroke volume (SV), CO, and preload indices including velocity time integral (VTI). However, its systematic application in obstetric anesthesia remains limited, particularly for evaluating fluid strategies during cesarean delivery [14].

We specifically compared albumin preload combined with crystalloid coload versus standard crystalloid coload alone because: (1) crystalloid coload represents current standard care, (2) this combination allows pragmatic implementation without abandoning established protocols, and (3) modest albumin volumes may provide hemodynamic benefits while minimizing cost. We hypothesized that 250 mL albumin 5% preload combined with 750 mL crystalloid coload would better maintain maternal cardiac output temporal patterns compared to 1000 mL crystalloid coload alone, as measured serially by TTE during cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia.

Patient and methods

This prospective randomized controlled, double-blind trial complied with Declaration of Helsinki principles and followed Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. Prior to subject enrollment, the protocol was reviewed and approved by the Regional Research Ethics Committee, registered at the National Committee of BIO&MED. Ethics (NCBE) NO. H-04-Q-001, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, Ministry of health, Al-Qassim Region, and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06654687; principal investigator: Mohamed A. Tolba; date of registration: (October 22, 2024). The study was conducted at the obstetrics and gynecology operation room, at Qassim University Medical City, from 27/10/2024 to 25/3/2025. The 1 st case was enrolled on 27/10/2024.

Eligible participants were American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status II parturients with full-term singleton pregnancy who were scheduled for elective cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia. The exclusion criteria comprised height < 150 cm, weight < 55 kg, and body mass index ≥ 40 kg/m2; women in labor or with contraindications to spinal anesthesia (patient refusal, elevated intracranial pressure, coagulopathy, or local skin infection); chronic or gestational hypertension; baseline systolic blood pressure > 140 mmHg; hemoglobin < 10 g/dL; diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, or renal diseases; and polyhydramnios or fetal anomalies.

Written informed consent was taken from eligible participants on the day of the procedure. The study participants were randomized via R statistical software version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Participants were randomly allocated to two equal groups (combination and crystalloid) utilizing the permuted block randomization technique with randomly chosen block sizes of 4 and 6. Group allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by a statistician not involved in patient care or outcome assessment. These envelopes were stored in a secure location and opened sequentially by one investigator (M.A.) only after confirming patient eligibility and obtaining informed consent.

Study solutions were prepared in identical bags by a pharmacist not otherwise involved in patient care or data collection, and were administered by (M.A). Another investigator (N.S.A), blinded (to types of fluids), titrated the phenylephrine dose, and administered ephedrine and glycopyrrolate when required. These two investigators in addition to the fluid bags and the infusion syringe containing phenylephrine were separated by an opaque sheet from the patients and the other investigators who assessed the intraoperative outcomes.

The first investigator (M.A.T.) and another three investigators (R.A.A), (A.F.A), and (R.S.A) recorded the baseline systolic blood pressure (SBP) and heart rate (HR), and (M.A.T.) obtained the baseline TTE images, then left the operating room and returned immediately after administration of spinal anesthesia to record the intraoperative and outcome data (systolic BP, heart rate, TTE images, APGAR score at 1 and 5 min, ephedrine and glycopyrrolate requirements, total phenylephrine dose, incidence of nausea/vomiting, and the urine output).

Study protocol

Baseline measurements

Study participants were positioned supine with a 15° left lateral tilt on the operating table using an obstetric wedge. Standard monitors were attached to all patients (noninvasive blood pressure, pulse oximeter, and electrocardiography). Baseline hemodynamic parameters (SBP and HR) were recorded following a stabilization period, utilizing mean values from three consecutive measurements at 2-minute intervals. Basal TTE images were obtained typically before administration of any type of fluids and before conduction of spinal anesthesia.

Fluid interventions

All patients received two 18-gauge IV lines in the right forearm. The combination group received 250 mL of human albumin 5% (BAXTER, Austria) via infusion pump over 10 min before spinal anesthesia, followed by 750 mL Ringer’s lactate over 5 min coinciding with intrathecal injection. The crystalloid group received 1000 mL Ringer’s lactate over 5 min at the time of intrathecal injection. To maintain blinding, patients in the crystalloid coload group underwent a 10-minute sham procedure with non-opened IV lines attached. In both groups, Ringer’s lactate at 1 mL/min was administered as a maintenance fluid until cord clamping.

Anesthesia technique

Neuraxial anesthesia was performed in the sitting position utilizing a 27-gauge pencil point spinal needle at L3-L4 interspace, administering hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 12.5 mg with fentanyl 15 mcg intrathecally. Spinal anesthesia was conducted, and the APGAR score was assessed respectively by an anesthesiologist and a pediatrician not involved in the study. After SA administration, all patients were positioned in the left lateral position. Surgery proceeded only after confirming adequate anesthesia (minimum upper sensory level of T4 via pinprick testing); patients failing to reach this threshold were excluded from the study as inadequate anesthesia necessitating conversion to general anesthesia or supplemental neuraxial medication would fundamentally alter the hemodynamic profile under investigation.

Following delivery and cord clamping, phenylephrine infusion was discontinued, and patients received 10 IU oxytocin diluted in 500 mL Ringer’s lactate solution delivered intravenously over 1 h.

Hemodynamic management protocol

Prophylactic phenylephrine infusion was initiated in both groups at 50 mcg/min at the time of intrathecal injection and continued until delivery. The infusion was titrated by the blinded investigator (N.S.A.) according to a standardized protocol: the rate was increased to 50–75 mcg/min if SBP decreased by > 20% from baseline, and to 75–100 mcg/min if SBP decreased by > 30% from baseline. The infusion was decreased or temporarily stopped if SBP exceeded baseline values. For hypotension accompanied by bradycardia (HR < 60 bpm), ephedrine 5 mg IV bolus was administered instead of increasing phenylephrine. If bradycardia persisted despite ephedrine treatment, glycopyrrolate 0.1 mg IV was given. This protocol was uniformly applied to all patients in both groups.

Outcome measurements and timepoints

Cardiovascular parameters (SBP and HR) were monitored at 1-minute intervals until delivery, then every 3 min until surgery completion. Hypotension (defined as SBP decrease > 20% from baseline) and bradycardia (HR < 60 beats per minute) were treated by the previously mentioned standardized hemodynamic management. CO, SV and VTI were measured at five key timepoints: baseline, 1 and 5 min after spinal anesthesia, immediately after delivery, and 1 h post-delivery.

TTE methodology

Transthoracic echocardiographic acquisitions were performed exclusively by the first investigator (M.A.T), a practitioner with documented expertise exceeding 500 examinations prior to protocol initiation, adhering to standardized methodology established by the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines.14 The use of a single operator throughout all measurements minimized inter-operator variability. Using 5 − 1 MHz phased array probe (FUJIFILM Sonosite, Inc. Bothel, USA), two views were obtained to calculate the left ventricle (LV) SV, the parasternal long axis view (LAX) to measure the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), and the apical five-chamber view to measure the VTI or the stroke distance across the LVOT using the pulsed wave (PW) Doppler function (Fig. 1). VTI measurements were obtained by averaging 3–5 consecutive cardiac cycles to minimize beat-to-beat variability. The CSA measurement derived from LVOT diameter was obtained once at baseline as this represents a stable anatomical parameter that does not change acutely with hemodynamic variations.

Doppler left ventricular outflow stroke volume calculation. A LV outflow tract diameter measurement from a parasternal long axis view. B Pulsed Doppler recording of LV outflow tract proximal to the aortic valve to measure the velocity time integral from an apical five chamber view

SV was calculated using the 2 variables CSA and VTI across the LVOT according to the following equation, SV = CSA (cm2) × VTI (cm), CO was serially measured noninvasively using the following equation, CO = SV × HR. Intraoperative image acquisition-maintained sterility of the surgical field via physical barrier draping and ultrasound probe sterile sheathing.

The primary outcome was the changes in CO at subsequent time points compared between groups. Secondary outcomes included: the SV changes compared between groups at subsequent time points, VTI changes compared between groups at subsequent time points, total phenylephrine dose, total ephedrine dose, total glycopyrrolate dose, incidence of hypotension, incidence of bradycardia, incidence of nausea and/or vomiting, APGAR score at l and 5 min, urine output at 1 h after end of the study.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated for the primary outcome (CO) using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) framework. Based on pilot data, the baseline CO standard deviation was 1.44 L/min with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.42 across repeated measurements. Five spaced time points were planned. The minimal clinically important difference for the group-by-time interaction was set at 0.64 L/min (≈ 10% change from baseline), representing the smallest physiologically relevant improvement likely to enhance uteroplacental perfusion. A random intercept for subject and normally distributed residuals were assumed. Power was assessed by simulation (500 iterations per scenario) in (R Core Team, 2024; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the lme4 package. For each simulated dataset, we compared the full model including the group-by-time interaction with a nested model excluding the interaction, using a likelihood ratio test at α = 0.05 (two-sided). Under these assumptions, 80 participants per group provided 82% power. Allowing for potential attrition, we targeted 81 per group (total 162 patients).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Modified intention-to-treat analysis encompassed all randomized participants who received the allocated intervention and achieved adequate spinal anesthesia (T4 sensory level) in their allocated groups, regardless of other protocol deviations. All 162 randomized patients achieved adequate anesthesia and completed the study; therefore, no patients were excluded from analysis. There were no missing data for primary or secondary outcomes; all participants completed all planned measurements. Distribution normality was assessed via histogram analysis and Kolmogorov-Smirnov testing. Data presentation followed standard statistical formats: mean ± standard deviation for normal distributions, median (range) for non-normal distributions, and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Intergroup comparisons utilized Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric outcomes and chi-square analysis for categorical variables.

Temporal analyses of hemodynamic parameters (SBP, HR, VTI, SV, CO) were analyzed using LMMs with fixed effects for group, time, and group-by-time interaction, a random intercept for subject, and baseline values as covariates. Tukey-style adjustments’ post-hoc tests were used for multiple comparisons at specific time points involving estimated marginal means (e.g., via emmeans in R). Model assumptions were assessed via residual diagnostics. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for primary and secondary outcomes. Exploratory analyses, including the overall group effect on CO and individual timepoint comparisons, were not prespecified and were interpreted descriptively without adjustment for multiple comparisons. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, as the risk of type I error increases with multiple testing. Future studies should prespecify such analyses or use appropriate corrections for multiplicity.

Vasopressor requirements did not differ significantly between groups. The median total phenylephrine dose was 666 (454–1200) mcg in the crystalloid group compared to 600 (424–1150) mcg in the combination group (p = 0.142). Ephedrine requirements were identical between groups with a median dose of 5 (0–10) mg in the crystalloid group and 5 (0–15) mg in the combination group (p = 0.856). The proportion of patients requiring ephedrine was similar between groups (crystalloid 34.6% vs. combination 32.1%, p = 0.73), with comparable timing from spinal injection to first ephedrine administration (median 8 min [range 2–18] vs. 9 min [range 3–17], p = 0.52). Glycopyrrolate use was minimal, with a median dose of 0 (0–100) mcg in both groups (p = 0.921) (Table 2).

Neonatal outcomes were excellent in both groups. In both groups, the median 1-minute APGAR score was 9 (8–10) (p = 0.783), and the median 5-minute APGAR score was 10 (9–10) (p = 0.892) (Table 2). The incidence of nausea and vomiting in the combination group did not differ compared to the crystalloid group, crystalloid group: 25 patients (30.9%), colloid group: 17 patients (21.0%) (p = 0.148) (Table 2). Blood loss in both groups was comparable (Table 2). When comparing urine output between the two groups, patients in the combination group demonstrated significantly higher urine output; 350 (150–950) compared to those in the crystalloid group; 300 (100–900) (p = 0.04) (Table 2).

Discussion

This prospective, randomized, double-blind study demonstrated no statistically significant differences in both group as regard CO changes at different time points during cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia when using a combination of 250-mL albumin 5% preload and 750-mL crystalloid coload compared with 1000-mL crystalloid coload. The SV changes, total phenylephrine dose, total ephedrine dose, total glycopyrrolate dose, incidence of hypotension, incidence of bradycardia, incidence of nausea and/or vomiting, and APGAR score at l and 5 min did not significantly differ between the two groups. VTI measurements at different time points and urine output at 1 h after the end of the study were higher significantly in the combination group. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use TTE to directly compare the effects of human albumin preload combined with crystalloid coload versus crystalloid coload alone on cardiac function during cesarean delivery.

We selected temporal CO dynamics as our primary outcome rather than static point measurements because this longitudinal approach comprehensively evaluates each fluid strategy’s capacity to maintain cardiovascular stability throughout critical perioperative transitions, including neuraxial blockade, surgical intervention, and post-delivery recalibration. This addresses the clinically relevant question: ‘Which strategy better preserves CO throughout the entire procedure?’ rather than at isolated moments, avoiding the risk of missing important hemodynamic response and recovery patterns.

We used 250 mL of 5% albumin to balance efficacy with cost-effectiveness. Albumin provides physiologically compatible oncotic pressure with superior safety compared to synthetic colloids, avoiding coagulopathy, tissue accumulation, and potential renal dysfunction [15]. This modest volume was selected based on pilot data from our institution demonstrating adequate hemodynamic effects, and pharmacokinetic principles demonstrating sustained plasma expansion despite limited volume [16]. Our intent was to test whether a small, pragmatic albumin preload alters cardiac filling dynamics. Recent regulatory restrictions on synthetic colloids [17] make this albumin-based approach particularly relevant for contemporary obstetric practice.

CO measurements showed similar trends between groups without statistically significant differences, though the combination group trended toward higher values at 1-minute post-spinal (difference 329.4 mL, p = 0.0663) and post-delivery (difference 327.6 mL, p = 0.0678). Both groups reached maximum CO post-delivery (approximately 7.5 L) before returning to near-baseline at 1-hour, aligning with physiological timelines documented in both historical [18] and contemporary studies [19] showing CO normalization within 60–90 min post-delivery These findings parallel McDonald et al. [4] who reported modestly higher CO with colloid (0.5–0.7 L/min difference) that also did not reach significance, suggesting both fluid strategies adequately maintained CO during critical perioperative periods.

However, VTI measurements revealed significant differences between groups, with the combination group demonstrating higher values at both 1-minute post-spinal anesthesia (difference of 1.87 cm, p = 0.0024) and at 1-hour post-study (difference of 1.40 cm, p = 0.0233). VTI measures the distance that blood travels during each ejection phase. Unlike CO, VTI provides a more direct reflection of cardiac filling and preload conditions without being confounded by the compensatory mechanisms, [14, 20] offering a more reliable indicator of volume status and potentially explaining the significant VTI differences observed in our study despite similar CO measurements. As colloids have higher oncotic pressure and remain in the intravascular space longer, [21] this leads to more sustained plasma volume expansion and more effective prevention of hypotension than crystalloid pre-load [21]. These points could explain both, the 1-minute post-spinal difference (1.87 cm) indicating better initial volume status and maintained cardiac filling due to the effect of the administration of 250-mL colloid before spinal anesthesia, and the persistent difference at 1-hour (1.40 cm) demonstrating sustained effect of colloids.

The observed VTI increase of 1.32 cm corresponds to approximately 5 mL additional stroke volume per beat, equating to 0.3–0.5 L/min increased CO (4–8% over baseline) at typical heart rates. However, the clinical significance remains speculative. Despite statistical significance, this VTI advantage did not translate into reduced vasopressor requirements (p = 0.142), improved neonatal outcomes (p > 0.78), or fewer maternal adverse events. Whether this difference would benefit higher-risk populations (cardiac disease, preeclampsia, hemorrhage) requires adequately powered, outcome-focused trials. These findings are hypothesis-generating, not definitive evidence of clinical superiority.

Temporal hemodynamic analysis showed significant intergroup differences in both pre- and post-delivery phases. Pre-delivery, the combination group had lower heart rates at minutes 1, 2, and 5 (p < 0.05), indicating reduced chronotropic response. Similarly, SBP measurements showed greater stability in the combination group, with significant differences at minutes 2 and 4 (p < 0.05). This hemodynamic advantage persisted into the post-delivery phase, where the combination group maintained more consistent SBP parameters (within a narrow range 114–115 mmHg) and exhibited less HR variability at multiple timepoints (minutes 9, 18, and 21; p < 0.05). These findings partially parallel our previous investigation [22] comparing synthetic colloid preload with crystalloid coload, which demonstrated improved SBP at 3 min post-injection, though without significant heart rate differences.

Vasopressor requirements were comparable between groups, with median phenylephrine doses of 600 mcg in the combination group versus 666 mcg in the crystalloid group (p = 0.142). Our doses align with recent studies employing prophylactic infusion protocols ranging from 500 to 1000 mcg during cesarean deliveries [23, 24]. The similarity in vasopressor requirements despite different fluid strategies suggests that prophylactic phenylephrine effectively maintained maternal blood pressure regardless of fluid choice.

Maternal and neonatal outcomes were excellent in both groups. APGAR scores at 1 and 5 min were comparable (median 9 and 10, respectively; p > 0.78), indicating adequate uteroplacental perfusion. The combination group had significantly higher urine output (350 vs. 300 mL, p = 0.038), possibly reflecting different fluid distribution patterns. Nausea and vomiting incidence were similar between groups (21% vs. 30.9%, p = 0.148).

Some issues should be acknowledged. First, while we observed trends in CO differences, the study may have been underpowered to detect statistically significant differences in this primary outcome. Second, despite using standardized TTE techniques following American Society of Echocardiography guidelines, these measurements can be technically challenging in the perioperative setting, particularly post-delivery due to movement artifacts during surgery and interference from surgical instruments and cautery. We tried to address this by minimizing the probe manipulation time and efficient communication with the surgical team. Also, while our TTE measurements were performed by a single experienced operator, we did not formally assess intra-rater variability or reproducibility. Future studies should include formal reproducibility assessments with blinded re-analysis of stored images to quantify measurement precision. Third, we limited the analysis of the predelivery serial changes in HR and SBP up to 5 min after intrathecal injection, which was the shortest spinal-to-delivery time, potentially missing later effects. Fourth, given the significant impact of nausea and vomiting and fetal outcomes on these types of patients, we recommend that future research specifically investigate the direct effects of fluid management strategies on these patient-centered outcomes to optimize patient care. Finally, our protocol focused specifically on SBP as this is the most used parameter for defining and managing spinal-induced hypotension in cesarean delivery and aligns with most published studies in this field. However, diastolic and mean arterial pressure measurements would have provided additional hemodynamic information, particularly regarding perfusion pressure, and should be considered in future investigations.

In conclusion, this randomized controlled trial found no statistically significant difference in the temporal patterns of CO (group-by-time interaction) between combination albumin preload plus crystalloid coload versus crystalloid coload alone during cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia. While the combination strategy showed improvements in VTI measurements and urine output, these differences did not translate into clinically meaningful improvements in vasopressor requirements, maternal hemodynamic stability, or neonatal outcomes. The clinical significance of the observed VTI differences requires validation in larger studies with patient-centered outcome measures. Current evidence does not support routine preference for the more expensive albumin-containing regimen over standard crystalloid coload protocols.
