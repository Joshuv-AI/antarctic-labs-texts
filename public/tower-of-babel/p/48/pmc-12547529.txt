Effect of Entrapped and Free Calcium Hydroxyapatite Particles on Rheological Properties of Hyaluronic Acid–Calcium Hydroxyapatite Composite Hydrogels
Authors: Hai Xin, Anne Seigneur, Aude Bernardin, Sabitoj Singh Virk, Akashdeep Singh Virk, Maria Calderon Vaca, Samiul Amin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Injectable hydrogels are intensely studied for biomedical and cosmeceutical applications where a polymer matrix is cross-linked to incorporate therapeutic molecules or particles and injected to achieve drug delivery, tissue regeneration, skin wound healing, etc. However, to realize these potential applications, a deeper understanding of rheological and viscoelastic behaviors of the hydrogel systems and matrix-particle interactions is required for future formula optimization and product development. To fill this gap, in the present work, we prepared chemically cross-linked hyaluronic acid (HA) and incorporated calcium hydroxyapatite (CaHA) particles to fabricate HA-CaHA hydrogel composites. CaHA fillers were integrated into the HA network before or after the cross-linking reactions to create either entrapped (before cross-linking) or free (after cross-linking) particles, and the resultant specimens were analyzed with various rheological tests to investigate their effects on the viscoelasticity, creep recovery, yield stress, and flow behaviors of the overall HA-CaHA hydrogel composites. To the best of our knowledge, the preparation of biopolymer-based injectable hydrogels incorporating both entrapped CaHA and free CaHA particles has not been reported by far. The present work not only provides a systematic rheological profile of HA-CaHA injectable hydrogels but also investigates the influence of two types of CaHA particles on the overall viscoelastic and rheological properties of the hydrogel system. The results will present crucial information to support future optimizations in hydrogel design, biomaterial selection, and structural analysis for various wet soft materials with promising biomedical and cosmaceutical applications.

Introduction

Hydrogels are three-dimensional networks with super water-absorbing capabilities, and they have great potential for various biomedical, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic applications thanks to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, porosity, and tunable mechanical and rheological properties. , Hydrogels have been widely researched as promising candidates for tissue engineering, drug deliveries, skin wound healing, biosensors, and cartilage and soft tissue substitutes. Among the various types of hydrogels, injectable hydrogels have recently received intense attention. These hydrogels are prepared by synthetic or biomacromolecules, and the matrix polymers can be covalently cross-linked or physically cross-linked to form a gel network which can flow and be injected under the skin to delivery therapeutical drugs, proteins, growth factors, nanoparticles, cells, and exosomes. ,− Injectable hydrogels can be synthesized via physical cross-links such as ionic chelation and hydrogen bonding, etc. These physical hydrogels exhibit reversible cross-linking but mechanical instability and stress relaxation. − On the other hand, covalently cross-linked hydrogels demonstrate enhanced mechanical stability due to their covalent bonds which are more stable and difficult to break than physical interactions. , The cross-linked polymer networks can absorb water, thus yielding a biocompatible matrix which can be used as a cargo depot to accommodate multiple types of therapeutic molecules. When nanoparticles are involved in the polymer network, the obtained products are referred to as nanocomposite hydrogels. − Rheological properties such as viscoelasticity, thixotropy, and creep recovery not only play important roles in real-life applications of injectable hydrogels in biomedical and cosmeceutical fields but also reveal important polymer–particle interactions. However, there is currently a shortage of studies focused on the effects of incorporated particles or freely mixed fillers on the rheological performance of the overall hydrogel composites. A lack of knowledge on this aspect will limit future formulation, material characterization, and product development.

Thus, the present work aims to conduct systematic rheological characterizations on covalently cross-linked hyaluronic acid (HA) hydrogels which incorporated calcium hydroxyapatite (CaHA) particles to expand the current research frontier to address this remaining issue. Hyaluronic acids were used here as the template polymer, since it is one of the most used biopolymers with excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and tunable mechanical properties and it has been widely used in biomedical and pharmaceutical research. Calcium hydroxyapatites (CaHA) are inorganic particles which are available in both micro- and nanoscales. It has been shown to play active roles in cartilage, bone, and skin regeneration. , In the present work, CaHA fillers were incorporated into the HA network before or after the cross-linking reactions. The CaHA particles added before the cross-linking reactions were entrapped or locked into the cross-linked polymer network, while the particles mixed with the hydrogel after the cross-linking were considered as free particles. , For the hydrogel preparation, the concentration of either free or entrapped CaHA was varied while the other one was retained constant, and the resultant hydrogel samples were tested with different rheological protocols. It has been found that entrapped CaHA particles had a larger effect on structural rigidity and modulus increase for the hydrogel composite, while free CaHA particle is a bigger contributor to the increase of viscous flow. In creep recovery tests, all of the hydrogel composites showed significant structural recoverability, and their deformation-recovery curves are composed of an instantaneous elasticity and time-dependent viscoelasticity components. The formula prepared with 10 wt % entrapped CaHA and no free CaHA demonstrated almost full creep recoverability. The research outcome of the present work provides important information on structure–property relationships of injectable biopolymer hydrogels, which lays a firm foundation for the development of drug delivery, tissue scaffolding, skin wound healing, and cosmetic patches based on hydrogels.

This study presents a systematic rheological evaluation of hyaluronic acid–calcium hydroxyapatite composite hydrogels, comparing the effects of adding free CaHA particles versus entrapped CaHA particles. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to directly contrast these two particle configurations within a hyaluronic acid/calcium hydroxyapatite composite hydrogel. Characterizing the impact of particle entrapment on the flow curve, yield stress, viscoelastic moduli, and structural recovery is essential for the design of biomedical and pharmaceutical formulations. In the context of dermal fillers, for instance, rheological profiles determine product performance and suitability for specific anatomical regions. Furthermore, the present work also contributes systematic rheology characterization protocols that can be utilized to assess and analyze various gel systems for biomedical and cosmaceutical applications.

Experiments

The cross-linked hyaluronic acid gels with either entrapped or free CaHA fillers were supplied by Allergan Aesthetics (Pringy, France). CaHA particles used in this study were characterized by the supplier. The reported particle size distribution was as follows: D10 = 26.4–27.7 μm, D50 = 33.4–35.1 μm, and D90 = 43.3–44.3 μm, indicating that 10, 50, and 90% of the particles were smaller than these respective diameters. All values fall within the range (1–1000 μm). According to the supplier, the particles exhibit a dense, spherical morphology with smooth surfaces and a low specific surface area (SSA < 0.1 m2/g). To prepare the HA-CaHA hydrogel composite with entrapped CaHA, CaHA fillers at various weight percentages were added to the mixture before the cross-linking reaction. These fillers were locked by the cross-linked polymer network following the reaction. The CaHA fillers mixed following the HA hydrogel cross-linking in this approach were considered free particles, which allowed more motion flexibility. In each group, one formulation variable was changed, while the other two remained unchanged or similar.

Sample homogeneity was evaluated by using rheological, optical, and extrusion force tests. For rheology, frequency sweep measurements were performed on multiple syringes with both the first and second halves of each syringe tested independently. No systematic differences were observed between halves, and coefficients of variation (CV) for G′ and G″ were typically below 5%. The only exception was formulation F10–45, which showed higher variability (CV = 13% for G′), a level considered acceptable for manual, laboratory-scale preparation. To further assess homogeneity, two formulations with a higher free CaHA content (F10–45 and F10–20) were tested for opacity using an internal UV spectroscopic method. Measurements at the beginning, middle, and end of each syringe were highly consistent with CV values of 0.2 and 0.6%. Extrusion force tests were also conducted by compressing syringes fitted with a 27G needle. All formulations produced smooth and flat force–displacement curves, indicating a uniform particle distribution through the syringes.

Rheological Tests

All of the rheological characterizations in the present research were conducted with a NETZSCH Kinexus rotational rheometer with a parallel plate testing geometry. The upper plate used was PU25L2371SS, and its distance to the lower plate was set to 1 mm according to the specific testing profiles selected from the rSpace which was the software supplied by the rheometer manufacturer for controlling the rheometer operation and recording the testing data. Before the start of each test, the same amount of HA-CaHA hydrogels were injected to the lower plate, and a specific testing profile was selected from rSpace. A two-piece sample cap was sealed to cover the entire testing area to prevent water loss during the run. All rheological tests were conducted at room temperature to reflect the product’s condition prior to application. While the material is expected to reach physiological temperature (∼37 °C) upon administration, evaluating its behavior under those conditions was beyond the scope of this study and represents a valuable direction for future investigation. Due to limited sample availability, rheological measurements were performed in duplicate. The resulting curves showed strong overlap between replicates, indicating high reproducibility; therefore, a representative replicate is presented in the results. To measure the linear viscoelastic region (LVER), the amplitude sweep mode was applied wherein the upper plate oscillated at various shear strains from 0.1 to 100% while the frequency was maintained at 1 Hz. Elastic modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), and phase angle were recorded, and the LVER was measured as the shear strain by which the G′ decreased by 5% with respect to the original value. Then, LVER for each formula was applied in the following frequency sweep test, where the specimen underwent oscillatory shear from 10 to 0.1 Hz. G′, G″, and phase angle was recorded by rSpace software for further analysis. In the flow curve test, each specimen was sheared by varying the shear rates from 0.1 to 100/s, and plots of shear stress and shear viscosity to the shear rate were generated. Yield stress was measured by applying shear stress on each specimen from 0 to 200 Pa, and the stress corresponding to the peak viscosity was recorded as the yield stress. Finally, to measure the mechanical recoverability, a creep recovery test was performed on each formula. In the test, the specimen underwent a shear stress of 1 Pa for 3 min to initiate creeping strains, and then the shear stress was removed, and the strain was allowed to recover for 3 min. Such a shear-recovery cycle was repeated 5 times to measure the degree of recoverability for each formula.

Results

Effect of Free CaHA Particles

Figures – demonstrate the effects of changing the concentration of mixed CaHA particles (free) on a series of rheological properties of overall HA-CaHA hydrogel, whereas the concentration of entrapped CaHA was maintained at 10 wt %. Here, the mixed CaHA was added to the formula after the HA cross-linking, and thus they were considered as “free particles”. As shown in Figure , increasing the concentration of free CaHA particles led to a significant increase in both G′ and G″. The initial G′ was 151, 222, and 795 Pa for the formula containing 0, 20, and 45 wt % mixed CaHA particles, respectively, while their initial G″ increased from 17 to 27 to 170 Pa, respectively. For all three formulas, G′ exhibited a linear elasticity region before it decreased significantly, and in the present research, the LVER critical strain was estimated as the strain at which G′ decreased by 5% compared to its initial value. Thus, the estimated LVER was 17, 15.85, and 0.63% for the HA-CaHA hydrogel containing 0, 20, and 45 wt % free CaHA particles, respectively. Adding more mixed (free) CaHA remarkably reduced elasticity and increased viscosity of the overall formulation. As a result, the HA-CaHA composite hydrogel yielded a much smaller shear strain. This observation was also reflected by the frequency sweep test in Figure where the hydrogel formula with 45 wt % mixed CaHA particles showed a small distance between G′ and G″ curve and larger phase angle compared to the other two formulas (0 and 20 wt % free CaHA particles). Both results signal a decreased elasticity, and at high frequency the sample has more tendency to flow. Conversely, the other two hydrogels containing 0 and 20 wt % free CaHA exhibited parallel G′ and G″ with dominating elasticity.

Both F10–0 and F10–20 exhibited remarkable creep recoverability with minimal residual strain in each cycle, and their creep recovery curves were almost identical across the five test cycles. Almost 100% creep strain was recovered in F10–0 which was synthesized with 10 wt % entrapped CaHA but 0 free CaHA. F10–45 was prepared with 45 wt % free CaHA, and this formula also exhibited substantial compliance recovery (from 73 to 93%). However, its initial shear compliance significantly decreased in subsequent cycles. This may signal a progressive tightening or hardening of the hydrogel network, and the possible mechanism is attributed to the HA-CaHA interactions. Under the repetitive shear stress, free CaHA fillers and HA polymer chains were rearranged to form even tighter interactions to resist the deformation.

Effect of Entrapped CaHA Particles

Figures and demonstrate the effects of varying the concentration of entrapped CaHA particles on the rheological behavior of HA-CaHA hydrogel composites. In the three formulations studied here, the concentration of mixed (free) CaHA particles were kept constant at zero while the concentration of entrapped CaHA varied at 0, 10, and 30 wt %. The amplitude sweeps measurements (Figure ) clearly indicate an increased G′ and G″ with increasing the amount of entrapped (cross-linked) CaHA particles. The initial G′ was 64.78 Pa for the hydrogel composite containing 0 wt % cross-linked CaHA particles, and it rose to 151.30 Pa for 10 wt % and to 376.50 Pa for 30 wt %, respectively. The initial G″ also exhibited a substantial increase from 7.34 Pa (0 wt %) to 17.41 Pa (10 wt %) to 48 Pa (30 wt %). Thus, similar to free CaHA, adding more entrapped (cross-linked) CaHA fillers also increased the ability of the hydrogel composite to resist the external shear. Meanwhile, all three formulas showed a clear LVER plateau, but the LVER strain decreased from 19.95% for the hydrogel containing 0 wt % cross-linked CaHA to 15.85% (for 10 wt % cross-linked CaHA) and 10% (for 30 wt % cross-linked CaHA), respectively. These results showed that the entrapped CaHA also played a role in reducing the elasticity and increasing the viscosity of the overall hydrogel formula. Following the amplitude sweep tests, frequency sweep tests were carried out by shearing the specimens from 10 to 0.1 Hz within their LVER regions. Similar to Section , all three formulas exhibited dominating G′ which was paralleled with G″, and a slight increase was observed in both moduli curves as a function of rising oscillation frequency. Increasing the concentration of entrapped CaHA from 0 to 30 wt % exerted no change to the solid-like nature of HA-CaHA hydrogel. When the specimen was sheared at a high frequency, shorter time was allowed for the HA polymer chains and entrapped CaHA particles to rearrange or relax in response to the external shearing. Consequently, the sample would exhibit a higher deformation resistance. On the other hand, when low frequency was applied, more time was allowed for the polymer network to relax or reorient in line with the shearing force and thus reduced their resistance (Figure ).

In creep recovery tests, all three hydrogel composites exhibited viscoelastic strain and recovery behavior similar to that discussed in Section . For each hydrogel, its total creep compliance consisted of an elastic compliance which occurred immediately responding to the stress and a delayed and viscoelastic compliance that was dependent as a function of time. Meanwhile, similar to Section , the hydrogel composite prepared with a higher concentration of solid CaHA particles showed smaller creep compliances in each of the five test cycles. Increasing the content of cross-linked CaHA enhanced structural rigidity and deformation resistance.

As shown in Table , the three hydrogel samples tested here were synthesized with 0, 10, or 30 wt % entrapped CaHA, respectively, without any free CaHA. The samples were sheared at 1 Pa for 3 min, and their creep strains were allowed to recover for 3 min. All three formulas exhibited substantial creep reversibility within the set time frame, but the formula E10–0 (10 wt % entrapped CaHA) demonstrated a much faster recovery rate with approximately 100% compliance recovered within 3 min. This observation may suggest the importance of optimizing the concentration of entrapped CaHA. This trend suggests that while moderate levels of entrapped CaHA enhance network stability, excessive entrapment may hinder the hydrogel’s ability to recover from deformation. The increased filler content likely introduces internal resistance and promotes viscous flow, thereby delaying recovery. These findings underscore the need to fine-tune the concentration of entrapped CaHA to achieve an optimal balance between structural reinforcement and elastic responsiveness. A critical amount of entrapped CaHA is required to stabilize and restrict the relaxation of HA polymer chains. Otherwise, the chain relaxation of the HA polymer will lead to a prolonged creep recovery time, as shown in Figure a for the formula E0–0 (pure HA hydrogel). On the other hand, excessive CaHA fillers will result in an increase of viscous flow and also a delayed creep recovery as shown in Figure c for formula E30–0 (30 wt % entrapped CaHA).

Sections and 3.2 discuss the effects of adding free or entrapped CaHA particles on the rheological and viscoelastic behavior of HA-CaHA hydrogel composites. In order to provide a clear comparison between these two types, a series of rheological properties were measured on three different formulas, as shown in Table c. Here, the hydrogel prepared with 11 mg/g HA and zero CaHA particles was used as the baseline (E0–0), compared with the other two hydrogels which were synthesized with either 30 wt % mixed CaHA (E0–30) or 30 wt % cross-linked CaHA (E30–0).

As shown in Figures and , both E0–30 and E30–0 exhibited much higher G′ and G″ than E0–0, confirming the role played by the CaHA addition in elevating the ability of the sample to resist the shearing deformation. Compared with free CaHA, the entrapped CaHA appeared to have a bigger effect on the modulus increase and structural rigidity. E0–30 (0 wt % entrapped CaHA and 30 wt % mixed free CaHA) had an initial G′ and G″ of 212 and 30.69 Pa, respectively, while E30–0 (30 wt % entrapped CaHA and 0 wt % mixed free CaHA) showed an initial G′ and G″ at 377 and 48 Pa, respectively. This observation suggests that the CaHA particles entrapped with HA cross-linking achieved a better reinforcement to the HA network than the free CaHA particles which were mixed into the hydrogel following the cross-linking. Furthermore, E0–0 formula (no CaHA added) showed a LVER critical strain at 19.95%, and the critical strain decreased to 1.26% for E0–30 (30 wt % mixed free CaHA) and 10% for E30–0 (30 wt % entrapped CaHA). These results clearly demonstrate that compared with entrapped CaHA, free CaHA fillers had a larger effect on the increase of viscosity dominance for the hydrogel composite.

Discussion

Hydrogel is the most promising type of biomaterial that has been designed and optimized for a variety of biomedical, pharmaceutical, and cosmeceutical applications. As a biocompatible and water-retaining matrix, biopolymer-based hydrogels such as HA have been utilized to incorporate therapeutic molecules, nanoparticles, and cells to prepare injectable drug deliveries and aesthetic products. , How the incorporated particles interact with a cross-linked hydrogel network and influence its structure and properties are important and imperative information for future hydrogel research. The present work evaluated cross-linked HA hydrogels. CaHA particles were added into the HA network before or after the cross-linking reaction to create two types of CaHA particles: entrapped (before cross-linking) or free (after cross-linking). The concentrations of entrapped or free CaHA particles were varied systematically, while the other synthesis parameters were kept constant to investigate the effects of free or entrapped CaHA on the rheological properties of HA-CaHA hydrogel composites. We carried out a series of rheological measurements to understand the impact of incorporated fillers on the viscosity, shear stress, yield stress, LVER, time dependence, and creep recovery of the overall hydrogel system. These rheological properties are determining factors to design and optimize the novel hydrogels in the above-mentioned fields. More importantly, the test results reveal critical interactions between the polymer host and particle fillers, which underlies future hydrogel structural design, formulation, and product development. These insights have direct implications for the design of dermal fillers with improved handling properties, drug delivery systems with controlled release profiles, and scaffolds with tunable mechanics for regenerative medicine. Looking ahead, a better mechanistic understanding of filler–polymer interactions could enable the comprehensive design of hydrogels tailored to emerging biomedical and cosmeceutical needs.

As shown by the results from Figures –, increasing the concentrations of either free or entrapped CaHA particles increased the stiffness (G′ and G″), shear viscosity, and yield stress of HA-CaHA hydrogel composites. These observations are due to the presence of more solid particles in the hydrogel network, which could increase the polymer-particles and interparticle frictions under the shear. Although the primary focus of this study was not to investigate the influence of particle size on rheological behavior, it is worth noting that the CaHA particles used were predominantly microscale in size, with D10, D50, and D90 values ranging from approximately 26–44 μm. The particles were dense, spherical, and smooth with low specific surface area. These characteristics may influence the observed viscoelastic behavior by modulating particle–polymer interactions, network structure, and flow resistance. Future studies could explore how variations in particle size and morphology affect the mechanical and rheological performance of CaHA–HA hydrogels. Comparable trends have been reported in recent literature on particle-reinforced HA hydrogels and other nanocomposite systems, where filler concentration and morphology strongly influence stiffness, viscoelasticity, and creep recovery. Consequently, the obtained HA-CaHA hydrogels exhibited an enhanced resistance to flow and deformation. An optimal concentration of solid fillers needs to be established by formulation chemists. , Figures – compare the effects between free and entrapped CaHA at 30 wt %. The entrapped CaHA had a larger effect on the elevation of the modulus, shear stress, and viscosity of the hydrogel composite. It has been proposed that the CaHA fillers added prior to HA cross-linking are finally interlocked by a cross-linked polymer network. Due to their limited mobility, the entrapped CaHA fillers cannot make as many contributions as free CaHA to the viscous flow. Instead, these fillers are relatively fixed between the cross-linked polymer chains and in return they reforce and stabilize the HA network, making an increase of structural stiffness and resistance to deformation. On the other hand, the CaHA added after the HA cross-linking is free particles which allow more structural space and flexibility to initiate viscous flow under the shear. With much less restriction imposed by a cross-linked polymer, free CaHA are able to move and slide more easily to increase the viscosity and reduce the elasticity of the overall hydrogel composite. As shown in Figure , compared with locked CaHA, free CaHA more greatly influenced the reduction of LVER critical strain. The different roles played by free and cross-linked CaHA in structure and rheology of the present hydrogel composite provide critical information on future properties optimization and structural tuning. Whether the free CaHA fillers possess a more efficient diffusing kinetics due to their motion flexibility is an important question to be addressed by further studies. Moreover, if cross-linked CaHA has a bigger role to play in stabilizing the HA network, its optimal concentration and diffusion behavior need to be studied.

In the creep recovery tests, all of the HA-CaHA hydrogel formulas demonstrated a typical viscoelastic strain and recovery curve. The curves consisted of an immediate and recoverable elastic creep compliance and a delayed viscoelastic strain that could be recovered with time. As shown in Figures and , the HA-CaHA hydrogel composite containing a higher concentration of either free or entrapped CaHA demonstrated a smaller creep compliance in each test cycle. This observation again confirmed the role of solid CaHA fillers in increasing the resistance of the hydrogel to external flow and deformation. More importantly, it is imperative to understand how entrapped and free CaHA particles interact with hyaluronic hydrogel networks. Whether those particles are simply embedded inside the hydrogel structures or form any physical or chemical associations will be an important question to be addressed. A detailed investigation on the microscopic mechanism to explain and describe the viscoelastic recovery of the HA-CaHA composite is necessary as well, as this is closely related to the structural evolution in real application conditions. Although long-term stability testing was not performed on the experimental samples presented in this study, such evaluations are routinely conducted during product development. These include monitoring rheological and mechanical properties under ICH stability conditions for up to 24 months. Additionally, commercial formulations are tested for performance under repeated shear stress to simulate injection. These studies help ensure that the hydrogel maintains its functional properties during administration and handling, which is critical for maintaining its performance throughout its shelf life. In addition to long-term stability, biological safety is also a key consideration in the development of HA–CaHA hydrogel formulations. Although biocompatibility and cytotoxicity testing were not performed on the experimental samples in this study, which is a limitation for the present work, such evaluations are routinely carried out during later stages of product development. The individual components of these formulations, hyaluronic acid and calcium hydroxyapatite, are well-established biomaterials with demonstrated safety and biocompatibility in both in vitro and clinical settings. For example, hyaluronic acid has been extensively reviewed and confirmed to be safe for cosmetic and medical use, and calcium hydroxyapatite nanoparticles have shown selective cytotoxicity toward cancer cells while sparing normal cells. These assessments are typically conducted under standardized protocols once the final formulation is defined.

Conclusions

In the present work, we successfully synthesized chemically cross-linked HA hydrogels which incorporated CaHA particles before or after the cross-linking reactions to create two possible particle types, i.e., entrapped particles (before cross-linking) and free particles (after cross-linking). A series of rheological characterizations were performed on the formulas, which were prepared by varying the concentrations of either free or entrapped CaHA while the other synthesis conditions were maintained the same. It has been found that adding more CaHA particles (both free and entrapped) significantly increased the shear viscosity, yield stress, G′, and G″ of HA-CaHA hydrogel composites. Compared with free CaHA, entrapped CaHA fillers were locked by cross-linked HA network. The close and tight polymer–particle interactions stabilized the movement of both HA chains and entrapped CaHA under the shear. Thus, entrapped CaHA played a bigger role than free CaHA in structural rigidity and modulus increase. On the other hand, free CaHA were allowed flexibility and motions. They could slide past each other more easily and contribute more significantly than cross-linked CaHA to the viscous behavior of the hydrogel. All of the HA-CaHA samples demonstrated time-dependent viscoelastic strain and recovery during the creep recovery tests. Even though all hydrogel formulas demonstrated a high level of creep recovery, the sample prepared with 10 wt % entrapped CaHA showed the best creep recoverability. The research outcomes of the present work revealed important structural information and mechanisms regarding the effects of free and entrapped filler particles on the rheological behavior of the hydrogel network. It is critical for future structure modification, formula optimization, and product development of hydrogel-based cosmeceuticals and dermal fillers. Meanwhile, the injectable hydrogels reported in the present work possess cross-linked network, creep recovery, tunable rheology, and ability to accommodate bioactive particles. These characteristics provide the present hydrogel system with great potential for future applicability in drug delivery, tissue regeneration scaffolds, and skin wound dressings. In order to fulfill these proposed applications, further investigations on materials characterization techniques, biocompatibility, drug delivery kinetics, cellular proliferations, and differentiations are required.
