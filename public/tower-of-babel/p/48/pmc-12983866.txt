Clinical trial landscape of CAR-based cell therapy for gastrointestinal malignancies
Authors: Wenjie Li, Jiahui Yang, Qingyan Kong, Zheyu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal malignancies (GIM) impose a substantial global health burden, accounting for approximately 33% of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Although chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-based cell therapy has achieved remarkable success in hematological malignancies, its application in solid tumors, particularly GIM, remains in its nascent stages. This comprehensive landscape analysis systematically examined the clinical trial ecosystem of CAR-based cell therapy for GIM by retrieving 179 eligible trials from the Trialtrove database as of October 2025 (hepatobiliary and pancreatic malignancies were excluded). The analysis revealed a predominantly early-phase landscape, with Phase I studies constituting 70.39% of all trials. Geographically, China (70.77%) and the USA (16.41%) dominated trial initiation, while academic institutions sponsored 54.92% of investigations. Claudin 18.2, NKG2D(L), mesothelin, and CEA emerged as the most frequently targeted antigens. Autologous therapy administered via peripheral intravenous infusion represented the predominant therapeutic modality. Combination strategies incorporating chemotherapy and immunotherapy demonstrated promising synergistic potential, suggesting that multimodal approaches may enhance therapeutic efficacy while potentially mitigating resistance mechanisms. The convergence of innovative preclinical developments with increasingly supportive regulatory frameworks portends transformative advances in the field, positioning CAR-based cell therapy as an emerging cornerstone modality in the therapeutic armamentarium against GIM.Keywords: Chimeric antigen receptor - CAR, Combination therapy, Gastrointestinal Cancer

Gastrointestinal malignancies (GIM) represent a substantial global health burden, accounting for approximately 33% of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Despite considerable therapeutic advances, the prognosis for patients with advanced-stage GIM remains poor, with 5-year survival rates frequently below 15%.1 Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-based cell therapy has revolutionized cancer immunotherapy by engineering immune effector cells to recognize tumor-associated antigens with excellent specificity, achieving remarkable success in hematological malignancies.2 The abundant expression of targetable antigens in GIM provides a compelling rationale for CAR-based cell therapy; however, the translational trajectory from hematological success to solid tumor applications remains nascent and incompletely characterized. This landscape analysis comprehensively examined the current clinical trial ecosystem investigating CAR-based cell therapy for GIM, thereby providing critical insights to inform future research trajectories and accelerate clinical translation.

We conducted a systematic retrieval from the Trialtrove database (https://clinicalintelligence.citeline.com/), a premier global clinical trial intelligence platform that aggregates data from authoritative registries, including ClinicalTrials.gov, EU Clinical Trials Register, WHO ICTRP, and ChiCTR. As of October 24, 2025, we employed the following retrieval strategy: (Drug Type is Biological>Cellular>Cell technology type>chimeric antigen receptor) AND [(Disease is Oncology: Esophageal) OR (Disease is Oncology: Gastric) OR (Disease is Oncology: Colorectal) OR (Disease is Oncology: Small Intestine) OR (Disease is Oncology: Anal)]. This search strategy deliberately excluded hepatobiliary and pancreatic malignancies, representing a limitation of this study. However, this exclusion was justified given that hepatobiliary and pancreatic malignancies exhibit distinct tumor microenvironments and therapeutic trajectories warranting independent evaluation. We included all chimeric antigen receptor-modified cellular therapies, encompassing CAR-T, CAR-natural killer (NK), CAR-NKT, CAR-macrophages, and in vivo CAR engineering approaches. Non-CAR cellular immunotherapies (TCR-T, TILs, non-engineered NK) were excluded even when combined with other modalities. Trials lacking clear cell type specification after cross-referencing published literature and conference proceedings were categorized as “not reported” to maintain analytical cohort integrity.

Two independent (WL and JY) investigators systematically screened the retrieved records, manually excluding observational studies and extracting key data elements, including geographical distribution, trial status and phase, patient segment, sponsorship patterns, types of GIM, target antigens, cell types and sources, administration routes, combination strategies, and primary/secondary endpoints. Both reviewers independently performed data abstraction and cross-validation with unstructured information verified manually across multiple sources, including published literature and conference proceedings, to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the extracted trends and conclusions. Furthermore, through manual cross-verification of project identification numbers, titles, principal investigators, and sponsorship information, we excluded all potentially duplicated clinical trial entries from the registry. For clinical trials that remained incomplete with last modification dates exceeding 2 years, we traced their current status based on disclosed information and manually updated the relevant data accordingly. Subsequently, a third independent investigator (QK) verified the extracted data to ensure its accuracy and completeness. Residual limitations following rigorous quality control procedures did not substantially affect the overarching patterns or interpretations derived from this comprehensive analytical framework. Consistent with landscape analysis methodology, we did not perform formal risk-of-bias assessments, as our objective was descriptive ecosystem characterization rather than meta-analytic synthesis. Registry entries exhibit considerable variability in both completeness and update frequency, and categories designated as “not reported” reflect inherent limitations in data availability rather than methodological deficiencies. For trials investigating multiple targets or indications, we employed inclusive counting wherein each element was tabulated separately; consequently, summed frequencies exceed unique trial numbers. Statistical data derived from inclusive counting were specifically highlighted, whereas unique-trial counting was employed for all other instances. We ensure that our study is compliant with TITAN Guidelines 2025.3

After screening 180 eligible trials, we excluded one out-of-scope record (NCT05268172), yielding a final cohort of 179 studies. Temporal profiling demonstrated a generally upward trajectory in trial number (figure 1A). With respect to the trial stage, phase I studies predominated (70.39%), followed by phase I/II trials (20.67%). In terms of trial status, most trials remained open, with completed studies constituting the next largest category (figure 1B). Geographically, the vast majority of trials were initiated in China (70.77%) and the USA (16.41%), whereas participation from other countries was comparatively modest (figure 1C). Regarding patient stratification, most studies enrolled patients with stage III-IV disease (74.30%), and the second-line setting was the most frequently investigated treatment line (figure 1D). Considering sponsorship, academic institutions supported 54.92% of the trials, whereas industry-funded institutions supported 43.01% (figure 1F).

Tumor antigen target analyses indicated that Claudin 18.2 (19.08%), NKG2D(L) (12.72%), mesothelin (12.14%), CEA (11.56%), GUCY2C/GCC (8.09%), HER2 (8.09%), and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-(L)1) (8.09%) were the most frequently targeted molecules (figure 1E; online supplemental table 1). These targets demonstrate heterogeneous tumor-type specificity and safety considerations. Claudin 18.2 demonstrates preferential expression in gastric and gastroesophageal junction tumors with limited distribution in normal tissues, thereby mitigating off-target toxicity. While CEA and mesothelin exhibit broad expression across GIM, their wider distribution in normal tissues necessitates enhanced safety monitoring. NKG2DL, as stress-induced antigens, offers broad applicability across multiple tumor types. In CAR-based cell therapy, 87.85% of trials employed T cells as effectors, and 72.38% were autologous; peripheral intravenous infusion was the most common delivery route (44.69%), and nearly one-tenth of the studies explored locoregional administration, such as intra-arterial infusion (figure 1G). For cell sourcing, 56.67% of the trials collected effector cells from peripheral blood, with only a small minority employing unconventional sources (online supplemental figure). We systematically categorized combination strategies into: lymphodepleting chemotherapy (cyclophosphamide/fludarabine), cytotoxic chemotherapy (paclitaxel, FOLFOX), checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy (anti-programmed cell death protein-1/PD-L1, anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4), targeted therapies (anti-HER2, tyrosine kinase inhibitors), CAR cell auxiliary agents (interleukin (IL)-2/aldesleukin, IL-15), and supportive treatment (tocilizumab). Regarding combination regimens, cyclophosphamide, fludarabine, tocilizumab, paclitaxel, and anti-PD-(L)1 monoclonal antibodies were most frequently incorporated as adjunctive therapies (figure 1H; online supplemental figure 1 and online supplemental table 2). Notably, classical lymphodepletion predominates, whereas rational checkpoint inhibitor combinations designed to counteract T-cell exhaustion remain substantially under-represented despite compelling preclinical rationale, representing an important future trial design opportunity. With respect to endpoint selection, primary endpoints prioritized safety and tolerability assessments, whereas secondary endpoints emphasized antitumor activity metrics including overall survival, progression-free survival, and overall response rate (online supplemental table 3).

The preponderance of early phase trials underscores the exploratory nature of CAR-based cell therapy in GIM, with safety and feasibility assessments constituting fundamental priorities. 43 completed studies have begun to elucidate the therapeutic potential and safety profile of CAR-based cell therapy for GIM. Claudin 18.2-targeted CAR-based cell therapy has demonstrated encouraging preliminary activity, with several trials reporting higher objective response rates in heavily pretreated populations, accompanied by manageable toxicity profiles (NCT03874897, NCT03159819). Similarly, CEA-directed constructs have exhibited promising clinical activity and favorable safety profiles in GIM, although response durability remains suboptimal, necessitating further optimization of the therapeutic strategies. (NCT02416466, NCT05240950). Emerging evidence suggests that locoregional administration strategies may enhance intratumoral CAR cell infiltration while mitigating systemic toxicity (NCT04652219 and NCT02541370). Notably, therapeutic regimens that integrate CAR-based cell therapy with other antitumor agents or strategies have demonstrated potential for synergistic effects, and multiple trials have reported that such combination approaches can enhance the persistence of CAR cells and improve survival outcomes. (NCT04581473, NCT04511871). Comprehensive evidence supports multimodal therapeutic integration, demonstrating that chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and tumor microenvironment modulation substantially augment CAR cell efficacy in solid tumors through enhanced antigen presentation, immunosuppression reversal, and improved trafficking.4

The convergence of cutting-edge preclinical investigations with contemporary regulatory frameworks and interdisciplinary technological integration portends transformative advances in CAR-based cell therapies for GIM. Key biological barriers in solid tumors, including limited CAR-T cell infiltration, poor persistence, and tumor microenvironment-mediated resistance, represent critical challenges that require innovative combination approaches to enhance CAR-T antitumor activity.4 Recent preclinical developments have prioritized overcoming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through armored CAR cell constructs engineered to secrete immunomodulatory cytokines, checkpoint inhibitors, or matrix-degrading enzymes, thereby enhancing T-cell infiltration and persistence within the hostile solid tumor milieu.5 Furthermore, synthetic biology approaches have enabled the development of next-generation CAR cell architectures incorporating logic-gated circuits, switchable receptors, and orthogonal signaling domains that provide unprecedented spatiotemporal control over therapeutic activity while mitigating on-target off-tumor toxicities.6 7 Nanotechnology-enabled delivery systems, including lipid nanoparticles and synthetic viral vectors, are facilitating in vivo CAR gene delivery, potentially obviating ex vivo manufacturing complexities and dramatically reducing production costs and timelines.8 The integration of artificial intelligence-driven antigen discovery platforms has accelerated the identification of novel tumor-restricted surface markers, thereby expanding the targetable antigen repertoire. Recent policy initiatives, including the US Food and Drug Administration RMAT (Regenerative Medicine Advanced Therapy) and PRIority MEdicines, coupled with China’s progressive regulatory reforms facilitating expedited clinical development of innovative biologics, have collectively catalyzed the unprecedented expansion of CAR-based cell therapy trials observed in our analysis, particularly within the Chinese healthcare ecosystem.9 It is important to note that the aforementioned discussion pertains exclusively to GIM, with hepatobiliary and pancreatic cancers deliberately excluded due to their fundamentally distinct tumor microenvironments and mechanisms of tumorigenesis and progression. Consequently, the conclusions derived from this study must be applied with caution when extrapolating to the broader spectrum of digestive system malignancies.

This landscape analysis revealed the burgeoning clinical development of CAR-based cell therapies for GIM. While preliminary outcomes demonstrate encouraging antitumor activity with manageable safety profiles, early phase predominance underscores nascent therapeutic maturity. The synergistic integration of emerging technologies with supportive regulatory frameworks augurs a transformative trajectory toward establishing CAR-based cell therapy as a cornerstone modality in the therapeutic armamentarium against GIM.
