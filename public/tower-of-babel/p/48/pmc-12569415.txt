Central Fever–Associated Thrombocytopenia Following Pontine Hemorrhage: Resolution With Targeted Temperature Management
Authors: Sang Jin Park, Kyu Sun Yum
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Pontine hemorrhage is a rare but life-threatening neurological emergency. It typically results from hypertensive vascular injury, vascular malformations, or anticoagulant use, and is characterized by abrupt onset of coma, brainstem dysfunction, and autonomic instability. The prognosis is poor, with mortality rates reaching 50% within the first 24 hours and up to 90% within three weeks of onset.1

Among the clinical factors associated with poor outcomes, hyperthermia is a particularly ominous sign, often arising from disruption of thermoregulatory centers within the pons.2 Central hyperthermia is frequently unresponsive to conventional antipyretic therapy and may reflect direct injury to descending inhibitory pathways or compression of hypothalamic inputs.3 Various strategies—including surface cooling, intravascular temperature modulation, and pharmacologic agents such as dantrolene and bromocriptine—have been attempted.4

In parallel, hyperthermia has been implicated in hematologic dysfunction, particularly thrombocytopenia. Elevated core temperatures may induce platelet apoptosis and accelerate peripheral clearance.5 Although not traditionally emphasized in the context of hemorrhagic stroke, such heat-induced thrombocytopenia could exacerbate bleeding risk and impede neurological recovery.

While current guidelines recommend maintaining platelet counts above 100,000/µL in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage, recent evidence indicates that significant platelet decline occurs within the first few days, correlating with larger hematoma volumes and worse clinical outcomes.678 This underscores the importance of recognizing and managing contributing factors such as sustained hyperthermia.

Targeted temperature management (TTM) has been widely adopted as a neuroprotective strategy in patients with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy and severe brain injury.9

We present a case of pontine hemorrhage complicated by central hyperthermia and secondary thrombocytopenia, in which TTM was temporally associated with both thermal and hematologic stabilization.

A 63-year-old male with a history of hypertension presented to the emergency department approximately 90 minutes after a sudden loss of consciousness. He exhibited generalized seizures and vomiting, decreased level of consciousness (GCS E1V1M4). His body temperature was markedly elevated (39.0℃), and blood pressure was also significantly elevated at 223/107 mm Hg. Brain computed tomography (CT) revealed a pontine hemorrhage measuring 40×20 mm, without evidence of intraventricular hemorrhage. The patient’s body temperature rose despite antipyretic therapy, reaching a peak of 42.1℃. Laboratory tests were unremarkable, including PT (INR) 0.94 (≤1.2), aPTT 30.2 s (25.6–34.4 s), D-dimer 0.6 µg/mL (0–0.5 µg/mL), CRP 0.08 mg/dL (≤0.3 mg/dL), procalcitonin <0.05 ng/mL (<0.5 ng/mL).

TTM was initiated with a target temperature of 36.5℃. Normothermia was achieved within eight hours after admission. TTM was maintained for nine days with sustained normothermia. On admission, the patient’s platelet count was 217,000/µL, but this declined sharply to 37,000/µL within 24 hours. The platelet count increased to over 50,000/µL by day 3 and normalized (>150,000/µL) by day 5. After tracheostomy, spontaneous respiration was maintained without ventilatory support (Fig. 1).

In this case, severe central hyperthermia and acute thrombocytopenia developed shortly after the onset of pontine hemorrhage. Despite the administration of antipyretics and continuous platelet transfusions, both conditions remained refractory until the initiation of TTM, which resulted in rapid normalization of body temperature, hematologic recovery, and overall clinical stabilization.

Sustained hyperthermia has been shown to worsen secondary brain injury by increasing cerebral metabolic demand, exacerbating excitotoxicity, and compromising the integrity of the blood–brain barrier. Furthermore, elevated core temperature is associated with accelerated platelet apoptosis and dysfunction, a phenomenon documented in clinical contexts such as heat stroke and therapeutic whole-body hyperthermia. Proposed mechanisms include mitochondrial damage, membrane destabilization, and enhanced phagocytic clearance of platelets.5 In our patient, the rapid decline in platelet count—from 217,000 to 37,000/µL within 24 hours—was likely mediated by these temperature-induced effects, compounding the risk of hemorrhagic progression.

TTM has been well established as a neuroprotective intervention in patients with cardiac arrest and ischemic stroke. Its application in hemorrhagic stroke, however, remains relatively limited. Recent studies suggest that TTM reduces brain edema and protects the blood–brain barrier in intracerebral hemorrhage, and may also suppress neuroinflammation and reduce cell death.910 However, data on its hematologic benefits—particularly in the context of hyperthermia-induced thrombocytopenia—remain scarce.

Medications like dantrolene or bromocriptine for central fever have variable efficacy, delayed onset, and potential for serious side effects. In contrast, TTM enables rapid temperature control. To our knowledge, this is one of the first reported cases demonstrating the successful use of TTM to address both central hyperthermia and secondary thrombocytopenia in pontine hemorrhage. These findings suggest that TTM may have broader therapeutic potential beyond neuroprotection, potentially modulating systemic inflammatory and hematologic responses in acute brain injury.
