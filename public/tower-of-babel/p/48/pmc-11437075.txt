Microbial communities and metagenomes in methane-rich deep coastal sediments
Authors: Binghe Zhao, Mucong Zi, Xiaoyu Zhang, Yong Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoastal sediments are rich in embedded recalcitrant organic carbons that are biotransformed into methane. In this study, gas composition (carbon dioxide, methane and nitrogen) and chemical indicators (total nitrogen, total carbon, and total sulfate) were examined in five deep sediment cores (up to 130 m in length) obtained from the Hangzhou Bay. The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene amplicons was amplified and sequenced for the prokaryotic community analysis. The species composition, along with the physicochemical factors of the sediments, revealed a strong correlation with methane content in one of the sediment cores. We then obtained metagenomes of the two sediment samples selected for their high methane content and enrichment of methanogenic Bathyarchaeota with phylogenetic evidence. A total of 27 draft genomes were retrieved through metagenomic binning methodologies and were classified into Bathyarchaeota, Asgard archaea, Planctomycetes, and other microbial groups. The data provided are valuable for understanding the relationship between methane generation and microbial community composition in deep sediment core samples from coastal to marine environments.Subject terms: Soil microbiology, Carbon cycle

Background & Summary

Coastal zones have now become the most densely populated areas globally, particularly the zones in swiftly depositing nearshore regions, especially in estuarine delta areas. The increased population density in these coastal zones may lead to higher organic matter emissions from human activities. Here, abundant organic matter is often buried in the strata with the sediment, eventually forming natural gas (methane) through a series of long-term biochemical reactions1. Hangzhou Bay, situated in the east coastal plain of Zhejiang province, is the estuary of the Qiantang River. The area features organic matter-rich marine deposits from the middle Holocene epoch, suggesting the potential presence of natural gas in the strata2. Previous studies in Hangzhou Bay have shown that the presence of shallow gas correlates strongly with the distribution of archaea, particularly Bathyarchaeota, Thaumarchaeota, and Euryarchaeota, as well as methane-metabolizing microbes like Methanosarcinaceae and ANME-1a3. These findings highlight the importance of sediment type and environmental substrates, such as ammonium, in shaping microbial communities and gas distribution. Moreover, recent genomics and transcriptomics studies of microbial methanogens in mangrove sediments have revealed the vital roles of novel methanogens such as Methanofastidiosa and Methanomassiliicoccales in methane production, indicating that diverse methanogens contribute to the global methane budget and carbon cycling4.

The formation of methane in buried sediment involves a variety of factors. However, current research provides no clear consensus on the source of shallow gas in nearshore regions, with studies on biogenic methane in such geological environments being particularly sparse5. To assess the relationship between methane generation in core sediment and associated microbes, we drilled five sediment cores from different locations in the Hangzhou Bay (Fig. 1a,b). We collected five sediment cores, the longest being over 130 meters and the shortest 44 meters. Samples were taken at intervals of several meters from different depth layers of each core, resulting in a total of 60 samples. Specific depth information for each sediment core sample is presented in Table 1. Different depth layers of the sediment cores were sampled, and the microbial community structure of each layer was determined using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. Simultaneously, the methane, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen content, along with the chemical characteristics (total organic carbon, total nitrogen, total sulfur and sulfate) of each sediment core sample were examined.

The study utilized Illumina Miseq high-throughput sequencing technology to sequence the microbial 16S rRNA gene amplicons of 60 samples from the sediment cores. After quality control and chimera removal, a total of 2,946,414 qualified sequences were obtained. The qualified sequences were compared with the SILVA database to obtain the microbial community composition at different classification levels in the sediment samples. A total of 5,675 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) (one OTU at 97% similarity is equivalent to one species) were obtained, of which only 467 were annotated at the genus level. The relatively low annotation rate (8.23%) may be due to the fact that the samples were deep sediment mud layers, and many prokaryote have not yet been reported previously. At the class level (Fig. 2), Gammaproteobacteria dominated most of the samples in terms of relative abundance, followed by Dehalococcoidia and Bathyarchaeia. Remarkably, in the sediment samples S-2-10 and S-2-5, the most abundant microbes were Crenarchaeota, which includes a large proportion of Bathyarchaeota, potentially methane-producing archaea6. A phylogenetic tree shows that Bathyarchaeota OTU7, OTU13, OTU19, OTU27, and OTU48 from the S-2 core are closely related to known methanogenic lineages BA1 and BA27, which aligns with the high methane content in this sediment environment (Fig. S1). In the S-2-3, S-2-4, and S-2-6 samples, organisms of the highest relative abundance belong to Chloroflexi, whose major ecological function is the degradation of complex organic substances in sediments8. The highest ACE index is shown in sediment cores S-2 and S-5, followed by S-4, and the lowest in S-3 and S-1 (Fig. 1c). Through genus-level taxonomic classification and sample clustering heatmap analysis (Fig. 3), Proteobacteria was found to be the most widely distributed and abundant, with Acinetobacter, Caldimona, and Erythrobacter displaying the highest abundance, followed by Alihewanella and Phingomona. Yet, in sample S-4-3, M23-31 of Calitrichaeota phylum has the highest abundance. The diversity levels in the sample group from site S-2 showed remarkable variation compared to other sites, consistent with what is indicated by the ACE diversity index. Additionally, the canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) was conducted to explore the relationships between prokaryotic community structure and environmental factors, including total nitrogen (TN), total sulfur (TS), total organic carbon (TOC), sulfate (SO4²−), methane (CH4), nitrogen (N2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) (Fig. 4a–c). The two CCA dimensions explained 4.67% of the total variance, with methane and total organic carbon showing a more important effect on the community structure of these samples. This analysis indicated that the prokaryotic community structures in the S-2 core were more closely associated with variations in methane content. Aside from methane and TOC, other environmental factors such as total nitrogen and TS also contributed to variations in community structure, albeit to a lesser extent (Fig. 4d).

In total, we identified several sediment layers of higher methane content. From these layers, two samples with relatively high contents of methane and Bathyarchaeota were selected for metagenomics studies. Through metagenomic sequencing of the two samples S-2-5 and S-2-10, we acquired 10 Gbp of metagenomics data for each sample (S-218 and S-237, respectively). Using the binning approach, a total of 27 draft genomes were generated (Table S1). Classification of these genomes by the GTDB database revealed the presence of Bathyarchaeota (n = 7), Asgard archaea (n = 2), and Planctomycetes (n = 2), which aligns with the finding based on the 16S rRNA gene amplicon data. Four out of the seven Bathyarchaeota MAGs were classified as the BA1 of Bathyarchaeota by GTDB (Table S1).

To the best of our knowledge, this research marks the first to consider the connection between methane production and prokaryotes in estuarine deep sediment layers. The experimental design, sequencing data and environmental factors provide the valuable dataset for investigating microbial communities and their ecological contribution in sediment layers of nearshore areas. This dataset will ultimately provoke a wide interest on potential linkage between methanogenic microbes and gas hydrates.

Methods

Sampling processing

The five sediment cores (S-1, S-2, S-3, S-4, and S-5) were obtained from the sites near the Hangzhou Bay Bridge of Zhejiang Province, China, (121°08′E, 30°27′N) by drilling in December of 2019. The specific sediment layer information for investigation of microbial community structure is shown in Table 1. The sediment and gas samples were collected on-site after retrieving the cores. The sediment samples were frozen at −20 °C, the gas samples were kept by dry ice, and those for microbial studies were stored in liquid nitrogen tanks. The samples were delivered to the laboratory for analysis on the same day of collection.

DNA extraction and 16S V3-V4 region amplicon sequencing

DNA was extracted from 0.25 g of sediment from different layers using the PowerSoil® DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio, Cat. No.12888) as per manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration and purity were checked by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, followed by Nanodrop for DNA concentration and purity measurement. Specific primers (V3-V4: 341 F: CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG 805 R: GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC)9 equipped with barcodes were utilized to amplify amplicons from the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA genes via PCR. The Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs) was employed for all PCR reactions. The PCR products were validated through 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The amplicons underwent purification using Beckman DNA Clean Beads and were quantified with a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), with an average Qubit value of 2.13 ng/µL. Post amplification, the PCR products were combined in equal density ratios. The mixed PCR products were subsequently purified utilizing the Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) and indexed accordingly. The quality of the library was evaluated by a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system. Based on quality assessment, the final libraries were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform, which generated 2 × 300 bp paired-end reads.

Metagenomic sequencing

The DNA for metagenomic sequencing was extracted from about 5 g of the sediment samples S-2-5 and S-2-10. The qualified DNA samples were randomly fragmented into about 300 bp pieces by Covaris ultrasonic disruptor (Covaris, USA), and then subjected to end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, purification, PCR amplification, and other steps to complete the library preparation. After the library construction, the libraries were first quantified by Qubit 2.0, diluted to 2 ng/μl, and then the insert size was checked by the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies Inc, USA). After confirming that the insert size met the expectations, the effective concentration of the libraries was accurately quantified by the q-PCR method (library effective concentration > 3 nM), to ensure the library quality. After quality control, the different libraries were pooled according to the effective concentration and the target data volume and sequenced on the Illumina Hiseq X-Ten system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), producing paired-end reads of 2 × 150 bp. This resulted in a total of 37,074,615 reads for sample S-2-5 and 45,748,113 reads for sample S-2-10.

Bioinformatic processing of 16S rRNA gene amplicons and metagenomes

The sequencing output, derived from the Illumina platform, was subjected to initial processing to segregate individual sample data based on barcode and PCR amplification primer sequences. Subsequent to the removal of these sequences, the reads for each sample were assembled using PEAR v1.2.1110, resulting in the generation of raw tags. These raw tags were then subjected to a stringent quality control process, in line with the protocols established by Qiime v1.9.111. This involved two key steps: 1) Tags trimming: The Raw Tags were truncated at the first occurrence of a low-quality base (default quality threshold ≤ 19) if the sequence of consecutive low-quality bases reached a predetermined length (default length is 3); 2) Tags length filtering: the Tags dataset underwent further refinement to exclude Tags where the length of consecutive high-quality bases was less than 75% of the total Tags length. Following these quality control measures, the focus shifted to the elimination of chimeric sequences from the Tags. Utilizing the UCHIME algorithm, the Tags sequences were compared against the Gold database for the identification and subsequent removal of chimeric sequences. This process culminated in the acquisition of effective tags. The effective tags from all samples were clustered using Uparse software v8.1.186112. By default, sequences were clustered into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) at 97% identity. The representative sequences for these OTUs were selected based on the frequency of occurrence within the OTUs, adhering to the algorithmic principles of Uparse, which prioritizes the most abundant sequence in an OTU as its representative. For taxonomic annotation of the OTU representative sequences, the uclust method was employed in conjunction with the Silva database (http://www.arb-silva.de). This analysis facilitated species annotation across various taxonomic levels: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. Consequently, the community composition of each sample was statistically evaluated at these levels. Further, the PyNAST software v1.213 was utilized in conjunction with the “Core Set” data from the Silva database to perform rapid multiple sequence alignment, elucidating the phylogenetic relationships of all OTU representative sequences. Finally, the data from each sample were normalized. This involved standardizing the data volumes across samples, using the sample with the least amount of data as a baseline. Subsequent analyses, including Alpha and Beta diversity analyses, were based on this normalized dataset. All samples from each site and layer were analyzed, and data visualization was performed using R software v4.3.114. Bar and line charts were generated using ggplot2 v3.4.415.

For the metagenomic data, adapter sequences were removed, and the low-quality reads (length less than 15 bp, average quality score less than 15, or containing more than five N bases) were filtered using fastp v0.22.016 with default parameters. Then, all quality-controlled reads were co-assembled with SPAdes v3.15.317 with k-mer values of 21, 33, 55, 77, 99, and 127. The genome bins were recovered using the MetaWRAP v1.3.218 pipeline, which includes MaxBin 2.02219, metaBAT 2.02320, and CONCOCT v1.0.02421 metagenomic binning software. Subsequently, the bin_refinement module (parameters: -c 50, -x 10) was employed to recover consolidated sets of bins. All MAGs were aggregated and dereplicated at 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) using dRep v2.3.222 (parameters: -comp 50 -con 10 -sa 0.97 -nc 0.30 -cm longer). MAGs were classified using the classify_wf workflow of GTDB-Tk v2.1.023 with GTDB release R207_v2 (default parameters). The quality assessment of all bins was performed using CheckM v1.2.224(parameters: lineage_wf).

Phylogenetic analysis

The representative amplicons of Bathyarchaeota OTU7, OTU13, OTU19, OTU27 and OTU48 were used to reconstruct a phylogenetic tree with the 16S rRNA genes of BA1 (accession number: LIHJ01000010) and BA2 (accession number: LIHJ01000010)7 from the NCBI database. The 16S rRNA genes of Methanohalarchaeum thermophilum strain HMET1 (NR_177347.1) and the Bathyarchaeota archaeon (GCA_007050865.1) were collected from NCBI database as an outgroup of the phylogeny. The representative reads from our dataset and reference sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.50525 with the parameters set to -maxiterate 1000 -localpair. The resulting alignments were further processed with trimAl v1.426 using the -automated1-phylip setting. To construct a Maximum-Likelihood phylogenetic tree of the 16S rRNA genes, we employed IQ-TREE 2 v2.2.027, with the settings -m MFP -B 1000 -alrt 1000 -T AUTO.

Core sediment and porewater analysis

Sediment cores were systematically sampled at various layers and placed in PVC tubes. For methane production inhibition in sediments, a 5 mL syringe (decapitated at the zero mark) was used to extract 3 mL sediment, which was then transferred into an anaerobic bottle pre-filled with 6 mL of 1 mol/L NaOH28. The sediment samples in the anaerobic bottles were sealed with butyl rubber stoppers to ensure airtight conditions. For interstitial water analysis, sediment samples from different core layers were promptly packed in plastic sealable bags. Within 12 hours, the samples underwent low-temperature centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 15 minutes. The resultant interstitial water was filtered through a needle-type filter (membrane diameter = 0.45 μm) and stored in separate containers. For methane carbon isotope analysis, sediment samples were placed in 500 mL glass containers. All sediment samples were purged with N2 for 1 minute and then stored below 4 °C in a sealed condition. Methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2) concentrations in the core sediments were determined using the static headspace method, followed by gas chromatography analysis. CH4 concentrations were measured using an Agilent 6850 (Thermo, USA), while CO2 concentrations were determined using a Trace GC ULTRA (Thermo, USA). Nitrogen (N2) gas component and N-isotope analysis were performed using a GasBench II pre-treatment setup, which included a GC PAL autosampler, a PoraPlotQ chromatographic column (30 m × 0.32 mm) from Agilent, and a constant temperature sample tray. The detector used was a Delta V Advantage (Thermo, USA). In conjunction with the gas component analysis in the sediments, TOC, TN, and TS analyses were conducted on selected layers of the sediment cores. Additionally, sulfate (SO42−) concentrations in the pore water were measured. Elemental analysis was performed using a Flash 2000 Elemental Analyzer (Thermo, USA), and sulfate concentrations were determined using Ion Chromatography ICS-1100 (Thermo, USA).

Technical Validation

Quality assessment of the raw 16S rRNA sequencing reads was performed using Qiime v1.9.111. The initial step involved truncating raw Tags at the first low-quality base, defined by a quality score of < = 19 for a sequence of bases with a default minimum length of three. Next, Tags were filtered out if high-quality bases constituted less than 75% of the total Tag length. The resulting Tags underwent chimera removal using the UCHIME algorithm against the Gold database, producing the final set of effective Tags. Stringent quality filtering of metagenomic sequencing reads was conducted using fastp v0.22.016 with default settings. This process included the removal of adapter sequences and the filtering of low-quality reads: specifically, those shorter than 15 bp, with an average quality score below 15, or containing more than five N bases.

The procedure and instrument parameters for determining gas content with gas chromatography and element analysis are as follows:

An Agilent 6850 (Thermo, USA) apparatus equipped with an FID detector (temperature 250 °C) and Porapark Q 6Ft (1.83 M) × 1/8-inch column is used for CH4 concentration detection. Injection port temperature was set at 200 °C, with column oven at 120 °C. Nitrogen acts as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 20 min/L and sample injection volume is 0.3 mL. Standard gas concentrations used are 4.99 ppm, 10.1 ppm, 50.2 ppm, and 104.6 ppm. Standard gas samples at each concentration were measured twice, and the average was taken for standard curve plotting. For the sample, four points were remeasured and the results were averaged, with a relative deviation of 0.10-0.81.
