A case of transient tachy-bradycardia syndrome following combined atrial fibrillation ablation and left atrial appendage closure
Authors: Yijing Tao, Qiyin Zhang, Hongwu Chen, Qianwen Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atrial fibrillation (AF) and atrial flutter (AFL) are common arrhythmias, with incidence increasing with age. Catheter ablation and left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) are guideline-recommended treatments for patient with AF and high thromboembolic risk of stroke. We report the case of a patient with paroxysmal AF/AFL at high thromboembolic risk who developed paroxysmal rapid AF with sinus arrest following combined pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) and LAAC. Notably, both the arrhythmia and sinus node dysfunction gradually resolved over three months without pacemaker implantation. We propose that a watchful waiting strategy may be appropriate in similar clinical scenarios.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and atrial flutter (AFL) are prevalent arrhythmias in the middle-aged and elderly population. For symptomatic patients, catheter ablation has become a well-established therapeutic strategy, with evidence demonstrating its advantages over antiarrhythmic drug therapy in improving quality of life [1]. Although the combined “one-stop” procedure of catheter ablation and left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) has gained widespread adoption, reports of post-procedural arrhythmic complications remain scarce. This case presents a patient who rapidly developed tachy- and bradyarrhythmias following the combined procedure. The close temporal association with the intervention, particularly the acute hemodynamic alterations related to device closure, suggests a pathophysiological interplay among procedural factors, atrial remodeling, and patient-specific substrate. Notably, during follow-up, the arrhythmia burden significantly decreased and atrial function recovered, indicating that such electrophysiological disturbances may be reversible.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 57-year-old male was admitted for elective AF ablation with a history of ischemic stroke six months prior, which presented as acute limb weakness and was followed by full recovery. Diagnostic workup revealed AF and atypical AFL. He was maintained on rivaroxaban but had irregular, self-discontinued use of propafenone. Due to concerns over long-term bleeding risk and a preference to avoid lifelong medication, the patient declined continued oral anticoagulation (OAC). Given his elevated stroke risk (CHADS₂-VASc score = 3), after multidisciplinary evaluation, LAAC was planned as a non-pharmacological alternative for stroke prevention in conjunction with the ablation procedure. The patient was scheduled for a combined, one-stop procedure consisting of pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) followed by LAAC. Despite the presence of sustained arrhythmias accompanied by a rapid ventricular rate, the patient denied any subsequent episodes of palpitations or anginal symptoms following the initial stroke. Routine preoperative laboratory studies, including biochemistry and thyroid function panels, were within normal limits. Preprocedural multimodality cardiac imaging, comprising contrast-enhanced thoracic computed tomography (CT) for left atrial appendage assessment and coronary CT angiography, ruled out intracardiac thrombus and significant obstructive coronary artery disease.

Clinical findings

On July 13, 2024, the patient underwent a one-stop procedure involving PVI and LAAC. Vascular access was obtained via the right femoral vein. After systemic heparinization and two transseptal punctures, electroanatomical mapping of the left atrium was performed. Circumferential PVI was then achieved using a contact force-sensing radiofrequency ablation catheter (ThermoCool SmartTouch® Carto, Johnson & Johnson). Ablation was delivered with a power of 40–45 W, temperature limit of 43 °C, and saline irrigation at 15 mL/min, with a target contact force of 5–20 g. This power range aligns with contemporary high-power short-duration strategies aimed at achieving efficient and transmural lesions [2]. The Ablation Index was used as a lesion quality marker, with targets of 400–550 for anterior/roof segments and 350–380 for posterior segments (Fig. 1A and B). The ablation endpoint was durable pulmonary vein isolation, confirmed by demonstrating bidirectional block. Entry block was defined by loss of pulmonary vein potentials on a Pentaray catheter. Exit block was confirmed by pacing within PVs, as evidenced by failure to conduction to the atrium. A 20-minute waiting period ensured no reconnection, and no pharmacological provocation was utilized.

Subsequently, a WATCHMAN 2.5 device (Boston Scientific) was implanted in the LAA under multiplanar angiographic guidance. The device size selected was 33 mm. Pre-deployment left atrial pressure measured 32/19 mmHg. Angiography revealed a cauliflower-shaped appendage with matching ostial diameter and depth (27 mm), confirming the size selection. The device was deployed according to standard PASS criteria. Post-deployment pressure was 35/21 mmHg, with no significant pericardial effusion observed (Fig. 1C and D).

The patient was placed on continuous telemetry post-procedure. Laboratory findings on postoperative day two were unremarkable; however, a mild isolated elevation in high-sensitivity troponin I was noted (0.35 µg/L), with otherwise normal cardiac biomarkers.

Postprocedural oral amiodarone (0.2 g every 8 h) was initiated for rhythm control. Approximately 38 h after the procedure, the patient experienced palpitations, and telemetry confirmed rapid AF. Amiodarone (0.3 g in 50 ml of 0.9% sodium chloride) was immediately administered via intravenous pump infusion at a rate of 10 ml/h. In addition, intravenous potassium supplementation was given to maintain electrolyte balance. Approximately 80 min after the onset of this rapid AF, the patient experienced a near-syncopal episode (transient blackout without loss of consciousness). Telemetry captured a spontaneous cessation of the ongoing rapid AF, followed by a prolonged RR interval consistent with a conversion pause (Fig. 2). This event prompted the immediate cessation of amiodarone therapy. On the day following this event, a 24-hour Holter monitor was performed, which recorded sinus rhythm with paroxysmal AF and brief atrial tachycardia. Notably, 349 prolonged pauses exceeding 2 s were documented, with the longest measuring 5.78 s.

Follow-up and outcomes

No temporary or permanent pacemaker was implanted. A 24-hour patch-based Holter monitor on July 22, 2024, revealed sinus bradycardia (mean heart rate 59 bpm) with occasional brief atrial arrhythmias. The patient was discharged on propafenone for arrhythmia control. At the three-month follow-up (October 17, 2024), the patient reported no recurrent syncope or loss of consciousness. Repeat Holter monitoring showed no AF/AFL and no prolonged RR intervals (Fig. 3). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated positive cardiac remodeling, evidenced by reduced left atrial diameter (32 mm) and normalized diastolic function. A subsequent electrocardiogram (ECG) obtained over one year later, in September 2025, was within normal limits.

Discussion

AF and AFL are highly prevalent sustained arrhythmias that significantly increase the risk of stroke and heart failure [3]. Catheter ablation, particularly circumferential PVI, is a well-established rhythm-control strategy for symptomatic AF [4, 5], while LAAC offers an effective stroke prevention alternative for patients unsuitable for long-term anticoagulation [6].

Based on recent pooled analyses, overall and major complication rates for AF catheter ablation are 4.51% and 2.44%, respectively [7]. Although complication rates have declined in recent years, procedure-related mortality remains low. Rates do not significantly differ by AF type, ablation modality, or adjunctive strategies beyond PVI. For concomitant LAAC, pericardial effusion/tamponade is most frequent, other risks include device embolization, device-related thrombosis, and peri-procedural stroke.

Recurrence of paroxysmal AF or AFL within 3 months following a successful procedure, accompanied by sinus arrest, bradycardia, or prolonged sinus pause upon cardioversion, is infrequently reported. In contrast, such events observed after radiofrequency catheter ablation for AF often necessitate temporary or even permanent pacemaker implantation [8]. While previous reports have attributed such sinus arrest or prolonged pauses to inadvertent injury to the sinus node or its arterial supply, or to reflex vagal responses [9, 10], in the present case, superior vena cava isolation was not performed, thus ruling out sinus node injury from this specific maneuver as a causative mechanism. Notably, the arrhythmias in this case emerged only after exclusion of common alternative etiologies such as significant coronary artery disease and electrolyte imbalances. Clinical evidence now suggests that such complications can be reversible, as demonstrated by the gradual resolution of sinus pauses and reduced arrhythmia burden over a three-month period without any device-based intervention. This recovery may be linked to dynamic atrial remodeling and pressure changes following LAAC. The observed peri-procedural electrical instability, encompassing both tachyarrhythmias and bradyarrhythmias, may reflect an underlying atrial cardiomyopathy (ACM). This condition provides a pathophysiological substrate of structural and electrical remodeling which predisposes the atria to such dynamic perturbations and subsequent stabilization [11].

This case highlights key peri-operative considerations for combined radiofrequency ablation and LAAC, particularly the judicious use of antiarrhythmic drugs like amiodarone. While effective against atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, amiodarone may exacerbate bradycardia or conduction abnormalities, especially in patients with pre-existing sinus node dysfunction or structural heart disease, necessitating careful dosing and monitoring to balance efficacy and proarrhythmic risk [12, 13]. This is particularly relevant in elderly patients with atrial enlargement or prior stroke, who show heightened susceptibility to conduction impairment. The occurrence of prolonged RR intervals or sinus pauses post-procedurally should not be automatically attributed to direct injury; instead, acute hemodynamic changes, pharmacological suppression, and the potential unmasking of a vulnerable atrial substrate, such as that seen in the setting of ACM, must be evaluated. Decisions regarding permanent pacing should be individualized, as the observed reversibility of sinus pauses in this case suggests that not all require immediate device implantation—a nuance underemphasized in current guidelines [14, 15].

Mechanistically, the observed peri-procedural electrical instability likely arises from the convergence of distinct yet synergistic pathways affecting atrial electrophysiology and autonomic balance. Firstly, antral pulmonary vein isolation inherently involves modulation of the adjacent ganglionated plexi, which can lead to partial denervation or altered parasympathetic input, potentially contributing to transient sinus node dysfunction or bradyarrhythmic events. This autonomic modulation constitutes a well-documented effect of thermal ablation. Systematic evidence confirms that conventional thermal ablation, such as the radiofrequency energy employed in this case, induces a significantly larger post-procedural increase in both heart rate and serum S100B, a biomarker of neural injury, compared to pulsed field ablation. This finding underscores the substantial impact of thermal ablation on the autonomic nervous system [16]. The observed increase in heart rate is interpreted as reflecting parasympathetic withdrawal secondary to collateral damage of the peripulmonary ganglionated plexi [16]. Secondly, and specific to the combined procedure, the mechanical manipulation and device deployment at the LAA ridge provide an acute stimulus to local stretch receptors, which may trigger reflex autonomic responses and further perturb the neural milieu. While ablation primarily modifies intrinsic neural circuits, device implantation introduces an extrinsic mechanical and hemodynamic perturbation. The acute alteration in atrial compliance and pressure dynamics induced by LAAC exacerbates this atrial stretch. Furthermore, the quality of the ablation lesions themselves is critical. Evidence from surgical ablation indicates that failure to achieve complete, transmural bidirectional block across ablation lines is associated with an increased risk of post-procedural AF recurrence [17]. These combined procedural effects, encompassing autonomic modulation from ablation, acute mechano-electrical disturbance from closure, and chronic hemodynamic change, can collectively promote both triggered activity and bradyarrhythmia. These acute changes may interact with a pre-existing ACM background, where factors like fibrosis and neurohormonal dysregulation influence atrial stability [11]. The cessation of symptomatic arrhythmias and pauses during the observation period is consistent with transient hemodynamic and neurohumoral adaptation rather than permanent nodal injury. However, the possibility of underlying sinus node dysfunction with long-term implications cannot be excluded.

Study limitations

This case report has a limited follow-up period, which constrains the generalizability of the findings and the ability to infer causality. Furthermore, rhythm assessment relied on 24-hour Holter monitoring, which may be insufficient to capture intermittent tachy-brady episodes. The use of longer-term monitoring strategies (e.g., extended patch monitors or implantable loop recorders) could have provided more comprehensive rhythm surveillance. Although a temporal association was observed between the combined procedure and the onset of severe arrhythmias, a causal link cannot be established. Direct hemodynamic monitoring (e.g., real-time left atrial pressure) was not performed, and detailed autonomic assessments were lacking. These gaps hinder the mechanistic interpretation of the observed sinus node dysfunction in relation to potential LAAC–induced pressure changes. Other potential confounding factors, such as inflammatory responses and subtle structural remodeling, were not systematically evaluated. Future studies with larger cohorts, systematic hemodynamic and imaging assessments, prolonged arrhythmia monitoring, and longer follow-up are warranted to elucidate the incidence, mechanisms, and natural history of complex arrhythmias following combined ablation and LAAC.

Conclusion

This case highlights that acute hemodynamic changes after “one-stop” procedure can trigger electrical instability in patients with borderline sinus node function, potentially presenting as tachy-brady syndrome. In such scenarios, immediate discontinuation of antiarrhythmic drugs and intensive monitoring are advised, while deferring pacemaker implantation, as these disturbances are often transient. These observations underscore the importance of enhanced perioperative monitoring—including continuous telemetry and early ambulatory ECG—along with systematic long-term follow-up in high-risk patients undergoing combined AF ablation and LAAC, enabling timely detection and management of potentially reversible post-procedural arrhythmias.
