Mitophagy in bladder cancer: a double-edged sword in tumor progression and therapy
Authors: Qi Chen, Tao Chen, Lifeng Gan, Yiran Lu, Biao Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBladder cancer (BC) is the second most prevalent malignancy of the urinary system, characterized by high recurrence rates and aggressive behavior. Although treatment modalities have advanced, patient prognosis remains poor, largely due to late-stage diagnosis, postoperative recurrence, and the development of therapy resistance. Mitophagy, a selective form of autophagy responsible for degrading dysfunctional mitochondria, is a critical mechanism for maintaining cellular homeostasis. Dysregulation of mitophagy leads to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria and is implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases, including cancer.Main bodyThis review synthesizes current understanding of the molecular mechanisms by which mitophagy regulates the initiation and progression of BC. Concurrently, we critically evaluate its context-dependent functions in disease biology and therapeutic response. The role of mitophagy in BC is dual and highly context-dependent. It can function as either a tumor promoter or a tumor suppressor, with its net effect determined by multiple factors, including tumor stage, genetic background, tumor microenvironment composition, and the extent of autophagic activation. While targeting mitophagy represents a promising therapeutic strategy, its functional duality necessitates approaches that extend beyond simple inhibition or activation. Future therapeutic development must therefore focus on precise, individualized modulation tailored to specific tumor contexts.ConclusionMitophagy plays a multidimensional and pivotal role in BC pathogenesis and treatment response. A deeper understanding of its nuanced mechanisms not only advances the fundamental knowledge of BC pathology but also unveils innovative avenues for diagnosis and targeted therapy, offering a promising strategy to overcome current clinical challenges.Keywords: Mitophagy, Bladder cancer, Duality, Targeted therapy, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) ranks as the tenth most common cancer globally and the second most prevalent malignancy of the urinary system, with approximately 549,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths occurring annually [1, 2]. It is further classified into two clinical stages based on tumor infiltration depth: non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) [3]. NMIBC accounts for approximately 75% of all BC cases and is typically treated via transurethral resection of the bladder tumor followed by adjuvant intravesical chemotherapy. The remaining 25–30% of BC patients are diagnosed with MIBC, necessitating more aggressive therapeutic approaches. These include systemic neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with targeted immunotherapy, followed by radical cystectomy [4]. Despite significant advances in the diagnosis and treatment of BC in recent years, considerable challenges remain. NMIBC exhibits high recurrence and progression rates, whilst MIBC demonstrates rapid local and systemic progression characteristics. Chemotherapy and immunotherapy, as adjuvant treatments for advanced BC, have substantially improved its prognosis. However, after several treatment cycles, some patients inevitably develop resistance to chemotherapeutic agents and immunotherapy, constituting a primary cause of mortality and one of the greatest therapeutic challenges in BC. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies to address BC. Deepening our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying malignant progression and drug resistance is crucial for enhancing treatment sensitivity and preventing disease progression in BC.

Mitochondria are indispensable bioenergetic and biosynthetic hubs, crucial for normal cellular function and human health. A growing body of research indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction is pivotal in cancer initiation and progression [5, 6]. Mitophagy, a selective form of macroautophagy, maintains mitochondrial quality control by eliminating dysfunctional or superfluous mitochondria via the autophagosome-lysosome pathway. This process is governed by intricate signaling pathways and molecular networks. However, dysregulation of mitophagy disrupts mitochondrial turnover, leading to either excessive degradation or the accumulation of damaged organelles. Mounting evidence now associates such dysregulation with multiple diseases, including BC. The function of mitophagy in carcinogenesis is complex and context-dependent, exhibiting both tumor-suppressive and tumor-promoting activities. Its precise, dual role in BC remains to be fully elucidated, a knowledge gap that motivates this investigation into the intrinsic link between the two. This review aims to systematically elucidate the dichotomous functions of mitophagy in the initiation, progression, and therapeutic response of BC. We will delve into its core molecular mechanisms and critically assess the potential and challenges of targeting mitophagy as a novel therapeutic strategy. This endeavor seeks to provide a theoretical foundation and future research directions for understanding the pathophysiology of BC and developing innovative treatments (Table 1).

Regulation of mitophagy in BC: a summary of key pathways, functions, and therapeutic implications

Evidence acquisition and synthesis

We searched the PubMed database for the most recent and relevant publications, covering the period from January 2016 to January 2026. The search strategy employed the following combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords: (‘Mitophagy’[Mesh] OR “mitophagy”[Title/Abstract] OR ‘mitochondrial autophagy’[Title/Abstract] OR ‘mitophagic’[Title/Abstract]) AND (‘Urinary Bladder Neoplasms’[Mesh] OR ‘BC’[Title/Abstract] OR ‘bladder carcinoma’[Title/Abstract] OR ‘bladder tumor’[Title/Abstract] OR ‘urothelial carcinoma’[Title/Abstract] OR ‘transitional cell carcinoma’[Title/Abstract]).The initial search yielded 46 publications. These records were screened for eligibility based on their relevance to the central theme of mitophagy in BC, methodological rigor, and the detail of their mechanistic reporting. Following this screening process, 15 primary research articles were selected for final inclusion and analysis in this review.

The molecular mechanisms of mitophagy

Mitophagy is a selective form of autophagy that specifically targets and degrades damaged mitochondria, thereby preventing the accumulation of dysfunctional organelles and maintaining cellular homeostasis. As a physiological process, it is regulated by complex and diverse molecular mechanisms. In contrast to general, non-selective autophagy, mitophagy requires the recruitment of specific receptor proteins to the mitochondrial surface and the activation of dedicated signaling pathways [7]. Current research classifies mitophagy mechanisms into two primary types: the ubiquitin-dependent pathway, mediated by PINK1/Parkin, and the ubiquitin-independent pathway, which involves receptors such as BNIP3, BNIP3L/Nix, and FUNDC1. With ongoing research, several non-canonical pathways have been identified, including those mediated by BCL2L13, PHB2, FKBP8, and DRP1 [8] (Fig. 1). A deeper exploration of these regulatory networks will enhance our understanding of disease pathogenesis and inform the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Molecular mechanisms of mitophagy. Following cellular stress or damage, mitochondria undergo depolarization, triggering mitophagy. This process occurs through two primary pathways. The ubiquitin-dependent pathway is initiated by the stabilization of PINK1 on the OMM, which recruits and activates the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin. Parkin ubiquitinates OMM proteins, and these ubiquitin chains serve as a signal for autophagy adaptor proteins (e.g., p62/SQSTM1, OPTN, NDP52), which bridge the damaged mitochondria to the LC3-positive phagophore. The ubiquitin-independent pathway is mediated by mitophagy receptors (e.g., BNIP3, BNIP3L/NIX, FUNDC1) located on the OMM. These receptors contain an LIR motif that binds directly to LC3 on the expanding phagophore. Both pathways culminate in the engulfment of the damaged mitochondrion to form a mitophagosome, which subsequently fuses with a lysosome for degradation and component recycling

PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy

The PINK1/Parkin pathway is a primary ubiquitin-dependent regulatory mechanism for mitophagy [9]. PINK1, a highly conserved mitochondrial kinase encoded by the PARK6 gene, plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial function [10]. It contains an N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence that facilitates its binding to mitochondria. Under physiological conditions, PINK1 is imported into the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) via the TOM/TIM complex and is rapidly cleaved and degraded by the presenilin-associated rhomboid-like protease (PARL) [11, 12]. However, upon mitochondrial depolarization or damage, this import is impaired, leading to the stable accumulation and activation of PINK1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) [13]. There, PINK1 phosphorylates ubiquitin molecules on OMM proteins. This phospho-ubiquitin acts as a signal to directly recruit autophagy adaptor proteins such as p62/SQSTM1, optineurin (OPTN), and nuclear dot protein 52 (NDP52). These adaptors promote mitophagy initiation by recruiting core autophagy machinery like ULK1 and, through their LC3-interacting regions (LIRs), tether the ubiquitin-tagged mitochondria to the growing autophagosomal membrane (labeled by LC3). Furthermore, PINK1 phosphorylates the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin at its Ser65 residue, which activates and recruits it to the mitochondria [14, 15]. Parkin then catalyzes extensive ubiquitination of OMM proteins, amplifying the phospho-ubiquitin signal and creating a robust “eat-me” signal for the autophagic machinery [16, 17]. The resulting ubiquitin chains are recognized by adaptor proteins like p62/SQSTM1, which further facilitates the encapsulation of damaged mitochondria into autophagosomes [18–20]. Beyond the canonical PINK1/Parkin axis, several alternative ubiquitin-dependent pathways can induce mitophagy. Other E3 ubiquitin ligases, including Gp78 [21], SMURF1 [22], SIAH1 [23], ARIH1 [24, 25], and MUL1 [26–28], can ubiquitinate mitochondrial proteins and promote mitophagy, often compensating for the absence of Parkin.

Ubiquitin-independent pathway

Unlike the ubiquitin-dependent PINK1/Parkin pathway, ubiquitin-independent mitophagy is mediated by mitochondrial cargo receptors (MCRs). These receptor proteins contain LIR motifs, which enable them to bind directly to LC3/GABARAP proteins on the phagophore membrane, thereby targeting mitochondria for autophagic degradation. Crucially, the expression and activity of these MCRs are regulated by specific cellular stresses that induce mitophagy, such as mitochondrial depolarization, hypoxia, and nutrient deprivation. In mammals, the primary MCRs include Bcl-2-interacting protein 3-like (BNIP3L/NIX), Bcl-2-interacting protein 3 (BNIP3), and FUN14 domain-containing 1 (FUNDC1).

BNIP3 and BNIP3L (NIX), which share 56% sequence homology, constitute a pro-apoptotic subfamily of the Bcl-2 protein family. They possess BH3 domains and function primarily as stress-induced MCRs. BNIP3 is a hypoxia-inducible adaptor protein that is upregulated under low oxygen conditions. It is anchored to the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) via its C-terminal transmembrane domain, while its cytosolic N-terminus exposes an LC3-interacting region (LIR) motif. Phosphorylation of Ser17 and Ser24 within this LIR is critical for its interaction with LC3 [29, 30]. BNIP3 deficiency impairs mitophagy and promotes apoptosis, contributing to pathologies such as renal injury [31]. Under basal conditions, BNIP3 exists as a cytoplasmic monomer. During cellular stress, it forms stable homodimers via its transmembrane domain, a conformation that, while not essential for OMM localization, significantly enhances LIR/LC3 binding and the subsequent recruitment of autophagosomes compared to the monomeric form [32].

BNIP3L was initially identified for its role in mediating mitochondrial clearance during erythrocyte maturation, and its deficiency leads to anaemia due to impaired red blood cell development [33]. Like BNIP3, BNIP3L localizes to the OMM, and phosphorylation at Ser34 and Ser35 within its LIR motif strengthens its binding to LC3 [34]. BNIP3L/Nix-mediated mitophagy has been shown to protect against ischemic brain injury independently of Parkin [35]. The expression of both BNIP3 and BNIP3L is rapidly induced at the transcriptional level by stresses such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, primarily through HIF-1α activation in response to hypoxic conditions and elevated ROS [36, 37]. Their activity is further regulated post-translationally by phosphorylation at key serine residues adjacent to critical tryptophan residues in their LIR motifs (Ser17 in BNIP3 and Ser35 in BNIP3L) [29, 38].

FUNDC1 is another crucial MCR localized to the OMM. This trimeric transmembrane protein also interacts directly with LC3 via a conserved N-terminal LIR motif to mediate mitophagy. The activity of FUNDC1 is primarily regulated by phosphorylation; phosphorylation of Ser13 (by CK2) and Tyr18 (by Src kinase) within its LIR motif inhibits its interaction with LC3. Conversely, dephosphorylation of Ser13 by the phosphatase PGAM5 promotes LC3 binding and induces mitophagy [39]. Although FUNDC1-mediated mitophagy is Parkin-independent, it is modulated by the mitochondrial E3 ubiquitin ligase MARCH5, which ubiquitinates FUNDC1, targeting it for degradation and thereby limiting mitophagy under hypoxia [40]. In summary, FUNDC1-induced mitophagy is dually regulated by opposing phosphorylation events and ubiquitin-mediated degradation.

Mitophagy and cancer

Given the pivotal role of mitochondria in energy production, calcium homeostasis, redox signaling, and cell death, mitochondrial dysfunction is closely associated with numerous diseases, including cancer. Extensive research indicates that mitophagy plays a significant yet dichotomous role in tumorigenesis, functioning as either a pro-survival or tumor-suppressive mechanism. During early carcinogenesis, cells encounter diverse stresses—such as oncogenic signals, hypoxia, and reactive oxygen species (ROS)—that damage mitochondria. Mitophagy can counteract this by removing damaged organelles, scavenging ROS, and maintaining genomic stability, thereby suppressing cancer initiation [8, 41]. Conversely, excessive or impaired mitophagy can disrupt mitochondrial networks and energy metabolism, creating a cellular environment conducive to cancer progression [42]. Environmental pollutants are associated with a variety of human diseases [43, 44]. The heavy metal antimony, for instance, has demonstrated carcinogenic effects in various cancers, including BC, potentially through mitochondrial dysfunction. Studies show that antimony exposure promotes intracellular ROS accumulation and impairs mitochondrial membrane potential. Crucially, it inhibits mitophagy by disrupting the PINK1/Parkin pathway [45]. This impairment prevents the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria and ROS, leading to their accumulation. Consequently, this buildup promotes genetic instability and pro-tumorigenic signaling, resulting in metabolic dysregulation and increased DNA damage that drives BC initiation and progression. The finding that the mitophagy activator CCCP can reverse antimony-induced BC cell proliferation further substantiates the model that antimony promotes carcinogenesis by suppressing mitophagy. Thus, the targeted regulation of mitophagy may represent a critical factor and a potential therapeutic avenue in cancer biology.

Once a tumor is established, cancer cells face a hostile microenvironment characterized by persistent hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and therapeutic stress. At this stage, the pro-survival function of mitophagy is co-opted by cancer cells to evade apoptosis and necrosis. By degrading and recycling cellular components, mitophagy provides essential energy and nutrients, becoming a critical adaptive mechanism for tumor cell survival [46]. A key adaptation in many cancers is the Warburg effect, where cells favor glycolysis for energy even in oxygen-rich conditions. Mitophagy supports this metabolic shift by eliminating functionally redundant or metabolically unsuitable mitochondria, thereby optimizing the metabolic network for rapid proliferation [47, 48]. The high metabolic activity of cancer cells generates substantial reactive oxygen species (ROS). At moderate levels, ROS function as signalling molecules that promote proliferation and metastasis. Mitophagy can exert tumor-suppressive effects by scavenging ROS and preventing its overaccumulation. For instance, in breast cancer, ULK1 depletion impairs mitophagy, leading to excessive ROS that activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and drives bone metastasis [49]. Conversely, excessively high levels of ROS are cytotoxic. In this context, mitophagy promotes tumor cell survival by removing ROS-producing dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby maintaining redox homeostasis. This protective mechanism enhances proliferation and confers resistance to treatment. For example, in BC, TTK activates ULK1/FUNDC1-mediated mitophagy to clear cytotoxic levels of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), thereby promoting cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis [50]. Furthermore, under hypoxic and nutrient-deprived conditions within solid tumors, mitophagy is activated to clear surplus mitochondria, reduce oxygen consumption, and recycle nutrients to sustain cancer cell survival [42]. Mitophagy also contributes to therapy resistance. Since many chemotherapeutic agents and radiotherapy kill cells by inducing mitochondrial apoptosis, mitophagy can pre-emptively remove mitochondria that are primed to initiate cell death, thereby enhancing the resilience of cancer cells. In summary, mitophagy promotes cancer progression by sustaining tumor cell survival through these pro-survival mechanisms. Conversely, excessive mitophagy can exhibit cytotoxic effects. For instance, corosolic acid, a plant-derived pentacyclic triterpenoid, demonstrates a concentration-dependent effect in BC. At low concentrations, it suppresses proliferation by inhibiting DNA replication and mitosis. At high concentrations, it induces cell death by significantly downregulating key mitophagy markers (LC3, SQSTM1/p62, NBR1, TAX1BP1, UBB), reducing mitochondrial membrane potential, and triggering excessive, lethal mitophagy [51]. This illustrates the delicate balance and context-dependent role of mitophagy in oncology.

In summary, mitophagy exhibits a fundamental duality in cancer. Its role—whether tumor-suppressive or tumor-promotive—is highly context-dependent, determined by variables such as tumor type, stage, genetic background, and the extent of mitophagic activation. The ultimate outcome hinges on whether the process successfully clears dysfunctional mitochondria to maintain cellular fitness or, conversely, depletes functional mitochondria to a degree that compromises cell viability.

Mitophagy acts as a promotor of BC progression

While the precise role of mitophagy in BC continues to be elucidated, a growing body of evidence indicates that it primarily functions to promote tumor progression. Key mechanisms underpinning this oncogenic role include the inhibition of cellular senescence and apoptosis, the maintenance of cancer stemness, the generation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and the induction of chemotherapy resistance. The specific pathways through which mitophagy facilitates BC advancement are detailed in the following sections (Fig. 2).

Mechanisms by which mitophagy promotes BC progression. Upstream regulators, including SRSF1, DARS2, TTK, SMYD2, H3K18la, and lncRNA-RP11-498C9.13, activate mitophagy through distinct signaling pathways. The induction of mitophagy subsequently drives BC progression through multiple mechanisms: inhibition of cellular senescence, suppression of mitochondrial apoptosis, maintenance of CSC stemness, generation of an immunosuppressive TME, and the development of chemotherapy resistance

Mitophagy serves as a mechanism to counteract cellular senescence

Cellular senescence is a state of stable cell cycle arrest that acts as a barrier to the proliferation of damaged cells, often associated with a pro-inflammatory secretome known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [52]. The role of senescence in cancer is complex and dualistic. While it functions as a natural tumor-suppressive mechanism, the SASP can also promote tumor progression by facilitating angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, and suppressing immune surveillance, thereby creating a permissive environment for cancer dissemination [53–56]. Growing evidence positions mitophagy as a key regulator of cellular senescence [57–59], suggesting that the modulation of senescence is a critical pathway through which mitophagy influences BC progression. Through bioinformatics analysis, Li et al. identified DARS2 as a pivotal regulator of BC progression, with correlations to mitophagy-related proteins [60]. Experimentally, DARS2 knockdown in BC cells led to reduced levels of the key mitophagy proteins PINK1 and LC3-II, increased ROS, and a decrease in functional mitochondria, indicating impaired mitophagy and compromised mitochondrial quality control. Functionally, DARS2 was shown to promote the G1-to-S phase transition by upregulating CDK4, thereby inhibiting cellular senescence and driving proliferation [60]. Crucially, the study established a direct link between DARS2-mediated mitophagy and senescence. Overexpression of PINK1, which enhanced mitophagy, was able to partially rescue the CDK4 downregulation and G1 phase arrest caused by DARS2 knockdown. This demonstrates that mitophagy acts downstream of DARS2 to inhibit senescence and promote tumor growth in BC [60]. In summary, this work elucidates a novel mechanism where DARS2 promotes BC progression by enhancing PINK1-mediated mitophagy, which in turn suppresses cellular senescence and stimulates proliferation. These findings highlight the DARS2-mitophagy-senescence axis as a promising therapeutic target in BC.

Mitophagy suppresses the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway in BC

The evasion of programmed cell death, or apoptosis, is a hallmark of cancer that facilitates tumor progression [61]. The serine/threonine kinase TTK, a key regulator of the cell cycle, is overexpressed in multiple cancers [62, 63]. In BC, Chen et al. [50] demonstrated that TTK is significantly overexpressed in both tissues and cell lines, and its high expression correlates with poor patient prognosis. Functionally, TTK promotes BC cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis. Mechanistically, TTK was found to reduce mtROS, which are key inducers of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. mtROS can trigger mitochondrial membrane depolarization and the release of cytochrome c, leading to caspase-9 activation and cell death. Mitophagy, by clearing damaged mitochondria, can suppress this pathway [64, 65]. The study confirmed that TTK potently enhances mitophagy in BC cells through two synergistic mechanisms. First, TTK directly binds to and phosphorylates ULK1, a core initiator of mitophagy, at Ser477. This enhances ULK1 kinase activity, leading to downstream phosphorylation of the mitophagy receptor FUNDC1 and inducing mitophagy. Second, acting as a splicing regulator, TTK phosphorylates SRSF3 at Ser108, which prevents aberrant splicing of ULK1 pre-mRNA and stabilizes ULK1 transcripts, ensuring its sustained high expression. In summary, through coordinated post-translational and post-transcriptional regulation, TTK maximizes ULK1 activity and abundance to drive mitophagy. This enhanced mitophagy facilitates the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria, preventing the activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway and thereby promoting BC cell survival and tumor growth. This study elucidates a novel mechanism wherein the TTK-ULK1 axis fine-tunes mitochondrial quality control to enable apoptosis evasion.

Mitophagy maintains BC stem cell stemness

The maintenance of cancer stemness is closely associated with tumor recurrence, drug resistance, and immune tolerance. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are a small population of undifferentiated cells with self-renewal capacity and high tumorigenic potential, capable of initiating tumors even when present in low numbers [66, 67]. Consequently, targeting CSCs is a promising strategy for improving BC outcomes. A growing body of research indicates that mitophagy plays a key role in maintaining CSC stemness [68, 69]. For instance, studies report enhanced mitophagy in bladder CSCs, which helps sustain mitochondrial respiration and inhibit AMPK signaling, thereby suppressing a transition from autophagy to apoptosis. The DNA replication initiator MCM7 has also been shown to promote mitophagy and stemness by facilitating autophagosome-lysosome formation [70]. A pivotal regulator in this process is the protein lysine methyltransferase SMYD2, which is overexpressed in bladder CSCs and correlates positively with stemness markers. SMYD2 knockdown impaired spheroid formation, downregulated stemness genes, and reduced in vivo tumorigenicity. Mechanistically, SMYD2 was found to transcriptionally upregulate PYCR1, a key enzyme in proline biosynthesis, by depositing the active histone mark H3K4me3 at its promoter [71]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the long non-coding RNA RP11-498C9.13 stabilizes PYCR1 and promotes its expression. In BC models, PYCR1 overexpression has been shown to enhance cancer cell proliferation and invasion by promoting ROS-induced mitophagy [72]. This establishes a clear SMYD2-PYCR1 axis in mitophagy and stemness maintenance. Functionally, PYCR1 promotes mitophagy. Proline, the product of PYCR1’s metabolic activity, can induce mitophagy by activating AMPKα and upregulating Parkin [73]. Furthermore, PYCR1 stabilizes the PINK1 protein, facilitating Parkin translocation to mitochondria and thereby activating the PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy pathway. Crucially, inhibition of this PINK1/Parkin pathway significantly compromises BC stem cell (BCSC) stemness, demonstrating that functional mitophagy is essential for its maintenance [71] In summary, this work elucidates a novel epigenetic-metabolic-mitophagic axis: SMYD2 upregulates PYCR1, which in turn stimulates PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy to maintain BCSC stemness. Therefore, inhibitors targeting SMYD2, PYCR1, or the mitophagy pathway itself represent potential therapeutic strategies for eradicating BCSCs to prevent tumor recurrence and metastasis.

Mitophagy maintains an immunosuppressive microenvironment

Despite advances in immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), their efficacy in BC remains limited due to immune evasion within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [74, 75]. A key driver of this evasion is the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) towards an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, which promotes tissue repair, angiogenesis, and suppresses anti-tumor immunity [76–78]. The accumulation of M2 macrophages correlates with poor prognosis across multiple cancers. Emerging evidence highlights a critical role for mitophagy in regulating macrophage polarization and function [79, 80]. Deng et al. [81] investigated this link in BC, finding that M2-associated genes were enriched in pathways related to mitophagy and lysosomal function. During M1-to-M2 differentiation, key mitophagy genes, including PINK1 and PRKN (which encodes Parkin), were upregulated. Single-cell analysis confirmed high PRKN expression in M2 macrophage subpopulations, where it strongly co-localized with the M2 marker CD163. Functionally, knockdown of PRKN significantly suppressed mitophagy and reduced the expression of M2 markers (CD206, ARG1), underscoring the necessity of Parkin-mediated mitophagy in sustaining the M2 phenotype. Furthermore, PRKN-high macrophages exhibited enhanced communication with other immune cells, and high PRKN expression correlated with a positive response to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, highlighting its role in shaping the immunosuppressive TME. This process is metabolically orchestrated by the tumor cells themselves. Through the Warburg effect, cancer cells undergo aerobic glycolysis, producing large amounts of lactate. Lactate can serve as a precursor for histone lactylation, a novel epigenetic mark that directly regulates gene transcription [82]. Specifically, histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la) has been shown to suppress pro-inflammatory genes in M1 macrophages while activating M2-associated genes like ARG1 and CD206 [83]. Deng et al. [81] integrated these findings, demonstrating that PRKN expression is regulated by H3K18la. Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) revealed significant enrichment of H3K18la at the PRKN promoter. Consequently, modulating histone lactylation with specific promoters and inhibitors correspondingly increased and decreased the expression of PRKN, CD206, and ARG1. This confirms that H3K18la epigenetically upregulates PRKN to drive M2 polarization. In summary, a novel metabolic-epigenetic-mitophagic axis promotes immunosuppression in BC: lactate from tumor cells drives histone H3K18 lactylation, which upregulates PRKN expression to enhance Parkin-mediated mitophagy, thereby facilitating M2 macrophage polarization. Targeting this axis represents a promising strategy for reversing immunosuppression and enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy.

Mitophagy induces chemotherapy resistance in BC

Cisplatin-based chemotherapy is a first-line treatment for BC [84]. However, its clinical utility is often limited by the development of resistance, a major cause of treatment failure and disease progression [85]. A growing body of evidence implicates mitophagy as a key mechanism that promotes tumor survival and enhances cisplatin resistance [86, 87] The serine/arginine-rich splicing factor 1 (SRSF1), a proto-oncogene and crucial regulator of pre-mRNA splicing [88], has been identified as a central player in this process. Wu et al. [89] demonstrated that SRSF1 is highly expressed in BC and correlates with poor patient prognosis. Functionally, SRSF1 overexpression conferred cisplatin resistance both in vitro and in vivo. The study linked this resistance to mitophagy, showing that SRSF1 knockdown reduced key mitophagy indicators (decreased Beclin-1, LC3B-II/LC3B-I ratio, and mitochondrial membrane potential; increased p62), while SRSF1 overexpression had the opposite effect. Furthermore, cisplatin treatment itself was found to induce mitophagy by reducing mitochondrial membrane potential. Investigations into the specific pathway revealed that SRSF1 acts through the mitophagy receptor BNIP3. Knockdown of SRSF1 significantly reduced BNIP3 expression without affecting other receptors like PINK1, Parkin, NIX, or FUNDC1. Crucially, knocking down BNIP3 reversed the enhanced mitophagy, proliferation, migration, and cisplatin resistance caused by SRSF1 overexpression, confirming BNIP3’s essential role downstream of SRSF1.Mechanistically, SRSF1 interacts with pre-mRNA of HIF1A via its RRM1/RRM2 domains, promoting alternative splicing that enhances HIF-1α protein production. The HIF-1α transcription factor then binds directly to the BNIP3 promoter, upregulating its expression and driving BNIP3-mediated mitophagy. This pathway ultimately blunts cisplatin sensitivity and promotes BC progression [89] In conclusion, the SRSF1/HIF-1α/BNIP3 axis promotes mitophagy to drive cisplatin resistance in BC. Therefore, combining inhibitors of SRSF1 or mitophagy with cisplatin may represent a promising strategy to re-sensitize resistant tumors to chemotherapy.

The tumor-suppressive role of mitophagy in BC

While substantial evidence indicates that mitophagy promotes BC pathogenesis, a growing body of research demonstrates that its inhibition can also suppress tumor progression. In these contexts, impairing mitophagy can inhibit metastasis and potentiate anti-tumor immunity, thereby restraining disease advancement. The following section details the mechanisms through which mitophagy can exert these tumor-suppressive effects in BC (Fig. 3).

Tumor-suppressive mechanisms of mitophagy in BC. Upstream negative regulators, including SFXN1, MCUB, and DUSP6, suppress mitophagy through specific signaling pathways, thereby accelerating BC progression. Conversely, active mitophagy inhibits tumor progression through multiple mechanisms: it suppresses EMT and metastasis by inhibiting TGF-β signaling; it degrades PD-L1 to counteract immune evasion and enhance anti-tumor immunity; and it directly suppresses cell proliferation and invasion. Furthermore, mitophagy can protect against carcinogen-induced damage, as it clears dysfunctional mitochondria to inhibit antimony-induced proliferation. Finally, pharmacological agents like Nitazoxanide can induce a lethal impairment of autophagic flux by concurrently triggering mitophagy initiation and inhibiting lysosomal function, leading to BC cell death

Mitophagy inhibits BC metastasis

Metastasis is a pivotal stage in cancer progression, with the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) serving as a critical driver. While reactive ROS are known to promote cancer metastasis [90], mitophagy can counteract this by removing the dysfunctional mitochondria that are a primary source of ROS, thereby exerting tumor-suppressive effects. The association between mitophagy and the suppression of tumor metastasis is well-documented [91, 92], with specific evidence emerging in BC. Zhang et al. [93] identified SFXN1, a protein located on the mitochondrial inner membrane, as being highly expressed in BC tissues, particularly in metastatic lesions, where its levels correlate with poor patient survival. Functionally, SFXN1 knockdown suppressed cancer cell invasion, migration, and lung metastasis in mice, while its overexpression enhanced metastatic potential. Crucially, this pro-metastatic function was linked to its inhibition of mitophagy, independent of its canonical role in serine transport. SFXN1 knockdown increased mitophagy (evidenced by enhanced mitochondria-LC3B colocalization), reduced mtROS, and lowered mitochondrial membrane potential. Furthermore, when mitophagy was chemically blocked, the anti-migratory effects of SFXN1 knockdown were abolished, confirming that mitophagy induction is required for this metastatic suppression. Mechanistically, SFXN1 interacts with the proteases PARL and MPP-β on the inner mitochondrial membrane, promoting the processing and degradation of PINK1. This prevents the stable accumulation of PINK1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane, thereby inhibiting the initiation of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy. The resulting accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria leads to increased mtROS, which in turn activates the TGF-β signaling pathway. Since TGF-β is a dominant inducer of EMT-activating transcription factors [94], this cascade ultimately drives EMT and cell migration.In summary, this study demonstrates that SFXN1 promotes BC metastasis by repressing PINK1-dependent mitophagy. Therefore, the induction of mitophagy, by clearing damaged mitochondria and limiting mtROS-driven TGF-β signaling, can function as a natural brake on the metastatic cascade in BC.

Mitophagy inhibits immune evasion by degrading PD-L1

Immunotherapy has revolutionised BC treatment, but its efficacy is often limited by immune evasion. A central mechanism of this evasion is the overexpression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) on tumor cells, which suppresses cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cell function by engaging the PD-1 receptor. Beyond its canonical role in mitochondrial quality control, mitophagy has been found to non-canonically degrade specific non-mitochondrial proteins, including PD-L1. As summarised by Cui et al., mitophagy is critical for regulating PD-L1 turnover and anti-tumor immune responses [95], with PINK1-dependent mitophagy reported to enhance anti-tumor immunity by degrading PD-L1 [96] Furthermore, mitochondrial calcium uptake is a pivotal signaling mechanism that regulates both mitophagy and downstream immune responses [97, 98]. The mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) complex is a key regulator of mitochondrial calcium signaling, which in turn modulates mitophagy. Within this complex, the MCUB subunit acts as a dominant-negative inhibitor, suppressing calcium influx into the mitochondrial matrix [99, 100]. Huang et al. [101] reported that MCUB is highly expressed in MIBC, where its levels positively correlate with PD-L1 overexpression, reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and poor patient prognosis. Functionally, MCUB knockdown significantly reduced PD-L1 protein levels without affecting its mRNA, indicating post-translational regulation. The study linked this to mitophagy, demonstrating that MCUB stabilises PD-L1 by inhibiting Parkin-mediated mitophagy. Mechanistically, MCUB acts through a dual pathway: first, by reducing mitochondrial calcium uptake to diminish Parkin activation, and second, by directly binding to the Arg51 residue on Parkin, which impairs its E3 ligase function. This dual inhibition disrupts the mitophagic degradation of PD-L1.In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that MCUB knockdown enhanced T-cell-mediated killing of cancer cells, inhibited tumor growth, and was associated with decreased PD-L1 levels and increased activation of tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells. In summary, this work reveals a novel immune regulatory axis: Parkin-mediated mitophagy targets PD-L1 for degradation, and MCUB acts as a key upstream inhibitor of this pathway. By suppressing mitophagy, MCUB stabilises PD-L1 and drives immune evasion. Therefore, strategies to inhibit MCUB or activate mitophagy could enhance anti-tumor immunity and improve the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in BC.

Mitophagy inhibits BC proliferation and invasion

Dual-specificity phosphatase 6 (DUSP6), a member of the DUSP family, is implicated in the progression of various malignancies. In BC, elevated DUSP6 expression is associated with more aggressive disease and poorer prognosis, while its silencing inhibits BC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Bioinformatic analyses suggest DUSP6 is involved in mitochondrial function and mitophagy pathways. Consistent with this, DUSP6 knockdown enhanced autophagic flux, increased the number of intracellular autophagosomes, and upregulated the expression of PARKIN, PINK1, and the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio. These findings indicate that DUSP6 promotes BC malignancy by suppressing mitophagy, a conclusion further supported by the reversal of DUSP6 knockdown effects using mitophagy inhibitors [102] Mechanistically, DUSP6 knockdown reduced mTOR phosphorylation. Given that the mTOR pathway is a well-established negative regulator of mitophagy [103, 104], this suggests DUSP6 suppresses mitophagy by activating mTOR. Corroborating this, treatment of DUSP6-knockdown cells with an mTOR agonist reduced ROS levels and restored mitochondrial membrane potential. In summary, this study demonstrates that high DUSP6 expression promotes BC progression by inhibiting mTOR-mediated mitophagy, leading to poor patient prognosis. These findings provide direct evidence that mitophagy itself functions to inhibit the proliferation and invasion of BC.

The potential of mitophagy-related biomarkers in BC diagnosis and prognosis

Biomarkers provide invaluable insights into cancer biology, facilitating early detection, accurate diagnosis, treatment guidance, and prognostic assessment. A growing body of evidence indicates that mitophagy-related biomarkers are closely associated with the initiation, progression, and clinical outcomes of various malignancies. For example, an analysis of the ONCOMINE database revealed that FUNDC1 mRNA expression is significantly elevated in multiple tumor types, including prostate, pancreatic, breast, ovarian, lung, colorectal, and cervical cancers, compared to normal controls [105]. Furthermore, high FUNDC1 mRNA levels correlate significantly with reduced survival rates in breast and cervical cancer patients [105]. These findings underscore the broad relevance of mitophagy-related molecules as potential diagnostic and prognostic indicators across cancers.

In BC, Huang et al. employed bioinformatics analyses to identify four mitophagy-related biomarkers—CTSK, MTERF3, SRC, and CSNK2B—with significant differential expression in tumor tissues [106]. While CTSK was downregulated, MTERF3, SRC, and CSNK2B were markedly upregulated. Based on this four-gene signature, patients were stratified into high-risk (HRG) and low-risk (LRG) groups, with HRG patients exhibiting significantly poorer survival. The risk score correlated strongly with clinicopathological parameters, including T stage, N stage, tumor grade, and overall stage. Notably, expression levels of these biomarkers were also associated with immunotherapy response, chemosensitivity, and patient survival [106]. These findings provide a foundation for developing novel prognostic tools and personalized therapeutic strategies in BC.

Despite these advances, early diagnosis of BC remains a critical challenge for reducing mortality. While urine cytology offers a simple, non-invasive approach, its limited sensitivity restricts its diagnostic utility. Cystoscopy with biopsy, though the current gold standard, is invasive, costly, and impractical for widespread screening. Thus, there is an urgent need for more sensitive, specific, and non-invasive biomarkers. Notably, whether circulating or urinary mitophagy-related proteins or their regulatory factors could serve as such tools for early BC detection has not yet been explored in the literature. This represents a promising and critical avenue for future investigation.

The therapeutic potential of targeting mitophagy in BC

The pivotal role of mitophagy in driving BC progression and chemotherapy resistance establishes it as a compelling therapeutic target. Consequently, the strategic modulation of mitophagy represents a promising avenue for treatment development. Its integration with established modalities like chemotherapy and immunotherapy holds significant potential to improve patient outcomes [107, 108]. It is important to note that while mitophagy-targeted therapies for BC remain at the preclinical stage, they have demonstrated considerable therapeutic promise in animal models. In established tumors, where mitophagy is co-opted to promote cancer cell survival, its targeted inhibition offers a viable strategy. For instance: Inhibiting DARS2-mediated mitophagy promotes cellular senescence, suppressing tumor proliferation [60]. Targeting the TTK-ULK1 axis induces mitochondrial apoptosis [50]. Disrupting SMYD2/PYCR1 signaling or the downstream PINK1/Parkin pathway impairs cancer stem cell maintenance, potentially reducing recurrence [71]. Suppressing mitophagy in M2-polarized macrophages remodels the tumor microenvironment toward an immune-active state, which may synergize with immunotherapy69]. As mitophagy is a key mechanism of chemotherapy resistance, combining SRSF1 inhibitors with mitophagy blockade can re-sensitize cisplatin-resistant BC cells, suggesting a strategy to overcome drug resistance in patients [89]. Collectively, these findings underscore the significant potential of targeting mitophagy, both as a monotherapy and as part of combination regimens, for improving BC treatment.

Nitazoxanide (NTZ), an FDA-approved antiparasitic drug, has recently garnered attention for its broad-spectrum antitumor activity against various cancers, including bladder [109], ovarian [110], thyroid [111], prostate [112], and colon cancer [113]. Its anti-tumor effects are multifaceted. In BC, studies indicate that NTZ acts by simultaneously targeting mitochondrial and lysosomal function [114]. Treatment with NTZ induces mitochondrial damage, triggering the initiation of mitophagy, as evidenced by the significant upregulation of PINK1 and the autophagy receptor NDP52. Under normal conditions, this initiated mitophagy would progress to degradation via autophagosome-lysosome fusion. However, NTZ concurrently inhibits lysosomal function, creating a “logjam” in the mitophagy pathway. This disruption in autophagic flux leads to the massive accumulation of undegraded autophagosomes and persistently damaged mitochondria. This cellular crisis exacerbates oxidative stress, triggers metabolic catastrophe, and ultimately induces apoptosis or other forms of programmed cell death to inhibit tumor growth. Thus, although NTZ molecularly initiates mitophagy, its functional outcome is the suppression of complete mitochondrial quality control. This concept is reinforced by combination therapy: using NTZ with the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) further exacerbates the flux impairment and synergistically enhances apoptosis. This strategy of disrupting an entire biological pathway—autophagic flux—rather than merely inhibiting a single target, plunges cancer cells into a state of irreparable dysfunction and is typically less prone to the development of resistance.

Emerging evidence suggests that inducing mitophagy can also be a viable anti-cancer strategy. For instance, high concentrations of corosolic acid trigger excessive mitophagy, leading to BC cell death [51]. Furthermore, studies indicate that enhancing mitophagy can suppress BC metastasis and immune evasion. For instance, knocking down SFXN1 inhibits EMT in BC cells by promoting PINK1-mediated mitophagy [93]. Targeted inhibition of MCUB-PRKN enhances T-cell killing of cancer cells, and combining this with immunotherapy may amplify its efficacy [101]. Similarly, rapamycin, a canonical mTOR inhibitor, can induce autophagy and mitophagy to promote apoptosis, demonstrating significant therapeutic potential in BC. Young Mi Whang et al. [115] reported that in bladder urothelial carcinoma, rapamycin activates autophagy-related proteins, elevates autophagic activity, impairs mitochondrial membrane potential, increases reactive ROS, and ultimately induces autophagic cell death. Interestingly, the efficacy of such treatments can be amplified in specific genetic contexts. LKB1 deficiency itself causes mitochondrial dysfunction and energy stress. In LKB1-deficient urothelial carcinoma cells, rapamycin-induced mitophagy, acting on this pre-existing mitochondrial defect, synergistically enhances cancer cell death. A parallel study found that downregulating the selective autophagy receptor NBR1 also produces a synergistic effect with rapamycin, more effectively inducing cell death [116]. Mechanistically, the absence of either LKB1 or NBR1 independently induces mitochondrial dysfunction, rendering cancer cells more vulnerable to the additional metabolic stress imposed by mTOR inhibition, thereby driving them toward apoptosis. These studies exemplify the classic concept of ‘synthetic lethality’. While knocking down LKB1 or NBR1 alone, or administering rapamycin alone, may be insufficient to kill cancer cells efficiently, their combination yields a lethal effect. This offers a novel therapeutic insight for urothelial carcinoma: developing LKB1 or NBR1 inhibitors for use in combination with mTOR inhibitors to modulate mitophagy represents a promising and effective treatment strategy.

In summary, the targeted modulation of mitophagy represents a promising yet complex therapeutic frontier in BC. Its functional role is highly context-dependent, exhibiting diametrically opposed effects—tumor-suppressive or tumor-promotive—contingent on tumor stage, molecular subtype, and microenvironmental cues. Consequently, a simplistic strategy of indiscriminately inhibiting or enhancing mitophagy is unlikely to achieve broad clinical success. Future preclinical investigations must therefore employ animal models that more faithfully recapitulate the heterogeneity and dynamic complexity of the human tumor microenvironment, thereby enabling the development of precise, context-aware therapeutic interventions.

Conclusion and outlook

Mitophagy plays a complex and pivotal role in BC. It is no longer merely a cellular quality control mechanism, but rather a profoundly integrated process involved in tumor microenvironment signaling, oncogenic pathways, and cellular metabolic reprogramming, thereby emerging as a significant disease-promoting factor (Fig. 4). Existing research clearly demonstrates that mitophagy powerfully drives the malignant progression of BC by maintaining mitochondrial functional homeostasis, suppressing apoptosis, and promoting stress adaptation. It constitutes one of the core mechanisms underpinning resistance to chemotherapy drugs such as cisplatin. However, mitophagy also suppresses BC EMT and metastasis by clearing ROS and inhibiting TGF-β signaling activation. Furthermore, it enhances anti-tumor immunity by degrading PD-L1.

Mitophagy critically regulates BC development and progression by integrating into oncogenic pathways to modulate diverse cancer cell behaviors

The dual role of mitophagy in BC arises from complex mechanisms. For instance, appropriate levels of ROS support tumor growth and metastasis; mitophagy removes ROS conducive to tumor growth, thereby exerting tumor-suppressive effects. Conversely, excessive ROS exhibit tumor cytotoxicity. In such scenarios, mitophagy eliminating surplus ROS facilitates apoptosis signaling, ultimately promoting tumor progression. Mitophagy in BC is also intertwined with the tumor microenvironment. Promoting mitophagy in immune cells (macrophages) drives M2 macrophage polarization, thereby suppressing anti-tumor immunity. Conversely, enhancing mitophagy within cancer cells boosts anti-tumor immunity by degrading PD-L1. In summary, mitophagy is neither an absolute suppressor nor promoter in BC. Cells in normal tissues, BC cells, and tumor microenvironment cells all utilize the stress-relieving properties of mitophagy for survival, yet can also be induced to undergo cell death by mitophagy. This dual nature may represent the fundamental reason for mitophagy’s ambivalent function in BC.

Despite breakthroughs in this field, it remains largely in its infancy, with numerous critical issues demanding further investigation. Presently, research into mitophagy and BC remains confined to animal and cellular models, and numerous challenges persist in advancing its clinical translation. Firstly, owing to the dual nature of mitophagy in BC, merely inhibiting or enhancing mitophagy cannot guarantee successful cancer treatment. The dynamic equilibrium mechanisms of mitophagy in BC require further investigation and clarification. Future therapeutic development should focus on individualized, precision regulation. Secondly, targeting mitophagy—such as developing specific small-molecule inhibitors or activators—offers novel avenues for BC intervention. However, achieving tumor- or cell-type-specific targeted delivery to avoid systemic side effects remains a major challenge for future drug development. The introduction of nanoparticles for targeted regulation of mitophagy holds promise as a prospective method for targeted delivery [117–119]. Finally, whether circulating mitophagy markers or their regulatory proteins can serve as novel non-invasive biomarkers for early BC diagnosis, risk stratification, or prognostic prediction warrants validation in both basic research and clinical cohorts. Targeting mitophagy as a therapeutic strategy for BC presents formidable challenges that must be overcome; failure to do so may cast doubt on its clinical value as a therapeutic target. In summary, the in-depth exploration of mitophagy in BC not only broadens our understanding of its pathological mechanisms but also opens new diagnostic and therapeutic perspectives. Despite the formidable challenges ahead, targeting this critical cellular process undoubtedly offers a promising new direction for overcoming therapeutic bottlenecks in BC treatment.
