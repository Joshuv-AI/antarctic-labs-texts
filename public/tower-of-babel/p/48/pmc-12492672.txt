Revealing the potential value of urine microbiota in ureteral stent encrustation after renal transplantation via Illumina sequencing of 16S rRNA genes
Authors: Juan Chen, Wenjun Song, Shi Yan, Long He, Qing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe complication of ureteral stent encrustation (USE) has brought many challenges and troubles to clinical treatment after renal transplantation. The aim of this study was to explore the microbiota that may be involved in the formation and progression of encrustation in ureteral stents.MethodsIn total, 15 patients with ureteral stent non-encrustation (USNE) after renal transplantation and 18 patients with USE after renal transplantation were included in this study. The urine microbiota was analyzed by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, followed by composition analysis, difference analysis and PICRUSt function prediction. In addition, random forest classification diagnostic models were constructed based on urine microbiota.ResultsCompared to the USNE-U group, the microbial community diversity, evenness, and coverage of the USE-U group were significantly reduced. At the phylum level, Actinobacteriota, Patescibacteria and Desulfobacterota may be the important microbiota involved in the formation of USE. At the genus level, Staphylococcus, Escherichia-Shigella, Dermabacter, Corynebacterium and Streptococcus may play an important role in USE. The functions of microbiota that may be involved in USE formation were also discovered, such as defense mechanisms, amino acid transport and metabolism, and L-rhamnose degradation I. In addition, the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) result showed that the classification model had excellent diagnostic accuracy in detecting USE.ConclusionThe results of this study suggest that urine microbiota may be closely related to the formation of USE, which provides a new perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of USE, which may contribute to early clinical diagnosis and treatment of USE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04307-7.

Introduction

Renal transplantation is the treatment of choice for patients with end-stage renal disease, which improves the quality of life and extends the life expectancy of the recipient [1]. Urinary leakage, ureteral necrosis and ureteral stricture are common urinary complications after renal transplantation, which can also damage graft function and increase mortality [2]. The rational application of ureteral stents is very important to ensure the function of renal transplantation and the patency of ureter [3]. However, the complication of ureteral stent encrustation (USE) has brought many challenges and troubles to clinical treatment [4]. Encrustation is the deposition of mineral crystal on the surface and lumen of the ureteral stent. Crystal deposition can hinder drainage through the stent lumen and interact with the urinary tract skin of the ureter, leading to ureteral injury [5]. The mechanism of USE is complex and multifaceted, with risk factors including length of stent retention, bacterial colonization, urinary system infection, patient-specific factors, and physical characteristics of the stent [4, 6]. Therefore, continuous understanding of USE is of great significance for medical staff to develop more effective prevention strategies, reduce the occurrence of related complications, ensure the function of renal transplantation, and ultimately improve the long-term prognosis and quality of life of patients.

Urine has a unique microbial community [7, 8]. Ureteral stent is a hollow catheter placed in the ureter from the renal pelvis to the bladder. Due to the continuous contact of the ureteral stent tube with urine, urinary crystal deposition and bacterial biofilm formation are common on the stent [9, 10]. Most indwelling ureteral stents are colonized by bacteria, which typically continue to form more mature biofilms [11]. It is believed that the formation of biofilms and encrustation are interdependent, and bacterial colonization is considered the driving force behind these two phenomena [12]. The stent-associated microbiota was dominated by common urinary bacteria [13]. However, no studies have been reported on the differences in urine microbiota between patients with ureteral stent non-encrustation (USNE) and those with USE, aiming to explore the microbiota that may play a potential role in the formation of encrustation.

Sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene using the Illumina MiSeq platform has emerged as a crucial method for studying the composition and distribution of microbiota [14]. In this study, 15 urine samples from patients with USNE after renal transplantation and 18 urine samples from patients with USE after renal transplantation were collected. Research has shown that although the urine microbiota may initially colonize on the stent, the microbiota on the stent will form and accumulate due to bacteria with competitive adhesion ability, ultimately resulting in significant differences from the microbiota in urine [13]. Therefore, 18 encrustation samples were also collected from patients with USE after renal transplantation to explore the possible differential microbiota between encrustation and urine samples. In addition, random forest classification diagnostic models were constructed based on microbiota, hoping to contribute to the diagnosis of USNE and USE patients. The aim of this study was to explore the microbiota that may be involved in the formation and progression of encrustation in ureteral stents.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and grouping

Patient inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Patients underwent allogeneic renal transplantation for the first time due to uremia, and ureteral stent was placed during the operation; (2) The implanted ureteral stents were of the same type; (3) Patients whose the stent in a normal position and who had the stent successfully removed in the first stage under cystoscopy; (4) The anti-rejection regimen applied to the patients after the operation consisted of tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and adrenocortical hormones. Patients with the following conditions were excluded: (1) Patients with obvious symptoms of lower urinary tract infection or systemic infection; (2) Patients with positive urine bacterial culture; (3) Patients who had used antibiotics within one month before stent removal; (4) Patients with significant abnormalities in uric acid levels that had not been controlled; (5) Patients with delayed graft function (DGF); (6) Patients with hyperparathyroidism or malignant tumors. In this study, a total of 15 urine samples from patients with USNE after renal transplantation (labeled as USNE-U group), 18 urine samples from patients with USE after renal transplantation (labeled as USE-U group) and 18 encrustation samples from patients with USE after renal transplantation (labeled as USE-E group) were collected. The samples in USE-U and USE-E groups were from the same patients. In order to explore microbiota differences between encrustation and urine samples from patients with USE after renal transplantation, using the same patient population can effectively reduce confounding variables introduced by inter-individual differences. The urine was collected using the method of random clean-catch mid-stream urine. Before collection, the urethral orifice was cleaned following the routine procedure, and the patients were not required to fast or abstain from water. An aseptic disposable urine culture container was used for urine collection. After collection, the sample was ensured to be delivered to the laboratory within 2 h and stored in a −80℃ refrigerator.

Illumina MiSeq sequencing

In this study, the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA kit was used for DNA extraction. DNA was detected by using NanoDrop2000 and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, PCR amplification was performed using TransGen AP221-02: TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase in the ABI GeneAmp® 9700 (PCR instrument) [15]. The primer sequences were as follows: the forward primer (F) was 5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’, and the reverse primer (R) was 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’. The resulted PCR products were extracted from a 2% agarose gel and further purifed using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (AXYGEN). Then, quantification was performed using QuantiFluor™-ST (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Illumina’s TruseqTM DNA sample Prep Kit was used to construct DNA library. Then, sequencing was performed using the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Processing of sequencing data

The raw data obtained from MiSeq sequencing needs to be quality controlled, filtered, and spliced using FASTP (v0.19.6) and FLASH (v1.2.7) to obtain optimized sequence. In addition, there may be some PCR amplification errors and sequencing errors in the optimized sequence. Therefore, the sequence was further denoised by DADA2 to remove PCR amplification errors or sequencing errors in the optimized data as much as possible to obtain the real sequence information amplicon sequence variant (ASV) in the sample. To obtain the species classification information of each ASV, classify-sklearn (Naive Bayes) algorithm was used to taxonomic analysis on the ASV representative sequence based on QIIME2 (version 2022.2) software. The species annotation database was Silva (version 138, https://www.arb-silva.de/). The classification confidence level was set to 0.7. Moreover, to minimize the impact of sequencing depth on subsequent data analysis, rarefaction normalization was performed on all sample sequences using the usearch (v11).

Diversity analysis of microbiota

The vegan in the R package (v3.3.1) was used for Pan/Core species analysis. Pan species were used to assess the increase in the number of total species as the number of samples increased. Core species were used to assess the decrease in the number of shared species as the number of samples increased. The mothur software (v1.30.2) was used for alpha diversity analysis. The t-test was used to analyze the differences in alpha diversity indices between groups. Subsequently, beta diversity analysis was also performed, including principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA) analysis and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis. The unweighted UniFrac distance algorithm was used to calculate the distance between samples. The ANOSIM was used to test the differences between groups.

Composition and differential analyses of microbiota

Circos map is a visual circle map that describes the corresponding relationship between samples and species. The Circos map can not only reflect the proportion of dominant species in each group of samples, but also reflect the distribution proportion of each dominant species in different groups. Wilcoxon rank sum test was performed using the stats package to analyze the microbiota with significant differences between the groups according to community relative abundance data (P < 0.05).

Phylogenetic investigation of communities by reconstruction of unobserved States (PICRUSt) functional prediction analysis

PICRUSt2 (v2.2.0-b) is a tool for predicting microbial community function. It is based on 16 S rRNA gene sequence data and predicts the functional potential of microbial communities by integrating microbial phylogenetic information and reference genome databases [16]. PICRUSt2 predictions based on several gene family databases are supported by default, including the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Clusters of Orthologous Group (COG) and MetaCyc [16, 17]. KEGG is an integrated database resource, which is composed of multiple databases such as KEGG module and KEGG pathway [18]. COG is used to functionally classify orthologous gene families of different organisms based on evolutionary relationships [19]. MetaCyc is a metabolic pathway database that contains metabolic pathway information collected and collated from a large number of organisms. These metabolic pathways cover the synthesis and decomposition of various substances in organisms [20]. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to analyze the differences between groups.

Model prediction analysis

The randomForest package was used to construct a random forest diagnostic classification model. The importance of microbiota species was sorted from large to small according to the Mean Decrease Accuracy value by using the random forest algorithm. In accordance with the order of the random forest sorting results, add one species successively from top to bottom. For each of these scenarios, classification is performed using the random forest algorithm, and the area under curve (AUC) value is calculated using the 10-fold cross-validation process. The optimal biomarkers were selected based on the maximum value of AUC. The receiver operator characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed using the pROC package to verify the diagnostic accuracy of the diagnostic classification model. AUC > 0.9 indicates that the model has a excellent diagnostic accuracy [21].

Alpha diversity analysis (Table S1) revealed that the shannon and invsimpson indices [22] in the USE-U group were lower compared to those in the USNE-U group. Conversely, the simpson index in the USE-U group was higher than that in the USNE-U group. This indicates that the community diversity within the USE-U group was lower than that of the USNE-U group (Fig. 1A-C). Besides, the shannoneven and pielou_e indices [23, 24] demonstrated that the USE-U group had lower values compared to the USNE-U group, suggesting that the community evenness was less in the USE-U group (Fig. 1D and E). The results showed no significant difference in shannon, simpson, invsimpson, shannoneven, pielou_e and coverage indices between USE-U and USE-E groups (Fig. 1G-L). Afterward, beta diversity analysis was also performed. The results of PCoA and NMDS analysis revealed that the distribution pattern exhibited a significant difference between the USNE-U and USE-U groups (Fig. 2A-B). Nevertheless, the difference between the USE-U and USE-E groups was not significant (Fig. 2C-D). Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the differences in microbiota between patients with USE and those with USNE after renal transplantation. The results provide valuable insights into the potential role of microbiota in stent encrustation. Pan/Core species analysis revealed that the total number of species increased and the number of core species decreased with the increase of sample size, which was consistent with many previous studies on the dynamic changes of microbial communities. For example, similar phenomena were also observed in the study of gut microbiota, indicating that the complexity and dynamics of microbial communities were universal in different niches [25–27]. Alpha diversity analysis showed that compared to the USNE-U group, the microbial community diversity, evenness, and coverage of the USE-U group were significantly reduced. The change of microbial community structure may be closely related to the occurrence of USE. Previous studies have shown that decreased microbial community diversity is often associated with disease status, and similar diversity trends have been found in studies related to urinary system infections [28–30]. In addition, the results of PCoA and NMDS analyses in beta diversity analysis showed that there were significant differences in the distribution patterns of microbial communities between USNE-U and USE-U groups, which further confirmed the importance of microbial community structure in the occurrence of USE. However, there were no significant differences in the alpha diversity index and beta diversity between the USE-U and USE-E groups. This may suggest that the microbial communities in urine and those after encrustation formation are relatively stable in terms of diversity, evenness, coverage and the degree of change in community composition.

Actinobacteriota may play a crucial role in the process of USE. Research has shown that Actinobacteriota is one of the common microbial phyla in the analysis of the microbial community associated with ureteral stents [31]. The change of urine composition caused by bacteria is one of the important mechanisms of USE [32]. Some bacteria in Actinobacteriota have special metabolic capacity, which may affect the metabolism and transformation of substances in urine, thus creating conditions for the encrustation of ureteral stents. For example, some of these strains can decompose urea, produce ammonia, and alkalize urine, which is conducive to the precipitation of salts such as phosphates, thereby promoting the formation of encrustation [13, 31]. Patescibacteria is a diverse group of bacteria that accounts for a large proportion of microbial dark matter [33]. Desulfobacterota is an important class of sulfate reducing bacteria. In microenvironments such as the human gut, the Desulfobacterota may be involved in regulating microbial community balance, affecting host metabolism and health status [34, 35]. Currently, research on Patescibacteria and Desulfobacterota is limited. This study for the first time reports that Patescibacteria and Desulfobacterota may be related to USE. Herein, Actinobacteriota, Patescibacteria, and Desulfobacterota were significantly different microbiota that may play a role in the formation and progression of USE.

At the genus level, there were 23 significantly different microbiota species between USNE-U and USE-U groups, such as Staphylococcus, Escherichia-Shigella, Dermabacter and Corynebacterium. A study has shown that Staphylococcus has strong adhesion ability and can be attached to the surface of the ureteral stent [36]. Once Staphylococcus attach to the surface of the stent, a biofilm begins to form. The biofilm provides a relatively stable living environment for Staphylococcus, allowing it to resist the body’s immune defences as well as the effects of antimicrobial drugs [37]. Some species of Staphylococcus, with urease activity, can decompose urea in urine to produce ammonia [38, 39]. This process changes the urine environment, leading to the deposition of salts. Corynebacterium has been reported to be associated with encrusted pyelitis [40] and encrusted cystitis [41]. Encrusted uropathy is caused by infection with urease-producing bacteria, mainly Corynebacterium. The characteristic of these diseases is the deposition of struvite and carbonate apatite in the urothelial deposition, leading to encrustations and ulceronecrotic inflammation of the urothelium and surrounding tissues [42]. Struvite and carbonate apatite are two common mineral deposits that may play an important role in the formation of USE. Moreover, Corynebacterium also has certain adhesion characteristics [43, 44]. Escherichia-Shigella is regarded as a conditional pathogen which triggers infection and activates immune and inflammatory responses [45–47]. Moreover, it may be associated with infection in patients with ureteral stents [11]. At present, there is relatively little research on the role of Dermabacter in the urinary system. Previous studies suggest that it may be related to inflammation [48, 49]. Herein, it is speculated that Staphylococcus, Escherichia-Shigella, Dermabacter and Corynebacterium may play important roles in the formation process of USE, and the specific mechanisms deserve further investigation.

At the genus level, there were 15 significantly different microbiota species between USE-U and USE-E groups. In addition to Staphylococcus, Dermabacter and Escherichia-Shigella described above, it also includes various microbial communities such as Streptococcus. Streptococcus is widely distributed in the human body and plays an important role in a variety of infectious diseases [50]. Streptococcus has certain adhesion ability and can be effectively colonized on human tissues and stents [51–53]. Although the urine microbiota may initially colonize on the stent, significant differences in the microenvironment between the stent surface and the urine body, coupled with varying competition and selection mechanisms faced by bacteria during survival, result in differences in the microbiota between encrustation and urine. These different microbiota in encrustation and urine may play a more direct and critical role in the occurrence and development of USE.

PICRUSt analysis showed that urine microbiota may be involved in the regulation of USE formation by targeting multiple functional pathways, such as defense mechanisms, amino acid transport and metabolism, L-rhamnose degradation I, cytochrome aa3-600 (menaquinol oxidase) and monobactam biosynthesis. Under normal circumstances, immune components in urine can recognize and bind to pathogens such as bacteria attached to the surface of the stent, activate the complement system, and promote phagocytic clearance of bacteria by phagocytic cells. However, when ureteral stent is implanted, the formation of biofilm can interfere with this defense process [13]. Amino acids are essential for immune cell function [54], and amino acid deficiency or impaired transport may lead to impaired immune cell function, reducing the body’s ability to clear bacteria from the stent surface and increasing the risk of encrustation. In addition, L-rhamnose degradation I and cytochrome aa3-600 (menaquinol oxidase) are also involved in affecting the function of the human immune system. Urinary system infection is one of the important risk factors for USE [6]. Monobactam biosynthesis is closely associated with urinary tract infections [55]. At present, there is a lack of research on the role of L-rhamnose degradation I and cytochrome aa3-600 (menaquinol oxidase) in urinary system infections and USE. The results of this study showed that defense mechanisms, amino acid transport and metabolism, L-rhamnose degradation I, cytochrome aa3-600 (menaquinol oxidase) and monobactam biosynthesis were significantly different between USNE-U and USE-U groups. It is speculated that they play an important regulatory role in USE, and the specific mechanism is worthy of further study. However, PICRUSt analysis has its inherent limitations. PICRUSt’s functional prediction is based on inference from a small gene fragment, which may lead to inaccuracies in the prediction of the full range of functional pathways related to urine microbiota. Therefore, while the current PICRUSt analysis results suggest potential links between the identified pathways and USE, future research should incorporate more direct experimental validation and potentially more comprehensive functional profiling methods to corroborate and refine these initial insights.

Random forest is a powerful machine learning algorithm, which is widely used in many fields such as the construction of diagnostic classification models [56–58]. The main purpose of the 10-fold cross-validation process is to evaluate the performance of the model [59]. Moreover, overfitting of the model can be effectively prevented [60, 61]. In this study, random forest classification model was constructed based on urine microbiota to distinguish between USNE and USE. ROC results suggest that this classification model had excellent diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.974). It is implied that the classification model can accurately classify USNE and USE, but further validation in clinical samples is needed.

In this study, a variety of microbiota were identified that may be involved in USE formation, such as Staphylococcus, Escherichia-Shigella, Dermabacter, Corynebacterium and Streptococcus. In addition, the functions of microbiota that may be involved in USE formation were also discovered. Furthermore, a random forest classification model with excellent diagnostic accuracy was constructed based on urine microbiota to distinguish USNE and USE. In summary, this study provides a new perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of USE, which may contribute to early clinical diagnosis and treatment of USE. However, this study also has certain limitations. Firstly, although the results suggest that urine microbiota may have potential value in the formation of USE, the specific molecular mechanism of microbiota affecting the occurrence of USE has not been explored in depth. This limits our comprehensive understanding of this pathological process. Secondly, the random forest diagnostic model based on urine microbiota has not been verified in a large number of clinical samples. Thirdly, the sample size in this study was relatively small, which may limit the understanding of the relationship between urine microbiota and USE. With a small sample, the representativeness of the unique population studied may be compromised, which may hinder the ability to produce conclusive results that can be generalized to a wider range. Therefore, a large number of clinical samples need to be collected for verification in the later stage.
