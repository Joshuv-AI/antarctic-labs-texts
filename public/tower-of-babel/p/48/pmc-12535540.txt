Heterologous expression and optimization of the antimicrobial peptide acidocin 4356 in Komagataella phaffii to target Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Authors: Ali Akbari, Mahbubeh Talaee, Mohammad Mir-Derikvand, Navid Dad, Sima Modiri, Fatemeh Saadati, Negin Noorbakhsh, Hossein Shahbani Zahiri, Hojatollah Vali, Kambiz Akbari Noghabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, particularly Pseudomonas aeruginosa, pose a serious global health threat due to their increasing prevalence and limited therapeutic options. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) offer promising alternatives to traditional antibiotics, yet their large-scale application remains constrained by high production costs and technical challenges. This research sought to develop a yeast-based system for the cost-efficient synthesis of acidocin 4356 (ACD), an antimicrobial peptide proven effective against P. aeruginosa. A codon-optimized ACD gene was cloned into the pPICZα-A expression vector and integrated into the Komagataella phaffii (formerly Pichia pastoris) GS115 genome. Colony PCR confirmed successful integration, and specific transformants demonstrated expression of the 6 × His-ECS-rACD fusion protein, as verified by SDS-PAGE and dot blot analysis. After Ni–NTA chromatography and enterokinase digestion, rACD was found at ~ 20 kDa instead of 8.3 kDa, suggesting oligomerization or post-translational modifications. Response surface methodology determined the optimal temperature, pH, and methanol concentration for peptide synthesis. Under optimal circumstances (21 °C, pH 6.24, and 1.089% methanol), rACD synthesis increased by 34.12% over baseline conditions (30 °C, pH 6, 1% methanol). AlphaFold structural modeling identified three α-helices in high-confidence regions, implicated in bacterial membrane disruption. Antimicrobial assays demonstrated potent rACD activity against P. aeruginosa, yielding a 58.29% reduction in growth at 150 µg/mL and MIC50 and MIC90 values of 143.04 and 320.64 µg/mL, respectively. These findings underscore K. phaffii as a robust platform for AMP production and highlight rACD’s therapeutic potential as an effective agent against MDR P. aeruginosa, warranting further investigation into its clinical and industrial applications.Graphical Abstract Key points• Developing a novel K. phaffii strain for heterologous expression supports efficient rACD peptide production.• Optimized conditions boosted expression yield by 34.12% above the reference fermentation settings.• Recombinant acidocin suppressed Pseudomonas aeruginosa growth by 58%, indicating anti-MDR activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13584-1.

Introduction

The increasing incidence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria signifies a major worldwide health concern, requiring the immediate exploration of new antimicrobial strategies (Chinemerem Nwobodo et al. 2022). Multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, especially those belonging to the ESKAPE group (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species), represent a substantial threat to public health due to their role in hospital-acquired infections that exhibit escalating resistance to multiple antibiotics (de Oliveira et al. 2020). P. aeruginosa has garnered significant attention due to its inherent resistance mechanisms, prominent biofilm development, and capacity to induce serious infections in immunocompromised individuals and patients using medical devices such as intravenous or urethral catheters (Ishizaki et al. 2023; Ostapska et al. 2022). These infections are notoriously challenging to treat and are associated with elevated morbidity and mortality rates. The worldwide ramifications of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) are significant, with projections indicating that by 2050, AMR may lead to 10 million fatalities per year (Angst et al. 2025; Diaz Caballero et al. 2023; Luo et al. 2022). This alarming trend highlights the urgent need to discover and develop novel antimicrobial agents (Xu et al. 2021). Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have emerged as promising alternatives in this context (Cao et al. 2018; Xuan et al. 2023). AMPs are naturally occurring molecules that form part of the innate immune systems of various organisms. They have broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, target bacterial membranes, and are less likely to induce resistance (Cao et al. 2018; Li et al. 2022). Their amphipathic and cationic characteristics enable significant contact with negatively charged bacterial membranes, frequently resulting in fast bactericidal activity (Chen et al. 2023; Hoelscher et al. 2022). Despite their therapeutic potential, AMPs present considerable barriers to widespread clinical application, owing to high manufacturing costs and the technical complexity involved in large-scale synthesis (Cao et al. 2018; Chaudhary et al. 2023). Conventional chemical synthesis methods, such as solid-phase peptide synthesis, can be prohibitively expensive, ranging from $100 to $600 per gram, and often face limitations with longer peptides and hydrophobic peptides (Chaudhary et al. 2023). Recombinant DNA (rDNA) technology offers a practical alternative, allowing for cost-effective, scalable production of AMPs in heterologous expression systems while preserving or even improving their biological properties via molecular engineering (Roca-Pinilla et al. 2022; Unver and Dagci 2024). Selecting an appropriate host system is critical to achieving high yields and functional integrity of rAMPs (Roca-Pinilla et al. 2022). Recombinant proteins have been produced using a variety of biological systems, including bacteria, yeast, and mammalian hosts (Berlec and Štrukelj 2013; Zhang et al. 2021). Escherichia coli has historically been the most commonly employed bacterial host due to its fast growth and well-defined genetics (Berlec and Strukelj 2013). However, AMPs’ intrinsic lethality to bacterial hosts and susceptibility to proteolytic degradation pose considerable hurdles (Berlec and Štrukelj 2013; Cao et al. 2018; Du et al. 2022). Although fusion protein techniques have been developed to mitigate toxicity and degradation, these efforts frequently produce inadequate yields, ranging from 1 to 30 mg/L (Cao et al. 2018). Yeast hosts, notably Komagataella phaffii (formerly Pichia pastoris), have significant benefits over bacterial systems for rAMP generation. Furthermore, K. phaffii is resistant to AMP-mediated toxicity, allows for high cell-density fermentation, and has strong genetic stability (Unver and Dagci 2024). It is appealing for industrial-scale applications due to its effective secretion routes, which make downstream processing easier, and the FDA’s Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) designation (Unver and Dagci 2024). Additionally, K. phaffii can be grown in low-cost, nearly protein-free substrates, which lowers production costs and lowers the possibility of contamination (Unver and Dagci 2024). Crucially, the carbon supply tightly regulates the induction of the methanol-inducible alcohol oxidase 1 (AOX1) promoter in K. phaffii, which enables the high-level production of heterologous proteins. Protein production can be tailored to fulfill specific industrial needs based on the methanol utilization phenotype, which can be Mut+ (methanol utilization plus), MutS (methanol utilization slow), or Mut− (methanol utilization minus) (Unver and Dagci 2024). Furthermore, the generation of functionally active recombinant peptides is guaranteed by the system’s ability to undergo post-translational changes (such as glycosylation and acylation) (Unver and Dagci 2024). In our prior research, we identified and characterized acidocin 4356 (ACD), an antimicrobial peptide isolated from Lactobacillus acidophilus ATCC 4356 (Modiri et al. 2020). ACD displayed potent activity against P. aeruginosa through membrane disruption, reduction of virulence factors, and effective biofilm degradation. Moreover, its stability under physiological conditions and minimal hemolytic activity underscore its potential as a therapeutic agent. However, the high cost and complexities of traditional AMP extraction motivate the pursuit of alternative production strategies.

Given these encouraging findings, our current study aims to expand on this knowledge by producing rACD using K. phaffii GS115. This research focuses on cloning and optimizing the expression of rACD in K. phaffii through heterologous expression, offering a potentially cost-effective and scalable approach for AMP production. Response surface methodology (RSM) was utilized to optimize critical physicochemical parameters—temperature, pH, and methanol concentration—to improve the synthesis and expression efficiency of rACD and establish a reliable production process. Ultimately, this work seeks to contribute a practical solution to the pressing need for new antimicrobial agents capable of overcoming resistance in challenging pathogens, particularly P. aeruginosa.

Materials and methods

Transformation of E. coli

Based on codon usage data, a codon-optimized ACD gene sequence for K. phaffii was designed, synthesized, and cloned into the methanol-inducible expression vector pPICZα-A (GenScript Corporation) (Fig. 1). The entire nucleotide sequence of the codon-optimized ACD gene, along with the corresponding peptide sequence, is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Following standard procedures, competent E. coli DH5α cells were generated using the calcium chloride method, and the resultant recombinant vector harboring the ACD gene was introduced into these cells (Froger and Hall 2007). Yeast transformation and screening of recombinant strains

The restriction enzyme SacI (Thermo Fisher), which targets the AOX1 gene region within the vector, was used to linearize the purified recombinant pPICZα-A-ACD plasmid. The linearized DNA was introduced into K. phaffii GS115 via electroporation (2500 V, 200 Ω, 25 µF), allowing homologous recombination with the AOX1 gene locus. The transformed colonies were screened on YPDS medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% glucose, 1 M sorbitol, 2% agar) supplemented with 100 µg/mL Zeocin (Invitrogen) and incubated at 30 °C. Gene integration at the AOX1 locus can occur via either single- or double-crossover events. In single-crossover events, the recombinant gene is introduced upstream or downstream, while the original AOX1 gene is maintained, resulting in Mut + colonies that can efficiently utilize methanol. Double-crossover events, on the other hand, substitute the recombinant gene for the AOX1 gene, which causes MutS colonies to use methanol more slowly due to the absence of AOX1 (Unver and Dagci 2024). In this study, Mut + colonies emerged due to gene fusion, indicating that the pPICZα-ACD gene cassette carried a copy of the AOX1 gene, enabling these colonies to consume methanol efficiently. Colonies with the ACD gene construct resistant to zeocin were cultivated on YPD solid medium to verify effective integration. The presence of the 831-bp coding region was confirmed by colony PCR using AOX1 5′ (GACTGGTTCCAATTGACAAGC) and AOX1 3′ (GCAAATGGCATTCTGACATCC) primers, carried out at an annealing temperature of 54 °C. Positive colonies were those that amplified the 831-bp product effectively. The Taq polymerase master mix, proteins, primers, and DNA markers used in this investigation were all obtained from SinaClon Co.

Heterologous expression of 6 × His-ECS-rACD peptide

Recombinant K. phaffii colonies were cultured in 25 mL of BMGY medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 100 mM potassium phosphate at pH 6, 4 × 10⁻5% biotin, and 1% glycerol) at 30 °C and 200 rpm until an OD600 of 3–4 was reached, typically after 16–18 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 5 min, and the yeast pellet was resuspended in BMMY medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 100 mM potassium phosphate at pH 6, 4 × 10⁻5% biotin, 0.5% methanol) to adjust the OD600 to 1. Methanol was added daily to a final concentration of 1% for 4 consecutive days to induce expression under the AOX1 promoter. Protein expression was analyzed by gradient SDS-PAGE (4–20%), as described by Modiri et al. (Modiri et al. 2020), followed by silver nitrate staining (Gallagher and Sasse 2021). A dot blot assay, using an HRP anti-His tag antibody (Cytomatingene Co.), further confirmed the presence of the 6 × His-ECS-rACD peptide.

Purification of 6 × His-ECS-rACD

After induction, the culture supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min. Buffer exchange was performed by placing the supernatant in a dialysis bag (MWCO: 3500) and dialyzing it against binding buffer (300 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8) at a 1:100 peptide-to-buffer ratio. This process was carried out overnight in a cold room to ensure efficient buffer exchange. The dialyzed supernatant was then purified using a Ni–NTA column. Despite having the same base composition (300 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaH2PO4, pH 8), the binding buffer, washing buffer, and elution buffer had varying imidazole concentrations (10 mM for the binding buffer, 20 mM for the washing buffer, and 250 mM for the elution solution).

Cleavage of the enterokinase protease region and release of rACD

Enterokinase (also referred to as enteropeptidase) is a serine protease present in the mucosa of the duodenum and jejunum. Enterokinase converts trypsinogen into the active form of trypsin by recognizing the DDDDK sequence and cutting the peptide bond after the amino acid lysine (Babcock et al. 2023). The purified 6 × His-ECS-rACD peptide was buffer-exchanged to eliminate imidazole before incubating with enterokinase enzyme. For the reaction, 43 µL of peptide solution was mixed with 5 µL of cleavage buffer (200 mM Tris–HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM CaCl₂, pH 7.4) and 2 µL of enterokinase enzyme. The mixture was incubated at 22 °C for 16 h. Subsequently, the mixture was passed through a Ni–NTA column to separate the cleaved peptide. The residue collected was then buffer-exchanged against phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 6.5 using a dialysis bag (MWCO: 3500).

In silico structural modeling of rACD peptide

The Quark web server and DOPE analysis were previously employed to assess the structure of the ACD peptide (Modiri et al. 2020). In this study, structural modeling was performed using AlphaFold. Among several proposed models, one was selected based on factors such as identification level, predicted aligned error (PAE), high-scoring segment pair (HSP) score, and per-residue confidence score (pLDDT) (Jumper et al. 2021; Varadi et al. 2024). The selected model was visualized and analyzed for amino acid hydrophobicity and peptide structure using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2024 Client software. The peptide’s physicochemical properties and predicted post-translational modifications were analyzed using the ProtParam tool from the ExPASy database and the peptide tool from the Protpi program online.

Evaluation and optimization of factors influencing rACD expression using RSM design

The cultures were first cultivated at pH 6 in BMMY medium, supplemented with 1% methanol every 24 h, and incubated at 30 °C. After methanol induction, samples were taken every day for eight days to evaluate the total protein concentration (g/L), wet cell weight (g/L), and OD600. The induction period was eight days since the cells would have entered the death phase after that. Three important parameters (temperature, pH, and methanol concentration) were chosen based on relevant studies to assess their impacts and interactions with the efficiency and expression level of the rACD peptide. Each factor was examined at three specified levels based on prior research on P. pastoris, the AOX1 promoter, and the expression system. RSM with central composite design (CCD) was used to design 20 experiments, including 6 central points (Table 1). The primary responses were OD600, wet cell weight, and total secreted protein. Antimicrobial activity

The ESKAPE strains used in this investigation—Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 (sequence accession no. CP009072), Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 (CP009361), Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700603 (CP014696), Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 19606 (CP045110), Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853 (CP015117), and Enterococcus faecium ATCC 51559 (NZ_JSVT00000000)—were cultivated overnight in Müller-Hinton broth at 37 °C and 180 rpm, adhering to the protocols established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (Chaudhary et al. 2023). The cultures were then diluted to an OD600 of 0.01 in fresh Müller-Hinton broth and transferred to a 96-well plate. A BCA assay was used to assess the different concentrations of rACD (50, 100, and 150 µg/mL), while control wells did not receive any peptide. After 18 h of incubation at 37 °C, bacterial growth was measured at OD600 to test rACD’s inhibitory effects, especially against P. aeruginosa. MIC50 and MIC90 values were calculated using the trendline equation. Each assay was conducted in triplicate for three independent experiments, and results were presented as means ± SD.

Statistical analysis

Means and standard deviations were calculated using SPSS software version 26.0. Graphics were designed by BioRender.com. Optimization experiments (RSM) were designed and modeled using Design-Expert version 13 software. The graphic diagrams of this study were designed and drawn using SigmaPlot software version 15.0. ImageJ software version 1.54d was used to compare protein bands.

Results

Integration of the ACD gene cassette into the K. phaffii GS115 genome

The integration of the recombinant pPICZα-ACD gene cassette into the K. phaffii GS115 genome was confirmed through colony PCR. The integration, achieved via electroporation and homologous recombination in the AOX1 gene region, verified the presence of the target gene in recombinant colonies. The recombinant colonies displayed the Mut + phenotype, indicating a single-crossover event at the AOX1 gene. This event maintained the original AOX1 gene while incorporating the recombinant cassette either upstream or downstream. The retained AOX1 promoter allowed effective methanol consumption. PCR analysis was performed utilizing the AOX1 5′ and AOX1 3′ primers to verify the integration’s correctness. The amplification of the 2.2-kb sequence unique to the AOX1 gene in the wild-type K. phaffii strain GS115 served as a control. Expression and purification of 6 × His-ECS-rACD peptide

Recombinant K. phaffii colonies harboring the ACD gene were cultured for 4 days in culture media containing 1% methanol to induce peptide expression. Gradient SDS-PAGE analysis of culture supernatants revealed a unique protein band (~ 25–35 kDa) in the fermentation supernatant of colony 1, but no band was observed in the control strain’s supernatant or colony 2 (Fig. 2b). Although colony 2 contained the recombinant gene, its protein profile was indistinguishable from the control, implying improper gene integration or suboptimal expression, further confirming the absence of rACD expression (Fig. 2b). Dot blot analysis using an anti-6 × His antibody was performed to validate protein production (Fig. 2d). The 6 × His-tagged control protein (lane 1) and the fermentation supernatant from colony 1 (lane 4) demonstrated positive signals, confirming the presence of the 6 × His-ECS-rACD peptide. However, no signal was observed for the control strain (lane 2) or colony 2 (lane 3), supporting the hypothesis that colony 2 failed to express the target protein (Fig. 2d). To further validate the production of the peptide, the supernatant was purified using Ni–NTA chromatography (Fig. 2c). Gradient SDS-PAGE analysis, stained with silver nitrate, showed that the 6 × His-ECS-rACD peptide mass was within the expected molecular mass range of 25–35 kDa. Following digestion with enterokinase, the purified peptide was analyzed again via SDS-PAGE. The removal of the 6 × His-ECS tag resulted in the detection of rACD with an approximate molecular mass of 20 kDa (Fig. 2c). The findings confirm the successful expression and purification of rACD from colony 1, highlighting its potential for further application.

ACD structure

Structural modeling of ACD predicted three alpha helices, with two helices exhibiting high predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores ranging from 70 to 90, indicating a high-confidence prediction (Fig. 3a, b). These alpha helices play a crucial role in the peptide’s ability to bind to the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) of Gram-negative bacteria (Scott et al. 2000). The pLDDT confidence analysis revealed regions in blue with high structural reliability, while regions in yellow and orange reflected lower confidence. The predicted aligned error (PAE) plot illustrates the spatial arrangement and alignment reliability between residues. A low PAE score between amino acids of distinct alpha helices (residues 25–44 and 55–79) indicates minimal prediction error and supports the distinct spatial localization of these helices (Fig. 3c). This structural arrangement is significant for ACD’s antimicrobial activity. Surface hydrophobicity analysis further confirmed the peptide’s interaction potential with bacterial membranes. Areas with high hydrophobicity, depicted in orange, are concentrated on the surface, emphasizing ACD’s capability to disrupt membrane integrity (Fig. 3d). Optimization of 6 × His-ECS-rACD peptide synthesis

The growth kinetics of K. phaffii transformants were monitored for eight days under standard culture conditions (30 °C, pH 6, 1% methanol). Growth profiles were compared against the native K. phaffii GS115 strain under identical conditions to evaluate any differences in growth patterns. As shown in Fig. 4a, the cultures reached a maximum OD600 (9.27) on day 6, after which they entered stationary and death phases. Accordingly, all subsequent experiments used six days of induction for optimal growth. A total of 20 experiments, varying temperature, pH, and methanol concentration, were conducted to optimize peptide production (Table 1). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) indicated that these factors significantly impacted OD600, cell wet weight, and total protein yield, with similar patterns across all responses. To minimize redundancy, the ANOVA results based on OD600 are presented in Table 3. The resulting regression model achieved a high accuracy in describing the experimental data, with an R2 value of 99.8%, closely aligned with the adjusted R2 of 99.7%, and the predicted R2 of 99.04%, indicating strong predictive power. The model’s lack of fit was insignificant (p > 0.05), confirming its suitability for predicting optimal conditions. Antimicrobial properties of rACD

The antimicrobial activity of the rACD was assessed against the ESKAPE pathogens, with P. aeruginosa showing the highest sensitivity. Dose–response assays at rACD concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 µg/mL revealed a concentration-dependent reduction in P. aeruginosa growth (Fig. 6c). At 150 µg/mL, rACD reduced the proliferation of P. aeruginosa by 58.29% compared to the untreated control (normalized to 100%). Given its superior activity against P. aeruginosa, additional analyses were performed to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration required to inhibit 50% (MIC50) and 90% (MIC90) of bacterial growth. Using a concentration range of 50–350 µg/mL and logarithmic regression analysis, MIC50 and MIC90 values were calculated as 143.04 and 320.64 µg/mL, respectively (Fig. 6d). Comparison of these values with native ACD (Modiri et al. 2020) confirmed that rACD retains or surpasses the antimicrobial potency of the natural peptide (Table 4). Collectively, these results underscore rACD’s promise as a potent antimicrobial, particularly against MDR P. aeruginosa, and support continued investigation into its efficacy in targeting biofilm-associated infections. Discussion

Antibiotic resistance is a pressing global health concern, driven by the emergence of multidrug-resistant pathogens that challenge conventional therapeutic strategies (Nwobodo et al. 2022). Among these pathogens, P. aeruginosa is particularly concerning due to its intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms, which complicate treatment options (Langendonk et al. 2021). The increasing prevalence of MDR P. aeruginosa infections underscores the urgent need to explore alternative therapeutic strategies beyond conventional antibiotics (Xu et al. 2021). AMPs have garnered significant attention as promising candidates for combating antibiotic-resistant bacteria (Xuan et al. 2023). AMPs offer potential as therapeutics for infections caused by MDR pathogens, including P. aeruginosa (Xuan et al. 2023). However, their application in clinical settings is limited by challenges related to production, stability, and toxicity (Jiang et al. 2021). Various strategies have been explored for AMP production, including chemical synthesis, extraction from natural sources, or recombinant DNA technology (da Cunha et al. 2017). Chemical synthesis, while effective, is often costly and unsuitable for large-scale production (Marr et al. 2006). Extraction from natural sources is labor-intensive and yields small quantities of peptides, limiting its feasibility for commercial applications (de Oliveira et al. 2022). Recombinant production has emerged as a viable alternative, offering scalability, cost-effectiveness, and the potential for tailoring peptide properties (Wibowo and Zhao 2019). Producing AMPs in prokaryotic systems faces challenges like protease degradation, improper folding, and low yields. Yeast systems, such as P. pastoris and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, offer advantages like efficient secretion, post-translational modifications, and resistance to proteolytic degradation. Additionally, yeast is safe (GRAS) and ideal for large-scale recombinant AMP production (Unver and Dagci 2024). In our previous study conducted by Modiri et al. (Modiri et al. 2020), the native ACD peptide purified from Lactobacillus acidophilus showed potent antimicrobial, anti-biofilm, and anti-virulence activities against P. aeruginosa. The results highlighted the remarkable potential of ACD as a candidate for further exploration in therapeutic and clinical applications due to its broad-spectrum efficacy and promising safety profile. Building on these findings, the current study aimed to overcome the limitations associated with native production by expressing ACD using recombinant technology in yeast.

A key outcome of this work is the successful integration of the ACD gene cassette into the K. phaffii GS115 genome, confirmed by colony PCR and characterized by the Mut + phenotype. This confirms the reliability of homologous recombination at the AOX1 locus, an approach frequently employed for high-level recombinant protein expression in yeast. Comparable findings were reported by Meng et al. (Meng et al. 2017), who employed a similar electroporation and integration technique to stably express the PaDef gene cassette in P. pastoris. Chen et al. (2011) also utilized methanol-inducible expression for hPAB-β peptide production, underscoring the essential role of the AOX1 promoter in achieving tight induction and minimal background expression.

Notably, we observed differential expression profiles among transformant colonies, with only some colonies producing detectable levels of the 6 × His-ECS-rACD peptide. This underscores the influence of integration efficiency, gene copy number, and potential genetic or epigenetic variations among transformants, which can lead to variable expression levels. Other investigations on yeast-based AMP production have demonstrated similar variations (Chen et al. 2011; Meng et al. 2017). Ensuring consistent, high-yielding transformants often requires screening multiple colonies to identify clones with optimal expression characteristics, which is a critical step for large-scale applications.

Our optimization results further highlight the critical importance of culture conditions, specifically temperature, pH, and methanol concentration, in maximizing recombinant AMP production yields in K. phaffii, a finding that is well aligned with prior works on yeast-based expression systems (Batra et al. 2014; Jafari et al. 2011). Among these factors, pH emerges as a particularly influential factor, influencing not only yeast cell viability and growth but also protein stability and proteolytic activity (Batra et al. 2014). While many studies default to a pH range of 5–6, high-pH environments have also been leveraged to inhibit certain classes of proteases, thereby improving overall yields (Batra et al. 2014; Inan et al. 1999; Xie et al. 2005). Conversely, some studies have adopted a lower pH to suppress serine proteases (Charoenrat et al. 2013; Jahic et al. 2003), illustrating that the “optimal” pH is context-dependent and may vary substantially with the type of target protein and host strain. Temperature also plays a critical role in recombinant protein production by influencing both protease activity and protein folding (De Brabander et al. 2023). Although lower temperatures might slow down proteolysis and increase the stability of proteins, these advantages must be weighed against the necessity of maintaining high enough yeast growth rates, which are frequently attained close to the organism’s ideal growth temperature (De Brabander et al. 2023). Therefore, an intermediate temperature, rather than a sharp reduction, frequently provides the best compromise between reducing proteolytic damage and sustaining robust cell growth. Methanol concentration is another critical driver of protein expression in K. phaffii, given the induction mechanism governed by the AOX1 promoter (Batra et al. 2014). While a minimum concentration of 0.5% methanol is often required to sustain expression, excessive methanol can lead to the accumulation of toxic byproducts such as formaldehyde and hydrogen peroxide, which, in turn, compromise cell viability (Batra et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2000). Consequently, establishing an optimal methanol range is essential for securing high expression levels without incurring methanol-induced cytotoxicity. By systematically balancing these three factors and quantifying their interactions through statistical modeling, we achieved a notable (34.12%) improvement in 6 × His-ECS-rACD peptide expression compared to baseline conditions. Our high model fit (R2 = 99.8%) further confirms the reliability of this multifactorial approach for predicting and scaling up protein yields. These findings mirror results reported by other researchers who have also harnessed P. pastoris under methanol-inducible systems for recombinant peptide production. For example, while rPaDef expression was optimized at 1.5% methanol with a shorter induction time (Meng et al. 2017), and Wang et al. (2008) focused solely on the impact of methanol without considering temperature or pH, our study demonstrates that the interaction of pH, temperature, and methanol concentration can be equally, if not more, important for maximizing yield. Taken together, these observations highlight that successful AMP production in yeast depends on the careful coordination of multiple physiological and biochemical factors. By using a multifactorial optimization approach, it is possible to fully leverage the robustness and scalability of K. phaffii for industrial-scale recombinant peptide production. In addition, employing the CCD in our experiments was crucial for elucidating both individual and interactive effects of these culture parameters. The coefficient diagram revealed that pH exerted the strongest impact on the optimal production point, demonstrating an inverse relationship between pH and OD600, thereby suggesting that as pH increased, overall cell growth (and hence recombinant yield) decreased. Interestingly, the interactions of temperature × pH and temperature × methanol were identified as the most influential on peptide production, underscoring the importance of controlling temperature in conjunction with pH and methanol concentration to optimize growth and expression. By contrast, temperature alone had only a minor direct effect on rACD yield, implying that its primary role might be tied to moderating the effects of pH and methanol rather than acting independently. Collectively, these insights confirm that a multifactorial strategy, which examines and fine-tunes the interplay among temperature, pH, and methanol, is vital for boosting rAMP production in K. phaffii. This approach not only enables a deeper understanding of each parameter’s role but also provides a robust framework for further scale-up and industrial applications.

Notably, rACD displayed a higher apparent molecular mass on SDS-PAGE than the theoretically predicted 8.3 kDa. This observation is consistent with multiple reports highlighting how AMPs can migrate aberrantly in SDS-PAGE due to their unique amino acid compositions, which often include polar and hydrophobic residues (Azari et al. 2020; Zhan et al. 2021). Furthermore, these peptides frequently form oligomeric structures when expressed heterologously at high expression levels, leading to bands that may appear two to four times larger than the predicted mass (Wang et al. 2019). This phenomenon is further documented in heterologous expression systems, particularly yeast, where post-translational modifications or altered folding may also contribute to larger apparent sizes (Lee et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2020). Cleavage of the 6 × His-ECS fusion tag reduced the rACD mass to ~ 20 kDa, indicating successful tag removal yet still exceeding the peptide’s theoretical size. Such observations underscore the importance of complementary analytical methods, such as mass spectrometry, to validate final peptide size and modifications.

Functionally, rACD peptide exhibited potent antimicrobial activity, particularly against P. aeruginosa, achieving a 58.29% reduction in proliferation at 150 µg/mL, and demonstrated MIC50 and MIC90 values of 143.04 and 320.64 µg/mL, respectively. Although these values are higher than those reported for the native form of ACD (79.43 and 128.22 µg/mL, respectively) (Modiri et al. 2020), rACD still displays effective inhibition against this notoriously resistant pathogen. The discrepancy may be attributed to altered peptide conformation or post-translational modifications in the yeast expression system. Recent findings (Talaee et al. 2025) further confirmed native ACD’s strong efficacy against A. baumannii (MIC50: 70.51, MIC90: 104.39 µg/mL) and P. aeruginosa (MIC50: 87.85, MIC90: 129.97 µg/mL), including significant biofilm disruption at half the MIC. Notably, co-culture models revealed minimal cytotoxicity to human cells, suggesting an encouraging therapeutic index under near-physiological conditions (Talaee et al. 2025). Collectively, these results underscore ACD’s broad-spectrum potential and highlight rACD as a promising agent, particularly against biofilm-associated MDR pathogens. Future research could explore rACD’s efficacy in more complex in vitro and in vivo models, targeting biofilm-associated infections.

The versatility of K. phaffii as a host for AMP expression is further supported by studies on other peptides like mytichitin-A (Meng et al. 2016). While mytichitin-A production was optimized only for methanol concentration and showed primarily Gram-positive activity (MIC values from 30.7 to > 500 μg/mL), rACD underwent multifactorial optimization (pH, temperature, and methanol) and demonstrated a broader spectrum of inhibition, including a significant impact against P. aeruginosa. These differences not only reflect the importance of robust optimization strategies but also emphasize the versatility of K. phaffii for producing functionally diverse AMPs.

In conclusion, the heterologous expression of ACD in K. phaffii GS115 underscores the feasibility of scaling up AMP production through an optimized, yeast-based platform. While native ACD has more bioactivity, the recombinant variant is more effectively generated under ideal yeast growing conditions, making it more suitable for commercial scale-up. These findings, the first extensive investigations into AMP production in yeast systems, shed light on the potential and the constraints of using heterologous expression for AMP development. Although our results highlight the value of yeast-based platforms, detailed structural studies remain crucial to fine-tune AMP expression, refine process parameters, and assess how physicochemical properties influence therapeutic efficacy. Moving forward, research should further evaluate alternative expression systems and delineate the structure–activity relationship of ACD, ultimately advancing the clinical and commercial potential of this promising antimicrobial agent in the fight against multidrug-resistant pathogens.
