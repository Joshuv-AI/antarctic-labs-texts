Comment on “Older Adults’ Lived Experience With Postoperative Symptoms at Home After Major Elective Surgery”: Opportunities for Nonpharmacologic Approaches
Authors: Nicole L. Bohr, Ted A. Skolarus, Neda Laiteerapong, Ryan Merkow
Published: 2024

Opioids were historically the preferred treatment of postsurgical pain, but an ongoing US opioid epidemic has demanded a change in practice. Moreover, opioids are known to cause harmful side effects that may limit surgical recovery, including reduced bowel motility, and physical function, and lead to acute cognitive issues such as delirium.1 The impact of opioids on surgical recovery is particularly relevant among older adults. Notably, surgical volumes in older adults in the United States are rapidly growing, including nearly 4 million surgical procedures performed annually among adults aged 65 years and older.2 Given these issues, there is a pressing need to address postoperative pain among older adults using alternative strategies.

In their impactful study in Annals of Surgery, Keny et al3 explored how postoperative symptoms after elective major surgery affected the quality of life among older adults. Through qualitative interviews, the investigators found that pain was the most common bothersome symptom impacting daily life and psychosocial well-being with 35% of participants still struggling with pain 3 months after surgery. Additionally, depressive symptoms emerged at 3 months in one-third of participants. Furthermore, patients largely reported feeling surprised at the intensity and duration of these symptoms. These themes point to the limitations of traditional postoperative pharmacologic pain management and the need to consider both surgical and nonsurgical influences on pain. We believe there should be more recognition and intentional implementation efforts focused on nonpharmacologic pain management strategies to improve surgical recovery among older adults.

There are extensive, evidence-based, nonpharmacologic pain management strategies relevant to surgery that can be better understood using the Biopsychosocial Model of Pain. This model postulates that individual experiences of pain are due to dynamic interactions between overlapping biological, psychological, and social/cultural factors that must be understood to provide an effective, holistic, individualized, and person-centered approach to pain management.4 Concerns emerging in Keny et al’s3 work demonstrate the need to more fully address those “other” factors that influence pain. Nonpharmacologic pain management techniques are well situated to fill in those gaps. For example, the impact of postoperative symptoms on activity and psychosocial well-being can be addressed through cognitive behavioral therapy approaches such as Acceptance and Commitment Therapy. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy is an evidence-based cognitive behavioral treatment long used for chronic pain that works by retraining a patient’s focus away from the noxious experiences and toward their reactions to them and the effect these reactions have on mental health and functioning. The results are an improved ability to re-engage in meaningful life activities and a decreased focus on pain.5 Recently, this approach demonstrated efficacy as a preventative intervention, decreasing pain and supporting opioid cessation in adults undergoing joint replacement surgery.6

Another nonpharmacologic pain management strategy, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), appears particularly effective for movement-evoked versus resting pain. This approach may be more suitable for older adults as it employs low-voltage electric currents to block the transmission of pain signals while also causing a release of endorphins. In a recent study following inguinal hernia repair, TENS use was associated with reduced pain and satisfaction with postsurgical care.7 This demonstrates that not only are nonpharmacologic pain management techniques such as TENS beneficial but they are also welcomed by patients. This holds true for other nonpharmacologic pain management approaches, with annual rates of physical therapy, massage therapy, and acupuncture to treat surgical pain significantly increasing since 2011.8

Aligned with Keny et al’s findings, patients are interested in alternative pain management approaches, including nonpharmacological management. For instance, 73% of patients in one study were willing to participate in an exercise program, with 70% interested in learning relaxation techniques.9 While a large proportion of older adults are interested in self-managing their health care, they are not necessarily given the opportunity. In that same study, only 16% and 4%, respectively, were offered the opportunity to fully participate in learning those modalities.9 Participants also believed that more education, support, and resources were needed for effective symptom management at home. This emphasizes the need to integrate training for these nonpharmacologic approaches into preoperative and inpatient care in addition to the overall request for pain education suggested by the patients interviewed in Keny et al.3

However, integration and implementation of nonpharmacologic pain management practices have not become routine standard of care. The lack of translation to clinical practice is multifactorial; this includes individual components, such as limited provider knowledge related to modalities and unclear expectations of patient needs, and larger system components such as inaccessibility of the equipment/training needed, inability to charge for those items, and inadequate time and/or infrastructure to provide these interventions in the appropriate time, context, and setting. In order to offer these evidence-based approaches and improve pain outcomes, we need to move beyond acceptability among patients, as this seems clear, and assess other implementation outcomes such as appropriateness, feasibility, effectiveness, and fidelity to these interventions among patients and providers alike.10

Overall, leveraging these nonpharmacologic pain management approaches among patients undergoing major elective surgery, particularly older adults, is an evidence-based and promising approach to decrease postoperative pain complications aligned with the major themes uncovered by Keny et al. Given the increasing amount of elective surgery among older adults and in the context of this study, patients would be better served if we understood how to effectively implement these modalities into routine practice.
