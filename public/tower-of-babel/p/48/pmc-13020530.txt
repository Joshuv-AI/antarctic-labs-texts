Using Network Analysis to Understand the Associations Between Menopausal Symptoms and Mental Health in Women With Intellectual Disabilities
Authors: Stephanie Corrigan, Philip McCallion, Mary McCarron, Jan de Vries, Éilish Burke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWomen with intellectual disabilities typically face high rates of mental health and communication difficulties. Given associations between menopause and mental health in the general population and the lack of research on this topic in women with intellectual disabilities, it is critical to investigate associations between menopause and mental health in this population.MethodCross‐sectional data were drawn from a cohort study in Ireland (n = 104). Measures included data on menopause, challenging behaviours and psychotropic medication. Associations between menopausal symptoms and mental health were examined through regression and network analysis.ResultsVasomotor symptoms, fatigue, and mood changes were associated with menopause‐specific anxiety, which was further linked to challenging behaviour and psychotropic medication use.ConclusionsMenopausal symptoms were associated with mental health challenges, which, in turn, could influence challenging behaviour and psychotropic medication use in this population; implying that menopause‐specific mental health symptoms may be overlooked and incorrectly treated.

Introduction

Historically, women with intellectual disabilities have been omitted from research on sexual and reproductive health (Earle et al. 2024; Greenwood and Wilkinson 2013). Despite the pervasive health inequalities faced by this population (Burke et al. 2023; O'Leary et al. 2018), they continue to be excluded from research on topics such as menopause (Anto et al. 2025; Corrigan et al. 2025; Earle et al. 2024; Moore et al. 2023), resulting in deficits in the evidence base, which can lead to poorer health outcomes due to their specific medical needs (Robertson et al. 2021; Spong and Bianchi 2018).

Women with intellectual disabilities typically face higher rates of mental health conditions (Adams and Matson 2015; Cooper et al. 2007), poor communication (Smith et al. 2020) and elevated levels of psychotropic medication use (Odalović et al. 2024). Despite this, the mental health effects of menopause in women with intellectual disabilities are yet to be explored in detail (Corrigan et al. 2025; Langer‐Shapland et al. 2023). However, some progress has been reported in recent years. A systematic review conducted by Anto et al. (2025) exploring women's experiences of menopause in the UK included women with intellectual disabilities, promoting their necessary inclusion in mainstream conversations about sexual and reproductive health (Wiseman and Ferrie 2020). Furthermore, a recent qualitative study by Langer‐Shapland et al. (2023) provided valuable insights into the menopausal experiences of women with intellectual disabilities, highlighting the significance of knowledge, the experience of heightened irritability and the perceived need to manage menopausal symptoms independently. While such advancements are underway, however, other available menopause research is dated and in danger of lacking relevance for new generations of women with intellectual disabilities (Corrigan et al. 2025; Earle et al. 2024).

There has been a recent surge in menopause research in the general population showcased in prominent, high‐impact publications (Brown et al. 2024; Martin‐Key et al. 2024, 2023). Recent mixed methods research on the general population, suggests that menopause‐specific mental health symptoms are being overlooked (Martin‐Key et al. 2023), despite extensive evidence suggesting that menopause can heighten mental health difficulties (de Kruif et al. 2016).

In the general population, menopausal symptoms have been studied using ‘network analysis’ (Martin‐Key et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2023). Network analysis offers a unique approach to investigate the complex interactions of large symptom networks (Hevey 2018). Zhao et al. (2023) used this technique to explore symptomatic relationships that arise during menopause in Chinese women. It was found that the most central or influential symptoms of menopause were anxiety, vaginal dryness, depressive mood and irritability. Following on from this, Martin‐Key et al. (2024) used network analysis to examine the associations between menopause and depressive symptoms in British women. Once again, ‘feeling depressed’ was found to be one of the most central reported symptoms of menopause. Overall, it would seem that there is a link between menopause and vasomotor symptoms, which in turn are associated with increased levels of anxiety, depression (Llaneza et al. 2012), anger and irritability (Harper et al. 2022). These psychological impacts vary cross‐culturally depending on how distressing the symptoms are perceived to be (Melby et al. 2011).

It is not unlikely that these symptoms would also affect women with intellectual disabilities. Yet, as they may find symptoms confusing or distressing due to lack of understanding (Langer‐Shapland et al. 2023), the impact on their mental health may be even more pronounced and detrimental (Taggart et al. 2010). Communication difficulties (Smith et al. 2020) and polypharmacy (Odalović et al. 2024) may further exacerbate the impact of menopause with the potential to reduce quality of life. Atypical presentation of symptoms could possibly result in challenging behaviours, such as aggressive destructive behaviours occurring due to heightened irritability, which could lead to diagnostic overshadowing through misattribution of symptoms to a person's intellectual disability and not to menopause (Langer‐Shapland et al. 2023). Furthermore, it is possible that diagnostic overshadowing underscored by lack of available research and knowledge could result in incorrect treatments (e.g., prescription of psychotropic medication to treat mental health symptoms) as opposed to appropriate evidence‐based menopausal treatments tailored to the needs of this population (Corrigan et al. 2025; Spong and Bianchi 2018). Considering the lack of existing evidence on this front, it is timely to investigate this issue.

Study Aim

The limited available literature indicates that menopause‐specific symptoms could have adverse effects on the mental health of women with intellectual disabilities leading to potential difficulties regarding challenging behaviours, diagnostic overshadowing and psychotropic medication use. Therefore, the current study set out to examine any associations between menopausal symptoms and mental health concerns in women with intellectual disabilities.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This is a cross‐sectional study using data from a longitudinal cohort study which examines predictors of healthy ageing in a representative sample of people with intellectual disabilities over the age of 40 in Ireland (McCarron et al. 2023). Each iteration of the study is called a ‘Wave’ with data collected at 3‐year intervals. Please see (Ryan et al. 2023) for more information regarding study methodology.

For the current study, cross‐sectional data were drawn from the most recent iteration of the longitudinal cohort study (N = 762). A representative sub‐sample (n = 104) of menopausal women with intellectual disabilities who had reported experiencing menopausal symptoms were drawn from the larger sample for inclusion in the current study (see, Figure 1).

Flowchart of study sample.

Data Collection

Data collection took place from 2022 to 2023. Data for the study were collected in two mediums. Participants were invited to complete a pre‐interview questionnaire (PIQ) which was posted to participants a minimum of 1 week in advance of the interview to allow sufficient time for participants to complete with support if required. The PIQ was collected on the scheduled interview day by the field researcher who subsequently engaged participants in a computer‐assisted personal interview (CAPI). Both the PIQ and CAPI capture a comprehensive overview of the health and wellbeing of the older adult with an intellectual disability based on the underpinning conceptual framework of the study, which included measures on women's physical and mental health for use in the current study (insert identifiable reference to study methodology). Inclusion of participants from all levels of intellectual disability was achieved by using three interview scenarios: firstly, self‐report interviews could be conducted on a one‐to‐one basis directly with participants; secondly, participants could opt to have a support person present to assist with their self‐report interview; or finally, for participants who were unable to self‐report, assent was obtained from proxy respondents on their behalf and the interview was conducted with a close proxy respondent stipulated to have known the participant for more than 6 months.

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the Faculty of Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee at Trinity College Dublin (Ref: 220204) and all intellectual disability services involved in the study. A consent declaration from the Health Research Consent Declaration Committee (HRCDC) was obtained to ensure inclusion of participants with all levels of intellectual disability.

Measurement and Assessment Tools

A previous scoping review has identified three main areas for further exploration linked to menopause‐specific mental health in this population: changes in mood; lack of reporting of psychological symptoms and risk of diagnostic overshadowing (Corrigan et al. 2025). Based on these findings, three main outcome measures were selected to determine any associations between menopausal symptoms and mental health: menopause‐specific anxiety, challenging behaviours, and psychotropic medication use.

Menopausal Status and Symptoms

Menopausal status and symptoms were determined through a short suite of survey questions about menopause (see, Table S1). These questions were adapted from a comparable cohort study conducted in the general population (Scarlett et al. 2025) whose survey items are based on items from the Health and Retirement study (Sonnega et al. 2014). This outcome was measured using self‐reported survey questions rather than a previously validated scale, as to date, no menopause assessment scales or tools have been adapted for use in women with intellectual disabilities.

It has been previously reported in previous publications using data from the cohort study that women with intellectual disabilities have poor awareness of the menopausal transition (Burke et al. 2023; Ryan et al. 2025), which is widely corroborated in the literature (Langer‐Shapland et al. 2023; Moore et al. 2023; St. John et al. 2025). Therefore, if participants did not have knowledge about menopause, they would be supported by a carer or staff member to answer questions on menopause, as per the cohort study methodology (Ryan et al. 2023).

For the current study, ‘menopausal women’ were defined as those that answered ‘yes’ to either ‘gone through the menopause already’ or ‘currently going through the menopause’, that is, postmenopausal or perimenopausal women. Participants were subsequently asked to record any menopausal symptoms that they might have experienced including physical and mental health symptoms.

Please note that in the current study menopause‐specific mental health symptoms are distinct from doctor's diagnosed mental health symptoms. For example, menopause‐specific anxiety was identified as a menopausal symptom in the menopause‐specific questions and general doctor's diagnosed anxiety was identified when asked ‘Has a doctor ever told you that you have emotional, nervous or psychiatric problems?’.

Behaviour Problems Inventory—Short Form (BPI‐S)

The BPI‐S for individuals with intellectual disabilities (Rojahn et al. 2012a) was used to determine prevalence of challenging behaviour. The BPI‐S is an informant‐based behaviour rating tool designed specifically for use with individuals with intellectual disabilities. The questionnaire consists of three subscales measuring self‐injurious behaviour, aggressive destructive behaviour and stereotypical behaviour. Each subscale contains several items classified under that behaviour type, that is, self‐biting is classed as a self‐injurious behaviour. All three subscales contain a measure of frequency for each behaviour item. In addition, the self‐injurious behaviour and aggressive‐destructive behaviour subscales contain a measure of severity. The BPI‐S is considered psychometrically sound, demonstrating good internal consistency and construct validity (Leader et al. 2020; Mascitelli et al. 2015; Rojahn et al. 2012a, 2012b), and has shown acceptable reliability and validity in large samples of individuals with intellectual disabilities (Mascitelli et al. 2015; Rojahn et al. 2012b). Overall challenging behaviour prevalence was determined using the frequency measure for the three individual subscales, a participant demonstrated prevalence of behaviour if they had a frequency greater than zero in any of the individual behaviour items under any of the three subscales in the past 2 months.

Psychotropic Medication Data

Participants are asked to record the details of all medications they take on a regular basis, including prescription and non‐prescription medications, over‐the‐counter medicines, vitamins and herbal and alternative medicines. Medication data were recorded by brand/generic name and where available, the dose and frequency (O'Dwyer et al. 2016) and are classified by pharmacists into different medication classes according to the WHO Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification codes.

For the current study, medications classed as psychotropic were included. Psychotropic medication classification was based on the five subclasses as detailed by the World Health Organisation's ATC codes, as follows: N05A: AntipsychoticsN05B: AnxiolyticsN05C: Sedatives/hypnoticsN06A: AntidepressantsMood‐stabilising agents: Anti‐seizure medications (N03A) reported by people without a diagnosis of epilepsy and lithium (N05AN01)

Psychotropic drugs were analysed in line with their licensed indication. Accordingly, several reclassifications were undertaken to reflect main clinical use, in line with previous studies (O'Dwyer et al. 2017; Odalović et al. 2024): lithium was reclassified as a mood stabilising agent; prochlorperazine was reclassified as an antiemetic/antinauseant; clonazepam was reclassified as an anxiolytic in participants who had no diagnosis of epilepsy and reported diagnosis of a mental health condition; rectal diazepam and clobazam were removed from anxiolytics; midazolam was removed from the sedative/hypnotic subclass.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28.0.1.1 and R studio version 2024.12.0 for R version 4.4.2. Univariate descriptive statistics were computed to determine population characteristics and prevalences. Chi‐squared tests were utilised to determine bivariate associations between the three outcome measures (reported menopause‐specific anxiety, behaviour prevalence, psychotropic medication use) and potential covariates to determine potential confounding factors for inclusion as independent variables in regression analyses (see, Supporting Information for more detail). Covariates were derived from the literature and included, but were not limited to, demographic variables, prevalence of general mental health conditions, disease prevalence, menopausal symptoms and other validated mental health measures.

Regression

Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted using the glm() function in R to determine predictors of the three outcome variables (menopause‐specific anxiety, behaviour prevalence and psychotropic medication use). All assumptions for logistic regression were tested, and careful selection of confounding variables occurred as a result. All significant predictors for each outcome variable (see, Tables S2–S4) were included in the initial regression analyses (see, Tables S5–S7). The three regression models were then subjected to stepwise regression based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC, Akaike 1998) using the step() function in R to produce reduced models with fewer predictors. Model selection using AIC aims at achieving a parsimonious model with the optimal predictive power by selecting the model with the fewest independent variables whereby the fitted values of the model are closest to the observed values (Cavanaugh and Neath 2019). Fewer independent variables in the model also ensure greater model reliability by reducing the ratio of positive outcome events of the dependent variable to number of predictor variables (Peduzzi et al. 1996). Results from the full models (not subject to selection by AIC) are available in Tables S5–S7 and results from the AIC reduced models only will be presented in the results.

Network Analysis

In addition to regression analysis, network analysis was utilised in line with recent research in the general population (Martin‐Key et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2023) to examine patterns of statistical association between menopausal symptoms and mental health outcome measures (Borsboom et al. 2021). Each variable in the network was represented by a node, with the relationship between each node represented by an edge. Thicker edges between variables represent stronger associations and variables located more centrally in the network tend to be more influential. Communities of related variables have the same‐coloured nodes.

To estimate the network, the eLasso procedure for binary data was utilised (van Borkulo et al. 2014). This method is based on the Ising model and combines logistic regression with model selection based on a goodness‐of‐fit measure and has been validated for use with binary data across a variety of sample sizes (van Borkulo et al. 2014). eLasso can be implemented using the R package IsingFit which is freely available at (http://cran.r‐project.org/web/packages/IsingFit/IsingFit.pdf). The eLasso procedure regresses each variable in the network on all other variables in turn. Each regression function is then regularised to mitigate any problems associated with regressing one variable on a large number of other variables (Dalege et al. 2017). The IsingFit function in R was applied to a data frame containing data from the current study (n = 99) which produced a weight adjacency matrix containing the regression coefficients indicating the strengths of the relationships (edges) between the connected variables (nodes) while conditioning on the other variables. As the network is based on the Ising model for binary networks, causality cannot be inferred from the network (van Borkulo et al. 2014). The most central (influential) node was determined using the centrality measure of strength: whereby the absolute values of the edges connected to a given node were summed to determine the influence a given node has on the network. Communities of related variables were detected using the ‘walktrap’ algorithm via the cluster_walktrap() function in R. Please see Dalege et al. (2017) for more detail on the methodology utilised for network estimation.

A Note on Regression and Network Analyses

The regression models were utilised to determine directional relationships between predictors and outcome variables while controlling for potentially confounding factors. The regression analysis set up the basis for the network analysis which enabled the comparison of all variables of interest in one model. Findings from both were interpreted in the results and the discussion, as while they are linked, both have generated findings of importance due to different strengths of each analytic technique.

Discussion

This study is the first of its kind to directly examine the effect of menopause on the mental health and behaviour of women with intellectual disabilities globally. Recent scoping review findings indicate that the mental health implications of menopause in this population have been omitted from research to date (Corrigan et al. 2025). The current study found that menopausal symptoms such as vasomotor symptoms, fatigue and mood changes can be predictive of menopause‐specific anxiety which can further manifest in challenging behaviours and psychotropic medication use in women with intellectual disabilities.

Network analysis uncovered two distinct communities, or highly interconnected clusters of related variables, which were critical in understanding the experience of menopause in women with intellectual disabilities. Vasomotor symptoms, fatigue and menopause‐specific anxiety formed the red community, suggesting an association between menopausal symptoms and menopause‐specific anxiety. The second community consisted of blue nodes, connecting mood changes with challenging behaviour and challenging behaviour to psychotropic medication use. This suggests that menopause‐specific mood changes could result in psychotropic medication use by presenting as challenging behaviours in women with intellectual disabilities.

Fatigue was the strongest predictor of menopause‐specific anxiety in the red community. Interestingly, fatigue has not been identified as a prevalent symptom in the limited menopause literature for women with intellectual disabilities, despite being recognised as one of the most common and most detrimental menopausal symptoms in the general population due to its effect on quality of life (Taylor‐Swanson et al. 2018). The current study is the first to identify fatigue as a menopausal symptom with a potentially detrimental effect on mental health in women with intellectual disabilities. Fatigue may cause distress and frustration and can intensify symptoms of anxiety which could in turn be alleviated using appropriate menopausal treatments. It is important to recognise that fatigue could easily be seen only as a side effect of medication due to high levels of polypharmacy in this population (O'Dwyer et al. 2016, 2017) or simply as a comorbidity of intellectual disability with women often struggling with energy levels and maintaining an active lifestyle (Faust and Morin 2022).

Similarly, vasomotor symptoms had a strong relationship with menopause‐specific anxiety in the red community. Highlighting this association is critical as vasomotor symptoms and their effect on mental health have yet to be investigated in women with intellectual disabilities despite extensive examination in the general population. They have been found to be bidirectionally linked to symptoms of anxiety and depression (Llaneza et al. 2012); with vasomotor symptoms found to be predictive of adverse mental health symptoms (Burleson et al. 2010; Chedraui et al. 2009) and the converse also true (Freeman et al. 2009; Melby et al. 2011). Additionally, symptom severity can vary due to socioeconomic status, potentially due to lower levels of behavioural health and education (Chedraui et al. 2009), which could be pertinent to women with intellectual disabilities, especially considering other aforementioned difficulties faced by this population.

The blue community provides empirical evidence to support the idea that menopausal mood changes could result in behaviour change which is directly related to psychotropic medication use. This chain of events highlights the potential mechanisms behind menopause‐specific behaviour change and the potential risk for diagnostic overshadowing that could occur due to menopause in this population (Dell'Armo and Tassé 2024; Taggart et al. 2010). This could directly result in potentially erroneous prescribing of psychotropic medication for menopausal symptoms that could instead be treated with hormone‐focused treatments, that is, hormone replacement therapy (Adams et al. 2025). Such findings reflect the historic problem of over‐reliance on psychotropic medication in this population with no official diagnosis of mental health problems (Sheehan et al. 2017).

Notably the strongest relationship in the network was found between the most central variable, menopause‐specific anxiety, and psychotropic medication use, which serves as a bridge between the two distinct communities. This shows the potential influence of menopause‐specific anxiety throughout the network, illustrating how menopausal symptoms, such as vasomotor symptoms and fatigue, can directly impact mental health through feelings of anxiety. This relationship again highlights the risk of diagnostic overshadowing, as women who reported menopause‐specific anxiety were more likely to be prescribed psychotropic medication. In this way, it is possible that menopause‐specific mental health symptoms may be mistaken for general mental health problems or behavioural issues and treated with psychotropic medication instead of more suitable treatments. This was echoed in a recent study by Langer‐Shapland et al. (2023) which discussed how mood changes such as anger could manifest in behaviours such as screaming or shouting, opening up the risk of diagnostic overshadowing. This is, particularly, pertinent in the current study, considering only 41.4% of menopausal participants in the current study reported symptoms of menopause, suggesting symptoms may be different to verbalise. It showcases the importance of recognising signs of menopause in women with intellectual disabilities, particularly, in women with severe and profound intellectual disabilities, to ensure correct treatments are provided.

The findings from the network analysis were strengthened as most of the significant associations from the adjusted regression models were retained in the network. The network extended the individual findings from the regression analyses by facilitating the inclusion of all variables in one model and examining interactions between them (Hevey 2018). The results demonstrate the potential adverse effects menopausal symptoms may have on mental health in women with intellectual disabilities through menopause‐specific anxiety which in turn can result in challenging behaviours and psychotropic medication use. Therefore, if menopause‐specific symptoms are not identified in women with intellectual disabilities, they may be misattributed to general mental health difficulties or challenging behaviour which could result in the erroneous prescription of psychotropic medication leading to unnecessary side effects (Costello et al. 2022).

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

This study explores adverse mental health implications of menopause using quantitative research methodology in a representative sample of women with intellectual disabilities. The data used for the outcome variables were derived from robust measures collected as part of a well‐established longitudinal cohort study. Menopausal symptomatology was determined using menopause‐specific questions, ensuring that symptoms were reported in relation to menopause; thereby reducing misattribution to other causes. The BPI‐S is a widely used informant‐based measure validated for use to detect challenging behaviours in people with intellectual disabilities. Medication data utilised were rigorously analysed by qualified pharmacists to categorise them into the appropriate classifications.

Network analysis is a powerful analytic technique that has been used in recent papers examining associations between menopausal symptoms in the general population (Martin‐Key et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2023). This technique allowed the researcher to examine the variables as a system to determine how they relate to one another (Hevey 2018). Typically, network analysis is utilised with larger sample sizes; however, this technique was suitable for use in the current study as even in smaller sample sizes the main network connections are retained (van Borkulo et al. 2014).

Limitations

The network validated the majority of the relationships indicated by the regression analyses but failed to detect the relationship between menopause‐specific anxiety and challenging behaviour prevalence. This discrepancy could be attributed to low sample size (n = 99) as, according to van Borkulo et al. (2014), some relationships in networks with smaller sample sizes will not be detected by eLasso. Alternatively, it could be possible that the relationship could be explained by another variable in or outside the model. It is possible that menopause‐specific anxiety could have an indirect effect on challenging behaviour prevalence through other mechanisms such as menopause‐specific mood changes. Or indeed, a direct effect as indicated by the regression analysis and by previous literature which suggests that menopause‐related emotional impacts could result in challenging behaviour due to communication difficulties faced by women with intellectual disabilities (Adams et al. 2025; Langer‐Shapland et al. 2023). Additionally, there was a new relationship detected between psychotropic medication use and challenging behaviour prevalence that was not significant in the regression analyses. Demographic covariates (age, residence, level of intellectual disability) were not controlled for in the network which could account for this discrepancy. All remaining relationships were preserved which validate the results of the regression analysis and strengthen the findings.

Additionally, despite the use of AIC model selection to obtain model parsimony, large confidence intervals for two odds ratios were obtained in two of the final models due to uneven branching within the predictor variables. This resulted in very small cell counts which increased the standard error exponentially. This did not affect the overall fit of the model. Additionally, data on depression related to menopause were not available for use in the current study. However, such data will be available in future iterations of the cohort study.

Implications for Policy, Practice and Research

This study provides strong evidence to suggest that menopausal symptoms are associated with adverse mental health and behavioural outcomes for women with intellectual disabilities. Greater awareness of menopause is needed across all levels, encompassing the education of women with intellectual disabilities, comprehensive staff training on symptom recognition and management, and the appropriate prescribing of menopausal treatments for this population (Willis et al. 2010, 2011).

The recent NICE guidelines (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence [NICE] 2024) recommend individualised treatment for menopause care. However, specific subgroups such as women with intellectual disabilities are not specifically mentioned, despite evidence to suggest that menopause may present differently in marginalised groups (Anto et al. 2025). For example, NICE recommend cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) as a treatment for depression related to vasomotor symptoms, which has been validated for use in menopausal women in the general population (Hunter 2021). Although CBT has been validated for use for people with mild intellectual disabilities (Fynn et al. 2023), it has yet to be tested for suitability in relation to menopause in women with intellectual disabilities, highlighting the need for the validation of specific treatments in this population.

Menopause is often undetected in women with intellectual disabilities, leaving them vulnerable to diagnostic overshadowing and erroneous prescription of psychotropic medication, as indicated by the current findings. Some psychotropic medications, such as antidepressants, have been used to treat menopause‐specific symptoms and mental health issues in the general population (Wu et al. 2020) to varying degrees of success (Grady et al. 2007; Speroff et al. 2008). However, it has been argued that antidepressants in combination with hormone therapy (Graziottin and Serafini 2009; Tseng et al. 2023) are the most efficacious treatment option, further emphasising the need for better education and knowledge of menopausal status in this population to ensure appropriate prescribing. Considering the protective effects of oestrogen on cognition reported in the general population (Henderson et al. 2005; Mosconi et al. 2018), coupled with the heightened risk of dementia found in menopausal women with Down syndrome (Schupf et al. 2018, 2003, 2006, 1997; Seltzer et al. 2001); clinical trials including women with intellectual disabilities exploring the protective effects of hormone therapy for both cognition and mental health are urgently needed (Tseng et al. 2023).

It is essential not to exclude women with severe and profound intellectual disabilities who are at the greatest risk for diagnostic overshadowing. The role of the familiar support person is vital when it comes to menopausal symptom recognition and management, particularly, considering how symptomatology and presentation may vary depending on level of intellectual disability. Cycle‐tracking could be an effective mechanism adopted by younger women and their carers to identify changes in menstrual cycles and symptoms to prepare for menopause (Earle et al. 2024).

Future research should seek to employ mixed methodology to inform targeted, specialised care interventions for this population. Furthermore, it is critical that women with intellectual disabilities are included in mainstream studies with women from the general population to ensure treatments are validated for this population (Anto et al. 2025).

Finally, women with intellectual disabilities have also been omitted from recent policies surrounding sexual and reproductive health (McAteer 2022; Wiseman and Ferrie 2020). For example, in Ireland this group was excluded in the recent Women's Health Action Plan, despite acknowledgement of other minority groups (Department of Health 2024). Likewise, women with intellectual disabilities were also excluded from recent Women's Health Policy in the UK (Department of Health and Social Care 2022). It is hoped that the results of the current study will provide evidence to underpin the need for inclusion of women with intellectual disabilities in policy and to inform future policy recommendations.

Conclusion

The above findings suggest that menopause and associated symptoms can have a detrimental effect on the mental health and behaviour of women with intellectual disabilities. Network and regression analyses identified specific menopausal symptoms such as vasomotor symptoms, fatigue and mood changes which were found to have an association with menopause‐specific anxiety. It is critical to distinguish this specific form of anxiety from general anxiety, as it is directly linked to menopause and can be caused by specific symptoms that occur due to hormonal changes. Additionally, women who reported menopause‐specific anxiety were more likely to demonstrate challenging behaviours and increased levels of psychotropic medication use. These findings highlight the risk of diagnostic overshadowing in this population due to atypical presentation of menopausal symptoms, increasing the possibility of misattribution to an incorrect cause which could result in inappropriate medication prescribing. Raising awareness of menopause and the associated implications for mental health and challenging behaviours is critical in this population. Future research and advancements in policy must be geared towards the provision of tailored menopause care for women with intellectual disabilities to ensure equitable health outcomes for all.
