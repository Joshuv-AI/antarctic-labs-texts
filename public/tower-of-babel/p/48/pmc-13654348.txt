Comparison of ASCVD Risk Prediction Models in STEMI
Authors: Mohit Dayal Gupta, Shekhar Kunal, MP Girish, Ankit Bansal, Vishal Batra, Rajeev Malhotra, Ruchi Sharma, Vimal Mehta, Jamal Yusuf, Arman Qamar, Prashant Mishra, Aradhna Gupta, Vikash Jaiswal, Deepak L Bhatt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular (CV) risk prediction models guide primary prevention, yet most were derived in Western populations and may not generalize to the South Asian population.ObjectivesThe authors aimed to compare risk classification, agreement, and discriminative patterns of major atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk prediction models in first acute myocardial infarction (AMI).MethodsIn this retrospective analysis, 4,975 consecutive patients aged 40 to 79 years presenting with first AMI were evaluated. Pre-event demographics, clinical characteristics, and laboratory data were extracted from outpatient records. Ten-year pre-event CV risk was calculated using 5 prediction models: Framingham Risk Score (FRS), American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association ASCVD 2013, World Health Organization (WHO) CVD charts, Joint British Societies' cardiovascular risk score version 3 (JBS-3), and PREVENT equations. Patients were categorized as low (<7.5%), intermediate (7.5% to <20%), or high (≥20%) risk. Statistical analyses included Pearson correlation, intraclass correlation, weighted Cohen's kappa, and repeated-measures generalized linear modeling with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons.ResultsPREVENT classified 19.8% of patients as high risk, compared with 20.2% using FRS, 15.0% using WHO charts, 11.7% using JBS-3, and 12.3% using ASCVD 2013. PREVENT demonstrated the widest risk distribution (0.2%-91%). Agreement between PREVENT and other models was poor (κ = 0.228; 95% CI: 0.204-0.252 with ASCVD, κ = 0.311; 95% CI: 0.288-0.33 with FRS, and κ = 0.063; 95% CI: 0.038-0.088 with WHO), despite moderate positive correlations.ConclusionsMajor CV risk algorithms classify Indian AMI patients very differently, with substantial potential for misclassification. Although PREVENT and FRS showed nearly similar classification of high risk, 80% of patients were still not identified as high risk before their event, underscoring the need for South Asia–specific risk score development and validation and alternative preventive strategies.Key words: acute myocardial infarction, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular risk prediction, risk stratification, South Asian population

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) persists as the leading cause of global mortality and loss of disability-adjusted life years, with a disproportionate and rising burden affecting low- and middle-income countries.1 India in particular is at the epicenter of this epidemic, accounting for a significant portion of this burden amid a rapid epidemiological transition.2 Characterized by a steeper rise in prevalence, a younger age of onset, and higher case-fatality rates compared to Western nations, the CVD landscape in India presents unique public health challenges.2,3 Alarmingly, acute myocardial infarction (AMI) often serves as the first manifestation of underlying atherosclerotic disease in a substantial proportion of South Asian patients, many of whom were previously undiagnosed or, crucially, classified as low risk by conventional prediction tools.4 This underscores the urgent need for accurate cardiovascular risk stratification to enable early detection and targeted primary prevention, which is pivotal to curbing the escalating CVD burden.

Clinical practice guidelines widely recommend the use of 10-year risk prediction models to guide preventive interventions, such as statin therapy. Established tools like the Framingham Risk Score (FRS),5 the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) ASCVD 2013 pooled cohort equations,6 World Health Organization (WHO) CVD risk charts,7 and the Joint British Societies' cardiovascular risk score version 3 (JBS-3) risk calculator8 form the cornerstone of this approach. These algorithms traditionally integrate conventional risk factors, including age, sex, blood pressure, lipid levels, diabetes, and smoking status. More recently, the AHA introduced the PREVENT (Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs) score, a novel tool designed to reflect contemporary risk factors and treatment patterns, and which incorporates additional variables such as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).9

A critical limitation, however, is that most of these established models were derived and validated predominantly in cohorts of European descent.5, 6, 7 Their applicability to ethnically, genetically, and socioeconomically distinct populations, particularly South Asians, remains contentious.10 A growing body of evidence indicates that these Western-derived models may not generalize well to South Asian populations, particularly those in India, likely because of differences in underlying risk profiles, the higher prevalence of diabetes, and the earlier onset of disease.3,10,11 Such cross-population discordance may contribute to the misclassification of high-risk individuals, resulting in missed opportunities for life-saving interventions and contributing to preventable morbidity and mortality.12,13

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate and compare the retrospective predictive performance of 5 primary prevention risk models—FRS, ACC/AHA ASCVD 2013, WHO, JBS-3, and PREVENT—in a large cohort of Indian patients presenting with AMI. By analyzing how these patients would have been classified by each model based on their pre-event risk factors, we seek to assess the potential accuracy and clinical utility of these tools for guiding specific primary prevention strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study design and population

NORIN MI is an ongoing single-center, prospective observational study conducted at a tertiary care teaching hospital in India that is enrolling consecutive patients aged 40 to 79 years who were admitted with a first episode of AMI. The diagnosis of AMI was based on the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction.14 Patients with missing baseline clinical or biochemical data necessary for risk score computation (<5%: 230 patients) or those with a known history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) before the index myocardial infarction (including prior myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, or stroke: 120 patients) were excluded from the current analysis (Central Illustration).

Data collection and risk score computation

Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, blood pressure, lipid profiles, diabetes status, smoking history, and medication use, were extracted from the most recent outpatient records dated prior to the AMI admission. Laboratory values, specifically total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), were recorded from the most recent available tests before the event.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients classified as high risk (≥20%) by each model before their AMI event. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. For each risk model, we calculated: 1) the mean predicted risk, 2) the proportion of patients classified into each risk category, and 3) concordance between models using weighted Cohen's kappa (κ) with quadratic weight, which assign higher penalty for large disagreement. Pearson's correlation coefficients were computed to evaluate linear associations between the risk scores. All scores were first normalized using (x – minimum) × 100/(maximum – minimum), and repeated-measures general linear model (GLM) was applied to compare normalized mean risk estimates, with “Risk Score” as the within-subjects factor. Multivariate tests (Pillai's Trace, Wilks' Lambda, Hotelling's Trace, and Roy's Largest Root) assessed the overall effect. Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared (η2). Estimated marginal means were obtained, and Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were used to test differences between individual scores.

For categorical agreement, the weighted Cohen's kappa statistic was calculated using quadratic weight to quantify agreement between PREVENT and the legacy scores. Kappa thresholds were interpreted as follows: <0.20 = poor, 0.21-0.40 = fair, 0.41-0.60 = moderate, 0.61-0.80 = good, and >0.80 = very good agreement. Two-way mixed analysis of variance was used for finding the intraclass correlation among the 5 scores. As a sensitivity analysis, each risk score was categorized according to thresholds recommended in the original derivation study or corresponding clinical guideline. Because some models include a borderline risk category, borderline and intermediate risk categories were combined into a single “moderate risk” category to ensure comparability across models. Agreement between models was assessed using Cohen's kappa, weighted kappa, and percentage agreement. All tests were 2-sided, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS (v25, IBM Corp) and R (v4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Written informed consent was received from all the patients, and proper approval for the study was taken from the institutional ethics committee (F.1/IEC/MAMC/85/04/2021/No. 486).

Results

A total of 5,325 patients with ASCVD were enrolled. After exclusion, 4,975 patients were included in the final analysis, of whom 4,089 (82.2%) were males and 886 (17.8%) females (Table 1). The mean age of the participants was 54.4 ± 11.7 years. Almost half of the study population (2,446 of 4,975; 49.2%) reported current or past smoking, 1,156 (23.2%) had diabetes, 1,306 (26.3%) had hypertension, 72 (1.4%) had dyslipidemia, and 33 (0.7%) had heart failure. The majority of the patients (1,782; 35.8%) belonged to the lower-middle socioeconomic strata. A significant proportion of participants were illiterate (2,142; 43.1%), with only 311 (6.2%) having education beyond high school. Only 438 (8.8%) held professional occupations (Table 1).

Risk stratification scores

The median [25th-75th percentile] estimated risk scores in the study cohort (Table 1) were PREVENT: 5.90% [2.90%-13.2%], ASCVD 2013: 9.5% [5.1%-14.4%], FRS: 12.0% [10.0%-16.0%], JBS-3: 13.0% [10.0%-17.0%], and WHO: 10.0% [6.0%-15.0%]. Among these, the PREVENT tool exhibited the widest distribution (0.2%-91%), indicating greater sensitivity to extreme values and broader dispersion across the population. In contrast, WHO, JBS-3, and FRS scores were notably compressed, with maximum values <40%, reflecting a narrower distributional range that assigns lower absolute risk estimates to higher-risk individuals compared with PREVENT. The JBS-3 demonstrated a relatively narrow distribution in this cohort (range: 1%-30%), with clustering around the mid-risk range. These differences underscore substantial variability in risk stratification between models, highlighting how the choice of tool can influence population-level risk categorization.

Risk stratification across scores

The distribution of patients across low-, moderate-, and high-risk categories varied considerably depending on the risk prediction model applied (Figure 1). Using the PREVENT score, 2,145 of the 4,975 patients (43.1%) were classified as low risk, 1,843 (37.0%) as moderate risk, and 987 (19.8%) as high risk. This distribution suggests that PREVENT applies more stringent criteria for elevated risk classification in this population. In contrast, the FRS assigned a markedly smaller proportion to the low-risk category (786; 15.8%), while the majority were categorized as moderate risk (3,184; 64.0%), with 1,005 (20.2%) classified as high risk. The ASCVD 2013 model produced an intermediate distribution, with 1,200 patients (24.1%) categorized as low risk, 3,162 (63.6%) as moderate risk, and 613 (12.3%) as high risk. The WHO classified 1,473 patients (29.6%) as low risk, 2,755 (55.4%) as moderate risk, and 747 (15.0%) as high risk. JBS-3 demonstrated a distribution comparable to ASCVD 2013 and WHO, with 1,006 patients (20.2%) classified as low risk, 3,386 (68.1%) as moderate risk, and 583 (11.7%) as high risk. Notably, all models except PREVENT classified the majority of patients (55%-68%) in the moderate-risk category, though with substantial variation in low-risk classification, ranging from 15.8% (FRS) to 43.1% (PREVENT).

Weighted Cohen's kappa agreement

The weighted Cohen's kappa agreement, percentage of agreement between the risk scores, are highlighted in Table 3 and Figure 2. Categorical agreement between risk prediction models was generally limited, with weighted Cohen's kappa with quadratic weight values ranging from poor to moderate despite statistical significance across all comparisons (P < 0.001). Weighted Cohen's kappa values indicated low agreement between PREVENT and the other scores. PREVENT demonstrated poor agreement with the ASCVD 2013 (κ = 0.228; 95% CI: 0.204-0.252) and fair with FRS (κ = 0.311; 95% CI: 0.288-0.333), and showed poor agreement with WHO (κ = 0.063; 95% CI: 0.04-0.09). The strongest agreement was observed between JBS-3 and FRS (κ = 0.602; 95% CI: 0.583-0.621), indicating moderate concordance and suggesting similarity in underlying calibration and threshold behavior between these 2 algorithms. Fair agreement was also observed between the JBS-3 and ASCVD 2013 (κ = 0.458; 95% CI 0.432-0.483), and between WHO and FRS (κ = 0.344; 95% CI: 0.315-0.373), reflecting partial alignment among several legacy models. These results reflect low categorical agreement among scoring systems. This may reflect differences in population characteristics or scoring criteria.

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation coefficients among the cardiovascular risk scores are presented in Table 4. All pairwise correlations were positive and statistically significant (all P < 0.001). The strongest association was observed between the JBS-3 and FRS (r = 0.728; 95% CI: 0.714-0.740), indicating substantial shared variance. JBS-3 also demonstrated moderate correlations with the ASCVD 2013 (r = 0.627; 95% CI: 0.609-0.643), the WHO (r = 0.467; 95% CI: 0.445-0.488), and the PREVENT (r = 0.413; 95% CI: 0.391-0.437). Among the legacy models, the WHO showed moderate correlations with the FRS (r = 0.484; 95% CI: 0.463-0.505) and the ASCVD 2013 (r = 0.417; 95% CI: 0.449-0.492), while correlations involving the ASCVD 2013 were generally weaker (r range: 0.217-0.471). These findings highlight that although the risk models are directionally aligned, the strength of association varies considerably, reflecting meaningful differences in underlying risk estimation (Figure 3). Intraclass correlation was performed to find the agreement between the 5 risk scores. The JBS-3 had moderate to good agreement with PREVENT, WHO, and FRS, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) [95% CIs] of 0.531 [0.504-0.556], 0.622 [0.601-0.643] and 0.836 [0.827-0.845], respectively. The PREVENT score had mild to moderate agreement with WHO and FRS, with ICCs of 0.346 [0.309-0.382] and 0.511 [0.483-0.537], respectively. There was good agreement between WHO and FRS, with an ICC of 0.650 [0.630-0.669].

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analyses using model-specific thresholds, substantial heterogeneity in risk classification persisted across models. The proportion of individuals categorized as high risk varied considerably, ranging from 8.8% with FRS to 19.8% with PREVENT and 15.0% with WHO (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). Agreement between models remained modest, with Cohen's kappa values ranging from −0.052 to 0.464, indicating poor to moderate agreement despite the use of model-specific thresholds. The highest agreement was observed between JBS-3 and FRS (κ = 0.464; 76.18% agreement), while PREVENT and WHO demonstrated poor agreement (κ = −0.052; 31.06% agreement). These findings were consistent with the primary analysis using uniform thresholds, indicating that discordance in risk classification is driven by intrinsic differences in model structure rather than threshold selection.

Repeated-measures generalized linear model analysis

Repeated-measures multivariate analysis demonstrated a highly significant overall effect of risk model on estimated cardiovascular risk. Repeated-measures multivariate testing confirmed that the scores differed significantly when considered together. All 4 multivariate criteria—Pillai's Trace, Wilks' Lambda, Hotelling's Trace, and Roy's Largest Root—were statistically significant (all P < 0.001) (Supplemental Table 4), confirming that the distribution of estimated risk differed substantially across the evaluated algorithms. Pillai's Trace was 0.791, indicating that approximately 79% of the variance in predicted risk was attributable to differences between scoring methods, representing a very large multivariate effect size. These findings demonstrate that the choice of risk algorithm materially influences absolute cardiovascular risk estimation within this cohort.

Pairwise comparisons of risk scores

Direct pairwise comparisons (Supplemental Table 5) demonstrated that all 5 scoring systems produced significantly different mean risk estimates (all P ≤ 0.01) after Bonferroni adjustment. Using the ASCVD 2013 as the reference standard, all other tools classified patients at significantly higher risk levels. JBS-3 demonstrated the largest positive deviation from ASCVD 2013 (estimated marginal mean difference +27.31 normalized units; 95% CI: 26.74-27.88), followed closely by FRS (+26.42; 95% CI: 25.66-27.17), PREVENT (+12.68; 95% CI: 11.56-13.79), and WHO (+11.20; 95% CI: 10.46-11.93). These findings confirm that ASCVD 2013 assigns the lowest mean risk estimates among all 5 models in this cohort, producing systematically lower values than the other calculators. Both JBS-3 and FRS generated markedly higher estimates than PREVENT and WHO. Most notably, FRS and JBS-3 produced remarkably convergent risk estimates, with a minimal difference between them (−0.89; 95% CI: −1.48 to −0.30; P < 0.001), the smallest discrepancy observed across all pairwise comparisons. This convergence is particularly striking given that JBS-3 incorporates Asian ethnicity calibration whereas FRS does not, suggesting that ethnic adjustment in JBS-3 did not meaningfully differentiate its performance from the uncalibrated FRS in this South Asian population. PREVENT and WHO clustered more closely together, with WHO assigning slightly lower risk than PREVENT (−1.48 U), although the magnitude of this difference was modest. These findings demonstrate that model selection alone materially alters absolute cardiovascular risk estimation within the same population. Notably, while ASCVD 2013 appears comparatively conservative, JBS-3 and FRS yielded substantially higher estimates. However, higher absolute estimates did not necessarily translate into improved categorical agreement, underscoring persistent discordance in risk stratification across algorithms.

Discussion

Cardiovascular risk prediction models are pivotal for guiding primary prevention, yet their generalizability beyond the Western populations in which they were derived remains contentious. This study provides a critical, head-to-head comparison of 5 prominent models—the Framingham Risk Score (FRS), the ACC/AHA ASCVD (2013) score, the WHO CVD risk charts, JBS-3, and the novel PREVENT equation—in a large cohort of 4,975 Indian patients presenting with AMI. Our central finding is a profound discordance in risk classification, with the choice of model drastically altering the perceived risk profile of the population. This has direct implications for treatment eligibility and resource allocation, underscoring the perils of applying nonvalidated, Western-centric tools to South Asian populations.

The South Asian phenotype and intermodel discordance in risk classification

South Asians face a disproportionately high burden of ASCVD, characterized by earlier onset and a more aggressive course.3,10,15 This “South Asian phenotype” is driven by a high prevalence of insulin resistance, atherogenic dyslipidemia (high triglycerides, low HDL-C, and often elevated lipoprotein(a) despite moderate LDL-C), and a propensity for central adiposity.3,11 Our findings align with prior evidence that Western-derived models may not perform consistently in South Asian populations. The most striking pattern is the phenomenon of “risk compression.” In our cohort, with a mean AMI age of 54.4 years highlighting premature disease, the FRS, ASCVD 2013, JBS-3, and WHO models clustered 55% to 68% of patients into a single, ambiguous “moderate-risk” category. This compression obscures clinical decision-making, failing to clearly distinguish individuals who would benefit from intensive intervention from those who may not. In contrast, the PREVENT score demonstrated a broader distribution, classifying 43.1% as low risk and 19.8% as high risk. This finding suggests that PREVENT may offer better risk discrimination, a known limitation of older models like FRS, which exhibit compression at higher ages.16

The clinical implication of this intermodel discordance is noteworthy: Given that all patients in this cohort ultimately developed AMI, the substantial proportion classified as low or moderate risk before the event—ranging from 58.9% (FRS) to 80.1% (PREVENT)—highlights the limitations of current risk stratification tools in identifying this South Asian population at risk for major adverse cardiovascular events. However, without formal calibration analyses comparing predicted to observed event rates, definitive conclusions about whether any model over- or underestimates true risk cannot be drawn from the present data. The ASCVD 2013 model, the most conservative in our analysis, identified only 12.3% as high risk, consistent with studies suggesting it may assign lower risk estimates in South Asian populations, possibly related to differences in derivation cohort composition and the omission of variables such as lipoprotein(a) and markers of insulin resistance.6,17

The PREVENT score: enhanced discrimination but persistent gaps

The PREVENT model demonstrated several advancements over legacy tools. Its wider risk distribution (0.2%-91%) indicates a greater dynamic range and sensitivity to extreme values, potentially mitigating the risk compression noted elsewhere.16 Its strong internal consistency (η2 = 0.912) and logical alignment with adverse clinical markers (higher systolic blood pressure, hemoglobin A1c, and lower eGFR) affirm its pathophysiological validity. The significant gradient in eGFR across PREVENT categories is particularly noteworthy, underscoring the growing recognition of chronic kidney disease as a cardiovascular risk multiplier, a relationship not as robustly captured in older models.9 However, a deeper analysis reveals critical discrepancies. When we examined the mean risk scores of other models within PREVENT's risk categories (Table 4), a profound misalignment emerged. Most paradoxically, the mean ASCVD 2013 score was lower in PREVENT's high-risk group (∼11.4%) than in its moderate-risk group (∼16.5%). This suggests that PREVENT identifies a high-risk phenotype potentially driven by factors such as renal impairment or older age that the ASCVD 2013 algorithm fails to recognize. Furthermore, although the WHO and FRS scores increased across PREVENT categories, their gradients were much flatter (eg, WHO: ∼11% to ∼14.5%), indicating they only partially capture the risk gradient identified by PREVENT. The stark dichotomy between significant positive correlations and poor categorical agreement (κ: 0.063-0.330) is a critical lesson in risk assessment, previously noted in comparisons between FRS and the pooled cohort equations (PCE).18 It demonstrates that, while scores generally move in the same direction, they frequently place patients in different, clinically decisive risk categories. The low-chance agreement with the WHO charts (κ = 0.063; 95% CI: 0.038-0.088) is particularly notable, suggesting substantial divergence in underlying risk estimation frameworks between the 2 models. The WHO charts, designed for simplicity in low-resource settings, trade granularity for broad applicability, and they may assign lower absolute risk estimates in urban, transitioning populations such as ours compared with more contemporary models.7

The JBS-3 paradox: why ethnic adjustment did not improve categorical agreement

One rationale for incorporating JBS-3 into our analysis was its explicit inclusion of Asian ethnicity as a risk modifier. Given concerns that Western-derived models may not adequately capture cardiovascular risk in South Asians, it was reasonable to hypothesize that JBS-3 would demonstrate improved alignment in this cohort. Despite ethnicity adjustment, JBS-3 did not show superior categorical agreement or clearer discriminatory separation across PREVENT-defined risk strata. Its risk distribution was relatively narrow (mean: 13.8% ± 5.26%, range: 1%-30%), with minimal variation across PREVENT-defined categories. Although JBS-3 demonstrated moderate agreement with FRS (weighted κ = 0.602; 95% CI: 0.583-0.621) and fair agreement with ASCVD 2013 (weighted κ = 0.458; 95% CI: 0.432-0.483), agreement with PREVENT remained modest (weighted κ = 0.330; 95% CI: 0.310-0.360), and overall discordance across models persisted. These findings suggest that incorporating “Asian ethnicity” as a calibration factor within Western-derived algorithms does not necessarily translate into meaningful population-specific risk stratification. Cardiovascular risk in South Asians is driven not only by ethnicity but by complex metabolic, phenotypic, and socioeconomic determinants such as insulin resistance, central adiposity, elevated lipoprotein(a), and earlier atherosclerotic burden, which may not be fully captured within conventional variable frameworks. Moreover, models derived from populations with different baseline event rates and health care structures may not translate reliably to contemporary Indian cohorts characterized by premature CAD.

Etiology of discordance and the imperative for population-specific tools

The observed discrepancies are not random but stem from foundational differences in model design, primarily:

1)Cohort derivation and temporal context: The limited generalizability of models derived from Western populations is a well-known phenomenon. Our cohort, with its unique demographics and high burden of illiteracy and specific socioeconomic strata, is poorly represented in the legacy derivation cohorts. PREVENT, with its more contemporary data, may better reflect current treatment patterns and risk factor distributions.9

Our data, consistent with the landmark INTERHEART study, which found that standard risk factors operate at different thresholds in South Asians,19 strongly advocate for the development of customized risk algorithms. Key considerations for a South Asia–specific model must include 1) incorporation of ethnicity-specific risk factors (eg, lipoprotein(a), apolipoprotein B), 2) calibration for earlier disease onset, and 3) integration of relevant socioeconomic determinants.13

Beyond risk scores: the potential role of coronary artery calcium in South Asians

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring has emerged as a promising imaging-based tool to overcome limitations of ethnicity-adjusted clinical risk models. Evidence from MASALA and MESA demonstrates that South Asians may have comparable or higher CAC burden despite differences in traditional risk factors, suggesting CAC provides a more objective assessment of subclinical atherosclerosis.20 Recent data also indicate that CAC meaningfully refines contemporary risk equations, including PREVENT, by improving discrimination and reclassifying individuals who may be misclassified by clinical algorithms alone.21 Given the persistent burden of ischemic heart disease in South Asians22 and the suboptimal calibration of traditional risk calculators in this population, incorporation of CAC into risk assessment frameworks may enhance stratification accuracy. However, cost-effectiveness analyses tailored to Indian health care settings are required before widespread implementation.

Study Limitations

Its retrospective, single-center design introduces potential for selection bias and limits generalizability across India's diverse populations. The absence of data on novel biomarkers (eg, lipoprotein(a), apolipoprotein B, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein) prevents a mechanistic exploration of certain misclassifications. Crucially, the lack of a prospective cohort with hard ASCVD endpoints means we can assess comparative classification but not the calibration or true prognostic accuracy of these models in our population; conclusions about whether any model over- or underestimates absolute risk therefore cannot be drawn. The repeated measure ANOVA was applied to the normalized scores and comparisons were made using estimated marginal mean on normalization score. Therefore, the result of this estimated marginal mean should be may not be interpreted as direct absolute values.

Conclusions

Our analysis demonstrates significant disparities in how contemporary cardiovascular risk models classify patients in an Indian AMI cohort. The novel PREVENT score offered a more differentiated risk landscape, likely because of its modern cohort and updated risk factors, yet it still failed to correctly classify ∼80% of patients who ultimately experienced an AMI. The profound discordance with legacy models underscores that the uncritical adoption of any single, internationally derived tool produces substantially different risk classifications in South Asian populations, with important implications for treatment eligibility and preventive strategies. Future efforts must prioritize the development and validation of region-specific risk scores that incorporate ethnicity-specific factors and social determinants or entirely different approaches such as imaging to truly optimize the precision of primary prevention in South Asians.
