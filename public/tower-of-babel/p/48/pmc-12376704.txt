Lithium-doped calcium silicate scaffolds-activated M2-polarized macrophage-derived miR-145-5p-riched extracellular vesicles to enhance osteoimmunomodulation for accelerating bone regeneration
Authors: Ting-You Kuo, Tsung-Li Lin, Yen-Hong Lin, Cheng-Yu Chen, Der-Yang Cho, Yi-Wen Chen, Ming-You Shie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bone healing is intricately associated with dynamic macrophage polarization. Modulating macrophages toward the M2 phenotype has emerged as a promising strategy in bone tissue engineering. Calcium silicate, known for its superior osteoconductivity, is widely used as a bone substitute and is particularly effective in promoting bone tissue regeneration when incorporated with bioactive ions. Recent studies have highlighted lithium’s immunomodulatory effects, with extracellular vesicles (EVs) identified as potential mediators of these actions. Although M2 macrophage-derived EVs (M2-EVs) have been shown to promote bone regeneration, the underlying mechanisms through which biomaterial-stimulated M2-EVs regulate bone regeneration remain unclear. This study investigated the immunomodulatory effects of lithium-doped calcium silicate (LiCS) scaffolds on macrophages and associated inflammatory cytokine profiles. Notably, miR-145-5p was significantly upregulated in both LiCS-stimulated macrophages and their secreted EVs, suggesting a potential regulatory role. Characterization of these miR-145-5p-enriched EVs revealed enhanced anti-inflammatory responses, stimulation of angiogenesis, and upregulation of osteogenic markers in relation to M1 macrophages, mesenchymal stem cells, and endothelial cells. These findings elucidate the molecular basis of LiCS-stimulated M2-EV-regulated bone regeneration via miR-145-5p, providing new insights into developing immunomodulatory biomaterials in regenerative medicine.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Bone healing is a complex, multistep physiological process that relies on the coordinated interplay of diverse cell types, signaling pathways, and molecular mechanisms [1]. Recent research highlights the pivotal regulatory role of macrophage phenotypic plasticity in the early stages of bone repair. Macrophages, as adaptable immune cells, respond to microenvironmental cues by polarizing into either pro-inflammatory M1 or anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes [2–4]. M1 macrophages predominate during the initial phase of bone injury, secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which help eliminate pathogens and necrotic tissue, while initiating the repair cascade [5]. As bone healing progresses, M2 macrophages become increasingly dominant, releasing anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10) and pro-regenerative growth factors (e.g., TGF-β and IGF-1), which promote angiogenesis, osteoblast activation, and new bone formation [6]. Therefore, the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages represents a critical immunological shift in the bone healing process and constitutes a promising therapeutic target for designing next-generation biomaterials [7–9]. In bone tissue engineering, ideal bone graft substitutes must exhibit good degradability, biocompatibility, and osteoinductivity. Currently, immunomodulation strategies that direct macrophage polarization have emerged as effective approaches to enhance bone regeneration. Traditional bone graft materials, including hydroxyapatite, tricalcium phosphate, and bioactive glass, are widely used; however, bone cement and synthetic ceramics often lack osteoinductive properties [10–12].

Calcium silicate has garnered attention in orthopedic applications owing to its superior osteoinductive potential relative to conventional materials [13, 14]. In previous studies, various metal ions have been incorporated into calcium silicate to further enhance its bone regenerative performance. For example, doping with magnesium (Mg) and strontium (Sr) has been shown to promote both angiogenesis and osteogenesis [15]. More recently, lithium (Li)-doped bioceramics have attracted increasing attention for their dual role in promoting bone formation and modulating bone immunity [16–18]. Li ions are known to modulate mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) migration and osteogenic differentiation by activating the Wnt/GSK-3β/β-catenin pathway [19, 20]. Additionally, sustained release of Li ions may promote M2 macrophage polarization via activation of the JAK1/STAT6/STAT3 pathway, subsequently enhancing the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2), which collectively support bone regeneration [21]. Further studies indicated that lithium exhibits immunomodulatory effects in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages. For example, Zhai et al. reported that Li-based bioceramics may favor M2 macrophage polarization, thereby promoting tissue maturation [22]. Therefore, Li-doped CS may exert synergistic effects on multiple cells to enhance bone tissue regeneration.

Recent research has also indicated that extracellular vesicles (EVs) regulate the immune microenvironment and promote tissue repair [23]. EVs are nanoscale (90–150 nm) bilayer-enclosed vesicles present in the secretome of various cell types and are known for their therapeutic potential in tissue regeneration [24, 25]. Among these, MSC-derived EVs are widely used due to their enrichment in growth factors and low immunogenicity [26, 27]; however, more recently, attention has shifted toward M2 macrophage-derived extracellular vesicles (M2EVs) [28–30]. M2EVs are known to inherit diverse anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as TGF-β and IL-10, and express chemokine receptors like (CCR2) from their parent macrophage surface [31]. In a mouse model of rheumatoid arthritis, Kim et al. demonstrated that M2EVs effectively modulated the M1/M2 macrophage balance within synovial tissue [32] and can promote bone regeneration via activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [33]. EVs are enriched in miRNAs, which regulate gene expression during tissue repair [34–36]. Notably, the miRNA content of EVs can be significantly altered when the parent cells are exposed to different stimuli [37–39]. However, the specific miRNA profile alterations in M2EVs following biomaterial stimulation, particularly in the context of bone regeneration, remain poorly characterized.

Currently, the molecular mechanism by which LiCS scaffolds influence selective packaging of miRNAs into EVs remains unclear, particularly regarding how EV-associated miRNA expression patterns diverge from those of their parent cells. Therefore, this study investigates the immunomodulatory effects of LiCS scaffold extract. First, EVs secreted by LiCS-induced M2 macrophages were characterized, and their miRNA profiles were analyzed by next-generation sequencing (NGS). The functional roles of these EVs in immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis were evaluated using M1 macrophages, Wharton’s jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJMSCs), and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), respectively (Fig. 1). Furthermore, gain- and loss-of-function experiments using miRNA mimics and specific inhibitors have confirmed the crucial role of miRNA-145-5p in immunomodulation, macrophage reprogramming, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis. Despite these findings, further investigations are required to elucidate how external stimuli regulate these miRNAs’ selective packaging into EVs. This study aims to clarify how LiCS-stimulated M2EVs regulate bone regeneration, focusing on the regulatory role of EV-associated miRNAs. Understanding these mechanisms will provide valuable insights for developing advanced biomaterials capable of modulating the immune microenvironment to enhance regenerative outcomes.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of LiCS powder and scaffold Preparation

The LiCS bioceramics were synthesized according to previously established laboratory protocols [40]. Briefly, 65 g calcium oxide (CaO), 25 g silicon dioxide (SiO2), and 5 g aluminum oxide (Al2O3) (all from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were uniformly mixed. Subsequently, 10 g of lithium oxide (Li2O; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to dope the material with lithium, replacing an equivalent mass of CaO. The mixed powders were placed in an alumina crucible (SSA-T Saya series, Japan) and sintered at 1400 °C for 2 h, then naturally cooled to room temperature. The sintered powder was wet-milled with agate balls in 99.5% ethanol using a planetary ball mill (Retsch PM-100; Retsch GmbH, Germany), and the mixture was oven-dried. For scaffold preparation, following previously developed methods, the milled LiCS powder was dispersed in ethanol and incorporated into pre-melted polycaprolactone (PCL, Mw = 43,000–50,000, Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA), and continuously stirred until the ethanol completely evaporated. Finally, the mixture was loaded into printing cartridges in the molten state. Using a bioprinting system (BioScaffolder 3.1, GeSiM, Grosserkmannsdorf, Germany), scaffolds were fabricated with 500 μm diameter struts and 500 μm pore spacing by adjusting various printing parameters, including temperature, extrusion pressure, and printing speed. The experimental groups were labelled as CS or LiCS based on the presence of lithium oxide.

Physicochemical analysis of LiCS scaffold

To confirm the crystal phase composition of sintered powder and LiCS scaffolds, high-resolution X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker D8 SSS, Karlsruhe, Germany) was performed at a scanning rate of 1˚/min over the range 2θ = 20°–50°. The mechanical strength of the printed scaffolds was analyzed according to ISO standard 13314:2011 using a mechanical testing machine (EZ-Test; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at a speed of 1 mm/min. The results were presented as stress-strain curves, ultimate break points, and Young’s modulus.

Hydroxyapatite formation was observed using scanning electron microscopy after the scaffolds were immersed in simulated body fluid (SBF) for 7 days and dried. XRD analysis was performed to analyze the hydroxyapatite crystal phase composition of the scaffolds.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were conducted according to the regulations of the China Medical University Animal Center (CMUIACUC-2024-067). A total of 20 New Zealand White rabbits weighing approximately 2 kg were used. Prior to surgery, rabbits were immobilized and anaesthetized using isoflurane gas. After sedation, cylindrical defects (7 mm diameter × 8 mm depth) were created in the distal femur, and scaffolds measuring 6 mm (diameter) × 6 mm (height) were implanted into each defect. The rabbits were sacrificed using carbon dioxide after three days, and the bone tissue surrounding the implants was harvested for analysis.

Histological analysis

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was used to evaluate the tissue response after scaffold implantation. Specifically, 3 days after the CS and LiCS scaffolds were implanted into the experimental animals, femoral samples were collected for paraffin embedding and sectioning. After pretreatment such as antigen retrieval and blocking of endogenous peroxidase, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TNF-α, IL-6, CD163, and CD206, respectively. The next day, the corresponding secondary antibodies were used for incubation at room temperature for 1 h, followed by DAB color development. An optical microscope was used to observe and take pictures, and Image J software was used to count and statistically analyze the positive cells. At the same time, the peripheral blood of experimental animals was collected, and the levels of TNF-α and IL-6 in serum were detected by ELISA. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and statistically analyzed using a t-test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Macrophage differentiation

Human monocytic cells (THP-1) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cell suspensions (5 × 106 cells/mL) were prepared for differentiation into M0, M1, and M2 macrophages. M0 macrophages were generated by treating cells with 200 nM Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma) for 24 h, followed by washing with PBS to remove residual PMA and restabilization in RPMI-1640 for 24 h. M1 macrophages were induced by treating with 10 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Sigma) and 20 ng/mL INF-γ (Sigma) for 24 h. M2 macrophages were induced by culturing cells with 20 ng/mL IL-4 and 20 ng/mL IL-13 for 3 days, and subsequent M2-EV collection as control samples.

Preparation of LiCS scaffold extract

The extracts were prepared according to ISO 10993-12 guidelines. Sterilized LiCS and CS scaffolds were immersed in the RPMI-1640 medium at a ratio of 1 g of scaffold to 5 mL of medium and incubated at 37 °C under agitation (120 rpm) for 24 h. The extracts were filtered through 0.22 μm filters, and concentrations of Ca, Si, and Li ions were analyzed using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES; Perkin-Elmer OPT 1MA 3000DV, Shelton, CT, USA).

Biocompatibility of LiCS extract

To assess the cytocompatibility of the LiCS extract on M0 macrophages, the cells were divided into three groups: control and CS-or LiCS extract-stimulated groups, with six samples per group. M1 macrophages (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates for 24 h, followed by addition of 100 µL/well of different extracts for 1 and 3 days. After removing extracts, PrestoBlue was added and cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer (Infinite Pro M200; Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Macrophage polarization

To evaluate the effect of LiCS extract on macrophage polarization, M0 macrophages were cultured with the extract for 0 and 3 days. M1 and M2 phenotypes were identified using fluorescently labelled monoclonal antibodies against CD86 (Apc-labelled, M1 marker) and CD280 (Pe-labelled, M2 marker). Flow cytometry was performed using a BD Accuri C6 Plus (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA), with a minimum of 10,000 events recorded per sample. Data analysis was conducted using the BD Biosciences software, with unlabeled macrophages serving as controls.

Cytokine and enzyme activity assessment of LiCS extract

The Human Cytokine Antibody Array was used for cytokine analysis. After three days of culture with the extracts, supernatants from M0 macrophages were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 ×g for 10 min. The array membranes were blocked at room temperature for 30 min, followed by overnight incubation with the processed supernatants at 4 °C under gentle agitation. After washing with PBST, a biotin-labeled detection antibody mixture was added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-streptavidin for 30 min. Signals were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. Particular attention was given to the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, TNF-α, VEGF, SDF-1, and angiogenin. Additionally, ELISA was performed to quantify IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, and VEGF expression in M1 macrophages cultured with the extracts.

Extracellular vesicle extraction, purification, and characterization

M2 macrophages served as controls, and M0 macrophages were seeded at 5 × 106 cells per culture dish. After three PBS washes, cells were cultured in serum-free RPMI medium for 24 h, then treated with CS and LiCS extracts for 72 h. Supernatants were collected and sequentially centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min and 2000×g for 15 min to remove cell debris, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane. The filtrate was subjected to tangential flow filtration (MAP.03-plus TFF system; Lefo Science, Taipei, Taiwan) with a 300 kDa molecular weight cut-off membrane to obtain M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV. All EVs were resuspended in PBS and either used immediately at 4 °C or stored at −80 °C for future use.

Particle size and concentration of M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV were analyzed using ZetaView® NTA (ZetaView®; Particle Metrix GmbH, Meerbusch, Germany) based on Brownian motion, with results presented as particle size (X-axis) versus concentration (Y-axis). For morphological analysis, EVs were fixed with 2% formalin for 30 min at room temperature, treated with 1% phosphotungstic acid on copper grids for 2 min, air-dried, and observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL JEM-1400, Tokyo, Japan) at 20–30 kV. Protein concentration was determined using Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). Expression of M2-related markers CD280 and CD206, along with the transmembrane proteins CD146, CD73, CD105, and CD14, were analyzed by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX Nano Flow Cytometer; Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX Nano, Indianapolis, IN, USA).

EV uptake

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV were resuspended in PBS at 1010 exosomes/mL and labeled with ExoGlow dye for 5 min in the dark. After centrifugation at 21,000 × g for 6 min, the supernatant was removed, and the pellets were resuspended in PBS. M1 macrophages, WJMSCs, and HUVECs were cultured on µ-Slides and incubated with labeled EVs for 24 h. After PBS washing, cells were fixed with 4% formalin for 10 minutes, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes, and stained with DAPI and Alexa Fluor™ 594 Phalloidin. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (TCS SP8; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and analyzed using flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6 Plus).

Expression of immunosuppressive factors by EVs

To compare the immunosuppressive effects of M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV on M1 macrophages, EVs were co-cultured with M1 macrophages, for 72 h. Immunosuppressive factors, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, and TGF-β were quantified using ELISA.

In vitro angiogenesis

In vitro angiogenesis was assessed using ECMatrix gel assay. First, 30µL of ECMatrix was added to each well of a 96-well plate and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 1 h. The HUVECs were seeded at a density of 104 cells/well and treated with M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV. After 8 h, images were recorded using an EVOS M7000 Imaging System. Additionally, VEGF expression was measured using ELISA after co-culturing EVs with HUVECs.

Osteogenic differentiation

To evaluate the effect of EVs on osteogenic differentiation, WJMSCs were cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium supplemented with M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV. Expression of osteogenic markers, including ALP, OC, and BSP, was measured by ELISA on days 3, 7, and 14.

RNA sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted using NucleoSpin® RNA kit (Macherey-Nagel GmbH, Düren, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality, quantity, and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) above 8.0 were used for RNA-seq analysis. Barcoded libraries were generated using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 2 × 151 bp paired-end reads. Raw data were filtered using SOAPnuke and heat maps were generated using PheatMap (version 1.0.8). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.4.5) with Q-value ≤ 0.05. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were conducted using Phyper algorithm with Bonferroni correction (Q-value ≤ 0.05).

Functional validation and analysis of miR-145-mediated effects

To validate the functional role of miR-145, M2 macrophages were transfected with 50 nM miR-145-5p mimic or 100 nM miR-145-5p inhibitor using Lipofectamine 3000. EVs secreted by transfected cells were collected for subsequent functional experiments. Flow cytometry was used to evaluate the phenotypic transformation of M1 macrophages after EV treatment by measuring the expression levels of CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker). Additionally, ELISA was performed to measure the protein expression levels in cell culture supernatants, including pro-inflammatory factors (IL-1β, TNF-α), angiogenesis-related factors (VEGF, Ang-1), and osteogenic markers (BSP, OC). All experiments were performed in sextuplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way statistical analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess statistical significance among groups, and Scheffé’s multiple comparison test was performed to determine the significant deviations of each sample. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

Micro-computed tomography (µCT) analysis

Harvested tissue samples containing implants were fixed in formalin and subjected to µCT scanning using a micro-CT system (MIlabs u-CT, Utrecht). 3D reconstruction models were generated using a grayscale threshold of 140 (on a 0-1000 scale, corresponding to 258.7 mg HA/cm3). Bone repair was quantified as BV/TV, and images were analyzed and reconstructed using Avizo software.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data in this study are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), applying one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value of less than 0.05 (*) was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Characterization of 3D-printed LiCS scaffolds

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of CS and LiCS powders (Fig. 2A) revealed distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to Ca2SiO4 at (2θ = 29.6°, 32.7°, and 36.6°) and Ca3SiO5 at (2θ = 31.2° and 34.2°) [41]. Additionally, characteristic peaks of Li2SiO3 were observed at 2θ = 28.3° and 31.4°, indicating successful Li incorporation into the calcium silicate matrix without compromising the structural integrity of CS. Notably, the persistence of CS crystalline phases, suggests that Li incorporation does not significantly alter the basic crystal structure of CS. Figure 2B shows that the appearance of the CS and LiCS scaffolds, fabricated using 3D printing, exhibited controllable heights and pore sizes with excellent structural integrity and uniformity. The designed pore size of approximately 500 μm is critical for maintaining mechanical stability, cell migration, and nutrient diffusion. Previous literature suggests that a pore size range of 300–500 μm optimally supports vascularization and osteogenic proliferation and differentiation [42]. Moreover, this porous structure provides sufficient surface area for cell adhesion and growth factor incorporation. To evaluate the mechanical strength of the scaffolds, static compression tests were performed using a mechanical testing machine. The stress-strain curve (Fig. 2C) indicated no statistically significant differences in compressive strength (Fig. 2D) and Young’s modulus (Fig. 2E) among the groups. The compressive strength ranged from 8.5 ± 1.2 to 9.0 ± 0.9 MPa, increasing linearly with scaffold deformation. Because bone tissue is a load-bearing area, appropriate mechanical strength of scaffolds is crucial during bone repair. Given that native human cancellous bone exhibits compressive strength between 2 and 12 MPa, these findings indicate that the designed scaffolds, despite their high porosity, demonstrate mechanical competence suitable for early-stage bone regeneration [43]. Effective induction of hydroxyapatite deposition by biomaterials is crucial, as it both provides structural support and accelerates bone healing, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes while reducing recovery time. Therefore, hydroxyapatite formation serves as a key indicator for evaluating the performance of bone regeneration materials [44]. In this study, following seven days of immersion in culture medium, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2F) revealed dense hydroxyapatite layers on both CS and LiCS scaffolds. XRD analysis further confirmed hydroxyapatite formation, with a characteristic peak at 2θ = 25.9°, and the LiCS scaffolds showing the highest peak intensity (Fig. 2G). Ion release curves (Fig. 2H) showed that calcium (Ca2+) concentrations in culture media were highest in the control group (1.75 mM ± 0.04 mM), slightly lower in CS (1.41 mM ± 0.13mM), and lowest in LiCS (1.35 mM ± 0.12 mM), likely due to Ca2+ binding with free hydroxyl groups and subsequent precipitation onto scaffold surfaces. Silicon (Si) concentrations were comparable between CS (0.92 mM ± 0.08 mM) and LiCS (0.85 mM ± 0.07 mM), but negligible in the control. Previous research by Shie et al. found that Si ions promote osteoblast differentiation, independent of osteoinductive factors [45]. In this current study, Li ion concentration in LiCS scaffold immersion showed approximately 0.76 mM ± 0.09 mM, which is equivalent to 5.27 µg/mL. This is well below the cytotoxic threshold of 14.56 µg/mL reported by Liu et al., indicating that the scaffolds in this study are non-cytotoxic and are suitable for subsequent extract preparation [46].

In vivo inflammatory response

Growing evidence suggests the critical role of immune modulation in bone repair. Effective repair involves not only osteoblast activation and differentiation but also coordinated immune cell participation [47]. Upon bone injury, circulating monocytes infiltrate the site and differentiate into immune cells (such as macrophages), which subsequently influence the progression of bone tissue repair by adopting either pro-inflammatory (M1-type) or anti-inflammatory (M2-type) factors [48]. Previous studies have demonstrated that lithium can promote M2 polarization by regulating inflammatory cytokine expression, potentially creating a bone regenerative microenvironment [49]. To verify lithium’s immunomodulatory effects, scaffolds were retrieved three days post-implantation, and local inflammatory markers were evaluated via immunohistochemistry (IHC). M1 macrophages markers (TNF-α and IL-6) and M2 macrophages markers (CD163 and CD206) were examined. Additionally, blood levels of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines were measured. IHC (Fig. 3A) revealed that tissues surrounding CS scaffolds exhibited elevated expression of pro-inflammatory factors, including TNF-α (Fig. 3B) and IL-6 (Fig. 3C), three days post-implantation. In contrast, LiCS-implanted tissues showed significantly reduced M1 markers, while demonstrating increased expression of M2-associated markers such as CD163 (Fig. 3D) and CD206 (Fig. 3E). Quantitative analyses confirmed these observations. Furthermore, serum cytokine profiling three days post-implantation demonstrated that pro-inflammatory factor levels of TNF-α (Fig. 3F) and IL-1β (Fig. 3G) in the CS scaffold group were approximately 40% higher than those in the LiCS scaffold group. These findings collectively confirmed that lithium incorporation effectively suppressed both local and systemic tissue inflammatory responses.

LiCS stimulates Macrophage-derived EVs

Based on the IHC results, to further elucidate how LiCS scaffolds promote bone tissue repair through immunomodulation at femoral defect sites, a series of systematic in vitro analyses were conducted using CS and LiCS extracts (Fig. 4A). Cytotoxicity analysis of the extracts on macrophages (Fig. 4B), demonstrated no significant differences in macrophage viability across groups after three days of culture, indicating the biocompatibility of both scaffold extracts. In vitro experiments simulated macrophage polarization, as illustrated in Fig. 4C. When M0 macrophages were exposed to CS and LiCS extracts, the decrease in CD86 and increase in CD280 expression indicated macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype. Notably, LiCS-treated macrophages showed significantly higher expression of M2 markers compared to those treated with CS, suggesting that lithium incorporation further promotes anti-inflammatory responses. Although the initial inflammatory response following implantation is predominantly M1-mediated, it typically involves blood monocytes traversing the vessel wall and differentiating into M0 macrophages, with subsequent polarization direction determined by the inflammatory microenvironment [50]. The presence of pro-inflammatory factors around the implant drives macrophages toward M1 polarization, hence our experimental design started with freshly activated M0 macrophages. Human Cytokine Antibody Array analysis (Fig. 4D) revealed the secretion profiles of immune-related factors and angiogenic factors from stimulated macrophages. Compared to the control group, LiCS treatment significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β (Fig. 4E) and TNF-α (Fig. 4F), with this inhibitory effect being more pronounced than in CS. Simultaneously, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 4G) was upregulated in both CS and LiCS treatment groups, indicating LiCS extract’s capacity to promote anti-inflammatory factor secretion. Regarding angiogenic factors, VEGF showed the most significant upregulation in LiCS, with SDF-1 and angiogenin following similar trends (Fig. 4H). The expression of these three factors was lithium-dependent, with the LiCS group showing more pronounced promotional effects. ELISA analysis monitored changes in different cytokines over a three-day culture period. Specifically, pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α showed significant reduction in the LiCS group compared to both Ctl and CS groups after three days of culture. Conversely, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 and angiogenic factor VEGF demonstrated notable upregulation in the LiCS group. Particularly after three days of culture, IL-10 and VEGF concentrations in the LiCS group reached peak levels, showing statistically significant differences compared to the control group. These results further confirm the dual functionality of CS-based scaffolds in suppressing inflammatory responses and promoting angiogenesis, with LiCS demonstrating superior efficacy, particularly evident after three days of culture.

Characterization of CS- and LiCS-stimulated macrophage-derived EVs

Based on the finding that CS and LiCS extracts can effectively promote M2 polarization, the characteristics and biological functions of EVs secreted by these material-stimulated macrophages were further investigated [51]. We hypothesized that such polarization may enhance the bone regenerative therapeutic properties of EVs by functionally modulating key biological processes (Fig. 5A). TEM images (Fig. 5B) showed that EVs derived from all groups (M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV) exhibited typical spherical morphology with intact bilayer membranes. Particle size analysis (Fig. 5C) showed that all three groups of EVs display unimodal distribution with peaks concentrated around 100 nm, with concentrations ranging between 0 and 9 × 10⁶ particles/mL. Western blotting (Fig. 5D) verified the presence of characteristic EV markers (ALIX, HSP70, CD9, CD63 and Calnexin) in all groups. Total protein quantification (Fig. 5E) showed that compared to M2EV, CSM2EV and LiCSM2EV groups contained significantly higher total protein content, with LiCSM2EV showing the highest (p < 0.05), indicating that material stimulation may enhance protein secretion in EVs. To verify the characteristics of the obtained EVs, the expression of different surface markers was evaluated, including CD206 and CD280 (M2-related), CD73, CD146, CD14, and CD105, based on their established roles in angiogenesis and osteogenesis (Fig. 5F). Specifically, CD73 and CD146 have been proven to have significant pro-angiogenic effects [52, 53]; CD14-positive cells exhibit osteoinductive potential; and CD105, highly expressed in regenerating endothelial cells, participates in tissue repair and angiogenesis by regulating the TGF-β signaling pathway [54, 55]. Flow cytometry analysis showed that in the expression of CD206/CD280, CD73/CD146, and CD14/CD105 marker pairs, LiCSM2EV demonstrates the highest double-positive ratios across all marker pairs, indicating that after LiCS stimulation, macrophage-secreted LiCSM2EV exhibits enhanced functional characteristics, particularly in angiogenesis and osteoinduction.

Expression of immunosuppressive factors by EVs

The effects of EVs on macrophage polarization were further investigated in vitro (Fig. 6A). Confocal microscopy (Fig. 6B) revealed effective internalization of ExoGlow-labelled EVs (green) into macrophages, confirmed by co-staining with F-actin (red) and nuclear (blue). M1 macrophages internalized a greater amount of LiCSM2EV compared to CSM2EV and M2EV. Quantitative analysis by flow cytometry confirmed that LiCSM2EV demonstrated the highest EV uptake efficiency (73.1%), significantly higher than those of CSM2EV (63.5%) and M2EV (41.1%) (Fig. 6C). To understand the role of the LiCSM2EV in modulating the M1 macrophage phenotype. Then, the levels of M1 macrophage-specific marker CD86 and the M2 macrophage-specific marker CD280 were analyzed after 3 days of culture with different EV (Fig. 6D). Compared to the M2EV, the expression of CD86 decreased significantly from 38.3 to 11.0% with LiCSM2EV. In the case of CD280, the levels enhanced 15.4%, 26.0%, and 37.0% with the addition of M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV, respectively. ELISA analysis of immune factors following 3 days of LiCSM2EV co-culture significantly reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β (Fig. 6E) and TNF-α (Fig. 6F) in macrophages (P < 0.05), while markedly increasing anti-inflammatory factors IL-10 (Fig. 6G) and TGF-β (Fig. 6H) expression (P < 0.05). These findings suggest that LiCSM2EV may facilitate M2 polarization by regulating cytokine secretion. The immunomodulatory effects observed are likely mediated by the delivery of bioactive cargos, such as TGF-β and specific anti-inflammatory miRNAs, which have been implicated in the direct reprogramming of macrophages toward an M2 phenotype [56]. Recent studies have shown that enhancing M2 macrophage polarization and utilizing their derived EVs can significantly facilitate bone regeneration. For instance, Cheng et al. demonstrated that M2-EVs promoted osteogenesis and improved titanium implant osseointegration under diabetic conditions [28]. Likewise, Liu et al. reported that macrophage-derived small EVs enhanced biomimetic collagen-mediated endogenous bone regeneration by modulating the immune microenvironment [51]. These examples highlight the dual potentials of M2-EVs in orchestrating immunomodulatory and osteoinductive functions to accelerate bone healing.

In vitro angiogenesis

The effects of M2EVs on angiogenesis in HUVECs were further investigated in vitro (Fig. 7A). Similar to M1 macrophages, LiCSM2EV demonstrated greater uptake (Fig. 7B), with flow cytometry indicating that LiCSM2EV exhibited the highest cellular uptake efficiency (73.0%), compared to CSM2EV (55.3%) and M2EV (40.8%) (Fig. 7C). In vitro angiogenesis experiments (Fig. 7D), by measuring the total number of loops (Fig. 7E) and branch points (Fig. 7F) in tubular structures, revealed that HUVECs co-cultured with LiCSM2EV significantly enhanced tube formation capability. Further VEGF secretion analysis showed that, compared to the other groups, LiCSM2EV-treated HUVECs exhibited the highest VEGF secretion levels after 3 days of co-culture (Fig. 7G), collectively underscoring the pro-angiogenic potential of LiCSM2EV. The enhanced angiogenic effect observed may be attributed to the enrichment of angiogenesis-associated surface markers, such as CD146 and CD73, which are known to facilitate endothelial cell migration and tube formation [57]. In addition, EVs have been shown to deliver pro-angiogenic factors, including VEGF and miRNAs, which synergistically activate angiogenic signaling pathways in endothelial cells [58]. These findings align with recent studies reporting that promoting M2 macrophage polarization and harvesting their derived exosomes can significantly enhance angiogenesis and wound remodeling [59]. The superior angiogenesis-promoting ability of LiCSM2EV highlights its potential for vascularized tissue engineering applications.

Osteogenic differentiation assay of Li-M2-EV

The effects of M2EVs on osteogenesis in WJMSCs were further investigated in vitro (Fig. 8A). To evaluate the effects of EVs on WJMSC osteogenic differentiation, immunofluorescence staining results showed that among the three groups (M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV), LiCSM2EV was more readily taken up by WJMSCs (Fig. 8B). Flow cytometry confirmed that LiCSM2EVs were internalized by 91.9% of WJMSCs, which was significantly higher than CSM2EV (81.4%) and M2EV (70.6%) (Fig. 8C). Analysis of osteogenic differentiation-related proteins revealed that with increased culture time, WJMSCs treated with LiCSM2EVs exhibited significantly higher alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity (Fig. 8D), greater expression of bone sialoprotein (BSP, Fig. 8E) and osteocalcin (OC, Fig. 8F), compared to the other groups, with LiCSM2EV reaching peak values at 14 days of culture (p < 0.05). This enhanced osteogenic differentiation may be attributed to the elevated expression of osteogenesis-related surface markers, such as CD105 and CD146, present on LiCSM2EV. These markers are implicated in promoting MSC osteogenic commitment via TGF-β signaling and vascular niche interactions [60, 61]. Furthermore, EVs have been widely recognized as potent paracrine mediators facilitating osteogenesis by delivering osteoinductive proteins and regulatory RNAs to target cells [62].

RNA sequencing analysis

Although the preceding experiments demonstrate that EVs derived from LiCS-stimulated macrophages possess multiple regulatory functions, including immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and bone regeneration, further investigation is warranted. With the rapid advancement and widespread adoption of next-generation sequencing (NGS), this technology has become an essential analytical tool for exploring biological mechanisms. NGS enables comprehensive analysis of gene expression dynamics and non-coding RNA regulatory networks, offering new insights into the molecular mechanisms by which LiCS regulates macrophage-derived EVs. Through NGS analysis, the miRNA profiles of EVs was systematically characterized to elucidate their specific mechanisms in immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis. The results showed significant gene expression differences between CSM2EV and LiCSM2EV. Volcano plot analysis identified 273 differentially expressed genes—159 genes downregulated and 114 upregulated (Fig. 9A). Heatmap analysis of differentially expressed genes demonstrated distinct expression patterns between the two sample groups (Fig. 9B). Gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis revealed that these differentially expressed genes are mainly involved in stem cell differentiation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and regulation of multiple signaling cascades, including the MAPK, Wnt, and VEGF (Fig. 9C and D). Notably, miRNA profiling revealed significant upregulation of several functional miRNAs in LiCSM2EV (Fig. 9E). These miRNAs can be classified into three major categories based on their known biological roles: anti-inflammatory (miR-145 and miR-122) [63, 64]. pro-angiogenic (miR-130a and miR-10b) [65, 66], and osteogenic (miR-375 and miR-154) [67, 68]. Heatmap analysis confirmed elevated expression of these miRNAs in LiCSM2EV, suggesting that LiCS-stimulated EVs may exert important regulatory effects on anti-inflammation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis via miRNA-mediated pathways. These results corroborate our previous analysis of EV membrane proteins, which showed that LiCSM2EVs are enriched in functional membrane proteins such as CD73, CD146, CD14, and CD105. These membrane proteins play multiple roles in tissue regeneration: CD73 inhibits immune cell activation by hydrolyzing extracellular ATP to adenosine, thereby regulating the inflammatory microenvironment; CD14, a key immunomodulatory molecule, regulates macrophage polarization and promotes the formation of the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [54]. CD146 facilitates vascular endothelial cell proliferation and migration and stimulates the secretion of angiogenic factors, accelerating angiogenesis [69]. CD105 enhances bone tissue regeneration by regulating the TGF-β signaling pathway and promoting MSC differentiation into osteoblasts [55]. Multi-omics cross-validation further confirmed the multiple biological functions of LiCSM2EV in tissue regeneration.

Mechanistic role of Li-enhanced miR-145-5p in EV-mediated tissue regeneration

To analyze the regulatory mechanism of Li ions, miRNA sequencing of CSM2EV and LiCSM2EV was performed to identify the differences in miRNA expression between the two EV groups. Among these, miR-145 expression in LiCSM2EV was significantly higher than that in CSM2EV, making it one of the most differentially expressed miRNAs, and a prime candidate for subsequent experiments. The regulatory effects of Li ions on miR-145 expression in macrophages (Fig. 10A) and their secreted EVs (Fig. 10B), showing that compared to both untreated M2 macrophages and CSM2, macrophages stimulated with the LiCS extract (LiCSM2) exhibited significantly increased intracellular miR-145 expression (Fig. 10A). Analysis of the miR-145 content in EVs secreted by these cells (M2EV, CSM2EV, and LiCSM2EV) revealed that LiCSM2EV contained notably higher miR-145 content than the other groups (Fig. 10B), indicating that Li enhances cellular miR-145 expression and subsequently produces EVs that maintain higher miR-145 content. To confirm the functional roles of miR-145, M2 macrophages were overexpressed in miR-145-5p using mimics or inhibitors. To verify transfection effectiveness (Fig. 10C), changes in miR-145-5p content in EVs secreted by these cells with modified miR-145-5p expression were also confirmed (Fig. 10D), demonstrating that intracellular miR-145-5p expression levels directly influenced the content of secreted EVs.

EVs with regulated miR-145-5p expression were subsequently used in functional experiments. When co-cultured with M1 macrophages, these EVs exerted significant immunomodulatory effects. Flow cytometry revealed that EVs containing miR-145-5p effectively suppressed M1 marker CD86 expression (Fig. 10E), while upregulating M2 marker CD206 expression (Fig. 10F), indicating successful modulation of the macrophage phenotype. Correspondingly, pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β (Fig. 10G) and TNF-α (Fig. 10H) were significantly suppressed, creating an anti-inflammatory microenvironment. In the angiogenesis assay, these miR-145-5p-enriched EVs significantly enhanced VEGF (Fig. 10I) and Ang-1 (Fig. 10J) expression in the target cells. Similarly, in the osteogenic differentiation assay, these EVs promoted the expression of the bone markers BSP (Fig. 10K) and OC (Fig. 10L). However, when miR-145-5p was inhibited, these EV-mediated biological effects were significantly suppressed. Previous studies have established the crucial role of miR-145 in regulating multiple biological processes. In immunomodulation, miR-145 exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the TLR4 signaling pathway, and thereby reducing the production of pro-inflammatory factors [70]. During angiogenesis, miR-145 regulates vascular endothelial cell proliferation and migration by targeting the VEGF signaling pathway, thereby affecting blood vessel formation [71]. Furthermore, in osteogenesis, miR-145 can influence osteogenic differentiation by regulating transcription factors such as Runx2 and Osterix, and by modulating SMAD3 signaling pathway. These findings collectively reveal an important regulatory mechanism in which Li upregulates miR-145 expression in macrophages, promoting the release of EVs enriched with miR-145-5p, which in turn modulate key biological processes, including immune response, angiogenesis, and osteogenic differentiation, ultimately contributing to tissue regeneration [72]. This discovery not only elucidates a new mechanism of Li ion regulation of macrophage function but also provides new insights for developing EV-based tissue engineering strategies.

In vivo bone regeneration

Building upon previous experiments demonstrating the effects of Li + ions on immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis, the bone regenerative capacity of CS or LiCS scaffolds was evaluated using a rabbit femoral defect model. After 4 and 8 weeks of implantation with either CS or LiCS scaffolds, bone regeneration was assessed via micro-CT examination (Fig. 11A). The Bone Volume/Total Volume (BV/TV) and Trabecular Thickness (Tb.Th) ratios were calculated. In bone microstructure analysis, BV/TV represents the bone volume fraction, quantifying the proportion of bone tissue volume within the region of interest, while Tb.Th indicates trabecular bone thickness; both are key parameters for evaluating bone quality. The micro-CT results showed that LiCS scaffolds significantly increased both BV/TV (Fig. 11B) and Tb.Th (Fig. 11C) compared to the CS group at both time points, indicating enhanced bone tissue formation with a denser trabecular structure. At 4 and 8 weeks post-implantation, histological examination was performed on the harvested tissue sections using hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Masson’s trichrome (MT), and von Kossa (VK) staining (Fig. 11D). HE staining revealed more organized tissue architecture in the LiCS groups compared to CS groups at both time points. MT staining (blue) demonstrated more extensive collagen formation in the LiCS groups, which was further confirmed by quantitative analysis showing significantly higher collagen area percentage in LiCS groups (Fig. 11E). The VK staining showed more extensive mineralization in LiCS groups, particularly at 8 weeks, with quantitative analysis revealing significantly higher new mineral area percentage (Fig. 11F).

The enhanced bone regeneration observed with LiCS scaffolds can be attributed to several interconnected mechanisms. Li ions are known to activate the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway by inhibiting GSK3β, which plays a crucial role in osteogenic differentiation and bone formation. Through this pathway, Li + promotes osteoblast differentiation while enhancing the expression of bone-specific proteins and mineralization [49, 73]. This is evidenced by our micro-CT analysis, which demonstrated significant increases in bone volume fraction and trabecular architecture in the LiCS group. The increased collagen deposition observed in our MT staining further supports the role of Li ions in enhancing extracellular matrix formation and organization. Beyond exploring the mechanism of scaffold-mediated bone regeneration, LiCS scaffolds also exhibit remarkable immunomodulatory functions, where Li ions not only promote bone regeneration but also exert potent immunomodulatory effects through the regulation of macrophage phenotypes. Our immunohistochemical staining results demonstrated increased M2 marker expression and decreased M1 marker expression in the LiCS group, confirming that Li ions effectively promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 (anti-inflammatory) phenotype. This phenotypic transition creates an optimal environment for bone healing through coordinated inflammatory responses. Moreover, Li ions has been shown not to only promote M2 polarization but also enhance angiogenesis through the TGF-β/Snail pathway, corresponding to our in vitro findings of LiCS-enhanced vascularization [16, 22]. Yang et al.‘s research further substantiated these findings, confirming that lithium chloride exerts significant immunomodulatory effects capable of effectively regulating inflammatory responses and promoting bone tissue repair [74]. These research findings collectively elucidate the dual mechanism of Li ions in bone regeneration: directly promoting bone formation and concurrently modulating immune microenvironment to optimize the repair process, underscoring the exceptional therapeutic potential of LiCS scaffolds in bone tissue engineering applications.

Conclusion

In this study, the immunomodulatory effects of lithium and its role in promoting bone tissue regeneration via regulation of macrophages and secreted extracellular vesicles (EVs) were systematically investigated. The findings revealed that Li promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 (anti-inflammatory) phenotype, creating a pro-regenerative immune microenvironment. Notably, Li + significantly increases the expression of miR-145-5p in both macrophages and their secreted EVs. These miR-145-5p-enriched EVs attenuate inflammation, promote angiogenesis, and upregulate osteogenic markers. In a rabbit femoral defect model, LiCS demonstrated superior bone tissue regeneration capabilities, exhibiting higher bone volume fraction and trabecular thickness, improved tissue integration, and enhanced mineralization. In conclusion, this study elucidates the molecular mechanism by which Li + modulates immune response, angiogenesis, and bone formation through the regulation of miR-145-5p in macrophage-derived EVs. This study not only advances the understanding of Li-mediated bone tissue regeneration mechanisms but also provides novel insights into the design of immunomodulatory biomaterials, and establishes a theoretical foundation for the application of EVs in tissue engineering.
