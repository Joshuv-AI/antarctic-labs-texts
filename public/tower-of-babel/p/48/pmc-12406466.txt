Exploring the effects of altered concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents on the incidence risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus and glucose and lipid indicators: a cohort study in Northwestern China
Authors: Minzhen Wang, Chen Sang, Yinqian He, Hao Chen, Jia Jia, Shan Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and its constituents (SO42−, NO3−, NH4+, OM, BC) is strongly linked to the incidence of diabetes. This study aimed to assess the health benefits of improved air quality on the incidence risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsData from 19,884 participants in the Jinchang Cohort were analyzed. The concentration levels of PM2.5 and its constituents were obtained from the China Air Pollution Tracking (TAP) dataset. The effects of reduced pollutant concentrations on the risk of T2DM and glucose and lipid indicators were assessed via Cox proportional hazards models and generalized linear models (GLMs). Exposure‒response relationships were plotted, with subgroup analyses by sex and age. The combined effects of multiple pollutants were also evaluated.ResultsA decrease in the concentration of mixed pollutants was significantly associated with a reduced risk of T2DM (HR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.52,0.62), as well as significantly lower levels of fasting plasma glucose variation (ΔFPG; β = -0.62, 95% CI: -0.79, -0.45), total cholesterol variation (ΔTC; β = -0.62, 95% CI: -0.98, -0.26), triglyceride variation (ΔTG; β = -5.08, 95% CI: -6.76, -3.40), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol variation (ΔLDL-C; β = -3.99, 95% CI: -4.42, -3.55). In comparison, similar inverse associations were also observed in single-pollutant models. The effects appeared to be slightly stronger among females and older adults than among males and younger individuals.ConclusionReductions in the concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents were significantly associated with a lower risk of T2DM and improvements in glucose and lipid metabolism, particularly for decreases in mixed pollutant concentrations. Subgroup analyses suggested that women and older adults may be the primary beneficiaries of improved air qualities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24282-y.

Background

In recent years, as research on the association between environmental exposures and metabolic diseases has advanced, air pollution—particularly fine particulate matter (PM2.5) has increasingly been recognized as a potential environmental risk factor for metabolic disorders [1], – [2] beyond traditional risk factors such as obesity, dietary patterns, and genetic susceptibility [3]. – [4] Due to its small aerodynamic diameter and long atmospheric residence time, PM2.5 can adsorb various toxic substances and undergo complex physicochemical reactions on its surface. Its major sources include coal combustion, motor vehicle emissions, industrial activities, and construction dust [5], which contribute to the heterogeneity and enhanced toxicity of its chemical composition [6]. Epidemiological studies have found that PM2.5 is not only associated with respiratory [7]– [8] and cardiovascular diseases [9]– [10] but is also significantly linked to metabolic dysregulation and the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [11]. – [12] A growing body of evidence suggests that PM2.5 exposure may disrupt glucose and lipid metabolism by triggering systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, adipose tissue dysfunction, insulin resistance, and endothelial impairment—mechanisms that are similarly implicated in the effects of its chemical constituents on T2DM [13]. – [14] In previous studies, both our team and other researchers have reported significant associations between exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents and increased risk of T2DM, as well as elevated levels of fasting plasma glucose(FPG) and lipid parameters [15–17]. For example, our previous study revealed a significant positive joint effect with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.27 (95% CI: 1.09, 1.49) for each IQR (Interquartile range) increase in PM2.5 on the increased incidence of T2DM [18]. An NHIS-NSC cohort study in South Korea revealed that when PM2.5 concentrations were increased by one IQR, there was a significant positive effect on the incidence of T2DM [19]. – [20] When the PM2.5 concentration was in the fourth quartile (Q4), FPG was elevated by 1.18 (95% CI: 0.886, 1.475) mg/dl. A study from Mexico reported that Serum total cholesterol (TC) and low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) increased by 2.25 mg/dl and 2.66 mg/dl [21], respectively, for every 10 µg/m3 increase in exposure to PM2.5. In contrast, few studies have investigated the beneficial health effects of improved air quality, and most of these studies have focused on the effects of a reduced risk of hypertension morbidity and mortality [22], – [23] increased life expectancy [24], and improved lung function and respiratory symptoms [25]. – [26].

Since 2013, China has been implementing the Action Plan for Prevention and Control of Air Pollution [27], which focuses on combating the primary air pollutant, PM2.5 [28]. The implementation of strategies aimed at reducing coal combustion and vehicle emissions, along with other related measures [29], has produced significant reductions in PM2.5 concentrations across most regions of China. A study has shown that PM2.5 concentrations in the northwest region showed a downward trend during this period [30]. Despite the progress made in reducing PM2.5 levels, it is unclear whether the reduction of atmospheric pollutant concentrations brings about health benefits in terms of lowering the incidence of T2DM. Furthermore, it is uncertain whether management of environmental pollutants should be a priority strategy for the prevention and control of T2DM.

Jinchang city is located in northwestern China, where air pollution is a serious problem due to the region’s harsh natural ecological environment, complex geomorphology, floating and dusty weather patterns, and industrial pollution. Furthermore, the overall prevalence rates of diabetes and prediabetes in the Jinchang cohort population were 7.5% and 16.8%, respectively [31]. which represents a significant public health concern. In view of the dual needs of air pollution control and disease prevention, a prospective cohort study was implemented with the Jinchang cohort as a platform, with the cohort matched at baseline and after the first follow-up visit, to investigate the potential health benefits of changes in the concentration of PM2.5 and its constituents (sulfate [SO42−], nitrate [NO3−], ammonium [NH4+]), organic matter [OM] and black carbon [BC]) on the risk of T2DM and FPG and lipid indicators (serum total cholesterol [TC], triglyceride [TG], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HLD-C]) and to investigate whether air quality improvement can effectively address related public health problems. This study provides a basis for the development of future air pollution prevention and control measures.

Methods

Study population

Participants were derived from a fixed longitudinal cohort study conducted in Gansu Province, China, known as the Jinchang Cohort [32]. This cohort includes all current and retired employees of the Jinchuang Group Co., Ltd. in Jinchang City. This study is based on individuals who completed the baseline and first follow-ups between 2011 and 2015. From June 2011 to December 2013, a total of 48,001 participants were enrolled at baseline. Among them, 14,646 participants were lost to follow-up due to relocation to other geographical areas, job changes, or death [32]. Between January 2014 and December 2015, the first follow-up was completed for 33,355 participants, and no new participants were added during the follow-up period. A total of 11,652 individuals were excluded due to missing information on residential address (n = 11,511) and incomplete data on glucose and lipid indicators (n = 141). To ensure the accuracy of the findings, we further excluded 14,646 participants who had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes at baseline. This left a total of 19,884 individuals for further analysis, with the aim of investigating the impact of PM2.5 and its constituents on the risk of T2DM and the associated glucose and lipid indicators (Fig S1).

A self-designed structured questionnaire was used, and following the acquisition of informed consent from the study participants, the survey was conducted by investigators who had undergone uniform training in the form of face‒to-face interviews. The survey information included demographic and sociological characteristics (gender, age, education, marital status, occupation, income, etc.), lifestyle factors (smoking, drinking, vegetable and fruit intake, exercise, etc.), past disease history (cancer, respiratory diseases, circulatory diseases, endocrine and metabolic diseases, etc.), family history of diseases, occupational history and other relevant information.

The physical examination was carried out by professional medical staff at the Physical Examination Centre of Jinchuan Company Staff Hospital and included height, weight, and blood pressure. Height and weight were measured by computerized body scales (Model: SK-X80/TCS-160D-W/H) produced by Shenzhen Shuangjia Electronics Co., Ltd. The participants stood barefoot in the centre of the scales, with their torsos naturally straight, heads aligned, and eyes looking straight ahead. The measurement results were accurate to one decimal point. Blood pressure was measured via an electronic sphygmomanometer (Model: BP705) manufactured by AMPall (South Korea). The subjects rested calmly for 30 min before measurement and then had two consecutive measurements of blood pressure in the sitting position; if the measured blood pressure value was higher than 140 mmHg/90 mmHg, a retest was needed, and the average of the two consecutive measurements was recorded. On the day of the physical examination, fasting venous blood was collected from the study subjects, and FPG, TC, TG, LDL-C and HDL-C were measured by a fully automatic biochemical analyser from Hitachi (model: 7600–020).

Outcome ascertainment

In accordance with the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Control of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (2020 Edition) [33], T2DM is defined as an FPG ≥ 126 mg/dl and/or self-reported clinical diagnosis of T2DM (patients are required to provide diagnostic certificates from hospitals of the second level or above) and/or self-reported use of antidiabetic drugs. Prediabetes was defined as 110 mg/dl ≤ FPG < 126 mg/dl.

According to the Chinese Guidelines for Prevention and Control of Dyslipidemia in Adults (2016 Revision) [34], the diagnostic criteria include TC ≥ 240 mg/dl, TG ≥ 200 mg/dl, LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dl, HDL-C < 40 mg/dl, self-reported clinical diagnosis of dyslipidemia (patients are required to provide diagnostic certificates from hospitals of the second level or above), and self-reported use of lipid-lowering drugs. Dyslipidaemia was defined as any of the above criteria.

Pollutant exposure assessment

In this study, the daily mean concentration data of PM2.5 and its five constituents (SO42−, NO3−, NH4+, OM and BC) were obtained from the TAP (Tracking Air Pollution in China, http://tapdata.org/) website for the period between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2015 in Jinchang [35], – [36] Gansu Province. The spatial matching of the latitude and longitude of the study participants’ home addresses to the 10-km resolution pollutant data was conducted using the time of the physical examination as the matching date. This was done to assess the exposure levels of individual PM2.5 and its constituents. Given that all participants were current or retired employees of the Jinchuan Group and received biennial health examinations conducted locally in Jinchang, their residential locations were considered geographically stable and consistent with their registered addresses. This regular on-site follow-up approach minimized potential exposure misclassification and enhanced the validity of pollutant exposure assessment based on local ambient air quality data.

Covariates

In light of the findings of previous studies examining the relationship between PM2.5 exposure and diabetes [2, 12, 37, 38], a comprehensive set of covariates was selected for analysis, as these variables have the potential to act as confounding factors or influence health outcomes. These included age; sex; body mass index (BMI; kg/m²); monthly household income per capita; educational level; marital status; occupation; physical activity; smoking; drinking status; weekly consumption of fruits and vegetables; occupational exposure to gases, dust, or fumes; presence of high blood pressure; and family history of T2DM.

Statistical methods

The general characteristics of the baseline population were analysed with descriptive statistics, expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{\text{x}\:}\pm\:\text{s}$$\end{document}) or median and interquartile range (IQR); categorical variables were expressed as the number of cases and proportions (n, %). Differences between groups were compared via the chi-square test.

The annual average concentrations of PM2.5 and five constituents (SO42−, NO3−, NH4+, OM and BC) in Jinchang city from 2010 to 2015 were described, and the trends of pollutants were analysed via the Spearman rank correlation coefficient method [39]. RS < 0 indicates a decreasing trend, and vice versa; when RS is greater than the critical value WP in the rank correlation coefficient table, it indicates that the trend of change is statistically significant.

A Cox proportional hazards model was fitted to assess the associations between changes in PM2.5 and its constituents and the risk of T2DM, with the presence or absence of new-onset T2DM in the follow-up population as the dependent variable. Changes in the concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents were defined as the 1-year lagged mean pollutant exposure at the follow-up survey minus the 1-year lagged mean exposure at the baseline survey [22, 40], and changes in glucose and lipids were defined as the concentration measured at follow-up minus the concentration measured at baseline [41]. A GLM was used to explore the effects of changes in PM2.5 and its constituents on changes in glucose and lipid indicators, adjusting for confounding factors such as sex, age, BMI, education, occupation, monthly household income per capita, marital status, and lifestyle habits, to construct a single-pollutant model.

In addition, the pollutant data were categorized into tertiles (T1, T2, and T3) based on the 33.3th percentile and 66.7th percentile to assess the effects of improving and deteriorating air quality on the risk of T2DM and the levels of glucose and lipids. Specifically, T1, T2, and T3 represent the lowest, middle, and highest thirds of pollutant concentration distributions, respectively, with T1 corresponding to relatively better air quality and T3 indicating relatively poorer air quality. The effect estimates were expressed in units of change per 0.1 µg/m3 reduction in the changing concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents. Dose‒response relationship analyses were performed via restricted cubic spline functions, which were stratified by sex and age. Finally, quantile g-computation (QgC) was used to explore the joint effects of changes in exposure concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituent mixture on the risk of T2DM and changes in glucose and lipid index levels.

Data collation, analysis, and graphing were performed via R version 4.4.1, SPSS version 25.0 and GraphPad Prism version 8. All analyses were performed via a two-sided test with α = 0.05, with P < 0.05 indicating statistically significant results.

Results

Characteristics of the baseline population

A total of 19,884 participants (56.22% men) had a mean age of 48.18 (standard deviation, 8.15) years at baseline (Table S1). Among them, the highest proportion (78.80%) were workers, with the main types of work being electricians, pliers, belt workers, crushers, etc. A total of 5,783 people (29.08%) experienced high blood pressure. In addition, in the total population, 791 new-onset diabetes cases were reported, and 19,093 nondiabetic cases were reported. The glucose and lipid levels of the different populations are shown in the table.

Trends in air pollutants from 2010 to 2015

The average concentrations of PM2.5, SO42-, NO3-, NH4+, OM and BC in Jinchang city were 35.53, 4.20, 4.53, 3.31, 6.82, and 1.44 µg/m3, respectively, from 2010 to 2015. During this period, the concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents (SO42-, NO3-, NH4+, OM and BC) exhibited an overall decreasing trend, with decreases of 0.43, 0.27, 0.09, 0.02, 0.31, and 0.16 µg/m³, respectively. With the exception of NO3-, all the pollutant concentrations reached their lowest values in 2013 (Fig. S2). The results of the Spearman rank correlation coefficient method indicated that the rank correlation coefficients (Rs) of the annual mean concentrations of PM2.5, SO42-, NO3-, NH4+, OM and BC were − 0.771, −0.771, −0.486, −0.429, −0.696 and − 0.771, respectively. The absolute values of these variables were found to be lower than the critical value of Wp (0.829), and the trend of the changes was not found to be significant.

Effects of changes in the concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents on the risk of T2DM and glucose and lipid indicators

Table 1 shows that in the sin gle-pollutant models, the risk of T2DM was reduced by 2%, 20%, 8%, 14%, 12% and 51% for each 0.1 µg/m3 decrease in the concentration of PM2.5 and its five constituents, respectively. A significant linear trend was observed between decreasing concentrations and lower T2DM risk (P-trend < 0.001). Compared with T2, lower T2DM risk was observed at T1 concentrations of ΔPM2.5, ΔSO₄²⁻, ΔNO₃⁻, ΔNH₄⁺, ΔOM, and ΔBC, whereas elevated risks were found at T3 levels of ΔSO₄²⁻, ΔNH₄⁺, ΔOM, and ΔBC.

Table 2 shows that for every 0.1 µg/m3 decrease in PM2.5 and its five constituents in the single-pollutant models, the ΔFPG, ΔTC, ΔTG, and ΔLDL-C values in the total population were significantly lower. The levels of ΔFPG, ΔTC, ΔTG, and ΔLDL-C were all lower at ΔPM2.5 than at T2 for the total population. The ΔFPG and ΔTG levels decreased when the five constituents were present at the T1 concentration. Moreover, the ΔLDL-C levels decreased when the ΔNO3- and ΔNH4+ concentrations were at T1. Conversely, the level of ΔHDL-C was found to increase when the ΔSO42-, ΔNO3- and ΔNH4+ concentrations were at T3.

In the total population, increased ΔLDL-C levels were observed at the ΔPM2.5 concentration of T3 compared with the T2 level ΔFPG. The ΔTG and ΔLDL-C levels increased when the ΔSO42− and ΔBC concentrations were at T3. Similarly, the levels of ΔFPG, ΔTC, and ΔLDL-C increased when the ΔNO3− and ΔNH4+ concentrations were at T3. Furthermore, the ΔFPG, ΔTC, ΔTG, and ΔLDL-C levels increased when the OM was at the T3 concentration. ΔHDL-C levels increased when ΔPM2.5, ΔNO3− and ΔNH4+ were at the T3 concentration. The HDL-C level decreased when the ΔSO42−, ΔOM and ΔBC concentrations were at T3.

In the new-onset diabetes population, ΔLDL-C levels significantly decreased with every 0.1 µg/m3 reduction in the concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents. In the nondiabetic population, the ΔFPG, ΔTC, ΔTG, and ΔHDL-C levels were significantly reduced for every 0.1 µg/m3 decrease in the concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents. Dose‒response relationship

After adjusting for potential confounding variables, nonlinear dose‒response relationships were observed between the changes in the concentrations of PM2.5, SO42−, NO3−, NH4+, OM, and BC and the risk of T2DM (P < 0.001, Pnonlinear <0.05). Subgroup analyses

The sex-stratified analyses (Fig. 3 A) demonstrated that a reduction in the concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents was associated with a decreased risk of T2DM across different sexes. The effect of SO42− on reducing the risk of T2DM was more pronounced in females than in males. Conversely, the impact of the remaining pollutants on T2DM did not show any significant sexes differences. The age-stratified analyses indicated that reductions in the concentrations of PM2.5, NO3− and NH4+ had a more pronounced impact on the risk of T2DM in people aged ≥ 60 years, with statistically significant intergroup differences.

The results of sex- and age-stratified analyses (Fig. 3B) revealed that, in the total population, decreases in PM2.5, SO42-, NH4+, OM, and BC had more significant effects on the reduction in ΔFPG levels in males and in those aged < 60 years. The impact of declining PM2.5 and its five constituents on ΔTG levels was more pronounced in males than in females; furthermore, the influence of SO42-, NH4+, OM, and BC on ΔTG levels was more pronounced in individuals aged 60 years and above. These findings indicated that lower concentrations of PM2.5 and its five constituents were associated with lower ΔLDL-C levels across sex and age groups. Notably, the observed associations were more pronounced in females than in males for PM2.5 and NH4+ and in individuals aged < 60 years than in those aged ≥ 60 years for the five constituents. An additional finding was that lower PM2.5, SO42- and NH4+ concentrations had a more pronounced effect on elevated ΔHDL-C levels in females, whereas lower BC concentrations had a more pronounced effect on elevated ΔHDL-C levels in those aged ≥ 60 years.

Table 3 demonstrates that a decrease in the concentration of mixed pollutants was significantly associated with a reduced risk of T2DM (HR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.52, 0.62; P < 0.001), indicating that reducing mixed exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents may effectively lower the incidence of T2DM. In the decomposition of weights for each pollutant, NO3− exhibited the strongest positive effect (β = 0.530; weight = 98.4%), suggesting a substantial contribution to increased T2DM risk. In contrast, reductions in OM (β = −0.482; weight = 43.8%) and BC (β = −0.294; weight = 26.7%) were associated with decreased T2DM risk and contributed significantly to the overall effect. The sum of positive effects was 0.539, while the sum of negative effects was − 1.10, indicating that the total effect was primarily driven by negative contributions. This suggests that declines in OM and BC concentrations may be key factors in reducing the risk of T2DM.

Table 4 shows that reductions in mixed exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents were significantly associated with improvements in multiple glucose and lipid metabolic indicators, with variations observed across different population subgroups. In the total population, decreased pollutant concentrations were significantly associated with reductions in ΔFPG (β = −0.62, 95% CI: −0.79, −0.45), ΔTC (β = −0.62, 95% CI: −0.98, −0.26), ΔTG (β = −5.08, 95% CI: −6.76, −3.40), and ΔLDL-C (β = −3.99, 95% CI: −4.42, −3.55), all with P-values < 0.001. These findings suggest that reduced exposure to mixed pollutants may contribute to improved glucose and lipid profiles. Among individuals without diabetes, the protective effects appeared more pronounced. Significant decreases were observed in ΔFPG, ΔTC, ΔTG, and ΔLDL-C (all P < 0.01), with the greatest reduction in triglycerides (β = −4.95, 95% CI: −6.63, −3.28). In contrast, among individuals with newly diagnosed diabetes, only the reduction in ΔLDL-C was statistically significant (β = −5.74, 95% CI: −8.12, −3.36, P < 0.001), while no significant changes were observed for the other indicators. Additionally, changes in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (ΔHDL-C) were not statistically significant in any subgroup (P > 0.05), suggesting that decreased pollutant exposure may have limited effects on HDL-C levels.

Discussion

This study found that the joint effect of reductions in mixed exposure concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents on the risk of T2DM and changes in glycemic and lipid indicators was consistent in direction with the effects observed for individual pollutants. Specifically, reductions in PM2.5 and its constituents (SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺, OM, and BC) were associated not only with a decreased risk of T2DM but also with lower levels of ΔFPG, ΔTC, ΔTG, and ΔLDL-C. In addition, reductions in SO₄²⁻, OM, and BC were associated with increases in ΔHDL-C levels. Improvements in fasting glucose and lipid profiles may help prevent the onset and progression of diabetes and its related complications. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, indicating that improved air quality can exert a beneficial influence on the health status of a population. A longitudinal study conducted in Taiwan Province, China, demonstrated that for every 5 µg/m3 reduction in the PM2.5 concentration [41], the annual mean ΔFPG was reduced by 0.19 (95% CI: 0.14, 0.25) mg/dl, and the risk of T2DM was reduced by 22% (95% CI: 18%, 26%). Compared with those at the T2 concentration, the mean annual level of ΔFPG and the risk of T2DM were found to be lower at the PM2.5 exposure concentration of T1; conversely, the mean annual level of ΔFPG and the risk of diabetes mellitus were observed to increase at the PM2.5 exposure concentration of T3. Another study in Taiwan Province, China, yielded comparable findings, with a 16% (95% CI: 14%, 18%) reduction in the risk of hypertension for every 5 µg/m³ reduction in PM2.5 [22]. Compared with that in the control group, the risk of hypertension in the population was lower (HR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.78, 0.85) when the PM2.5 air quality improved, whereas the risk was 16% greater (95% CI: 1.11, 1.21) when the PM2.5 air quality worsened. In addition, we found beneficial effects of air quality improvement on different health outcomes on the basis of other previous studies: long-term improvements in air quality not only had a positive impact on lung function development in children and on lung function in elderly women [25, 42] but were also associated with a reduction in bronchiolitis symptoms and a reduction in the prevalence of nonallergic respiratory symptoms in children [43]. – [44] Moreover, few studies to date have investigated the mixed effects of reductions in PM2.5 and its chemical constituents on T2DM-related indicators. However, existing evidence supports a significant association between combined exposure to PM2.5 constituents and T2DM outcomes. A study based on baseline data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) reported a significant positive joint effect of mixed exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents (SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺, OM, and BC) on FPG levels (β = 0.125 mmol/L, 95% CI: 0.084, 0.166) [38]. Another CHARLS-based study yielded similar findings [45], indicating a positive association between the concentration of mixed PM2.5 constituents and FPG levels. A longitudinal study of 76 non-smoking Chinese adults aged 60–69 years found that mixed exposure to 17 metal constituents of PM2.5 was positively associated with increased levels of TC and LDL-C, but showed no association with HDL-C; furthermore, TG levels were affected by pollutant mixtures under low exposure conditions [46]. In addition, the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort (CMEC) study demonstrated that joint exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents was positively associated with dyslipidemia (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.11) [47]. However, another study using CHARLS data reported no statistically significant individual or combined effects of long-term exposure to five PM2.5 constituents on the risk of developing T2DM [45]. These inconsistencies highlight the heterogeneity across studies, which may stem from differences in pollutant sources, exposure assessment methods, geographic settings, population susceptibility, and study design. Further research is warranted to clarify the health effects of individual PM2.5 constituents and their mixtures.

Furthermore, such epidemiological studies based on policy-driven air quality improvements are consistent with the “experimental” principle of Hill’s criterion for causal inference, which states that [48], assuming positive associations between PM2.5 and its constituents and the risk of diabetes mellitus and disorders of glucose‒lipid metabolic disorders are causative, the opposite health effects should be observed in experiments with reduced pollutant exposure concentrations [49]. The study demonstrated a notable decline in particulate matter concentration in China during the implementation of the Action Plan for Prevention and Control of Air Pollution from 2013 to 2017. The average concentration of PM2.5 markedly decreased from 72 µg/m3 in 2013 to 47 µg/m3 in 2017, representing a 34.7% decrease. Additionally, the concentrations of SO42−, NO3−, NH4+, OM, and BC demonstrated a parallel trend during the same period, aligning with the observed decline in the PM2.5 concentration [50]. During the implementation of the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan, China’s PM2.5 air quality improved significantly, resulting in corresponding health gains. For example, for every 10 µg/m3 reduction in the PM2.5 concentration [51], LDL-C and TC decreased by 2.71 (95% CI: 0.10, 5.32) mg/dl and 4.16 (95% CI: 1.24, 7.08) mg/dl, respectively; [52] Tao et al. and Huang et al. reported that improved air quality was significantly associated with increased life expectancy and reduced mortality and years of life lost (YLLs) related to air pollutant control in the population. In addition [23], the number of deaths attributable to PM2.5 exposure decreased from 1.22 million to 1.1 million between 2013 and 2015, representing a 9.1% reduction [53], and the number of premature deaths declined by 14% in 2017 compared with 2013 due to a reduction in long-term PM2.5 exposure concentrations [54].

The Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan has thus alleviated the air pollution crisis to some extent, and the decline in PM2.5 concentrations provides an opportunity to assess the causal link between air quality improvement and health effects. However, the benefits of pollution prevention and control measures are not uniform across regions because of differences in industrial structure, emission strategies, and the focus of prevention and control measures. In addition, since the magnitude of effects and exposure‒response relationships for harmful and positive effects induced by increased air pollutant concentrations and improved air quality may differ [41], it is crucial to conduct relevant studies to assess the beneficial effects of improved air quality on public health directly. This will enable a more accurate reflection of the health benefits of environmental pollution control measures and provide a scientific basis for disease prevention and air pollutant control.

This study has several strengths. First, a prospective large-sample cohort study was employed to examine the favourable impact of enhanced air quality resulting from PM2.5 and its constituents on diabetes mellitus, as well as glucose and lipid indicators. Second, these indicators have the potential to reflect the health benefits of environmental pollutant control measures, thereby providing a scientific basis for disease prevention and air pollution management.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting and generalizing our findings. Firstly, the lack of information on participants’ time–activity patterns prevented us from accounting for individual exposure differences related to workplace environments, commuting, or time spent indoors versus outdoors, which may have introduced non-differential exposure misclassification and biased the estimates toward the null. Secondly, we lacked data on other key air pollutants (e.g., SO2, NO2, O3, PM10), which may also contribute to T2DM and metabolic disorders, independently or synergistically. Thirdly, exposure was estimated using citywide average concentrations without personal monitoring or high-resolution spatial modeling, which may have failed to capture within-city variability and led to residual exposure misclassification. Fourthly, although improvements in diabetes incidence and metabolic indicators were observed after the implementation of the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan, other simultaneous factors may have also contributed to better population health. Finally, while single-pollutant models help elucidate the effects of individual pollutants such as PM2.5 and its constituents, they may not adequately represent combined exposure scenarios, and their results should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

In summary, this study is one of the few prospective cohort studies conducted in Northwest China that systematically evaluated the individual and combined effects of improved air quality—particularly the reduction in PM2.5 and its constituents—on the incidence of T2DM and changes in glucose and lipid metabolic indicators. In both single-pollutant and multi-pollutant models, reductions in the concentrations of PM2.5 and its five major constituents were associated with decreased incidence of T2DM, as well as lower levels of ΔFPG, ΔTC, ΔTG, and ΔLDL-C. Besides, the associations appeared to be slightly stronger among females and older adults compared to males and younger individuals. These findings suggest that targeted reductions in ambient PM2.5 and its constituents may yield considerable public health benefits in reducing the burden of diabetes and metabolic disorders. Our results provide timely evidence to inform future air pollution control policies and urban health planning. Further investigations should be carried out to comprehensively assess the sources and health effects of PM2.5 and its constituents through systematic comparisons.
