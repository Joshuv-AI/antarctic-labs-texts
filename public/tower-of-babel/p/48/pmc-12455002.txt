Genitourinary system related tumors and coronary atherosclerosis risk: Evidence from a Mendelian randomization study on multi-cohorts
Authors: Shanshan Chen, Yuanyuan Zhao, Yang Fan, Mengchen Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Prostate cancer was the risk of coronary atherosclerosis.•ER-positive breast cancer was the risk of coronary atherosclerosis.•Hormone-related genitourinary tumors may promote coronary atherosclerosis.Keywords: Prostate cancer, ER+ breast cancer, Coronary atherosclerosis, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Due to the advancement of early screening methods, the development of treatment and new targeted drugs, the long-term survival rate of cancer patients has been greatly improved [1]. The increasing survival rate will also increase the incidence of cancer complications, therefore, the risk of non cancer deaths exceeds that of cancer-related deaths, such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) [2,3].

CVD and cancer are the leading causes of mortality globally, CVD is also the leading cause of death for cancer patients [1,4]. Among 28 types of cancer, 8 cancer sites have a mortality rate lower than CVD mortality risk for at least one calendar year [5]. Furthermore, according to follow-up data, CVD has the highest risk of death within one year after cancer diagnosis [5]. Accumulating evidence suggests that cancer patients have a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular disease [[6], [7], [8]]. At the same time, numerous studies have shown that cardiovascular disease can directly affect tumor growth, various large-scale follow-up studies have confirmed that myocardial infarction, heart failure, and acute coronary syndrome significantly increase the risk of developing malignant tumors [[9], [10], [11], [12]], indicating a mutually influential relationship between the two diseases [13]. CVD and cancer may have a bidirectional relationship because both diseases share common risk factors, mechanisms, and physiological and pathological processes, including age, smoking, diabetes, obesity, hyperlipidemia, unhealthy diet such as insufficient dietary fiber intake, excessive red meat intake and high salt diet [[13], [14], [15]]. From the perspective of pathophysiological mechanism, they also have similar physiological basis, including inflammation, genomic instability, cellular metabolism, and oxidative cellular stress [13]. More and more studies have found that these conditions are fundamentally interrelated, making individuals susceptible to both diseases [14,16].

Despite increasing recognition of the close relationship between cancer and cardiovascular disease, there is little research exploring the specific role of hormone level changes in both diseases. Among male and female cancer patients, the highest number of patients have different types of cancer. Data in 2022 shows that the number of male prostate cancer patients ranks first (3,523,230 patients), and the number of female breast cancer patients ranks first (4,055,770 patients) [5], these indicate that hormone levels are involve in the development of tumors. Similarly, it is generally accepted that hormone levels also play an important role in the occurrence of different cardiovascular diseases. Epidemiology shows that the incidence rate of CVD in premenopausal women is lower than that in men, at the same time, compared with age matched men, CVD occurs 10 years later in women than in men [17]. Furthermore, research has confirmed that estrogen has a protective effect on blood vessels [18,19].

Meanwhile, studies have confirmed that hormonal pathways also play a specific role in malignant tumors, especially in genitourinary system related tumors. In breast cancer models, p300-mediated H3K27 acetylation determines estrogen receptor (ER) expression, while in prostate cancer models, p300-mediated androgen receptor (AR) acetylation determines AR stability [[20], [21], [22]]. Sex hormones can not only participate in cancer development through epigenetics, such as KDM6A mediated demethylation promoting female tumor suppressor phenotype and EZH2/PRC2 methylation driving male carcinogenic phenotype, but also synergize with immune cell receptors to participate in cancer development. For example, androgens directly induce T cell depletion, while estrogen reduces the proportion of M1/M2 tumor associated macrophages, constructs an immunosuppressive microenvironment, promotes metastasis, and indirectly inhibits immunity [[23], [24], [25]]. In addition, GnRH and its receptors directly inhibit proliferation, metastasis and angiogenesis in a variety of tumors, such as castration resistant prostate cancer and ovarian cancer [26,27], as well as estrogen activated ER α pathway drives tumor progression in hormone receptor positive breast cancer [28,29]. Currently, researches on the association mechanisms among tumors, hormones, and cardiovascular diseases remain insufficient. The mechanisms by which hormones influence tumor development and progression, as well as cardiovascular disease processes, are extremely complex. The impact of sex hormones on the cardiovascular system involves the interaction of multiple factors, including the regulation of vascular elasticity, endothelial function, and lipid metabolism. Existing researches often focus on issues other than individual differences, however genetic factors can lead to different sensitivities of individuals to certain chemotherapy drugs, thereby increasing their susceptibility to cardiovascular disease.

Through Mendelian randomization, researchers can simultaneously analyze the effects of multiple genetic variations, eliminate confounding risk factors, accurately determine causal relationships, and better understand the genetic basis and complexity of diseases influenced by multiple factors. On the other hand, the application of precision medicine in the research of tumors, hormones, and cardiovascular diseases is insufficient. Further strengthening researches are needed for precise assessment and personalized treatment strategy for different individuals. Overall, in order to explore the association between hormones, tumors and cardiovascular diseases, as well as provide further evidence for personalized treatment strategies for different individuals. We performed a two-sample MR analysis to assess the association between sex hormone-related cancers and coronary atherosclerosis, namely prostate cancer and ER+ breast cancer. The schematic diagram of this study was shown in Fig. 1. There are a total of three populations with prostate cancer and six populations with ER positive breast cancer as the exposure cohorts, and three populations with coronary atherosclerosis as the outcome cohorts. We evaluated whether genitourinary system related tumors such as prostate cancer and ER positive breast cancer may contribute to the development of coronary atherosclerosis.

Associations between prostate cancer and coronary atherosclerosis

MR results by using MR-Egger (OR = 1.003, 95% CI: 1.001-1.005, p = 0.015) and weighted median (OR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.000-1.003, p = 0.024) methods showed the consistent causal effect of prostate cancer on the risk of coronary atherosclerosis when ieu-b-85 was treated as the exposure group and ukb-d-I9_CORATHER was treated as the outcome group (Fig. 2). The Cochran’s Q test via MR-Egger and IVW with the p values of 0.023 and 0.016, respectively, showed some heterogeneity existing in these MR analyses (Table 1). The MR-Egger regression intercept-test (intercept = -0.00019, se = 0.000108, p = 0.076) didn’t present horizontal pleiotropy, but the MR-PRESSO global test (p-value 0.015) showed some horizontal pleiotropy (Table 2).

Discussion

In our recent study, we have established the causal association between genitourinary system related tumors and coronary atherosclerosis. We have confirmed that prostate cancer and ER positive breast cancer are significant risk factors, increasing the likelihood of coronary atherosclerosis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first causal study investigating the relationship of two common genitourinary system related tumors with the risk of coronary atherosclerosis. During the study, we collected three populations with prostate cancer and six populations with ER positive breast cancer as the exposure cohorts, and three populations with coronary atherosclerosis as the outcome cohorts to perform the two-sample MR analysis. These findings suggest that genitourinary system related tumors such as prostate cancer and ER positive breast cancer may contribute to the development of coronary atherosclerosis.

Many previous studies have observed that, in comparison with the general population, survivors of several cancers, including breast cancer, lymphoma, and testicular cancer experience an increase in the risk of coronary atherosclerosis [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. A previous study from Hooning et al observed incidence of cardiovascular disease in 4,414 10-year survivors of breast cancer and found a significant association between breast cancer with the treatment of radiotherapy and cardiovascular diseases. Interestingly, a recent study from Chang et al. examined the association of total serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA)- a tumor marker for prostate cancer- with coronary artery calcium (CAC) score and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. In the results, they demonstrated that serum total PSA levels within the reference range showed an inverse association with subclinical atherosclerosis and CVD mortality in young and middle-aged Korean men [34]. This report similarly revealed the potential association between prostate cancer and atherosclerosis. While these studies have not demonstrated the direct causal association between the common genitourinary system related tumors, such as prostate cancer and breast cancer, and coronary atherosclerosis. In fact, existing evidence of a causal link between atherosclerosis and cancer is sparse and conflicting. Vinter et al. conducted a historical population-based cohort study of 28,549 cancer-free patients to detect if they had coronary artery disease and cancers (total, tobacco-related, lung, prostate, breast, and colorectal). Finally, they only did observe an association between coronary artery calcium score (CACS) and risk of lung cancer in women, while there was no association between coronary atherosclerosis and other types of cancer detected in the study [35]. On the other hand, a large-scale genome-wide association study of coronary artery disease in genetically diverse populations performed by Tcheandjieu et al. intriguingly found that one-third of the novel loci associated with the heritability of coronary artery disease (CAD) link to either a cancer or to height [36]. This observation is buttressed by our findings from the mendelian randomization study. Furthermore, gene BRCA1 overexpression has been shown to protect against atherosclerosis and improve endothelial function [37]. As we known, BRCA1/2 have been identified as key tumor suppressor genes for several cancers, including breast and prostate cancers. In our study, we also demonstrated that genitourinary system related tumors such as prostate cancer and ER positive breast cancer may contribute to the development of coronary atherosclerosis. Therefore, our findings provide important new insights into the genetic basis of tumors and atherosclerosis. In addition, these findings also guide us to pay attention to against coronary atherosclerosis in the treatment of patients with genitourinary system related tumors.

For the specific mechanisms involved in the association between genitourinary system related tumors and atherosclerosis, there are some potential explanations. First, significantly distinct sex differences emerged in the manifestation of coronary atherosclerosis. Specifically, men generally have a higher prevalence and greater extent of coronary plaque burden than women [38]. In addition, women usually have more fibro-fatty and mixed plaques in the clinical manifestation of coronary atherosclerosis [39], while men are more likely to present calcified and fibrous plaques. Estrogen has been thought to play an important role in the sex differences. In addition, as we mentioned above, the human kallikrein family of serine protease PSA has also been reported to be associated with nonprostate conditions including cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and insulin resistance [[40], [41], [42], [43]]. Second, cell types prioritized for coronary atherosclerosis commonly include endothelial cells fibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells, which comprise the vast majority of the cells in the normal vasculature including glandular tissues. Thirdly, chronic inflammation was a key trigger and driver of cancer and also atherosclerosis [44]. Several molecular and cellular signaling pathways have been identified linking inflammation and chronic diseases such as cancers and cardiovascular diseases [45,46]. In addition, other pathological risk factors such as tobacco use, poor diet, environmental toxins, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction also contributed to both cardiovascular diseases and cancers. For example, recent studies demonstrated that in the process of obesity, oxidative stress could impair insulin action, increase cardiovascular complications such as hypertension and atherosclerosis, dyslipidemia, and several kinds of cancer including breast, colorectal, and liver [47]. Based on these, we thought that genitourinary system related tumors and atherosclerosis may involve coincident genetic basis. However, follow-up studies are needed to further understand the specific mechanisms, to identify the causal genes, and to develop population-specific novel therapies. Mahdavi et al investigated the electrical impedance characteristics of breast cancer tissue and demonstrates a clinically validated method for distinguishing malignancies based on their bioelectrical signatures [48]. Subsequently, Moharamipour et al presented a platform that analyzes cancer cell behavior in electric fields, offering mechanistic insights into cell charge properties, which may be relevant to systemic physiological interactions such as those involving vascular systems [49]. These useful tools and methods may provide may provide biological grounding for the epidemiological associations between cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis and cancers.

Finally, there are some limitations to our study. First, we were unable to find appropriate data to validate our results in several ethnic populations. Second, the association between genitourinary system related tumors and atherosclerosis were not validated in all cohorts we collected in the study. Third, we didn’t detect the effect of tumors in the development of atherosclerosis in all the types of genitourinary system related tumors. Further studies are needed to confirm and expand upon these findings, particularly in larger clinical cohorts. Forth, concerning the exposure and outcome groups, we thoroughly examined the population information and discovered that the patients in the group were only diagnosed with cancer types and atherosclerosis, without further details. Consequently, we could not conduct further analysis for stratified analysis. Therefore, future studies should aim to collect data from an independent population to validate these findings in recent studies.

Methods

Ethical statement

All the data this study used were publicly available; no additional ethical approval was required. All the datasets were European populations.

Study design

In the present study, a two-sample MR analysis was carried out to assess the association between sex hormone-related cancers and coronary atherosclerosis, namely prostate cancer and ER+ breast cancer. All the datasets used in the study were selected on the platform: the MR database (http://app.mrbase.org/). The group IDs of the phenotype of prostate cancer were ieu-b-85, ieu-b-4809, and ebi-a-GCST006085; the group IDs of the phenotype of ER+ breast cancer were ieu-a-1167, ieu-a-1134, ieu-a-1127, ieu-a-1132, ieu-a-1133, and ieu-a-1161; the group IDs of the phenotype of coronary atherosclerosis were ukb-d-I9_CORATHER, finn-b-I9_CORATHER_EXNONE, and finn-b-I9_CORATHER. Details of the GWAS datasets were given in Supplementary Table 1. The SNPs with a p-value less than 5 × 10−8 were chosen and were regarded as independent when the linkage disequilibrium r2 < 0.001 (clump distance > 10,000 kb). All the F statistic values of the extracted SNPs were greater than ten. The extraction of instrumental variables (IVs) and all the analyses were processed using the TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.11) package in R (version 4.3.3) [50].

MR analyses

Mendelian randomization-Egger (MR-Egger) regression, weighted median (WM), and inverse variance weighted (IVW) were performed to assess the causal effect of prostate cancer or ER+ breast cancer on coronary atherosclerosis. Results were presented as an odds ratio (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), and the forest plots were shown. The p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant [51].

Sensitivity analyses

For outcome heterogeneity derived from the IVW and MR-Egger methods, Cochran’s Q statistic was used for testing, and the Q_pval < 0.05 was considered to be heterogeneous. The MR-Egger regression intercept and its 95% CIs were employed to assess the degree of bias in causal estimates due to directional pleiotropy. Additionally, horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-PRESSO global test, with all outlier SNPs identified by the MR-PRESSO outlier test excluded. The p-value < 0.05 was considered to be the presence of horizontal pleiotropy. Subsequently, potential changes in the results were examined after removing the outlier instrumental variables, and the causal estimates were reassessed. The leave-one-out analysis and single SNP analysis were used to clarify the influence of individual SNPs on the primary causal relationship [52].
