Efficacy and safety of ruxolitinib for graft-versus-host disease prophylaxis in patients with aplastic anemia undergoing PBSC-only allogeneic stem cell transplantation: a prospective phase II study

Background

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) remains a curative option for patients with aplastic anemia (AA) [1–3]. In China, mobilized peripheral blood stem cells (PBSCs) have become the predominant graft source for HSCT because of their higher CD34⁺ cell content—approximately two to three times greater than that of bone marrow—and a tenfold increase in T-cell content, which facilitates more rapid engraftment [4, 5]. However, this elevated T-cell count is associated with a greater risk of acute graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD), which occurs in 40%–50% of patients undergoing peripheral blood HSCT (PB-HSCT), with severe aGVHD developing in 10%–15% and increased chronic GVHD incidence [6–9].

Given these risks, there is a growing need to optimize GVHD prophylaxis strategies in PB-based transplants. The pathogenesis of aGVHD involves key inflammatory cytokines such as interferon (IFN) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), both of which signal through the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway [10]. Preclinical studies have established the central role of JAK1/2 signaling in aGVHD, and inhibition of JAK1/2 has demonstrated efficacy in reducing GVHD in experimental models. Clinically, the JAK1/2 inhibitor ruxolitinib is already approved for the treatment of both aGVHD and chronic GVHD (cGVHD) [11–13]. Building on this evidence, integration of JAK inhibition into prophylactic platforms represents a logical next step [14, 15]. Early-phase clinical trials investigating ruxolitinib for GVHD prevention have reported encouraging preliminary outcomes, highlighting its potential role in prophylactic regimens [15–17]. In this Phase II clinical trial, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ruxolitinib as part of a GVHD prophylaxis regimen following PB-HSCT.

Methods

Patient population

This open-label, single-arm, Phase II clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05914714) evaluated the addition of ruxolitinib to standard GVHD prophylaxis in patients with AA. The study was conducted at the Stem Cell Transplantation Center, Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, between June 2023 and December 2024. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital, and all patients or their legal guardians provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

In addition to a documented AA diagnosis, the key inclusion criteria comprised as follows: age ≥ 14 years and negative testing for HIV, hepatitis B virus, and hepatitis C virus. Exclusion criteria included: uncontrolled infection at enrollment or requirement for mechanical ventilation, prior allogeneic HSCT, severe hepatic insufficiency or end-stage renal impairment, active solid tumor malignancy, clinically significant psychiatric disorders, inability to comply with study treatment and monitoring, or unwillingness to provide informed consent. AA was diagnosed based on the International Agranulocytosis and Aplastic Anemia Study Group criteria [18] and disease severity was determined using the modified criteria described by Camitta [19] and Bacigalupo [20].

A historical control cohort that received standard GVHD prophylaxis was identified from existing institutional clinical databases covering the period January 2019 to May 2023. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to both the historical control group and the ruxolitinib-treated cohort.

Study design and transplantation procedures

Ruxolitinib was initiated at the start of conditioning and continued for 3 months post-transplant at a dose of 5 mg twice daily. The dose was reduced in cases of severe adverse events, in accordance with the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4 (CTCAE v4).

Conditioning regimens were administered as previously described, using either fludarabine, cyclophosphamide and antithymocyte globulin (FAC) or busulfan, fludarabine, cyclophosphamide and antithymocyte globulin (BFAC) protocols. The FAC regimen consisted of fludarabine (30 mg/m2/d, days –5 to –1), cyclophosphamide (30–37.5 mg/kg/day, days –5 to –2), and rabbit antithymocyte globulin (rATG, 2.5 mg/kg/day, days –5 to –1). The BFAC regimen added busulfan (3.2 mg/kg/day, days –7 to –6) to FAC.

The standard GVHD prophylaxis included cyclosporine A or tacrolimus combined with short-course of methotrexate. Haploidentical donor (HID)-HSCT recipients additionally received mycophenolate mofetil, which is consistent with institutional practice. Supportive care and prophylaxis protocols followed institutional standards. All patients received granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF, 5 μg/kg/day subcutaneously) beginning on day + 7 post-HSCT until neutrophil recovery. Chimerism studies were assessed by short tandem repeat analysis of total bone marrow cells on days 30, 90, 120, 180, and 365 posttransplant.

Infection prophylaxis included cotrimoxazole and acyclovir or valacyclovir for the prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii and herpes simplex virus, respectively, administered for 1 year posttransplantation. Antifungal prophylaxis was provided with posaconazole or an alternative systemic agent as required until day + 90 post-HSCT. Letermovir was used for cytomegalovirus (CMV) prophylaxis. CMV PCR threshold for pre-emptive treatment was defined as 1000 copies/ml. EBV PCR threshold for pre-emptive treatment was defined as 500 copies/ml. Preemptive therapy with ganciclovir, valganciclovir, and/or foscarnet was initiated in cases of CMV reactivation [21].

GVHD treatment

If patients developed GVHD, initial therapy consisted of methylprednisolone (2 mg/kg/day in two divided doses) for a minimum of 7 days, followed by dose tapering, combined with intravenous cyclosporine A (2 mg/kg twice daily; target trough level 150–250 ng/mL). Prolonged maintenance of ruxolitinib is used in the ruxolitinib group. Second-line therapy, including anti-CD25 antibody, mesenchymal stem cells, or vedolizumab, could be initiated at the researcher’s decision.

Endpoint

The primary objective was to evaluate the incidence of aGVHD by day + 180 after allo-HSCT. Secondary endpoints included cGVHD, OS, GVHD-free failure-free survival (GFFS), treatment-related mortality, engraftment, and infection. The severity of GVHD was assessed using the Mount Sinai Acute GVHD International Consortium (MAGIC) criteria for acute GVHD [22]. The grade of cGVHD was assessed following the National Institutes of Health consensus criteria [23]. The definition of treatment response to aGVHD was evaluated at 28 days after treatment [24]. Complete response (CR) was defined by the complete absence of aGVHD symptoms, and partial response (PR) was defined as an improvement of at least one stage in a single organ without worsening in others. No response (NR) was characterized as either no improvement, worsening symptoms in any organ, or the emergence of new GVHD-related symptoms. Additionally, GVHD progression after 3 days of therapy or lack of improvement within 7 days was also considered NR. Primary graft failure (GF) was defined as the failure of myeloid engraftment until day + 28. Secondary poor graft function (sPGF) was defined as recurrent pancytopenia with obviously hypocellular BM after achieving a documented engraftment in the presence of full donor chimerism [25, 26]. GFFS (GVHD-free, Failure-free survival) is defined as survival without the occurrence of grades III-IV acute GVHD, extensive chronic GVHD or treatment failures as mentioned above. Transplantation-related mortality (TRM) was defined as death without GF.

Sample size

Sample size estimates were based on the assumption of a log-rank test for between-group comparison of the primary endpoint, the incidence of grade II–IV aGVHD was 20% in the experimental group and 40% in the control group. We assumed a 1-year enrollment period and 1 year of follow-up. A sample size of 43 patients was calculated with a two-sided type I error of 0.05 and 80% statistical power for detecting differences in the cumulative incidence of aGVHD. Accounting for an expected 5% loss to follow-up, we planned to include 46 patients in the ruxolitinib prophylaxis group. Sample size calculations were performed with PASS (Power Analysis and Sample Size) software, version 15 (NCSS, Number Cruncher Statistical System).

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized as median values (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and as counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Overall survival (OS) and GVHD-free and failure–free survival (GFFS) probabilities were estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. The cumulative incidence method was used to estimate probabilities of aGVHD and cGVHD, the neutrophil and platelet engraftment, and transplant-related mortality (TRM). Analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.2.3 (http://www.r-project.org).

To address baseline imbalances, propensity score–based inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied. Propensity scores were estimated from a multivariable logistic regression model that included the following covariates: age, sex, diagnosis, use of antithymocyte globulin (ATG) before HSCT, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) involvement, HSCT type, pre-HSCT infection, conditioning regimen, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation–Comorbidity Index (HCT-CI), mononuclear cell count (MNC), CD34 cell dose, and blood type. These weights were used to generate a weighted pseudo-population with balanced baseline characteristics.

To evaluate heterogeneity of treatment effects across prespecified subgroups, we stratified patients by established risk factors for HSCT outcomes in AA: age ≥ 50 years, transplantation in the presence of active infection, use of a non-sibling fully matched donor, blood group antigen system (ABO) incompatibility, prior ATG treatment failure, and AA subtype (severe vs non-severe), duration from diagnosis to transplantation, and CMV reactivation. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. Subgroup effects were assessed in two ways: (i) Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests, both before and after IPTW, using weighted log-rank tests for IPTW-adjusted comparisons; and (ii) Cox models applied to the unweighted sample (unweighted HRs) and to the IPTW-weighted pseudo-population (weighted HRs). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.1.0.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between June 2023 and December 2024, a total of 58 patients with AA were screened for eligibility in the ruxolitinib prophylaxis study. Of these, seven were excluded because they were younger than 14 years, two because of uncontrolled infections prior to transplantation, and three because of concomitant use of non-protocol drugs in the conditioning regimen. Ultimately, 46 patients were enrolled in the ruxolitinib group, all of whom completed treatment and follow-up.

For the historical control group, 97 patients were screened between January 2019 and May 2023. After excluding five pediatric patients, one patient with an uncontrolled infection, and 55 patients treated with non-protocol conditioning drugs, 36 patients were included (Fig. 1). GVHD

The primary objective was to evaluate the incidence of aGVHD by day + 180 after allo-HSCT. Six patients (13.0%) in the ruxolitinib group developed grade II–IV aGVHD, and none progressed to severe disease. A total of 14 patients (38.9%) in the control group developed grade II–IV aGVHD, including 5 patients (13.9%) with III–IV disease (Table 2). Comparative analysis showed that the cumulative incidence risk of grade II–IV aGVHD was significantly lower with ruxolitinib compared with control (HR 0.24, 95% CI 0.08–0.66; weighted log-rank p = 0.004; Fig. 2A). As no patients in the ruxolitinib group developed severe aGVHD, the incidence was significantly lower compared with the control group (0% vs 15.8%; weighted log-rank p = 0.008; Fig. 2B). Patterns of organ involvement and disease severity were comparable between groups (Table 2, Fig. 2D).

At the day + 28 GVHD treatment evaluation, the ruxolitinib group achieved a 100% CR rate, whereas the control group showed a CR rate of 78.6% (11/14), with the remaining cases comprising two PR and one NR. By day + 60, the CR rate in the control group reached to 92.9% (13/14). All aGVHD cases in the ruxolitinib group achieved complete resolution with therapy, with a median time to resolution of 32.5 days (range, 14–90). Whereas the median GVHD treatment duration was 37 days in control group. Importantly, no deaths attributable to aGVHD occurred in the ruxolitinib group. In both groups, 50% patients required more than three second-line agents (Fig. 2E, supplementary Table).

Consistent with these findings, the ruxolitinib group also showed a trend toward reduced incidence of cGVHD compared with controls (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.14–1.48; weighted log-rank p = 0.221; Fig. 2C).

Engraftment

In the control group, one patient died of severe infection before neutrophil engraftment. Among the remaining evaluable patients, neutrophil engraftment was achieved in 100% of both groups. However, the 28-day platelet engraftment rate was significantly higher in the ruxolitinib group (84% vs 53% in controls; weighted p = 0.03) (Table 2). Neutrophil recovery kinetics were similar between groups (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.60–1.51; p = 0.789) (Fig. 2F). In contrast, ruxolitinib was associated with a significant advantage in platelet recovery (HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.24–0.75; weighted log-rank p = 0.002; Fig. 2G). Secondary poor graft function occurred in 5 patients (10.9%) in the ruxolitinib group and 12 patients (33.3%) in the control group (Table 2). The causes of secondary PGF differed between the two groups. Specifically, secondary PGF in the control group was primarily driven by GVHD (5/12, 41.7%) and CMV infection (7/12, 58.3%). In contrast, the ruxolitinib group exhibited a more diverse range of causes, including GVHD (1/5, 20%), COVID-19 infection (1/5, 20%), fungal/bacterial pneumonia (2/5, 40%), and drug-related effects (1/5, 20%). Notably, the drug-associated case achieved hematologic recovery after reduction of the ruxolitinib dose to 5 mg once daily at day + 36 post HSCT (Supplementary Table).

Infection

No significant differences were observed between groups regarding bacterial or fungal infections. However, the ruxolitinib group exhibited a markedly lower rate of CMV reactivation (13% vs 61.8%; p < 0.001), attributable to the use of letermovir prophylaxis, which was implemented in 2022 for all enrolled patients in mainland China. By contrast, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) reactivation occurred more frequently in the ruxolitinib group (34.8% vs 8.8%; p = 0.010). The incidence of post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) did not differ significantly between groups (Table 2).

Transplant-related mortality and outcomes

At a median follow-up of 417 days (range, 112–725), three patients in the ruxolitinib group died from EBV-associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (EBV-HLH), severe pulmonary infection, and intracranial hemorrhage, yielding a treatment-related mortality (TRM) rate of 7.9% (95% CI 0–16.3%). In the control group, deaths were primarily attributed to GVHD (n = 3) and severe bacterial infections (n = 2), for a TRM rate of 14.2% (95% CI 1.8–24.9%). Notably, no GVHD-related deaths occurred in the ruxolitinib group.

The estimated 1-year OS was 92.1% (95% CI 83.7–100%) in the ruxolitinib group compared with 85.8% (95% CI 75.1–98.2%) in the control group (HR of 0.50, 95% CI 0.12–2.16; weighted log-rank p = 0.318; Fig. 3A). Although not statistically significant, the OS curve demonstrated a numerical advantage of approximately 10% in the ruxolitinib group.

Survival analysis in the rux and the control groups. Overall Survival (A), GVHD-free Failure-free Survival (B) and transplant-related mortality (C) in the ruxolitinib (rux) and the CTL (control) groups

Importantly, 1-year GFFS was significantly superior in the ruxolitinib group compared with controls (91.6%, 95% CI 82.7–100% vs 72.1%, 95% CI 58.8–88.4%; HR 0.23, 95% CI 0.06–0.86; weighted log-rank p = 0.012; Fig. 3B). This improvement in GFFS, despite no significant differences in TRM (Fig. 3C) and OS, underscores the prophylactic efficacy of ruxolitinib. Collectively, these findings support its potential as a valuable addition to standard GVHD prophylaxis, particularly in reducing morbidity and enhancing GVHD-free survival.

Subgroup analysis

We next performed subgroup analyses to evaluate whether ruxolitinib provided superior efficacy compared with standard prophylaxis across predefined risk strata. Subgroups included HLA disparity (matched vs mismatched), pretransplant infection history, age (< 50 vs ≥ 50 years), blood group antigen system (ABO) incompatibility, prior ATG exposure, AA subtype (severe vs non-severe), duration from diagnosis to transplantation, and CMV infection. These factors are well-established predictors of inferior HSCT outcomes in AA, given their association with graft rejection, delayed immune reconstitution, and non-relapse mortality.

As shown in Fig. 4, ruxolitinib demonstrated consistent benefit across most high-risk subgroups. Notably, patients undergoing haploidentical HSCT (HR 0.15, 95% CI 0.04–0.55; weighted log-rank p = 0.001) and those with documented pretransplant infection (HR: 0.23, 95% CI 0.06–0.90; weighted log-rank p = 0.028) derived significant benefit. Among other high-risk categories, including older patients (≥ 50 years; HR 0.25, 95% CI 0.02–3.74; p = 0.289), those with prior ATG exposure (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.06–3.36; p = 0.489), and ABO-incompatible recipients (HR, 0.64, 95% CI 0.13–3.03; p = 0.510), a favorable trend was observed, though without statistical significance. We also performed subgroup analyses stratified by disease severity (VSAA/SAA vs. non-SAA) and time from diagnosis to transplantation. In the VSAA/SAA subgroup, ruxolitinib was associated with a significant survival benefit (HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.06–0.79; weighted log-rank p = 0.007). Analysis by transplant timing showed that patients transplanted within 6 months from diagnosis achieved a 100% GFFS rate in the ruxolitinib group. In patients with delayed transplantation (> 6 months from diagnosis), ruxolitinib also showed a potential protective effect (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.13–2.29; weighted log-rank p = 0.341).

Driven by differences in letermovir availability and CMV reactivation, a subgroup analysis based on CMV infection was performed. In the subgroup of patients with CMV reactivation, the GVHD incidence remained lower in the ruxolitinib group than control group (0% vs. 52.4%, p = 0.054). Although statistical significance was not reached, a trend toward improved survival was observed in the ruxolitinib group (weighted log-rank p = 0.217) (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that ruxolitinib may mitigate the adverse impact of traditional risk features in AA transplantation.

Immune reconstitution and inflammatory cytokine analysis

To assess whether ruxolitinib influenced inflammatory cytokine production, serum levels of IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, IL-17, IFN-α, and IFN-γ were measured at the time of hematopoietic recovery (control, n = 25; ruxolitinib, n = 26). Discussion

In this prospective study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of incorporating ruxolitinib into the GVHD prophylactic regimen for patients with AA undergoing PB-HSCT. The addition of ruxolitinib was associated with a significantly lower cumulative incidence of aGVHD, contributing to improved OS and GFFS. These findings support the therapeutic potential of JAK inhibitors in combination with standard prophylaxis. Furthermore, ruxolitinib was safe and did not adversely affect engraftment or non-relapse mortality.

Conventional GVHD prophylaxis relies primarily on calcineurin inhibitors, mycophenolate mofetil, and posttransplant cyclophosphamide [27]. In contrast, the use of JAK inhibitors introduces an innovative prophylactic platform that must balance efficacy with safety. The rationale for prophylactic JAK inhibition is grounded in preclinical evidence highlighting the central role of the JAK–STAT pathway in aGVHD pathogenesis [28, 29]. Several clinical studies have reported encouraging outcomes with JAK inhibitors, including ruxolitinib, itacitinib, and baricitinib, for GVHD prevention [30, 31]. Ruxolitinib, a selective JAK1/2 inhibitor, modulates both the initiation and progression of GVHD while preserving immune tolerance. It is already recommended as a first-line treatment for steroid-refractory aGVHD and cGVHD [27]. Our previous retrospective analysis [17] suggested that adding ruxolitinib to standard prophylaxis halved the rate of severe GVHD without compromising engraftment or survival. In this prospective trial, ruxolitinib reduced the incidence of grade II–IV aGVHD from 38.9% to 13% (p < 0.01). Moreover, the ruxolitinib group achieved a GFFS rate of 91.6%, representing an absolute improvement of 20% over conventional prophylaxis (72.1%). Subgroup analyses demonstrated ruxolitinib's unique ability to mitigate traditional risk factors, particularly in patients undergoing haplo-HSCT and those with pretransplant infections. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in certain high-risk subgroups, such as patients ≥ 50 years, those with prior ATG failure, or those receiving ABO-incompatible transplants, consistent trends favored ruxolitinib. These findings suggest that limited sample size may have constrained the statistical power of subgroup analyses, underscoring the need for larger, targeted studies.

Engraftment was neither impaired nor delayed with ruxolitinib prophylaxis, and no cases of primary graft failure occurred, alleviating historical concerns about JAK inhibitor–induced myelosuppression. Furthermore, the reduced incidence of secondary PGF or better platelet recovery in the ruxolitinib group may be attributed to its effect on reducing GVHD rates, whereas sPGF in the control group primarily resulted from CMV infection and GVHD treatment. On the other hand, our dosing strategy (5 mg twice daily from conditioning through day + 90) was feasible, with only a minority of patients requiring dose reduction for thrombocytopenia. In addition, patients receiving ruxolitinib exhibited significantly accelerated recovery of CD4 + Tregs, achieving a 2.1-fold increase at 3 months compared with controls, consistent with previous reports. Enhanced Treg recovery may support durable immune tolerance. These results align with murine models showing that JAK inhibition augments Treg-mediated suppression of alloreactive T cells [29, 32, 33]. Enhanced Treg recovery, consistent with prior reports, may contribute to improved GVHD control.

Beyond lymphocyte reconstitution, ruxolitinib also significantly suppressed proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-12, and IFN, during the critical peri-engraftment phase, thereby dampening the cytokine storm that drives early GVHD. This mechanistic insight provides a strong rationale for peri-transplant initiation of ruxolitinib.

Although JAK inhibition may raise concerns about infection risk, our study demonstrated no significant increase in bacterial or fungal infections. Interestingly, ruxolitinib prophylaxis revealed a paradoxical virologic pattern: a significant reduction in CMV reactivation accompanied by an increase in EBV reactivation, including fatal cases of EBV-HLH. This rise in EBV requires careful contextual interpretation. Patients in the ruxolitinib group widely received letermovir for CMV prophylaxis, available in mainland China after August 2022, whereas most historical controls did not. Several Chinese centers have reported increased EBV reactivation in the letermovir era, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear [34, 35]. Thus, the observed EBV increase may reflect either enhanced GVHD prophylaxis from ruxolitinib or the broader phenomenon of EBV resurgence associated with letermovir use. Supporting the latter, EBV reactivation rates in our ruxolitinib cohort (34.8%) were consistent with nationwide reports (20–58.1%). Prospective studies are warranted to further examine EBV reactivation under combined ruxolitinib and letermovir prophylaxis. On the other hand, analysis of the CMV-infected subgroup revealed a consistent trend toward potential benefit with ruxolitinib prophylaxis. This observation implies that the administration of letermovir for CMV prophylaxis may substantially mitigate the negative prognosis impact of CMV, even under the ruxolitinib involving prophylactic strategies.

This study provides the first prospective validation of ruxolitinib prophylaxis in AA, though certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-center design and moderate sample size necessitate confirmation in larger, multicenter trials. Second, evolving CMV prophylaxis practices, particularly the introduction of letermovir, partially confound the infection analyses. Third, a longer follow-up beyond 2 years is needed to fully assess the impact of ruxolitinib on cGVHD.

Conclusion

This study establishes ruxolitinib as a promising prophylactic strategy for GVHD. By reducing grade II–IV aGVHD while preserving engraftment, ruxolitinib helps overcome a major limitation of PB grafts. Importantly, its ability to mitigate donor- and infection-related barriers may broaden transplant eligibility for historically high-risk populations.
