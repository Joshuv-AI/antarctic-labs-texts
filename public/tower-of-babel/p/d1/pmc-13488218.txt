Pyrethroid resistance intensity and preserved susceptibility to novel insecticides in Anopheles gambiae s.l. along a south–north transect in Benin
Authors: Casimir Dossou Kpanou, Albert Sourou Salako, Mohammed N. A. Fagbemi, Steve Zinsou Hougbe, Ousmane Diaby, Pierre Faya Leno, Arnauld M. S. Djissou, Almamy Ousmane Deen Camara, Arthur Sovi, Razaki A. Ossé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe emergence and widespread distribution of insecticide resistance in Anopheles gambiae s.l. raise serious concerns regarding the long-term effectiveness of vector control tools. Assessing resistance intensity provides more operationally relevant information than diagnostic-dose bioassays alone.MethodsThe phenotypic intensity of resistance to pyrethroids (permethrin and deltamethrin), bendiocarb, chlorfenapyr and clothianidin in Anopheles gambiae s.l. was evaluated along a south–north transect in Benin using WHO tube tests and CDC bottle bioassays. Mortality was recorded at diagnostic doses and at higher concentrations, in accordance with WHO guidelines.ResultsAt diagnostic doses, mortality rates observed with permethrin and deltamethrin were low, ranging approximately from 5–30% to 10–45%, respectively, depending on the study sites. Even at 5 × and 10 × concentrations, pyrethroid-induced mortality often remained below 90%, indicating high-intensity resistance. Resistance levels were consistently higher for permethrin than for deltamethrin, in both WHO tube tests and CDC bottle bioassays. In contrast, bendiocarb induced high mortality rates, generally exceeding 90–98% in most localities, with low to moderate resistance detected in only a few sites. Complete susceptibility of Anopheles gambiae s.l. was observed to chlorfenapyr and clothianidin, with mortality rates ≥ 98% across all communes.ConclusionsThe intense and widespread pyrethroid resistance observed in Benin represents a major threat to interventions relying on this insecticide class. WHO tube tests and CDC bottle bioassays showed similar qualitative trends, although differences in mortality levels were observed between the two methods. The relatively preserved susceptibility to bendiocarb, as well as to chlorfenapyr and clothianidin, supports their potential use in insecticide rotation strategies, including bi-treated insecticidal nets (e.g., Interceptor® G2, PermaNet® Dual, Yorkool® G5, and Mont Inari) as well as indoor residual spraying campaigns using bendiocarb or clothianidin.

Background

Malaria remains a major global public health problem, with a particularly high burden in sub-Saharan Africa, which accounts for the majority of cases and deaths [1]. Over the past two decades, substantial progress has been achieved through the large-scale implementation of vector control interventions, notably long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS) [2]. These strategies rely primarily on the use of insecticides, which constitute a cornerstone of malaria vector control.

In Benin, malaria control is mainly based on nationwide LLIN distribution campaigns, typically conducted every three years, along with targeted IRS interventions in high-transmission areas, particularly in the northern regions of the country. These interventions have contributed to reducing malaria morbidity, although transmission remains high and heterogeneous across regions. This heterogeneity is partly explained by the diversity of ecological, climatic, and socio-economic contexts that influence malaria transmission dynamics and vector distribution [3].

However, the effectiveness of insecticide-based interventions is now threatened by the rapid emergence and spread of resistance in mosquito populations. Resistance to pyrethroids, historically considered the only class of insecticides approved for LLIN impregnation, is now widespread in Benin and across West Africa [4–6]. This resistance is driven by strong selection pressure resulting from the extensive use of insecticides in public health, as well as their widespread use in agriculture, particularly in cotton-growing areas where pyrethroids are heavily applied [5].

Traditionally, insecticide resistance monitoring has relied on bioassays using diagnostic doses, which classify populations as either susceptible or resistant [7]. However, this binary approach does not adequately capture the complexity or intensity of resistance, nor does it reflect its operational impact on the effectiveness of vector control interventions. To address this limitation, the World Health Organization recommends the use of resistance intensity assays based on exposure to increased concentrations (5 × and 10 ×) of the diagnostic dose [8]. These assays provide a more refined assessment of resistance levels and are more informative for programmatic decision-making.

In addition, different methods are used to assess insecticide resistance, including WHO tube assays and CDC bottle bioassays [9]. Although both methods are widely used, they are based on different principles (e.g., exposure time and insecticide delivery) and may produce variable results [10]. A better understanding of their respective performance and applicability in operational settings is therefore needed.

Despite the growing body of literature on insecticide resistance in West Africa, few studies have systematically assessed resistance intensity along large-scale ecological gradients using harmonized protocols. In Benin, available data remain fragmented and are largely based on diagnostic-dose bioassays, limiting the understanding of the true magnitude of resistance and its operational implications.

In this context, the present study aims to assess the phenotypic intensity of insecticide resistance in Anopheles gambiae sensu lato along a south–north transect encompassing diverse ecological settings in Benin, using both WHO tube assays and CDC bottle bioassays. By providing standardized and spatially comparable data, this study contributes to filling an important knowledge gap and provides a valuable baseline for monitoring resistance trends and informing vector control strategies in the country.

Methods

Study sites

The study was conducted from September 2022 to September 2023 in 13 municipalities selected along a north–south transect in Benin in order to capture the full range of the country’s geo-climatic, ecological, and epidemiological settings (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Map of Benin showing the municipalities of Cotonou and Porto-Novo (urban market-gardening zone), Missérété, Ouidah, Bantè, and Allada (cereal-growing zone), Bohicon (forest zone), Malanville (rice-growing zone), Kandi, N’Dali, and Parakou (cotton-growing zone), and Dassa and Savè (hilly zone)

Map of Benin showing the municipalities of Cotonou and Porto-Novo (urban market-gardening zone), Missérété, Ouidah, Bantè, and Allada (cereal-growing zone), Bohicon (forest zone), Malanville (rice-growing zone), Kandi, N’Dali, and Parakou (cotton-growing zone), and Dassa and Savè (hilly zone)

Cotton-growing zone (Kandi, N’Dali, and Parakou)

This zone is characterized by intensive cotton cultivation and heavy use of pesticides for cotton pest control. Multiple classes of insecticides are routinely applied, resulting in strong and sustained selection pressure on mosquito populations.

Rice-growing zone (Malanville)

The Malanville area includes a 70-hectare irrigated rice perimeter where two rice cropping cycles are practiced annually, including one during the dry season. This corresponds to off-season rice cultivation with prolonged availability of aquatic breeding habitats.

Urban market-gardening zone

This zone includes the cities of Cotonou and Porto-Novo. Although no agricultural insecticide treatments are applied, vector control relies heavily on long-lasting insecticidal nets, aerosol insecticide sprays, and mosquito coils used by households.

Cereal-growing zone

This zone comprises the localities of Missérété, Bantè, Ouidah, and Allada, located in the Ouémé, Collines, and Atlantique departments. Agricultural activities are dominated by cereal production, with moderate use of agrochemicals.

Forest zone

This zone is represented by Bohicon, a municipality located in the Zou department. Agricultural activities mainly include millet and maize cultivation.

Hilly zone

Located in central Benin, this zone is characterized by rocky outcrops and heterogeneous landscapes. It is represented by the municipalities of Dassa and Savè.

Collection and rearing of immature mosquitoes

Mosquito larvae and pupae were collected during the rainy seasons, a period characterized by increased availability of breeding sites and high vector densities, allowing the collection of sufficient and representative samples of local mosquito populations. Sampling was carried out from different types of larval habitats using standard dipping methods. Collected larvae and pupae were transferred into labeled containers containing water from their original breeding sites to minimize transport-related stress.

In the insectary, larvae were sorted and reared to adulthood under controlled environmental conditions of 25 ± 2 °C and 75 ± 10% relative humidity, with a 12 h:12 h light–dark photoperiod. Larvae were fed daily with finely ground fish food until pupation. Pupae were subsequently transferred into emergence cages for adult emergence. Emerged adults were maintained in holding cages and provided with a 10% sugar solution. Non-blood-fed female mosquitoes aged 2–5 days were used for bioassays. Adult mosquitoes were morphologically identified under a binocular microscope using standard taxonomic identification keys [11]. Larval collection was preferred because it allows the collection of large numbers of individuals from diverse breeding habitats while minimizing biases associated with the physiological status of adults (e.g., blood-fed, gravid, or old females) commonly encountered during adult collections. Furthermore, rearing larvae to adulthood ensures that mosquitoes used in bioassays are of known age and uniform physiological status, which is essential for the standardization of insecticide susceptibility testing.

Resistance intensity testing

WHO susceptibility tube tests

Insecticide susceptibility tests were conducted in accordance with standard protocols of the World Health Organization (WHO) for monitoring insecticide resistance in malaria vector mosquitoes [7]. Non-blood-fed female Anopheles gambiae sensu lato, aged 2–5 days, were used for all bioassays.

Exposures were performed using insecticide-impregnated papers supplied by WHO Collaborating Centres, containing the following insecticides at diagnostic doses (1 ×): permethrin (0.75%), deltamethrin (0.05%), and bendiocarb (0.1%). Higher concentrations corresponding to 5 × and 10 × the diagnostic doses were also used to assess resistance intensity, in line with WHO recommendations.

For each insecticide and concentration, approximately 20–25 mosquitoes were introduced into each of four exposure tubes lined with treated papers and exposed for 60 min. In parallel, two tubes lined with untreated papers were used as controls.

After exposure, mosquitoes were transferred to holding tubes and maintained under standard insectary conditions (27 ± 2 °C and 75 ± 5% relative humidity), with continuous access to a 10% sugar solution. Mortality was recorded 24 h post-exposure [7].

CDC bottle bioassay

CDC bottle bioassays were performed following standard protocols of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for monitoring insecticide resistance in mosquito vectors. The insecticides used were permethrin, deltamethrin, and bendiocarb, obtained as technical-grade active ingredients from certified suppliers (Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

Stock solutions were prepared by dissolving insecticides in analytical-grade acetone. The diagnostic doses used were in accordance with CDC recommendations: 21.5 µg per bottle for permethrin, 12.5 µg per bottle for deltamethrin, and 12.5 µg per bottle for bendiocarb. Higher concentrations corresponding to 5 × and 10 × the diagnostic doses were also prepared to assess resistance intensity.

Each 250-ml Wheaton glass bottle was coated with 1 ml of the insecticide solution and gently rolled to ensure uniform distribution across the surface. Bottles were then left to dry for at least 24 h at room temperature to allow complete evaporation of the solvent.

For each insecticide and concentration, approximately 20–25 mosquitoes were introduced into each of 4 treated bottles. A control bottle coated with acetone only was included for each test. Mortality was recorded at the diagnostic time of 30 min, in accordance with CDC guidelines [7].

Bottle bioassays with chlorfenapyr

The stock solution of chlorfenapyr was first diluted in acetone supplemented with MERO. Glass Wheaton bottles (250 ml) and their caps were then coated with 1 ml of chlorfenapyr solution at a concentration of 100 µg/ml by rotating and inverting the bottles to ensure uniform coverage. At the same time, 2 control bottles were coated with 1 ml of acetone only. All bottles were subsequently covered with aluminum foil and left to dry in the dark for 24 h.

Batches of 25 wild female mosquitoes were introduced into bottles coated either with chlorfenapyr (100 µg/ml) or acetone (control) and exposed for 60 min. After exposure, mosquitoes were transferred into paper cups covered with untreated netting and provided with cotton pads soaked in 10% sugar solution (renewed daily). Mortality was recorded immediately at 60 min and subsequently at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h post-exposure to assess delayed mortality.

Bottle bioassays with clothianidin

Bottle bioassays with clothianidin were performed using the CDC bottle assay method in accordance with WHO guidelines [7]. Glass bottles of 250 mL capacity were coated with a clothianidin solution prepared using acetone supplemented with MERO as solvent and adjuvant, respectively, at the diagnostic concentration of 4 µg/bottle. After coating, bottles were dried at room temperature and protected from light before use.

For each test, batches of 20–25 non-blood-fed wild female mosquitoes aged 2–5 days were introduced into the treated bottles and exposed for 60 min. Control bottles coated only with the solvent mixture (acetone + MERO) were used in parallel for each test series.

At the end of the exposure period, mosquitoes were transferred into paper cups covered with untreated netting and maintained under standard insectary conditions (25 ± 2 °C and 75 ± 10% relative humidity). Mosquitoes were provided with cotton pads soaked in 10% sugar solution. Mortality was recorded 24 h post-exposure.

Resistance intensity maps were generated using QGIS software (version 3.28). Mortality data from WHO tube assays were used to classify resistance intensity according to WHO criteria. Each site was categorized (low, moderate, high resistance intensity) and visualized using predefined color codes.

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.2.0). Mortality rates were compared across concentrations using analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Differences between WHO and CDC bioassays were assessed using paired t-tests. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied.

Results

Response of Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations to increasing doses of deltamethrin

Using WHO tube assays, Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations from all 13 localities exhibited very low mortality rates (< 50%) 24 h after exposure to the diagnostic dose of deltamethrin (0.05%), indicating widespread resistance across all study sites. Increasing the deltamethrin concentration to 5 × resulted in higher mortality rates; however, mortality remained low, ranging from 40 to 85%, suggesting sustained resistance. At the 10 × concentration, mortality increased further but resistance was not fully overcome, with mortality rates ranging from 66 to 98%. Discussion

The present study highlights a high level of phenotypic resistance to pyrethroids and an overall preserved susceptibility to carbamates in Anopheles gambiae sensu lato along the south–north transect in Benin. The results are discussed separately for WHO tube assays and CDC bottle bioassays, given the methodological differences between these two approaches.

Results from WHO tube assays revealed widespread and high resistance to permethrin and deltamethrin across all study sites. The low mortality rates observed at diagnostic doses, as well as mortality remaining below 90% even at higher concentrations (5 × and 10 ×), indicate a high intensity of resistance. These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in Benin and across West Africa [4–6] as well as studies from other African countries such as Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, and Nigeria, where intense pyrethroid resistance has been widely reported [12–14]. The higher resistance observed to permethrin compared to deltamethrin is consistent with findings from several African settings, where type I pyrethroids (e.g., permethrin) are often less effective than type II pyrethroids (e.g., deltamethrin) [15]. With regard to bendiocarb, WHO tube assay results showed generally high susceptibility across most sites, with mortality rates approaching or reaching 100%. These findings are in agreement with previous studies conducted in Benin [4–6], as well as studies from other African countries, including Ghana and Cameroon, where carbamates have remained relatively effective [16, 17]. However, emerging resistance to carbamates has been reported in some parts of West Africa, particularly in association with the ace-1 mutation, suggesting that this susceptibility may be transient if selection pressure increases [15, 18]. The local variations observed in this study may reflect differences in agricultural practices, insecticide usage, or vector species composition across sites.

CDC bottle bioassays also confirmed significant resistance to pyrethroids, although mortality rates were generally higher than those observed in WHO tube assays. Similar discrepancies have been reported in comparative studies, suggesting that CDC bioassays may sometimes overestimate mortality due to more uniform insecticide exposure [19, 20]. Despite these quantitative differences, the overall trends were consistent with WHO assay results, confirming high levels of pyrethroid resistance in the studied populations. These findings support the robustness of the observed resistance patterns, regardless of the method used.

Regarding bendiocarb, CDC bioassays also indicated high susceptibility across most sites, corroborating the WHO results. Similar observations have been reported in several African countries, where carbamates continue to show high efficacy despite widespread pyrethroid resistance [13, 16, 17].

The differences observed between WHO and CDC assay results confirm that these two methods are not directly comparable, as highlighted in recent WHO guidelines [8]. These discrepancies may be attributed to variations in experimental conditions, including exposure time, insecticide formulation, and mode of contact. In this study, WHO tube assays were prioritized for operational interpretation in line with international recommendations, while CDC bioassays were used as a complementary tool to support and validate observed trends.

The tests conducted with new-generation insecticides revealed a high susceptibility of Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations to chlorfenapyr across all study sites, with mortality rates reaching or exceeding 98%. This high efficacy, particularly evident at 72 h post-exposure, is consistent with the specific mode of action of chlorfenapyr. As a pro-insecticide belonging to the pyrrole class, it disrupts mitochondrial energy production, leading to delayed mortality [21]. Its relatively slow mode of action, compared to conventional neurotoxic insecticides, explains the lower mortality observed at 48 h in certain localities such as Parakou, N’dali, and Dassa, followed by a marked increase at 72 h. Similar trends have been reported in West Africa, where high delayed mortality rates were observed with chlorfenapyr in pyrethroid-resistant populations [22, 23]. The slight reduction in mortality observed in Parakou, even after 72 h (99%), although marginal, may reflect local variability in the response of vector populations. This variability could be associated with differences in insecticide selection pressure or environmental factors influencing mosquito metabolism [21]. Nevertheless, the recorded mortality rates remain well above the WHO susceptibility threshold, indicating no evidence of operational resistance to chlorfenapyr in the study areas.

Regarding clothianidin, Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations exhibited near-complete susceptibility across all communes, with mortality rates close to 100% as early as 24 h post-exposure. This high efficacy confirms the strong potential of this neonicotinoid insecticide, which acts on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, causing rapid paralysis followed by death [24]. The slight variations observed in Parakou (98%) and Porto-Novo (99%) are negligible and remain consistent with full susceptibility according to WHO criteria.

These findings are consistent with several recent studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, which have also demonstrated the high efficacy of clothianidin against Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations exhibiting multiple resistance to conventional insecticides [25, 26]. The absence of detected resistance in the present study may be explained by the relatively recent introduction of this molecule into vector control programs, thereby limiting the selection pressure exerted on mosquito populations.

The high level of pyrethroid resistance observed suggests a potential reduction in the effectiveness of conventional LLINs, highlighting the urgent need to strengthen resistance management strategies. These may include the deployment of next-generation LLINs incorporating multiple active ingredients or synergists (e.g., Interceptor® G2, PermaNet® Dual, Yorkool® G5, and Mont Inari), as well as the targeted use of alternative insecticides in indoor residual spraying interventions. The preserved susceptibility to bendiocarb suggests that carbamates may represent a viable alternative in this context. However, their use should be carefully managed to prevent the rapid development of resistance, as has been observed with pyrethroids.

This study has several limitations. First, the lack of data on species composition within the Anopheles gambiae s.l. complex, due to limited funding, restricts the ability to fully explain observed variations across sites, as different species may exhibit distinct resistance profiles. Second, the absence of molecular analyses, including genotyping of resistance markers such as kdr and ace-1, prevents identification of the underlying mechanisms driving the observed phenotypes. Finally, the study did not account for environmental and anthropogenic factors, such as agricultural practices or domestic insecticide use, which may influence resistance levels.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important standardized data on insecticide resistance intensity in Benin and represents a valuable baseline for monitoring resistance trends and informing vector control strategies.

Conclusion

The intense and widespread pyrethroid resistance observed in Benin represents a major threat to interventions relying on this insecticide class. WHO tube tests and CDC bottle bioassays showed similar qualitative trends, although differences in mortality levels were observed between the two methods. The relatively preserved susceptibility to bendiocarb, as well as to chlorfenapyr and clothianidin, supports their potential use in insecticide rotation strategies, including bi-treated insecticidal nets (e.g., Interceptor® G2, PermaNet® Dual, Yorkool® G5, and Mont Inari) as well as indoor residual spraying campaigns using bendiocarb or clothianidin.
