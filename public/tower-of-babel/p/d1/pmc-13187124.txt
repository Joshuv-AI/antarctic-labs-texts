Validation of the inferior vena cava collapsibility as a predictive marker of fluid responsiveness in spontaneously breathing patients
Authors: Benoît Ter Schiphorst, Claire Bourel, Arthur Durand, Camille Ternynck, Thierry Onimus, Michael Howsam, Alexandre Pierre, Julien De Jonckheere, Raphael Favory, Sebastien Preau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In a previous derivation cohort, we found that inferior vena cava collapsibility (cIVC) thresholds of ≥ 33% during non-standardised breathing (cIVC-ns) and ≥ 44% during a standardised breathing manoeuvre (cIVC-st) identified fluid responsiveness (FR). The aim of this study was to validate these findings through a secondary analysis of a prospective validation cohort. We measured cIVC-ns and cIVC-st in spontaneously breathing adult patients in a sub-xiphoid, long-axis view, using the two-dimensional mode, at 4 cm from the cavo-atrial junction. An increase in the subaortic time-velocity integral during a passive leg raising test (∆VTI) ≥ 10% was used to define FR. Among 61 intensive care unit (ICU) patients included, a cIVC-ns ≥ 33% predicted FR with a sensitivity and a specificity of 84% and 83%, respectively. A cIVC-st ≥ 44% predicted FR with a sensitivity and a specificity of 94% and 87%, respectively. A cIVC-ns ≥ 33% and a cIVC-st ≥ 44% misclassified 16.4% and 8.2% of the patients respectively (p = 0.06). Measuring cIVC is a reliable, non-invasive tool to predict FR in pontaneously breathing patients admitted to ICU. Introduction

Volume expansion is frequently used in intensive care, with the aim of increasing stroke volume1 and improve patients’ prognoses. However, only 40 to 70% of patients hospitalised in intensive care units (ICU) are fluid responsive (FR), defined as an increase in cardiac output or stroke volume in response to volume expansion2. Although the optimal volume of fluid to administer is difficult to determine, both hypovolemia and fluid overload adversely affect patient prognosis3,4. Therefore, it is essential to predict whether initiating, continuing, or stopping fluid administration is likely to be beneficial.

Several dynamic indices to predict FR have been proposed2,5, but they are not widely used in ICUs, in part because of their technical nature and requirement for invasive monitoring6, but also because many have not been validated in spontaneously breathing patients7,8.

Ultrasound assessment of the collapsibility of inferior vena cava (cIVC) is a non-invasive tool that has been proposed to predict FR in these patients9–12, but its use in ICU remains limited, perhaps as a result of contradictory data in the literature13,14. These studies used different measurement methods, notably in terms of ultrasonographic views and measurement sites of the inferior vena cava (IVC), which may account for their contradictory findings. Conversely, the use of ventilatory manoeuvres has been shown to improve the accuracy of FR prediction, by enhancing the cyclic changes in intrathoracic and transpulmonary pressures15,16.

Our team has shown that measurements of cIVC are most reliable in a longitudinal, two-dimensional mode at 4 cm from the IVC abutment in the right atrium, and that the use of a standardised, easily achievable inspiration manoeuvre during measurement improves the accuracy of cIVC to predict FR17–19. In a derivation cohort, optimal cIVC thresholds were defined to predict FR: namely ≥ 33% during non-standardised breathing (cIVC-ns) and ≥ 44% during a standardised inspiration manoeuvre (cIVC-st)19.

The present validation cohort was designed to prospectively assess the ability of these cIVC thresholds to predict fluid responsiveness in spontaneously breathing ICU patients.

Methods

Trial design

This is a secondary analysis of a prospective single-centre study, registered under the number NCT03780660 on ClinicalTrials.gov20. It was conducted in 6 ICUs of the Lille university hospital (CHU Lille). Inclusions were performed from February 2019 to April 2021, after the ethical approval from the Ile de France ethics committee (2018-A01449-46). Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients, after written and oral information regarding the objectives of the study and the course of the experiment. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for medical research involving human participants.

Patients

Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged 18 years or older, admitted in one of the participating ICUs, spontaneously breathing without mechanical ventilation or tracheostomy, and equipped with a bladder catheter suitable to measure intra-abdominal pressure.

Exclusion criteria comprised refusal to participate to the study, lack of national French health insurance, legal incapacity, pregnancy, active exhalation, a need for urgent hemodynamic treatment during the protocol, a lack of appropriate ultrasound sections of IVC impeding IVC-diameter measurements, or it being impossible to assess FR via a passive leg raising (PLR), namely in the event of intracranial hypertension, lower limb amputation, high-grade aortic insufficiency, echogenicity unsuitable for measuring the left ventricle outflow tract (LVOT) or velocity-time integral (VTI), or intra-abdominal hypertension defined as an intra-abdominal pressure > 16 mmHg21,22.

Data collection

Patients’ characteristics and clinical data were collected prospectively and included age, sex, body mass index, medical and surgical history, prognostic scores calculated on the day of inclusion in the study (simplified acute physiology score (SAPS) II; sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, and Charlson comorbidity index), length of stay in ICU at the time of inclusion, or presence of clinical signs of shock : mottled skin, hypotension (systolic arterial pressure < 90 mmHg or a decrease of 40 mmHg in a previously hypertensive patient), tachycardia (heart rate ≥ 100/min) and oliguria (urine output < 0.5 mL kg− 1 h− 1 over one hour or more).

Study protocol

The course of the protocol is described in Fig. 1. The experiment consisted of breathing normally for 30 s through a device measuring inspiratory pressure, each of these ventilatory cycles corresponding to non-standardised breathing. Then, the patient was asked to perform a standardised inspiration, which consisted of a deep, continuous and brief (< 5 s) inspiration, generating a depression greater than − 3 mm H2O. This deep, standardised inspiration was repeated 3 times. IVC recordings were performed simultaneously, in order to remotely measure cIVC-ns and cIVC-st.

A first Doppler recording of the LVOT was performed in a semi-seated position. We then performed a PLR manoeuvre, which consisted in the inclination of the trunk to 0°, with an angle being kept at 45° with the lower limbs. Another Doppler recording of the LVOT was performed within two minutes of the start of the PLR. These Doppler measurements allowed PLR-induced changes in the LVOT-VTI (ΔVTI), our gold-standard test to assess FR.

The minimum inspiratory pressure was recorded with a manometer MP101 (0–100 mmH2O) (KIMO instrument, Montpon, France) during both non-standardised and standardised inspirations, as previously described18(Supplementary Figure S1). A decrease in inspiratory pressure greater than 3 mm H2O during inspiration defined a standardised inspiration, based on previous studies by our team, and we have shown that this threshold is both easily achievable in ICU patients and sufficient to improve the performance of cIVC measurements to predict FR17,18.

Echography recordings

Echocardiography recordings were performed using a Vivid S5 ultrasound machine (General Electric, Solsingen, Germany) equipped with a trans-thoracic probe. All observers were trained in echocardiography23.

The IVC recordings were performed by a first observer using a sub-xyphoid long-axis view in two-dimensional mode, with the patient sitting in a semi-recumbent position, as described previously18. Prior acquisition of the ultrasound loops, the focus was upon being able to see the largest possible IVC diameter, in an attempt to obtain the widest section of the vessel in the sagittal plane during the whole ventilatory cycle. All IVC measurements (including the ones used for Intra- and inter-observer reliability) were remotely performed in 2022 specifically for this secondary analysis. The measurements were conducted using the EchoPAC Software (General Electric Healthcare, Chicago, USA) after anonymisation. They were performed by a different operator from that which measured ΔVTI. Minimum inspiratory and end-expiratory diameters of the IVC were measured perpendicular to the IVC wall, from the trailing edge of the anterior wall to the leading edge of the posterior wall. Importantly, measurements were performed at 4 cm from the termination of the IVC in the right atrium, as previously described18(Fig. 1). The IVC diameters were measured during non-standardised and standardised non-consecutive ventilatory cycles. The mean of three measurements was used to calculate cIVC, with the following formula being used to calculate each of the triplicate measurements: cIVC (%) = 100 x (expiratory diameter - inspiratory diameter)/expiratory diameter.

Doppler recordings of the LVOT were performed using pulsed Doppler mode in an apical 5-cavity view. They were performed by a second observer just before PLR and within 2 min of a PLR. Recordings were stored digitally and anonymised. A second observer, blinded to clinical data and IVC measurements, performed additional measurements in 2021 as part of the original publication by Bourel et al.20. The LVOT-VTI were obtained using a trans-thoracic view, measuring the area under the Doppler velocity curve of the sub-aortic blood flow during systole. Values were averaged over fifteen consecutive cardiac cycles. A PLR manoeuvre was performed as a self-fluid challenge. The ΔVTI was calculated as follows: ΔVTI (%) = 100 * (LVOT-VTI during PLR – LVOT-VTI at baseline)/LVOT-VTI at baseline.

A ΔVTI ≥ 10% defined FR7,24–27. This threshold was used because it has been shown to be sufficient to be clinically relevant, as long as both LVOT-VTIs are measured by the same observer8,26–28.

Sample size calculation

Sixty-eight patients were required to predict FR with an α risk of 5% (2-sided test), a β risk of 10%, and an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for cIVC tests greater than 0.7, anticipating a FR rate of 60% and an area under the ROC curve of 0.89, as obtained in our previous study on cIVC during a standardised inspiratory manoeuvre19.

Statistics

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages), and continuous variables as medians (interquartile range). Normality was checked graphically and using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons of patients’ characteristics according to FR were performed using the Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables.

Diagnostic values (and their 95% confidence intervals) of the published optimal thresholds of cIVC-ns (namely cIVC-ns ≥ 33%) and cIVC-st (namely cIVC-st ≥ 44%) to predict FR were evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive likelihood ratio and negative likelihood ratio19. The rates of good and poor classification of these thresholds were compared between non-standardised and standardised measures using McNemar’s test.

As well as this single-threshold analysis, we also designed a grey-zone approach using scatterplot diagrams to provide visual representations of the grey-zones (Supplementary Appendix l).

Correlations between cIVC-ns and cIVC-st on the one hand, and IAP or ΔVTI on the other hand, were assessed by calculating Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients.

Intra- and inter-observer reliability (between 3 observers) for cIVC-st were evaluated by calculating the intraclass correlation coefficient.

All statistical tests were performed at the two-tailed α level of 0.05 using SAS® software, Version 9.4. Copyright © 2023, SAS Institute Inc. SAS and all other SAS Institute Inc. product or service names are registered trademarks or trademarks of SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA.

Results

Of the 90 patients screened, 22 were not enrolled, including 10 due to neurological reasons that prevented their informed consent and could have potentially hindered the standardisation of inspiration. Overall, 68 patients were included. After exclusion of 7 patients (5 for unusable corrupted files and 2 for inappropriate recordings), 61 patients were finally analysed of which 38 (62%) were fluid responsive (Fig. 2). All patients had at least one indication for hemodynamic assessment: 28 (45.9%) clinical signs of shock, 6 (9.8%) intravenous norepinephrine, 28 (45.9%) acute respiratory failure, 17 (27.9%) diuretic therapy, and 47 (77.0%) peripheral oedema, fluid accumulation or a central venous pressure > 10 mmHg. Inter-observer agreement for cIVC-st measurements was 0.87 (95%CI, 0.57; 0.98), while agreement among the 3 intra-observer measurements were 0.93 (95%CI, 0.88; 0.96), 0.97 (95%CI, 0.95; 0.98) and 0.98 (95%CI, 0.97;0.99). During non-standardised inspiration, 19 of the 49 patients (39%) for whom this parameter was measured, achieved a drop in inspiratory pressure greater than − 3 mm H2O. Measurements of cIVC-st were obtained in 58 patients (95.1%). A standardised inspiration manoeuvre resulted in a median inspiratory pressure significantly lower than during non-standardised breathing (−7.5 versus − 1.8 mm H2O; p < 0.001). Boxplots and scatterplots of individual values for cIVC in responders and non-responders to PLR are presented in Fig. 3. At baseline, cIVC-ns and cIVC-st were positively correlated with ∆VTI (r = 0.58 and 0.56 respectively; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S2), but not with intra-abdominal pressure (p = 0.15 and 0.26, respectively). The areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves for prediction of FR were of 0.86 (95% CI, 0.75 to 0.97) and 0.86 (95% CI, 0.73 to 0.98) for cIVC-ns and cIVC-st, respectively. Sensitivity and specificity for prediction of FR were, respectively, 84.2% and 82.6% for a cIVC-ns ≥ 33%, and 94.3% and 87% for a cIVC-st ≥ 44% (Table 3). These previously determined thresholds allowed us to classify the patients as follows: 51 (83.6%) patients were correctly classified for cIVC-ns, compared to 53 (86.9%) for cIVC-st (p = 0.41); 10 (16.4%) patients were misclassified for cIVC-ns, compared to 5 (8.2%) for cIVC-st (p = 0.06); no inconclusive results for cIVC-ns, compared to 3 (4.9%) for cIVC-st. Standardisation of the inspiration allowed a decrease in both false positive (3 versus 4) false negative (2 versus 6) results of cIVC measurements’ prediction of FR. Individual data of the false negative and false positive patients using cIVC-st can be found in Supplementary Table S1. Algorithms using cIVC to guide the decision on administering fluids with a single-threshold and a two-threshold approaches are suggested in Figs. 4 and S3, respectively. A grey-zone analysis is detailed in the Supplementary Appendix I, Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Figures S4 and S5.

Discussion

This secondary analysis of a prospective cohort validates, for the first time, our previously proposed cIVC thresholds for predicting FR in spontaneously breathing patients19. A standardised inspiration manoeuvre during echocardiography measurements (cIVC-st) yielded a threshold of cIVC-st ≥ 44% as being predictive of FR with a sensitivity of 94.3% and a specificity of 87%. Over 86% of the patients studied here were correctly classified with respect to FR, and the low negative likelihood ratio of 0.1 affords a high level of confidence in excluding FR if cIVC-st is below this threshold. In terms of clinical application, we propose employing these robust cIVC-st thresholds in the first place, even during non-standardised breathing. Compared with this simple strategy, a more complicated approach using two distinct thresholds (33% for cIVC-ns and 44% for cIVC-st) (Supplementary Figure S3) or a grey-zone (Supplementary Figure S5) did not increase the predictive accuracy of the algorithm to predict FR in this cohort. In the event of hypoperfusion, and if cIVC-ns is superior or equal to 44%, the patient would likely benefit from fluid expansion without the need for a further measurement during a deeper inhalation. Conversely, if cIVC-ns is less than 44%, then a standardised inspiration should be performed and the 44% threshold applied to determine FR (Fig. 4).

As a result of the inclusion criteria in this study, our patients were not severely ill: all the patients were spontaneously breathing, without mechanical support, and only 10% were in receipt of intravenous vasopressors. Therefore, the results should be directly applicable in emergency, intermediate care or peri-operative patients. This is an interesting point, as these patients are generally less subject to monitoring by invasive devices and may therefore benefit from this non-invasive approach.

It is noteworthy that the present results remain accurate only if measurements of IVC diameters are performed as follows: in the sub-xyphoid long-axis view, using the two-dimensional mode, perpendicular to the IVC wall, at a distance of 4 cm before its termination in the right atrium19. Unlike transverse incidence or time-motion measurements, a long-axis view in two-dimensional mode enables measurements of both inspiratory and expiratory diameters at the same site. This minimises the bias resulting from the caudal movement of the IVC, averaged at 21.7 mm during quiet inspiration29. In our opinion, while these conditions represent a constraint, the improved reproducibility of the measurements makes adhering to them worthwhile. This opinion is supported by our excellent inter-observer reliability, which is better than previously reported in two-dimensional mode, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.87 (95%CI, 0.57; 0.98) compared with 0.58 (95%CI, 0.34; 0.74) in the study of Fields and colleagues30. However, unlike the study by Fields et al., inter-observer agreement in our study was calculated based on the same pre-recorded video loops, which may have enhanced the correlation. The reproducibility of cIVC measurements under real-life conditions, using the precise methodology described here, has not yet been assessed, and should be explored in future studies.

Concerning the three false positive results of cIVC-st, excessive inhalation can be discounted as an explanation. Indeed, these patients also reached the threshold of 44% in non-standardised breathing, with inspiratory pressure values comprised between − 0.1 and − 9.0 mmH2O. From a clinical point of view, false positives are of concern to the physician because these patients might therefore be exposed to unnecessary volume expansion. However, as discussed in a previous publication, in these patients the crossing point between venous return and cardiac function would likely be at the junction between the ascending and the flat section of the Frank-Starling curve17. Consequently, in the event of administering volume expansion, these patients’ hearts would shift to the plateau portion of the curve and they would become true negatives if re-assessed before a second fluid administration. Accordingly, every false positive cIVC-st in a previous study led to non-collapsible IVC after volume expansion18 and fluid administration would therefore be stopped, limiting the risk of fluid overload. Unfortunately, we were unable to measure cIVC with sufficient precision during the PLR because of the transient hemodynamic effects this manoeuvre induces (1–2 min). Since we prioritised the evaluation of the VTI during PLR, measurement of cIVC might have been performed too late after leg raising, and the PLR-induced volume expansion had already diminished. It is noteworthy that PLR has already been shown to induce a decrease in cIVC31.

Among the 2 false negative results using cIVC-st, one patient did not attain an inspiratory pressure below the − 3 mmH2O threshold. The key message here is to ensure that patients perform a sufficiently deep inspiration to avoid as many false negatives as possible. Furthermore, inspirations involving only the chest wall have been shown to induce a lower cIVC than diaphragmatic inspirations32,33. We did not measure diaphragmatic excursion, nor direct patients to breathe in a specific manner, and therefore cannot exclude that inspiration exclusively using the chest wall may have been involved in our false negative patients.

Unlike the original derivation study19, the number of patients correctly or incorrectly classified as fluid responders did not differ significantly between cIVC-ns and cIVC-st. This may be attributable to insufficient statistical power, particularly since this analysis was not the primary outcome. Alternatively, it could reflect differences in patient severity, as suggested by lower SAPS II scores in this cohort compared to the derivation study (26 (19;32) versus 34 (24; 42))19. This could have led to better basal state breathing performance in our patients than in the derivation study, apparently confirmed by the lower inspiratory pressure levels seen here during non-standardised breathing (respectively − 1.8 mmH2O (−4.0; −0.5) versus − 0.5 mmH2O (−1.0; 0.0))19. Sufficient inspiration could also explain the high sensitivity of cIVC-ns to predict FR in this study compared with previous data in ICU, which reported lower sensitivities ranging from 31% to 76%9,18,34,35. Moreover, the fact that patients in the present study may have inhaled more deeply during non-standardised breathing could also explain the smaller relative improvement of using a standardised inspiration manoeuvre than seen in the derivation study.

There are some limitations to our study. Firstly, this is a single-center study which limits the generalizability of the findings to other centers or populations. Secondly, this secondary analysis was conducted in a cohort that largely overlapped with the population of the original publication20. While we cannot exclude the possibility that the overall rate of fluid responders in the study by Bourel et al. was known to the operators at the time of IVC measurements, the investigators were blinded to the individual fluid responsiveness status of patients during the IVC analysis, minimizing the risk of classification or confirmation bias. Thirdly, an ultrasonographic view of the IVC could not be obtained in 5 (6%) patients, which is in line with previous studies that reported this to occur in between 5% and 18% of spontaneous breathing patients17,18,30,36,37. Fourthly, our results cannot be applied to the excluded patients, notably for active exhalation. Similarly, none of our patients were known to have significative tricuspid regurgitation or acute right heart failure, which limits extrapolation to such patients. Finally, the measurements of cIVC face some practical limitations including the cooperation required from the patient, limited interpretation in the event of a high ventilatory frequency or diaphragmatic dysfunction, etc. However, although we recommend that an inspiration should be deep enough to achieve reliable measurements, our experience is that it remains easily achievable and seems easier than other methods previously proposed, such as a Valsalva manoeuvre16 or an inspiration lasting more than five seconds17. This makes it suitable for application even in our ICU patients, including those requiring high-flow nasal oxygen: while only 9.8% of patients required high-flow nasal oxygen in the current study, this percentage was 19% in the derivation study38. Additionally, the feasibility of the short standardized inspiratory maneuver was lower than that of non-standardized breathing but remained high, as it could be performed in 95.1% of included patients. This supports our proposed two-step approach: an initial non-standardized assessment, followed—only when necessary—by a standardized inspiratory maneuver to enhance diagnostic performance.

Conclusions

This secondary analysis of a prospective cohort confirms that cIVC measurements are a useful non-invasive test to predict FR in spontaneously breathing patients in ICU. However, the clinical added value of a standardised breathing manoeuvre to improve its diagnostic accuracy warrants further investigation in dedicated studies.
