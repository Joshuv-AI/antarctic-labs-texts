Subacute Diarrhea: A Rare Presentation of Anaplastic T‐Cell Lymphoma
Authors: Nivedita Singh, Prateek Jain, Julian Chung, Amrit Devkota, Amogh Verma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnteropathy‐associated T‐cell lymphoma type II should be considered in older patients who present with subacute diarrhea, abdominal pain, and cytopenias—even in the absence of celiac disease. Its rapid progression to bowel perforation demands prompt recognition and a multidisciplinary approach to improve outcomes.

Introduction

T‐cell lymphoma is a rare subgroup of non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, accounting for nearly 10% of all cases [1]. It is broadly classified into T‐lymphoblastic lymphoma/leukemia and peripheral T‐cell lymphoma [2]. Enteropathy‐associated T‐cell lymphoma (EATL), as per the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, is a rare form of peripheral T‐cell lymphoma with an incidence of 0.1 per 100,000 individuals in the United States and higher rates observed in Northern Europe, corresponding to the prevalence of celiac disease in these regions [3, 4, 5, 6]. In most cases, EATL is strongly associated with celiac disease and is subdivided into two types: Type 1, associated with celiac disease, constitutes 80%–90% of cases, whereas Type 2, which is not linked to celiac disease, accounts for 10%–20% [7, 8]. We present the case of a 61‐year‐old man with subacute diarrhea, who was eventually diagnosed with an anaplastic variant of EATL, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and timely intervention.

Case History/Examination

A 61‐year‐old Caucasian male presented to the emergency department of our tertiary care center in Baltimore, MD, with a three‐week history of cramping abdominal pain, occasional maroon‐colored diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting. His symptoms worsened 4 days before hospital admission, with the onset of dull and intermittent right lower quadrant pain. He had no history of fever, chills, headache, dizziness, cough, shortness of breath, or urinary symptoms. Initial vital signs revealed hypotension (BP 92/66 mmHg) along with laboratory findings of hyponatremia (123 mmol/L), hypokalemia (3.3 mmol/L), hypochloremia (96 mmol/L), and hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin 1.8 mg/dL and direct bilirubin 0.72 mg/dL). A complete blood count showed bi‐cytopenia (WBC 3.9 × 103/μL and platelets 73 × 103/μL) with normal hematocrit (39.7). Imaging, including ECG and chest X‐ray, was unremarkable, but a computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis revealed distal small bowel thickening consistent with infectious enterocolitis and splenomegaly. The patient was initially managed with intravenous fluids, morphine, ceftriaxone, and metronidazole, which resulted in temporary symptomatic relief.

Subsequently, the patient developed bright red bleeding in the rectum and bloody bowel movements, leading to hypovolemic shock and requiring transient vasopressor support. A repeat CT scan showed minimal changes, except for newly identified shotty mesenteric lymph nodes. During his hospital course, the patient experienced sudden‐onset severe abdominal distension, tachycardia, and hypotension, prompting a CT scan that revealed a small bowel perforation with peritoneal free air. Emergency exploratory laparotomy identified a 1.5 cm perforation necessitating resection and anastomosis, as shown in Figure 1. Owing to additional microperforations, a subsequent surgery was performed involving resection of another 6 cm of the small bowel, as shown in Figure 2. Pathological analysis of the resected bowel segment revealed high‐grade T‐cell lymphoma, consistent with an anaplastic variant of EATL. Methods

Differential Diagnosis

Given the patient's presentation of abdominal pain, diarrhea, and hypotension, the initial differential diagnosis included infectious enterocolitis, ischemic bowel disease, and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Infectious enterocolitis, which often presents with fever, leukocytosis, and diarrhea, can mimic EATL. However, imaging findings such as diffuse bowel wall thickening without lymphadenopathy may help differentiate between the two [9, 10]. Additionally, microbiological testing, including stool cultures and Clostridium difficile toxin assays, is critical for ruling out infectious causes [11].

Ischemic bowel disease, another important differential diagnosis, is frequently observed in older adults with cardiovascular risk factors [12]. It typically presents as sudden abdominal pain and rectal bleeding [13, 14]. Imaging findings such as segmental bowel wall thickening and reduced bowel wall enhancement on CT angiography can distinguish ischemic bowel disease from neoplasms like EATL [15]. IBD, including Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, often presents with chronic diarrhea and abdominal pain [16]. However, IBD‐associated complications such as strictures or fistulas have distinctive endoscopic and histological findings [17]. Unlike EATL, IBD rarely leads to bowel perforation without longstanding disease or steroid use [18].

The persistence of symptoms and subsequent complications, such as bowel perforation, suggests a more severe underlying pathology, raising the suspicion for malignancies such as intestinal lymphoma. Distinguishing EATL from other intestinal lymphomas, such as diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL), requires immunohistochemical markers and histopathological examination. For instance, T‐cell markers, such as CD3 and CD30, and a high Ki‐67 index (> 90%) are characteristic of EATL [19, 20], whereas B‐cell markers (CD19 and CD20) favor DLBCL [21, 22]. Ultimately, the diagnosis of EATL was established based on histopathology and immunohistochemistry findings, highlighting the importance of considering this rare entity in atypical presentations of gastrointestinal symptoms.

Investigation

CT revealed bowel perforation, which prompted surgical intervention. Histopathological examination of the resected bowel segments confirmed high‐grade T‐cell lymphoma that was positive for T‐cell markers (CD2, CD3, CD4, CD30, CD43, and CD45) and negative for CD5, CD7, CD8, CD34, CD56, EMA, GATA3, and ALK. A higher magnification (20×) demonstrated the presence of a high‐grade infiltrating neoplasm, as shown in Figure 4. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated positivity for CD2, CD3, CD4, CD30, CD43, and CD45, with a Ki67 proliferation index > 90%, as shown in Figure 5 [23]. Additional immunohistochemistry showed positivity for TIA1, Granzyme B, and TCR‐bF1, confirming the diagnosis of EATL [7]. The patient was also found to be HIV‐negative, and the hepatitis panel was negative.

Treatment

The patient was initially started on methylprednisolone, which led to symptomatic relief. However, he remained pancytopenic and was scheduled for bone marrow biopsy and positron emission tomography (PET)‐CT before initiating chemotherapy. Unfortunately, the patient developed hypotension due to anastomotic leak, which continued to deteriorate. Despite resuscitative efforts, he experienced cardiac arrest with pulseless electrical activity secondary to severe acidosis and shock. Given the poor prognosis, life‐sustaining measures were withdrawn, and the patient subsequently died during hospitalization.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient's clinical course was marked by severe complications, including bowel perforation and anastomotic leakage, ultimately leading to the withdrawal of life‐sustaining measures and his death. Despite aggressive surgical intervention, his condition worsened, precluding chemotherapy. This case highlights the poor prognosis of EATL in advanced stages and emphasizes the need for early recognition and intervention.

Discussion

Intestinal T‐cell lymphomas, including EATL, typically present with nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms, making early diagnosis challenging. These lymphomas are often clinically evident only after complications such as bowel perforation occur [24]. Evidence suggests that the median time to diagnosis from symptom onset can range from 2 to 5 months due to the overlapping presentation with other gastrointestinal disorders [25]. EATL arises predominantly in the small intestine, with a predilection for the jejunum and proximal ileum, although it can also affect other organs [26]. The jejunum's higher density of Peyer's patches and intraepithelial lymphocytes likely contribute to its vulnerability [27, 28]. EATL can also present as multifocal intestinal lesions, increasing the risk of perforation or obstruction [29].

Diagnosis before complications is difficult due to the lack of specific symptoms, and EATL is often mistaken for other conditions such as celiac disease [4]. Although most cases of EATL Type 1 are linked to celiac disease [30], the absence of serological markers, as in this case, is typical of Type 2 EATL. Histopathological examination revealing intraepithelial lymphocytosis and atrophic mucosa remains a cornerstone for diagnosis [29]. Advances in imaging modalities, such as fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)‐PET/CT, may aid in earlier detection by identifying hypermetabolic lesions in the small bowel [25, 31]. A multidisciplinary approach involving gastroenterologists, pathologists, and oncologists is crucial for managing EATL. Surgical resection is the primary intervention for bowel perforation, but chemotherapy with anthracycline‐based regimens (e.g., CHOP: cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) is essential for systemic disease control [32]. However, outcomes with chemotherapy alone remain suboptimal, with a 5‐year survival rate below 20% [33].

Emerging therapies such as autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) and targeted agents (e.g., brentuximab vedotin targeting CD30‐positive lymphomas) have shown promise in improving survival [31, 34, 35]. In one study, patients receiving ASCT had a 3‐year progression‐free survival rate of 52%, compared to 22% with chemotherapy alone [36]. Further studies are needed to evaluate the role of immunotherapies, such as checkpoint inhibitors, in EATL management. Despite the absence of celiac disease markers, the patient developed an aggressive form of EATL, ultimately leading to a poor outcome. The mainstay of treatment for EATL includes surgical resection and chemotherapy, although the prognosis remains poor, particularly in cases presenting with advanced disease and complications [37, 38]. Unfortunately, owing to the rapid clinical deterioration of the patient, chemotherapy could not be initiated.
