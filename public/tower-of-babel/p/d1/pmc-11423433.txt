Treatment of a urethral stricture by image‐guided placement of a custom‐made absorbable stent in a standing, sedated horse
Authors: Milda Baltrimaite, Clodagh Kearney, Anne O'Brien, Michael Duggan, Benoît Cuq
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 10‐year‐old Irish Sport Horse gelding developed complications from a general anesthesia resulting in sling support and recurrent urinary catheterization. The horse subsequently presented signs of dysuria and pollakiuria, was diagnosed with sabulous cystitis, and developed a urethral stricture from the repeated catheterizations, which was confirmed on urethroscopy. Clinical signs persisted despite conservative management with topical corticosteroids and urethral bougienage with balloon dilators. An image‐guided approach was used to treat the stricture with a custom‐made polydioxanone stent placed in the urethra after which the horse was able to void normally and fully empty his bladder. Repeat urethroscopy and ultrasonography 6 months after the procedure showed the stent to have completely reabsorbed with urethra remaining patent. Nineten months after the procedure, the owner reports the horse remaining at his intended level of athletic performance with no dysuria.

INTRODUCTION

Obstruction of the urinary tract in horses is an infrequent but important disease because of the severity of the clinical signs and potential sequelae. 1 Stricture formation is reported in up to 30% of horses after perineal urethrostomy and is typically associated with trauma to the urethral mucosa. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Signs of this condition include dysuria, tenesmus, pollakiuria and stranguria, dribbling urine, dropping of the penis and colic. 6 , 7

Treatment options for urethral strictures are well described in dogs and cats and include urethral resection and anastomosis, urethroplasty and urinary diversion techniques, cystotomy tube placement, urethral balloon dilatation, and urethral stent placement. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 In horses, the most commonly described treatment option is a permanent perineal urethrostomy. 4 , 12 Absorbable and nonabsorbable stents have been used for treatment of urethral strictures in humans and dogs. 13 , 14 There is a single report of the use of an absorbable urethral stent in a stallion where the procedure was performed under general anesthesia. 7 The aim of this report is to describe the successful treatment of a urethral stricture in a standing gelding by placing an absorbable urethral stent using sedation and regional anesthesia, as well as a combination of cystoscopic and ultrasonographic guidance.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 10‐year‐old Irish Sport Horse gelding (720 kg) used for general purpose riding developed postanesthetic myopathy neuropathy syndrome (PAMNS) after general anesthesia for bilateral tibiotarsal joint arthroscopy, necessitating sling support and repeated urinary catheterization over 48 hours. The horse recovered well but 10 weeks later developed dysuria and pollakiuria. Hematological and biochemical blood analysis did not reveal any abnormalities; urinalysis showed the presence of white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), and bacteria. Urine culture revealed a heavy growth of Streptococcus zooepidemicus. Cystoscopy revealed a moderate amount of sabulous material on the floor of the bladder. The bladder was lavaged, and a 5‐day course of trimethoprim potentiated sulfonamides (Trimediazine Plain, Vetoquinol, France; 30 mg/kg, PO, q12h) and phenylbutazone (Equipalazone, Dechra, United Kingdom; 2.2 mg/kg, PO, q12h for 2 days and q24h for further 3 days) administered. Improvement was noted, but clinical signs recurred, necessitating repeated treatments 3.5, 6, 18, and 24 months after the initial PAMNS. Over time, the dysuria and pollakiuria developed into stranguria and urinary incontinence and at 29 months after PAMNS urethroscopy revealed a urethral stricture extending from 30 cm to 40 cm proximal to the urethral orifice. The stricture was measured using endoscopy and percutaneous ultrasonography: 100 mm in length; 6.5 mm in diameter at the narrowest point. Treatments with eight balloon dilatations were performed over a 5 month period. Each balloon dilatation procedure was performed under standing sedation using a combination of acepromazine (Calmivet, Vetoquinol, France; 0.2 mg/kg, IM), detomidine hydrochloride (Detonervin, Animalcare, United Kingdom; 0.01 mg/kg, IV), and morphine sulfate (0.1 mg/kg, IV). A multipurpose stylet wire balloon dilator measuring 80 mm long and 20 mm wide (Hobbs Medical Inc, Connecticut, USA) was passed into urethra alongside the 5 mm video gastroscope (Olympus, Japan) until immediately proximal to the stricture. A splash block of 20 mL of lidocaine hydrochloride (Lidobell, Bella Pharm, Germany) and 20 mL of dexamethasone (Dexa‐Ject, Dopharma, the Netherlands) was instilled through a transendoscopic lavage catheter at the proximal end of the stricture, and endoscope was removed. Percutaneous ultrasonography was used to confirm the exact placement of the dilator. The balloon dilator was then inflated with water to its maximal diameter (20 mm) to a pressure of 4 atm and sustained for 60 seconds. These treatments resulted only in transient improvements. Based on the lack of long‐term improvement, it was elected to place a custom‐made absorbable polydioxanone stent using a minimally invasive, standing technique modified from that reported by Trela et al. 7

A preprocedural stent planning urethroscopy was performed using 5 mm diameter video gastroscope (Olympus, Japan), with a mechanical fluid pump ensuring maximal urethral dilatation (Arthrex Continuous Wave 4, Germany). The fluid pump was set at 25 mmHg with 100% flow, regulation of flow being provided manually through using a clip on the extension‐set line. Percutaneous ultrasonography combined with urethroscopy allowed the measurements of the approximate length of the stricture as well as location and width of its narrowest point (Figure 1). The absorbable polydioxanone self‐expanding stent (Infinity Medical, Redwood City, USA; Figure 2) was custom made (120 mm in length, 20 mm in width) according to these measurements.

Custom‐made sterile absorbable polydioxanone stent (120 mm long, 20 mm wide with 25 mm wide flares at both ends) and the delivery system including guide wire and introducer.

Prednisolone (Equisolon, Dechra, United Kingdom; 1 mg/kg, PO, q24h) was administered for 7 days before the stenting procedure. Three balloon dilatation procedures using the high pressure (30 ATM rated burst pressure, 10 mm diameter) ureteroscopic balloon dilatation catheter (X‐Force U30, BD, USA) were performed as described previously 24 and 48 hours apart to maximize the urethral diameter, break down any adhesions that might be present ahead of stent placement and allow the introduction of the stent delivery system (9 mm in diameter). The urethral stricture's narrowest point measured 6.5 mm in diameter and initially increased up to 7.2 mm with balloon dilator inflated to its maximum capacity (Figure 1).

The gelding was sedated with a combination of acepromazine (Calmivet, Vetoquinol, France; 0.2 mg/kg, IM), detomidine hydrochloride (Detonervin, Animalcare, United Kingdom; 0.01 mg/kg, IV), and morphine sulfate (0.1 mg/kg, IV). Sedation was maintained by variable rate infusion of detomidine hydrochloride to effect (0.16‐0.6 μg/mL). Perioperative oxytetracycline (Oxtra DD, Fatro S.p.A, Italy; 6.6 mg/kg, IV), flunixin meglumine (Flunixin Injection, Norbrook, United Kingdom; 1.1 mg/kg IV), and prednisolone (1 mg/kg, PO) were administered. Caudal epidural anesthesia was achieved using xylazine (Chanazine 2%, Chanelle Pharma, Ireland; 0.17 mg/kg) and mepivacaine hydrochloride (Mepidor, Animalcare, United Kingdom; 0.2 mg/kg). Pudendal nerve blocks were performed using mepivacaine hydrochloride. 16 The glans penis and external urethral orifice were aseptically prepared.

A splash block of 20 mL of lidocaine hydrochloride (Lidobell, Bella Pharm, Germany) was instilled through a transendoscopic lavage catheter at the proximal end of the stricture. The final balloon dilatation procedure was performed immediately before the urethral stent placement as described above, reaching a diameter of 9 mm at the narrowest point.

The stent was loaded into the delivery system just before implantation. The delivery system was advanced alongside the flexible endoscope and positioned under ultrasonographic and endoscopic guidance. The introducer catheter was then held in place with one hand while the sheath was slowly withdrawn in a continuous motion with the other hand. Urethroscopy confirmed correct placement of the stent with a full expansion throughout its whole length. Ultrasonography confirmed an average urethral diameter within the deployed stent of 19.7 mm. The horse recovered well from sedation and was able to void a normal stream of urine upon returning to the stall.

The gelding was maintained on prophylactic postoperative treatment of oxytetracycline (6.6 mg/kg, IV, q12h) and flunixin meglumine (1.1 mg/kg, IV, q12h) for 24 hours, then switched to doxycycline (Doxivet, Duggan Veterinary, Ireland; 10 mg/kg, PO, q12h) on discharge from the hospital for a further 3 days. Antimicrobial treatment was based on the history of sabulous cystitis and culture and sensitivity testing. Treatment with prednisolone (1 mg/kg, PO, q24h) was continued for 5 days and then reduced (0.5 mg/kg, PO, q24h for another 5 days and q48h for a further 5 days). The gelding remained comfortable, with normal voiding of urine over 24 h and was therefore discharged the next day.

The gelding was represented 14 days after the stent placement with hematuria and mild colic signs. Urethroscopy showed the stent well embedded in the mucosa with areas of moderate mucosal hypertrophy, hyperemia, and mild mucosal hemorrhage (Figure 3). The gelding was hospitalized for 2 days and received flunixin meglumine (1.1 mg/kg, IV, q24h). Treatment with prednisolone was increased up to 1 mg/kg PO q24h for 7 days, then 0.5 mg/kg PO q24h for a further 7 days. Clinical signs resolved completely.

Endoscopic appearance of the stent 14 days after implantation—mucosal hypertrophy covering the proximal part of the stent (A) and mucosal inflammation characterized by hyperemia and mild hemorrhage with fibrous strands at the narrowest point of the stricture (B).

Repeat urethroscopy at 4 weeks after the stent placement showed the stent partially covered by urethral epithelium. Focal urethral stenosis caused by mucosal hypertrophy at the proximal and distal end of the stent was observed. The stenotic areas were injected with triamcinolone acetonide (Adcortyl, Bristol Meyers Squibb, Ireland; 10 mg submucosal) transendoscopically. The urethral diameter at the narrowest point within the stent was 16.5 mm. Repeat urethroscopy and ultrasonography 6 months after the procedure showed the stent to have completely reabsorbed with a urethral diameter of 12.3 mm at the narrowest point (Figure 1). The surface of the mucosa was now smooth and regular in appearance with no hyperemia nor fibrinous tags. The gelding was able to void a full stream of urine without any clinical evidence of discomfort and was back to previous exercise levels. The horse remains asymptomatic and with an excellent quality of life 19 months after the procedure.

DISCUSSION

This case describes the successful placement of a custom‐made absorbable urethral stent in a standing, sedated horse with ultrasonographic and cystoscopic guidance. The gelding had suffered from severe PAMNS requiring urinary catheterization and leading to development of sabulous cystitis, urethral stenosis, and stricture. Urethral stricture formation is a complication infrequently documented with only a single equine case report of treatment using an absorbable stent. 7 The most common cause for the development of urethral strictures in small animals and horses is obstruction with uroliths or direct epithelial damage from urethral catheterization, as it is suspected to be the case in this report. 17

Sabulous urolithiasis is defined as an accumulation of urine sediment causing secondary inflammation in the urinary bladder and is usually a consequence of urinary incontinence associated with neurologic dysfunction. 18 , 19 In this case, sabulous cystitis was managed for 3 years with a combination of repeat cystoscopy and bladder lavage, antimicrobial, and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (NSAID) treatment, which are the primary management options for this condition; however, they only resulted in short‐term clinical improvement. 4 , 18 Twenty‐four months after the initial presentation the gelding displayed urinary incontinence and pollakiuria, which were caused by urethral stricture. These clinical signs resolved immediately after the placement of the stent which raises a question whether the sabulous cystitis in this case was partially driven from the start by the urethral inflammation, which occurred because of repeat bladder catheterization and might have resulted in constant incomplete emptying of the bladder. A combination of recurrent dysuria, urethral inflammation, and repeat urethroscopy could have aggravated both the development of urethral stenosis and sabulous cystitis.

A range of treatment options for urethral strictures have been reported in small and large animals including resection, urethrostomy, balloon dilatation, and stenting. 11 , 12 , 20 Balloon dilatation has been described as a 1st‐choice treatment option to manage urethral strictures in small animals with successful resolution of clinical signs from 18 to 22 months. 19 , 21 Radial force applied to the stricture site by the balloon dilator minimizes mucosal injury and decreases the risk of stricture recurrence. 10 , 21 A similar balloon dilatation technique has been reported to treat a urethral stricture in a stallion that had been refractory to urinary catheterization and the described balloon dilatations. 7 Unlike the reported case, this gelding was managed using balloon dilatation for a period of 5 months, although increased frequency of recurrence of clinical signs and persistent stenosis observed on each urethroscopy indicated this treatment option was failing, and it was discontinued.

Urinary stenting has been more frequently reported in recent years in veterinary species with polymeric‐made stents most commonly used. 22 Polydioxanone biodegradable self‐expanding stents have been used successfully in a porcine biliary tree stricture model, rabbit airway model and canine urethral model providing luminal opening for 12 weeks and causing only mild inflammatory reaction and minimal scar tissue formation. 23 , 24 , 25 Placement of a polydioxanone urethral stent has been described in a horse providing luminal patency with the stent being undetectable by ultrasonography 155 days after implantation. 7 The polydioxanone stent used for this case was custom made to fit the measurements of the stricture and the urethra. The diameter of the stent was chosen to be around 10% to 15% greater than the normal diameter of distal urethra in a horse and the length was designed to be 20 mm longer than the stricture to allow extension of 10 mm beyond the cranial and caudal borders of the stricture to reduce the risk of stent migration and further damage to the urethral mucosa. 14 , 15

The ordering, manufacturing, and shipment of the stent was a large financial commitment for the client. Had the stenting procedure been undertaken earlier, the client could have saved money that was spent on ultimately unsuccessful treatments, in addition to the horse being spared a more prolonged period of discomfort. Considering the immediate and complete resolution of clinical signs after the stenting procedure, we believe that absorbable urethral stent placement could be considered as a treatment option in early stages of urethral stricture.

In the previous report, the procedure was performed under general anesthesia, but for this case, it was decided to attempt the procedure standing. Considering the gelding's weight (720 kg), and previous history of severe PAMNS leading to the present situation, general anesthesia seemed counter intuitive. The gelding has been under the consistent care of the same specialist veterinary team who had performed multiple transurethral endoscopic procedures, which were extremely well tolerated under sedation. While standing sedation reduces the risks of morbidity and mortality as well as deleterious effects of recumbency on tissue perfusion, it comes with its own risks of inducing ataxia, causing the horse to fall or causing hypersensitivity to stimulation in superficial plane of sedation. Appropriate patient selection is key to a successful standing procedure; therefore, stressed, anxious horses who resist manual restraint should be excluded. 26 , 27

On examination 4 weeks after the stent placement, mucosal hypertrophy was observed at the proximal and distal end of the stent causing mild focal stenosis. Submucosal triamcinolone injections were performed using a transendoscopic needle. Local triamcinolone injections were described in dogs and cats with benign esophageal strictures after balloon dilatation and stent placement resulting in good outcomes, albeit with no determination whether the injections affected the outcomes. 28 Submucosal triamcinolone injections are widely reported in human medicine as an adjunctive treatment for urethral strictures after an internal urethrotomy. 29 In this case, corticosteroid injections were used to manage mucosal inflammatory reaction seen after the stent placement and resulted in reduction of the focal mucosal hypertrophy. As these injections were done after the stent placement, the specific effect of corticosteroids on the treatment of urethral strictures cannot be determined.

In conclusion, this procedure was technically feasible using a combination of percutaneous ultrasonography and urethroscopy. Placement of an absorbable urethral stent is a viable minimally invasive treatment option for urethral strictures in the standing male horse and should be considered in cases refractory to other treatment options. The horse remains asymptomatic and back to previous athletic level 19 months after the procedure.
