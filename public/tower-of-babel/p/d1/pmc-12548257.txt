Predicting 3-year depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older adults in rural China using random forest: insights from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: LiHan Lin, XiaoYang Liu, Delong Li, YiKun Zheng, YiPing Liu, GuoPeng Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnder China’s dual economic structure of urban and rural areas, rural regions face issues such as low socioeconomic status, inadequate healthcare resources, and neglect of mental health, leading to a higher prevalence of depression among middle-aged and older adults (above 45 years) in this area.MethodsThis prospective cohort study used data from 6,183 rural Chinese middle-aged and older adults in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2018–2020). A random forest model was developed to predict 3-year incidents of depressive symptoms. Independent risk factors were identified via chi-square tests followed by binary logistic regression (Odds Ratios [ORs] and 95% Confidence Intervals [CIs] reported for significant variables, p < 0.05). The model’s performance and clinical utility were assessed using standard metrics and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values determine the individual feature impact on predictions. A subgroup analysis also compared depression-related characteristics in middle-aged (45–59 years) versus older adults (≥ 60 years) with incident depressive symptoms.ResultsOver a 3-year follow-up, 1,629 (26.35%) participants developed incident depressive symptoms. A Random Forest model, optimized using Recursive Feature Elimination (RF-RFE), which selected 28 key predictors from an initial 33. After threshold adjustment (optimal threshold = 0.43) to maximize the F1-score, the model achieved an accuracy of 0.736, precision of 0.499, recall of 0.607, F1-score of 0.548, and an AUC of 0.776 (95% CI: 0.763–0.788). The mean Brier score was 0.163 ± 0.006. DCA confirmed its clinical utility. Key protective factors identified via logistic regression included being male, higher education, and internet access. Conversely, increased age, poor self-rated health, lower life satisfaction, and functional limitations were significant risk factors for incident depressive symptoms.ConclusionThe random forest model demonstrates moderate predictive ability to estimate the risk of depressive symptoms in individuals aged 45 and above in rural China over the next 3 years. It offers a potentially valuable screening tool for rural regions with low mental health awareness and high depression prevalence, enabling more targeted interventions and prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03513-2.

Introduction

Depression is a severe mood disorder that significantly impairs individuals’ physical and mental health, daily functioning, and quality of life, and contributes to increased mortality due to physical illness [1, 2]. Depressive symptoms, such as low mood, insomnia, and fatigue, represent subclinical manifestations of depression [3]. Patients with major depressive disorder face a lifelong risk of suicide [4]. Globally, depression has emerged as a major public health challenge, with a growing lifetime prevalence [5, 6]. As of 2017, over 300 million people worldwide were affected [7], and the World Health Organization has identified depression as the leading cause of disability [8]. According to the Global Burden of Disease study, mental disorders including depression are among the top contributors to the global disease burden [9]. In China, depression is also a leading cause of suicide [10] and is influenced by various factors such as demographic characteristics, health status, lifestyle, family environment, and social security [11–14].

Notably, China’s pronounced urban–rural divide contributes to substantial disparities in socioeconomic conditions, public services, and healthcare access [15–17]. These structural imbalances have been linked to a significantly higher prevalence of depression among middle-aged and older adults in rural areas compared to their urban counterparts [18, 19]. Beyond economic and healthcare inequalities, rural populations also face compounding structural and contextual challenges [20–22]. Social barriers—including limited mental health awareness, stigma, and inadequate access to professional care—further hinder early recognition and treatment of depressive symptoms. At the same time, demographic shifts such as population aging and declining fertility have reduced the resilience of rural communities. Additionally, large-scale rural-to-urban migration has led to the fragmentation of traditional family structures, while rapid urbanization continues to concentrate resources and services in cities. These intersecting factors collectively erode social support networks and increase the psychological vulnerability of rural older adults. A nationwide cross-sectional survey conducted in 2018 revealed that the prevalence of depressive symptoms among rural elderly aged 45 and above in China was 34.8%, significantly higher than the 22.3% observed among their urban counterparts [23].

Therefore, early identification and intervention for depressive symptoms in economically underdeveloped rural areas of China are essential to prevent their progression into clinical depression and to reduce the overall disease burden. However, there is currently a lack of effective tools to screen individuals who are at risk of developing depressive symptoms in the future. Developing a simple, rapid, and accessible risk assessment method for identifying high-risk individuals is thus critical for advancing depression prevention and management efforts in rural Chinese communities.

In recent years, the rapid progress of artificial intelligence technologies has underscored the growing value of machine learning (ML) in healthcare [24–27]. ML models—especially Random Forest (RF)—are well-suited for handling complex, non-linear relationships, high-dimensional and imbalanced data, and missing values, while also ranking variable importance automatically [28, 29]. Moreover, they are efficient, scalable, and low-cost, making Random-Forest-based screening particularly attractive in resource-constrained rural settings where conventional psychiatric assessment is often impractical. Traditional diagnosis of depression typically relies on one-on-one clinical interviews conducted by trained specialists, sometimes supplemented by standardized rating scales or laboratory tests [30, 31]. However, these procedures require professional expertise and diagnostic infrastructure that are often lacking in rural regions. In addition, such assessments are usually retrospective or cross-sectional, identifying individuals already exhibiting depressive symptoms. They are not designed to predict future risks, which limits their utility for proactive intervention and prevention in vulnerable populations. Internationally, many depression-prediction studies rely on electronic health records or clinician interviews [32–34]; although effective, such inputs are costly and difficult to obtain outside well-resourced systems. By contrast, our approach uses structured, easy-to-collect questionnaire data, offering a practical and community-adaptable alternative for identifying rural older adults at risk of future depressive symptoms.

Research on predictive models for depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older adults in rural China remains limited. To address this gap, the present study utilized data from the 2018–2020 wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [35], focusing specifically on rural populations aged 45 and above. An RF model was developed to predict the 3-year risk of depressive symptoms in this population. The model is intended to facilitate early identification and enable personalized prevention strategies for high-risk individuals, thereby helping to reduce the incidence of depression and lessen its economic and health burdens in rural China.

Materials and methods

Data source

CHARLS, conducted by the National School of Development at Peking University, is a comprehensive interdisciplinary survey project. The survey covers 150 counties and 450 communities (villages) across 28 provinces, gathering longitudinal data from a nationally representative sample of individuals over 45 years old through face-to-face household interviews. These data encompass various dimensions, including socioeconomic status and health conditions, providing a robust foundation for research in aging science. This study was carried out based on data extracted from the CHARLS public database, and all methods were performed according to the relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal agents before the commencement of any study process. The ethics approval for the collection of CHARLS data has been approved by the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Review Committee (IRB00001052-11015). Detailed descriptions, including the sampling procedures, questionnaire and raw data can be accessed at https://charls.pku.edu.cn and the supporting information (S1 and S2).

Study population

This study utilized data from the CHARLS collected between 2018 and 2020. Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) 554 participants who were younger than 45 years or missing age and gender data; (2) 5,470 participants who lived in urban areas; (3) 6,218 participants who with depressive symptoms at the baseline or had missing baseline CESD-10 data; (4) 1,391 participants missing CESD-10 data in the 2020 follow-up. After applying these criteria, a total of 6,183 rural middle-aged and older adults were included in the final analysis. The detailed participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Research variable

Outcome variable

The diagnosis of depressive symptoms was defined using the CESD-10 scale [36] in CHARLS 2020, which is known for its strong reliability and validity, particularly in assessing depression symptoms in older populations [37–39]. The scale comprises 10 items and employs a Likert4 scoring method. Possible total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores reflecting greater severity of depressive symptoms and a cut-off score of 10 indicating screening positive for depression [40].

Candidate predictor variables

Guided by prior literature, the structure of the available dataset, and the specific sociocultural and healthcare context of rural China, a total of 33 baseline variables from the 2018 wave of the CHARLS survey were selected as candidate predictor variables. These variables were chosen for their simplicity, feasibility of collection in low-resource settings, and theoretical relevance to depressive symptoms. The variables were grouped into five conceptual domains: socio-demographic variable, behavioral variable, health status, psychological factors, and home environment. All variables were assigned as detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Socio-demographic variables included age, gender, education level, marital status, and work status. Education was categorized into four levels: below primary school, primary school, junior high school, senior high school, and above. Marital status was grouped as married/partnered and divorced/widowed/never married/others. Work status distinguishes currently working from retired.

Behavioral variables included current smoking status, drinking (alcohol consumption) habits, exercise habits (physical activity), sleep duration, and number of social activities. Physical activity (exercise habits) was self-reported. Sleep duration was the average hours of sleep per night. Social activity was based on the number of distinct activities participated in.

Health status variables included self-reported disability, hearing function, memory problems, pain, number of chronic diseases, experience of falling, functional limitations, and use of auxiliary devices. Disability, pain, experience of falling, and use of auxiliary devices were recorded as binary variables (yes/no). Hearing and memory were based on self-rated function. The number of chronic diseases was derived from self-reported physician diagnoses. Functional limitations were measured using activities of daily living (ADLQ) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) questionnaires.

Psychological variables included life satisfaction and self-report of health, categorized as “good,” “fair,” and “bad.”

Home environment variables assessed physical infrastructure and living conditions. These included: residence used for business; structure of building; type of building; house year (year of construction); access to running water; access to coal gas or natural gas supply; availability of telephone and internet services; presence of an air cleaner; as well as indicators of household tidiness and house temperature conditions.

Random forest model

To address the class imbalance in the dataset, where only 26.3% of participants exhibited depressive symptoms after three years, Adaptive Synthetic Sampling (ADASYN) was applied to generate additional synthetic depressive symptoms samples [41, 42]. This technique generates synthetic instances for the minority class by focusing on samples that are harder to classify, thereby improving class balance and model robustness (see S5 Methods supplement for details). The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). ADASYN oversampling was applied only to the training set to avoid information leakage, while the test set retained its original class distribution to ensure an unbiased evaluation of model generalizability.

To identify the most informative predictors, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was applied using a Random Forest algorithm (RF-RFE) [43], which ranks variables based on their importance scores and iteratively removes the least informative ones to obtain an optimal subset. Compared to regularization-based methods such as LASSO, RF-RFE is more compatible with tree-based algorithms like Random Forest, making it a more suitable feature selection approach for this study [44, 45].

The selected features were used for all subsequent model development, hyperparameter tuning, and validation. Hyperparameter tuning for the random forest classifier was conducted using grid search within a nested cross-validation framework, with grid search in the inner loop and model performance evaluated in the outer loop using 10-fold stratified cross-validation on the entire dataset. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used as the primary optimization criterion [46]. Optimism-corrected performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUC, Brier score, and calibration slope, were reported as mean ± standard deviation across the outer folds and compared at multiple representative decision thresholds. The optimal threshold for classification was determined by maximizing the F1-score within the cross-validation framework. Recall was used as a secondary metric to prioritize the identification of high-risk rural middle-aged and older adults, given the importance of minimizing missed cases in depression screening for this population. ROC curves and calibration plots were generated to assess model discrimination and calibration, respectively. Additionally, interpretation. To facilitate clinical interpretation, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were estimated at each threshold under varying prevalence scenarios to facilitate clinical interpretation.

To assess the clinical utility of the predictive model and account for the potential harms of false positives and false negatives, we conducted Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) [47]. This method estimates net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, allowing comparison against default strategies such as “treat-all” or “treat-none.” Finally, to enhance the interpretability of model predictions, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were calculated based on the training set [48, 49]. Rooted in cooperative game theory, SHAP values quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to the prediction, offering detailed insights into how individual predictors influence the risk of depressive symptoms in rural middle-aged and older adults.

Statistical analysis

This study employed SPSS 26.0 and Python 3.9 for data processing and analysis. Missing values in predictor variables were imputed using MissForest [50], a random forest-based method suitable for mixed-type data and capable of capturing nonlinear relationships. To avoid potential data leakage, imputation was performed strictly within each training fold during cross-validation, ensuring that no information from the validation fold was used during model training or preprocessing. This approach improves robustness compared to mean or mode imputation. Details of missingness are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Chi-square tests were employed as univariate analyses to compare the frequency distributions of categorical variables between participants with and without incident depressive symptoms. Variables with p < 0.05 were subsequently included in the binary logistic regression model to identify independent predictors. Model fit was evaluated using Nagelkerke’s pseudo R², which provides an index of explanatory power for the fitted model. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported to quantify the strength and direction of these associations. To investigate potential age-related differences in depressive symptom risk across key life stages, participants were categorized into two age groups: 45–59 years (middle-aged) and ≥ 60 years (older adults). The definition followed World Health Organization criteria, as in previous studies [51, 52]. Logistic regression analyses were then conducted separately for each age group to assess their risk of depressive symptoms, and these group-specific risks were subsequently compared.

Result

Baseline characteristics

Among the 6,183 participants included in the final analysis, 1,629 (26.35%) developed incident depressive symptoms by follow-up. Compared to those without depressive symptoms, the incident group had a higher proportion of females (56.72% vs. 40.69%), participants aged ≥ 65 years (41.37% vs. 31.42%), widowed individuals (10.74% vs. 7.27%), and those with lower education levels. They were also more likely to retire, sleep less than 6 h, and report poor self-rated health, lower life satisfaction, impaired memory, limitations in ADL and IADL, multiple chronic conditions, pain, and hearing problems. Additionally, fewer participants with depressive symptoms had internet access (35.05% vs. 49.28%), engaged in more than two social activities, or lived in tidy households. Except for exercise habits, disability status, building type, and electricity access, most baseline characteristics showed statistically significant associations with the onset of depressive symptoms (P < 0.05).

Key factors associated with incident depressive symptoms

Variables that showed a statistically significant association with incident depressive symptoms (p < 0.05) in the univariate chi-square tests were included as candidate predictors in a binary logistic regression model using backward stepwise selection. In the final model, only variables that remained statistically significant (p < 0.05) are presented in Table 2.

Results from the binary logistic regression analysis identified several key predictors of incident depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older adults in rural China. Compared with females, males had a significantly lower risk (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.53–0.69, p < 0.01). Increasing age was associated with elevated risk, particularly among those aged ≥ 75 years (OR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.15–1.90, p < 0.01). Higher education was protective: participants with senior high school education and above had significantly reduced odds compared to those with less than primary school education (OR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.44–0.75, p < 0.01).

Sleep duration of 6–8 h or > 8 h was associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms compared to < 6 h (OR = 0.74 and 0.63, respectively, both p < 0.01). Poor self-rated health, lower life satisfaction, impaired memory, and functional limitations in ADL and IADL were significantly associated with increased risk. Notably, participants with poor life satisfaction had over twice the odds of incident depressive symptoms (OR = 2.46, 95% CI: 1.86–3.25, p < 0.01) compared to those reporting good satisfaction. Pain, a history of falling, and poor hearing also emerged as significant risk factors. In contrast, internet access (OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.62–0.80, p < 0.01) and the availability of coal or natural gas for household use (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.67–0.99, p = 0.04) were associated with reduced odds of developing depressive symptoms. Model fit was moderate (Nagelkerke’s R² = 0.259).

Comparative analysis of risk factors for depressive symptoms by age group

The age-stratified analysis of 6,183 participants demonstrated a higher three-year incidence of new depressive symptoms in older adults (≥ 60 years: 30.4%, 983/3,238) compared to middle-aged individuals (45–59 years: 21.9%, 645/2,945). Descriptive statistics (Supplemental Table S2 and S3) indicated numerous baseline differences between those who developed depressive symptoms and those who did not. Among older adults, 28 of 32 examined characteristics differed significantly between these groups, compared to 22 of 32 characteristics among middle-aged individuals (P<0.05).

Multivariable logistic regression analyses identified independent risk factors for incident depressive symptoms in middle-aged (Table 3) and older adult (Table 4) cohorts. Both age groups shared several significant risk factors for depressive symptoms. These included being female, lower educational attainment, short sleep duration, poor self-rated, low life satisfaction, experiencing pain, and lack of internet access. Distinct risk factors also emerged. For older adults, difficulties with ADLQ (OR = 1.43) and IADLQ (OR = 1.33), untidy living conditions (protective OR for tidy = 0.83), and lacking gas supply (protective OR for supply = 0.75) were significant. For middle-aged adults, self-reported bad memory (OR = 1.60) and experiencing a fall (OR = 1.41) were uniquely significant predictors. Model fit was moderate across strata (Nagelkerke’s R² = 0.254 in middle-aged adults; 0.266 in older adults.

Feature selection

RF-RFE is a feature selection technique that iteratively removes the least important features based on their importance scores, identifying a subset that optimizes predictive performance. In this study, RF-RFE was applied to the initial set of 33 candidate predictors to determine the most informative subset of variables. As shown in Fig. 2, the model achieved the highest AUC when 28 variables were retained, indicating optimal model performance. Figure 3 presents the important scores of these 28 selected features, including Age, House Year, Education Level, Pain, Exercise Habits, Number of Social Activities, Self-reported Health, Hearing, Number of Chronic Diseases, Life Satisfaction, Sleep Duration, and Memory. Additional influential predictors include Drinking Status, Type of Building, Tidiness, Running Water, House Temperature, Internet Access, Gender, Work Status, Telephone Ownership, Experience of Falling, Current Smoking Status, IADLQ, Structure of Building, Marital Status, Coal or Gas Supply, and ADLQ. These features collectively contributed the most to the model‘s predictive capability, reflecting their relevance in identifying individuals at risk for incident depressive symptoms.

Model discrimination and calibration

The random forest model achieved good overall discrimination, with a mean AUC of 0.776 (95% CI: 0.763–0.788) based on 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 4). Calibration analysis showed that the predicted probabilities closely matched observed event rates, with a calibration slope of 1.06 (Fig. 5), indicating well-calibrated risk estimates. Explanations of feature importance

To understand the contributions of individual features to the prediction of depressive symptoms, a SHAP analysis was performed on the random forest model, examining the 20 most influential predictors.

The global importance of these features, as determined by their mean absolute SHAP values, is presented in Fig. 7A. This analysis revealed that ‘Gender,’ ‘Pain,’ ‘Internet,’ ‘Self-report of health,’ and ‘Sleep duration’ were the top five contributors to the model’s predictive performance, indicating their substantial overall impact on distinguishing between individuals with and without depressive symptoms.

Further insights into the directionality and magnitude of these influences at the individual prediction level are provided by the SHAP summary plot (Fig. 7B). Being female and ‘Bad’ reported of Pain were consistently associated with increased SHAP values, contributing to a higher predicted risk of depression. Conversely, greater access to ‘Internet,’ more favorable ‘Self-report of health’ status, longer ‘Sleep duration,’ and higher ‘Education level’ generally corresponded to decreased SHAP values, thereby lowering the predicted risk of depression. The older ‘Age’ variable also consistently contributed positively to the SHAP values, indicating its association with an elevated predicted risk. The distributions also highlight that for features like ‘Life satisfaction’ and ‘Number of chronic diseases,’ a clear separation exists where lower satisfaction and a higher number of diseases push SHAP values towards predicting higher depression risk.

These SHAP findings collectively delineate the key drivers of depressive symptom prediction in the model, quantifying both the overall importance of each feature and the specific impact of their varying levels on individual risk assessment.

Discussion

This study explored the potential of a random forest algorithm to develop a 3-year risk prediction model for incident depressive symptoms among middle-aged and elderly individuals in rural China. From a cohort of 6,183 rural participants surveyed between 2018 and 2020, 1,629 (26.35%) developed depressive symptoms over the 3-year follow-up. After adjusting the decision threshold to optimize the F1-score (optimal threshold = 0.43), our predictive model achieved an accuracy of 0.736, precision of 0.499, recall of 0.607, F1-score of 0.548, and an AUC of 0.776 (95% CI: 0.763–0.788). The mean Brier score was 0.163 ± 0.006, indicating good overall calibration. DCA further demonstrated a clear net clinical benefit across a relevant range of threshold probabilities, supporting the model’s utility for identifying individuals at higher risk. Key predictors associated with a lower risk of developing depressive symptoms included being male, having higher education levels, optimal sleep duration (6–8 h or > 8 h vs. <6 h), internet access, and having coal/natural gas supply. Conversely, increased age, poor self-rated health, lower life satisfaction, impaired memory, functional limitations (ADL/IADL), pain, a history of falling, and poor hearing were significant risk factors for incident depressive symptoms in this rural Chinese population.

Prior to this study, numerous researchers have used machine learning to predict the risk of depressive symptoms in specific populations. For instance, Wei [53] predicted depression risk among Chinese epilepsy patients using ML, achieving an AUC of 0.86. Zhang [54] predicted depression in pregnant women with an AUC of 0.88, while Ali [55] predicted depression in menopausal women with an accuracy of 97.11%. Stephania [56] predicted depressive symptoms in the elderly, with an accuracy and precision of 87%. These studies often employed a wide array of comprehensive predictive indicators, including detailed socio-demographic variables, extensive psychological health histories, specific medical comorbidities, medication prescriptions, and granular clinical features, likely contributing to their reported high predictive accuracies (e.g., AUCs typically exceeding 0.85).

However, direct comparisons between these models and our own model (AUC = 0.779) require careful consideration of several underlying factors. The seemingly higher performance in some of these studies, such as Wei et al. [53], may be primarily attributable to key differences in their target populations (e.g., specific patient groups like those with epilepsy, who may present a more homogeneous risk profile or distinct predictors) and the inherent nature of the features utilized. Studies focusing on clinical cohorts, for instance, often have access to more detailed medical records and biomarkers not readily available in broad community-based settings, particularly in resource-limited rural areas.

In contrast, our study consciously navigated a trade-off between maximizing predictive accuracy and ensuring practical applicability and interpretability within rural Chinese settings, characterized by limited economic development and scarce medical resources. Our model purposefully selected 28 predictive indicators that are readily obtainable through face-to-face interviews and validated scales, thereby minimizing the burden on both healthcare systems and participants. This approach aligns with other machine learning studies that have developed predictive models for health outcomes based primarily on questionnaire data or similarly accessible features, which have reported AUCs generally ranging from approximately 0.72 to 0.84 [27, 57–59]. While this emphasis on readily accessible data may contribute to a slightly more modest AUC compared to studies with more extensive and complex feature sets, it underscores the model’s vital utility for early screening and intervention in underserved populations. Furthermore, variations in sample size, the prevalence of depressive symptoms, and the specific definitions of outcomes within different study cohorts can also influence reported model performance. Notably, our results demonstrate that lowering the classification threshold to maximize recall substantially increases the number of false positives, as reflected by the decrease in precision and PPV across thresholds. In rural primary-care settings, this trade-off may be acceptable, as maximizing case detection is often prioritized to avoid missing high-risk individuals, particularly when follow-up resources for additional assessment are available. However, the increased burden from false positives should be carefully balanced against local healthcare capacity and the potential consequences of both false positives and false negatives.

While public health initiatives support China’s rural elderly, persistent depressive symptoms suggest current interventions may inadequately address specific local risk factors. Our logistic regression analysis (Table 2) identified several key modifiable risk factors for incident depressive symptoms in this rural population, highlighting these gaps and guiding our proposed data-driven, phased intervention strategy to bolster existing efforts.

For instance, our findings align with broader epidemiological evidence indicating a higher prevalence of depression in women compared to men [60], potentially linked to psychosocial factors such as role stress and lower social status, particularly during reproductive and later life stages [61]. Similarly, the observed increase in depressive symptoms with age in rural areas [62, 63], as seen in our cohort, may be attributable to declining physiological functions, reduced psychological resilience, and associated lifestyle inconveniences. Furthermore, consistent with previous research [64, 65], our study identified lower education levels as a significant risk factor. Lower educational attainment in rural middle-aged and elderly individuals is often intertwined with poorer economic conditions, diminished social support, and limited access to health information, hindering effective coping strategies against life stressors and health issues. Factors such as lack of internet use in our study reflect broader challenges in social engagement. Indeed, multiple studies confirm that regular social interaction are crucial protective factors against depression [66–68], and our data similarly suggests a link between increased social activities, and a lower likelihood of depressive symptoms.

Crucially, our logistic regression highlighted that poorer self-reported health, the presence of chronic illnesses, disabilities (ADLQ, IADLQ), hearing impairments, prevalent pain, and experiences of falling are strongly associated with an increased risk of depressive symptoms. This is consistent with extensive literature confirming health status as a critical predictor [69–71]. Declining health often leads to a cascade of negative outcomes including a lower quality of life, functional limitations, increased daily difficulties, and heightened psychological distress, fostering feelings of helplessness and despair [72, 73].

Furthermore, our age-stratified logistic regression analyses (Tables 3 and 4) underscore the necessity of tailoring psychological interventions to specific age groups. For older adults (≥ 60 years), interventions should particularly focus on mitigating the psychological impact of functional limitations (difficulties with ADLQ and IADLQ) and improving living conditions (e.g., tidiness, access to essential utilities like gas supply). Addressing these factors may involve enhancing home care support, providing assistive devices, and community initiatives to improve environmental safety and comfort. In contrast, for middle-aged individuals (45–59 years), psychological interventions might more effectively target concerns related to cognitive health (such as addressing bad memory, potentially through cognitive training or early screening) and fall prevention strategies. Recognizing these age-specific vulnerabilities allows for more targeted and potentially more effective mental health support within the rural Chinese population.

These complex interplays between socio-demographic factors, lifestyle, health status, and mental well-being, particularly pronounced in resource-limited rural settings, underscore the need for targeted and multifaceted interventions. Therefore, our proposed data-driven, phased, and regionally differentiated intervention strategy aims to address these identified vulnerabilities: Phase one of our strategy aligns with the national Healthy China initiative, which has already been implemented in relatively affluent rural areas of southeastern China. This phase focuses on community-based health literacy programs (targeting individuals with lower educational attainment), digital inclusion efforts (addressing lack of internet access), and the promotion of chronic disease self-management, pain and fall prevention, and hearing health awareness. Phase two aims to expand these efforts by improving access to primary care, advocating for better infrastructure (e.g., household gas supply), and strengthening social support networks to enhance life satisfaction and reduce functional limitations. This approach is designed to align with the diverse resource landscapes of rural China and to inform practical enhancements to current public health initiatives.

However, this study has several limitations. First, for practical applicability in resource-limited rural settings, our model pragmatically excluded detailed clinical symptoms and physiological indicators. Although highly relevant to depression [74–76], their collection within the rural healthcare contexts would impose an undue burden. Consequently, while highly accessible, our model’s diagnostic depth may be limited by the absence of such clinically nuanced data. Second, the use of self-reported data may introduce recall bias and social desirability bias, leading to underreporting of stigmatized behaviors or undiagnosed conditions. For instance, the number of chronic diseases may be underestimated, potentially affecting the accuracy of predictor variables and model performance. Third, depression severity was not assessed, limiting the model’s ability to differentiate levels of symptom burden. Future studies should incorporate clinically validated data and symptom severity to improve prediction and risk stratification. Fourth, our model was developed and validated solely within the CHARLS cohort. Consequently, external validation in independent rural cohorts, ideally from diverse geographical regions within China or later CHARLS waves, is essential to confirm its generalizability and robustness before wider application. Future research should also investigate region-specific risk factors to develop tailored predictive models for depressive symptoms across different rural Chinese localities, accounting for potential socio-cultural and economic variations.

Conclusions

The prediction model developed in this study effectively assesses the risk of depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older adults in rural China over 3 years. Utilizing the CHARLS data and the Random Forest algorithm, the model demonstrated strong predictive performance. Key factors influencing depressive symptoms include age, education level, sleep duration, social activities, chronic diseases, self-reported health, exercise habits, life satisfaction, hearing difficulties and disability. This model provides a valuable tool for targeted interventions, aiming to reduce depression prevalence and alleviate the associated economic and health burdens in rural Chinese people​​.
