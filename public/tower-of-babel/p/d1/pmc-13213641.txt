Message framing, identity congruence, and polio vaccine acceptance in Pakistan: Testing a social identity-based communication framework
Authors: Shahbaz Aslam, Sobia Abid, Arshad Ali, Muhammad Zahid Bilal, Babar Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transition from traditional biomedical-based vaccination campaigns in Pakistan has faced many difficulties. This paper proposes that the central problem is not the content of vaccine-related messages per se, but whether their targets perceive those messages as aligned with their identities. The objective of this study is to examine the effects of message framing and perceived identity threat on polio intention to vaccinate through the theoretical perspectives of Framing Theory and Social Identity Theory (SIT). A survey sample of 600 adults in Sindh and Balochistan, two regions understudied in empirical research on this topic, was employed. Hierarchical multiple regression and moderation analyses were used to test the hypotheses. Collective identity framing was the most significant positive predictor of intention to vaccinate (β = .41, p < .001). Identity threat, the perception that an anti-polio message contradicts the respondents' religious or cultural identity, was the strongest negative predictor (β = −.45, p < .001). Peer-norm salience significantly mediated the link between framing uptake and collective framing among community-oriented respondents. Critically, religious commitment was non-significant once identity threat was controlled, demonstrating that identity threat, not religiosity, is the proximal driver of refusal. The Identity-Anchored Message Uptake (IAMU) Model is proposed based on these findings.Keywords: Vaccine hesitancy, Message framing, Social identity theory, Identity congruence, Polio, Pakistan

Introduction

The last two polio-endemic nations in the world are Pakistan and Afghanistan.1 The Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI) has invested substantial resources in door-to-door visits, publicity campaigns, and religious leaders2 in an effort to eradicate the disease, but the virus keeps spreading, particularly in Sindh, Balochistan, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. This can't be attributed to logistics. The opposition towards vaccines is so firmly rooted in the sense of identity and communal belonging that it cannot be solved by studying parents' attitudes, but needs to be explored through the lens of identity and communal feeling. While the GPEI's Social and Behavior Change (SBC) strategy in Pakistan focuses on mobilizing communities, creating demand, and using interpersonal communication such as Lady Health Workers and local volunteers, vaccine hesitancy remains in endemic areas and is not limited to technical or logistical issues.2, 3

Previous empirical quantitative research has employed theories such as the Health Belief Model (HBM) or the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), where individual risk-benefit perceptions are the basis for hesitancy. These models are helpful but do not take into account the process of decision-making within socially constructed contexts and do not recognize religious and cultural elements as the communicational context where health messages must operate. Recently, Aslam, Hussain et al. (2026) qualitatively examined the role of faith, culture, and family in polio communication in Pakistan, bringing forward the concept of ‘Faith-Family-Frontline' in Pakistan. The insights are substantial, but there is no quantitative assessment of the underlying mechanisms.

We argue that the content of a message is not the only factor that is important in vaccine communication; whether the message is interpreted as being congruent with the receiver's identity is equally important 4.5; Health messages that evoke a sense of outsider, by positioning vaccination as a requirement outside rather than an Islamic obligation or community norm, the ummah (World Muslim Community), evoke a sense of threat to identity, which is the immediate reason for not vaccinating rather than religiosity itself. The study is carried out in Sindh and Balochistan. Five hypotheses about the effect of message framing, identity congruence, identity threat, and norm salience on vaccine uptake are tested, and the Identity-Anchored Message Uptake (IAMU) Model is proposed as a practical tool for GPEI stakeholders.

Literature review

Message framing and health behavior

Framing theory holds that the way information is communicated affects the meaning that audiences understand, as does its content 4. Gain frames highlight the benefits of action; loss frames highlight the cost of inaction, but neither type of frame outperforms the other because effectiveness depends on the values of the target group.6 When appealing to the individual, one might think about appealing to communal duty appeals, such as protecting the ummah, fulfilling parental obligation, which may be more convincing for Muslim-majority, collectivist audiences.7 However, the public-health campaigns usually employ biomedical and individualist frames that sometimes appear alien to the target communities.

Social Identity Theory and health communication

According to Social Identity Theory,5 an important component of a person's self-concept is from group memberships. Consistent messages with valued social identities (religious, ethnic, familial) are more likely to be accepted. However, when a message threatens identity (identity threat), psychological reactance and refusal ensue even if the information is correct 8. The fear of Western interference in Muslim communities is a form of identity threat in Pakistan, where the polio campaign is viewed as such. Importantly, SIT also anticipates that identity threat, rather than the content of the underlying beliefs, is the more proximal driver of rejection.

Peer norms and collective decision-making

Individual health decisions are influenced by social norms, especially those reinforced by in-group leaders like imams, elders, and female kinship groups, in high-collectivism contexts 9. In turn, peer norm salience (how much community norms are intentional in decision making) is theorized as a moderator in the framing effect: collective frames are especially effective when there is high peer norm salience.10,11 In Bangladesh and Indonesia, there is evidence of such moderation,12,13 but not quantitative testing of such moderation in Pakistan.

Message framing for polio: evidence from the field

Evidence specifically addressing framing effects in polio campaigns is instructive. In a study that documented the effects of biomedical risk framing on community resistance to vaccination campaigns, Soofi et al.14 and Jin et al.15 found that campaigns that are perceived as foreign-led lead to suspicion, rather than compliance. However, in vaccine communication against COVID-19, cross-national evidence also showed that messages focused on collective duty and in-group protection repeatedly performed better with high-collectivism populations, with these messages similar to those that worked well in polio contexts.7,16 Additionally, there is qualitative evidence that cultural narratives related to religion, family dynamics, and community leadership have a significant influence on vaccine perceptions and resistance (Aslam, Hussain et al., 2026). This body of work brings a common message: If audiences see vaccination as an outside edict, rather than an inside norm, then the content of the message is less important than how it is framed.

Theoretical framework

This study integrates Framing Theory and Social Identity Theory into a single analytical model. Framing Theory explains why the same vaccine information produces different responses depending on how it is packaged. SIT explains why identity-inconsistent messages produce rejection independent of informational content. Together, they predict that effective vaccine communication in Pakistan requires messages experienced as affirming rather than threatening to the recipient's collective identity.

The core construct is perceived identity congruence: the degree to which a respondent experiences a health message as consistent with their religious and cultural self-concept. We theorize that (a) collective framing increases perceived identity congruence; (b) identity threat decreases intention to vaccinate directly and independently of religious belief intensity; and (c) peer-norm salience moderates the framing–uptake relationship, amplifying collective framing effects among community-oriented respondents. This framework is operationally distinct from HBM/TPB and does not overlap with the Faith-Family-Frontline model (Aslam, Hussain et al., 2026), which is qualitative and does not test message-processing mechanisms.

Hypotheses

Collective identity framing is positively associated with polio intention to vaccinate.

Perceived identity threat is negatively associated with polio intention to vaccinate, even after controlling for religious belief intensity.

Perceived identity congruence mediates the relationship between collective framing and intention to vaccinate.

Peer-norm salience moderates the framing uptake relationship, such that collective framing is more effective among respondents with higher community orientation.

Religious belief intensity alone is not a significant predictor of intention to vaccinate. Once the identity threat is controlled.

Methodology

Design and sample

We used a cross-sectional quantitative survey design. The study population comprised adults aged 18 and older in urban and semi-urban areas of Sindh (Hyderabad and Mirpur Khas districts) and Balochistan (Quetta and Turbat districts), areas with high hesitancy rates that are underrepresented in existing quantitative research 14. We chose these provinces because they remain underrepresented in existing quantitative studies on vaccine hesitancy, and because sampling Sindhi and Baloch communities permits analysis of identity processes distinct from those documented in Pashtun-majority settings 14.

Multistage stratified sampling was employed. Districts were selected purposively using GPEI case-report data (specifically, districts with documented polio case activity within the preceding 24 months). Within districts, systematic random sampling was used (every 8th household), with one adult caregiver invited per household. Respondents were allocated proportionally across provinces and districts based on estimated adult caregiver populations derived from Pakistan Bureau of Statistics17 figures; no post-stratification weighting was applied, and findings should accordingly be interpreted as district-level rather than province-representative. The target sample was 600 (Cochran formula: 5% margin of error, 95% confidence interval). Of 660 approached, 600 responded (response rate: 90.9%). The institutional review board of the University of Gujrat granted ethical approval (No. CMS/123/2025). Informed written consent was obtained from all respondents.

Instrumentation

The questionnaire was designed and translated into Urdu and Sindhi using the back-translation method. It contained 32 items in six sections. The Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) were conducted to establish construct validity with factor loadings of all items greater than .60. The internal consistency of all scales was good (see Table 1). The full questionnaire can be found in the Supplementary Material. Demographic items included age, sex, education, income, province, and type of residence. The message framing exposure was measured using six items (twice each collective/individual framed), adapted from the work of Rothman & Salovey6 and localized for Pakistan. The items were rated on 5-point Likert scales for perceived identity congruence (5 items), identity threat (4 items), and peer-norm salience (5 items). The intensity of religious beliefs was assessed using four items of the Muslim Religiosity Scale of Khan & Watson.18 Behavioral intention (intention to vaccinate) was the dependent variable measured by 6 items adapted from Fishbein & Ajzen.19

Analysis strategy

All data were analyzed using SPSS 26. H1, H2, H4, and H5 were tested using hierarchical multiple regression (Model 1: Demographic controls; Model 2: Main effects; Model 3: Moderator and interaction term). H3 was tested through mediation analysis using the PROCESS macro (Model 4, 5000 bootstrapped samples). Before creating interaction terms, all continuous predictors were mean-centered. All of the regression models included demographic variables (age, gender, education, and income) as controls.

Results

Sample profile

52% respondents were male and 48% female. Mean age was 34.2 years (SD = 9.8). Education ranged from no formal schooling (14%) to a university degree (22%), with the modal category being secondary education (38%). Monthly household income was below PKR 30,000 for 61%. 52% of respondents were from Sind, and 48% from Balochistan.

The hierarchical regression is shown in Table 2. After controlling for demographics (Model 1, R2 = .07, p < .001), the main predictors (Model 2) explained an additional 29% of variance (ΔR2 = .29, p < .001). Collective framing exposure was a significant positive predictor of intention to vaccinate (β = .41, SE = .04, p < .001), supporting H1. Perceived identity threat was the strongest negative predictor (β = −.45, SE = .04, p < .001), supporting H2. Critically, religious belief intensity was not significant in Model 2 (β = −.06, SE = .05, p = .19), supporting H5; once identity threat is controlled, religiosity does not independently drive refusal.

Mediation: identity congruence (H3)

Mediation analysis (Table 3) confirmed that perceived identity congruence partially mediated the relationship between collective framing and intention to vaccinate. The indirect effect via identity congruence was significant (b = .18, 95% CI [.12, .25]); the direct effect of framing remained significant (b = .23, p < .001) after including the mediator. This supports H3: collective framing increases intention to vaccinate partly because it makes recipients feel that vaccination is consistent with who they are.

The interaction term (Collective Framing × Peer-Norm Salience) was significant in Model 3 (β = .22, p < .001; Table 2), supporting H4. Discussion

Three points that warrant special mention. Collective identity framing (β = .41) was the strongest positive predictor of intention to vaccinate. This reflects how messages become effective when experienced as resonating with the collective "self" of the recipient. This is consistent with Entman's4 framing theory and with results from other collectivist Muslim contexts in which the framing of communal responsibility was more effective than the framing of individual benefit.12,16 Therefore, the emphasis needs to be on re-engineering the vaccination campaigns as participation in an act of belonging, a responsibility of a good Muslim parent and a good member of the community, instead of just including religious language within the existing messaging.

Second, and most importantly, identity threat was the most powerful predictor of refusal (β = −.45), even more so than religious belief intensity. Even when controlling for identity threat, religiosity was not significant (β = −.06, p = .19). This finding challenges the common view that religiosity is a barrier 20.21,22; The proximal driver of rejection is not having a high level of religious commitment, but whether they experience a health message as questioning "Who are you?" If a respondent is very religious and thinks vaccination is in line with Islamic beliefs, their intention to vaccinate is not less than that of a low-religiosity respondent. Campaigns focusing on "religious" communities as a problem category may be misallocating resources by focusing on communicating vaccination in ways that do not challenge religious identity, but affirm it.

Third, the framing effect was significantly moderated by peer-norm salience (β = .22, p < .001). Collective framing was twice as effective (b = .58 vs.21) among high peer-norm salience respondents, as predicted by SIT that identity cues would be more salient when social identity is chronically salient.5 Norm activation, public endorsements from imams, community mothers, and respected elders, should precede household-level messaging, particularly in high norm-salience communities.

These findings are mechanistically distinct from those of Aslam, Hussain et al. (2026), who found divine fatalism, patriarchal gatekeeping, and Lady Health Worker constraints as thematic barriers. Our quantitative findings lend mechanistic specificity to the relationship between cultural context and behavior: the journey from cultural context to behavior goes via identity threat, and is addressable through message design.

These findings carry three direct implications for GPEI partners. First, campaign messages should be based on collective Islamic duty and community protection, not individual risk reduction, applied to visual design, spokesperson selection, and channel choice. Second, proposed messages need to be pre-tested for reactions to identity threat, not for attitude or knowledge, in community scoping studies before scaling; the relevant metric is identity threat, not knowledge or attitude. Third, norm activation through local imams, women community leaders, and respected elders should precede household-level outreach, especially in high norm-salience communities.

The identity-anchored message acceptance (IAMU) model

Based on these results, we propose the Identity-Anchored Message Uptake (IAMU) Model (Fig. 1). The model positions perceived identity congruence as the key mediator between message framing and intention to vaccinate. It specifies three upstream antecedents of identity congruence: (1) frame type (collective vs. individual), (2) identity proximity of the communicator, and (3) linguistic/symbolic register of the message. Peer-norm salience moderates the extent to which identity congruence converts to uptake. It is important to note that antecedents (2) and (3) were not operationalized in this study and await empirical testing in future experimental designs.

The Identity-Anchored Message Uptake (IAMU) Model. Collective framing increases perceived identity congruence (a = .29, p < .001), which predicts intention to vaccinate (b = .62, p < .001), yielding an indirect effect of .18 (95% CI [.12, .25]). A direct effect remains (β = +.23, p < .001). Peer-norm salience moderates the congruence–uptake link (β = +.22, p < .001). Identity threat has the strongest direct negative effect (β = −.45, p < .001). Religious belief intensity is non-significant once identity threat is controlled (β = −.06, p = .19).

The IAMU Model is different from the Faith-Family-Frontline model (Aslam, Hussain et al., 2026) in three key ways: it is quantitative with empirically tested pathways; it is grounded in SIT and Framing Theory (not HBM/TPB), enabling analysis of identity processes; and it is explicitly a communication design model with actionable levers. We present it as an empirically driven framework for future experimental intervention research, not as a fully validated causal structure.

Limitations and future directions

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; experimental designs would be needed to determine whether framing manipulations causally alter intention to vaccinate or whether the observed associations partly reflect pre-existing identity orientations. Self-reported intention to vaccinate may not correspond to actual behaviour; future studies should seek to link survey data with administrative vaccination records.

The sample is drawn from four districts; findings should not be generalized beyond these settings, nor taken as representative of Sindh or Balochistan as provinces, or of KPK and Punjab. The theoretical mechanisms identified are, however, potentially applicable to analogous polio-endemic communities across Pakistan. Although the pathways of the IAMU Model are supported by mediation and moderation analyses, experimental validation of each link is essential before they can serve as the basis for intervention design. Finally, two of the three upstream antecedents proposed in the IAMU Model, identity proximity of the communicator and linguistic/symbolic register, were not operationalized here; their inclusion in future experimental work is essential before the full model can be validated. Future studies should also examine how identity threat operates across online and offline channels, given the growing influence of social media in anti-vaccination discourse in Pakistan.

Conclusion

In Pakistan, vaccine hesitancy is not primarily an issue of religious faith or lack of information. It is an issue of identity, and therefore, an issue of communication design. This paper demonstrates that the strongest predictor of polio vaccine refusal is not individual religiosity but whether the health message threatens who the recipient is. Conversely, the greatest driver of intention to vaccinate is whether vaccination feels like something their community, their people, would do. The Identity-Anchored Message Uptake (IAMU) Model offers a practical, theoretically grounded framework for putting this insight to work. Campaigns that speak to community identity rather than individual risk, that activate norms before targeting households, and that are delivered by communicators perceived as genuine insiders will, the evidence suggests, perform significantly better than the biomedical status quo.
