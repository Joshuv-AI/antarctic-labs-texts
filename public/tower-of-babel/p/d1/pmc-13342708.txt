User-centered design of a patient-generated health data mobile application for primary care visits for older adults with chronic pain
Authors: Bryan Gibson, L Weaver, Usman Sattar, Alan Taylor Kelley, Joshua Judd, Sara J Knight, Adam J Gordon, Randall Rupper, Jorie M Butler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo design the provider-facing side of an application that collects and displays contextual patient-generated health data (PGHD) as a means of facilitating patient-centered care for older adults with chronic pain in the Veterans Health Administration (VHA).Materials and MethodsWe conducted one-on-one user-centered design (UCD) sessions with VHA clinicians who care for veterans age 65 years and older with chronic pain. We elicited information about clinical practices, barriers and facilitators to working with patients with chronic pain, and types of patient information that could provide utility in the delivery of care. We displayed a wireframe prototype of the PGHD application to elicit feedback and preferences about information to collect, desired functionality, visual design, and possible workflows.ResultsFrom 21 UCD sessions with clinicians, we identified 3 overarching themes: (1) specific contextual information could facilitate patient-centered care for older adults with chronic pain (information needs); (2) clinicians have specific desired uses for PGHD (functional requirements); and (3) clinicians have specific beliefs about how PGHD can and should be integrated into clinical practice (implementation strategies).DiscussionPGHD integration in clinical care for older adults with chronic pain was perceived favorably among VHA clinicians, with specific utility for this technology anticipated. The ways in which PGHD is accessed, integrated, and shared were perceived as critically important to its future implementation and potential for improving patient-centered care.ConclusionDisplay of contextual PGHD from older adults with chronic pain is promising to VHA clinicians, but barriers to implementation in clinical practice need to be addressed.Keywords: chronic pain, patient-generated health data, patient engagement

Background and significance

Chronic pain affects more than 24% of adults in the U.S. and often leads to significant disability.1 Older adults are disproportionately affected by chronic pain, which contributes to significant morbidity and early mortality.2 Drivers of chronic pain in older adults are multifactorial, and it is now widely recognized that effective treatment requires a multidisciplinary, multimodal approach that is responsive to patient preferences and context.3

To address the complexities involved in caring for older adults with chronic pain, several related approaches to care have been developed.4–8 Patient-centered care considers patient priorities, values, and goals as essential to clinical decision-making and has been shown to improve physical and social well-being and patient satisfaction in some settings.9 Shared decision-making (SDM)—a critical facet of patient-centered care—incorporates patient values and preferences into clinical decisions and has shown a positive impact on patient outcomes, particularly in the care of older Veterans.10 The Veteran’s Health Administration has instituted a “whole health” approach, which considers the context of the whole person and incorporates personalized patient coaching, and has shown to be helpful for reducing pharmacologic pain treatment use.11,12 However, mechanisms to incorporate patient values and preferences into real-world decisions for diagnosing, treating, and managing multifaceted conditions such as chronic pain have not been fully realized in practice.13–16

Patient-generated health data (PGHD) refers to the systematic generation of health data by patients (rather than data input by healthcare professionals) and could be used to provide clinicians with contextual information, such as the patient’s goals, social support, home environment, and personal beliefs.17 Currently, such contextual patient information is rarely documented by clinicians,18–20 While PGHD could facilitate patient-centered care by addressing this gap in the availability of contextual patient data, evidence is limited regarding which specific data elements are essential and how they should be summarized and integrated into clinical care workflows to optimize care for patients with chronic pain.21,22

In this project, our team designed an app to collect PGHD from older Veteran adults with chronic pain, and their informal caregivers, and summarize that data for providers. The goal of the project is for the application to be used to facilitate the delivery of patient-centered care to these patients. In this paper, we describe the design of the provider-facing component of the app (separate manuscripts are in preparation describing the design of the patient-facing component and pilot work testing the app). We sought to gather information on which types of PGHD would be most useful to clinicians (information needs), identify how they might want to use PGHD in their practice (functional requirements), and solicit clinician input on potential barriers and facilitators to using this application in clinical practice (implementation strategies). This project represents an important extension of prior PGHD research, which has measured health behaviors and disease processes (eg, physical activity, diet, substance use, and blood glucose logs),23 by focusing on contextual PGHD for older adults with chronic pain.

Conceptual framework: the clinician activated situation model

The Clinician Activated Situation Model developed for this research posits that a clinician’s engagement with contextual PGHD acts directly on their situational mental model of the patient, which then enables development of a treatment plan that is more concordant with the patient’s values, goals, and preferences (Figure 1). The theoretical backing for the model comes from work in situation models, a mental representation synthesized into a complex “read” on a situation involving the complexities and persons in a situation.24,25 Translated to a clinical situation, this may encompass the clinician’s view of the patient’s condition, its causes, and the processes that may alleviate it. Providing novel information to activate this model is, in effect, pushing clinicians into a different mental representation about their patients—requiring them to think more deeply, problem-solve, and generate a more nuanced situational mental model, which promotes understanding of the patient and enhances patient-centered care.

Integrated conceptual model.

For this work, contextual PGHD elements are classified in 3 categories—relational, whole-person, and behavioral—and we anticipate that providers’ having access to these 3 types of information about their patient will enable a comprehensive understanding of the patient’s environment, situation, and context.18,26 Relational PGHD describes aspects of the patient’s social life. Whole-person PGHD provides a holistic portrait of the patient best summarized by “what is important to you as a person.” Patient activation PGHD refers to behaviors or beliefs related to self-management of health and disease. Our hypotheses in developing this app are that (a) increased clinician engagement with contextual PGHD will enhance the process of shared decision-making and contribute to care plans that incorporate patient values and preferences, and are sensitive to patients’ circumstances and needs; and (b) well-designed implementations of contextual PGHD will increase patient-centered care.

Methods

Objective

To design the provider-facing side of a mobile application that collects contextual patient-generated health data (PGHD) from patients and summarizes it for providers as a means of facilitating patient-centered care for older adults with chronic pain in the Veterans Health Administration (VHA).

Setting

The Veterans Health Administration (VHA) serves more than 9 million Veterans nationally. For this study, we included VHA primary care and geriatric clinical staff (physicians, nurse practitioners, nurses, social workers, chiropractors, pharmacists, psychologists) who deliver care within a patient-centered medical home model to patients ≥65 years old who have chronic pain.

Design

We collected perspectives of primary care clinical staff in an iterative UCD process to guide both the selection of potential data elements and the design of a provider-facing summary display. Our approach was informed by established human factors and UCD frameworks, which emphasize designing technologies that align with users’ tasks, workflows, and contexts of use to improve usability, usefulness, and adoption in real-world settings.27 Key features of this approach include placing the user at the center of design, repeated cycles of design iteration and feedback, and considering the broader clinical work system in which the tool will be implemented.27,28 Prior work in mHealth app development has demonstrated that engaging target users across all stages of the design process—from formative work through usability evaluation—results in more useful and usable tools than design processes with limited user involvement.29 Prior work in eHealth intervention development has demonstrated that patients and clinicians have distinct and sometimes divergent needs and priorities. For example, clinicians may prioritize data sharing functionality while patients prioritize simplicity and ease of access, and that these differences are best addressed through separate, targeted design processes for each user group.30 Accordingly, we have previously conducted UCD sessions with patients and caregivers to design the data collection component of the tool (manuscript in preparation) and piloted its use to assess feasibility, acceptability, and response burden among these users. The present study focuses specifically on the clinician-facing component of the application, with the goal of ensuring that collected contextual PGHD can be effectively summarized and integrated into clinical workflows.

Recruitment

Prior to study initiation, we obtained approval from the VA Salt Lake City/University of Utah Institutional Review Board. Participants were recruited between July 2021 and April 2023. We recruited participants by the snowball method,31 starting with VHA clinicians known to the study team, followed by other clinicians referred to the study team by the initial participants. Additional recruitment occurred during a weekly clinic meeting in which interested clinicians were invited to contact the study team to participate.

Data collection

UCD sessions were conducted by authors BG and US (see Appendix S1 for the session guide). Each UCD session began with questions about the participant’s clinical experience and practice in treating older adults with chronic pain and then asked about situations in which they would like to have contextual PGHD when caring for older adults with chronic pain. The initial wireframe design was created by BG with input from JB, ATK, and US after the third design session. In all subsequent design sessions, after the first phase of eliciting information needs, we showed each participant the current iteration of the wireframes of the PGHD app and solicited input on the app’s proposed content, functionality, and visual design. The wireframe designs were iterated when the team agreed that recent input from participants warranted a revision. Design decisions for each iteration were made based on consensus among investigators (BG, JB, ATK, and US) who conducted the UCD sessions and analysis. All design sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed. Transcriptions were then uploaded to Dedoose for analysis.32 The UCD design process was continued until there was consensus among investigators (BG, JB, ATK, and US) that the most recent 3 sessions had yielded no significant new information.

Analysis

We analyzed design session transcripts with consensus-based, thematic analysis methods incorporating both inductive and deductive coding.33,34 The analysis was facilitated by the Dedoose qualitative analysis platform.32 Initially, 3 transcripts were reviewed by the coding team, initial codes were assigned based closely on the words of the participants, and a codebook was established with code descriptions. Remaining transcripts were then pair-coded, with disagreements in code assignment adjudicated by consensus. Codes relevant to the design of the PGHD application were prioritized. In particular, we coded the transcripts for mentions of contextual information or data clinicians use or would like to use in caring for veterans with chronic pain (information needs), actions participants would like to be able to perform using the app (functional requirements) and thoughts or concerns about the implementation of the app into clinical practice (implementation considerations).

Results

We conducted 21 user-centered design (UCD sessions, 19 of which were with geriatrics or primary care clinicians (MD, DO, NP), 1 with a social worker, and 1 with a chiropractor. All clinicians were affiliated with the VHA. Eleven participants worked in geriatric clinics, 6 in internal medicine clinics, and 4 in other settings. Thirteen of the sessions were conducted with clinicians working in Utah, 2 worked in Maryland, and 1 each were from Alabama, Texas, Georgia, Florida, Illinois, and New York. Fourteen of the participants were female. Data collection occurred between July 2021 and April 2023.

We identified 3 overarching themes: (1) specific contextual information could facilitate patient-centered care of older adults with chronic pain (ie, information needs reflecting possible data elements to include in the app); (2) clinicians have specific desired uses for PGHD (ie, possible functional requirements for the app); and (3) clinicians believe that how PGHD is integrated into clinical practice could impact its effectiveness and adoption over time (ie, possible implementation issues for the app). How PGHD is implemented in practice could impact its effectiveness and adoption over time (implementation issues).

Theme 1: specific contextual information could facilitate care of older veterans with chronic pain (information needs)

Participants often made references to types of information that would be useful to patient-centered care for geriatric patients with chronic pain (Table 1). These data elements were coded whether or not they were included in the current prototype of the app. Clinicians highlighted the importance of knowledge of the general state of a patient’s residence (ideally via photos), functional status, information related to prior experiences with treatment, and preferences related to treatment. In addition, clinicians indicated that providing photos or text of what is important to them may have a side benefit of being a valuable exercise in self-reflection for patients (“time where they communicate, in writing, how they feel”).

Theme 2: clinicians desire specific functions to facilitate patient-centered care (potential functional requirements)

Participants expressed potential uses for PGHD to improve patient-centered care; this suggests a desire for specific functional requirements for the app. We coded these instances regardless of whether the functionality described was included in the current iteration of our design or plans for implementation.

Clinicians highlighted the value of separate summaries of PGHD for clinicians and patients (eg, the patient-facing version would not require “translation” by medical staff). Clinicians wanted the ability to compare patient vs informal caregiver responses (eg, to compare reported function between the patient and caregiver). Furthermore, clinicians mentioned the importance of time to review PGHD before the visit (suggesting data collection should be completed 1 day prior), so that the care team could review it in their morning huddle (suggesting PGHD should be integrated into the EHR). In addition, clinicians expressed the desire to have private, separate conversations with patients and caregivers. Providers also suggested that the app should allow additional care team members (eg, social workers) to access the PGHD and act on it accordingly.

Theme 3: how PGHD is implemented in practice could impact its effectiveness and adoption over time (implementation issues)

During the sessions, participants made a variety of comments related to the pros and cons of integrating PGHD into clinical care. There were specific strategies mentioned that might facilitate or impede implementation. These included the idea that clinicians need to be “sold” on the idea that PGHD will improve practice, concerns about the time required to complete the included questionnaires and possible impacts on clinicians’ time, and concerns about specific kinds of patients who might have difficulty or be unable to use the app (eg, individuals with cognitive impairment).

The final provider-facing wireframe is presented in Figure 2 (all data and photos are simulated and were purely for collection of feedback from participants). In the upper right corner is a photo of the patient (in reality 1 of the investigators) and demographic data; a layout that mimics most EHRs. In the center right are the photos of specific components of the patient’s home environment that participants reported they would want included (eg, home entrance, medication storage, bathroom, etc). In the upper right is information about the caregiver, their relationship to and frequency of contact with the patient, and their self-reported workload. The left upper box (“today”) contains scores from standardized measures of anxiety and depression. The left middle box (“pain”) contains measures of pain self-efficacy and pain catastrophizing. The left lowest box contains measures of loneliness and social isolation. The center column contains the patient’s textual responses to open-ended questions intended to help providers get to know more about the patient as a person (eg, “What is Important to you”). The right upper box contains measures of function, particularly Activities of Daily Living and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (with both patient and caregiver reports shown together). Finally, the right lower box (“context”) contains scores from the patient’s report of any contextual red flags (such as difficulty getting meds or traveling to the clinic) and a brief measure of health literacy.

Discussion

In this paper, we describe the user-centered design of the provider-facing component of an app that captures contextual PGHD from patients and then summarizes it for providers as a means of facilitating patient-centered care for older Veteran adults with chronic pain. We found providers have specific information needs including: the general state of a patient’s residence (ideally via photos), functional status, information related to prior experiences with treatment, and preferences related to treatment. Participants expressed potential uses for PGHD to improve patient-centered care, Clinicians highlighted the value of separate summaries of PGHD for clinicians and patients (eg, the patient-facing version would not require “translation” by medical staff). Clinicians wanted the ability to compare patient vs informal caregiver responses (eg, to compare reported function between the patient and caregiver). Furthermore, clinicians mentioned the importance of time to review PGHD before the visit (suggesting data collection should be completed at least 1 day prior to the visit so that the care team could be reviewed in their morning huddle (also suggesting PGHD should be integrated in the EHR, since this is what care teams use for huddles). In addition, clinicians expressed the desire to have private, separate conversations with patients and caregivers and that the App should allow additional care team members (eg, social workers) to access the PGHD and act on it accordingly. Finally some participants suggested that clinicians need to be “sold” on the idea that PGHD will improve practice, expressed concerns about the time required to complete the included questionnaires and possible impacts on clinician’s time to review the data, and concerns about specific kinds of patients who might have difficulty or be unable to use the app (eg, individuals with cognitive impairment).

The design of our application builds upon similar prior studies. For example, Holt et al designed and implemented a PGHD app which captures patient-reported data on what is important to patients, their health, their preferences, their challenges, and visit agenda and implemented it in a primary care setting. In a study of clinician-users, they found that users felt that the system increased patient engagement by making the patients feel that the healthcare system “know them and cares about them”; and that pre-visit review of the PGHD by the care team improved both the efficiency and perceived quality of the visit. However, they also reported that some clinicians found it challenging to incorporate PGHD into the visit, particularly if not reviewed prior to the visit.49 Our results expanded upon those of Holt et al finding that clinician participants expressed specific information needs with respect to contextual PGHD; for example, clinicians would like to have photos of the patient’s home environment, information about the patient’s functional status, the patient’s goals and preferences, a summary of prior treatments, and the patient’s perception of those treatments. Participants also expressed the desire for specific functions in the PGHD app, including separate summary displays for patients and providers, visualization of discrepancies between patient and caregiver responses (eg, differences in how they perceive the patient’s functional ability), the possibility to have one-on-one communication with caregivers, and the ability to delegate specific PGHD to appropriate staff (eg, review of SDOH data by a social worker) and the ability take action on identified needs through the app itself (eg, place an e-referral). Finally, some participants suggested that balancing the depth of information in the app with time constraints of primary care clinic visits could be challenging.

Other prior studies have placed emphasis on streamlining context for clinicians by identifying “contextual red flags” (such as health literacy) that might impact the patient’s healthcare.50 Weiner et al designed and implemented an intervention intended created a typology of red flags from recordings of primary care visits and then conducted a trial randomizing providers to receive alerts about the presence of red flags.51 They found that contextualized clinical decision support increased the likelihood providers would probe more for contextual information during the visit and modify the care plan in accordance with the red flag.52 Our application design incorporates elements of each of this prior effort. For example, our app includes a measure of health literacy. However, our results suggested the importance of considering contextual PGHD which was not considered in these prior studies. For example, photos of the patient’s living environment and data on the patient’s functional abilities were the most requested PGHD data elements among our participants. It is possible that the reason for these differences stem from our focus on specialty care for older patients with chronic pain while prior work addressed a general primary care population. This suggests the need for additional studies to determine the information needs of clinicians in a variety of contexts and the possibility of tailoring the collection and summarization of PGHD to both patient and clinic context.

We are currently conducting a pilot trial to examine the effects of the PGHD app on care processes and patients’ perceptions of care. Based on the prior work described above, we hypothesize that use of the app will increase patient engagement and patient perceptions that care addresses their needs and also leads to improvements in contextualization of the care plan.52 At the same time, we expect challenges in making sure the app is efficiently integrated into clinical workflows. We intend to address this by training clinical teams to review the PGHD prior to the visit on a tablet computer as component of a “huddle.”53

It is important to note that not all information needs or desired functions expressed by participants in this study were translated into the current design of the PGHD app. For example, participants wanted information related to patients’ prior experiences with treatment. The research team deemed that this would require extracting the patient’s treatment history from the EHR, summarizing the treatment history (which in many older patients with chronic pain could be quite complex) providing a means for the patient/caregiver to annotate that summarized data, and finally providing that annotated history back to providers. The research team determined that while this may be a highly desirable set of data and functions, it was simply beyond the scope of the project and added this to the plans for future development of the app. Similar determinations were made for the desire to know about patient preferences related to treatment and the desire for the collected PGHD data to be integrated into the EHR. In particular, integrating contextual PGHD into the EHR would require significant work to clarify governance, workflow integration, and data management consideration. Finally, in the future we expect that we might add additional functionality to the app, such as the ability for the PGHD data to be analyzed for patterns and trends for each patient and for these trends to be visualized and shareable. Prior work suggests that this is important to the continued utility of PGHD in chronic disease.54,55

Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, we co-designed our application with potential end users of the application, this paper describes the provider- facing component of the app, a manuscript describing a parallel process with patients is in preparation. Second, since clinical practices can vary substantially, we sought a breadth of input from clinicians across the VHA’s large integrated system.

Limitations

All participants were recruited from within the VHA system and our results may not generalize to other settings and adaptation may be necessary for different settings. In addition our use of snowball sampling for recruitment method may have biased our participants pool toward clinicians who have a positive view of contextual PGHD. Examples of PGHD shown to clinicians were hypothetical and not specific to actual patients. As a result, we may not have fully elicited discussions of information needs, design concerns, that might arise in clinical practice. Finally, in design sessions, clinicians were primarily prompted to consider contextual PGHD, thus our findings may not reflect other PGHD types and the value of specific measures to clinicians (eg, enhanced ADLs vs ADLs). It would be worthwhile to consider future work in which alternative measures are proposed to providers and get their opinions on including/replacing specific measures. The addition of measures will needs to be balanced with concerns about response burden for patient and care givers.

Conclusion

We employed user-centered design methods to design an application that summarizes to contextual PGHD for providers and facilitate person-centered care. VA clinicians favor collecting pictures of the patient home environment, information on the patient’s functional status (from both patient and caregiver perspectives), and qualitative information about what is important to the patient via the app. They want to be able to use the app to place referrals, delegate tasks (eg, have the social worker review specific data), and, if needed, communicate privately with caregivers about patients.
