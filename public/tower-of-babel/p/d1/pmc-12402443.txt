Comparative evaluation of impact-based and hybrid approaches for real time earthquake shaking prediction
Authors: Raffaele Rea, Takao Kagawa, Aldo Zollo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Earthquake Early Warning (EEW) systems can provide seconds to tens of seconds of advanced notice before the arrival of destructive seismic waves. Their effectiveness, however, depends on how quickly and accurately each system forecasts ground motion using real-time data. This study compares the performance of two distinct EEW algorithms (PLUM (Kodera et al. in Bull Seismol Soc Am 108:983–1003, 2018, 10.1785/0120170085; Kagawa in Front Earth Sci 9, 2021, 10.3389/feart.2021.672613) a pure impact-based approach, and a P-wave Shaking Forecast Based EEWS (QuakeUp (Zollo et al. in Earth Space Sci. 10(4), 2023, 10.1029/2022EA002657), which is a hybrid method) by simulating the 2016 Mjma 6.6 Central Tottori earthquake. Our offline “playback” of the event demonstrates that PLUM offers consistently high alert accuracy (≥ 90% stations Successfully Alerted) once threshold is exceeded but provides limited lead-times for sites near the epicenter. In some cases, stations up to 30 km from the source received effective warnings too late for significant protective actions. Conversely, QuakeUp could issue alerts as early as 3 s after the origin time, yielding up to 12 s of lead-time at 40 km. However, its accuracy briefly dropped to 64% before converging to 100% by around 12 s, reflecting the time required for magnitude and location estimates to mature. Despite these differences, both algorithms delivered reliable alerts across much of Tottori Prefecture. The results highlight how algorithmic design and station coverage influence warning performance, with PLUM excelling under dense station networks and QuakeUp offering broader, earlier coverage where real-time source parameters can be accurately constrained.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17889-z.

Introduction

In earthquake-prone regions, timely access to information on imminent seismic shaking is a crucial factor for minimizing casualties and damage. Earthquake Early Warning (EEW) systems are designed to provide advance alerts (ranging from a few seconds to tens of seconds) before the arrival of the most destructive ground motions. This short yet potentially life-saving timeframe can enable the implementation of automatic or individual safety actions, such as halting production lines, slowing high-speed trains, opening elevator doors at the nearest floor, or prompting people to take protective measures4–7.

The fundamental premise behind EEW systems is the real-time mitigation of earthquake impacts, achieved by leveraging the difference in propagation speeds between P-waves, which are generally less destructive, and S-waves or surface waves, which typically cause more intense ground shaking. The difference between the propagation of the seismic wave and the propagation of the electromagnetic waves is also leveraged by these systems that could use radio and wi-fi signals to provide alerts at distant sites. In this sense, EEW systems serve a critical societal need, reducing both human and economic losses.

Over the last decades, different EEW methodologies have been investigated and implemented worldwide, reflecting regional seismic characteristics, network densities, and technological infrastructures8–11.

These systems can be broadly categorized into two main groups: source-based and impact-based methods. Source-based approaches primarily focus on the rapid estimation of earthquake location and magnitude to forecast the distribution of shaking intensity using ground-motion prediction equations (GMPEs). By relying on inferred event characteristics, these methods can quickly provide alerts over a wide area; however, their accuracy may be affected by uncertainties and complexities in modeling the earthquake source, such as rupture directivity or finite-fault effects and path effects such as site amplification/attenuation12–15. A clear example of failure occurred during the 2011 Tohoku-Oki Mw9.0 event16.

In contrast, impact-based approaches target the real-time measurement and propagation of observed ground shaking to predict the expected intensity and potential damage at surrounding locations.

This category encompasses both “pure” and “hybrid” impact-based methods17. Pure impact-based algorithms rely solely on the direct measurements recorded by seismometers in the network, and do not require estimation of earthquake source parameters. Thus, they can be less vulnerable to errors arising from source characterization. On the other hand, hybrid methods incorporate rapid source estimates to complement observations of ground shaking. By combining these two data streams, hybrid methods can predict ground-motion parameters in areas that have not yet been affected, typically providing warnings earlier than purely measurement-driven systems.

A prominent example of a pure impact-based method is the Propagation of Local Undamped Motion, or PLUM1that is currently used at the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA). This approach is particularly robust for large or complex ruptures, where conventional source-based algorithms might struggle with uncertainties or complexities in real-time magnitude estimation, which is generally based on the point-source approximation, leading to overpredictions of Intensity and PGV18–20. However, PLUM is highly dependent on network density: if large regions remain uncovered by seismic stations, the propagation of shaking levels may be underestimated or delayed1. Recent upgrades2 include predictions of on-site intensity from P-wave signals, yet PLUM still does not incorporate source parameter estimations, making it slower to predict strong shaking in areas not yet instrumentally sampled.

By comparison, the P-wave shaking-forecast method3 (acronym QuakeUp), represents a hybrid strategy. This method uses early P-wave observations to estimate source parameters in real time, enabling a forecast of peak ground velocity (PGV) at target sites before the arrival of damaging S-waves or surface waves.

Around the world, numerous impact-based or hybrid EEW systems have demonstrated strong potential in providing timely alerts17,21. For instance, Japan has integrated both source-based and impact-based elements within its operational EEW framework, while Taiwan and the United States continuously refine their source-based regional systems to optimize performance given local network configurations and geologic conditions4,8,9. Impact-based systems, in general, can adapt to complex rupture processes, but require dense networks to rapidly “track” the progression of shaking. Hybrid methods extend warnings to a wider region but rely on reliable and rapid source estimations.

In this paper, we compare the performance of the pure impact-based PLUM method1,2 with the hybrid QuakeUp approach3 by conducting an offline simulation of the 2016 Mjma 6.6 Central Tottori earthquake. This event, which caused significant property damage but fortunately resulted in no fatalities22provides a valuable case study for evaluating the relative strengths and limitations of these two EEW methodologies. The Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) has issued an alert for the Central Tottori earthquake, yet the limited real-time station coverage in the mountainous areas of Tottori Prefecture led to delayed warnings and highlighted potential blind zones for both source-based and impact-based approaches. Here, we specifically assess the lead-time (the time available for mitigation actions, or the interval between the time of the alert and the arrival of strong shaking waves) and the accuracy of impact prediction (the goodness of predicted shaking and corresponding alert level) of alerts produced by the two systems. Through this comparative analysis, we aim to quantify how these methods perform under realistic operational conditions, and to identify potential paths for enhancing future EEW implementations in areas where network coverage varies.

The objectives of this study are, therefore, threefold: (1) to document how each approach—pure impact-based vs. hybrid—operates in real time, (2) to measure their respective lead times and blind zones during the 2016 Central Tottori earthquake, and (3) to evaluate the reliability of their impact predictions, which we quantify through the timeliness and quality of alerts. This work ultimately seeks to inform the development of robust, context-specific EEW strategies that effectively protect lives and infrastructure during damaging seismic events.

Results

The performance of PLUM and QuakeUp was evaluated by examining how each method predicted the ground shaking at key sites in Tottori Prefecture during the 2016 Mjma 6.6 Central Tottori earthquake. Because PLUM does not provide source parameter estimates, the comparison focuses on the impact at seismic stations and populated areas, considering whether the methods issued timely and reliable alerts.

A first, broader perspective arises from comparing the real-time ShakeMaps23 generated by both approaches at specific time steps (Figs. 1 and 2, respectively).

PLUM, in its implementation for Tottori Prefecture2confines its forecasts strictly within the prefecture’s administrative boundaries. In Fig. 1, this spatial limitation appears in the distinct shape of Tottori Prefecture, where PLUM assigns different intensity values based on the recorded or predicted shaking, but provides no information beyond its borders. Early in the event (5 s after the origin time), the system forecasts predominantly low intensities, but then rapidly updates its intensity map: by 15 s, most of the prefecture has reached a nearly final distribution of predicted shaking. At 30 s, once the damaging S-waves have arrived at all stations, PLUM delivers a definitive intensity map. Within this progression, circular high-intensity patches sometimes appear around individual stations, likely because the radius used to propagate the recorded intensities can be too small, creating localized “hot spots” that may not smoothly blend with neighboring areas.

QuakeUp produces a slightly different pattern (Fig. 2). Its shake maps appear smoother overall, largely due to two contributing factors. First, QuakeUp combines local, station-based measurements of P-wave amplitudes with a Ground Motion Prediction Equation (GMPE), which provides a broader, source-based forecast across the region. Second, unlike PLUM, QuakeUp does not incorporate densely sampled site factors at regular intervals. Instead, it relies on the GMPE’s generalized distance- and magnitude-based attenuation relationships, which yield more uniform intensity contours around the epicenter or around individual stations that have already measured the P-wave. In Fig. 2, large circular zones near the epicenter at early times show areas where the GMPE dominates the intensity prediction, while smaller, station-centered circles appear where on-site measurements refine the local forecast.

For both systems, the timing and correctness of alerts were evaluated using a classification matrix of four alert outcomes: Successful Alert (SA), Missed Alert (MA), False Alert (FA), and Successful No Alert (SNA). This classification depends on whether the predicted intensity and the observed intensity each exceed a predefined threshold, here set to 3 on the JMA scale (threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{JMA}$$\end{document}= 3), corresponding to a ground shaking perceived by most people. Table 1 defines each category based on predicted and observed intensities. Figure 4 summarizes the evolution of these alert outcomes over time. Once PLUM begins producing forecasts (4 s after O.T.) the percentage of successfully alerted stations is very low, but after 10 s from the O.T., this percentage becomes very high (about 96.9%). This value remains relatively stable until the end of the simulation. By contrast, the right panel demonstrates that QuakeUp initiates predictions around 3 s after the origin, immediately yielding a good success rate (around 94%). Its accuracy temporarily drops between 7 and 12 s as the magnitude and location estimates converge but ultimately reaches 100% success once the source parameters stabilize.

Similar results were obtained using threshold IJMA=4 (Figure S3 of the Supplementary Material). Using this threshold, PLUM initially fails to provide a useful alert, and the system takes about 13 s from the O.T. to successfully alert 87.5% of stations. QuakeUp, instead, initially provides some successful alerts (17.6% at 3.5 s from O.T.), but this percentage drops after a while similarly to what we see in Fig. 4. After 16.5 s from O.T. 91.1% of the stations is classified as Successful Alert (Figure S3 of the Supplementary Material). We repeated the same analysis using IJMA=5 L, obtaining similar behaviors, with significative percentages of Successful No Alerts (68.7% for PLUM and 61.8% for QuakeUp; figure S5 in the Supplementary Material).

To address this limitation, a lead-time analysis was performed. We determined the time instants at which the predicted and the observed intensities first exceeded at the same threshold of 3 on the JMA scale (inverting the formula by Midorikawa et al.24 we can obtain the corresponding value of PGV = 0.78 cm/s).

Figure 5 plots both sets of time instants for every station as a function of epicentral distance. For PLUM (left panel), the results show a notable “blind zone” up to approximately 28 km from the epicenter, within which the predicted threshold exceedance occurs later than the measured exceedance. This implies no meaningful warning could be provided to sites within that radius. At larger distances, PLUM yields some positive lead-times (for example, around 40 km from the epicenter) up to roughly 5 s.

In the QuakeUp results (right panel of Fig. 5), most predicted threshold exceedances happen simultaneously or earlier than the observed exceedances for stations beyond about 12 km. This significantly reduces the radius of the blind zone, often granting larger lead-times. At 40 km, for instance, QuakeUp provides a lead-time near 12 s. The difference in performance stems from the GMPE’s ability to forecast shaking across the region as soon as a preliminary magnitude and location are available, rather than waiting for each station to detect actual ground motion above the threshold.

The lead-times were quantified using also thresholds IJMA=4 and IJMA=5 L (details in Supplementary Material, figures S4 and S6). Using threshold IJMA=4, PLUM provides a blind zone of about 19 km of radius, and lead-times between 1 s and 8 s for stations located from 20 km to 60 km from the epicenter. QuakeUp, instead, provides 1 s to 16 s of lead-time at the same stations, with a blind zone of about 15 km. Completely different results were obtained for threshold IJMA=5 L. Since this threshold is overcome at few stations very close to the epicenter, PLUM cannot provide any useful alert (figure S6), while QuakeUp provides a few seconds of lead-time at stations located within 20 km from the epicenter and 6 s at one station located at 38 km from the epicenter.

A more intuitive view of these blind zones can be found in the spatial maps of Fig. 6, which displays the area covered by successful alerts for PLUM (left panels) and QuakeUp (right panels) at 5, 10, and 30 s after the origin.

At the 5-second mark (when PLUM issues its very first forecasts) few stations have already encountered some portion of the S-wavefront, but many others have already recorded the P-wave signal. These stations located between the P-wavefront (shown as the yellow circle) and the S-wavefront (red circle) can still receive warnings in time to act. By 10 s, PLUM’s estimates have stabilized, although a small fraction of stations remains unalerted or incorrectly classified. QuakeUp continues to refine its shake maps during this interval; it initially relies on the GMPE for a wide-area prediction and gradually integrates more on-site measurements. At 30 s, when the entire region has been shaken, QuakeUp has classified all stations as Successful Alerts (SA). Meanwhile, PLUM still records a Missed Alert at one station, suggesting an underestimation of intensity at that location, despite neighboring stations having recorded higher values.

Both methods show a lack of False Alerts and Successful No Alerts, largely because the chosen threshold is relatively low (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{JMA}$$\end{document}= 3) and easily exceeded by the 2016 Tottori event. Although a lower threshold can increase false alarms in other scenarios, it is the operational standard for EEW issuance in Japan and is particularly relevant for an earthquake of this size. A threshold of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{JMA}$$\end{document}= 3 also maximizes the probability of providing a timely alert for residents and infrastructure located outside the immediate epicentral zone, where the ground shaking could still be disruptive. Even if early warnings are initially conservative and risk some overestimation, both PLUM and QuakeUp continually update their forecasts in real time, allowing any initial false alarms to be quickly corrected, using the real-time estimated Intensity at the station.

In summary, the results highlight how station density and algorithmic design strongly influence EEW outcomes. PLUM’s reliance on observed intensities reduces the uncertainties related to source characterization but may delay or underestimate warnings in areas where ground motion has not yet been recorded. In contrast, QuakeUp’s hybrid strategy offers earlier wide-area coverage through real-time magnitude and location estimates, but it may suffer transient instabilities until these parameters converge. Once QuakeUp reaches steady-state solutions, its forecasts become reliable across the region, providing substantial lead-times at many stations.

Discussion

In this study we highlighted the differences between two impact-based methods (a pure method and a hybrid one) by comparing different aspects of their output on the 2016 MJMA6.6 Central Tottori earthquake. Despite that only 6 stations in the prefecture work in real-time, we used all the 34 stations that recorded the event for our analysis. This was done for two reasons. Using just 6 stations none of the two methods would be able to work properly, because both of them require a minimum number of stations to estimate the ground motion (PLUM to propagate the wavefront and QuakeUp to have source parameters estimations). We also wanted to show the potential of the network if all the stations in it worked in real-time, even if their distribution is not optimal.

Despite the not optimal network, we chose to exclude in our analysis two stations that recorded the event, located in the Hyogo prefecture because the employed version of PLUM is implemented only for the Tottori prefecture. This administrative limitation can be overcome in an emergency, but in this case the usage of these two stations would not change the results we showed. These stations were too distant to heavily influence QuakeUp’s source parameter estimations and the event we considered is too small to produce high intensity at such distances, meaning that PLUM could use these stations to propagate the ground motion, but it would not be significant because the impact would be very small. For bigger earthquakes, producing strong impacts across different prefectures, the administrative limitation can be easily overcome as shown in other works1.

The comparison of the performance of the two methods suggests how hybrid methods work better than pure impact-based on inland events recorded by not so dense networks. Even if we showed the results for just one event and more studies are necessary to make strong conclusions, we can state that QuakeUp’s feature of using the GMPE to predict the ground motion permits us to have faster alerts. Other authors performed similar studies on larger datasets, reaching similar conclusions25.

In this scenario PLUM’s difficulty managing inland events emerged, moreover relying on a network that obstacles the propagation of the recorded ground motion (because of the gaps in it). Originally PLUM was created to overcome the problems in estimating the source location and magnitude for big offshore events (e.g. Tohoku 2011), therefore, this method would perform better than QuakeUp (or any other method that relies on source parameters estimations) when applied to offshore events, even if QuakeUp proved his reliability also in such scenarios26.

We must, however, consider other drawbacks of the hybrid method. In this specific case we showed how the alerts issue by QuakeUp have a variable quality. In Fig. 4b we showed how the percentage of Successful Alerts temporarily drops from the initial 94% to the 62% to reach at the end 100%. The main reason for this is that initially most stations are not yet reached by the P-wavefront and the predictions at those stations are performed using GMPE, but when the ground motion is recorded QuakeUp automatically replace the prediction calculated from the GMPE to the prediction calculated from onsite measurements (details in Methods section). Onsite predictions require some seconds of P-wave signal to correctly predict the PGV that will be measured and therefore when the P-wavefront reaches many stations all together for a few seconds we see a drop in the percentage of successful alerts. This problem emerged also in other scenarios27.

Initial wrong estimations by QuakeUp of magnitude and location of the source may also lead to severe PGV overpredictions (that could result in an high percentage of False alert at the beginning of an event). To mitigate this problem the user may consider combining the predictions of the two methods. One may consider running the two methods in parallel to have the faster (but less reliable) alert provided by QuakeUp and the slower (but more robust) alert provided by PLUM that could confirm the first one. We expect that the potential initial QuakeUp overpredictions would be corrected anyway by the Onsite measurements, but our results suggest that PLUM is more consistent in its predictions.

Conclusions

We conducted an off-line simulation of two Earthquake Early Warning (EEW) methods (PLUM1,2 and QuakeUp3 using data from the 2016 Mjma 6.6 Central Tottori earthquake. Although both algorithms aim to provide rapid alerts about impending ground shaking, they differ substantially in their underlying strategies.

PLUM is a pure impact-based method that propagates observed intensities once thresholds are exceeded, whereas QuakeUp is a hybrid approach, combining on-site P-wave measurements with real-time source parameter estimation and ground-motion prediction equations (GMPEs).

Our results show that PLUM delivers consistently high alert accuracy (over 90% of stations correctly alerted from the start of its simulation), aided by the fine spatial grid in Tottori Prefecture. However, it can only initiate predictions after stations have measured strong motion, leading to shorter lead-times and a larger blind zone (exceeding 20 km in some cases).

By contrast, QuakeUp starts issuing forecasts earlier (only a few seconds after the origin time) because it rapidly estimates earthquake location and magnitude from P-wave data. This approach can yield longer lead-times (up to about 12 s at 40 km) and a smaller blind zone, but its initial predictions can be unstable until source parameters converge. In our simulations, QuakeUp’s accuracy dropped briefly to 64% before stabilizing at 100% around 12 s, underscoring the trade-off between quickness and early-forecast reliability.

Despite these differences, both systems ultimately provided high-fidelity alerts to most sites in Tottori Prefecture. For a moderate or large earthquake, PLUM’s immediate reliance on observed intensities can yield robust snapshots of ongoing shaking (especially when a dense station network is available) whereas QuakeUp’s hybrid design can extend warnings to areas that have yet to be shaken, given fast and accurate source parameter estimation. Overall, these results illustrate complementary strengths: PLUM excels at purely measurement-based intensity forecasting, while QuakeUp can deliver earlier alerts over broader regions, provided it can handle uncertainties in real-time magnitude and location estimates. Both approaches therefore have a potential role in future EEW implementations, depending on network density, regional needs, and operational priorities for alert speed versus stability.

Methods

This section describes and compares the methodological frameworks of the two Earthquake Early Warning (EEW) approaches examined in this study: (1) the Propagation of Local Undamped Motion (PLUM) system, in the modified version proposed by Kagawa2and (2) the QuakeUp algorithm, a hybrid method developed by Zollo et al.3. Although both aim to provide rapid alerts of impending strong ground motion, each uses different strategies and data requirements.

PLUM system

Underlying principle and original implementation

PLUM1,28,29is a pure impact-based EEW approach that predicts earthquake shaking strictly from observed seismic intensities. Rather than estimating source parameters such as location or magnitude, PLUM measures ground motion at each station and propagates these intensities outwards in space and time. One key assumption is that seismic intensity does not change significantly over short distances. Consequently, if a station in the network records a seismic intensity above a certain threshold, neighboring grid points and stations within a defined radius are assumed to experience the same level of shaking.

This original approach performs optimally when a very dense seismic network is available, as highly clustered stations track the progression of shaking more accurately in real time. However, networks often include data gaps—especially in mountainous or remote areas—making it challenging to propagate intensity effectively in those regions.

Modified version by Kagawa (2021)

Recognizing the limitations of the original PLUM method, Kagawa2 introduced modifications to capture the seismic intensity distribution tailored for Tottori Prefecture, a region in western Japan where station coverage is heterogeneous due to mountainous terrain. The modified version seeks to address two main issues:

Overestimation of Intensities. When the measured intensity at one station is simply assigned to nearby locations, there is a risk of inflating the shaking level for uninstrumented sites if the actual ground motion weakens with distance.Delayed Alerts. Reliance on recorded peak shaking alone can lead to late warnings, because the system waits for S-wave (or damaging wave) arrivals at each station.

QuakeUp algorithm

Hybrid impact-based method

QuakeUp3 adopts a hybrid approach, combining both real-time source parameter estimation (location, magnitude) and observed P-wave amplitudes at local stations. This hybrid strategy aims to overcome the primary limitation of pure impact-based systems (that is, having to wait for ground shaking to be measured at multiple stations) by simultaneously forecasting ground motion over a wider area based on rapid, evolving source estimates.

Ground motion prediction equation (GMPE)

Once the system has inferred real-time estimates of the earthquake’s location and magnitude, a regional ground motion prediction equation (GMPE) is used to forecast shaking at stations or areas yet to experience strong ground motion. In Japan, QuakeUp commonly employs the GMPE by Si and Midorikawa33which correlates earthquake size and distance to an expected PGV (or other intensity measures). Thus, QuakeUp can issue alerts to regions ahead of the wavefront, augmenting or replacing the purely measurement-based local predictions as more information becomes available.

Key distinction of QuakeUp method from PLUM method is about the source-based component, i.e. QuakeUp incorporates magnitude and location estimates to predict shaking at not-instrumented sites before the waves arrive and hybrid data usage, i.e. in QuakeUP the observed P-wave amplitudes refine forecasts at triggered stations, while GMPE-based predictions extend coverage to the surrounding area.

Overall, these two methods offer complementary approaches to EEW. PLUM better adapts in situations where station density is high and source complexity might confound traditional source-based methods, while QuakeUp can issue alerts to a broader area more quickly by leveraging early estimates of earthquake source parameters (see Table 2).

In the following sections, we detail how both algorithms were configured and tested retrospectively for the 2016 Mjma 6.6 Central Tottori earthquake, providing a direct performance comparison in terms of alert timing and accuracy of predicted shaking.

Data

The Central Tottori earthquake occurred on October 21, 2016, with magnitude of 6.6 (Mjma). Situated in Tottori Prefecture, western Japan, this event caused significant structural damage but, fortunately, no direct fatalities16. Several factors make this earthquake a compelling test case for comparing Earthquake Early Warning (EEW) systems. Issues observed during the 2016 event (particularly regarding late or limited alerts in mountainous regions) motivated the development of the modified PLUM algorithm by Kagawa2. The Tottori Prefecture is the only one in Japan that applies the PLUM to its managed seismometer network. Municipal seismic intensity meters are mainly installed in city or town halls and, therefore, they tend to be concentrated in densely populated areas. This implies that large regions in the prefecture are poorly instrumented because of the presence of sparsely populated mountains.

The Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) manages EEW operations and issued an alert 12 s after the earthquake’s origin time (JMA, 2016; see link). The timing of that alert and the relatively sparse network coverage highlight key challenges for both source-based and impact-based EEW methods in this prefecture.

Although JMA operates six real-time EEW stations in Tottori, a total of 32 stations recorded the event across the prefecture (and an additional two stations in the neighboring Hyogo Prefecture). For our comparative analysis of PLUM and QuakeUp, we focus on the 32 Tottori stations to maintain consistency with the prefecture-based PLUM grid and to evaluate performance within a single administrative region.

Despite the relatively high total number of stations, their distribution is uneven. Data spatial gaps can delay or diminish the accuracy of intensity propagation (for PLUM) and real-time source parameter estimation (for QuakeUp). Thus, the network’s density (and crucially, the station geometry) plays a significant role in how quickly and accurately an EEW system can issue alerts.

In Japan, each prefecture typically manages its own grid mesh for implementing PLUM. As a result, the Central Tottori event is evaluated using Tottori’s prefecture-specific setup. While in theory the nearby Hyogo stations could inform alerts for bordering areas, they fall under a different prefectural grid, so the PLUM simulations for Tottori exclude these additional stations. This organizational structure reflects JMA’s operational framework, wherein each region is responsible for its own warning dissemination.

By focusing on the 2016 Central Tottori earthquake and the 32-station network, this study investigates how each EEW strategy responds to both the advantages (relatively well-instrumented lowlands) and drawbacks (unmonitored areas) of Tottori’s monitoring infrastructure. The analysis provides insight into how future network expansions or algorithmic refinements could enhance the speed and reliability of earthquake early warnings in similarly challenging settings.
