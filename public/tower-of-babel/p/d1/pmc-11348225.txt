Revealing the architecture of the membrane-bound Flotillin cage assembly
Authors: Brett M. Collins
Published: 2024

Across all domains of life, cells have evolved mechanisms to partition and compartmentalize their lipid membranes. The Stomatin, Prohibitin, Flotillin, and HflK/C (SPFH) family of proteins are found in bacterial and eukaryotic species including humans (1), implicated in the formation of distinct membrane microdomains involved in processes as diverse as clathrin-independent endocytosis (2), mitochondrial function (3, 4), intracellular trafficking (5, 6), and touch sensation (7, 8). In most cases the precise functions of these proteins are still unclear, but they share conserved properties of oligomerization into large cage-like assemblies and posttranslational lipidation that enhances their membrane interactions. In mammals, there are at least 10 SPFH family members, Prohibitin-1/2, Erlin-1/2, Podocin, Stomatin, Stomatin-like-2/3, and Flotillin-1/2 (also called Reggie-2/1) (9) and in bacteria, there are Flotillin-like YuaG and HflK/C proteins (1, 10). These all contain N-terminal SPFH1 and SPFH2 domains followed by coiled-coil domains of varying lengths. With the notable exception of Flotillin-1/2, other family members also possess an N-terminal type-II transmembrane helix allowing for membrane anchoring (Fig. 1A).

The structural basis for the assembly of these enigmatic proteins is slowly beginning to emerge, and in this issue of PNAS Fu and MacKinnon reveal an exciting new structure of the heteromeric complex of human Flotillin-1 and Flotillin-2 that provides fresh insights into the SPFH protein family (11). The first remarkable technical aspect of this study is that the endogenous complex was purified directly from either cultured human embryonic kidney (HEK) cells or isolated human red blood cells without requiring recombinant expression. Using different methods to produce either membrane-derived vesicles or secreted exosomes, large dome-shaped protein structures were observed by cryoelectron microscopy (cryoEM) in a small percentage of vesicles. Exhaustive data collection allowed enough particles to be imaged for single-particle structure determination (aided by symmetry averaging) at a final resolution sufficient to distinguish the similar Flotillin-1 and Flotillin-2 proteins in the cryoEM maps. The final structure reveals a fascinating cage-like assembly with a narrow “cytoplasmic” facing end and a wider membrane-attached base (Fig. 1B).

The Flotillin-1 and Flotillin-2 proteins form a circular cage structure with 22 heterodimers arranged in a ring with 44-fold pseudosymmetry. The membrane-attached surface is composed of the 44 separate SPFH1 and SPHF2 domains, the wall is formed by adjacent extended α-helices of each monomer (the CC1 domain), and this structure is capped by a short C-terminal region that forms a 44-stranded β-barrel. The SPFH1 domain is highly enriched in aromatic sidechains and lipid-modified residues, including myristylation at Gly2 (14) and sites of palmitoylation, that altogether lead to the strong membrane attachment even in the absence of the type-II membrane helix present in most other SPFH proteins. Strikingly, the helices that create the wall of the Flotillin cage possess repeat sequences but do not conform to the typical heptad repeat pattern of a coiled-coil structure; instead, they are stapled together by alternating charged residues that provide complementary electrostatic contacts between adjacent subunits (Fig. 1C). In many micrographs, the Flotillin cages were also found to bind a second membrane vesicle via the narrow C-terminal end. Although the resolution in this region of the structure precluded a detailed analysis of how these membrane interactions occur it suggests the intriguing possibility that Flotillin cages may be able to link adjacent organelles. One final observation was that Flotillin cages induced localized membrane curvature, promoted by the SPFH1 domain which encompasses ~700 Å2 of membrane, and the authors speculate that this may contribute to the mechanical properties of the bilayer and by extension play a role in mechanosensory channel activity as suggested by SPFH function in touch sensitivity (7, 8).

In addition to the cryoEM structure of the Flotillin-1/Flotillin-2 complex, Fu and MacKinnon used AlphaFold to predict the structures of related SPFH proteins including Stomatin, Erlin, and Prohibitin homologs (11). Overall, this suggests a common organization to these proteins, with differing cage dimensions depending primarily on the lengths of the C-terminal α-helical domains. Indeed, within the vesicle preparations used to solve the Flotillin structure, other dome-shaped structures were observed that match the expected dimensions of the Stomatin and Stomatin-like 2 proteins. Prior to the current work, the only other high-resolution SPFH complex structure was of the bacterial HflK–HflC assembly (10). A second lower-resolution structure of the HflK–HflC complex confirmed that the proteins are organized into a 24-subunit cage (with 12 copies of each), which is also able to encapsulate four copies of a hexameric AAA ATPase FtsH (15) (Fig. 1D). The major shell of the large ribonucleoprotein Vault complex also shares a related structure composed of two opposing cages with 39-fold rotational symmetry (Fig. 1D). As this work goes to press, two other preprints further add to our understanding of the SPFH family. A similar Flotillin-1/Flotillin-2 complex is reported by Lu and colleagues, with the same architecture and stoichiometry (16). In addition, extending into the cell itself, a low-resolution structure of mitochondria-associated Prohibitin-1/Prohibitin-2 was determined by cryoelectron tomography, revealing a 22 subunit cage (with 11 copies of each) and highlighting its broader organization within the mitochondrial matrix (17).

The various functions of the Flotillins and SPFH proteins in membrane trafficking, signaling, and mechanosensation clearly depend on their oligomeric properties, their potential for mediating other protein interactions and clustering, and on their ability to integrate into lipid bilayers and promote the formation of membrane nanodomains. This new work on Flotillin combined with recent studies of other SPFH family members now provides a relatively comprehensive understanding of the basic principles of SPFH protein assembly into oligomeric membrane-attached cages. These insights now provide a platform for understanding the basis for the diverse physiological roles of SPFH proteins at a mechanistic level.
