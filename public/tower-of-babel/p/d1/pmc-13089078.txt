Defining culturally responsive sleep health care through Australian First Nations leadership
Authors: Bushra Nasir, Roslyn Von Senden, Wayne Williams, Timothy C. Skinner, Romola S. Bucks, Yaqoot Fatima
Published: 2026

Understanding sleep health from First Nations perspectives

Sleep is often framed as a universal biological necessity, yet there is no universally accepted definition of sleep health. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples (hereafter respectfully referred to as First Nations peoples), the concept of sleep health is shaped by cultural knowledges, spiritual connections, and, regrettably, by histories of displacement, ongoing inequities, and intergenerational trauma.1 For many First Nations peoples, healthy sleep extends beyond rest and restoration. Sleep, particularly dreaming, is an opportunity to connect with ancestors, gain inspiration for art, songs, and dances, and strengthen connections with culture and traditions.2 Sleep health is deeply intertwined with connection to Country, kinship systems, and collective wellbeing. Despite this, mainstream sleep science continues to privilege Western biomedical norms while overlooking the cultural, social, and environmental contexts that shape how First Nations communities understand and experience sleep health. This gap has real consequences: limiting the relevance of sleep research; constraining clinical practice; and perpetuating inequities in sleep health and associated outcomes for First Nations peoples.3

Unpacking culturally responsive sleep health care

The term “culturally responsive care” is widely used in health research and service delivery.4,5 However, in the context of sleep health care, this concept remains underexplored and undefined. Over the past six years, the Let's Yarn About Sleep (LYAS) program, Australia's largest community sleep health program, has worked with First Nations community members to define and unpack culturally responsive sleep health care.6,7 Shaped by conversations with more than 600 Australian First Nations community members, and guided by community leaders, including co-authors Roslyn von Senden, a Kalkadoon woman, and Uncle Wayne Williams, a Wakka Wakka man, we present a First Nations endorsed definition of culturally responsive sleep health care. In proposing this definition, we aim to reorient the sleep health field toward honouring First Nations' lived experiences and knowledge systems and acknowledging the role of social and environmental factors in sleep health.“Culturally responsive sleep health care recognises sleep as a deeply personal experience shaped by culture, community, spirituality, environment, social contexts, and physiology, and delivers evidence-based care that is grounded in the lived realities of the people it serves.”

Integrating this First Nations-led definition into sleep health research and clinical care offers a pathway to more equitable, relevant, and impactful service delivery. This definition also challenges the sleep health field to expand its understanding of what shapes sleep health, who defines “healthy” sleep, and how care should be delivered. It invites sleep science and care to move beyond deficit-based narratives to recognise the resilience, knowledge, and strengths within First Nations communities.

Implementing culturally responsive sleep health care in practice

To implement this definition in practice, the LYAS group has developed a framework (Fig. 1) that provides guiding values and principles for culturally responsive sleep health care. This framework offers a way to think about sleep health that moves beyond a purely clinical or biomedical lens and centres how sleep is understood and experienced within First Nations communities. At the heart of the framework is sleep health equity. Surrounding this core is a reminder that the impact of sleep extends beyond physical health and is deeply connected with social-emotional and spiritual well-being. As a result, sleep health cannot be reduced to symptoms, devices, or diagnoses alone.

The second circle describes the values that should sit at the centre of culturally responsive sleep health care. These include respect, truth, reciprocity, integrity, innovation, reflexivity, trust, and partnership. Together, these values guide how we work, not just what we deliver. They remind us that relationships, accountability, and shared decision-making are as important as technical and clinical expertise. Arguably, these values should sit at the centre of culturally responsive care in all care fields.

The outer circle shows how these values translate into practice. This reflects the real-world practice of culturally responsive sleep health care: prioritising First Nations voices, strengthening local capacity, embedding community governance, employing strengths-based approaches, responding to local priorities, integrating cultural knowledges, ensuring community leadership, and working toward sustainable success.

The way forward for sleep health care

Culturally responsive sleep health care is not a niche concern. It is a necessary evolution to improve sleep health care pathways for all. Taken together, the definition of culturally responsive sleep health care and the guiding values encourage us to view sleep health not merely as a clinical problem to be fixed, but as part of a broader narrative of culture, connection, and self-determination.
