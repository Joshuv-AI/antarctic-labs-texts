Investigating the effects of co-supplementation with alpha-linolenic acid and L-carnitine on inflammatory status, oxidative stress, clinical symptoms, mental health and quality of life in women with migraine: a protocol for a randomized, triple-blind, placebo-controlled trial
Authors: Sahar Golpour-hamedani, Mohammad Bagherniy, Fariborz Khorvash, Awat Feizi, Manoj Sharma, Gholamreza Askari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine is a severe neurological disorder that is recognized as one of the most common debilitating diseases worldwide. Although the exact cause of migraine is not known, research suggests that inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and insufficient nutrients may contribute to its development. Studies indicate that nutrition-based approaches are safer and more cost-effective strategies for managing migraine symptoms compared to medication. In this regard, the impact of nutrition, as a complementary medicine, is largely attributed to that of certain nutrients on inflammation and mitochondrial function. It is hypothesized that alpha-linolenic acid and L-carnitine, which possess anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, may be synergically beneficial for migraine patients. Therefore, this study will be conducted to assess the efficacy of alpha-linolenic acid and L-carnitine co-supplementation in patients with migraine.MethodsThis is a parallel, randomized, triple-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial, in which 80 women aged 20 to 50 years with migraine will be assigned to receive either intervention group (n = 40) receiving both 1000 mg/day flaxseed oil and 500 mg/day L-carnitine simultaneously for 12 weeks, or control group (n = 40) receiving both 1000 mg/day paraffin oil and 500 mg/day maltodextrin as the placebos for the same duration. The primary outcomes include changes in clinical symptoms of migraine, including frequency, severity, and duration of attacks, serum levels of C–reactive protein (CRP), total antioxidant capacity (TAC), nitric oxide (NO), malondialdehyde (MDA), and superoxide dismutase (SOD). Secondary outcomes include mental health, sleep quality, and quality of life (QOL).DiscussionIn this study, we aim to investigate the potential benefits of combining alpha-linolenic acid and L-carnitine as a treatment option for migraine sufferers. Migraine, characterized by recurrent severe headaches, affects a significant portion of the population and can significantly impact an individual's quality of life. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Migraine is a common, painful, and in severe chronic cases, debilitating disorder characterized by episodic activation of the trigeminovascular system [1]. Typically, this condition is defined by one-sided and throbbing headaches, often accompanied by symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia. These signs usually last 4 to 72 h [2, 3]. These headaches affect approximately 14–15% of the world's population [4]. The prevalence of migraine has been reported to be 12–14% in Europe and America [5], and 7–18% in the adult Iranian population [6], and is three times more common in women than men [7].

The occurrence of migraine is a complex cycle of neurotransmitter release, neuronal activation, oxidative stress, vasodilatation, and release of inflammatory factors [8]. Although the primary etiology of migraine remains unclear, the pathogenesis of migraine attacks is influenced by a variety of factors, including genetic and environmental factors. The main causes of migraine include mutations in the MTHFR gene, atypical vitamin D levels, the presence of inflammatory mediators in the environment of nerves and cerebrospinal fluid, reduced serotonin levels, impaired mitochondrial functionality, and reduced metabolic enzyme activity [9, 10]. Among the factors related to nutrition, inflammation, and oxidative stress are the main causes of migraines. Oxidative damage to the endothelium can trigger inflammatory reactions in the meninges, leading to dilation of the large cerebral blood vessels. This phenomenon is correlated with increased pain levels in migraine sufferers [11]. Furthermore, oxidative stress, which is closely associated with migraines, disrupts the normal function of antioxidant enzymes, such as extracellular superoxide dismutase [10].

The main objectives of migraine treatment are pain relief, restoring function, and reducing headache frequency. First-line preventive treatments to decrease migraine intensity and frequency include beta-blockers (propranolol), calcium channel blockers (flunarizine), anticonvulsants, and serotonin reuptake inhibitors [12]. However, these are often non-specific with poor efficacy, tolerability, and adherence [13]. In addition to the aforementioned preventative treatments, studies have demonstrated certain nutrients like B vitamins, magnesium, coenzyme Q10, L-carnitine, and vitamin D can help prevent migraines [10]. Omega-3 fatty acids may also be effective for relieving migraine headaches due to their anti-inflammatory properties [14].

Despite the anti-inflammatory effects of omega-3 fatty acids, only a few studies have examined their clinical effects on migraine symptoms and generated conflicting results. In a study of migraine patients by Ramsden et al., increasing omega-3 fatty acid intake and decreasing omega-6 fatty acid intake were associated with reduced migraine intensity and frequency, as well as improved quality of life [15]. However, Fayazi et al. were unable to demonstrate a connection between omega-3 supplementation and migraine symptoms [16]. Their study compared sodium valproate plus 1 g omega-3 fatty acids to sodium valproate alone and found no significant difference between the groups in migraine frequency or intensity [16].

It should be noted that to date all studies conducted in this area have utilized animal-derived omega-3 fatty acids (eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)). In recent years, attention has turned to plant-based omega-3 fatty acids (alpha-linolenic acid).

Alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) plays a role in cardiovascular health, modulating inflammatory responses, and central nervous system structure and function [17]. In the field of neurological diseases, few studies have examined ALA's effects on brain structure and function. While ALA may have protective neurological effects through anti-inflammatory and antidepressant properties [18]. Another factor involved in migraine pathogenesis is disruption of mitochondrial metabolism and energy homeostasis in the brain. Mitochondrial dysfunction leads to impaired oxygen metabolism and energy production, which has been linked to migraine pathogenesis [19]. L-carnitine (LC) is integral to mitochondrial function and is considered protective for brain tissue [20]. Studies have shown L-carnitine deficiency leads to reduced responsiveness to drug treatments for all headache types [21]. The role of L-carnitine in preventing and controlling many neurological and non-neurological diseases has been investigated. However, few studies have examined L-carnitine's effects on migraines. In a study by Tariqat et al., 12-week supplementation with 500 mg L-carnitine significantly reduced migraine frequency and severity. However, L-carnitine combined with magnesium (500 mg/day) was more effective for reducing symptoms than L-carnitine alone [22]. Given the importance of L-carnitine for brain function, special attention to intake of this essential nutrient in migraine patients seems necessary.

Studies have shown co-administration of omega-3 fatty acids and L-carnitine results in higher serum antioxidant enzyme levels compared to either alone, likely indicating a synergistic effect [23]. Animal studies show adding L-carnitine to an omega-3 rich diet increases both omega-3 storage and antioxidant properties [24]. Various studies indicate synergistic effects when combining omega-3 fatty acids with other nutrients or pharmaceuticals, including Vitamin C, Vitamin E, and niacin [25–27]. A study by Mohammadzadeh et al. found omega-3 fatty acids plus curcumin led to greater reductions in clinical migraine symptoms compared to either alone [28]. Given no studies have examined simultaneous ALA and L-carnitine supplementation, this study will investigate the effects of co-supplementing ALA and L-carnitine on inflammatory state, oxidative stress, clinical symptoms, mental health, and quality of life in women with migraine.

Trial design {8}

This study is a parallel, triple-blind, randomized, clinical controlled placebo trial that will enroll a total of 80 migraine women for 12 weeks. The primary outcomes are changes in migraine attack symptoms including headache frequency, duration, and severity; Migraine Headache Index Score (MHIS); Headache Diary Result (HDR); and serum levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), total antioxidant capacity (TAC), nitric oxide (NO), malondialdehyde (MDA), and superoxide dismutase (SOD). Secondary outcomes include mental health, sleep quality, and quality of life. The study protocol was designed based on the SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) 2013 checklist [29]. The protocol flowchart and schedule are shown in Fig. 1 and Table 1, respectively. Study results will be reported following Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [30]. Any protocol changes affecting participant safety or benefits require approval by the Department of Clinical Nutrition and Food Sciences at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences and the Medical Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Isfahan, Iran, before implementation. Subsequent modifications will be submitted to the Trials Journal (https://trialsjournal.biomedcentral.com). This study protocol was approved by the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences ethics committee (Approval No. IR.MUI.RESEARCH.REC.1401.406) and registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (ID: IRCT20121216011763N57).

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

In addition to standard treatment, patients will be assigned to one of two groups: the intervention group receiving simultaneously flaxseed oil and L-carnitine supplements, or the control group receiving placebos. The intervention group will receive one soft gel containing 1000 mg of flaxseed oil and 500 mg of L-carnitine per day, while the control group will receive placebos containing 1000 mg paraffin oil soft gel and 500 mg maltodextrin tablet per day for a duration of 12 weeks. Previous studies have shown that alpha-linolenic acid and L-carnitine are safe even at high doses such as 3000 mg/day flaxseed oil and 2000 mg/day L-carnitine, respectively [32, 33].

Intervention description {11a}

Patients in the intervention group will simultaneously receive a soft gel containing 1000 mg of flaxseed oil (providing 350 mg of ALA; Barij, Iran) and a 500 mg L-carnitine tablet (Behta darou, Iran) daily for 12 weeks. The control group will receive a matching placebo soft gel with 1000 mg paraffin oil and a 500 mg maltodextrin tablet for the same period. To ensure blinding, the flaxseed oil/L-carnitine and placebo packages will be identical in color and shape. An independent person will label the packages of flaxseed oil/L-carnitine and placebos as A or B without knowing the study details. Participants will take one soft gel and one tablet with their main meal without changing their diet or physical activity. Every four weeks, participants will receive new packages. Weekly follow-up calls and clinic visits will monitor side effects and adherence.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Participants have the freedom to exit the study voluntarily at any point, regardless of the cause. The researcher has the authority to remove participants from the trial if it is necessary to ensure their well-being or if they are unable or unwilling to proceed with the study. Additionally, the specific reasons behind each participant's withdrawal will be thoroughly elucidated in the study findings.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

At weeks 1, 4, and 8, patients will receive packs containing the LC/ALA supplements or placebo. Every 4 weeks, patients will return the packs with any remaining contents and report any side effects. Compliance will be monitored by calling patients weekly and counting returned packs at study completion. Compliance is calculated as: (packs taken/packs prescribed) × 100. Patients with less than 80% compliance (considered poor compliance) [34] will be excluded from the final study analysis.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Patients will continue receiving their standard medical care and treatment from a specialist physician throughout the study period. The only additional interventions will be the ALA/L-carnitine supplements or placebos assigned to each patient group. Patients are permitted to continue any appropriate concurrent care and maintain their regular care regimens during the study.

Provisions for post‑trial care {30}

At the conclusion of the 12-week trial period, patients will resume only their usual medical treatments. The administration of the ALA/L-carnitine supplements and placebos will be discontinued after the 12-week study duration.

Outcomes {12}

This study is a parallel, triple-blind, randomized, clinical controlled placebo trial investigating the effects of simultaneous supplementation of ALA and L-carnitine on clinical and psychological symptoms in migraine patients. The primary outcomes include changes in clinical symptoms of migraine attacks, including headache frequency, duration, and severity, Migraine Headache Index Score (MHIS), Headache Diary Score (HDR), serum levels of quantitative C-reactive protein (CRP), total antioxidant capacity (TAC), nitric oxide (NO), malondialdehyde (MDA) and superoxide dismutase (SOD). Secondary outcomes include mental health, sleep quality, and quality of life. A neurologist will evaluate the migraine symptoms, including the frequency, severity, and duration of headaches. Participants will also be required to answer questionnaires about the duration and frequency of their attacks during the intervention. Headache frequency will be documented by noting how often they occur each month. The intensity of pain will be measured using a visual analogue scale (VAS) that ranges from 0 to 10. Participants will be instructed to rate their pain as 0 if they experience no pain and 10 if they experience excruciating pain. The study will assess primary and secondary outcomes at the beginning and end of the intervention. Blood samples will be collected from the patients before the intervention and again after 12 weeks of supplementation with ALA and L-carnitine or placebos. These blood samples will be taken in the morning after a 12-h fasting period. After collection, the serum will be separated from the blood samples through centrifugation and stored in 0.5-ml microtubes. The serum samples will then be frozen at -80 °C until further analysis.

Sample size {14}

The sample size for the present study was calculated by the related statistician (A.F.) based on the severity of migraine attacks, as the main outcome of Hajihashemi et al.’s study [35], taking into account a confidence interval of 95% and a power of 80% (α = 0.05 and β = 0.2). Recruitment {15}

The study will involve 80 individuals suffering from migraine who will be selected from a neurology clinic associated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences in Isfahan, Iran. Patient eligibility will be assessed using the criteria for the diagnosis of migraine defined in the CHD-3. An experienced neurologist (F.K.) will assess migraine and differentiate it from the different types of headache, such as cluster headache (CH), tension headache (TTH), medication overuse headache (MOH), menstrual cycle headache, etc. Written consent will be obtained from all eligible participants who willingly choose to take part in the study.

Migraine diagnosis

Migraine diagnosis is carried out by a single neurologist (F.K.) according to the International Classification of Headache Disorder III (ICHD-3) [31]. ICDH-3 is the most well-known and newest diagnostic tool in the field of headache in clinical research. According to the ICDH-3 criteria, chronic migraine is diagnosed when the patient experiences at least 5 headache attacks meeting these criteria:

A) Headache attacks lasting 4–72 h (either untreated or with unsuccessful treatment).

B) Headaches have at least two of the following four characteristics:

1) Unilateral location.

2) Pulsating quality.

3) Moderate or severe pain intensity.

4) Aggravated by or avoidance of routine physical activity (e.g. walking or climbing stairs).

C) During headaches, at least one of the following symptoms:

1) Nausea and/or vomiting.

2) Sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia).

Sequence generation {16a}

The present study will be conducted as a randomized, triple-blind, placebo-controlled trial followed by a stratified randomization method. Stratified permuted block randomization will be used for randomization. Participants will be stratified by age (20–35 and 35–50 years). Participants will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive an intervention or placebo with a block size of 4. Random assignment will be done using computer-generated random numbers prepared by statisticians, and patients will be randomly divided into either the intervention group (LC plus ALA, n = 40) or the control group (placebos, n = 40) by one person, who is not involved in the study.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

To maintain blinding, packs of L-carnitine/ALA supplements and placebo will be identically labeled as A or B by an independent person not involved in the study. Patients, laboratory staff, and investigators will remain blinded to treatment assignments throughout the trial.

Sealed, opaque envelopes will be used for allocation concealment, so those assigning patients are unaware of the randomization order. The randomization codes will only be unlocked after all participants complete the full study protocol.

Implementation {16c}

A clinical research coordinator who operates independently will be responsible for assigning patients to either the intervention group or the placebo group. The process of creating the sequence for patient allocation will be supervised by a second researcher (M.B.).

Assignment of interventions: blinding

People who will be blind {17a}

This study will be conducted using a triple-blind design, ensuring that patients, laboratory staff, and investigators will be unaware of the assigned groups. Furthermore, bottles of flaxseed oil soft gels and L-carnitine tablets are packaged in the same color and shape as their placebo packages for blindness insurance.

Procedure for emergency unblinding if needed {17b}

The identities of the supplements will remain concealed until the final analysis of the study. If any instances of revealing the identities occur during the study, they will be documented and disclosed in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials, the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, and in the final study results.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Demographic variables

Data on demographic variables, such as age, anthropometric measurements (height and body weight), education level, medical history, medication and supplement use, marital status, migraine onset, family history of migraine, physical activity levels, and dietary intakes will be collected from each participant at baseline and after the intervention using the validated tools.

Dietary intake assessment

To assess the participants' dietary intake and subsequently the association between energy, macro- and micronutrient intake, and migraine, participants will complete a three-day dietary record that includes two weekdays and one weekend. Participants will receive instructions on how to accurately complete dietary records from a qualified dietitian who is blind to the study protocol. These records will be collected at baseline and again after 12 weeks of the intervention. The amount of each food is then converted into grams/day using Iranian Household Measures [36] and then converted into the value of nutrients and calories using Nutritionist 4 software (First Databank Inc., Hearst Corp., San Bruno, CA, 161 USA).

Physical activity assessment

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), a 7-day recall tool with confirmed validity and reliability in the Iranian population [37], will be used to determine the level of physical activity among study participants. Physical activity levels will be reported as metabolic equivalent hours per day (MET/h/day).

Anthropometric assessments

Body weight will be measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a Seca scale, with participants wearing minimal clothing. Height will be measured to the nearest 0.5 cm using a portable stadiometer, with participants standing without shoes. Body mass index (BMI) will be calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared.

Migraine symptoms assessment

Migraine characteristics will be assessed using a questionnaire regarding severity, frequency (number of attacks per month), and duration (average duration of headache attacks in a day per month) of the headache attacks. Headache severity will be rated on a visual analog scale (VAS) from 0–10, where 0 is no pain and 10 is unbearable pain. Patients will select a number to rate their pain severity [38]. The Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6) validated questionnaire will evaluate migraine disability. It consists of 5 questions assessing how often migraine impacts daily life, with response options of "never" (6 points), "rarely" (8 points), "sometimes" (10 points), "most of the time" (11 points), and "always" (13 points). Total scores indicate the degree of headache-related disability. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire have been confirmed by previous studies [39]. Assessment of quality of life (QOL)

The Migraine-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire (MSQ) will be used to assess the quality of life of individuals suffering from migraine. This questionnaire consists of 14 questions aimed at assessing the quality of life of the patient in the last month. Each question receives a score between one and six (1 = not always; 2 = rarely; 3 = sometimes; 4 = quite often; 5 = most of the time; 6 = always). These scores are then summed, resulting in a minimum total of 14 and a maximum total of 84. To convert the initial scores into a scale from zero to one hundred, we will follow this formula: (raw score—14) / 70 × 100. A higher score indicates a higher quality of life. The validity of this questionnaire in Iran was confirmed by a previous study [40].

Assessment of mental health status

To check the mental health status at the beginning and end of this study, the modified DASS-21 questionnaire for Iran will be used [41]. The DASS-21 questionnaire includes 21 questions divided into three subscales: stress, anxiety, and depression, each containing seven questions. Each question is rated on a scale representing zero (does not apply to me at all), 1 (applies to me to some extent), 2 (applies to me to a considerable extent), and 3 (completely applies to me). To accurately assess symptom severity, subscale scores should be doubled [42].

Regarding depression, scores 0–4, 5–6, 7–10, 11–13, and ≥ 14 indicate normal, mild, moderate, severe, and extremely severe depression, respectively. Regarding anxiety, scores 0–3, 4–5, 6–7, 8–9, and ≥ 10 represent normal, mild, moderate, severe, and extremely severe anxiety. Regarding stress, scores 0–7, 8–9, 10–12, 13–16, and ≥ 17 in this questionnaire indicate normal, mild, moderate, severe, and extremely severe stress, respectively.

Assessment of sleep quality

Previous studies have shown that there is a relationship between migraine severity and sleep quality [43]. Therefore, we will assess participants' sleep quality both before and after the intervention using the validated Pittsburg Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) for the Iranian population [44]. The PSQI consists of nine questions that assess various aspects of sleep patterns and mental quality, including bedtime, awake times, and sleep duration per day. Each question is rated on a scale of 0 to 3, where 0 means they have not occurred in the past month, 1 means less than once per week, 2 represents once or twice per week, and 3 indicates three times or more per week.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow‑up {18b}

To promote active participation in the study, the potential benefits of using Omega-3 and L-carnitine supplements to prevent migraine complications will be clearly explained to participants. Participants will also receive reminder phone calls and text messages for upcoming study visits to facilitate engagement. Furthermore, in the case of necessity, methods such as sending reminders to take the supplements and scheduling follow-up visits after the intervention will be employed to ensure participant retention.

Data management {19}

A researcher will be responsible for assessing the coding, security, and storage of the data. Data will be electronically stored, and the forms will be archived. In addition, a second check of the data entry and values ​​will be carried out. If a patient reports adverse events, further information will be collected to decide whether they should be excluded from the study. According to the criteria established by the Medical Ethics Committee, it is permissible to disclose whether the patient received LC/ALA or placebos.

Confidentiality {27}

Every participant will receive a distinct identification code for the study, which will be utilized to store research data. The research team will have the ability to access the list of codes throughout the study. After the study is finished, the principal investigator will document and protect the code list in accordance with research guidelines. No personal information of the participants will be disclosed in any published materials.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation, and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

At the beginning and end of the study, laboratory evaluations will be performed. After a 12-h fasting period, a 10-ml blood sample will be collected and then centrifuged for 10 min at room temperature at a speed of 2500 rpm. The serum samples will be divided into microtubes and immediately frozen at a temperature of -80°C. The measurement of total antioxidant capacity (TAC) will be conducted by utilizing the CUPric Reducing Antioxidant Capacity method. Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels will be determined using the Thiobarbituric acid reactive substance assay, while nitric oxide (NO) levels will be assessed using the Griess method. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) will be measured based on the ability of Mn-SOD to inhibit the conversion of resazurin to resorufin. The manufacturer's instructions provided by Kiazist Life Sciences (Iran) will be followed for all these measurements.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

We will employ SPSS version 21.0 to perform statistical analysis. We will consider a P value of less than 0.05 to be statistically significant. To assess the normality of the data distribution, we will use the one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For the missing values, we will apply the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis and the multiple imputation approach. The initial values ​​of qualitative and quantitative variables will be compared using the chi-square test and the t-test, respectively. Categorical variables will be presented as numbers and percentages, while metric data will be expressed as means and standard deviations. The differences between groups will be assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test and independent t-test. The differences within the group will be also assessed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test and paired t-test. To control for confounding factors, we will perform a repeated measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) by identifying those by comparing variables between groups at pre-test.

Interim analyses {21b}

There are currently no scheduled interim analyses.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

There are currently no scheduled subgroup analyses.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non‑adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

The data analysis will follow an ITT approach by including outcomes data from all enrolled participants, even if they withdrew or were excluded from the study. For any participants who discontinue or deviate from the protocol, detailed explanations will be provided in the study results, with a focus on documenting any reported adverse events or side effects. To account for any missing data points, multiple imputation methods will be utilized prior to analysis. The multiple imputation approach will allow the estimation of missing values based on other available data from the participant, in order to perform complete case analysis aligned with the ITT principles.

However, any dataset shared will not contain individual participant information that could compromise confidentiality, in order to protect participant privacy.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Previous studies have shown that alpha-linolenic acid and L-carnitine are safe even at high doses such as 3000 mg/day flaxseed oil and 2000 mg/day L-carnitine, respectively [32, 33]. When taking 4000 mg alpha-linolenic acid/day, only a few adverse events like increased total cholesterol levels were reported. Therefore, no toxicities or special side effects are to be expected when taking alpha-linolenic acid and L-carnitine in the dosages mentioned in this trial. However, if adverse events occur, it will be reported to the Ethics Committee of Isfahan Medical University to make a decision.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the effects of concomitant administration of ALA and L-carnitine on clinical and psychological symptoms in migraine patients. Given the widespread prevalence of migraine, it is crucial to explore novel approaches to migraine management that can potentially improve overall well-being and quality of life for those affected. Considering the proposed positive effects of omega-3 fatty acids and L-carnitine on neuronal function [45, 46], the results of this study could provide valuable insights for both migraine prevention and treatment.

Previous studies have shown significant health benefits of omega-3 supplements, particularly in neurological diseases such as autism, dementia, depression, and hyperactivity [46]. However, previous studies primarily examined the effects of animal omega-3 fatty acids and suggested that omega-3 might influence migraine symptoms.

Emerging research indicates the potential physiological importance of ALA across a variety of disorders and conditions including cardiovascular disease, autoimmunity, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and more [17]. Both experimental and human studies have confirmed the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of ALA [17]. Specifically, ALA has been shown to inhibit activation of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, a key regulator of inflammatory responses. It can modulate the production and activity of inflammatory mediators like cytokines and prostaglandins [47, 48]. ALA appears to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) while promoting anti-inflammatory cytokines [49]. It can also stimulate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), particularly PPAR-γ [47], leading to the downregulation of inflammatory genes and upregulation of anti-inflammatory genes.

Given the involvement of inflammation and oxidative stress in migraine pathogenesis, ALA may have protective effects on migraine symptoms and associated complications through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Moreover, emerging evidence indicates mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to migraine attack onset and progression. Mitochondria play a vital role in neuronal energy metabolism through ATP production. Thus, mitochondrial impairments can disrupt brain energy homeostasis, increasing migraine susceptibility [35]. Through L-carnitine's involvement in mitochondrial energy production, it may help restore cellular energy levels, which could be beneficial in migraine management [50]. Additionally, L-carnitine has shown potential neuroprotective effects by maintaining mitochondrial function and antioxidant activity, reducing inflammation, and promoting cell survival. These neuroprotective properties can potentially reduce the frequency and severity of migraines [21].

It is thought that using ALA and L-carnitine together may have synergistic effects in the management of migraines. By reducing oxidative stress, inflammation, and optimizing mitochondrial function, this combination could potentially address multiple underlying mechanisms of migraine.

While this will be the first randomized, triple-blind clinical trial investigating the efficacy of combined ALA and L-carnitine supplementation for migraine, some limitations should be acknowledged. Serum levels of ALA and L-carnitine will not be measured at baseline and study completion to definitively assess supplement adherence. Additionally, for ethical reasons, the effects of ALA plus L-carnitine as a standalone therapy cannot be evaluated, since it would be unethical to advise participants to stop standard medications. The study is also limited to a specific dose and ratio of the two supplements, and cannot determine optimal dosing.

Described here is the protocol for a triple-blind, placebo-controlled study design evaluating the effects of concurrent supplementation with ALA and L-carnitine on clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers, oxidative stress, and quality of life in migraine patients. Administration of 1000 mg/day flaxseed oil plus 500 mg/day L-carnitine for 12 weeks is expected to result in relief of migraine symptoms, along with reductions in inflammation and oxidative stress, and increased quality of life in migraine sufferers. If we can find substantial evidence of the beneficial effects of ALA and L-carnitine, this study may provide a safe and affordable strategy for reducing migraine complications.
