O²RDL-net for joint risk classification and delay forecasting in logistics systems using interaction amplified deep
Authors: Xiao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransportation and logistics systems face frequent delays and operational risks due to congestion, human factors, and rapidly changing conditions. Predicting delays and identifying risk levels in advance is therefore essential for reliable logistics planning. Most existing methods handle delay forecasting and risk classification separately and often fail to capture complex temporal and interaction-driven patterns. To overcome these limitations, this study proposes O²RDL-Net, a load-aware and interaction-amplified deep learning framework for joint risk classification and delay forecasting. The proposed model integrates higher-order operational indicators, including the Risk Accumulation Index, Delay Sensitivity Indicator, Human–Operational Interaction Score, and Logistics System Representation, to capture cumulative risk, short-term sensitivity, and system-level interactions. A spatio-temporal attention encoder with risk regime modulation is used to improve learning stability and generalization. Experimental results show that O²RDL-Net achieves superior performance compared to baseline models, with a mean absolute error of 2.05, RMSE of 3.01, and R² of 0.91 for delay forecasting. For risk classification, the model reaches an accuracy of 92%, precision of 91%, recall of 92%, and F1-score of 91%. Interpretability analysis using SHAP and local explanations confirms that the model relies on meaningful and domain-consistent features. Overall, O²RDL-Net provides an accurate, stable, and interpretable solution for real-world logistics risk and delay management.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Logistics Network, Supply Chain, Machine Learning, Deep Learning, Feature Construction, Forecasting, Risk PredictionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Transportation and logistics networks play a vital role in modern economies by enabling the timely movement of goods across regions and markets. However, these networks operate under increasing pressure due to rising demand, congestion, limited infrastructure capacity, and human–system dependencies1. As a result, delivery delays and operational risks have become frequent and costly, affecting service reliability, customer satisfaction, and overall supply chain efficiency. Accurately predicting delays and identifying risk conditions in advance is therefore a critical challenge for logistics planners and decision-makers2.

In recent years, data-driven and machine learning-based approaches have been widely adopted for logistics analytics, including travel time prediction, delay estimation, and disruption detection3. While traditional statistical and machine learning models provide useful baseline insights, they often struggle to capture non-linear relationships, temporal dependencies, and interaction effects among heterogeneous operational factors4,5. Moreover, many existing studies treat delay forecasting and risk classification as separate problems, even though both are driven by shared underlying dynamics such as congestion buildup6, operational overload, and human performance variability7. Recurrent networks, as well as transformer-based frameworks based on deep learning, have demonstrated potential in the logistics forecasting of sequential and temporal logistics data even though other applications such as agriculture targets8. Under complicated conditions, attention mechanisms help models to pay attention to important steps and features to enhance accuracy in prediction9. Nonetheless, most deep learning techniques have a strong dependence on raw input characteristics and do not have explicit ways to capture cumulative risk, sensitivity to short-term variations, and higher-order interactions between the operation subsystems and human factors10. Moreover, these models have a black-box character that prevents their implementation in the real logistics setting where explainability and trust are crucial11.

Another important limitation in existing work is the lack of joint learning models that explicitly and jointly consider the issue of risk classification and delay prediction12. The risk states and the delay magnitude are also closely related because the increasing operational risk can be a precursor of serious delays, whereas ongoing delays may indicate the occurrence of risk conditions13. Not taking this relationship into account will lead to disjointed predictions and interventions. Also, not many studies directly deal with the issue of temporal stability and calibration, which is essential to achieve the credibility of usage in dynamical logistics systems14.

To address these challenges, this study proposes O²RDL-Net, an Operational Overload-Responsive and Load-Aware Interaction-Amplified Deep Learning model to address these issues to perform joint risk classification and delay prediction. The developed strategy presents the higher-order constructed indicators, such as the Risk Accumulation Index (RAI), Delay Sensitivity Indicator (DSI), Human-Operational Interaction Score (HOIS), and the Logistics System Representation (LSR), to reflect the cumulative, short-term, and interaction-driven effects. Modeling the complex dependencies among the elements involves using a spatio-temporal attention encoder, interaction-aware cross-attention and risk regime modulation, and a temporal consistency constraint is adopted to ensure the stability of prediction results. The three-fold main contributions of this work are to be appreciated. First, a joint deep learning architecture is defined for predicting the risk level and delay magnitude, providing uniform and coordinated decision support. Secondly, two feature representations, namely load-aware and interaction-amplified, are proposed to reflect the real-world dynamics in logistics. Third, the model’s performance is widely analyzed in comparison with other models by experimental studies and the post-hoc interpretability analysis demonstrates that the proposed model can provide better performance, stable generalization and transparency in decision making compared with other models. All these ideas together present a feasible and reasonable way to deal with logistics issues with risk-problem consciousness and delay-consciousness.

State-of-the-art techniques in predictive risk assessment for supply chains

Initial research had determined that ML could be used to do such tasks, with classical algorithms as a starting point and more complex deep neural networks and hybrid models over time15. The research was initially done on classifying deliveries as on-time and delayed instead of predicting the degree of delay. The study noted that performance can be enhanced by integrating the data of enterprise logistics with external data (e.g. weather, location), but most models do not take into consideration the environmental factors that can be easily integrated. This gap highlights the development of simple predictive models to more holistic models in recent years16. Predicting delays ML was already used early in its development to demonstrate the worth of data-driven approaches. The regression and tree-based model on more than 1 million U.S. flights gave an R2 of 0.94 and RMSE around 12.5 min when the feature such as a late arrival or carrier and weather delays are taken into consideration17. Likewise, another study examined the aspects of flight delays and achieved 84.5% accuracy with the help of a Gradient Boosting classifier. These works, however, did not completely consider class imbalance in data of delay, which the subsequent works tried to eliminate18. Coming to an airline dataset, comparing 11 algorithms and discovering that k-NN and CART classifiers performed best still, the authors also found that their models were limited because of the lack of feature engineering and imbalanced classes19. Advances in this area soon added more substantial architecture and imbalance repair to enhance predictive capacity20.

The emergence of deep learning prompted scientists to use neural networks to make more precise and less sensitive predictions. The FCF-Net proposed which is a cost-sensitive deep model that was used to identify the patterns of train delay propagation using CNN and fully connected networks. They successfully applied CNN images and class weighted training to their model to surpass the traditional models in performance21. Advanced deep models have also come out in the air transport industry. In literature, a delay-propagation network is used in conjunction with a multi-label Random Forest, achieving a higher rate of prediction of the arrival delay (86.7%) due to an explicit concurrent delay cascading model across flights22. Going even more, with hybrid CNN-LSTM models that can learn spatio-temporal relations within flight networks with an accuracy of about 90%23. These deep learning methods signaled a change to networks of intricate interdependencies in logistic networks to graph structures in airline hubs to spatiotemporal dynamics in railway systems. Worth noting, numerous recent works are not only focused on accuracy, but also interpretability and compatibility with other methods. As an example, the interpretable deep learning-based supply chain demand forecasting with self-organizing maps with ANN and SHAP analysis to explain their results. This gave precise forecasts as well as pointing out macroeconomic and demand conditions that encouraged delivery delays24. The other trend is the combination of ML and the metaheuristics or optimization. Using a hybrid method, which is a Decision Tree regressor and Firefly Algorithm to select the features and optimize the hyperparameters. They used a large Dataco supply chain dataset (180k records) to model shipment delay durations and the results obtained a very high R2 which was significantly higher than the baseline regressors25. Application of Firefly Algorithm enhanced the generalizability as it reduced overfitting. This shows the advantage of hybrid ML methods in dealing with logistics data complexity26.

The integration of deep learning with other innovative approaches has been shown to be useful in real-time and proactive risk management. Introducing a hybrid model which combines deep neural networks and reinforcement learning to classify supply chain delivery risk27. The study initially optimized CNN, LSTM, Bi-LSTM and XGBoost models and discovered stacked and bidirectional LSTMs to be generalized well when optimizing predictive late orders. Based on them, they launched a proximal policy optimization agent that considers the output of the deep model as its state. The resulting DL-RL hybrid obtained higher F1-score in differentiating between on-time and late deliveries, which is far higher than in individual learners28. Logistic areas and stress situations have also been implemented using deep learning. To predict risks in shipments during COVID-19 disruptions, we examined different DL models. They trained recurrent networks (LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU) and a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) on binary risk classification and evaluated six classifiers on the classifier trained features29. The TCN-based model on an export dataset of the pandemic era was able to predict with success rates of close to 100% whether a shipment would leave under restrictions. This level of performance, although potentially resilient, begs the question of overfitting to the exceptional circumstances of COVID30. Elsewhere, not to mention the crucial area, ML automated to anticipate seaport disruption threats caused by natural hazards. They tested 24 AutoML pipelines on a global port dataset of 2010–2019 and discovered that wide neural networks achieved the highest accuracy. Their model emphasized their key features of hazard severity, recovery time which determines whether port operations will experience big delays or not. This is useful information with regards to port resilience; however, the researchers acknowledge that the data is not detailed enough and suggest that using a more detailed and comprehensive multi-port interaction data31. would be helpful.

These collectively show that deep models can effectively predict the risk of disruption of a transport network and supply chain when trained on data relevant to a particular domain, such as a representation of extreme events. It has also been observed that there is a rise in the research on delay prediction in different modes of transport. In this research, the authors present a visual servo predictive control (VSPC) scheme based on neurodynamic for omnidirectional logistics robots to solve the nonlinearities and physical/visual constraints. The approach enables smooth motion and constraint satisfaction with quasi-min–max MPC with LMI optimization, which has been validated by experiments conducted on the real world. ML in the context of the public transit of ferry boats, combining weather and ridership data with general transit feed specification schedules. On two case studies (Sydney and NYC) they discovered that, although both SVR and deep neural nets were good in city-specific tasks, the Gradient Boosting machines had the best tradeoff between accuracy and efficiency33. A multi-task Temporal CNN (MT2CN) in the roads to simultaneously forecast traffic emissions and travel delays on the intersections of urban areas. Their model outdid single-output models because the interdependent nature of congestion on delay and pollution was learnt, to ensure more precise and comprehensive predictions to traffic management. They also used SHAP explainability to enable traffic engineers to learn about contributing factors34.

Recent developments in the railway field address the problem of delay propagation on the network level. Multivariate Event Hypergraph Diffusion to predict the delay of trains in regions. Through their representation of coordinated train schedules as a mixed hypergraph and diffusion convolution-based approach, they were able to model high-order spatiotemporal correlation between multiple lines of train schedules35. Evidently, in all types of transport, including the air, sea, road, and rail, society has been shifting towards the more customized deep learning techniques that consider the specifics of each of the networks. Supply chain resilience and risk mitigation through predictive analytics is also another direction worth mentioning. The case studies in the fields of Ecommerce36, pharmaceuticals, agriculture37, automotives38 and energy39 with five deep learning models (ANN, CNN, RNN, LSTM, GRU)40. DL models forecasted demand or logistics risks within each industry, and they were frequently more accurate than the conventional ones41. An example is that CNN had 89.3% accuracy in a pharmaceutical logistics case (avoiding stock-outs), and GRUs reduced errors in forecasts of food and automotive supply. These outcomes reveal the flexibility of DL in any supply chain setting42. Nevertheless, both models were fitted to a given industry dataset, hence there might be a lack of generalization. A big data strategy, developing a unified ML pipeline of supplier delay risk to a multinational manufacturer. These researchers used a combination of big data architecture and ML models to make predictions of late deliveries of supplies in an electronics supply chain. They did not only report the accuracy but also predicted the financial implications of errors in the predictions on the cost of inventory. They tested their model with real factory data to provide realistic risk estimation without the need for the conventional, yet unrealistic assumption of fixed lead times43. The practical orientation (predictions/expenditure) is a sign of maturity of the discipline to be used in decision-making.

To address these shortcomings, more modern studies have turned to the use of more advanced architecture such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs), convolutional neural networks (CNNs), attention-based transformers, graph neural networks and even AutoML systems. These new approaches improve predictive accuracy through directly incorporating the domain specific knowledge like transport schedules, supply chain structures and operational interactions among different modes into models45. Through this technical development there has come an increased focus on practical usefulness, especially about model interpretability. Model decisions based on trust, SHAP and LIME have become popular techniques to make more transparent decisions about the model, and to make the model more reliable46.

Despite these advances, however, there are several critical issues that remain to be addressed. Much of the literature available either discusses the detection of a delay or considers only simple regression problems, without considering the severity of the risk or quantifying the amount of uncertainty, considering the differences by aspects that are displayed in Table 2. While several models distinguish between late and on-time deliveries, relatively few are attempting to predict the actual number of delays and the impact downstream. Moreover, other factors, such as weather and economic variables, are not commonly applied and their addition could significantly improve prediction accuracy47. The problem of event class imbalance is still present, though these techniques like resampling and cost-sensitive learning have been adopted, they are not always implemented, thus causing bias prediction. Some hybrid and attention-based models which are domain-specific without features of interaction consciousness, and without time consistency. Also, interpretability is often viewed as a post-hoc procedure and not as a part of the modeling procedure. There are new avenues that are beginning to incorporate explainability and causal reasoning, but it’s not something that’s done regularly in business.

Proposed research methodology

This study proposes a Load-Aware Interaction-Amplified Deep Learning framework (O²RDL-Net) for joint risk classification and delay forecasting in logistics networks. The framework shown in Fig. 1, is designed to capture operational overload dynamics, temporal propagation effects, and human–system interactions within a unified architecture.

Methodological Framework Overview of the proposed architecture integrates risk-relevant inputs to dual prediction heads for risk classification and delay estimation.

Unlike conventional pipelines that treat risk and delay as independent problems, the proposed model jointly learns both tasks by integrating shared spatio-temporal representations and task-specific output heads. As illustrated in the proposed architecture, raw operational signals are first transformed into load-aware embeddings, which are subsequently processed through spatio-temporal self-attention and interaction-aware cross-attention mechanisms. A risk regime modulation module further adapts internal representations to different operational states, while a temporal consistency constraint enforces stability across time. Finally, post-hoc interpretability modules (LIME and SHAP) are employed to ensure transparent decision support for both classification and regression outputs.

Data preprocessing and input representation

The data that was utilized in this research based on real-world logistics processes that were recorded off an integrated logistics network that was happening in Southern California. It covers a period of three years between January 2021 and January 2024 and represents multifaceted dynamics in surfaces with high traffic and multimodality in the transport path. It is obtained at an hourly resolution and is provided using GPS trackers, IoT sensors, warehouse management systems, and external providers and covers the modes of trucking, rail and drone-delivery. Overall, the dataset consists of more than 210,000 shipment-level records containing telemetry of vehicles, their working condition, environmental conditions, and human behavior. Data has all been anonymized to ensure sensitive data has been saved without compromising the richness of analysis.

A time-based data splitting strategy was used on all modeling tasks to maintain the temporal integrity and ensure that there is no leakage of information. The data was chronologically divided into three subsets: training set contained the data between January 2021 and December 2022 (70% of the data was used to learn the model), the validation set covered January 2023-June 2023 (15%), and the testing set was July 2023 to January 2024 (15%), which is chronologically consistent, without the risk of temporal leakage. This sequential splitting can provide that the models are trained on past events and assessed on future time intervals, which is in line with the real-life deployment setting. Some of the most important target variables considered in the dataset are the Delivery Time Deviation, the probability of delay, likelihood of disruption, and risk classification, as reported in Table 3, which could be used to run both a regression and a classification activity. Multimodal and multi-source character of it improves the broadening of findings and predetermines its suitability in predictive modeling in the logistics risk and delay forecasting.

Class distribution & imbalance handling

The distribution of the sample size was also extremely skewed with 75% of the samples in the High-Risk category, 15% in the Moderate Risk category, and 10% in the Low-Risk category. This is likely to be a bias in the prediction towards the dominant class, and it was challenging model training with such a skewed distribution. To address this a preprocessing approach was used which entailed class-balancing by means of random under sampling of the majority class and oversampling the minority classes49. This resulted in an even distribution among the three risk groups (~ 33% in each group) as illustrated in Fig. 2. This enabled the model to learn uniformly in all the classes, improve the generalization and make equitable decisions in various levels of risk.

Model operation

The proposed architecture considers the combined model of risk classification and delay forecasting based on a common spatio-temporal representation. This model works by encoding heterogeneous operational inputs into load-sensitive embeddings, then propagating temporal dependencies using attention-based networks, enhancing significant interaction of features, and finally making task-specific predictions by using two output heads51. In contrast to traditional deep learning pipelines, the given architecture incorporates explicit interaction-aware attention, risk regime modulation, and temporal consistency regularization, which provides strong prediction in the conditions of changing and overloaded logistics. Performance evaluation measures

The proposed model is evaluated based on task specific metrics that would be suitable towards the objective of classification of risks and prediction of delays. The classification task model performances that can capture the level of overall correctness, class-specific reliability and robustness in the presence of imbalance between classes are Accuracy, Precision, Recall and F1-score. For regression tasks, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) is used to estimate the absolute error of prediction, while the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) is used to estimate the error in predicting variance, and in the process, goodness of fit is tested using the coefficient of determination (R2). For the sake of further gauging the reliability of a delay forecasting in case of the presence of operational variability, both Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) and symmetric MAPE (sMAPE) are added, which provides an error interpretation without scale59. All metrics are generated on held out test data, are unbiased and can be compared with baseline models.

Results confirm the proposed dual-task framework as an alternative solution to the traditional ones, and to integrate the domain knowledge in the field of supply chain forecasting, which will aid in the construction of more resilient and responsive logistic networks. To achieve a comprehensive comparison of contemporary models, a visualization of the results of the logistic and supply chain models on the logistic models data set and the supply chain data set is shown in Fig. 11, which shows significant findings of disparity in the sophistication of architecture, feature engineering and predictive performance as reported in Table 11. Moderate accuracy (F1: 82%, R2: 0.67) was provided by classical and shallow models such as MLP and decision tree hybrids, but they failed to offer strong learning in terms of temporal or risk-sensitivity. Deep temporal models including TCNs and LSTMs also outperformed by capturing temporal features more effectively (Acc: 8488, R2: 0.71), but they were restricted in their interpretability because they depended on standard operational variables. Recent literature that included explainability and hybrid attention mechanisms added interpretative value to the results but did not perform as well with increased error (MAE: 3.83). The proposed model, conversely, demonstrated much better accuracy (Acc: 92, F1: 91), regression strength (R2: 0.91, MAE: 2.05) due to its dual-task design and the presence of such special features as DSI, RAI, HOIS. These results put proposed model as a better predictor and interpretative framework of delay and risk prediction in cases of operational complexity.

Conclusion

In this study, O²RDL-Net, a load-aware and interaction-amplified deep learning approach for joint risk classification and delay forecasting in transportation and logistics networks is presented. The proposed model incorporates constructed indicators like the Risk Accumulation Index, Delay Sensitivity Indicator, Human–Operational Interaction Score and Logistics System Representation to effectively capture both the short and long-term operational dynamics. The experimental results show that O²RDL-Net is always more accurate in predicting, stable and well calibrated when compared to traditional machine learning models and advanced deep learning baselines for both regression and classification. The simultaneous learning of risk states and delays in the model facilitates more intelligent and timely decision making in dynamic and congested operations. In addition to the performance, detailed interpretability studies were conducted via SHAP and local contribution explanations, which also validate that the model is based on meaningful feature interactions and domain consistent feature relationships. By doing this, transparency increases trust and practical applicability, enabling logistics stakeholders not only to be able to see what the model predicts but also to see why this prediction is made. This study has the following limitations. The model has been tested on a given logistics data environment and may not perform the same way in a different region that may have different logistic aspects. Furthermore, the external effects like regulatory changes or extreme disruption events were not considered explicitly. Future efforts will involve extension of the framework to multi-region and real-time streaming datasets, addition of graph-based spatial dependencies, and adaptation to provide proactive decision optimization and adaptive intervention strategies.
