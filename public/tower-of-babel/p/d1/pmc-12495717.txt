Cellular and molecular features of acute myeloid leukemia investigation based on single-cell RNA sequencing
Authors: Zheng-fa Li, Jun-rong Lu, Yun Dai, Rui-jiao Mao, Yang-liu Lu, Rong Sun, Jiao Liu, Lu-lu Dong, Li-ling Xia, Yun-chao Xu, Tian Xia, Xiao Qin, Ting Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is defined as a highly heterogeneous clonal malignant disease of immature myeloid hematopoietic stem cells. The objective of this study was to identify the principal cell clusters in AML and their associated hub genes through the analysis of single-cell sequencing data. We present a comprehensive and valuable landscape of 58,717 cells and 27,311 genes derived from bone marrow samples obtained from 5 patients with AML and 3 controls. Then, 9 cell subclusters were identified. The ratios of common myeloid progenitor (CMP), Myelocyte, and Pro-Myelocyte were found to be significantly different between AML and control, as these cells were utilized as differential cells in the present study. A total of 26 differentially expressed genes were identified following the merging and de-weighting of the data. 4 hub genes, ELANE, LTF, S100A12, and SLPI, were selected for further analysis. Homogeneity analysis was conducted on the Myelocyte and Pro-Myelocyte cells, which were subsequently re-clustered into 11 and 8 cell clusters, respectively. In conclusion, we leveraged high-throughput single-cell transcriptomics to parse the cell landscape in the bone marrow from AML patients, 2 key cell types, myelocyte, and pro-myelocyte and related hub genes, ELANE, LTF, S100A12, SLPI were found, and biological functions, regulatory relationships, and developmental status of these key cells, as well as the biological pathways, the molecular regulatory mechanisms, and the related drugs, involved in the hub genes were demonstrated. Our data and findings provide novel insight into AML pathological development and can be inspired in the treatment of AML.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03962-0.

Introduction

Leukemia is a hematological malignant disease characterised by the aberrant clonal proliferation of hematopoietic stem cells [1]. The affected cells demonstrate uncontrolled proliferation, impaired differentiation, and impaired apoptosis. They are arrested at different stages of cell development, proliferation and accumulation in bone marrow and other normal hematopoietic tissues. Additionally, they exhibit inhibition of hematopoietic function, and infiltration of other non-hematopoietic tissues and organs through the blood circulation, which ultimately leads to organ failure and poor prognosis [2–4]. Based on the course of the disease, leukemia can be classified as acute or chronic, and based on the cells involved, it can be classified as myeloid or lymphoid [5]. Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) is defined as a highly heterogeneous clonal malignant disease of immature myeloid hematopoietic stem cells. It is related to various genomic changes and molecular mutations that affect clinical outcomes and provide potential targets for drug development [6, 7]. The mechanisms responsible for the initiation of leukaemia have been identified as falling into two main categories, endogenous and exogenous. Endogenous mechanisms entail a series of genetic and epigenetic alterations that result in aberrant gene expression and dysfunction of haematopoietic stem cells and haematopoietic progenitor cells. This, in turn, enhances their adaptability and ultimately induces malignant transformation. In contrast, extracellular mechanisms include the haematopoietic and non-haematopoietic stromal components of the leukaemia microenvironment, which associated with cell survival promotion, self-renewal and the development of resistance to therapy in these cells [8]. Metabolic function and immune regulation play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of AML [9, 10]. AML cells undergo metabolic reprogramming, including accelerated glycolysis and modulation of amino acid and lipid metabolism, to adapt to their rapid proliferation and evade apoptosis, which may contribute to malignant transformation [11, 12]. A comprehensive investigation of these mechanisms, coupled with the development of targeted therapeutic strategies, has the potential to provide AML patients with more effective therapeutic options and an improved [13].

The current mainstream treatment of AML includes cytarabine plus daunorubicin chemotherapy (7 + 3 regimen) [14] and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) [15]. With intensive research on the disease process in recent decades, novel targeted drugs have been applied. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have resulted in a discernible enhancement of prognosis for AML patients. Nevertheless, the long-term survival rate is still meager, with a 5-year survival rate below 40%, especially in elderly patients over 60 years of age, exhibiting a median survival rate of only 5 to 10 months [16]. Despite the fact that the majority of patients can achieve complete remission following induction chemotherapy during treatment, 75% of patients will relapse again and eventually develop relapsed and refractory acute myeloid leukemia (R/R-AML), which is associated with high mortality rates [17]. The wide variation in therapeutic outcomes may be attributed to the intrinsic heterogeneity or dynamic immunogenic features of the disease [18]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) encompasses both immune and non-immune microenvironments. The immune microenvironment includes myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), dendritic cells (DCs), T cells, B cells, and natural killer (NK) cells. The non-immune microenvironment comprises cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), extracellular matrix, mesenchymal stem cells, and various secreted factors [19, 20]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) refers to high-throughput mRNA sequencing after reverse transcription amplification at the single-cell level. scRNA-seq plays a vital role in characterizing cellular heterogeneity and molecular diversity and distinguishing the biology of different cellular subgroups [21, 22]. Recent studies mainly focus on the hematology of T cells and NK cells in AML [23–25], but few have reported the differentiation of leukemic cells and their progenitors.

The objective was to identify the principal cell types in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and their associated hub genes through the analysis of single-cell sequencing data. In this study, bone marrow samples were collected from 5 AML patients and 3 healthy controls for 10× genomics single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). The primary cell clusters were identified through the use of the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm, as implemented in the Seurat R package. The annotation of individual cells was conducted with the assistance of the singleR R package, with reference to data from the human primary cell atlas. The differential cells between AML and control samples were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test, with the proportion of different cells in each sample serving as the basis for comparison. Subsequently, the differential expression genes (DEGs) in differential cells were identified using the Seurat R package. The biological pathway of DEGs were analyzed using Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment. The hub genes were selected using 10 topology algorithms with the Cytohubba plugin, and a regulatory network was constructed. Additionally, the expression of hub genes in differential cells was identified, and ReactomeGSA was employed to analyze the functional enrichment of two pivotal cells, myelocyte and pro-myelocyte. Moreover, an analysis of cell metastasis and cell communication was conducted to elucidate the interactions between cells. The heterogeneity and trajectory differentiation of myelocytes and pro-myelocytes were analyzed (Fig. 1). These findings may provide novel insights into the pathogenesis and progression of AML.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample acquisition

Bone marrow samples and peripheral blood were collected from 5 patients with newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and 3 control subjects through bone marrow puncture during the routine diagnostic assessment at the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province in 2021. The patients were evaluated in accordance with the 2017 European Leukemia Net (ELN) criteria.Inclusion criteria for AML samples were: Inclusion criteria of AML samples: (1) patients who meet the diagnostic criteria of AML and are diagnosed by bone marrow cell morphology and blood examination; (2) patients who are newly diagnosed and have never received anti-cancer treatment before diagnosis; (3) The patient’s family members voluntarily sign the informed consent. Exclusion criteria were set as: (1) Other types of leukemia; (2) those with abnormal liver and kidney; (3) Patients with malignant tumors such as liver cancer, thyroid cancer and breast cancer; (4) Lack of clinical data and poor compliance; (5) Patients with systemic infectious diseases. The exclusion criteria of the control group arewere: (1) other hematological diseases (such as myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), autoimmune cytopenia); (2) those who have recently received bone marrow suppression drugs or radiotherapy; (3) those with severe liver and kidney dysfunction; (4) elderly patients with unidentified physiological cytopenia; and (5) those with secondary cytopenia such as infection and chronic inflammation. The human protocols were implemented strictly on the basis of the Ethical Guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of the first people’s hospital of Yunnan Province (No.: 2021YY192). Prior to sample acquisition, patients were fully informed about the intended usage of the sample and provided written informed consent, either themselves or through their legal guardians. Single-cell RNA library construction and sequencing

The cells were prepared in accordance with the instructions set forth in the 10× Genomics® Cell Preparation Guide (https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/single-cell-gene). The cell suspension was loaded into Chromium chips with 3’ (V2) chemistry and barcoded with a 10× Chromium Controller (10× Genomics, CA, USA). Subsequently, the RNA from the barcoded cells was subjected to reverse transcription. The sequencing libraries were constructed with the reagents provided in the Chromium Single Cell 3’ (v2) reagent kit (10× Genomics, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed with Illumina (HiSeq 2000), following the manufacturer’s protocols (Illumina, CA, USA). Raw reads were demultiplexed and mapped to the reference genome by the 10× Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline using default parameters.

Single-cell RNA data quality control and analysis

Following the generation of the gene expression matrix by the Cell Ranger count, the Seurat package proceeded to create Seurat objects from the single-cell transcriptome sequencing data. The distribution of the number of genes detected per cell (nFeature_RNA), the sum of all genes detected per cell (nCount_RNA), and the proportion of mitochondrial genes detected per cell (percent. mt) prior to or after quality control were exhibited as violin plots. Subsequently, the data underwent filtration according to the following criteria: 500 < nFeature_RNA < 3000, nCount_RNA < 10,000, and percent.mt < 10%. The data was normalized using the NormalizeData function from the Seurat(4.1.0) [26] R package(4.1.0). To identify genes with high variability (high expression in some cells, low expression in others) based on the relationship between mean and variance, the FindVariableFeatures function was used, which by default returns the top 2000 highly variable genes for downstream analysis. In order to remove the batch effect, the function FindIntegrationAnchors in the Seurat package was used to identify anchors using a list of Seurat objects as inputs, and the function IntegrateData in the Seurat package was used to integrate the dataset using the identified anchors. The data was normalized using the NormalizeData function in the R package Seurat. The FindVariableFeatures function was used to select highly variable genes (exhibiting high expression in some cells and low expression in others) based on the relationship between mean and variance, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were returned by default for downstream analysis. The data were normalized using the ScaleData function in the Seurat R package, and the UMAP cell clustering analysis was performed using the Seurat standard procedure, with the resolution of cell cluster identification set to 0.3.

Cell annotation and proportion

SingleR is an R package for automated cell type annotation of single-cell RNA-seq sequencing (scRNA-seq) data. Cells in the test dataset were labeled and annotated based on cell samples with known type labels as a reference dataset. In this study, we used the SingleR R package (1.8.0) [27] and celldex R package (1.4.0) [27] with HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData as a reference dataset to annotate the cell clusters of the Seurat objects saved in the previous step. Cell clusters annotation was performed on the Seurat object saved in the previous step. To find the marker genes of each cell clusters, the FindAllmarkers function was used with the parameters min.pct = 0.2 and only.pos = TRUE. The top 5 marker genes in each cell clusters were selected, and the results were plotted in an expression Bubble Heatmap. Histograms were plotted to show the proportion of different cell clusters between the two groups. In order to analyse the differences in cell clusters between AML samples and control samples, Wilcoxon test was performed according to the proportion of different cells in each sample. The whole process was controlled by Benjamin Hochberg method, and box plots were drawn accordingly.

Identification of differentially expressed genes and function enrichment

DEGs between AML and control group were identified in common myeloid progenitor (CMP), Myelocyte, and Pro-Myelocyte using the FindMarkers function in the Seurat R package. The parameters for differential gene identification were set as padj < 0.05&|log2FC|>0.5, and the gene was expressed in at least 25% of the cells of a cell population. In order to understand the distribution of differential genes as a whole, the ggplot2 R package(3.4.1) was used to create volcano plots to visualize the DEGs. The screened DEGs were merged and de-duplicated. GO annotation and Kyoto KEGG enrichment were performed by clusterProfiler R package (version 4.2.2) in R script [28]. P.adjust < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) and regulatory networks

PPI network analysis was performed on DEGs using protein interaction information from the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). The threshold was set to medium confidence > 0.4, and isolated targets were removed. The protein interaction information was imported into Cytoscape software(3.8.2) to construct the network, and 10 topology algorithms, Stress, Betweenness, Radiality, Closeness, BottleNeck, Edge Percolated Component (EPC), Degree, Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), Density of Maximum Neighborhood Component (DMNC), and Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) were applied to calculate the gene node scores by cytoHubba. Based on the gene scores calculated by the 10 algorithms, the top 10 genes from each algorithm were selected. The intersection of these selected genes across all 10 algorithms was then identified using the R package UpSet R package(1.4.0) to determine the hub genes [29]. The NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/) was used based on TarBase and miRTarBase to predict the miRNAs corresponding to the hub gene. The miRNA-mRNA relationship pairs obtained from the two databases were intersected. The lncRNAs related to miRNAs corresponding to common pairs were predicted by the miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/miRNet/home.xhtml). Cytoscape constructed the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network. Similarly, the transcription factors (TFs) corresponding to the hub genes were predicted by NetworkAnalyst based on the Jasper database, and Cytoscape constructed the TF-mRNA network. DisGeNET collects and integrates information on disease-gene associations from multiple data sources, including genetic associations, functional studies, expression analyses, biomarkers, and drug therapies. DisGeNET collects and integrates information on disease-gene associations from multiple data sources, including genetic associations, functional studies, expression analyses, biomarkers, and drug therapies. Here in this study, Diseases associated with the hub gene in DisGeNET were enriched using the disease_enrichment function in the disgenet2r R package(0.99.3), and diseases with pvalue < 0.05 were selected. The hub gene-disease relationship was imported into Cytoscape software to construct the network. Small molecule drugs corresponding to the hub genes were predicted using the DGidb database (https://dgidb.org/), and the network was constructed by Cytoscape.

Key cell functional enrichment and metabolic analysis

ReactomeGSA uses the Reacome database to integrate publicly available data from ExpressionAtlas and SingleCellExpressionAtlas to analyze scRNA-seq data. ReactomeGSA R package(1.12.0) [30] was used to analyze the functional enrichment of key cell types separately and calculate the pathway expression. The pathways that were differentially expressed between AML and control were then identified. The top 10 most differentially expressed pathways were visualized. To assess metabolic differences between key cells in AML and control samples from single-cell sequencing data, the metabolic activity of each cell was assessed using the AUCell quantification function in the scMetabolism R package(0.2.1) [31] to obtain a score of the activity of cells in each metabolic pathway. Significance p-values for differences in metabolic pathway activity scores between AML and control were calculated using the Wilcoxon test.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Cell communication analysis was performed on nine annotated cell types using the CellChat R package(1.4.0) [32] in this study. Cell communication networks were inferred after creating Cell Chat objects, importing the ligand-receptor database (CellChatDB.human), and pre-processing based on gene expression data and ligand-receptor database information. The inference process was as follows: if the transcriptome data indicated that A and B cells expressed the genes α and β, respectively, and α and β were queried as a ligand-receptor relationship through the database, then it was assumed that A-B communicated through the α-β pathway. Heatmaps and circle plots were utilized to demonstrate the number of interactions and the strength of interactions between different cell types. The trajectory analysis was performed using the Monocle R package(2.22.0) [33].

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

The total RNA from peripherial blood was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). After the assessment of RNA concentration and pnity via an ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), the RNA was reversly transcribed into cDNA using the FastKing RT Kit (With gDNase) (#KR116, TIANGEN, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Product #Q712-03, Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) with gene-specific primers designed and manufactured by Invitrogen (Shanghai, China). All reactions were run in technical triplicates, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous control for data normalization. Gene expression quantification was determined through the comparative threshold cycle (2-ΔΔCt) approach. The oligonucleotide sequences used in this study are presented below:

SPLI (F) : 5’-CTCTCTACCCAGTTCCCCGA-3’.

Statisctics analysis

The R software (version 4.1.2) was used for bioinformatics statistical analyses in this study. Unpaired parametric t test performed by Graphpad prism 10 was used to compare the data between two groups, All P values are two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise stated.

Results

Identification of cell population in AML bone marrow samples

Bone marrow samples from 5 AML patients and 3 healthy controls were subjected to 10× genomics scRNA-seq (Fig. 2A). Using the Seurat R package, 58,717 cells and 27,311 genes were initially analyzed as Seurat objects, with the distribution of nFeature_RNA, nCount_RNA, and percent.mt before quality control shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A. After filtering (500 < nFeature_RNA < 3000, nCount_RNA < 10000, percent.mt < 10%), 46,698 cells and 27,311 genes remained, with post-quality control distributions in Supplementary Fig. 1B. Prior to batch effect removal, samples showed clear differentiation by cellular population, indicating significant batch effects (Fig. 2B). Data integration via Seurat-identified anchors effectively eliminated batch effects, as UMAP clustering showed mixed samples by cellular population (Fig. 2C). A total of 14 cell clusters were identified (Fig. 2D) and annotated using the Human Primary Cell Atlas Data into 9 cell types: CMP, granulocyte and macrophage progenitor (GMP), G-CSF-mediated hematopoietic stem cell (HSC_G-CSF), monocyte, myelocyte, natural killer (NK) cells, CD34-B cell precursor, pro-myelocyte, and T cells (Fig. 2E), with CMP and GMP being the most abundant.Cellular annotations in AML vs. control groups are shown in Fig. 2F, and top 5 gene markers for each cluster in Supplementary Fig. 2. Compared to controls, AML samples had higher proportions of CMP and GMP, and lower proportions of myelocyte, pro-myelocyte, and monocyte (Fig. 2G, Supplementary Table 1). Wilcoxon test revealed significant differences in CMP, pro-myelocyte, and monocyte proportions between groups, which were thus defined as differential cells (Fig. 2H, Supplementary Tables 2, 3).

Identification of differential expression genes in differential cells

Then, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between AML and control samples in differential cells were identified when padj < 0.05&|logFC|>0.5, and the gene was expressed in at least 25% of the cells of a cell population. In Myelocyte, 10 differential upregulated genes, including DEFA3, LY6E, CST3, IFIT3, FTH1, G0S2, SLPI, S100A12, C4orf48, PI3 were found, and the expression of genes were displayed using bubble plot (Fig. 3A-B, Supplementary Table 4). In Pro- myelocytes, 17 DEGs were identified, including 10 upregulated genes and 7 downregulated genes. The expression of PRTN3, CTSG, ELANE, TIMP1, CST3, CFD, CD99, CALR, CST7, and GPX1 was higher, and the expression of MKI67, CRISP3, CLSPN, KKI67, CLSPN, KNL1, OLFM4, CAMP, and LTF was lower in AML samples than in control samples (Fig. 3C-D, Supplementary Table 5). While no DEGs were identified in CMP (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Table 6). A total of 26 DEGs were obtained after merging and de-emphasizing the above DEGs in each differential cells, respectively, DEFA3, LY6E, CST3, IFIT3, FTH1, G0S2, SLPI, S100A12, C4orf48, PI3, PRTN3, CTSG, ELANE, TIMP1, CFD, CD99, CALR, CST7, GPX1, MKI67, CRISP3, CLSPN, KNL1, OLFM4, CAMP, LTF (Supplementary Table 7). GO annotation and KEGG enrichment were performed to acknowledge the biological function of these genes. As results, 120 GO terms were enriched, including 79 biological process (BP) terms were enriched, including humoral immune response, negative regulation of endopeptidase activity, negative regulation of peptidase activity, leukocyte migration, mononuclear cell migration, neutrophil-mediated cytotoxicity, neutrophil extravasation, myeloid leukocyte activation, myeloid leukocyte migration, 23 celluar component (CC) terms were enriched, including secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, vesicle lumen, tertiary granule lumen, specific granule lumen, specific granule, tertiary granule, azurophil granule lumen, and 18 molecular function (MF) terms were enriched, including endopeptidase inhibitor activity, peptidase inhibitor activity, endopeptidase regulator activity, peptidase regulator activity, serine-type endopeptidase activity, serine-type peptidase activity, serine hydrolase activity, enzyme inhibitor activity, protease binding, cysteine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity (Supplementary Table 8). The bar chart of TOP 10 enriched terms ranked according to p.adjust was plotted (Fig. 3F; Table 2). Also, 2 KEGG pathways, Staphylococcus aureus infection, and Neutrophil extracellular trap formation were found enriched and involved genes such as ELANE, CTSG, CAMP, CFD, and DEFA3(Fig. 3G, Supplementary Table 9).

The screening of hub genes and their regulatory network prediction

To find the hub genes among the DEGs, the protein-protein interaction (PPI) was predicted by String and imported to Cytoscope. Ten topology algorithms from Cytohubba were used to calculate the gene node score. As a result, 4 genes, including ELANE, LTF, S100A12, and SLPI, were screened in all 10 algorithms (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Tables 10–11), and the PPI, including 23 nodes and 51 edges, was constructed (Fig. 4B). NetworkAnalyst predicted the relationship between hub genes and miRNAs based on TarBase and MiRTarBase to predict the regulatory network of the hub genes. 33 miRNA-mRNA pairs were predicted by MiRTarBase, 46 miRNA-mRNA pairs were predicted by MiRTarBase, and among these, 2 miRNA-mRNA pairs, including 2 mRNA, S100A12, SLPI, and 2 miRNAs, including hsa-mir-146a-5p, hsa-miR-335-5p were predicted (Fig. 4C). Then, the lncRNAs binding to the 2 miRNAs were predicted by miRNet, and 74 lncRNA-miRNA pairs, including 2 miRNAs and 69 lncRNAs, were predicted. The ceRNA network was constructed accordingly, including 73 nodes (2 mRNAs, 2 miRNAs, and 69 lncRNAs) and 76 edges (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table 12). Then, the transcription factors (TFs) were predicted. As a result, 25 TFs were predicted, and a network including 29 nodes (4 mRNAs and 25 TFs) and 34 edges were constructed (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Table 13). Then, DisGeNET was used to predict the diseases related to the hub genes. A network including 37 nodes (4 mRNAs and 33 diseases) and 39 edges were constructed (Fig. 4F, Supplementary Table 14). DGidb predicted that molecular drugs targeted hub genes. 18 drugs, including 8 targeted to ELANE, 5 targeted to S100A12, 4 targeted to LTF, and 1 targeted to SLPI, were predicted. A network including 22 nodes (4 genes and 18 drugs) and 18 edges were constructed (Fig. 4G, Supplementary Table 15).

The identification and regulation of key cells

Hub genes were validated in myelocytes and pro-myelocytes. UMAP plots showed the expression distribution of ELANE, LTF, S100A12, and SLPI in myelocytes (Fig. 5A) and pro-myelocytes (Fig. 5D), with significant expression differences in both cell types (Fig. 5B-C, E-F), identifying them as key cells. Also, the hub genes were detected in periphrial blood via qPCR. Results showed that, compared to cpntrol group, ELANE, S100A12, and SLPI were higher expressed, while LTF were lower expressed in AML group (Fig. 6A-D). ReactomeGSA enrichment analysis revealed top pathways for hub genes in key cells: in myelocytes, these included synthesis of hepoxilins (HX) and trioxilins (TrX), hydroxycarboxylic acid-binding receptors, and mitochondrial ABC transporters (top 10; Fig. 7A, Supplementary Table 16); in pro-myelocytes, pathways included HX/TrX synthesis, potassium transport channels, and classical antibody-mediated complement activation (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Table 17). Metabolic activity differences between AML and control groups were assessed via Wilcoxon test. In myelocytes, 46 pathways differed significantly, with top 5 being amino sugar/nucleotide sugar metabolism, folate biosynthesis, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pentose phosphate pathway, and starch/sucrose metabolism (Fig. 7C, Supplementary Table 18). In pro-myelocytes, 64 pathways differed, with top 5 including amino sugar/nucleotide sugar metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, and pentose phosphate pathway (Fig. 7D, Supplementary Table 19). Cell communication among 9 clusters was analyzed using expression matrices and ligand-receptor relationships, with interaction numbers/intensities visualized via heat maps and circle plots (Fig. 8A-D). Critical interactions included CMP-HSC_G-CSF, GMP-monocyte, myelocyte-monocyte, pro-myelocyte-myelocyte, and pro-myelocyte-monocyte. Bubble plots showed ligand-receptor-mediated communication probabilities: e.g., myelocytes to pro-myelocytes via ANXA1-FPR1, pro-myelocytes to myelocytes via RETN-CAP1, and myelocyte-myelocyte via RETN-CAP1 (Fig. 8D). Signal transduction analysis identified three communication patterns among cell populations (Fig. 8E-F): Pattern 1 (RESISTIN, GRN, CXCL, VISFATIN families) in HSC_G-CSF and pro-myelocytes; Pattern 2 (CCL, IL-16) in myelocytes, NK cells, CD34-B cell precursors, and T cells; Pattern 3 (MIF) in CMP and GMP.

The heterogeneity and trajectory differentiation of key cells

In order to clarify the heterogeneity of myelocyte and Pro-Myelocyte in the single-cell sequencing data, the key cells were clustered again. Cell lineages with significant differences were first extracted, and high-quality cells were secondly downgraded and clustered to annotate the cells into different sub-clusters, which were demonstrated using UMAP. The distribution of Myelocyte between AML and control group, and among each sample is shown in Fig. 9A. 11 subclusters of myelocytes were found, and all the subclusters of Myelocyte were more abundant in the control group compared to the AML group (Fig. 9B). The TOP 5 cell marker genes of each cluster were selected and shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. Also, the Wilcoxon test was used to analyze the statistical difference in cell abundance in subclusters. Cluster 1 (defined as S100A12+ TUBA1A+ PSAP+ FPR1+ NCF+ Myelocyte), 4 (defined as VNN2+ CXCL8+ G0S26+ FTH1+ LGALS1+ Myelocyte), 8 (defined as IFIT2+ TIMP1+ SAT1+ FCGR3B+ OLFM4+ Myelocyte), 9 (defined as NAMPT+ HMGB2+ FGR+ RGS+ BCL2A1+ Myelocyte) were found significantly different between AML and control group (Fig. 9C, Supplementary Table 20). Discussion

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a complex hematological malignancy, a group of clonal malignant proliferative disorders caused by acquired somatic genetic damage and accumulation of hematopoietic progenitor cells, which are highly heterogeneous and extremely invasive [34, 35]. Most patients do not claim any detectible early symptoms, and they usually present with acute complications of bone marrow failure [36]. This study presents a valuable landscape of 58,717 cells and 27,311 genes from bone marrow samples from 5 AML patients and 3 controls. Then, 9 cell clusters were identified, including common myeloid progenitor (CMP), Granulocyte and macrophage progenitor (GMP), Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor-mediated hematopoietic stem cell (HSC_G-CSF), Monocyte, Myelocyte, Nature killer (NK) cells, CD34- B Cell Precursor, Pro-Myelocyte, T cells. The tumor escape strategies in AML involve direct adaptation of the AML cells to hide from immune recognition and tumor-cell-mediated modifications of the immune cell compartment that include effector T cells, natural killer cells (NKs), and dendritic cells (DCs) [37]. Malignant cells orchestrate immunosuppressive cells, including MDSCs, and regulate cells (Treg) to dysfunction effector cells, including T cells or NK cells [1]. It is now believed that a considerable number of AML results are derived from the interaction of environmental factors with cytogenetic material. The immune environments can limit the expansion of tumor cells until subpopulations that evade or suppress host immunity emerge [38]. Conversely, intrinsic properties of AML cells, including expression of immunomodulatory factors and external microenvironmental changes, can lead to the accumulation of suppressor T regulatory cells and impair the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes [39]. Over the past decades, immunotherapy, such as T cells with chimeric antigen receptors (CAR-T), monoclonal antibodies, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, have been used for AML treatment [40]. Enhancing T cell or NK cell-mediated clearance of AML cells is an attractive therapeutic strategy, but immunotherapy trials have not been as successful as in other cancers [41]. Therefore, there is an urgent for better insight into the cellular components and heterogeneity of AML. Unlike solid cancers that require genetic modifications and cellular reprogramming to metastatic dissemination, dedifferentiated malignant leukocytes retained the benign ability of motility and survival in the blood circulation and acquired rapid and uncontrolled cell division [42]. GMPs and monocyte-dendritic cell progenitors (MDPs) produce monocytes during homeostasis. In response to increased demand during infection, both progenitor populations are thought to derive from CMPs [43]. AML and related myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) patients suffered frequent infections due to the disease-related impaired immunity [44]. The impairment of monocyte function increased with the disease severity, which may be the main reason for innate immune dysfunction [45]. The apparent accumulation of CMP and GMP was observed in the AML patients, which may indicate the impaired differentiation of CMP to monocytes. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) is a cytokine for the maturation and mobilization of bone marrow neutrophils, and it is now also found to promote pro-tumorigenic immune cell phenotypes [46]. G-CSF increases the HSC compartment and exerts an intense immunoregulatory effect on marrow T-cells, resulting in the shift from Th1 to Th2 phenotype with higher production of anti-inflammatory cytokines [47]. The bone marrow with G-CSF donor priming was now used as a source for allogeneic stem cell transplantation [48, 49]. An increase in HSC_G-CSF with no statistical difference was observed in the AML sample; this may indicate the endogenous resistance mechanism to AML pathology and progression.

This study designated CMPs, myeloid cells, and pre-myeloid cells as differential cell populations. Analysis revealed significant differences in the proportions of these cells between the AML group and the control group. Notably, no differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in CMPs between the two groups. This may be because CMPs represent an early stage of AML, where certain AML subtypes maintain relative quiescence in CMPs through intrinsic homeostatic regulatory mechanisms (e.g., maintaining proliferation-differentiation balance and reducing sensitivity to microenvironmental signals), preventing significant gene expression changes [50]. In myeloid cells, 10 DEGs were identified, while in pre-myeloid cells, the number increased to 17. After merging and removing redundancies, a total of 26 DEGs were obtained. Using Cytoscape’s cytoHubba tool and 10 topological algorithms, four core genes, ELANE, LTF, S100A12, and SLPI were screened.

Mutations in the human neutrophil elastase gene (ELANE) are closely associated with cyclic neutropenia (CyN) and severe congenital neutropenia (CN), not only leading to CyN but also significantly increasing the risk of myelodysplastic syndrome/acute myeloid leukemia (MDS/AML) [51–53]. Specifically, certain mutations such as G214R and C151Y directly elevate AML risk [51]. Mutations like G214R can drive disease progression to MDS/AML, fatal infections, and the need for transplantation, which represents more severe adverse outcomes, while nonsense and frameshift mutations also markedly increase MDS/AML risk [54, 55]. Additionally, heterozygous mutations like G185R correlate with leukemia onset and response to recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (rhG-CSF) [56]. Patients carrying only CN mutations exhibit significantly higher cumulative incidence of severe adverse events, including MDS, AML, stem cell transplantation, or death, compared to CyN patients or those with overlapping mutations [51]. Studies also reveal that ELANE is highly expressed in AML promyelocytes and may contribute to leukemogenesis by promoting a pro-inflammatory microenvironment and inducing DNA damage [57]. These findings provide a basis for patient risk stratification and prognosis evaluation, deepen the understanding of neutrophil development and dysfunction mechanisms, and suggest that ELANE mutation testing can not only aid in diagnosing CN/CyN and assessing AML transformation risk but also serve as a biomarker for AML clinical risk stratification and prognosis. Targeted modulation of its encoded protein holds potential therapeutic value.

Lactoferrin (LTF) is an iron-binding glycoprotein found in exocrine glands and secondary granules of mature granulocytes, whose serum levels reflect neutrophil status [58, 59]. Secondary granules are markers of promyelocyte differentiation into neutrophils [60], and impaired biosynthesis in promyelocytes is a major contributor to leukemia [61]. LTF serves as an independent prognostic indicator in AML morphological examination [62, 63]. We found that LTF was lower expressed in pro-myelocytes from AML samples. As a key molecule in anti-infection, LTF activates innate immune cells, and its immune complexes (LTF-ICs) exhibit unique monocyte/macrophage activation properties, which are potentially involved in autoimmune disease pathogenesis. Additionally, LTF regulates immune evasion in AML cells [64], functions as a targeted drug carrier [65], and influences AML cell proliferation and apoptosis through iron metabolism [66]. These findings provide new perspectives for the clinical application of LTF, particularly in AML diagnosis and treatment [67], with its targeted regulatory potential offering a basis for personalized drug development and optimized immunocombination therapies. The expression characteristics and functional mechanisms of LTF provide critical support for AML prognosis assessment, treatment selection, and novel strategy development, advancing personalized therapy toward a mechanism-driven paradigm.

S100A12 is a member of the S100 family of EF-hand calcium-binding proteins [68]. Human S100A12 is predominantly expressed and secreted by neutrophil granulocytes and, therefore, has been assigned to the S100 protein subfamily of calgranulins or myeloid-related proteins [69]. Recent clinical evidence suggests a high potential of S100A12 as a sensitive and specific diagnostic marker of localized inflammatory processes [70, 71]. S100A12 was found higher expressed in mononuclear cells of chronic lymphocytic leukemia [72]. We also found that S100A12 was highly expressed in myelocytes from AML samples. One of the predicted targeted drugs of S100A12, methotrexate, was a folic-acid antagonist commonly used in the treatment of Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [73]. It has been demonstrated that S100A12 plays a direct role in the antimicrobial process, inhibiting pathogen growth by promoting superoxide production, assisting macrophages in scavenging antigens and chelating nutrient metals required for bacterial growth. S100A12 may influence cells in the bone marrow microenvironment, such as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and osteoblasts, thereby indirectly affecting the growth and survival of AML cells [74, 75]. These characteristics provide promising avenues for the clinical diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and development of potential therapeutic strategies for AML, particularly in controlling infectious complications and modulating inflammatory responses [76]. Therefore, S100A12 is implicated in the pathogenesis of AML, potentially contributing to AML formation through inflammatory responses, inhibition of dihydrofolate reductase, and effects on the bone marrow microenvironment. As a predictive target protein for methotrexate, targeting S100A12 can exert traditional antimetabolic effects while also enhancing tumor-killing efficacy and reducing infection risk by modulating its mediated inflammatory and antibacterial processes. Furthermore, the development of novel targeted drugs or combination therapeutic strategies based on its metal-chelating and immune-regulating properties holds promise for offering new approaches to AML treatment.

Secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI), also known as antileukoprotease or secretory protease inhibitor, structurally contains a C-terminal with elastase inhibitory activity and an N-terminal with antimicrobial activity [77]. It has been found that the functions of SLPI include inhibition of granulocyte elastase [78], regulation of cell cycle progression [79], and anti-inflammatory [80]. We found that SLPI was higher expressed in myelocytes from AML samples, but its pathological role remains to be investigated. Studies have confirmed that when human mesenchymal stem cells derived from AML patients (hMSC-AML) are co-cultured with healthy hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), the high expression of SLPI may influence AML progression by altering HSC gene expression [81]. Since the inflammatory microenvironment in AML promotes tumor cell survival, meanwhile, SLPI can exert anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [82]. Thus, its anti-inflammatory role may affect AML progression, suggesting a link between SLPI and AML pathogenesis. Therapeutically, high SLPI expression may serve as a reference for clinical evaluation. As a molecule with both protease inhibitory and anti-inflammatory functions, it could be a potential target for translating research findings on SLPI into AML clinical applications, and modulating its expression or activity may become a novel therapeutic strategy.

All 4 hub genes (ELANE, LTF, S100A12, SLPI) significantly differed between AML and control in both Myelocyte and Pro-Myelocyte cells. Therefore, Myelocyte and Pro-Myelocyte cells were used as the key cells in this study. Functional enrichment analyses and metabolic analyses were performed on key cells to explore the functions that key cells are involved in. Both Myelocyte and pro-myelocyte were enriched in the function including the synthesis of Hepoxilins (HX) and Trioxilins (TrX), CYP2E1 reactions, Biosynthesis of DPAn-6 SPMs, NEIL3-mediated resolution of ICLs, Potassium transport channels. The related metabolic production includes Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, Folate biosynthesis, Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, Pentose phosphate pathway, Starch and sucrose metabolism, Arachidonic acid metabolism, Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids. This study revealed a notable enrichment of amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and other pivotal metabolic pathways in myeloid cells and premyelocytes. These pathways play a pivotal role in cell proliferation, energy production and biosynthesis, and their aberrancy may facilitate the growth and survival of tumor cells. It has been demonstrated that the metabolic pathways of amino sugar and nucleotide sugar are intimately associated with the progression of AML. The researchers discovered a correlation between the expression of Protein disulfide isomerase A3 (PDIA3) and the aberrant activity of amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolic pathways in AML cells [83]. Consequently, the modulation of this metabolic pathway may offer a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of AML. The significant difference in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis also indicates that AML cells are abnormal in energy metabolism, which may be related to the rapid proliferation and increased energy demand of tumor cells. It has been demonstrated that patients diagnosed with AML exhibit elevated glycolytic metabolism, which may be associated with a more favourable prognosis [84]. It is noteworthy that in the mouse model of AML, the deletion of glycolytic enzymes PKM2 and LDHA catalyses the production of cytoplasmic pyruvate and lactic acid, respectively. This effectively inhibits the initiation of leukaemia in vivo without affecting the function of normal haematopoietic stem cells. This further emphasises the importance of metabolic function in the pathogenesis of AML [85, 86]. Hepoxilins (HX) are biologically active metabolites of arachidonic acid (AA) [87]. Leukotrienes (LT) exert stimulatory effects on myelopoiesis and play a putative role in the pathophysiology of AML [88]. Cytochrome p450 2E1 (CYP2E1) is a detoxifying enzyme with a specific affinity for ethanol (EtOH) [89]. The AML risk for persons in combination of GSTP1 wildtype with CYP2E1 hybrid and mutant genotype can be further decreased [90]. HERG channel activity regulates cell proliferation in stimulated CD34(+) as well as in blast cells from AML patients [91]. These all proved that the proliferation of hematopoietic cells plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of AML.

Homogeneity analysis was performed for Myelocyte and Pro-Myelocyte, which were re-clustered to 11 cell clusters for Myelocyte cells and 8 cell clusters for Pro-Myelocyte cells. For Myelocyte, Cluster 1 (defined as S100A12 + TUBA1A + PSAP + FPR1 + NCF + Myelocyte), 4 (defined as VNN2 + CXCL8 + G0S26 + FTH1 + LGALS1 + Myelocyte), 8 (defined as IFIT2 + TIMP1 + SAT1 + FCGR3B + OLFM4 + Myelocyte), 9 (defined as NAMPT + HMGB2 + FGR + RGS + BCL2A1 + Myelocyte) were found significantly different between AML and control samples. Cluster 1 was mainly enriched in the early stage of cell differentiation. Cluster 8 was mainly enriched in the latest stage of cell differentiation. Cluster 4 was found to be distributed throughout the whole stage of the cell differential. As for Pro-myelocyte, cluster 1 (defined as CFD + ELANE + AC084033.3 + AZU1 + RPL32 + pro-myelocyte) was found to be significantly different in AMLsamples, which was mainly enriched in the final stage of cell differentiation. These results indicated the difference of myelocyte and pro-myelocyte with the development of AML pathology, and treatment strategy should altered with the change of the cell clusters. The present study demonstrates that the cell differentiation trajectory of each subgroup of myeloid cells is divided into five distinct stages. It is possible that there is a close relationship between the trajectory of cell differentiation and the progression of AML and drug resistance. As a heterogeneous blood system disease, AML is closely associated with the maturation differentiation disorder and malignant cloning of haematopoietic stem cells. The biological characteristics and functions of cells in different stages of differentiation are distinct. The cells in the early stages may exhibit a higher proliferation rate and a lower degree of differentiation, which increases the likelihood of malignant clone formation and disease progression. Despite the cells being in an advanced stage of differentiation, they may also display increased aggressiveness and drug resistance [92]. Furthermore, the persistence of haematopoietic stem cells or progenitor cells with a differentiation disorder in AML patients following treatment may result in their proliferation and the formation of new malignant clones, thereby contributing to disease recurrence [93]. During the process of cell differentiation, alterations in the expression of key genes are closely associated with the specific differentiation stage. These genes may be involved in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation and apoptosis, and may therefore play a key role in the pathogenesis and progression of AML. The mutation or aberrant expression of these hub genes may result in reduced sensitivity of AML cells to chemotherapeutic agents, thereby contributing to drug resistance. However, although pseudotime analysis and single-cell type annotation provide strong support for correlation predictions, their results still carry a certain degree of speculation. Subsequent validation with more experimental evidence is required to further consolidate the reliability of the conclusions. It is therefore of great significance to gain an understanding of the differentiation trajectory and stage distribution of AML cells, as well as the expression changes of hub genes, in order to develop personalised chemotherapy plans, improve the efficacy of drugs and reduce drug resistance.

The cell cycle is a precisely regulated process that controls cell growth and division. In AML, its regulatory mechanisms are often disrupted, leading to abnormal proliferation of leukemia cells. Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) must form complexes with cyclins to drive the cell cycle progression [94]. In AML, overexpression or aberrant activation of cyclins and CDKs accelerates the cell cycle, promoting uncontrolled cell proliferation. In addition, as a critical mechanism for maintaining tissue homeostasis, the apoptosis pathway is frequently suppressed in AML, allowing leukemia cells to evade cell death. On one hand, anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL are overexpressed in AML cells, blocking the intrinsic apoptosis pathway by inhibiting cytochrome c release [95]. On the other hand, the tumor suppressor protein p53 can activate apoptosis-related genes to induce cell death, but in AML, mutations or inactivation of p53 render it dysfunctional, preventing cells from responding to apoptotic signals [96].

We found that the hub genes mainly predicted 18 drugs, such as METHOTREXATE, SIVELESTAT and so on. Previous studies have shown that in allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (especially haploidentical haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (haplo-HSCT), methotrexate is used as part of the T-cell management strategy to reduce the T-cell-mediated alloreaction and thus effectively prevent early graft-versus-host disease (GVHD). This mechanism helps to reduce the incidence of GVHD and transplant-related mortality, making haplo-HSCT an effective alternative for the treatment of non-malignant diseases (such as primary immunodeficiency and haemoglobinopathies) and haematological malignancies such as acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) in children and adults without HLA-matched donors [97]. Further studies have shown that combining MTX with other targeted or immunotherapeutic drugs may enhance the efficacy of AML treatment [98]. This suggests methotrexate’s role in preventing transplant complications, expanding transplant application scenarios, and enhancing combination therapy effects, providing important clinical evidence for its use in hematopoietic stem cell transplantation and AML treatment. These findings contribute to optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient prognosis. On the other hand, SIVELESTAT, a selective inhibitor of neutrophil elastase, has shown beneficial effects in a mouse model of atherosclerosis. Sivelestat not only reduced the accumulation of toxic lipids, vascular monocyte infiltration and inflammatory cytokine levels, but also reduced intestinal permeability and endotoxemia in mice [99]. These findings suggest that SIVELESTAT may have a potential effect on improving blood health, including a beneficial effect on the vascular health of AML patients, although its direct therapeutic effect on AML requires further investigation. In addition, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is an important treatment for AML, but it may lead to various complications such as GVHD and infection after surgery [100]. Sivelestat may alleviate the occurrence of post-transplant complications by regulating inflammatory responses [101]. This suggests the potential of sivelestat in improving vascular health and regulating inflammatory responses, providing a potential application direction for its use in maintaining vascular health in AML patients and managing complications after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. It is expected to become a complementary means for optimizing comprehensive treatment strategies for AML.

Cell-to-cell communication is of vital importance in the context of the biological process. Our research indicates that the communication between CMP and HSC_G-CSF, GMP and monocytes, myelocytes and monocytes, premyelocytes and monocytes may play a pivotal role in maintaining the stability and functionality of the blood system. Furthermore, the specific ligand-receptor pairs (such as ANXA1-FPR1 and RETN-CAP1) identified in the results not only corroborated the potential for direct cell-to-cell communication, but also provided insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying this communication. By analysing the relationship between cell clusters and signal pathways, three different signal transduction modes were identified, which covered several important signal molecular families. This demonstrated the complexity of intercellular communication and the preference and specificity of different cell clusters in signal transduction. These findings have significant implications for future research, which can not only further verify the precise role of these ligand-receptor pairs in intercellular communication, but also investigate whether they are involved in specific biological processes or diseases. Additionally, we can conduct a comprehensive analysis of the alterations in these signal transduction patterns under diverse physiological and pathological conditions and their impact on cell function, thereby identifying potential targets and avenues for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

While this study has made significant advancements in the analysis of the cell landscape, key cell types and related core genes in the bone marrow of AML patients, it is important to acknowledge the remaining limitations. First, the small sample size makes it difficult to reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of AML, potentially reducing statistical power and limiting the generalizability and robustness of the results. Future studies should expand the sample size, validate results using public datasets, and optimize control group selection to minimize confounding factors. Second, due to the limited number of cases, the WHO classification system was not applied to categorize AML. Given that AML subtypes exhibit significant differences in pathological mechanisms, clinical features, and prognosis, this may hinder precise alignment of the results with the biological characteristics of specific subtypes. Subsequent research will incorporate WHO classification for subgroup analysis to explore subtype-specific regulatory patterns of core genes. Third, single-cell RNA sequencing has inherent limitations. Sequencing depth and dynamic expression range may affect data accuracy, with low-abundance transcripts prone to being masked or distorted by noise. Although batch effects caused by variations in library preparation can be mitigated, they cannot be entirely eliminated. Moreover, the absence of genomic sequencing data makes it challenging to elucidate genetic driving mechanisms. Future studies should integrate multi-omics data to explore the relationship between gene mutations and expression profiles. Furthermore, the statistical methods employed have shortcomings. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test did not adequately account for error accumulation in multiple testing, which may increase false-positive results when examining a large number of genes, thereby reducing reliability and reproducibility. Future analyses will adopt more suitable methods such as pseudo-aggregation or MAST to optimize the findings. Lastly, the study lacks sufficient experimental validation. Future work will involve functional experiments to verify computational model results and integrate clinical research to clarify the role of core genes in AML pathogenesis and their potential as therapeutic targets, thereby refining current discoveries.

In conclusion, we leveraged high-throughput single-cell transcriptomics to parse the cell landscape in the bone marrow from AML patients, 2 key cell types, myelocyte and pro-myelocyte, and related hub genes, ELANE, LTF, S100A12, SLPI were found, and biological functions, regulatory relationships, and developmental status of these key cells, as well as the biological pathways, the molecular regulatory mechanisms, and the related drugs, involved in the hub genes were demonstrated. Our data and findings provide novel insight into AML pathological development and can be inspired in the treatment of AML.
