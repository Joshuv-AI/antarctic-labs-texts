Women's Autonomy in Obstetric Care: A Qualitative Study on Violations and Experiences
Authors: Carme Perelló‐Iñiguez, Victoria García‐Garro, Desirée Mena‐Tudela, Ainoa Biurrun‐Garrido
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo explore how women experience and interpret autonomy in obstetric care across pregnancy, childbirth, abortion, and the postpartum period. A qualitative descriptive study was conducted using semi‐structured interviews with women who had received obstetric‐gynecological care. Interviews were audio‐recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed through hybrid thematic analysis. Participants described multiple constraints on autonomy associated with hierarchical professional relationships, limited information, and protocol‐driven care practices. Autonomy emerged as a multidimensional and relational process extending beyond formal informed consent and encompassing bodily, decisional, emotional, and relational aspects. These experiences influenced both childbirth perceptions and trust in healthcare professionals, indicating the need for greater attention to communication, shared decision‐making, and relational support in clinical practice. Constraints on women's autonomy in obstetric care raise important ethical and human‐rights considerations. Nurses specializing in obstetrics and gynecology play a key role in fostering respectful communication, supporting informed decision‐making, and promoting care practices aligned with dignity and autonomy.Keywords: bioethics, human rights, obstetric violence, personal autonomy, violence, women's health

Introduction

Obstetric care, including pregnancy, childbirth, postpartum, and abortion, constitutes a central area of nursing and midwifery practice, where clinical decision‐making, relational care, and ethical responsibility converge. Within these settings, respect for women's autonomy is a fundamental professional and ethical obligation, underpinning informed consent, shared decision‐making, and the provision of dignified, person‐centerd care (Beauchamp and Childress 2019; World Health Organization 2022). From a nursing perspective, safeguarding autonomy extends beyond the transmission of clinical information. It also involves sustained relational engagement, emotional support, and the recognition of women as active agents in their reproductive care. Despite its normative centrality, women's autonomy is frequently constrained in obstetric practice. Empirical research has shown that highly protocolized and hierarchical biomedical models often prioritize institutional routines, risk management, and legal defensiveness over women's experiential knowledge, preferences, and values (Olza et al. 2018; Sadler et al. 2016). In such contexts, informed consent may be reduced to a formal or administrative procedure, while women's actual capacity to influence decisions affecting their bodies and care trajectories remains limited. These dynamics place nursing and midwifery professionals in ethically complex positions, requiring them to navigate tensions between institutional demands and commitments to relational care, advocacy, and respect for dignity.

A persistent conceptual challenge in both clinical practice and research is the tendency to conflate autonomy with decision‐making capacity. In obstetric settings, women's autonomy is often implicitly conditioned by assessments of vulnerability, emotional distress, pain, or perceived compliance. This paternalistic logic may lead to the marginalization of women's voices, particularly during labor, pregnancy loss, or abortion, when they are framed as temporarily incapable of making valid decisions. Such practices have been widely described within the literature on obstetric violence (OV), understood as actions or omissions in healthcare that compromise women's physical, psychological, or social well‐being and undermine their ability to make free and informed choices (Olza 2018; Mena‐Tudela et al. 2023).

Importantly, OV should not be understood solely as overt abuse or isolated professional misconduct, but as a structural phenomenon embedded within institutional cultures, power asymmetries, and gendered norms shaping reproductive healthcare. In this study, OV is not treated as the primary object of analysis but as the contextual and structural setting through which constraints on women's autonomy are experienced in everyday obstetric care. Rather, it is understood as the contextual and structural setting through which violations of women's autonomy are experienced in everyday obstetric care. From a nursing perspective, these practices represent failures of ethical and relational care that may have enduring consequences for women's trust in healthcare systems and their engagement with future care.

While previous research has documented the prevalence of practices conceptualized as OV in diverse settings and its association with adverse health outcomes, less attention has been paid to how women experience autonomy across multiple, interrelated dimensions of care, as well as to the mechanisms through which these dimensions may be simultaneously constrained in routine clinical encounters. Feminist bioethics and relational autonomy approaches provide useful conceptual frameworks to address this issue by understanding autonomy not as an abstract or exclusively individual attribute, but as a situated, relational, and context‐dependent capacity shaped by interpersonal interactions, institutional conditions, and social power dynamics (Donchin 2001; Sherwin 2008). Within this framework, the aim of this study is to analyze how women experience autonomy in obstetric care across multiple interrelated dimensions and how these experiences are shaped within contexts described in the literature as OV. To this end, a theoretically informed qualitative approach is adopted, based on in‐depth interviews with women who received obstetric care in different reproductive contexts, in order to examine how autonomy is enacted, negotiated, and constrained within clinical practice.

Material and Methods

Study Design

The study was informed by a constructivist epistemological stance. A descriptive qualitative study was conducted to explore women's experiences of autonomy in obstetric care, including childbirth, abortion, and postpartum contexts. The study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines to enhance transparency and methodological rigor (Booth et al. 2014).

Interviews were conducted between March 2023 and July 2024 by the first author (C.P.I.), a qualitative researcher with training in interpretive methodologies and experience in sexual and reproductive health research. Participants were informed about the researcher's background, institutional affiliation, and the aims of the study. The analytic team included the first author, two midwives, one nurse with expertise in OV, and an independent qualitative researcher (A.B.G., V.G.G., and D.M.T., respectively), allowing for interdisciplinary triangulation.

Participants and Setting

Participants were eligible if they were adult women who had received obstetric care and later reflected on experiences related to autonomy, dignity, or decision‐making. Women who did not identify reflections related to autonomy or dignity were not included in the analytic sample. Participants were recruited through an open call published on the University of Barcelona's “Dona, salut i ètica” platform and through snowball sampling. A maximum variation sampling strategy was used to capture diversity in age, educational background, and type of healthcare received (public or private). Nine women participated in the study. They were aged between 31 and 76 years at the time of the interview, and between 20 and 38 years at the time of the obstetric experiences discussed (Table 1). The inclusion of one older participant (76 years) served a purely illustrative and contextual purpose. Data from this participant were not coded across all autonomy dimensions and were excluded from cross‐case thematic comparisons in order to preserve analytic coherence.

Participant Characteristics

The nine participants varied in educational level, employment status, and type of healthcare setting accessed (Table 1). All participants had experienced at least one obstetric event that they perceived as limiting their autonomy or dignity within the healthcare system.

Data Collection Procedure

Data were collected through in‐depth semi‐structured interviews lasting approximately 60 min. Interviews were conducted via secure video call to ensure accessibility and confidentiality. All interviews were audio‐recorded with participants' informed consent and supplemented with field notes and reflective observations.

The interview guide was developed by the research team, validated by experts in gender and perinatal health, and pilot tested with the first three participants. Minor wording adjustments were made following the pilot phase. The guide included open‐ended questions addressing perceptions of autonomy, decision‐making processes, relational care experiences, and institutional treatment.

Member checking was conducted by inviting participants to review their interview transcripts; two participants requested clarifications, which were incorporated into the final dataset.

Instruments

The semi‐structured interview guide comprised open‐ended questions focused on obstetric experiences, perceptions of autonomy, institutional practices, and decision‐making processes (Figure 1). Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using a hybrid thematic analysis approach, beginning with inductive open coding grounded in participants' narratives, followed by a theory‐informed deductive phase to organize themes within a multidimensional autonomy framework. Data segments that did not align with the autonomy framework were retained, discussed within the research team, and informed the refinement and consolidation of core dimensions. To ensure transparency in the analytic process, the transition from open coding to the final six‐dimension framework involved iterative discussions within the research team. Codes and preliminary categories that did not fit neatly into the autonomy framework were not discarded; instead, they were carefully reviewed and debated, with some informing the refinement of existing dimensions or the emergence of subthemes. Analytic decisions were negotiated collectively, with attention to preserving participants' nuanced perspectives while maintaining coherence in the theoretical structure.

Ongoing reflexivity was maintained through a research journal documenting analytic decisions, ethical considerations, and researcher positionality.

Rigor Criteria

Methodological rigor was ensured following Guba and Lincoln's (1994) criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Credibility was enhanced through member checking, triangulation among researchers, and peer debriefing. Transferability was supported by detailed descriptions of the research context and participants. Dependability and confirmability were ensured through an audit trail, systematic documentation of analytic decisions, and reflexive journaling.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics and Clinical Research Committee of the University of Barcelona in an ordinary session held on July 26, 2022. All participants provided digital informed consent prior to participation, ensuring the voluntary nature of their involvement.

Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained through alphanumeric coding and secure data storage. The names used in verbatim textual excerpts are fictitious to protect participant confidentiality. The study complied with EU Regulation 2016/679 and Spanish Organic Law 3/2018 on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights.

Results

The analysis identified six analytically distinct yet empirically overlapping dimensions of autonomy through which women experienced obstetric care, derived through iterative engagement between data and theory: informational autonomy; decisional/executive autonomy; bodily autonomy; relational autonomy; emotional–moral autonomy; and self‐identified/individual autonomy. Although analytically distinguishable, these dimensions were closely intertwined in participants' narratives. Constraints in one dimension often affected others, shaping women's overall experiences of care.

Informational Autonomy

Participants consistently reported limited access to clear, comprehensible, and sufficient information, which restricted their ability to understand clinical situations and participate meaningfully in decision‐making. The absence of explanations regarding procedures, alternatives, or potential consequences generated uncertainty, fear, and distress.

María, who had experienced multiple pregnancy losses, stated: “No one explained to us what had happened, what they had done… they left me there without saying anything. All I know is that I was covered in blood.”

Similar experiences were reported by Sara, Laia, Marcela, Lidia, and Gloria, who described confusion and anxiety when faced with unexplained interventions or unilateral clinical decisions. These accounts suggest that informational limitations were embedded in routine clinical interactions rather than isolated events.

Decisional and Executive Autonomy

Even when some information was provided, women frequently described limited opportunities to influence decisions about obstetric procedures or to enact their expressed preferences. Decisions were often overridden due to administrative constraints, institutional protocols, or professional judgment.

María explained that her documented birth plan was disregarded:

I didn't want an episiotomy. They didn't give a damn.

Gloria described requesting a caesarean section after prolonged labor, only to be refused until complications escalated: “They said, No, no, no, no, this is a normal delivery… and then they had to rush to do the C‐section.” Participants perceived these situations as a lack of shared reasoning and involvement, even when clinical decisions may have been medically justified. Institutional rigidity and power asymmetries limited both women's decisional autonomy and their capacity to act on decisions already made.

Bodily Autonomy

Participants frequently reported experiences in which their bodily integrity was compromised through procedures performed without prior explanation or explicit consent. Vaginal examinations, episiotomies, sutures, and exposure of the body were often described as routine practices carried out without consultation.

Paula reflected: “I feel violated when they do that technique… they don't explain anything, they just do it.”

Laia described being left exposed during an examination: “They left me naked, with the door wide open. Anyone walking by could see me.”

Such experiences were perceived as invasive and disempowering, contributing to a loss of control over one's own body within obstetric care.

Relational Autonomy

Relational autonomy was constrained when women were deprived of the presence of supportive companions during critical moments such as childbirth, miscarriage, or abortion. Despite explicitly requesting accompaniment, several participants described being left alone.

Lidia, reflecting on her miscarriage, stated:

You're completely alone at a moment when you most need support.

Similar accounts were shared by Sara, whose partner was excluded during a procedure, and Paula, who was unable to inform or contact her family. The absence of relational support intensified feelings of vulnerability and emotional isolation.

Emotional‐Moral Autonomy

Participants frequently described a lack of emotional support and the dismissal or moral judgment of their feelings and value‐based decisions. Women reported feeling treated as passive recipients of care rather than as moral agents whose emotions and values were relevant to clinical decisions.

Sara noted: “They treated me like just another number… this was my birth, my moment.”

Other participants recounted experiences of verbal abuse, stigmatization, or threats, which they perceived as undermining their moral authority over reproductive decisions. These interactions contributed to feelings of dehumanization and emotional distress.

Maria reflected on her experience: “I tried to explain why I didn't want certain interventions, but they looked at me like I was being difficult. It felt like my feelings didn't count at all.”

Marcela commented: “They told me I was exaggerating my emotions. It was as if my fear and wishes had no value compared to their protocols.”

Dora shared: “I wanted to make certain decisions about my birth according to my beliefs, but every time I mentioned them, they made me feel guilty or wrong.”

Self‐Identified and Individual Autonomy

Women described difficulties acting in ways consistent with their personal identities, values, and life plans. Decisions that diverged from dominant expectations of motherhood or compliance were sometimes questioned or ridiculed by healthcare professionals.

Laia recalled how her refusal of sterilization during a caesarean section was dismissed:

I said I didn't want anything irreversible, and they told me I was being dramatic.

Participants also reported limited opportunities to exercise informed, independent decision‐making due to lack of information and institutional paternalism. As Laia stated: “I didn't even know a birth plan existed.”

These experiences illustrate how institutional norms and information asymmetries constrained women's sense of authenticity and independent agency.

Historical Illustrative Case

One participant (76 years) described obstetric experiences from the 1970s. Her narrative illustrates historical changes in care practices and greater contemporary recognition of autonomy and dignity. This case is presented for contextual and illustrative purposes and was not treated as analytically equivalent to the remaining participants.

Summary of Findings

Collectively, participants' narratives depict recurring constraints on autonomy across informational, decisional, bodily, relational, emotional, and identity‐related dimensions. These experiences highlight how everyday institutional practices and power asymmetries shaped women's engagement with obstetric care and influenced their sense of dignity and agency.

Discussion

This qualitative study examines women's lived experiences of autonomy in obstetric care across pregnancy, childbirth, abortion, and the postpartum period, situating these experiences within the broader structural context often described in the literature as OV. In this study OV functions as a contextual lens rather than the main analytical focus. Using a theory‐informed, multidimensional framework comprising six interrelated dimensions of autonomy, the analysis contributes to existing literature by illustrating how constraints on autonomy extend beyond the formal informed consent requirements and are embedded in everyday clinical practices (Restrepo‐Sánchez 2025). Consistent with relational and feminist conceptualizations, autonomy emerges as a contextual, embodied, and relational process enacted, and limited, through interactions with healthcare professionals, institutional norms, and organizational structures (Urzúa 2016). Autonomy, rather than OV, constitutes the primary analytical category, operationalized through six interrelated dimensions that emerged through iterative engagement between data and theory (Figure 2).

Six‐dimension analytical framework of women's autonomy in obstetric care within a structural context.

Participants' accounts highlighted specific constraints across multiple dimensions of autonomy. For example, regarding informational autonomy, women consistently reported receiving insufficient, unclear, or inaccessible information about procedures, risks, benefits, and alternatives. Participants described feeling confused, fearful, and lacking control over decision‐making, experiences that echo findings in previous studies (Ely et al. 2025; Nicholls et al. 2022; Sturgeon et al. 2021; Perrotte et al. 2020; Shrivastava and Sivakami 2020). These empirical descriptions suggest that informational asymmetries may operate as structural constraints on meaningful participation in decision‐making (O'Brien and Newport 2023).

Similarly, decisional autonomy was constrained when women's preferences were disregarded, even after prior discussion or documentation. Participants described hierarchical and protocol‐oriented care environments, where standardized practices often superseded individual preferences. These narratives indicate tensions between institutional imperatives, risk management, and patient‐centered care, without imposing evaluative judgment in the reporting of their experiences (Yuill et al. 2020; Begley et al. 2019).

Executive autonomy, of the capacity to enact decisions, was limited by systemic factors including administrative barriers, limited resources, and rigid institutional procedures. Participants' accounts describe these barriers as practical impediments to exercising autonomy, illustrating the gap between formal rights and actualized capabilities (Eichinger et al. 2024; Leahy‐Warren et al. 2021).

Bodily autonomy emerged as a central concern. Participants reported experiencing procedures performed without sufficient explanation or consent, which they found distressing and disempowering. These testimonies highlight the body as a site of power imbalance within obstetric care, providing empirical evidence rather than normative judgment (Mengesha et al. 2020).

Emotional and relational dimensions were similarly affected. Women reported limited emotional support, particularly in situations involving loss, complications, or neonatal harm. Participants also described restrictions on access to supportive companions during critical moments, reinforcing feelings of isolation and vulnerability (Garcia 2023; Silva‐Fernández et al. 2025).

Participants' experiences of moral and self‐identified autonomy further illustrate how gendered expectations and social norms can undermine confidence and trust in healthcare systems (Delgado‐Herrera et al. 2024). Taken together, these findings suggest that autonomy in obstetric care may be constrained across multiple, interacting dimensions. From an ethical perspective, these patterns raise questions regarding how autonomy is supported in practice. From a nursing perspective, these patterns indicate that enhancing communication, relational support, and institutional responsiveness may contribute to practices that are more consistent with professional commitments to autonomy and dignity. Organizational policies and nursing education that attend to these dimensions may support the translation of formally recognized autonomy into everyday clinical practice.

Finally, self‐identified and individual autonomy were undermined by gendered expectations, stereotypes related to motherhood, and limited access to information. Consistent with previous research, such dynamics can erode women's confidence in their own judgment and reduce trust in healthcare systems over time (Delgado‐Herrera et al. 2024). Taken together, participants' accounts are compatible with interpretations in the literature that frame OV as a structural phenomenon arising from the intersection of medicalization, institutional rigidity, and gendered power relations, rather than as a series of isolated events (Brunello et al. 2024; Faheem 2022; Galego Carrillo 2015). Understanding how autonomy is constrained across multiple dimensions provides a more comprehensive account of women's experiences in obstetric care.

From a nursing science perspective, these findings resonate with relational and person‐centered models of care that conceptualize autonomy as embedded in everyday clinical interactions rather than confined to formal consent processes. Frameworks such as midwifery‐led continuity of care and the Fundamentals of Care emphasize relational presence, communication, and responsiveness to individual needs as core components of ethical practice. The multidimensional understanding of autonomy proposed in this study provides an empirically grounded lens through which nurses and midwives may critically examine how institutional routines, time pressures, and hierarchical structures shape women's capacity to exercise agency in practice. From a theoretical perspective, autonomy can be understood not only as an ethical principle but also as a practice‐based competency requiring organizational support, reflective training, and professional accountability.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the sample size was small (n = 9), which may limit the range of perspectives. However, this size aligns with qualitative designs prioritizing depth over statistical generalizability. Sufficient information power was achieved regarding women's experiences of autonomy in obstetric care, given the richness of the narratives and the clear research aim.

Second, recruitment through digital platforms may have excluded women with limited digital access or literacy, potentially underrepresenting some sociodemographic groups and affecting transferability.

Third, retrospective interviews may reflect memory recall and emotional processing rather than objective events. From a qualitative perspective, these narratives represent participants' meaningful interpretations, though temporal distance may have influenced how experiences were recounted.

Fourth, the inclusion of one older participant with obstetric experiences from decades earlier adds temporal heterogeneity. Treated as illustrative, this case may affect sample coherence. Future comparative or diachronic research could better examine intergenerational changes in autonomy.

Finally, the multidisciplinary research team (gender studies, bioethics, reproductive health) enriched the analysis but may have shaped the analytical lens. Reflexive practices and analytic triangulation were used to enhance trustworthiness.

Importantly, the sampling strategy may have favored participants who experienced constraints or tensions in care. As a result, experiences of strongly positive or largely unproblematic autonomy may be underrepresented, which should be considered when interpreting the findings and assessing their transferability to broader populations.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the understanding of women's experiences of autonomy in obstetric care by demonstrating how autonomy is enacted, negotiated, and constrained across multiple interrelated dimensions within everyday clinical practice. Rather than framing OV solely as a series of isolated events or procedural failures, the findings highlight how constraints on autonomy are embedded in institutional routines, professional hierarchies, and dominant biomedical models of care.

The analysis of six interrelated dimensions of autonomy underscores that autonomy cannot be adequately protected through access to information or formal decision‐making opportunities alone. Instead, its exercise depends on relational, emotional, bodily, and organizational conditions that enable women to participate meaningfully in their care. When these conditions are absent, autonomy may be formally acknowledged yet practically limited.

By foregrounding women's narratives, this study illustrates how institutional structures and care practices shape experiences of dignity, agency, and vulnerability across different reproductive contexts. These findings reinforce the value of conceptualizing autonomy as situated and relational, emphasizing that individual choices are influenced by broader social, interpersonal, and organizational environments.

From an ethical and professional standpoint, constraints on autonomy in obstetric care raise important concerns for nursing and midwifery practice. Understanding how autonomy operates across multiple dimensions may support more reflective and ethically attuned approaches to care that attend not only to clinical outcomes but also to women's lived experiences.

Implications for Clinical Practice

The findings of this study suggest that supporting women's autonomy in obstetric care requires attention to its multidimensional nature, including informational, decisional, bodily, emotional, and relational aspects. Constraints on autonomy were shown to arise not only from individual interactions but also from institutional norms, clinical routines, and organizational conditions.

For nursing professionals, these results highlight the importance of practices that foster transparent communication, relational continuity, and emotional support throughout the reproductive process. Nurses are often uniquely positioned to facilitate shared understanding, advocate for women's expressed preferences, and support meaningful participation in care decisions.

Incorporating relational and gender‐sensitive perspectives into nursing education and clinical training may contribute to greater awareness of how power dynamics and institutional constraints affect women's autonomy. At the organizational level, policies and care models that enable flexibility, continuity, and collaborative decision‐making may further support autonomy‐oriented practice.

Overall, a practice‐based understanding of autonomy may assist nursing and midwifery professionals in aligning ethical commitments with everyday clinical realities, promoting care that is respectful, responsive, and attentive to women's dignity and agency.
